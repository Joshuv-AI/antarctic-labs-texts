Study on the chloride ion binding rate of sulfoaluminate cement mortars containing different mineral admixtures
Authors: Yuhang Wei, Zhonglu Cao, Jinfang Hou, Bin Li, Guanyuan Jia, Pang Chen, Ping Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the chloride ion (Cl−) binding rate of sulfoaluminate cement (SAC) mortars containing different mineral admixtures was investigated. This is essential to improve the durability of concrete structures in high Cl− environments, especially where they are susceptible to Cl− attack such as coastlines and marine structures. The effects of Cl− concentration, curing age, and the type and amount of mineral admixture on the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortars were analyzed. It was found that the content of water-soluble Cl− in SAC mortars decreased with the increase of curing age, while the Cl− binding ratio increased accordingly, indicating that its resistance to internal Cl− permeation increased. The addition of fly ash (FA) and ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS) can significantly improve the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortars, and the Cl− binding rate increases to 46.6% with 20% of FA and 38.7% with 40% of GGBS. The effects of mineral admixtures on the microstructure and phase composition of SAC mortars were further investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. The results showed that the addition of FA and GGBS promoted the formation of C-S-H (calcium silicate hydrate) gels and improved the resistance of SAC mortars to Cl− penetration. On the other hand, the excessive addition of silica fume (SF) decreased the Cl− binding rate, whereas a moderate amount of limestone powder (LP) improved the Cl− binding rate. The study of the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortars can help to evaluate their resistance to Cl− erosion in real projects, thus guiding the optimization of concrete formulations and the improvement of durability.Keywords: SAC, Cl− content, Mineral admixtures, Curing ageSubject terms: Composites, Civil engineering

Introduction

SAC is a low-carbon cement known for its high early strength, good frost resistance, and low energy consumption in production1. However, SAC hydrates quickly and sets rapidly, resulting in a significant loss of workability over time. This results in critically short project construction times and problems with strength loss in later stages1. The formation and decomposition of ettringite (AFt) are related to temperature and the gypsum content within the system2. With sufficient gypsum content, calcium sulfoaluminate in SAC reacts with sulfate ions to form AFt, which is a primary source of strength for SACs2,3. As the hydration reaction proceeds and the gypsum content gradually decreases, it leads to the formation of low sulphate calcium sulfoaluminate hydration products (AFm)2,3. The decrease in the AFt/AFm ratio in the hydration products is one of the reasons for the decrease in the strength of the samples3.

In order to adapt the performance of cement mortar to the requirements of different projects and different environments, mineral admixtures and additives are usually added to cement mortar to improve the cement performance4,5. Mineral admixtures are waste by-products of the industrial production process, which can be used as alternative cementitious materials to cement and reduce the amount of cement6. Benli et al.6 reported the high temperature performance of steel fibre reinforced self-compacting concrete prepared by replacing Portland cement with pumice powder (GPP) at different substitution rates. The results showed that at a temperature of 200 °C, the compressive strength of the control and GPP10 mixes were increased by 5.34% and 3.55%, respectively6. Dener et al. used different proportions of basalt powder (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35 and 40%) and 10% SF as a partial replacement for silicate cement7. The study showed that the mixing of basalt powder with SF increased the strength of concrete, and the use of basalt powder had a positive effect on sulfate attack7.

FA, GGBS, SF and LP are mainly used as mineral admixtures for normal silicate cements, where FA, GGBS and SF can be used as lubricants to improve the grading of the system and the fluidity of the mortar after addition8–11. At the same time, it can fill the voids in cementitious materials and affect the structural density8–12. LP admixture is a cost-effective and readily available natural powder. It has a limited effect on the hydration reaction of cement mortar, but can provide a filling effect and improve the pore structure12. However, there are fewer studies on the Cl− binding rates of SACs doped with FA, GGBS, SF and LP6–12.

In the construction of marine engineering structures in coastal and island areas, it is common to use seawater sea sand with excessive free Cl− content in the proportioning of concrete structures13. This results in excessive Cl− content in the pore liquid phase of the hardened cement paste, causing corrosion of steel reinforcement13. Therefore, it is necessary to study the resistance of SAC mortars to the erosion of Cl− inside the material, which is also an urgent problem to be solved at this stage to promote the application of SAC in offshore structures. Cl− exist in two states within the cement mortar, free and bound Cl− 14,15. Only free Cl− cause corrosion of reinforcing bars inside reinforced concrete structures, not bound Cl− 16–18. One part of free Cl- will be adsorbed on the C-S-H gel by physical bonding. The other part of free Cl− will react with the AFm phase to form the Friedel’s salt (F salt) by chemical bonding. Therefore, increasing the amount of Cl- bound by hydration products (C–S–H gel and F salt) can reduce the free Cl- on the steel bar surface, which can improve the ability of the material to resist Cl− erosion.

SAC mortars containing various mineral admixtures differ in the type of ions in the liquid phase, the alkalinity of the pore fluid, and the amount of hydration products. This results in a different ability of SAC mortar to bind to free Cl-, which in turn affects the resistance of SAC mortar to Cl- erosion inside the material8–12. Therefore, this study investigated the Cl− resistance of SAC mortar materials containing different mineral admixtures at different Cl− concentrations and curing ages. This provides an experimental basis for its wide application in reinforced concrete structures.

SAC mortar material preparation

After mixing the SAC mortar, it was immediately poured into a curing box (type YH-40B) under preset conditions and cured for 24 h. The temperature of the curing box was set at 20 ± 1 ℃ and the relative humidity was not less than 90%. At the end of the curing process, the cured samples were removed from the curing box (SBY-40 C) and immediately demoulded. Subsequently, the samples were placed in water at a controlled temperature of 20 ℃± 1 ℃ to continue the curing process until the required age (0, 6, 28, 56 days) of the test was reached. XRD test

The phase structures were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Panalytical XPert3) using Cu Kα radiation with a wavelength of 0.15418 nm. After the SAC mortar underwent 1 day standard curing and 28 days water curing, samples were taken in the middle of the mortar. After grinding, the samples were screened with a 63 μm sieve to obtain XRD samples. The operating parameters for the XRD test were 40 kV voltage, 30 mA current, and the test angle 2θ was in the range of 5° to 90° with a step size of 0.02°, and the time spacing of 0.1 s.

Results and discussion

Working performance of SAC mortar materials

To ensure that SAC mortar materials containing mineral admixtures have good flow properties in engineering applications, i.e. to meet the basic working performance requirements and prevent water seepage phenomena in engineering applications. The test was conducted on the mineral admixture in 10–40% admixture proportion of water consumption analysis. Figure 6 shows the water consumption of FA, GGBS, SF, and LP in SAC mortars while maintaining the same working performance. It was observed that with the increase in the dosage of FA and GGBS, the required water consumption decreased gradually, and the water reduction effect of FA was greater than that of GGBS. This was because the FA particles were smaller than cement particles, enabling them to fill in the cement particles and form a better grading effect, thereby increasing slurry fluidity. In addition, the incorporation of FA increased the number of anisotropic charges within the cementitious material, which destroyed the flocculating structure of the original slurry and improved the cement mortar fluidity21. The mechanism of GGBS was similar to that of FA, but due to the irregular shape of GGBS particles, the effect of GGBS in improving the flow properties of cement mortar was weaker than that of FA.

Effect of mineral admixtures on water consumption of concrete mixtures.

On the other hand, the required water consumption of sulfoaluminate cementitious materials decreased and then increased with the increase of SF. The water consumption was minimized at 20% and 30% SF content. Since the average particle size of SF was between 0.1 and 0.15 μm, and the distribution was relatively uniform. These characteristics made SF admixture played a lubricating role, thereby increasing slurry fluidity and reducing slurry water requirement22. When the amount of SF was excessive, it tended to agglomerate due to its specific surface being between 15-27m2/g. This leads to a decrease in slurry fluidity21, and resulting in increased water consumption for maintaining constant slurry fluidity. The water consumption required for sulfoaluminate cementitious materials increased with the increase of LP. This was mainly due to the addition of LP increasing the specific surface area of the aggregate, resulting in a further increase in the water consumption of the mortar23.

Water-soluble Cl− analysis

Figure 7 shows that the mass of water-soluble Cl− in all SAC mortars decrease with increasing curing age at different Cl− concentrations (from 0.5 to 3% of the total cementitious material). It is worth mentioning that in order to quantify the Cl− content in SAC mortar, the experimentally measured Cl− content was multiplied by the density of cement (2350 g/cm2) to express the total mass of Cl− per unit volume of cement. As shown in Fig. 7a, the addition of 20% FA, LP and SF minerals slightly reduced the water-soluble Cl− content in SAC mortar when the Cl− concentration was 0.5%, with FA and SF having a more significant effect. The water-soluble Cl− content in SAC mortar with the addition of FA and LP was quite close to 5.01 g and 4.99 g at the curing age of 56 days, respectively.

In Fig. 7b, the addition of 10–30% FA and 20% and 40% GGBS significantly reduced the water-soluble Cl− content in SAC mortar at 1.0% Cl− concentration. At the curing age of 56 days, the SAC mortar with 20% FA exhibited the lowest water-soluble Cl− content of 9.62 g, followed by FA-10 with 9.83 g. When the Cl− concentration in SAC mortar was 2.0%, the water-soluble Cl− content of SAC mortar with 20% FA, GGBS, SF, and LP was lower than that of pure SAC mortar at the curing age of 3 days, as shown in Fig. 7c. In addition, at the curing age of 56 days, the water-soluble Cl− content of SAC mortar with 20% FA was the lowest, which was 18.79 g. As shown in Fig. 7d, when the Cl− concentration in SAC mortar was 3.0%, the water-soluble Cl− content of SAC mortar with 20% FA and GGBS was lower than that of the other SAC mortars at the curing age of 3 days. And the water-soluble Cl− content was lower than that of the other SAC mortars at the curing age of 56 days. The water-soluble Cl− content of SAC mortar with 20% FA and GGBS remained lower than that of other SAC mortars after 3 days of cement curing, and the water-soluble Cl− content of SAC mortar with 56 days of cement curing was 30.45 g and 29.79 g respectively.

The results indicated that the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortars increased as the curing age increased. The improvement in binding rate can be attributed to several key factors: (1) the continuous hydration reaction increases the content of C-S-H gel, which has a significant adsorption capacity to effectively immobilize free Cl− due to its large specific surface area (up to 3000 m²/g)16,17; (2) the concentration of Ca(SO)4 decreases with increasing curing age as it participates in the chemical reaction leading to formation of AFt (ettringite, CaO·Al2O3·3CaSO4·32H2O) and AFm (hydrated calcium sulfoaluminate, 3CaO·Al2O3·CaSO4·12H2O) phases, with the unstable AFt phase transforming into the more stable AFm phase24,25. During this transformation, free Cl− migrate within the material and react with the AFm phase to form F salt. This is an effective Cl− immobilization mechanism that reduces the amount of free Cl− in SAC mortars through physical adsorption and chemical binding24,25.

Pure SAC mortar exhibited a rapid hydration rate before the curing age reached 3 days, leading to the substantial production of C-S-H gel. This gel physically binds with free Cl−, resulting in a swift decrease in the content of water-soluble Cl−. However, as the curing age progresses, the rate of C-S-H formation diminished, which slowed the reduction of water-soluble Cl− 26. The introduction of mineral admixtures (FA, GGBS, SF, LP) slightly reduced the hydration rate of SAC mortar during the initial 3 days of curing. Notably, the incorporation of FA and GGBS significantly decreased the free Cl− content in SAC mortar after 3 days. With the increase of curing age, the active substances in FA and GGBS underwent secondary hydration reactions with the cement hydration product Ca(OH)2, producing more C-S-H gel, which led to a lower Cl− content26.

The reactive silicates and aluminates in FS and GGBS reacted with Ca2+ in cement to produce additional C-S-H gel and other hydration products. The fine particles of FS and GGBS also filled the voids between cement particles, which optimized the microstructure of cement and enhancing its density12. Furthermore, the reactivity of FS and GGBS was particularly pronounced in alkaline environments, where the alkalinity generated during cement hydration promotes the dissolution of their active ingredients8–12. The active ingredients could react with Ca(OH)2 to form the stable hydration products8–12. SF, with its high SiO2 content, underwent a pozzolanic reaction with calcium hydroxide in highly alkaline conditions to generate C-S-H gel12. While SF significantly impacted the hydration process, its role primarily lay in providing additional C-S-H gel through chemical reactions, rather than directly altering the rate of hydration12,23. LP, mainly composed of CaCO3, exhibited a weaker capacity for direct participation in the hydration reaction12,23. It increased the density of the cement mainly by physical action and made very little chemical change to the hydration process.

Analysis of Cl− binding rate

In engineering and construction materials analysis, the acid-soluble Cl− content was often used as an approximate estimate of the total Cl− content of cement. This practice is based on the assumption that under acid treatment conditions, all Cl− in the cement are completely released and dissolved in solution. Therefore, the acid-soluble Cl− content can be used as an approximate estimate of the total Cl− content. Here, samples of SAC mortar with different mix proportions at various ages all originated from the same mortar batch, differing only in their curing times. Hence, the acid-soluble Cl− content was related only to the internal Cl− concentration and the soaking time of the samples during the test preparation process27,28. When the soaking time was insufficient or the agitation was not thorough, cement failed to fully dissolve in the dilute nitric acid solution, leading to a measured acid-soluble Cl− content that was lower than the actual value. Therefore, when calculating the Cl− binding rate, the highest acid-soluble Cl− content measured in each mix proportion was taken as its total Cl− content29,30. The Cl− binding rate, denoted as δ, was introduced to characterize the resistance of SAC mortar containing mineral admixtures to internal Cl− erosion. The results indicated that the Cl− binding ability of SAC mortar increased with the extension of curing age. This enhancement in binding rate was closely related to the hydration degree of SAC mortar. With the passage of time, more hydration products such as C–S–H gel and AFm phases were formed, thereby increasing the capacity of SAC mortar to bind Cl−. Trend in the mass of acid soluble Cl− in both pure SAC mortar and SAC mortar blended with various mineral admixtures, across a range of (a) 0.5%, (b) 1.0%, (c) 2.0%, and (d) 3.0% Cl− concentrations, as a function of increasing curing age.

In addition, at the curing age of 56 days, the Cl− binding rate of SAC decreased as the doping concentration increases from 0.5 to 3%. The main reasons are as follows: (I) the adsorption sites in C–S–H gels adsorb not only Cl−, but also SO42− and OH−. As the concentration of Cl− increases, SO42− and OH− compete with Cl- for these adsorption sites31. Therefore, as the Cl− concentration increases, the available adsorption sites on the C–S–H gel may be occupied by SO42− and OH−, resulting in a decrease in the Cl− binding rate31; (II) The C–S–H gels release SO42− and OH− upon Cl− adsorption. These released ions increase the concentration of SO42− and OH− in the solution, thus increasing their ability to compete with Cl− for adsorption sites32. Therefore, as the concentration of Cl− increases, the concentration of released SO42− and OH− also increases, further inhibiting the Cl− adsorption32; (III) When the concentration of SO42− is high, it reacts with the F salt in the C–S–H gel to form the more stable Cl− and AFt phases, whereas when the concentration of OH− is high, OH- occupies the Cl− binding sites on the F-salt structure to form the OH−-AFm phase32. The structural stability relationship of these generators is SO4 − 2-AFm > F salt > OH−-AFm33. These reactions reduce the effective sites available for Cl− adsorption, thus decreasing the binding rate of Cl−.

The reason is as follows: Competition for adsorption: The adsorption sites in C–S–H gels not only adsorb Cl−, but also SO42− and OH−. When the concentration of Cl− increases, SO42− and OH− compete with Cl− for these adsorption sites. Therefore, as the concentration of Cl− increases, the available adsorption sites on the C-S-H gel may be occupied by SO42− and OH−, resulting in a decrease in the Cl− binding rate. Release Effect: C–S–H gels release SO42− and OH− upon Cl− adsorption. These released ions increase the concentration of SO42− and OH− in the solution, thus enhancing their ability to compete with Cl− for adsorption sites. Therefore, as the concentration of Cl− increases, the concentration of released SO42− and OH− also increases, further inhibiting the Cl− adsorption. Structural stability effects: When the concentration of SO42− is high, it reacts with the F salt in the C–S–H gel to form the more stable Cl− and AFt phases, whereas when the concentration of OH− is high, OH− occupies the Cl− binding sites on the F-salt structure to form the OH−-AFm phase. These reactions reduce the effective sites available for Cl− adsorption, thus decreasing the binding rate of Cl−. Therefore, the binding rate of Cl− in C–S–H gels decreases with the increase of the concentration of internally doped Cl−.

The W/B of samples doped with 10% FA, 10% GGBS, and 20% GGBS was similar to that of pure SAC mortar, as shown in Table 2. Figure 10 depicts the effect of these admixtures on the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortar. The results indicated that the incorporating FA and GGBS significantly enhanced the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortar. The hydration products were mainly C–S–H gels with the ability to adsorb Cl−. The gel could fill the pores and interstices of the cement stone, making the cement matrix denser and making it more difficult for Cl− to pass through. Reactive components such as silicates and aluminates contained in FA and GGBS could react with hydration products (e.g., AFt), thus slowing down the early hydration rate of the cement. The results showed that at the initial stage (1 day and 3 days), the samples doped with FA and mineral GGBS had similar Cl− binding rates as pure SAC mortar.

Effect of FA and GGBS on Cl− binding ability of SAC mortar.

As the time was increased to 28 days, the Cl− binding rate of the FA- and GGBS-doped samples was significantly increased compared to the pure SAC mortar. The Cl− binding rates for samples with 10% FA, 10% GGBS, and 20% GGBS content after 56 days increasing by 10.3%, 6.3% and 12%, respectively. The main reasons are as follows: (1) the FA and GGBS addition increases the Al2O3 and SiO2 content in the cement matrix, leading to the formation of AFm phases during hydration, which chemically combines with Cl− to form F salt, thereby enhancing the chemical binding rate of Cl− [16,26; (2) Silicates in FA and GGBS can react with calcium hydrates (e.g., Ca(OH)2) in cement to form a calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel. This gel is more stable than the C–S–H gel formed by the cement itself and is able to capture Cl− more efficiently16,26,32,34; (3) Aluminates in FA and GGBS can react with calcium hydrate compounds in cement to form calcium-aluminum hydrate (C-A-H) gels or alumina (AFt)16,26. These products effectively bind Cl−, limiting their diffusion and penetration into the concrete16,26,32,34; (4) FA and GGBS have a fine particle structure that fills the microscopic pores in the cement matrix and significantly improves the densification of the cement matrix16,26. Therefore, SAC mortar containing FA and GGBS exhibited an improved Cl− binding rate compared to the pure SAC mortar. Furthermore, as the GGBS content increases, the clinker content in SAC mortar gradually decreases, leading to the decrease of hydration products and Ca(OH)2, which affects the stability of F salt16,26. This weakened the chemical binding of Cl− by GGBS-containing SAC mortar, leading to an enhancement of the Cl− binding of SAC mortar with increasing GGBS content.

Figure 11 showcases the impact of different FA and GGBS contents on the Cl− binding ability of SAC mortar. In Fig. 11a, the Cl− binding rate of the cement matrix increased with the addition of FA. The optimal FA content was 20%, which was 46.6% higher than that of control group. The results from Fig. 10 indicated that the addition of FA improved the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortar. However, the Cl− binding rate of the 20% FA sample was higher than for the 30% FA sample. This phenomenon was due to the fact that an increase in FA content led to a decrease in the W/B of the sample containing FA. This resulted in a tighter pore structure within the hydrated product. This density hindered the transport of Cl− and weakened the Cl− binding rate. The decrease of W/B had a more significant effect on Cl− binding rate than the increase of FA content. When the FA content increased from 30 to 40%, the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortar increased by 11.3% and 21.4%, respectively. At this stage, the reduction in the W/B was small, so its effect on the Cl− binding rate was less than the increase of FA content.

Effect of different FA and GGBS contents on Cl− binding ability of SAC mortar.

Figure 11b despite a gradual decrease in the W/B of samples containing GGBS. The Cl− binding rate consistently improved with an increase in GGBS content. According to the results shown in Fig. 10, incorporating GGBS could increase the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortar. The reduction in the W/B generally weakened the cement matrix’s Cl− binding rate. However, the increase of GGBS content from 10 to 40% had a more substantial positive impact on the Cl− binding rate than the negative effect of a lowered W/B. The Cl− binding rates for the 56 days samples with 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% GGBS content increased by 6.3%, 12.2%, 32.9%, and 38.7%, respectively, compared to the control group.

Figure 12 depicts the effect of SF and LP on the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortar. In Fig. 12a, the relationship between the curing age and the Cl− binding rate of cement mortars incorporating 20% SF and LP is depicted. The results showed that the incorporation of 20% LP significantly increased the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortar. The incorporation of SF decreased the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortar. The water-cement ratio of SAC mortar changed from 0.39 to 0.28 and 0.53 after the incorporation of SF and LP, as shown in Table 2. This means that the addition of LP made the pore structure denser and less susceptible to erosion by water and other chemicals, whereas SF caused the structure to loosen.

Figure 12b demonstrates the influence of SF and LP at varying Cl− concentrations on the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortar. With SF at a 20% content, the Cl− binding rates were lower than that of the control samples in the range of 0.5–3% Cl− concentration, which decreased by 33.4%, 27.2%, 18.3%, and 27.3%, respectively. This indicated that SF inclusion failed to improve the resistance of SAC mortar to internal Cl− penetration. The effects of SF on SAC mortar include: (1) reducing the Ca/Si ratio of C–S–H gel, thereby diminishing its physical adsorption capacity for Cl− due to the adsorption capacity being related to the zeta potential between cations and the double layer; (2) lowering cement clinker content, thus reducing hydration product formation and Cl− binding rate; (3) reducing capillary pore numbers, which hinders free Cl− diffusion; (4) the secondary hydration reaction between SiO2 in SF and Ca(OH)2 produces an abundance of C–S–H gel, leading to a decrease in system pH and stability of both C–S–H gel and F salt12,20,26.

Conversely, the samples with 20% LP content showed a significant increase in Cl− binding rate compared to the control group. In the range of 0.5–3% Cl− concentration, the Cl− binding rate increased by 40.8%, 59.3%, 27.9% and 37.8%, respectively. This was attributed to the higher W/B in LP samples, which increased capillary pore numbers and enhanced Cl− transport and binding within the structure. Additionally, Ca(CO)3, the main component of LP, lacked pozzolanic activity, and thus the system maintained a high Ph. This favored the stability of F salts and C–S–H12,20,24. Finally, although the LP incorporation significantly reduced the content of cement clinker and aluminum phases, which could be regarded as unfavorable for Cl− binding. The effect of increasing the W/B on the Cl− binding rate was significantly greater than the effect of the reduction in cement clinker.

Hydration products and hydration mechanism analysis

The main components of SAC mortar clinker are dicalcium silicate (C2S) and anhydrous calcium sulfosalicate (C4A3S)35. The main hydration products are AFt (AFt), low-sulfurhydrated calcium sulfosalicate (3CaO·Al2O3·CaSO4·18H2O, AFm), Al2O3-3H2O (AH3), and C–S–H gel36. The total amount and distribution state of the hydration products greatly affect the compactness of SAC mortar samples35–37.

The formation of the AFt phase generally involves the following three steps: (1) The dissolution of cement components in water produces ions such as Ca2+, SO42−, OH−, and AlO₂−; (2) these ions combine with H2O to form the basic structural units of AFt, (Ca6(Al(OH)6)2·24 H2O)6+; (3) these basic structural units then react with SO42− and H₂O to precipitate AFt crystals36,37. The morphology of AFt in SAC mortar is related to the concentration of Ca(OH)2 in the liquid phase36,37; when the mass fraction of Ca(OH)2 in the cement paste is high, the AFt typically forms in needle-like shapes, as shown in Eq. (4). When the mass fraction of Ca(OH)2 in the cement paste is low, AFt generally appears in rod-like or columnar shapes, as indicated by Eq. (5). This occurs as a higher OH− concentration in the liquid phase leads to increased supersaturation for the AFt formation, which in turn aids in the creation of the fundamental structural units of AFt36,37.

The cross-linking of fine needle-shaped AFt crystals can form a fibrous, net-like structure in the cementitious system, enhancing its toughness. However, the continued formation of the AFt phase after the system hardens can lead to volumetric expansion and the development of microcracks, reducing the structural strength38. A high content of AH3 phase and a low content of AFt phase in hydration products of SAC mortar result in AH3 filling the pores of the SAC mortar38, leading to a denser system with the formation of columnar AFt phase. The hydration reaction progresses with C2S reacting with AH3 gel to produce C2ASH8, filling the interstitial voids and forming a dense structure.

The stability and structural composition of AFm in cement paste are influenced by the type of anions and the alkalinity of the system, usually taking a hexagonal plate shape. AFm phases usually include SO42−-AFm (C3A·CaSO4·12H2O), OH-AFm (C3A·Ca(OH)2·12H2O), CO3-AFm (C3A·CaCO3·11H2O), and Cl−AFm (F salt, C3A·CaCl2·10H2O)2,3,20,24. And An excess of SO4²⁻ is unfavorable for the formation of F salt. On the other hand, C–S–H gel varies in chemical composition with the silica-to-calcium ratio and the water-to-silica ratio. Two main sources of C–S–H gel in SAC mortar with mineral admixtures are: the hydration of dicalcium silicate in cement clinker to form C–S–H gel, and the secondary hydration of mineral admixtures like FA, which possess a pozzolanic effect, reacting with Ca(OH)2 released during clinker hydration to form C–S–H gel26,32,34. XRD analysis

Figure 14 shows the XRD spectra of pure SAC mortar and SAC mortar blended with FA, GGBS, SF, and LP after a curing age of 28 days, with a 1% Cl− content. In Fig. 14a, the diffraction peak near 9.1° shows that the intensity of the diffraction peak of AFt is slightly lower in the sample with 10% FA than that of the pure SAC mortar. Because there were still unhydrated FA particles at 28 days, and the addition of FA reduced the relative content of cement clinker, which led to a decrease in the content of hydration products, Aft36–38. Furthermore, it was observed that the intensity of the AFt diffraction peaks for SAC mortar with 10% and 20% GGBS content was higher than that of the pure SAC mortar. The results indicated that the crystallinity and content of AFt in the samples with 10% and 20% GGBS content were higher than that of pure SAC mortar. The increase in the amount of GGBS led to the emergence of more AFt19. Two reasons account for this result: the XRD spectrum of the sample with 10% GGBS content shows a higher intensity of the CaSO4 diffraction peak at 25.5°, inhibiting the conversion of AFt to AFm. The Ca(OH)2 diffraction peak intensity at 17.8° is high, suggesting that the AFt mainly forms well-crystallized acicular crystals.

XRD spectra of pure SAC mortar and SAC mortar blended with FA, GGBS, SF, and LP after a curing age of 28 days (1% Cl− content).

As shown in Fig. 14a, the diffraction peaks of C–S–H for pure SAC mortar, samples with 10% FA content, and samples with 10% and 20% GGBS content all appear near 26.7°. The intensity of the C–S–H diffraction peak could reflect the content of C–S–H gel in the system. Compared to the pure SAC mortar sample, the sample with 10% FA content possessed the lowest content of both AFt and C–S–H gel, exhibiting lower macroscopic resistance to Cl− penetration. The samples with 10% and 20% GGBS content had slightly lowerC–S–H gels but significantly higher AFt content. This indicated that the macroscopic performance of the samples slightly reduced the resistance to permeation of Cl−. The content of C–S–H gel in the samples with 10% and 20% GGBS content was higher than in the sample with 10% FA content, which was due to the higher CaO content and Ca(OH)2 diffraction peak intensity in GGBS than FA. The more alkaline environment favored the secondary hydration of GGBS to form C–S–H gel.

According to Fig. 14a, the CaSO4 diffraction peak still exists in the pure SAC mortar system. When the system possessed a sufficient content of CaSO4, the presence of AFt was relatively stable, with only a small part of AFt converting to the AFm phase. Therefore, the ability of pure SAC mortar to chemically bind Cl− was limited, which resulted in the diffraction peak of the F salt appeared at 11.2°. The intensity of the F salt diffraction peaks for the samples with FA, 10%, and 20% GGBS content were all higher than that of the pure SAC mortar. This indicated an increase in the crystallinity and content of the F salt. The F salt was formed from the binding of free Cl− by the AFm phase, and its content reflected the chemical binding rate of SAC mortar to free Cl−. Moreover, the chemical binding of AFm relative to free Cl− was more significant than the physical adsorption of Cl− by C−S-H gels16,17,26. This explained why the addition of FA and GGBS could enhance the Cl− binding rate.

Additionally, with the increase of GGBS content, the intensity of the Ca(SO)4 diffraction peak increased. The SO42− replaced the Cl− in F salt, causing it to decompose into AFt. In general, the product stability of anions adsorbed by AFm is SO42−-AFm > Cl−-AFm > OH−-AFm, and the product stability was also affected by the concentration of ions in the system33. Furthermore, the decalcification reaction caused by the interaction between SO42− and C–S–H gel also affected the adsorption capacity of C–S–H gel for Cl− 16,17,26. This was why the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortar with GGBS was lower than that of SAC mortar with FA.

In Fig. 14b, the characteristic peaks of the Aft phase appear near 9.1°, 15.7°, 23°, 27.6°, 32.3°, 34.9°, 40.9°, and 45.9°. The characteristic peak near 11.2° corresponds to F salt. The peaks near 9.9° and 19° correspond to the AFm phase; the peak near 17.8° corresponds to Ca(OH)2. The peaks near 20.9°, 26.5°, 36.6°, 39.5°, and 50.2° correspond to SiO2. the peaks near 25.5°, 31°, and 38.6° correspond to CaSO4. The peak near 29.5° corresponds to CaCO3 and possibly C–S–H gel. The peak near 18.2° corresponds to the AH3 gel phase. According to Bragg’s Law and the Debye-Scherrer equation, the broader AH3 phase diffraction peaks indicate a smaller crystal size39. The structure of the AH3 phase is similar to that of the gibbsite, with a short range of order and an amorphous state in XRD. As time goes by, the AH3 phase further reacts with CaSO4 to form AFt, leading to a decrease in the diffraction peak intensity of the AH3 phase in the late hydration period38. After a curing age of 28 days, the main components of the sulphate-aluminate cement clinker are C2S, C4A3S and CaSO4.

The AFt diffraction peaks of each component are pronounced, indicating good crystallinity of AFt. The AFt diffraction peak intensity of the sample with 20% SF is significantly lower than that of the other samples. This shows that the AFt content in the sample with 20% SF is lower because the main component of SF is SiO2, which primarily acts as a filler to lubricate voids in the early stages of hydration. As hydration progresses, the pozzolanic effect of SF triggers secondary hydration, reacting with Ca(OH)2 to form C–S–H gel, while the intensity of the Ca(OH)2 diffraction peak near 17.8° decreases. The W/B of the sample with 20% LP was higher than that of pure SAC mortar, leading to a significant increase in capillary pore content in SAC mortar. This provided enough space for the crystallisation of AFt, which increased the crystallinity of AFt in the 20% LP sample and enhanced the intensity of its diffraction peaks. Our previous study showed that a large number of short-columnar AFt grains were generated inside the cement paste during the secondary hydration process19.

The diffraction peaks of SiO2 appeared around 20.9°, and the peak heights of SF group were higher than that of pure SAC mortar, while those of LP group were lower than that of pure SAC mortar. This phenomenon was due to the different content of SiO2 in the mineral admixtures, and the main component of sand in the mortar samples was also SiO2. The diffraction peak intensity of CaCO3 near 29.5° for the 20% LP sample was significantly higher than that of the other four samples, which showed good crystallinity. This was due to the fact that LP is an inert material11,12. The diffraction peak near 29.7° corresponded to the C–S–H phase, which was amorphous and poorly crystalline20,21,26.

Figure 15 displays the XRD spectra of SAC mortar with various FA and GGBS contents after a curing age of 28 days, with a 1% Cl− content. In Fig. 15a, the diffraction peaks of AFt, AFm, and Ca(OH)2 appear at 9.1° and 17.8°. The intensity of the diffraction peaks of AFt, AFm and Ca(OH)2 was higher for the sample with 20% FA content, which indicated that the content of these substances in the system was higher at this time. As the FA content increased, the intensity of the diffraction peaks for AFt and Ca(OH)2 gradually decreased. This was due to a reduction in cement clinker content, leading to a decrease in AFt formation, while the secondary hydration effect of FA gradually strengthened. This depleted the Ca(OH)2 in the system to form C–S–H gels, the diffraction peak of which occurs around 26.7°, and its content tended to increase with the addition of FA. The C–S–H gel in FA-doped SAC mortar could fill the structural pores, which was macroscopically manifested by the enhanced permeation resistance to Cl− from the external environment, thus increasing the physical adsorption capacity for free Cl−. On the other hand, the decrease in Ca(OH)2 content and the consequent decreased in pH value affect the stability of F salt. This led to the decomposition of some F salt and the release of free Cl− 24,25,33, causing a decrease in the chemical binding rate of FA-doped SAC mortar for free Cl−. In general, samples with 20% FA content had the best Cl− binding rate of 0.5%.

In Fig. 15b, the intensity of diffraction peaks for AFt, AFm, Ca(OH)2, Ca(SO)4, and C–S–H appear near 9.1°, 19°, 17.8°, 20.5°, and 29.5°. With an increase in GGBS content, the intensity of the diffraction peaks for AFt, AFm, Ca(OH)2, Ca(SO)4, and C–S–H all gradually decreased, while the intensity of the C–S–H diffraction peak gradually increased. This led to a decrease in the content of AFt, AFm, and Ca(SO)4 produced by hydration, which in turn reduced the intensity of the diffraction peaks. At the same time, the increase of the active component SiO2 content in the raw materials enhanced the secondary hydration effect of GGBS. This consumed Ca(OH)2 in the system and increased the C–S–H content. Thus, as the GGBS content changed from 10 to 40%, the AFt content gradually decreased and the C–S–H gel content gradually increased. Macroscopically, the resistance to Cl− penetration in the external environment gradually increased, and the Cl− binding rate increased. The Cl− binding rate of the samples with GGBS content of 40% was 38.7% higher than that of the baseline group.

Conclusion

In this study, the impact of Cl− concentration, age, and the type and amount of mineral admixtures on the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortar was investigated. Additionally, XRD analysis was used to analyze the phase composition of SAC mortar. The principal findings were summarized as follows:

(1) At 1% and 2% internal Cl− concentrations, the Cl− binding rate of samples doped with 20% FA and GGBS was higher than that of pure SAC mortar, and FA was more effective than GGBS. However, at a Cl− concentration of 0.5%, the binding rate of samples doped with 20% FA, GGBS, and SF was lower than that of pure SAC mortar, while 20% LP dopant increased this capacity.

(2) The addition of FA and GGBS increased the Cl− binding rate of SAC mortar, and the role of FA was more obvious than that of GGBS. With the increase of FA content, the binding rate of SAC mortar containing FA first increased and then decreased, but remained above 20% of pure SAC mortar. The best Cl− binding rate was found in the FA mixture, which was 46.6% higher than that of pure SAC mortar. The binding rate of SAC mortar containing GGBS increased with the increase of the dosage, in which SAC mortar containing 40% GGBS had the strongest resistance to internal Cl− erosion, which was 38.7% higher than that of pure SAC mortar.

(3) The increase of FA doping content promoted the formation of C–S–H gel and enhanced the resistance to external Cl− penetration. The addition of FA promoted the formation of F salt and C–S–H gel, enhancing resistance to internal Cl− erosion. The addition of GGBS reduced the yield of C–S–H gel and the resistance to external Cl− penetration. The addition of GGBS was conducive to the formation of F salt, improved the Cl− binding rate, and enhanced the resistance to internal Cl− erosion. Compared with FA, the addition of GGBS to SAC mortar resulted in an increase in the CaSO4 diffraction peak, destabilizing the C–S–H gel and F salt, thereby reducing the enhancement of Cl− binding rate.

(4) The addition of appropriate SF dopants significantly improved the resistance to external Cl− penetration, but decreased the calcium silicate ratio of the C–S–H gels. This weakened its resistance to internal Cl− erosion. On the other hand, the addition of LP improved the binding of free Cl−.
