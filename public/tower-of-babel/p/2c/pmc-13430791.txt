Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics reveal Thbs1-CD36 crosstalk between Kupffer and hepatic stellate cells following mesenchymal stem cell-derived small extracellular vesicles transplantation, alleviating liver fibrosis
Authors: Lijun Chen, Ning Zhang, Yuqi Huang, Jingjing Qu, Yangxin Fang, Yin Yuan, Jiamin Fu, Dalong Wan, Qi Zhang, Lu Chen, Zuoshi Wen, Xin Chen, Li Yuan, Zhenyu Xu, Yifei Li, Chenxia Hu, Huadong Yan, Hiromi Izawa, Takayuki Yoshimoto, Lanjuan Li, Charlie Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmall extracellular vesicles (sEVs) derived from mesenchymal stem cells represent a novel regenerative therapeutic strategy for various liver injuries. Menstrual blood-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MenSCs)-derived EVs may have therapeutic potential in treating liver fibrosis. In this study, systemically injected MenSCs-derived sEVs migrated into the injured liver, ameliorated serological indices, and decreased collagenous fiber accumulation in carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced liver fibrosis mouse models. We aimed to explore the underlying mechanism for the therapeutic effect of MenSC-sEVs.ResultsSingle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics were combined to investigate the mechanism by which classical fibrosis-related genes are reduced to inhibit liver fibrosis, targeting Kupffer cells and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs). Fourteen major cellular clusters were determined in the livers of the control (or con), CCl4, and MenSC-sEV groups through scRNA-seq data. Thrombospondin 1 (Thbs1) and cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) pathways were identified as important links between Kupffer cells and HSCs in the CellChat analyses. Additionally, spatial transcriptomics further confirmed the reduced Thbs1 and CD36 expression after MenSC-sEVs injection in fibrotic livers. We investigated the relationship between the Kupffer cells and HSCs, highlighting the role of Thbs1/CD36-mediated molecular crosstalk following transplantation with MenSC-sEVs. Furthermore, the integration of scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics data revealed eight genes, C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, Ctsd, Cd5l, Ctss, Wfdc17, and Gpnmb, that were upregulated in the CCl₄ group across platforms. Thbs1 expression was reduced in primary mouse Kupffer cells stimulated with MenSC-sEVs, and the culture medium of primary mouse Kupffer cells stimulated by MenSC-sEVs reduced the expression of CD36 in primary mouse HSCs in vitro.ConclusionsIn this study, we identified interacting target cells (Kupffer cells and HSCs), signaling molecules (Thbs1-CD36), and underlying therapeutic targets of MenSC-sEVs in treating liver fibrosis.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04499-8.Keywords: Menstrual blood-derived MSCs, Small extracellular vesicles, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Spatial transcriptomics, Kupffer cell, Hepatic stellate cell (HSC), Liver fibrosis, Extracellular vesicles-based therapy, Thbs1/CD36 crosstalk

Background

Liver diseases represent a major global health challenge and social burden, accounting for over two million deaths annually [1, 2]. A common pathological hallmark of these chronic liver disorders is liver fibrosis, characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) [3, 4]. Key mechanisms include the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), disruption of the liver sinusoidal endothelial cell (LSEC) barrier, hepatocyte necrosis, and a sustained inflammatory response [5, 6]. Cellular fate mapping and in-depth phenotypic analysis have revealed that HSCs are a key source of myofibroblasts, the major collagen-producing drivers of liver fibrosis [7]. Despite being the most effective treatment for advanced chronic liver diseases, the widespread use of orthotopic liver transplantation is hindered by the limited availability of donor organs [8]. This limitation underscores the urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies. Numerous reports have demonstrated that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) can alleviate liver fibrosis primarily via paracrine mechanisms, including the release of cytokines and extracellular vesicles (EVs), that predominantly target liver non-parenchymal cells (NPCs) [9, 10]. Menstrual blood-derived mesenchymal stem cell (MenSC), originating from the endometrium, is a tissue-specific type of MSC. Since Meng et al. first identified MenSCs from menstrual blood in 2007, they have attracted increasing attention and research [11, 12]. Compared with MSCs derived from other sources, such as bone marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs) and adipose-derived MSCs (AD-MSCs), MenSCs possess some advantages, including abundant yield (~ 30 times that of BM-MSCs), non-invasive collection (less pain than BM-MSCs and AD-MSCs), periodic accessibility, strong proliferative capacity, lower immunogenicity, and lower ethical restrictions [13–15].

To seek better therapeutic approaches, researchers have conducted in-depth studies on EVs, which are important components of intercellular signaling and paracrine functions, and have attracted increasing attention to various diseases [16]. EVs, as key mediators of intercellular communication and paracrine signaling, have emerged as promising cell-free therapeutics [17]. EVs derived from MSCs of various origins, including bone marrow, umbilical cord, adipose, and placenta, have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in multiple liver diseases [18–23]. Recently, Vaiciuleviciute et al. compared the secreted proteins between MenSCs and other types of MSCs, and MenSCs had higher secretions of various growth factors than BM-MSCs [24]. MenSC-derived EVs (MenSC-EVs) have also shown beneficial effects in conditions such as fulminant hepatic failure [25, 26], although their role in liver fibrosis remains incompletely elucidated. Our team has investigated therapeutic roles of MenSCs in improving liver fibrosis through paracrine mechanisms [27, 28]. Therefore, MenSC-derived EVs may have therapeutic potential in treating liver fibrosis.

The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized the study of cellular and molecular mechanisms of various liver diseases [29]. Ramachandran et al. analyzed over 100,000 human single-cell transcriptomes, identifying NPC subtypes and their markers in healthy and cirrhotic livers, thereby providing unprecedented resolution for delineating the fibrotic microenvironment [30]. Subsequently, researchers have increasingly used scRNA-seq to dissect cellular interactions and signaling among liver NPCs [31, 32]. Furthermore, advances in spatial transcriptomics have deepened our understanding of biological complexity and liver disease mechanisms [33]. The integration of scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics offers powerful tools for exploring cellular crosstalk and spatial architecture in liver diseases [34, 35].

The complex liver microenvironment mediates extensive interactions between HSCs and surrounding cells, including LSECs, T cells, B cells, macrophages/Kupffer cells, natural killer (NK) cells, neutrophils, and dendritic cells (DCs), through direct contact or secretory signals [36, 37]. In particular, crosstalk between HSCs and macrophages plays a vital role in fibrosis progression. Experimental models have revealed that HSCs and macrophages mutually influence each other’s phenotypes via cytokines and chemokines, thereby promoting fibrotic phenotypes and remodeling the ECM [38]. Accumulated ECM further modulates cellular functions through specific receptors, driving fibrosis progression. Studies in murine models demonstrated that Kupffer cells activate HSCs and recruit triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 1-positive bone marrow-derived macrophages [39]. Depleting macrophages decreases HSC activation and liver fibrosis, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting macrophage–HSC interactions [40]. Anti-fibrotic strategies aimed at reducing macrophage infiltration may hold therapeutic potential during the progression phase of fibrosis [41].

Thrombospondin 1 (THBS1/TSP-1), an adhesive glycoprotein, mediates cell-matrix communication through interactions with receptors including cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36), αv integrins, β1 integrins, syndecan, and integrin-associated protein (CD47) [42]. In fibrotic diseases, upregulated Thbs1 facilitates the activation of the latent transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) pathway [43]. CD36, a transmembrane glycoprotein and fatty acid transporter, binds various ligands and initiates downstream signaling cascades [44]. The interaction between THBS1 and CD36 recruits Src homology 2-containing phosphatase-1 (SHP-1) to vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 (VEGFR-2), triggering intracellular signaling that induces reactive oxygen species and activates the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway, thereby promoting pro-inflammatory responses [45, 46].

In this study, we assessed the therapeutic potential of MenSC-small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) in carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced liver fibrotic mice using scRNA-seq combined with spatial transcriptomics. Through scRNA-seq, we characterized the cellular landscapes of livers from the control (Con), CCl4, and MenSC-sEVs groups. Subclustering of Kupffer cells and fibroblasts/HSCs revealed altered cellular compositions and interactions. CellChat analysis identified the Thbs1/CD36 pathway as a key mediator of Kupffer cell–HSC crosstalk. The primary mouse Kupffer cells and primary mouse HSCs were further verified in vitro. Our results demonstrate that MenSC-sEVs alleviate liver fibrosis in mice and reveal the critical role of Thbs1/CD36 signaling in mediating intercellular communication within the fibrotic liver microenvironment in vivo and in vitro.

Methods

Identification of MenSCs

Human MenSCs (no. 00612-210415P) were obtained from the Innovative Precision Medicine (IPM) Group (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China), originating from a healthy female donor and following previously established protocols [47, 48]. The donor has provided written informed consent. Briefly, menstrual blood was carefully gathered and aseptically cultured in α-Modified Eagle’s Medium (α-MEM; 32571101, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS; FBS-52A, Capricorn Scientific, Australia). For surface marker characterization, fifth-passage cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, SH30256.01, HyClone) for three times, and resuspended in Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences, USA) and incubated with corresponding antibodies targeting cluster of differentiation (CD)-29, CD34, CD45, CD73, CD90, CD105, CD117, and human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-DR according to the manufacturer’s recommended concentrations. Analysis was performed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer, with immunoglobulin G (IgG)-1 or IgG2a isotype controls used for gating; further details are presented in Table S1. The staining methodology followed previously reported procedures [28, 49].

To assess trilineage differentiation potential, MenSCs were induced using OriCell® human bone marrow MSC differentiation kits (Cyagen Biosciences, Guangzhou, China) for osteogenesis (HUXUC-90021), adipogenesis (HUXUC-90031), and chondrogenesis (HUXUC-90041), strictly following the manufacturer’s protocols. Induction was performed sequentially using specific differentiation media, as established in a previous study [50]. For osteogenic differentiation, 6-well plates (Corning) were coated with 0.1% gelatin (Cyagen Biosciences) and placed in a CO₂ incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, MenSCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells/cm² in complete medium (HUXUC-90021). When cell confluence reached 70%, the medium was replaced with complete osteogenic induction medium. The medium was refreshed every 3–4 days. After 4 weeks, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with Alizarin Red S to observe calcium deposits. For adipogenic differentiation, 6-well plates were coated with 0.1% gelatin, and fifth-passage MenSCs were seeded. When confluence reached 90%, the complete medium was carefully aspirated and replaced with adipogenic induction medium (Solution A and Solution B, HUXUC-90031). The culture was maintained for 3–4 weeks until sufficient lipid droplets formed. Before staining, the medium was aspirated; cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min and then stained with Oil Red O for 30 min to visualize lipid droplets. For chondrogenic differentiation, fifth-passage MenSCs were digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, neutralized, and centrifuged. The cells were resuspended in complete medium for counting (Countess 3, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Aliquots of 2.5 × 10⁵ cells were transferred into 15 mL centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 250 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was aspirated, and the cell pellets were resuspended in 0.5 mL of complete chondrogenic induction medium (HUXUC-90041). The medium was replaced every 3 days. After 4 weeks, the chondrogenic pellets were harvested, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sections were stained with Alcian Blue. Following several weeks of induction, the cells successfully differentiated into adipocytes, chondrocytes, and osteoblasts, then stained with corresponding dyestuff and subsequently visualized using an imaging system (Olympus, Japan).

Isolation and identification of MenSC-sEVs

To prepare the MenSC-sEVs, MenSCs were cultured in α-MEM supplied with 10% EV-depleted FBS (abs993, Absin, China). Small EVs were isolated via differential centrifugation (Beckman, USA) according to a previously described protocol [51]. Briefly, after 48 h of culture, the conditioned medium was collected and processed through a series of centrifugation at 4 °C: 300 × g for 5 min, 2,000 × g for 30 min, 10,000 × g for 30 min, and 100,000 × g for 60 min. Final purification was achieved by centrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min, followed by storage of the MenSC-sEVs at -80 °C.

The particle size distribution of MenSC-sEVs was assessed using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) on a ZetaView system (Particle Metrix). Morphological examination was conducted using transmission electron microscopy (JEM1400PLUS). A 20 µL aliquot of the vesicle pellet was adsorbed onto a carbon film for 5 min, negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid, and air-dried prior to imaging. The protein concentration of the suspended MenSC-sEVs was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (P0010, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Western blot (WB) analysis was performed to check specific MenSC-sEV markers, including CD9, ALG-2-interacting protein X (ALIX), tumor susceptibility gene 101 (TSG101), CD81, 70 kDa heat shock protein (HSP70), and calnexin; corresponding antibody details are presented in Table S1.

CCl4-induced liver fibrosis in mice and MenSC-sEV transplantation

Male C57BL/6 mice (20 ± 2 g) were purchased from the Hangzhou Medical College and maintained in the Laboratory Animal Center of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The facility provides a specific pathogen-free environment with a 12 h light/dark cycle, and ad libitum access to food and water. Animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Experiment Ethical Inspection of the Affiliated Hospital of Medicine, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, and have been approved (No. 2023 − 1405). The normal control group (Normal control; N = 10) was only injected with olive oil (No. A502795, Sangon Biotech, China). Liver fibrosis was induced in C57BL/6 mice by intraperitoneal administration of 10% CCl4 (Shanghai Macklin, China; dissolved in olive oil at a 10% solution, 1 mL/kg body weight) two times weekly for six weeks. Subsequently, MenSC-sEVs were administered via tail vein intravenous injection for one time: 25 µg for relative low-dose group (MenSC-sEV-low; N = 10) and 50 µg for relative high-dose group (MenSC-sEV; N = 10). An equal volume of PBS (CCl4 model group; N = 10) was used as a control for MenSC-sEV transplantation. Samples from the four groups (Normal control, CCl4 model, MenSC-sEVs-low, and MenSC-sEVs) were collected 14 days after MenSC-sEVs transplantation (Figure S1A). Fourteen days was selected as the experimental endpoint based on previous studies [27, 48].

Serum liver function and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Liver function was assessed using an automated biochemistry analyzer (LW C400; Shenzhen, China) by measuring serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bilirubin (TBIL), and albumin (ALB) in 10 mice for each group. Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues (n = 4) using TRIzol reagent (15596026CN, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and subsequently reverse-transcribed into cDNA using TransScript® One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (AT311-02, TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). The qRT-PCR was performed on a Roche LightCycler 480 II system using UltraSYBR Mixture (Beijing, China). The expression of the following genes was analyzed: Tgfb1, Acta2, Col1a1, C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, cathepsin D (Ctsd), Cd5l, cathepsin S (Ctss), Wfdc17, and Gpnmb, with β-actin serving as the internal control. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Histological staining, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and immunofluorescence

Routine paraffin sections were prepared and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Sirius Red (SR) staining, or immunostaining (IHC or immunofluorescence) by HaoKe Biotechnology. Each experimental group included four stained sections (n = 4). Histomorphological evaluation of liver lesions was based on H&E and SR staining. For IHC, α-SMA, Col-I, and TGF-β1 were detected according to previously reported procedures [48, 50]. Semi-quantitative analysis of IHC results was performed using Visiopharm (https://visiopharm.com/) software. Co-expression of Ctss/Ctsd with Des was followed by previously reported procedures [48, 50]. For the Thbs1, F4/80, CD36, and Des immunofluorescence assay (IFA) staining, mouse liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight, then dehydrated with gradient ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5-µm-thick slices. For antigen retrieval, the sections were immersed in citrate buffer and heated in a microwave oven at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by natural cooling to room temperature. After rinsing with PBS (pH = 7.4) three times, the sections were blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the sections were rinsed with PBS three times, then incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody for Poly-HRP Goat Anti Rabbit Secondary Antibody (HKI0026, HaoKe Biotechnology) in the dark for 1 h. Then, place the slides in PBS and wash with gentle agitation on a decolorizing shaker three times. For the multi-labeling experiments, repeat the above steps to label the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th antibody, and add corresponding color Flare signal amplification reagents (Flare520 (HKI0014), Flare570 (HKI0015), Flare480 (HKI0013), and Flare690 (HKI0017) with HKI0000-6 S, HaoKe Biotechnology). Place the slides in PBS and wash with gentle agitation on a decolorizing shaker three times. After washing with PBS three times again, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (HKI0005, HaoKe Biotechnology) for 10 min in the dark. Finally, the sections were mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium (HKI0007-1, HaoKe Biotechnology) and observed under a laser scanning confocal microscope. The images were captured and analyzed to calculate the positive rates of Thbs1⁺/F4/80⁺/CD36⁺/Des⁺ cells. F4/80, Des, Thbs1, and CD36 were quantified across five random fields. DAB solution (HKI0039, HaoKe Biotechnology) served as the chromogen, with development time monitored with a microscope (NIKON-ECLIPSE C1, Japan). The corresponding antibody specifications are provided in Table S1.

MenSC-sEVs stimulating for primary Kupffer cells and culture medium of Kupffer cells stimulating for primary HSCs

Mouse primary Kupffer cells (CP-M132) and mouse primary HSCs (CP-M041) were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China (https://www.procell.com.cn/) with their quality verified by the company [52, 53]. Mouse recombinant THBS1 (rTHBS1) was purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE; No. HY-P701325). Mouse TNF-alpha High Sensitivity ELISA Kit (RK04875, ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) and Mouse THBS1 ELISA Kit (E-EL-M3083, Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) were used to analyze the supernatant of mouse primary Kupffer cells, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Following digestion with lidocaine (12 mM), mouse primary Kupffer cells were seeded into 12‑well plates (Corning) and cultured in Mouse Kupffer Cell Complete Medium (CM‑M132). After 12 h, 100 ng/mL LPS (L2630‑25MG, Sigma) was added to stimulate Kupffer cells, and this was termed the LPS group. The primary Kupffer cells cultured in CM‑M132 without LPS addition were termed the control group. Concurrently, 100 ng/mL LPS and 10 ng MenSC-sEVs were added to stimulate Kupffer cells, and this was termed the MenSC-sEVs group. After another 12 h, the supernatant was discarded, and fresh CM‑M132 was added for an additional 12 h of culture in all groups, including control group, LPS group, and MenSC-sEVs group. Cells were collected for qRT-PCR in Thbs1 and TNF-α, with β-actin serving as the internal control (Table S2), and WB for checking THBS1 expression (Table S1). The supernatants were collected for the detection of TNF-α and THBS1 using ELISA.

Following digestion with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, primary HSCs were seeded into 12‑well plates and cultured in HSC complete medium (CM‑M041). After 12 h, the medium was discarded, and the cells were then stimulated with supernatant collected from Kupffer cells by the control, LPS, and MenSC-sEVs (divided into MenSC-sEVs and MenSC-sEVs + rTHBS1) groups. These were named the control group, the LPS group, the MenSC-sEVs group, and the MenSC-sEVs + rTHBS1 group. After another 12 h, removing the supernatant, fresh CM‑M041 was added, and the cells were cultured for a further 24 h. Subsequently, cells were collected for qRT-PCR in Cd36 and Acta2, with β-actin serving as the internal control (Table S2), and WB for analyzing CD36 expression (Table S1). And relative gray values (n = 3) of the WB protein bands were quantified using the software of ImageJ.

Tracking in vivo and cellular uptake in vitro of MenSC-sEVs

DiR (DiIC18(7), D12731, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was diluted in PBS to a concentration of 10 µg/mL and prepared in a light-protected environment [48]. DiR-labeled MenSC-sEVs (50 µg) in 500 µL PBS were injected into CCl4-induced liver fibrosis mice (N = 5), with an equal volume of PBS administered as the control (N = 5). In vivo imaging was performed at 3 and 14 days post-injection (Figure S1A). After each imaging session, the mice were euthanized, and the major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were collected and imaged using an imaging system (IVIS-Spectrum, PerkinElmer, MA, USA).

To assess MenSC-sEV uptake by Kupffer cells/macrophages, PKH26-stained MenSC-sEVs (red fluorescence) were used in the cytoplasm of mouse RAW264.7 cells [51]. After labeling with PKH26 (4 µM) for 30 min, MenSC-EVs were isolated by centrifugation (100,000 × g, 1 h, 4°C). The labeled EVs were then added to RAW264.7 cells, which had been seeded in confocal dishes at 5 × 104 cells/well and cultured for 24 h, at a final concentration of 100 µg/mL for another 24 h. Following incubation, the cells were washed with PBS for 3 times, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (HK2003, HaoKe Biotechnology, China) for 30 min, and finally immunostained for F4/80 as described [51]. DAPI was incubated for 10 min in the dark. Samples were examined and imaged using a full-spectrum confocal microscope.

Single-cell capture, cDNA synthesis, library preparation, and data processing

Liver tissues were digested via a mouse liver dissociation kit (Cat No. 130-105-807, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) for 30 min at 37°C under gentle rotation, which was optimized for a high yield of non-parenchymal mouse liver cells. To obtain comprehensive single-cell transcriptomes from the murine liver, NPCs and a subset of parenchymal cells (hepatocytes and ductal cells) were isolated from the following three groups in nine mice (Figure S1A): Con (n = 3), CCl4 (n = 3), and MenSC-sEVs (n = 3). Single-cell suspensions from nine mice were prepared and analyzed using scRNA-seq on the 10× Genomics platform through Beijing CapitalBio Technology (https://www.capitalbiotechnology.com; Beijing, China). Cell capture and cDNA synthesis were conducted by the Single-Cell 3’ Library & Gel Bead Kit V3.1 (10× Genomics, 1000121) and Chromium Single-Cell G Chip Kit (10× Genomics, 1000120). Cell suspensions were loaded onto the Chromium Single Cell Controller to generate gel bead emulsions following the manufacturer’s instructions, as described previously [48].

The scRNA-seq libraries were constructed using the Single Cell 3’ Library and Gel Bead Kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (CapitalBio Technology). Raw data were processed using the CellRanger-7.0.0 count pipeline to generate feature-barcode matrices and facilitate cluster detection. Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis, and clustering was conducted using both K-means and graph-based methods, supplemented by Seurat v4.2.0. Batch correction strategy was performed using the Harmony algorithm [54]. Cells were filtered according to the following criteria: number of detected genes (nGene) > 200, unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts below the 99th percentile value, proportion of mitochondrial genes < 25 (25%), and number of cells (ncell) > 3. Feature selection was performed using the vst method (selection.method = “vst”), and 2000 highly variable features were identified (nfeatures = 2000). And the further information was presented in Table S3.

Visium CytAssist spatial with histology in mouse liver

For histological processing, the Visium CytAssist Spatial Gene Expression protocol (CG000614) for fresh-frozen tissue was performed in three groups: Con (n = 1), CCl4 (n = 1), and MenSC-sEVs (n = 1). Cryosections were incubated on a thermal cycler adapter at 37 °C for 5 min, fixed in cold methanol at -20 °C for 30 min, and subsequently subjected to H&E staining using eosin (Millipore Sigma), hematoxylin (Dako), and bluing buffer (Dako). Bright-field imaging was performed at 10× magnification using a Leica DMi8 whole-slide scanner following the manufacturer’s guidelines, with all steps executed by Beijing CapitalBio Technology [55]. Subsequent processing utilized the Visium CytAssist Spatial Gene Expression kit for formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissues (10x Genomics, PN-1000521). Tissue sections underwent probe hybridization and ligation, after which analytes were transferred to gene expression slides using the Visium CytAssist instrument. RNA was digested using RNase (PN-3000593) and a tissue removal enzyme (PN-3000387) to release the bound probes for capture using slide-mounted oligonucleotides. Probe amplification and library construction followed the standard Visium protocol for fresh-frozen tissues (CG000495). Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument with a minimum of 50,000 reads per spot in PE150 mode, performed by Beijing CapitalBio Technology. Raw data were processed using the SpaceRanger-2.1.0 pipeline to generate feature-barcode matrices and facilitate cluster detection.

Enrichment, single-cell trajectories, and CellChat analysis

Functional annotation and categorization of Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were administered using the ClusterProfiler package in R. For cell type-specific scoring, marker genes for each cellular type were defined as signature genes and assessed via the AddModuleScore function in Seurat under default parameters. Single-cell trajectories were reconstructed using Monocle2 and CytoTRACE (R package), incorporating pseudotime inference. For ECM and fibrosis scores, marker genes were mainly referred to Cell Signaling Technology pathways (https://www.cellsignal.com/pathways) and related studies [55, 56]. Cell–cell communication networks were inferred from the scRNA-seq data using the CellChat R package, quantifying signaling pathway activity through significant ligand–receptor interactions across cell types. The computeCommunProb function incorporated the proportion of cells per group to estimate communication probabilities. Results were visualized using netVisual_bubble (dot heatmaps) and netVisual_chord_gene (chord diagrams). A detailed methodology has been presented in the previous studies [48, 57].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the Student’s t-test, χ² test, or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman’s rank-order correlation. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, denoted with P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), and P < 0.001 (***).

Results

Identification of MenSC and MenSC-sEVs

MenSCs exhibited high positivity for the mesenchymal markers CD29 (99.88%), CD73 (99.78%), CD90 (99.7%), and CD105 (99.25%), while being negative for CD34, CD45, CD117, and HLA-DR, as assessed by immunophenotyping (Fig. 1A). The cells displayed a spindle-shaped, fibroblast-like morphology and demonstrated multipotent differentiation capacity into adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic lineages (Fig. 1B), consistent with previous reports [48, 50]. Cryo-electron microscopy revealed that MenSC-sEVs were morphologically round with a bilayer membrane structure, and most EVs measured < 200 nm in diameter (Fig. 1C). NTA showed that the MenSC-sEVs had a peak diameter of 121.8 nm and a median (X50) size of 126.7 nm (SD value is 44.9 nm) in 11 positions. The original mean concentration of MenSC-sEVs was approximately 2.8 × 1010 particles/mL (Dilution Factor for 500) (Fig. 1D). WB analysis confirmed the presence of canonical EV markers—CD9, ALIX, TSG101, CD81, and HSP70—in both MenSCs and MenSC-sEVs, with strong expression of ALIX, TSG101, and HSP70 observed in the sEV fraction; and the expression of calnexin is negative in MenSC-sEVs but is positive in MenSCs (Fig. 1E).

(C) The structure of MenSC-sEVs using cryo-transmission electron microscopy. (D) The diameter of MenSC-sEVs using the Nanosight instrument. (E) Western blot analysis for assessing the expression of MenSC-sEVs and MenSC of CD9, Alix, Tsg101, CD81, Hsp70, and Calnexin. MenSC-sEVs improved pathological indicators in liver fibrotic mice

MenSC-sEVs were administered via tail vein injection (Figure S1B). No significant differences in body weight were observed between low-dose MenSC-sEVs (MenSC-sEVs-low group; N = 10) and high-dose MenSC-sEVs (MenSC-sEVs group; N = 10) at the time of initial body weight of mice before CCl₄ injection, body weight before MenSC-sEVs injection, and final body weight at two weeks post MenSC-sEVs transplantation (Figure S1C). Livers from normal control mice exhibited a smooth surface, whereas those from the CCl₄ group displayed a granular fibrotic morphology (Figure S1D). Serum biochemical analysis indicated that the CCl₄ group exhibited a significant deterioration in all liver function markers except ALB, compared with normal controls (Fig. 1F–J). Compared to the CCl4 group, low-dose MenSC-sEVs (MenSC-sEVs-low) exhibited significant improvements in ALT and AST levels, whereas high-dose MenSC-sEVs (MenSC-sEVs group) significantly improved almost all serum indicators, including ALT, AST, ALP, and TBIL levels, on day 14 post-transplantation (Fig. 1F–I). Although ALB levels showed an increasing trend in both MenSC-sEV-treated groups, the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 1J). Considering that low-dose MenSC-sEVs failed to induce a significant improvement in serological indices, high-dose MenSC-sEVs were used as the therapeutic group (MenSC-sEVs group) in all subsequent experimental studies to further focus on the therapeutic molecular mechanism.

Histological examination revealed that MenSC-sEV transplantation reduced lobular inflammation, hyaline degeneration, and ballooning degeneration (Fig. 2A). SR staining showed tightly arranged collagen fibers around central veins in control mice, whereas the CCl₄ group exhibited collagen deposition and pseudolobule formation. MenSC-sEV treatment markedly reduced collagen area compared with CCl4 model according to SR staining outcomes (Fig. 2B). Discussion

EVs possess several advantages over their parent cells, including reduced complexity, enhanced safety as cell-free agents, lower immunogenicity, improved drug delivery efficiency for the safe vehicle, and broader intercellular communication capabilities [63, 64]. Accumulating evidence indicates that the therapeutic benefits of MSCs are largely mediated via their secreted EVs to the tissue engineering and regenerative medicine [65–67]. MSC-derived EVs can ameliorate liver fibrosis by inhibiting HSC activation [19, 20, 66]. Kim et al. showed that in the CCl₄-induced liver injury model, cellular communication network factor 1 enhanced the clearance of apoptotic neutrophils by macrophages, thereby inducing HSCs to transdifferentiate into myofibroblast-like cells, driving the fibrogenic process [68]. EVs from bone marrow MSCs alleviated CCl4-induced liver fibrosis by inhibiting HSCs [23], while umbilical cord MSC-EVs were shown to effectively attenuate liver fibrosis in mice [69]. Ohara et al. further found that amniotic-derived MSC-EVs improved inflammation and fibrogenesis in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and liver fibrosis models by inhibiting Kupffer cell and HSC activation, as well as suppressing the LPS/TLR-4 pathway [70]. Our study demonstrated that MenSC-sEVs significantly alleviated liver fibrosis in mice by targeting Kupffer cells and HSCs, providing foundational evidence for the use of MenSC-derived EVs in treating liver fibrosis. We also revealed intercellular communication networks between Kupffer cells and HSCs involving the Thbs1/CD36 pathway. The detailed mechanism is still under investigation.

THBS1 has been identified as a predictive biomarker for short-term mortality in acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF). Hassan et al. reported that THBS1 was the most upregulated gene in patients with hepatitis B virus-related ACLF [71]. In experimental models of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)/NASH, Thbs1 levels were elevated in the liver, and myeloid-specific knockout of TSP-1 ameliorated obesity-induced liver injury, inflammation, and fibrosis by suppressing pro-inflammatory TLR-4 signaling via autocrine regulation of Smpdl3b [72]. Additionally, in vitro studies using human NASH organoids showed that TSP-1-deficient platelets inhibited HSC activation and slowed disease progression [73]. Our scRNA-seq data suggest that Thbs1 attenuates liver fibrosis by modulating CD36 expression in HSCs. CD36 is expressed in HSCs, vascular endothelial cells, and cholangiocytes, and plays a significant role in liver disease progression [46, 74]. Liu et al. summarized the therapeutic potential of MSC-EVs from various sources in treating CCl4-induced liver fibrosis [75]. Li et al. reported that in injured livers, monocyte-derived Kupffer cells (MoKCs) become predominant and exhibit protective effects, with reduced functional impairment, necrosis, and fibrosis [76]. In our study, we identified a Mo/MoMFs (also named MoKCs) subpopulation with lower ECM and fibrosis scores than Kupffer cells; whether this subpopulation (Mo/MoMFs) can exert anti-fibrotic role worthy of further investigation.

Cheng et al. identified four macrophage subpopulations in both normal and fibrotic livers: KCs (Mac2), macrophage-LSECs (Mac3), anti-fibrotic MDMs (Mac0), and pro-fibrotic MDMs (Mac1) [31]. The Mac1 subset, characterized by high Thbs1 expression, was established as a distinct Thbs1⁺ MDM subgroup and validated through immunofluorescence in fibrotic livers of both mice and humans. Existing evidence supports a role for Thbs1 in HSC activation and NAFLD/NASH progression, consistent with its function within Mac1. Notably, exogenous Thbs1 was found to activate primary mouse HSCs, an effect abolished by pharmacological inhibition of the pathway [31]. A recent study by Sun et al. demonstrated that EVs released by injured hepatocytes directly interact with neighboring macrophages/Kupffer cells, which in turn promotes the activation of HSCs in the damaged liver microenvironment and aggravates liver fibrosis in mice [77]. These findings demonstrate that EVs are capable of directly acting on macrophages/Kupffer cells in the mouse liver. Our findings demonstrate that MenSC-sEVs can affect Kupffer cells/macrophages via Thbs1, which further interacts with HSCs, driving activation primarily through the Cd36 signaling axis. Targeting this Thbs1-dependent crosstalk may offer a therapeutic avenue for alleviating Kupffer cells/macrophages-induced HSC activation. Through scRNA-seq analysis, we showed that MenSC-sEVs mitigate liver fibrosis in mice by suppressing THBS signaling between Kupffer cells and HSCs.

MenSC-sEVs downregulate Thbs1 expression in Kupffer cells, as shown by scRNA-seq analysis. Thbs1 acts as a key ligand binding to CD36 on HSCs, influencing the expression of downstream effector molecules, including C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, Ctsd, Cd5l, Ctss, Wfdc17, and Gpnmb, thereby attenuating fibrotic progression. Beyond its classical role in complement activation, C1q has been implicated in diverse cellular processes such as migration and, notably, HSC activation. Ramachandran et al. provided high-resolution insights into the fibrotic niche in cirrhosis, highlighting cellular interactions among liver NPCs and suggesting new mechanistic avenues for research [30]. Secreted by Kupffer cells, C1q family members (C1qa, C1qb, and C1qc) may further modulate HSC-mediated fibrotic processes. Eguchi et al. reported that serum C1q levels were elevated in patients with cirrhosis compared to those with chronic hepatitis and correlated with fibrosis and liver function markers [78]. These findings suggest that C1q promotes HSC activation in part by upregulating connective tissue growth factor and may serve as a prognostic biomarker in chronic liver disease. Research has demonstrated that Ctsd is highly upregulated in activated HSCs, where it supports their proliferation and survival in the context of liver fibrosis and MASLD [79, 80]. In the present study, the expression of Ctsd showed no significant difference between the CCl4 group and MenSC-sEVs group as assessed by immunofluorescence staining and qRT-PCR analysis. Parallel findings by Zuo et al. identified Ctss as a key regulator of ECM remodeling in human liver fibrosis through proteomic screening [81]. Ctss deletion significantly reduced endostatin levels and attenuated fibrotic progression, with further studies indicating macrophages as the primary cellular source of Ctss in the liver [81]. Consistent with these results, our study suggests that Ctss may represent a potential biomarker or candidate effector for reducing liver fibrosis following transplantation with MenSC-sEVs. Functional validation studies targeting Ctss will be included in future investigations.

In spatial transcriptomic analysis, Cluster 7 was exclusively identified in the normal control group and was absent under fibrotic conditions (CCl₄ and MenSC-sEV groups). The top 20 genes in this cluster may inform future investigations into mechanisms that maintain hepatic homeostasis. Beyond the interactions among HSCs, Kupffer cells, and the THBS pathway, other mechanisms—particularly those involving LSECs and Mo/MoMFs—warrant further exploration for therapeutic development. LSECs not only regulate vascular tone and facilitate fluid transport but also support liver regeneration through paracrine secretion of angiogenic factors [8]. Rapid loss of LSECs during CCl₄-induced injury disrupts their endothelial barrier function, which normally controls leukocyte infiltration and modulates inflammatory responses [82]. Ojha et al. identified endothelial RUNX3 as critical for maintaining LSEC phenotype and suppressing fibrosis [83]. Our scRNA-seq results, consistent with those of prior reports [48], confirm the depletion of LSECs post-CCl₄ injury, underscoring the need to investigate key molecular interactions between LSECs and HSCs in future studies. Additionally, according to the scRNA-seq, potential paracrine effects of MenSC-sEVs beyond Kupffer cells–HSC crosstalk should be further checked. The molecular cargo of MenSC-sEVs may target LSECs or other cells, which could be potential target cells for paracrine effects by MenSC-sEVs, such as miRNAs and proteins [24, 84]. Recently, de Pedro et al. reported that the secretome derived from MenSCs can modulate macrophage polarization, particularly when MenSCs are preconditioned [85]. Therefore, MenSCs-sEVs may regulate macrophage/Kupffer cell function, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation.

Over 70% hepatocytes in the normal liver, and hepatocytes played a very important role in liver fibrosis [2]. Our previous study and others also showed that the low percentage of hepatocytes in mouse liver tissues using scRNA-seq [48, 86–88]. Due to their sensitivity to tissue dissociation, hepatocytes have not been well represented in current single-cell liver disease datasets. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) minimizes these issues for maintaining the hepatocytes [89]. Thus, different methods for scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq can obtain different percentages of hepatocytes [90, 91], and snRNA-seq will truly reflect the number and characteristics of hepatocytes, which is another way for analyzing the relationship between hepatocytes and liver NPCs. Liver lobules are conventionally divided into three zones; however, spatial multi-omics technologies have unveiled a more refined distribution pattern. This zonation provides a spatial coordinate system for the hepatic microenvironment, which is heterogeneous yet functionally highly coordinated [34, 92]. Therefore, although HSCs are generally regarded as the main effector cells driving liver fibrosis, their predominant roles may vary across different zones, with some regions being inactive while others play critical roles [34, 93]. The IHC staining of liver tissue yields an overall integrated result without significant differences. In the future, the advanced spatial multi-omics technologies should be applied to investigate the underlying mechanisms.

In the present study, low expression of CD36 were observed in the subpopulation of the fibroblasts/HSCs according to the scRNA-seq analysis, suggesting the involvement of alternative signaling pathways that warrant further investigation. The immunofluorescence results showed a consistent trend (partly not significant differences); although the co-expression data for immunofluorescence are not perfect, our integrated in vivo, in vitro, and multi‑omics results collectively demonstrate that the Thbs1/CD36 signaling pathway plays an important role after MenSC-sEVs transplantation. We speculate that immunofluorescence may be due to Thbs1 and CD36 co‑expression in other cell types, which interfered with the semi-quantitative analysis for immunofluorescence. Therefore, this pathway is not the only mechanism involved, and other potential mechanisms should be explored in future studies. Additionally, we have neither demonstrated nor excluded the possibility that MenSC-sEVs act directly on HSCs or other cells independent of Kupffer cell-mediated signaling. These related experiments will be further investigated in future studies.

Moreover, MSC-EVs may solve many safety concerns and ethical limitations with regard to MSC-based therapies [94]. Although MSC-EVs offer some advantages regarding for patient safety, it is important to note that variations in the isolation, characterization, and administration protocols currently hinder reproducibility and comparability for MSC-EVs application [95]. Moreover, future efforts should focus on standardizing these methodologies for the isolation and detection to ensure reliable interpretation and validation of experimental outcomes [96]. In the present study, no mouse death was observed following the administration of MenSC-sEVs. Nevertheless, we plan to conduct further experiments to investigate potential immune rejection associated with MenSC-sEVs in future studies.

Conclusions

In the present study, we preliminarily demonstrated that MenSC-sEVs exert a therapeutic effect in mouse liver fibrosis. The subpopulations of Kupffer cells and fibroblasts/HSCs were re-clustered using scRNA-seq analysis, further revealing their interactions with Kupffer cells and HSCs via THBS pathway. Additionally, CellChat analysis revealed the interactions between Kupffer cells and HSCs, highlighting the underlying Thbs1-CD36 molecular crosstalk.
