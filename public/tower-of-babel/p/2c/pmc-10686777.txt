Expression of lysophosphatidic acid receptors in the rat uterus: cellular distribution of protein and gestation-associated changes in gene expression
Authors: Shiro KURUSU, Ryota TERASHIMA, Makoto SUGIYAMA, Miho TANAKA, Takuma KADOWAKI, Keiichiro KIZAKI, Mitsumori KAWAMINAMI
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThough lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) shows a variety of regulatory roles in reproduction, its action mechanisms in the gestational organs are still largely unknown. We here characterized cellular distribution of its six kinds of specific receptors (LPA1-6) in rat uteri by immunohistochemistry and quantitatively analyzed changes in Lpar1-6 mRNAs expression throughout pregnancy. Among LPA1-6, evident expression of LPA3, LPA4, and LPA6 was immunologically detected and less expression of immunoreactive LPA1 and LPA2 was also found. Luminal and glandular epithelial cells, stromal cells, and myometrial cells are sites of positive immunoreactions, and they are all likely to express three or more subtypes. All of Lpar1-6 mRNAs were expressed, and their alterations were variable depending on subtypes and gestational age. The present information suggests that diverse actions of LPA in the uterus involve varied expression of LPA receptors dependent on tissue/cell types, receptor subtype(s), and organ reproductive states and helps to understand uterine biology of LPA.Keywords: gestation, lysophosphatidic acid, lysophosphatidic acid receptor, uterus

Lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) has multiple roles in mammalian reproductive physiology and pathology [18, 32, 33]. LPA actions reported to date in uteri of rodent, ruminant, and other species include myometrial contraction [11, 16, 19, 29], implantation [11, 24, 34, 35], decidualization [2, 3, 24], and stromal cell proliferation [1] and prostaglandin (PG) production [3, 9, 15, 22, 30, 31]. A total of six kinds of G protein-coupled receptors, LPA1-6, exist to mediate effects of this lipid mediator in the body [7, 33, 37]. They are structurally classified into the endothelial differentiation gene (EDG) family (LPA1-3) and the P2Y purinergic family (LPA4-6). They couple to at least one or more of four G proteins (G12/13, Gq/11, Gi/o, and Gs) and generate a variety of signaling cascade that can be redundant and have crosstalk among each other [7, 33, 37]. These subtypes are also likely to have different expressional regulation and tissue/cell distribution, but our knowledge regarding these issues is still limited.

Previous studies that implicated LPA signaling in various uterine functions both in vivo and in vitro have focused on one or a few receptor subtypes (mainly LPA3 or LPA1) at a particular reproductive stage (around the period of implantation and decidualization). Demonstration of the tissue/cell level distribution of mRNA or protein of receptor subtypes is quite limited [14, 24, 34, 35]. A comprehensive analysis of uterine LPA1-6 has only been done for their mRNA expression in cultured endometrial stromal cells from women and mice [1, 4]. One report with human placenta, but not uteri, has demonstrated simultaneous LPAR1-6 mRNA expression and their alteration between the first and the third trimesters and distribution of immunoreactive LPA receptors in syncytiotrophoblast and extravillous trophoblast cells [5]. Comprehensive analyses of LPA1-6 gene expression in relation to gestational state and of protein expression/localization in tissue/cell level should provide fundamental information to deeply understand integrative roles of LPA in the uterine physiology. We here perform 1) immunohistochemical detection of LPA1-6 in uteri of rats of estrus (non-pregnant) and term, and 2) quantitative analysis of Lpar1-6 mRNAs expression throughout normal gestation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and uterine tissue preparation

Experimental procedures and animal handling were approved and conducted following the Guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of School of Veterinary Medicine (Approval Number, 20-067). Female rats of Wistar-Imamichi strain were kept in temperature (23 ± 3°C)- and lighting (14:10 light/dark cycle)-controlled rooms. Rat chow and tap water were available ad libitum.

Rats were 3 to 5 months old, weighed ranging from 250 to 400 g, and showed a regular 4-day estrous cycle. Pregnant rats were prepared as described previously [13, 21], and the day of estrous vaginal smear with sperm presence was designated as day 1 of pregnancy (depicted as PRG1 in this paper). Rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation under isoflurane anesthesia on the estrus, PRG6, 12, 15, 18, 21, 22 and 23 (just after delivery) (n=4, at each time point). Uteri were instantly excised out from the body, and in the case of gravid-uteri, fetal membranes, fetuses and placentas were freed and the remaining uteri were sampled. For gene expression analysis, uteri were sampled on PRG6, 12, 15, 18, 21, and 23. For the histological processing, uteri were obtained from rats of estrus and PRG22.

Immunohistochemistry

To try to identify the subtype(s) and cellular distribution of LPA receptor proteins in rat uteri, immunohistochemistry of LPA1-6 was carried out using antibodies commercially available. The characteristics and sources of the primary antibodies were summarized in Supplementary Table 1 with references that had applied the item for immunohistochemistry [17, 23, 24, 26,27,28] and immunocytochemistry. Antigenic peptides against which the antibodies were generated had very low and little homology in their amino acid sequence. Immunohistochemical procedure in general was similar to those of the previous reports [17, 27, 28]. Uteri were instantly fixed in Bouin’s solution, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Tissues were serially sectioned at 4 μm in thickness, deparaffinized, and examined immunochemically. Sections were stained with either hematoxylin and eosin (HE) or immunostained with the antibody against each antigen. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by pretreatment with 0.3% H2O2 in methanol for 30 min. After blocking with 3% normal bovine serum, the sections were incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4°C. Then, the second antibody anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated (Dilute kit 1: 4; Nichirei, Tokyo, Japan) was incubated for 60 min at 21–24°C. Antibody was revealed by treatment with 3, 3′-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride using the Histofine Simple Stain MAX-PO kit (Nichirei) for the appropriate time (4 to 6 min) depending on the antigen [27]. The sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted. Neighboring sections for each sample incubated with the non-specific immunoglobulin G (IgG) served as negative controls.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

The tissues harvested from rats on PRG6, 12, 15, 18, 21, and 23 (the day of delivery) were instantly snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using ISOGEN (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan) and then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with qDNA Remover (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). RT-qPCR for Lpar1-6 or ribosomal protein L19 (Rpl19) was performed using THUNDERBIRD STBR qPCR Mix (TOYOBO) at 95°C for 30 sec, 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 sec, and 60°C for 30 sec, respectively. Primer sets for 7 genes of interest were described in Supplementary Table 2. Relative gene expression levels were calculated by delta-delta CT method using Rpl19 as an internal control for normalization.

Statistical analysis

Numerical values were treated statistically and presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The means among different groups were analyzed by Tukey-Kramer multiple comparison test. A P value less than 0.05 was considered to be significant.

RESULTS

Immunolocalization of LPA1-6 proteins in rat uteri

The immunoreactions of LPA receptors in serial sections of estrous rat uteri were summarized in Fig. 1. Uterine tissues from 3 rats were investigated, and little individual-dependent differences in staining behavior were confirmed. Endothelium, stroma, and myometrium in transverse sections with HE staining were described as reference. Endometrial epithelial cells were intensely positive for LPA4 and LPA6, slightly positive for LPA2 and LPA3, and negative for LPA1 and LPA5. Stromal cells just beneath the epithelium were positive for LPA1, LPA4 and LPA6. Glandular epithelial cells were positive only for LPA4. Some stromal cells were evidently positive for LPA1 and LPA4. In the myometrium, the inner (circular) muscular layer and the outer (longitudinal) muscular layer were both intensely positive for LPA3 and LPA6, faintly positive for LPA4 and LPA1, and negative for LPA2 and LPA5. Considering the cell size and the section thickness, immunohistochemical results of different subtypes in serial sections demonstrate the presence and/or absence of each subtypes in the identical cells of epithelia, myometrium and stroma.

Localization of immunoreactive lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) receptors in the uterine endometrium, stroma, inner and outer myometrium are shown. I, inner myometrium; O, outer myometrium. Scale bars, 50 μm.

Subsequently the immunoreactions of LPA receptors in uteri at term (PRG22) were investigated and compared. Due to the presence of the growing fetus, rat uteri on this critical period were elongated, with a thin stromal region and close proximity between endometrium and myometrium (Fig. 2A). Endometrial and glandular epithelial cells were intensely positive for LPA6 and moderately positive for LPA4 and negative for LPA3 (Fig. 2B). As evidently shown by enlarged images (Fig. 2C), positive immunoreactions were specific to cell types and mainly in cytoplasm. Marked immunoreactions for LPA3 and LPA4 were present in stromal cells especially beneath the endometrial epithelium (Fig. 2B and 2C). In the myometrium at term that possess robust contracting activity both in spontaneous and LPA-stimulated conditions [19], the immunoreactivities for LPA3, LPA6, and LPA4 were positive, and in any subtypes the immunoreactivity was more intense in the outer muscular layer than in the inner one (Fig. 2B and 2C). Smooth muscle cells in stromal arteriole were also positive. Little reactions were detected in the inter-muscle layer sites where interstitial pace-maker cells of uterine spontaneous contraction are thought to localize. Immunoreactions for LPA1, LPA2, and LPA5 were negative in uterine tissues at term, as was similar in normal (control) IgG-applied section (Supplementary Fig. 1). Little LPA5 was detected by the current antibody in any samples of uterine tissue examined. The simultaneous run of immunohistochemical procedure on the samples of estrus and term made it possible to roughly compare the immunoreactivity of each subtype between the two time points. Compared to those in uteri from estrous rats, the immunoreactivities for LPA3 and LPA4 in endometrial stromal cells and for epithelial LPA6 were likely increased in uteri on PRG22 (Figs. 1 and 2). On the other hand, the immunoreactivities for LPA3 and LPA6 in the inner muscle layer were likely lower.

Expression of Lpar1-6 genes in rat uteri throughout gestation

As no comprehensive analysis of all Lpar expression in uteri throughout gestation has been reported, we measured their mRNA levels in a rat model. All of Lpar1-6 mRNA were detected in uteri of pregnant rats. Fluctuations of relative expression levels of Lpar during gestation differed depending on subtypes (Fig. 3). Though significant alteration was not found, there were trends for increase in Lpar1, Lpar3, and Lpar5 expression toward the time of parturition. Expressions of Lpar4 and Lpar6 were high on PRG6, around the time of decidualization, and decreased and remained low thereafter. Uterine Lpar2 expression was constant throughout gestation.

Gestation-associated changes in Lpar1-6 mRNA expression in rat uteri. Expression of Lpar (Lpar1-Lpar6) mRNAs relative to that of day 6 of pregnancy (PRG6) are expressed as mean ± SEM (n=4). Different alphabetical letters mean significant differences (P<0.05).

DISCUSSION

In accordance with previous reports on mouse endometrial stromal cells [1] and uterine strips at a particular time of pregnancy [34], we confirmed expression of all Lpar1-6 mRNAs in rat uteri and newly revealed their alterations depending on subtypes and gestational age. Moreover, LPA receptor proteins except for LPA5 were immunologically detected in uterine tissues in cell-type specific manner. Epithelial, myometrial, and stromal cells are all immunoreactive for three or more receptor subtypes, suggesting LPA-driven multiple signaling pathways in these cells.

Alteration in uterine Lpar mRNA levels throughout gestation is the first report in any species. Though little statistical difference, expression levels of Lpar1, Lpar3, and Lpar5 tended to increase in association with delivery, while those of Lpar4 and Lpar6 were highest in early pregnancy and depressed thereafter. The stage- and subtype-dependent dynamics suggests expressional regulation that is common and distinct in a set of receptor subtypes genes and is affected by gestation-associated endocrine milieu. A few direct evidences showed ovarian steroid hormones regulation of receptor mRNA or protein levels in uteri of mice [10, 34] and sheep [14]. Progesterone up-regulated only LPA3 expression, while estradiol-17β down-regulated all subtypes expression. These effects demonstrated in early pregnancy are, however, not accountable for the observed changes in Lpar levels in late pregnancy and at term, as circulating progesterone [12, 13] and estradiol-17β [6, 36] levels declined and sharply increased, respectively at term. Tissue-distinctive measurement of genes expression in the middle and late pregnancy and perturbation experiment will further be needed.

Uterine tissue distribution of LPA1-6 protein is also probably the first comprehensive analysis. A sole previous investigation of comprehensive immunohistochemistry of LPA1-6 in human placenta appeared to utilize the uncharacterized antibodies that were not utilized and evaluated by any other groups [5]. One of our limitations in this research is the failure of preparation of the ideal negative control specimens (the usage of antibodies pre-absorbed with each antigen). Our trial with less strict negative control (the non-specific antibody application), however, revealed that most, but not all, of antibodies currently used produced recognizable signals that were cell type-specific and variable in intensity. Immunoreactive LPA receptors expression in rat uteri depends on subtypes, tissue/cell types, and functional states. Preferential localization of the immunoreactivities in the cytoplasm rather than plasma membrane may be due to the possible decrease by the utilization of organic solvent(s) in the fixation and histological procedure and/or to actually temporal localization before transport to the plasma membranes. In our current investigation, the intensity of immunoreactions could be comparatively validated for an identical antigen among tissue/cell, but not for identical tissue/cell among antigens. We also recognize the inconsistency between the mRNA expression levels and the intensity of immunoreactivity of each subtype, for example at term. The mechanism for expressional regulation remains to be elucidated as another limitation of this study.

A part of our present findings is consistent with previous histological data of epithelial expression of LPA3 mRNA [34, 35] and protein [14, 24] and of stromal cell expression of LPA1 protein [14]. Multiple subtypes of LPA receptors are likely to co-localize in myometrium, stroma, and luminal and glandular epithelia. Given with their established potencies of increasing Ca2+ mobilization and Rho activation [7, 33, 37] and evidence for those in LPA-treated muscle cells in vitro [8, 20], significant co-existence in outer muscle layers of LPA3, LPA4, and LPA6 indicates their possible contribution to contractile function in response to LPA. Our recent functional study revealed that Ki16425, an LPA1/2/3 antagonist, significantly inhibited LPA-stimulated uterine contraction in vitro in rats of estrus but not at term [19]. It is therefore suggested that uterine myometrium co-expresses multiple subtype for LPA-stimulated contraction that alters dependently on physiological states. Similarly, LPA1-4 are likely co-expressed in stromal cells, whose proliferation and secretory function of PGs, growth and angiogenic factors are central processes of implantation and decidualization. By means of subtype-specific gene deletion or chemical agonist/antagonists, LPA3 and LPA1 are demonstrated to have critical roles in stromal cell proliferation and secretion in early pregnancy [1, 2, 25, 30]. Our data of LPA1-4 presence in rat endometrial stroma support these previous findings and suggest minor role for LPA2 and LPA4 in this tissue. Simultaneous expression of multiple LPA receptor subtypes may ensure LPA signaling, the regulation and significance of which are important issues in future. Our present finding of appearance of endometrial stromal cells intensely immunoreactive for LPA3 and LPA4 on PRG22 raises a new issue of LPA’s non uterotonic role at term.

In conclusion, this study is the first demonstration of all types of LPA receptors expression in the uterus comprehensively throughout normal pregnancy and parturition. We revealed expression of all Lpar1-6 genes and its regulation by gestational age and cell type-dependent localization of immunoreactive proteins except for LPA5 in rat uterus. All of myometrial, stromal, and epithelial cells appear to express at least three types of LPA receptors, further suggesting their usage of multiple signaling pathways to LPA stimulation. Previous mechanistic studies have often dissected LPA-LPA receptor signaling with focusing on one or a couple of receptor subtypes. Our current data is descriptive, but helps to interpret the previously reported findings of LPA’s action more concisely and to design the future experiments on receptor subtypes newly found to be expressed. It can also be fundamental to other studies on uterine-related reproductive diseases and with human and livestock animal subjects.
