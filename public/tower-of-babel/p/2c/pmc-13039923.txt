Fixed-time formation behavior control for unmanned ground vehicle-manipulators
Authors: Wenyan Xue, Wenjin Lu, Xiangzi Zhang, Houmin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnmanned ground vehicle-manipulators (UGVMs), which integrate mobility and dexterous manipulation, are increasingly deployed in complex environments. However, their formation control is challenged by nonholonomic constraints, external disturbances, and multi-task conflicts. This paper proposes a fixed-time formation behavior control (Fixed-FBC) method UGVMs operating in static obstacle environments under external disturbances and system uncertainties. The approach introduces a nonholonomic null-space behavioral control (N-NSBC) framework that integrates fixed-time stability strategy for rapid convergence, systematic incorporation of nonholonomic constraints to inherently avoid local minima by resolving yaw-position coupling, and transformation of multi-objective coordination into unified velocity commands. To address dynamic uncertainties, an adaptive fixed-time tracking controller is developed that employs adaptive laws to estimate unknown system parameters in real-time while utilizing sliding mode techniques to reject external disturbances. Simulation results demonstrate a reduction in settling time compared to conventional methods, along with effective coordination of formation maintenance, obstacle avoidance, and manipulator control.Keywords: Unmanned ground vehicle-manipulators, Fixed-time control, Formation behavior control, Adaptive tracking controller, Obstacle avoidanceSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Unmanned ground vehicle-manipulators (UGVMs) integrate mobile platforms with robotic manipulators, thereby combining mobility and dexterous manipulation capabilities. This unique structure enables them to perform complex tasks in unstructured environments, such as material handling in automated warehouses, cooperative assembly in manufacturing, and search-and-rescue in disaster scenarios1,2.

Studying the formation control of UGVM systems is of significant strategic and research importance for enhancing industrial production efficiency, as it breaks through the limitations of single equipment in operational range and efficiency, especially in the execution of coordinated tasks such as material transportation3 or facility inspection4. Nevertheless, the nonholonomic constraints of the mobile platforms, coupled with the manipulator dynamics and external disturbances, make the formation control problem particularly challenging.

Previous research has proposed various formation control methods. Behavior-based approaches5,6 define a set of basic behaviors (e.g., goal reaching, obstacle avoidance) and combine them through arbitration mechanisms. Although capable of resolving multi-task conflicts, conventional implementations of the methods often lack rigorous stability proofs. And consensus-based formation control strategies7,8, on the other hand, rely on inter-agent communication to achieve state agreement. For instance, an adaptive neural consensus tracking controller for multi-agent systems was developed to enable all followers to synchronize with the leader even under intermittent connectivity and network attacks9. However, while effective for coordinating a single collective task, consensus-based methods lack a structured mechanism to prioritize and reconcile multiple, often conflicting objectives. This limitation is critical for UGVM systems, which must concurrently execute essential tasks such as formation maintenance, obstacle avoidance, and manipulator operation, objectives that frequently compete in real-world scenarios. Beyond the above approaches, other advanced strategies have been explored for complex robot coordination. For obstacle avoidance and safe control, control barrier functions (CBFs) coupled with model predictive control (MPC) have gained prominence for their rigorous safety guarantees10. Nevertheless, their computational burden can be high for real-time multi-robot systems, and integrating them with other objectives (e.g., formation keeping, manipulation) often requires additional arbitration mechanisms. To overcome this fundamental limitation in multi-task coordination, behavior-based control architectures have been further developed. Among these, the Null-Space-based Behavioral Control (NSBC) method provides a rigorous mathematical solution by projecting lower-priority tasks onto the null space of higher-priority ones, thereby systematically merging multiple prioritized subtasks11. This framework has been extended in several directions. For example, an identifier-actor-critic reinforcement learning scheme was integrated into the NSBC framework, leading to the proposal of a behavior-based adaptive dynamic programming algorithm for multi-mobile manipulators12. Similarly, the NSBC framework was applied to the cooperative control of nonlinear second-order systems(i.e., Classical-FBC), and pioneering experimental tests were conducted in complex multi-task execution scenarios13. Yet, these existing methods are built upon traditional NSBC frameworks that only guarantee asymptotic convergence of task errors. To improve convergence efficiency, fixed-time control was first incorporated into the NSBC framework (i.e., Classical-NSBC), leading to the design of a distributed fixed-time behavioral strategy at the kinematic level to ensure fixed-time convergence of task errors for second-order MASs14. Notably, the aforementioned methods, including Classical-NSBC, are primarily designed for fully actuated systems and cannot be directly applied to nonholonomically constrained UGVM systems. This limitation may not only cause the system to fall into local minima during practical operation but also means that its convergence performance remains confined to asymptotic convergence. To the authors’ knowledge, no existing research has developed an NSBC framework suitable for nonholonomic systems, making the design of such a framework an urgent challenge to address.

Furthermore, given the significant uncertainties in UGVM dynamics, which are primarily due to external disturbances such as environmental variations and payload uncertainties and substantially increase system complexity15, several compensation strategies have been proposed. For instance, an adaptive robust control strategy has been developed by integrating radial basis function neural networks with nonlinear state observers16, which established a unified control framework that designed virtual velocity planning at the kinematic level and implemented torque control to track this velocity at the dynamic level. In parallel, a tracking controller combining MPC with adaptive fuzzy logic techniques was also designed for wheeled mobile manipulators17. An adaptive fractional-order sliding mode controller integrated with a nonlinear disturbance observer has been developed for continuum soft robots, demonstrating precise and robust tracking performance under substantial model uncertainty and disturbance18, but ignoring the coupling between nonholonomic constraints and formation coordination. In contrast, the aforementioned controllers only guarantee asymptotic convergence. To address this limitation, a kinematic model incorporating finite-time tracking controllers was proposed for nonholonomic scenarios (i.e., Finite-FBC)19. However, the convergence time of this approach remains dependent on initial system states, which are often difficult to obtain accurately. Subsequently, the fixed-time stability concept was introduced, in which the convergence time bound becomes independent of initial conditions20. Building on this concept, mobile manipulators were further decomposed into nonholonomic mobile platforms and holonomic manipulator subsystems, with their dynamic coupling treated as a disturbance, and a fixed-time controller was developed to handle parametric uncertainties and unknown bounded disturbances21.

In summary, existing methods typically address only isolated aspects of the intertwined challenges in UGVM formation tasks, including nonholonomic constraints, external disturbances, slow error convergence, and multi-task conflicts, without providing a unified solution that simultaneously ensures fast, robust, and conflict-free coordination.

To address the aforementioned limitations, this paper develops a two-layer Fixed-FBC method integrating kinematic-level task coordination and dynamic-level tracking control. Compared with existing methods, the proposed method differs in three key aspects: 1) the N-NSBC framework overcomes the incompatibility of classical NSBC with nonholonomic systems14; 2) the fixed-time strategy ensures convergence time independence of initial conditions, outperforming asymptotic and finite-time control methods13,16,17,19; 3) the integrated two-layer design achieves seamless coordination between multi-task handling and robust tracking, avoiding trade-offs between convergence speed and stability10,14,19. The main contributions are summarized as follows:

1) At the kinematic level, a novel N-NSBC framework is proposed, which explicitly incorporates nonholonomic constraints and resolves yaw-position coupling to overcome the incompatibility of classical NSBC methods with nonholonomic UGVM systems. Unlike the Classical-NSBC14 that relies on a decoupled point-mass model (prone to local minima) and fails to consider nonholonomic constraints, the proposed framework inherently avoids local minima while unifying multi-task coordination into unified velocity commands, ensuring full compatibility with UGVMs’ kinematic characteristics.

2) At the dynamic level, a fixed-time stability strategy is integrated into the N-NSBC framework and adaptive tracking controller, ensuring convergence time independence of initial conditions. This outperforms asymptotic convergence-based methods (Classical-FBC13, adaptive neural control16, MPC-based tracking control17) and initial condition-dependent finite-time control (Finite-FBC19), achieving rapid and predictable convergence for formation, obstacle avoidance, and manipulator motion tasks.

3) An integrated two-layer Fixed-FBC design is developed, seamlessly coordinating kinematic-level multi-task handling (formation maintenance, obstacle avoidance, manipulator motion) and dynamic-level robust tracking. By fusing adaptive control and sliding mode techniques, the dynamic controller provides real-time compensation for external disturbances and nonlinear uncertainties, avoiding the trade-off between convergence speed and stability that plagues existing methods (e.g., CBF-MPC10 with high computational complexity, Classical-NSBC14 with slow asymptotic convergence, Finite-FBC19 with initial condition sensitivity).

Remark 1

Assumption 3 is made to simplify the initial theoretical analysis, In practice, communication delays and irregular obstacle geometries can be addressed within the proposed framework through several extensions.

1) For slight communication delays: A prediction compensation mechanism can be integrated into the N-NSBC framework, where each UGVM predicts the states of other UGVMs using a Kalman filter based on historical received data. Additionally, an event-triggered communication strategy (similar to21) can be adopted to reduce unnecessary data transmission, balancing communication burden and control performance.

2) For irregular obstacles: the proposed framework can be extended in two primary ways. First, the conservative circumscribed sphere method27 offers a straightforward and computationally efficient solution by approximating any irregular obstacle with its minimum circumscribed sphere, enabling the direct use of the existing avoidance algorithm with strong safety guarantees, albeit potentially resulting in conservative paths. Alternatively, the precise discretization method28 seeks a better balance between safety and path optimality by decomposing a complex obstacle into a collection of overlapping or adjacent spheres (or cylinders). In future work, the latter method is intended for adoption, which would naturally extend the current avoidance behavior to evaluate multiple obstacles simultaneously—achieving more refined avoidance through distance calculations to the nearest sphere surface.

The Fixed-FBC method

This section proposes a Fixed-FBC method to address the fixed-time formation control problem for UGVMs, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The Fixed-FBC method is divided into two structures at the kinematic and dynamic levels. At the kinematic level, a framework that integrates fixed-time stability strategies with the nonholonomic NSBC method resolves task conflicts and constructs basic behaviors. These behaviors are subsequently synthesized via null-space projection into a composite behavior, which yields a unified desired velocity. The velocity is subsequently used as the input for an adaptive fixed-time tracking controller at the dynamic level, where an adaptive law is designed to provide real-time compensation for unknown external disturbances and unstable nonlinear dynamics. The method ensures fixed-time convergence while systematically addressing prioritization, disturbances, and system nonlinearities.

Composite behavior design for UGVMs

This section designs a desired velocity for each mobile manipulator. The goal is to achieve fixed-time convergence for all three behavioral task errors: formation maintenance (FM), formation obstacle avoidance (FOA), and manipulator motion (MM). This is accomplished by integrating their individual desired velocities within the NSBC framework.

Specifically, when the UGVMs perform formation-based object transportation and encounter obstacles on the desired trajectory, a task conflict arises between the FM and FOA behaviors. To ensure the safety of the transportation task, the FOA behavior is designated as the high-priority task, while the FM behavior is assigned as the low-priority task. The FM behavior is then projected into the null space of the FOA behavior. Comparative method

Firstly, we compare the proposed NSBC with nonholonomic constraints to the Classical-NSBC method14. The initial positions of the UGVMs, controller parameters, desired trajectory, and the desired trajectory for the FM task remain consistent with those in Section 5.1, while the target positions are modified to , , , , and an obstacle is introduced.

The simulation results for the Classical-NSBC method14 are shown in Fig. 10, while those for the proposed N-NSBC method are presented in Fig. 11. Since the Classical-NSBC method employs a point-mass kinematic model, it neglects the coupling between the yaw angle and position, allowing the robot to move to any position without adjusting its yaw angle. Directly applying the Classical-NSBC method to the UGVM model can lead to local minima. Figure 10 demonstrates that when an obstacle is positioned directly in front of the formation, the Classical-NSBC method fails to adjust the formation’s direction, causing the UGVMs to become trapped in a local minimum and resulting in FM task failure.

Simulation results of the proposed N-NSBC Method: (a) trajectories (b) distance between UGVMs and Obstacles.

In contrast, the proposed N-NSBC method explicitly incorporates the coupling between the yaw angle and position of the UGVMs. This capability enables it to achieve a more accurate representation of the nonholonomically constrained system. As shown in Fig. 11, when an obstacle lies directly ahead of the UGVM formation, the proposed method adjusts the formation’s direction to escape local minima and successfully accomplish the desired formation task. This comparative simulation verifies that the proposed NSBC method with nonholonomic constraints is better suited for UGVM systems than the Classical-NSBC method14, exhibiting stronger robustness against local minima.

Next, we compare the settling times of the proposed Fixed-FBC, finite-time formation behavioral control (Finite-FBC)19, and classical formation behavioral control (Classical-FBC)13. Relative to classical FBC, it achieves a reduction in the first settling time while maintaining comparable performance in subsequent phases.

In summary, the proposed Fixed-FBC method enables UGVMs to not only resolve the task conflict between formation and obstacle avoidance at the task level, achieving both formation and obstacle avoidance tasks within a fixed time, but also realizes fixed-time tracking control at the control level. Compared to the Finite-FBC method19 and classical-FBC formation method13, the proposed Fixed-FBC effectively reduces the settling time.

Conclusions

To address the collaborative formation control of UGVMs in static obstacle environments, this paper proposes a novel Fixed-FBC method that operates effectively at both kinematic and dynamic levels. At the kinematic level, we develop a nonholonomic NSBC method within the classical NSBC framework, incorporating a fixed-time stability strategy to construct three fundamental behaviors for formation transportation. We derive a desired merged velocity that guarantees fixed-time convergence of task errors. This desired velocity then serves as the control input for our dynamic-level adaptive fixed-time tracking controller, which employs advanced adaptive control and sliding mode techniques to enable real-time dynamic compensation for external disturbances and uncertain nonlinear dynamics. The proposed control scheme ensures that all UGVMs converge to their desired formations in fixed time while effectively avoiding obstacles. Finally, Simulation results demonstrate that the Fixed-FBC method reduces settling time by up to 76.1 compared with Finite-FBC19 and 40.9 compared with Classical-FBC13.

Future work

The proposed Fixed-FBC method achieves effective formation control of UGVMs in static obstacle environments, but there are still aspects that can be further improved and expanded, and the future research directions are as follows:

1) Event‑triggered communication with delay compensation: Future work will focus on designing event‑triggered communication schemes that not only reduce bandwidth usage but also explicitly account for bounded communication delays. By integrating predictive consensus protocols or delay‑robust event‑triggering conditions, the framework can maintain fixed‑time convergence while relaxing the ideal instantaneous‑communication assumption.

2) Dynamic and irregular obstacles via precise discretization: The current obstacle‑avoidance module will be extended to handle dynamic obstacles and arbitrary shapes by adopting a precise geometric discretization approach. Complex obstacles will be represented as a collection of overlapping/adjacent spheres (or cylinders), enabling fine‑grained distance queries and shape‑aware collision checking within the N‑NSBC behavioral layer. This will be coupled with real‑time perception and prediction to support dynamic environments.
