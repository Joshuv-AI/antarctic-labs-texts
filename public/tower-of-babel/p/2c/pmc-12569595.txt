Percutaneous Radiofrequency Ablation After Incomplete Adrenalectomy in a Lateralized Case of Primary Aldosteronism
Authors: Pierre-Antonin Rigon, Nicolas Villard, Karim-Alexandre Abid, Tobias Zingg, Sandrine Eimer, Grégoire Wuerzner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary aldosteronism (PA) is a common cause of secondary hypertension. When unilateral aldosterone secretion is identified, surgery is the treatment of choice in most patients and is associated with a high cure rate. Alternative treatments, such as radiofrequency ablation (RFA), are possible options, particularly when surgery is contraindicated.A 56-year-old patient presented with hypertension secondary to PA. A left lateralization was identified after adrenal vein sampling, and the patient underwent a left laparoscopic adrenalectomy. Postoperatively, a residual adrenal remnant was detected incidentally on a computed tomography scan. Persistent PA was confirmed by hormonal workup. To avoid open laparotomy, RFA was proposed as an alternative treatment. The procedure was a biological success, leading to normalization of PA markers.Although adrenalectomy failure is rare, RFA appears to be a possible alternative treatment strategy to prevent the need for repeat surgery and its associated risks.Keywords: primary aldosteronism, adrenalectomy, radiofrequency ablation

Introduction

The current prevalence of primary aldosteronism (PA) is estimated to be around 10% in hypertensive patients but reaches 30% among those with resistant hypertension (HTN) [1-3]. Its diagnosis is important as it may be a curable form of HTN. Furthermore, it is associated with a higher degree of target organ damage compared to essential HTN, despite equivalent blood pressure values [4]. The diagnostic workup begins with screening in suspected cases, such as young hypertensive patients, those with resistant HTN, or patients presenting with hypokalemia or atrial fibrillation. However, the latest European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommend systematic screening in all patients with confirmed HTN [5]. This screening is primarily based on measuring the aldosterone-renin ratio (ARR). In cases of elevated ARR, confirmatory tests must be performed, most often a saline loading test [6, 7]. Subsequently, it is necessary to distinguish between unilateral and bilateral disease. A computed tomography (CT) scan can raise suspicion if an adrenal nodule is present; however, the concordance of radiologic images with adrenal vein sampling (AVS) is only moderate [8]. If the patient wishes to undergo surgical treatment, the unilateral nature must be confirmed through AVS [9, 10].

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is an interventional radiology technique used for the treatment of solid tumors [11-13]. In the setting of adrenal tumors, RFA has been used as an alternative therapy in comparison to laparoscopic adrenalectomy in some studies [14]. A recent meta-analysis concluded a similar efficacy of both treatments with a shorter operative time and hospital stay regarding RFA [15]. RFA is also 3 times cheaper [16]. In some rare cases, microwave ablation, a similar technique to RFA, has been used following a surgical failure in the case of PA [17]. Here we describe the treatment success of PA by RFA following an incomplete adrenalectomy.

Case Presentation

A 56-year-old man was referred to the department of nephrology and hypertension at the Lausanne University Hospital for the discovery of HTN. This HTN was diagnosed in the context of a superficial thrombosis in the right eye. The patient had no prior medical follow-up and had not undergone HTN screening. His family history included HTN and myocardial infarction before the age of 60 in his brother and HTN in his sister. The patient was overweight, weighing 91 kg with a height of 183 cm, resulting in a body mass index of 27 kg/m². Office blood pressure was above 160/100 mmHg despite treatment with amlodipine 5 mg daily and valsartan 160 mg daily. Biologically, there was no hypokalemia, and renal function was normal. Treatment was first intensified by replacing valsartan with azilsartan 40 mg daily, introducing chlorthalidone 25 mg daily, and increasing amlodipine to 10 mg daily. Nevertheless, blood pressure remained above target.

Diagnostic Assessment

Investigations were undertaken to exclude a secondary cause of HTN. A contrast-enhanced CT scan showed no evidence of renal artery stenosis or adrenal abnormalities. Urinary free cortisol levels were within normal limits at 24.9 nmol/24 hr (9.02 µg/24 hr) [normal range (NR): 9.5-111 nmol/24 h; 3.44-40.2 µg/24 h]. Plasma free metanephrines and normetanephrines were also normal, at 0.28 nmol/L (0.05 µg/L) (NR: 0.03-0.85 nmol/L; 0.006-0.17 µg/L) and 0.55 nmol/L (0.10 µg/L) (NR: 0.04-1.39 nmol/L; 0.007-0.25 µg/L), respectively. Thyroid function was normal. However, there was a biochemical pattern suggestive of PA, with a markedly suppressed plasma renin activity (PRA) (<0.1 ng/mL/h; NR: 0.2-2.8 ng/mL/h), an elevated plasma aldosterone concentration of 678 pmol/L (24.45 ng/dL) (NR: <620 pmol/L; <22.3 ng/dL), and an ARR at 3,394 (pmol/L)/(ng/mL/h) [NR: <750 (pmol/L)/(ng/mL/h)]. Sodium intake, assessed by 24-hour urinary collection, was 144 mmol/24 hr, corresponding to an estimated intake of 8.4 g of sodium chloride. Aldosteronuria was also elevated at 30.8 µg/24 h (85 nmol/24 h) (NR: <12 µg/24 h; <33.3 nmol/24 h). Since sodium intake was within the normal range and both the ARR and aldosteronuria were elevated, no confirmatory test was performed. AVS was subsequently performed, revealing left-sided lateralization (Table 1).

Treatment

The patient was referred to the department of visceral surgery for a left adrenalectomy. The procedure was performed using a laparoscopic approach that was described as complex. The main challenge was the patient's overweight status. Histopathological analysis of the surgical specimen revealed focal adrenal hyperplasia, with no evidence of adenoma or malignancy (Fig. 1). Interestingly, the surgical specimen sent for pathological analysis consisted of an incomplete adrenal gland. Two weeks after the procedure, the patient developed a febrile condition, and a contrast-enhanced CT scan was performed. The CT was suggestive of right-sided pyelonephritis with no hematoma or abscess identified on the left side. However, a residual left adrenal remnant was still visible on the CT scan, indicating an incomplete adrenalectomy. This result is consistent with the pathological findings, indicating that only the lateral limb of the adrenal gland was removed during the surgery. The adrenal remnant was described by the radiologist as exhibiting nodular hyperplasia, raising suspicion of a persistent adenoma despite the focal hyperplasia identified in the histopathological analysis. A new hormonal assessment was conducted to investigate persistent PA. It showed a suppressed PRA (<0.1 ng/mL/h; NR: 0.2-2.8 ng/mL/h) associated with an aldosterone level of 283 pmol/L (10.2 ng/dL) (NR: <620 pmol/L; <22.3 ng/dL), resulting in an ARR of 1416 (pmol/L)/(ng/mL/h) [NR < 750 (pmol/L)/(ng/mL/h)] (Fig. 2). Because of the contrast between the absence of elevation of aldosterone and suppressed PRA, a second assessment was performed. It was consistent with a persistent PA with an aldosterone level of 687 pmol/L (24.76 ng/dL) (NR: <620 pmol/L; <22.3 ng/dL), a PRA of 0.25 ng/mL/h (NR: 0.2-2.8 ng/mL/h), and an ARR of 2748 (pmol/L)/(ng/mL/h) [NR < 750 (pmol/L)/(ng/mL/h)]. These findings suggested persistent PA, possibly related to the subtotal adrenalectomy. Given the patient's overweight status, an open laparotomy would be required to maximize the chances of success in a redo surgery. An alternative approach using RFA was discussed and proposed to the patient. The procedure was performed on the left adrenal remnant under general anesthesia. A cluster of 3 Covidien Cool-tip electrodes (2.5 cm active) was placed within the left adrenal gland using a posterior approach. The correct positioning of the electrodes was confirmed under CT guidance. Prior to the first thermal cycle, hydrodissection was performed to help secure the procedure. During the first cycle, the patient experienced an increase in heart rate and blood pressure, leading to a temporary interruption of the procedure. The second cycle was uneventful, with a final temperature of 79˚C. The control CT scan showed good coverage of the lesions with no complications (Fig. 3).

Outcome and Follow-up

The postoperative course was uneventful. At the 1-week follow-up, hypertension persisted, leading to the reintroduction of amlodipine 10 mg daily. A follow-up hormonal assessment was performed 6 weeks after RFA, corresponding to 6 weeks after spironolactone discontinuation, while the patient remained on amlodipine therapy (Fig. 4). It suggested a biochemical cure, with a PRA of 1.65 ng/mL/h (NR: 0.2-2.8 ng/mL/h) and an aldosterone level of 419 pmol/L (15.1 ng/dL) (NR: <620 pmol/L; <22.3 ng/dL), resulting in an ARR of 254 (pmol/L)/(ng/mL/h) [NR < 750 (pmol/L)/(ng/mL/h)]. Biochemical remission was therefore not accompanied by clinical remission. Despite the intervention, the patient still required azilsartan 40 mg daily and chlorthalidone 25 mg daily for blood pressure control. During follow-up, medication was modified to candesartan 16 mg daily and amlodipine 10 mg daily due to erectile dysfunction (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In cases of PA with lateralization, adrenalectomy should be proposed as a curative treatment. The laparoscopic approach is preferred over laparotomy due to shorter hospital stays and fewer complications [18]. RFA can be an alternative to surgery. It is currently widely used for the treatment of malignant tumors or metastases [11-13] and is increasingly being employed in the management of PA. This technique offers several advantages, including lower costs and a shorter hospital stay [15, 16]. Under CT or ultrasound guidance [19], RFA can target a very precise area, sometimes allowing the selective ablation of the adenoma rather than the entire adrenal gland [14]. Currently, no randomized study has compared surgery and RFA. However, available data suggest that outcomes may be less favorable with RFA than with surgery. The estimated complete clinical cure rate is 0% to 47%, with an additional proportion achieving partial success [14]. However, these results may be biased by the fact that RFA is often performed in more comorbid and frail patients. Such comorbidities increase the likelihood of coexisting essential HTN [20, 21]. A randomized controlled trial is currently underway to compare RFA and surgery (NCT05405101).

RFA has limitations, including the absence of histological confirmation and the risk of catecholaminergic crises [22]. This event is less common with the surgical approach due to the preventive ligation of the adrenal vein. One potential advantage of RFA is the possibility of performing the procedure under local anesthesia. However, in our center, general anesthesia is preferred, as it ensures complete patient immobility. Furthermore, RFA cycles can be painful, and achieving effective local anesthesia may be challenging, particularly in obese patients. Finally, in the event of a catecholamine surge, general anesthesia facilitates hemodynamic management.

Notably, in this case, RFA was employed following surgical failure, illustrating a novel application of this technique. This failure is likely attributable to the patient's overweight status, which made adequate exposure of the adrenal gland difficult during the laparoscopic procedure. After discussion, a secondary approach with RFA was chosen to avoid an open laparotomy and its associated postoperative consequences. Paradoxically, the patient's obesity was advantageous for the RFA procedure, as the abdominal fat provided a protective buffer between the adrenal remnant and adjacent structures. Furthermore, the use of hydrodissection prior to thermal ablation helped secure the procedure. This technique is facilitated by the presence of abdominal fat. RFA resulted in the patient's biochemical remission, with normalization of the ARR and measurable PRA. To our knowledge, this is the second reported case of radiological ablation following surgical failure. The first case was described by Mills et al [17]. It involved a 57-year-old man with PA, who had also been treated for bladder cancer. Due to an adrenal adenoma with lateralization, surgical treatment was chosen, with a retroperitoneoscopic approach. However, due to adhesions related to the previous bacillus Calmette-Guérin immunotherapy, the surgery was challenging. An intraoperative decision was made to perform an adenoma resection. Despite histological confirmation of complete adenoma removal, PA persisted. A similar technique to RFA, microwave ablation [23], was then used to complete the PA treatment. The patient showed sustained biochemical and clinical remission 6 years postprocedure. Another case of RFA use was reported in 2024 by Ahmadzade et al, involving a very different patient profile—a pheochromocytoma case [24]. A 35-year-old woman with multiple endocrine neoplasia had previously undergone right adrenalectomy for pheochromocytoma. Upon detection of a left adrenal lesion, RFA was chosen as a highly localized treatment to prevent adrenal insufficiency that would result from complete adrenalectomy. Despite an intraprocedural hypertensive crisis, the outcome was favorable, leading to disease remission.

To conclude, RFA can be a valuable alternative to surgery. In cases of failed surgical treatment, the potential persistence of residual adrenal tissue should be considered. When reoperation is necessary, RFA appears to be a promising option to minimize the postoperative consequences of an open laparotomy, particularly in high-risk patients. A multidisciplinary approach is essential for optimal patient management.

Learning Points

Surgery may result in incomplete adrenalectomy, leading to persistent PA. In such cases, RFA may represent a viable alternative treatment strategy, potentially avoiding the need for repeat surgery and its associated risks.
