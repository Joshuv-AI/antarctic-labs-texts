Bronchial Compression Following Surgical and Transcatheter Interventions for Coarctation of the Aorta
Authors: Jihyun Park, Brent M. Gordon, Brian S. Pugmire, Tom Roberts, Aamisha Gupta, Matthew T. Brigger, Aparna Rao, Howaida G. El-Said
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBronchial compression (BC) can occur after surgical repair of coarctation of the aorta (CoA); however, its incidence after stent implantation is not well understood. This study investigates the incidence, risk factors, and mechanisms of BC in patients undergoing surgical repair and stent implantation for CoA.MethodsA retrospective review of patients aged 0–25 years who underwent CoA procedures at Rady Children’s Hospital (San Diego, CA) between January 2007 and November 2022 was performed. Patients with computed tomography were included. BC was defined as an affected bronchus measuring <80% of the contralateral bronchus. Patients were divided into 3 groups: (1) group A—stent implantation only; (2) group B—surgical repair only; and (3) group C—surgical repair followed by stent implantation.ResultsAmong 205 patients with CoA, 148 met the inclusion criteria. The cohort was 64% male, with a median age at diagnosis of 2 days (IQR 0-54); 18% had single ventricle physiology. Group A included 15 patients (10%), group B had 119 patients (80%), and group C had 14 patients (9%). Surgical patients were younger (median 16 days [IQR, 8–54]) than stent patients [median 1939 days [IQR, 1220–2538]) at the time of the first intervention. BC was observed in 39 (26%) patients. Ten had preprocedural BC; 3 worsened postprocedure. De novo BC incidence was 7% (1/15) in group A, 18% (22/119) in group B, and 43% (6/14) in group C. Three patients required additional BC interventions.ConclusionsBC incidence in CoA patients is increased and can be exacerbated by procedures. Although most patients with BC did not require intervention, considering preprocedural and postprocedural BC monitoring is recommended for timely diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Coarctation of the aorta (CoA) is a common form of congenital heart disease (CHD) that comprises 5% to 8% of all CHD.1 It is often associated with other cardiac lesions, such as a bicuspid aortic valve or hypoplastic left heart syndrome, in addition to genetic syndromes, such as Turner syndrome.1,2 Surgical repair has been the gold standard of treatment for CoA, particularly for patients presenting in the neonatal period or early childhood.1,2 However, if amenable, transcatheter interventions, such as balloon angioplasty or stent implantation, have been used increasingly more frequently as an alternative.1,2 Bronchial compression (BC) has been reported following surgical repair of CoA.3, 4, 5 In a study by Ando et al,5 it was proposed that the mechanism for BC in patients following repair of arch obstruction is because of the anterior displacement of the descending aorta. However, the incidence and mechanism of BC after stent implantation are not well understood. This study aims to investigate the incidence, risk factors, and mechanisms of BC in patients who have undergone surgical repair and/or stent implantations for CoA.

Methods

Study design

Medical records, computed tomography (CT) images, and direct laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy (DLB) results for patients younger than 25 years of age between January 2007 and November 2022 with a diagnosis of CoA at Rady Children’s Hospital, San Diego, were reviewed retrospectively. The study received Institutional Review Board approval.

Study population

Inclusion criteria comprised of the following: (a) diagnosis of CoA, (b) history of either surgical repair and/or stent implantation for CoA, and (c) age <25 years old. Exclusion criteria included the absence of CT imaging and patients who only underwent balloon angioplasty of CoA.

Patients were divided into 3 groups based on the type of initial interventions that they received: (1) group A—stent implantation only; (2) group B—surgical repair only; and (3) group C—surgical repair followed by stent implantation. For patients who developed BC, any procedure performed before BC was diagnosed was considered an initial procedure.

Definition of BC

BC was diagnosed by either DLB or CT imaging. Currently, there are no standardized diagnostic criteria for BC in pediatric patients, and only limited data exist defining normal bronchial dimensions in the pediatric population.6 In general, the left mainstem bronchus is smaller in dimension compared with the right and the typical ratio of left mainstem bronchial dimension to right mainstem bronchial dimension is reported to range from 0.77 to 0.83.7 Therefore, we defined CT-based diagnosis of BC as following: diameter of the affected bronchus measuring 80% or less than that of the contralateral bronchus at the same level. Measurements were obtained in the sagittal and axial planes for both the narrowest segment of the compressed bronchus and the corresponding segment of the contralateral bronchus. Ratio of the bronchi was obtained by measurements, dividing the smallest diameter of the affected bronchus in the axial or sagittal plane by the diameter of the contralateral bronchus at the same level in the same plane. The smallest ratio was taken between axial and sagittal measurements for defining BC.

Statistical analysis

Data from subjects within the study population were compared utilizing several different statistical methods. Due to nonnormality in the data, continuous variables such as weight and age at the first intervention were analyzed using Mann–Whitney U tests when comparing 2 groups (Supplemental Table S1) and Kruskal–Wallis tests when comparing 3 groups (Table 1). Fisher exact test was used for categorical variables with expected cell counts less than 5, including the comparison of the incidence of BC among the 3 groups A, B, and C (Table 2). For all other categorical variables with expected cell counts greater than 5, χ2 tests were implemented. Results

Demographics

The study population comprised 148 subjects based on the inclusion/exclusion criteria, of which 95 (64%) were male. The initial procedure was surgery in 133 (90%) and stent implantation in 15 (10%) (Central Illustration). Re-intervention with stent implantation was performed in 15 patients (10%) following initial surgery due to re-CoA. In the entire cohort, the median age of subjects at the time of CoA diagnosis was 17 days old (IQR 8, 113), and the median weight of subjects at the time of CoA diagnosis was 3.38 kg (IQR 3.00, 4.41). BC

BC was observed in 39 (26%) patients. Ten patients had a BC diagnosis before any procedures, and 29 patients developed de novo BC following procedures (Central Illustration). Of the patients who had preprocedural BC, the compression worsened after stent implantation in 1 patient and after surgery in 2 patients.

The incidence of de novo BC differed by procedure type. It occurred in 1 of 15 patients (6.7%) after stent implantation alone (group A), in 22 of 119 patients (18.5%) following surgery alone (group B), and in 6 of 14 patients (42.9%) who received a stent following initial surgery (group C). The only statistically significant difference in the incidence of BC observed among groups was between groups A (stent implantation) and C (surgical repair followed by stent implantation) (P = 0.028) (Table 2).

In group B, 4 patients who developed de novo BC following initial surgeries also underwent stent implantation at a later time because of re-CoA, despite the existing BC. Interestingly, 2 of the 4 had improvement in the degree of BC following stent implantation.

Mechanisms of BC

In the preprocedural BC group (n = 10), the most common etiology of BC was due to poststenotic dilation of the descending aorta (n = 7) (Table 3). The remaining 3 patients had BC due to other reasons, and the affected areas were (a) the right BC, at the level of a right-sided Glenn anastomosis; (b) the proximal bronchus, near its bifurcation from the trachea; and (c) near the ductal arch, in the setting of a large patent ductus arteriosus (PDA). However, within the de novo BC group, a greater number of cases (n = 13) were attributed to these types of other causes rather than to stent-related issues or poststenotic dilation (Table 3). These 13 patients had BC at the level of proximal descending aorta (presumably at the level of previous CoA) without significant residual CoA or poststenotic dilation (Table 3). Procedures for BC

Two patients with preprocedural BC required additional interventions due to associated clinical symptoms (Figure 1). One patient had left left-sided aortic arch with discrete CoA without additional cardiac anomalies, but had BC due to poststenotic dilation. This patient initially had stent implantation as the initial procedure at 2 years old, with subsequent mild improvement in BC. However, persistent shortness of breath and exercise intolerance led to placement of an interposition graft from the ascending to the descending aorta at 8 years old without removal of the existing stent. Due to persistent symptoms despite the intervention, this patient ultimately had aortopexy via left thoracotomy at 10 years old with subsequent improvement in symptoms. The second patient also had preprocedural BC due to poststenotic dilation. The patient had hypoplastic left heart syndrome with mitral atresia and aortic atresia. They underwent the Norwood/Sano procedure at 15 days of life. They had multiple cannulations onto extracorporeal membrane oxygenation due to hypoxia, respiratory failure, and hemodynamic instability. They ultimately had their bronchial stent placed at 4 months of age, shortly after BC was noted for the first time. However, this patient passed away shortly after bronchial stent placement, unrelated to the procedure, as the family decided to withdraw care given her ongoing neurologic concerns and overall poor prognosis.

Lastly, one patient with de novo BC following surgery and stent implantation required an additional procedure because of BC. This patient had Williams syndrome with supravalvar aortic stenosis and pulmonary stenosis with CoA. The patient initially underwent patch augmentation of the aortic arch at 2 months of age. Subsequently, they required stent implantation for recurrent coarctation at 5 months and again at 11 months of age. Following the second stent procedure, BC was diagnosed. At 19 months old, they underwent patch plasty of the aortic arch. Intraoperative bronchoscopy, however, revealed persistent bronchial compression despite the aortic arch repair. Consequently, the existing coarctation stent was removed, and the aortic arch was replaced with a graft.

Degree of BC

Based on the definition for CT diagnosis of BC, all patients had an affected bronchus measuring <80% of the contralateral bronchus at the same level. Out of 39 patients with BC, 18 patients had an affected bronchus measuring <50% of the contralateral bronchus, and of those, 5 measured <30% of the contralateral bronchus.

However, the need for further interventions for BC did not correlate with the degree of BC based on CT measurements. None of the 5 patients with BC measuring <30% of the contralateral bronchus required interventions for BC, and the 3 patients who required interventions had BC measuring 33%, 50%, and 32% of the contralateral bronchus, respectively.

Associated conditions

Various cardiac, airway, and genetic lesions were evaluated for potential conditions that may be associated with a higher risk of BC (Supplemental Table S1).

VSD was the most associated cardiac lesion in both groups (56% in the BC group and 44% in the no BC group), followed by bicuspid aortic valve (36% in the BC group and 40% in the no BC group). However, there was no significant difference between the 2 groups for the prevalence of different cardiac lesions.

In addition, only 1 patient (3%) in the BC group and 7 patients (6%) in the no BC group had preprocedural airway abnormalities. These airway abnormalities included pig bronchus, tracheomalacia, cleft lip and palate, choanal atresia, and subglottic stenosis. However, the presence of a preexisting airway condition had no significant association with the incidence of BC (Supplemental Table 1).

The incidence of BC was not strongly associated with any specific genetic syndrome (Supplemental Table 1). The most common syndromes differed between groups: Down and Williams syndrome (5% each) in the BC cohort, and Turner syndrome (4%) in the non-BC cohort. Importantly, the overall prevalence of any clinically significant genetic syndrome was low and comparable between the BC and non-BC groups (15% vs 13%, respectively).

Discussion

Incidence of BC in patients with CoA was 26.4% (n =39) in our study. This is consistent with previously published data evaluating the incidence of airway compression following repair of the aortic arch, which noted the incidence of BC to range from 18.3% to 25.6%.8,9 However, in contrast to previous publications evaluating the incidence of BC following surgical aortic arch repair only, our study also looked at the incidence of BC prior to interventions and after transcatheter stent placements. Patients who underwent stent implantation alone (n = 15) had less incidence of BC (n = 1, 7%) compared with those who underwent surgery or surgery with stent implantation (n = 133), with a BC incidence of 21% (n = 28). This is a notable finding in that while BC can be seen in patients with CoA, different types of procedures may pose varying risks for the development and/or worsening of BC.

Also of interest are the mechanisms of BC in these patients who underwent different series of procedures on their aortic arch. Previously conducted studies evaluating the incidence and etiologies of BC in patients following aortic arch repair surgeries proposed that the change in the configuration of the aortic arch following surgeries is likely responsible for the development of BC.8,10 In general, a repaired aortic arch forms a more acute angle compared to a normal native arch, with anterior displacement of the descending aorta reducing the distance between the ascending and descending aorta.5,8,10 This can result in compression of the left mainstem bronchus, which lies between the ascending and descending aorta.5,8,10 Consistent with this hypothesis, the most common level of BC in patients who underwent surgical aortic arch repair in our study cohort was in the proximal descending aorta, likely because of the change in the geometry of the arch. For those who underwent stent implantation, in both groups A and C, BC was observed only at the level of the implanted stent. However, it is important to note that there was significantly more de novo BC in group C (patients with stent implantation after initial surgery [n = 6, 42.9%]), compared with group A (patients with stent alone [n = 1, 6.7%]) (Table 3). This raises the question whether the stent implantation in a previously surgically treated aortic arch has a higher risk of developing BC compared with that in a native aortic arch because of the difference in the underlying geometry of the aortic arch.

Lastly, screening for BC may be considered for all patients with CoA requiring intervention. Based on available guidelines for adult patients, cardiac MRI and/or CT are recommended to be obtained routinely to evaluate the extent and anatomy surrounding the CoA, but also to evaluate associated findings such as potential aneurysms and the presence of a bicuspid aortic valve.11 However, airways are not always the focus of the exam, and there are no standardized guidelines for routine airway evaluation in patients with CoA. In our study, utilizing bronchial diameter measurements on CT to diagnose BC appeared to be the most accurate and reliable means of diagnosing BC in patients. Consideration for developing a standardized evaluation for airways utilizing advanced imaging such as CT or MRI could be beneficial. During this retrospective review, 13 patients were newly diagnosed with BC based on the CT measurements by the definition outlined in this study. DLB was helpful to identify other associated airway lesions or to confirm the diagnosis, but not all patients had DLB. In fact, only 8 patients in this study had both DLB and CT, which confirmed the diagnosis of BC on both modalities. Previous studies have demonstrated that CT alone or used in conjunction with DLB has been the most sensitive in diagnosing BC, as DLB alone may detect a lower incidence of BC.8,9 Even though the majority of patients in our study had no clinically significant symptoms related to BC requiring further interventions, airway compression is frequently associated with respiratory distress in infants and young children with higher morbidity and mortality.10,12 Therefore, it would be reasonable to consider incorporating a routine screening process for BC utilizing imaging modalities such as CT for early detection and management, alongside DLB when indicated.

Limitations

This was a single-center retrospective study. There was a limited sample size, especially with the number of potential subjects without CT and the limited number of patients having BC. Some patients were lost to follow-up, and we were unable to obtain long-term prognosis on BC observed in the majority of the subjects that otherwise remained asymptomatic during the initial study follow-up period. Obtaining long-term data would help identify potential long-term effects on the BC and potential contributing factors that may later become complex over time.

There are no standardized radiologic diagnostic criteria for BC. During the retrospective chart review, there were minor differences in the CT results regarding the presence of BC in certain patients, depending on the reading radiologist. To improve the validity of the data, we used acceptable normal ranges of right and left mainstem bronchi and the expected ratio of left to right mainstem bronchi in healthy individuals that are available in the literature. In addition, 2 investigators, including 1 experienced radiologist, obtained bronchial measurements on CT to improve inter-observer reliability and overall accuracy of the data. Furthermore, to develop the definition of the BC, we considered 2 methods: (1) affected bronchus measuring <80% of the contralateral bronchus at the same level or (2) affected bronchus at the narrowest measuring <80% of the proximal portion of the same bronchus. These 2 methods yielded the same results in defining whether the patient had the BC or not. However, this alternative definition may be considered for future studies or patient management if there is a question of BC diagnosis based on the definition defined by our study.

Not all patients within our study population had preprocedural and postprocedural CT images available to review. Therefore, it is possible that a few of the subjects may have had preexisting asymptomatic BC that were not detected prior to intervention or worsening of asymptomatic BC following intervention. However, it is important to note that the BC observed in these patients was clinically insignificant.

In addition, a portion of the subjects had no clear identifiable mechanisms for the BC. Most of these patients had some distance between the bronchus and the aorta at the level of compression, suggesting a potential alternative etiology other than vascular compression. However, the majority had BC at the presumed level of CoA that was previously intervened on. It will be beneficial to evaluate other etiologies of BC in these types of patients in the future.

Lastly, although some subjects had high-resolution CT to evaluate their airway and lung fields, most patients had cardiac CT for evaluation of their cardiac anatomy. The measurements for the bronchi used in this study were obtained from any CT imaging that the patient had. Images obtained for cardiac CT were not specifically timed for the inspiratory or expiratory phase. Additionally, the caliber of the bronchi alters throughout the respiratory cycle, especially as they may measure narrowly during the expiratory phase. Therefore, this may have falsely exaggerated the degree of BC in some of the asymptomatic patients. For future studies and patient management, it is important to consider obtaining inspiratory and expiratory CT for accurate diagnosis of BC.

Conclusion

BC can exist in patients with CoA, frequently due to poststenotic dilation. Both surgery and stent implantation for CoA can either exacerbate preexisting BC or cause de novo BC. There was no significant difference in the incidence of BC among patients who underwent surgery versus stent implantation. Although most patients with BC did not require intervention, preprocedural and postprocedural monitoring for BC may be considered to ensure timely diagnosis and management.
