Introduction

Celiac trunk dissection refers to a tear in the intima of the celiac artery leading to formation of a false lumen within the vessel wall. This injury is exceedingly uncommon in blunt abdominal trauma – celiac artery injuries comprise as little as 0.01 % of all trauma cases [1], making it one of the rarest abdominal vascular injuries [1]. A literature review identified only a 21 reported cases (Table 1) of traumatic celiac artery dissection without an associated aortic injury [2]. Despite its rarity, this injury carries significant clinical importance due to the celiac artery's perfusion of the liver, spleen, stomach, and pancreas. Interruption of flow can result in visceral ischemia with potentially devastating consequences [3]. Diagnosis is often incidental during imaging for trauma assessment. Clinical presentation varies, ranging from asymptomatic cases to signs of visceral ischemia. Management strategies are diverse and depend on the hemodynamic status of the patient, extent of dissection, and associated injuries. We report a rare case of isolated celiac trunk dissection following a motor vehicle accident in a 44-year-old female pedestrian, successfully managed with conservative treatment.

Presentation of case

A 44-year-old previously healthy female presented to the emergency department after being struck by a car while crossing the street. On arrival, she was hemodynamically stable with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15. She complained of moderate upper abdominal pain but denied vomiting, chest pain, or dyspnea.

On examination, her vital signs were within normal limits. Abdominal palpation revealed tenderness in the epigastric region without guarding. No other external injuries were noted.

Initial laboratory investigations were unremarkable, including normal hemoglobin, lactate levels, and liver function tests. A contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was performed as part of the trauma workup. The scan revealed an isolated dissection of the celiac trunk extending from its origin, with preserved flow in the hepatic, splenic, and gastric branches. No signs of ischemia or intra-abdominal hemorrhage were identified. No other visceral or skeletal injuries were observed (Fig. 1).

Abdominal computed tomography in axial (A) and sagittal (B) planes showing the dissection of the coeliac trunk extending from its origin at arterial time.

Given the absence of ischemic complications and the patient's stable condition, a multidisciplinary decision was made to proceed with conservative management. She was admitted in our department for monitoring and initiated on prophylactic anticoagulation with low molecular weight heparin to prevent thromboembolic complications. Blood pressure was managed to avoid hypertension (to reduce shear stress on the injured artery), and the patient was kept NPO (nothing by mouth) for 24 h with IV fluids. Serial clinical exams were performed, focusing on abdominal tenderness, liver enzyme trends, and lactate levels to detect any early sign of visceral ischemia. Over the next 48 h, the patient remained stable. She had moderate epigastric soreness but no rebound or guarding, and her vital signs normalized. Laboratory monitoring showed a transient mild elevation in hepatic transaminases (AST and ALT peaked at ∼80 U/L on day 1, then trended down) – a pattern consistent with contusion or transient hypoperfusion but far from the levels seen in frank hepatic ischemia. No leukocytosis or lactic acidosis developed.

By hospital day 3, the patient was started on a light diet, which she tolerated without postprandial pain. After transitioning to oral antiplatelet therapy in place of heparin, the patient was discharged on hospital day 5 in good condition. At discharge, she had normal liver function tests and remained free of abdominal pain.

Discussion

Several anatomic and biomechanical factors have been postulated to influence the likelihood of celiac artery injury in blunt trauma. The celiac axis arises from the anterior aorta at the T12 level and is relatively short, branching quickly into the splenic, left gastric, and common hepatic arteries [3]. It is anchored by the dense celiac plexus and the median arcuate ligament of the diaphragm, which normally may shield it from minor trauma [3]. Paradoxically, in high-energy deceleration injuries, this ligamentous tethering can contribute to injury by pinning the vessel against the aorta, leading to intimal tearing or avulsion [4]. Reported mechanisms in published cases include sudden deceleration in vehicle collisions, direct impact from motorcycle handlebars, crush injuries, and falls from significant heights. These high-force mechanisms reflect that celiac artery dissection is often a marker of severe trauma and seldom occurs in isolation [3].

The clinical presentation of a celiac trunk dissection after blunt trauma is variable, ranging from entirely asymptomatic to fulminant abdominal catastrophe. Many patients who survive long enough to be evaluated are hemodynamically stable, because an intimal dissection or partial occlusion of the celiac artery may not cause immediate exsanguination. In some cases, especially when collateral circulation from the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) is sufficient, initial symptoms can be mild or nonspecific. Patients might report epigastric or upper abdominal pain, sometimes radiating to the back, or they may simply have unexplained nausea. On examination, abdominal tenderness is often minimal or even absent, which can be misleading. For example, Rosenthal et al. [5] described a patient who denied any abdominal pain despite having an 85 % stenosis of the celiac artery on imaging. Likewise, Gorra et al. [6] reported a case that was essentially asymptomatic – the patient had no abdominal complaints, and the injury was discovered only by imaging findings. These asymptomatic or subtle presentations are dangerous because they delay suspicion.

On the other end of the spectrum, some patients do present with signs of organ ischemia or internal bleeding. Severe epigastric pain (often with guarding or rigidity) can occur if the hepatic circulation is critically compromised or if a retroperitoneal hematoma is expanding [7]. Signs of mesenteric ischemia or hepatic injury may include vomiting, ileus, or rising liver enzymes. Laboratory tests are not diagnostic but may provide clues: isolated elevation of liver transaminases (AST, ALT) out of proportion to abdominal findings should raise concern for celiac axis compromise. In one case Linuma et al. [7], reported a patient with celiac occlusion that had AST/ALT levels in the 300–400 IU/L range shortly after injury, reflecting acute hepatocellular ischemia. On the other hand, Nguyen et al. [8] described a 68-year-old man whose celiac artery dissection led to continued hypotension and rising lactate; despite initial management, he developed infarctions of the stomach, liver, spleen, and duodenum within 48 h and did not survive.

This variability mandates a high index of suspicion in any blunt trauma patient with unexplained upper abdominal pain or abnormal lab findings.

Many cases have been detected on initial trauma CT scans, especially with the widespread use of contrast-enhanced whole-body CT in high-energy injuries. The key imaging findings of a celiac artery dissection on CTA include an intimal flap or dissection flap within the vessel, a focal luminal narrowing or filling defect (which may indicate a false lumen or thrombus), and often a periarterial hematoma or fat stranding around the celiac origin [4]. It is important that the CT scan includes an arterial phase dedicated to evaluating the abdominal vasculature. Standard trauma CT protocols emphasize portal venous phase imaging for solid organ injuries, which may miss a subtle vascular injury. Kirchhoff et al. [9] And Li et al. [10] reported exactly this pitfall: the initial whole-body CT (which did not specifically visualize arterial flow in the abdomen) failed to show the celiac injury, leading to a delayed diagnosis only when an arterial-phase CT was done days later. Such delays can be fatal. Therefore, trauma centers are advised to obtain dedicated CTA of the abdominal vessels if any signs point to possible visceral vascular injury.

Management of celiac artery dissection following blunt trauma is guided by the patient's hemodynamic status, symptoms, and imaging findings. Stable patients without signs of organ ischemia are often treated conservatively with hemodynamic monitoring, antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy which are optional and may not be necessary [11]. Several case reports document favorable outcomes using this approach—for instance, Rosenthal et al. [5] reported success with aspirin alone, while Han et al. [2] used intravenous heparin. Conservative management depends on intact collateral circulation from the superior mesenteric artery and requires close monitoring for clinical deterioration. Endovascular stenting is preferred when imaging reveals flow-limiting stenosis, pseudoaneurysm, or progressive ischemia. Covered stents can restore arterial integrity and prevent rupture, as demonstrated in the case reported by Mutluoglu et al. [4] where an ostial dissection with pseudoaneurysm was successfully treated with a stent. Open surgery is reserved for unstable patients, active hemorrhage, or cases where endovascular therapy is not feasible. Techniques include celiac artery ligation or vascular reconstruction, with ligation often tolerated due to collateral supply from the superior mesenteric artery. Iinuma et al. [7] described a case in which surgical ligation of a bleeding celiac trunk led to a favorable outcome. However, surgical intervention remains high risk and is generally used as a damage-control measure in critical trauma cases. In summary, conservative management is often effective in stable patients, endovascular repair is suitable for select complications, and open surgery remains necessary for life-threatening presentations. An individualized, multidisciplinary approach remains the cornerstone of care.

Conclusion

Celiac trunk dissection after blunt trauma is an exceptionally rare but significant vascular injury. Our comprehensive review of the literature reveals that while no universal treatment guideline exists, there is a convergence toward certain best practices. Hemodynamically stable patients without signs of organ compromise can usually be managed nonoperatively, with adjuncts like anticoagulation as appropriate. Endovascular stenting is an effective and less invasive option for those who need arterial repair. Open surgery remains a life-saving modality in unstable patients or when endovascular methods are not available, but it is needed in only a minority of cases. Timely imaging and multidisciplinary decision-making are essential for optimal outcomes.
