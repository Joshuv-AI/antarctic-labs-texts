Delayed-type Hypersensitivity to a Zinc-containing Drug as a Cause of Acute Tubulointerstitial Nephritis: A Case Report
Authors: Ken Aoki, Takahiro Uchida, Noriko Yoshikawa, Rie Fujii, Tadasu Kojima, Muneharu Yamada, Takashi Oda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 60-year-old Japanese woman with a history of chronic kidney disease presented with various electrolyte abnormalities and anemia and was admitted to our department. She received mineral replacement therapy consisting of potassium, iron, and zinc preparations but thereafter developed fever. Laboratory results showed mild eosinophilia and acute kidney injury with elevation in the markers of tubular injury. Acute tubulointerstitial nephritis (TIN) was clinically suspected, and steroid treatment was initiated. Renal biopsy revealed acute TIN with massive infiltration of macrophages and lymphocytes, intermingled with a little eosinophil infiltration, and substantial chronic histological changes. The drug lymphocyte stimulation test for zinc acetate hydrate, which was prescribed after admission, and other zinc-containing drugs was zinc-dose-dependently positive. The patient’s patch test for zinc chloride was also positive, and a final diagnosis of acute TIN due to delayed-type hypersensitivity to zinc was made. Zinc acetate hydrate was discontinued, and the steroid dose was tapered gradually. Subsequently, her renal function returned to its original level. Zinc supplementation is now performed for the treatment of not only hypozincemia but also treatment-resistant anemia. The present case suggests that severe TIN due to delayed-type hypersensitivity may occur in some patients treated with zinc-containing drugs.Index Words: Delayed-type hypersensitivity, drug lymphocyte stimulation test, patch test, tubulointerstitial nephritis, zinc

Kidney anemia is a major complication of chronic kidney disease (CKD), and its prevalence increases with the progression of CKD stages.1 Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESA) or hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors are now widely used for the treatment of kidney anemia; however, some patients present with treatment resistance, of which a shortage in micronutrients is a common cause.

Zinc is an essential micromineral involved in numerous enzymatic reactions in the body. Its deficiency causes various symptoms, including treatment-resistant anemia, and CKD patients are reportedly prone to zinc deficiency.2 In Japan, the off-label use of polaprezinc, which is approved for treatment of peptic ulcers, has commonly been preferred for zinc supplementation.3 Zinc acetate hydrate (ZAH) and zinc histidine hydrate are zinc-containing drugs that are now approved for the treatment of hypozincemia in Japan, and reportedly, their use increases serum zinc levels and improves ESA resistance.4 On the contrary, a decrease in serum copper levels is a well-known adverse effect because zinc and copper antagonize each other in absorption. In addition, there have been several reports of zinc allergy, especially in patients receiving insulin injection,5, 6, 7 as many insulin preparations contain zinc. However, to the best of our knowledge, no cases of zinc allergy with biopsy-proven renal injury have been reported.

Herein, we report a case of CKD in which acute kidney injury (AKI) occurred after the initiation of a zinc-containing drug. In the present case, acute tubulointerstitial nephritis (TIN) was diagnosed by renal biopsy, and a drug lymphocyte stimulation test (DLST) showed a positive reaction to several zinc-containing drugs in a zinc dose-dependent manner.

Case Report

A 60-year-old Japanese woman, who had been treated conservatively for CKD with an unknown primary disease accompanied by hypokalemia (baseline serum creatinine level of approximately 1.7 mg/dL, corresponding to CKD stage G4), complained of severe fatigue and visited our department. Prominent hypokalemia (2.2 mEq/L) was noted, and the patient was admitted for emergency care. She had a history of alcohol use disorder and bronchial asthma but did not have a history of metal allergy.

On examination, her vital signs were as follows: blood pressure, 100/64 mm Hg; pulse rate, 84/min, and blood temperature, 36.5 °C. Imaging tests showed that the bilateral kidneys were somewhat atrophic; the size was 3.5 inches, whereas renal cyst formation, which would suggest hypokalemic nephropathy, was not observed. The laboratory results are summarized in Table 1. Although she received ESA treatment periodically in the outpatient department, slight anemia with iron deficiency and hypozincemia was observed. Mineral replacement therapy consisting of potassium preparation, iron preparation, and ZAH was initiated.

Several days later, the patient developed a slight fever, AKI (serum creatinine, 3.33 mg/dL), and a mild increase in blood eosinophils (approximately 10%) (Table 1). At this time, urinary N-acetyl-b-glucosaminidase and β2-microglobulin, markers of renal tubular injury, were markedly elevated (45.2 IU/L and 42850 μg/L, respectively), although there was mild proteinuria; proteinuria level was 0.64 g/g Cr. These clinical findings suggested TIN, and prednisone (30 mg daily) was initiated. Furthermore, newly initiated drugs were all discontinued.

A percutaneous needle renal biopsy was performed on the same day as the start of the steroid treatment, and light microscopy revealed diffuse inflammatory cell infiltration in the tubulointerstitial area, accompanied by severe interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (Fig 1A). Of the 6 glomeruli present, 3 were globally sclerotic and the remaining 3 glomeruli showed only minor abnormalities (Fig 1B). Immunoperoxidase staining for CD68, CD3, and major basic protein (a marker of eosinophils) showed that the infiltrating cells in the tubulointerstitial area consisted of numerous macrophages and T lymphocytes, intermingled with a few eosinophils (Fig 1C). No granulomas were observed. Immunofluorescence staining for immunoglobulins and complements was negative in both the glomeruli and tubules (Fig 1D), and no electron-dense deposits were observed by electron microscopy (Fig 1E). Thus, acute TIN superimposed on severe interstitial fibrosis was pathologically diagnosed, although the primary cause of CKD could not be determined.

Because type IV hypersensitivity was suspected based on clinical and pathological findings, we performed a DLST against the newly administered drugs, namely, potassium preparation, iron preparation, and ZAH, which yielded positive results only for ZAH (1445%; reference range, 0%-180%). A patch test using a standard metal series yielded positive result for zinc chloride. Furthermore, DLST against other zinc-containing drugs was performed; the results were positive for zinc histidine hydrate (containing a higher dose of zinc, 3,391%) and a generic product of ZAH (1,634%), but negative for polaprezinc, which contains a lower dose of zinc. The patient’s hypersensitivity to zinc and drug-induced TIN were thus confirmed.

The patient’s renal function improved soon after discontinuation of ZAH. Although the steroid dose was gradually tapered and finally withdrawn, the patient maintained clinical improvement during the 1-year follow-up period (serum creatinine level of approximately 1.7 mg/dL). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a patient with a zinc allergy who showed biopsy-proven acute TIN accompanied by infiltration of numerous macrophages and T lymphocytes. Compared to the massive infiltration of these cells, the renal interstitial infiltration of eosinophils was little. However, interstitial eosinophil infiltrates and eosinophiluria are reportedly not the specific finding of drug-induced TIN.8 Such pathological findings and the clinical course of the patient, that is, the development of a slight fever and AKI that appeared several days after the start of ZAH, and the positive result of the patch test against zinc chloride and positive reaction of the DLST against zinc-containing drugs, strongly suggested type IV hypersensitivity to zinc.

The present case was followed up for CKD with an unknown primary disease accompanied by hypokalemia. Although genetic testing was not performed, we considered the cause of hypokalemia to be a shortage of intake, which was soon ameliorated by potassium supplementation (Fig S1). Hypokalemic nephropathy is known to cause chronic TIN, and renal tubular deposition of complement C3, which supposedly results from renal ammonia production-mediated complement activation, is a pathological characteristic finding.9 Multiple renal cyst formations, usually formed in the bilateral medulla, is a characteristic shown during image testing.10 However, both of these findings were absent in our patient. Nonetheless, it is possible that hypokalemia was an aggravating factor in the patient’s disease course. There is another possibility that the alcohol use disorder of the patient was related to CKD and chronic lesions on renal biopsy, such as extensive glomerulosclerosis and severe interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy, because alcohol consumption has reportedly been associated with CKD.11,12

Drug-induced TIN is the most common cause of acute TIN, accounting for approximately two-thirds of the cases, and its pathogenesis is considered to be delayed-type hypersensitivity.13, 14, 15 Antibiotics, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and proton pump inhibitors are the most common causative agents; however, any drug can cause TIN.13,14 Indeed, more than 250 drugs have been described as triggering agents.16 The present case suggests that ZAH could also be a causative drug for TIN.

Zinc is an essential micromineral, and its deficiency is known to cause various symptoms. On the contrary, there have been several cases of zinc allergy in which zinc-containing insulin injections were administered. These previously reported cases include both immediate-type and delayed-type hypersensitivity to insulin preparations.5, 6, 7 Although why some patients show allergic reactions to zinc, an essential micromineral, needs to be investigated in a future study, it should be noted that zinc supplementation could induce severe adverse effects, including TIN. Regarding renal injury caused by drug side effects, 2 types of mechanisms are assumed: direct toxicity of the drug occurs in a dose-dependent manner, and hypersensitivity reactions generally occur in a dose-independent manner. It is interesting, however, that the side effects of zinc supplementation in the present case seem to be caused mainly by a hypersensitivity mechanism but occur in a dose-dependent manner: DLST was positive for zinc histidine hydrate, containing 50 mg of zinc, and ZAH (both original and generic products), containing 25 mg of zinc, but was negative for polaprezinc, containing a lower dose of zinc (17 mg per tablet). A previous report showed that zinc concentrations were correlated with the severity of clinical symptoms,7 which was in accordance with the results of DLST in our patient, showing a zinc-dose-dependent positive reaction; however, it remains unclear whether the difference in the small amount of zinc affected the results of DLST. Because the sensitivity of the DLST is not high, which is reportedly 60%-70%,17 there remains a possibility of a false-negative DLST result against polaprezinc in our patient. Thus, we intend to avoid prescribing any zinc-containing drugs, including polaprezinc, to the patient. It should also be stressed that there is insufficient evidence to support routine zinc supplementation in patients with CKD. The safety and efficacy of zinc preparations in CKD population should be investigated in future studies.

In conclusion, we report a case of acute TIN caused by delayed-type hypersensitivity to zinc. Although it has become easier to diagnose and treat the shortage of various micronutrients, and zinc supplementation is now widely performed in routine clinical practice, the present case suggests that severe adverse effects, such as TIN, could occur in some patients.
