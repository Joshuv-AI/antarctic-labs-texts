Introduction

Diagnosis of cerebral meningioma in pregnancy is rare and a challenge for both obstetricians and neurosurgeons. Frequent headaches and seizures are mistakenly attributed to eclampsia, leading to delay or even misdiagnosis. Several cases of brain tumors diagnosed during pregnancy have been reported [1]. However, many epidemiological, pathogenic, and therapeutic questions remain unanswered. The role of pregnancy in brain tumor development and the interaction of brain tumors with pregnancy are of particular concern to the medical community [2]. The work has been reported concerning the SCARE 2020 criteria [3].

Presentation of case

A 37-year-old, primigravida, primiparous, low socioeconomic, housewife with a history of poorly monitored chronic hypertension on central-acting antihypertensive therapy (Methyldopa 1000 mg daily), was admitted to the maternity ward with prepartum eclampsia, in the context of a poorly monitored pregnancy estimated at 34 weeks' amenorrhea.

An emergency cesarean section for maternal rescue was performed under general anesthesia, The patient had a blood pressure of 150/90 mmHg with negative proteinuria, and she did not receive magnesium sulfate. The cesarean section gave birth to a healthy female newborn weighing 2200 g. The patient was then transferred to the intensive care unit for monitoring.

On the first postpartum day, symptoms were marked by the onset of incoercible vomiting with behavioral disturbances. There was no motor deficit, visual blur, or fever. On questioning the family, it emerged that the patient had been suffering from persistent vomiting and severe headaches for a month, which did not respond to paracetamol, but she had never consulted a health professional.

This symptomatology prompted a post-partum cranioencephalic CT scan, which revealed a well-limited right frontal extra-axial process with regular contours and a broad base of meningeal implantation, intensely and heterogeneously enhanced after injection of contrast medium, responsible for a mass effect on neighboring structures with discrete peri-lesional edema and sub-floral involvement, suggestive of a meningioma (Fig. 1).

The patient was put on symptomatic treatment (anti-oedematous and anti-epileptic drugs) and then transferred to the neurosurgery department for possible surgical management. Complete removal of the tumor was achieved, and the postoperative course was uncomplicated with no complications.

An anatomopathological study confirmed the diagnosis of a classic grade 1 meningothelial meningioma.

Discussion

The incidence of brain tumors during pregnancy is lower than that expected in non-pregnant women of childbearing age. Meningioma is the most common brain tumor (more than a third of all intracranial tumors), and is more common in women, with a female-to-male ratio of 3:2 [1]. Meningiomas rarely develop during pregnancy, with an estimated 5.6 cases per 100,000 pregnancies [2]. One of the reasons for the low incidence is the fact that the fertility window is around 15 to 45 years of age, during which time tumors in general are relatively uncommon. This is also true of meningiomas, whose incidence is highest between the ages of 60 and 70 [2,4].

The biological behavior of meningioma during pregnancy is different, and this is explained by the complex physiological changes and hormonal differences during pregnancy. The increased meningioma growth observed during pregnancy is probably the result of endocrine mechanisms, notably increased serum levels of progesterone, human placental lactogen (hPL), and prolactin (PRL). Vascular factors (vascular endothelial growth factor) also play a crucial role in the development of peri-tumoral cerebral edema [5].

Because of the variability in the anatomical location of tumors, it is not possible to establish a general rule regarding the clinical symptoms and biological behavior of meningiomas [2,6]. Occasionally, even small meningeal tumors can cause severe symptoms, while larger tumors may remain asymptomatic for a prolonged period. The tumor's compression of surrounding structures is at the root of the complaints and clinical manifestations observed. The most frequently encountered symptoms include headaches, dizziness, focal deficits, and seizures [6].

The base of the skull is a frequent site for meningiomas, and their accelerated growth during pregnancy can cause visual disturbances. Large meningiomas can cause a rapid rise in intracranial pressure. Compression of the optic nerve disrupts venous flow, resulting in blurring and edema of the optic disc. Rising intracranial pressure also results in symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, and confusion. Ultimately, deterioration can lead to a coma-like state as a result of transtentorial herniation and brainstem compression [6,7].

The harmful effect of the tumor on the fetus is not direct but manifests itself through its consequences, such as intracranial hypertension and epileptic seizures, which can be life-threatening due to placental hypoperfusion, the latter being responsible for in-utero growth retardation or oligohydramnios, in some cases requiring termination of pregnancy for fetal salvage. [1,6].

Because of these non-specific symptoms, differential diagnosis with other pregnancy complications may be difficult. The most common conditions with similar symptoms are eclampsia, hyperemesis gravidarum, and post-partum psychosis [1,7].

Cerebral CT is still the most commonly used examination for exploring these tumors, due to its availability and affordability. It is often sufficient to evoke the diagnosis and intervene. During pregnancy, MRI is the preferred choice for the positive diagnosis of cerebral meningioma [7].

The general principle of treatment is based on symptomatic and oncological therapy, which must be both rapid and personalized, depending on the characteristics of the intracranial process, the stage of pregnancy, and the general condition of the fetus and mother [1,8]. Methyl prednisolone-based corticosteroid therapy and antiepileptic treatment were instituted throughout the pregnancy and enabled the pregnancy to be carried to term [1,8,9]. While corticosteroid therapy has the advantage of promoting fetal lung maturation in addition to its antiedematous effect, antiepileptic drugs are associated with a high risk of fetal malformation. Some antiepileptic drugs are reported to have a lower or even zero teratogenic risk. The administration of mannitol is not recommended except in cases of life-threatening cerebral edema, as it significantly reduces uteroplacental blood flow [9].

Although some reports claim that precisely calculated whole-brain radiotherapy with particular emphasis on protection of the abdominal region is a low-risk and effective local therapy during pregnancy, focal tumor irradiation is currently the preferred option in rapidly growing malignant meningiomas [1].

Surgical resection remains the main curative treatment for meningiomas. If the tumor is unresectable, antiprogesterone (mifepristone) and chemotherapeutic drugs (hydroxyurea) may be considered. Urgent surgery is usually required for malignant meningiomas, classically untreatable hydrocephalus and progressively enlarging potentially fatal tumors. As brain surgery during pregnancy is extremely risky for both mother and child, the tendency is to defer surgery to the post-partum period as long as possible, or at least until the possibility of delivery of a viable fetus [1,10]. Advances in neurosurgery and anesthesia have made it possible to perform neurosurgical procedures during pregnancy without interrupting the pregnancy and with few adverse effects on the course of the procedure and the fetus. Continuous intraoperative monitoring and high-quality professional cooperation between brain surgeons, obstetricians, and anesthetists are unavoidable [1,11].

About the mode of delivery, the elective cesarean section under general anesthesia is recommended to avoid any risk of aggravation of the potentially fatal intracranial hypertension [1].

Conclusion

Pregnancy is an aggravating factor in cerebral meningioma. On the other hand, cerebral meningiomas have no direct harmful effect on the course of pregnancy, nor the quality of the product of conception. The clinical picture remains classic, although pregnancy may delay the diagnosis of the brain tumor, and some signs may be wrongly attributed to the gravid state. Nevertheless, the diagnostic approach should not be altered. Management is multidisciplinary, involving anesthetists, obstetricians, neurosurgeons, and pediatricians.
