A Wild Emmer Wheat B3 Transcription Factor Regulates Seed Growth and Nutrient Allocation
Authors: Davinder Sharma, Zechariah Haber, Manas R Prusty, Raz Avni, Mijal Perez, Moshe Ronen, Rakesh Kumar, Arava Shatil‐Cohen, Paz Haboub, Chen Omer, Gilor Kelly, Hadar Aviv, Adi Doron‐Faigenboim, Din Moshe, Weronika Jasinska, Leah Rosental, Eitan Millet, Hanan Sela, Or Sperling, Amir Sharon, Anna Mintz Dub, Yariv Brotman, Nir Sade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeed weight (SW) and nutrient allocation are key determinants of yield and grain quality in wheat, yet the regulatory basis of naturally occurring variation in these traits remains poorly resolved. Wild emmer wheat (Triticum dicoccoides), the progenitor of modern wheat, retains extensive eco‐geographically structured genetic diversity that was largely eroded during domestication. Here, we identify a B3‐domain transcription factor (B3TF) as a key regulator of seed growth and metabolic partitioning in wheat. Genome‐wide association analysis of ~460 wild emmer accessions reveals a major locus on chromosome 2BL associated with SW, seed area and nitrogen (N) content, displaying pronounced climatic differentiation across environmental gradients. Introgression of the 2BL wild segment into the hexaploid wheat cultivars Chinese Spring and Bethlehem increases SW in cultivated backgrounds. Independent loss‐of‐function alleles generated by EMS mutagenesis in the tetraploid wheat cultivar Kronos produce larger seeds. Further, RNA‐seq of EMS mutants revealed metabolic reprogramming with upregulated fatty acid, nitrogen and phenylpropanoid pathways and downregulated carbohydrate metabolism and sugar transport. Metabolomic, lipidomic and ICP‐MS data showed increased essential amino acids, sugars, lipids, N content and minerals (Zn, Fe, Mo). Furthermore, CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated editing in the hexaploid wheat cultivar Fielder produced similar increases in SW and N content as observed in the EMS mutants, establishing this gene as a negative regulator of seed growth across ploidy levels. In addition, natural haplotypes show reciprocal climatic distributions, linking regulatory variation to environmental adaptation. Our findings uncover a TF underlying natural seed trait variation in wild wheat, providing a framework for exploiting regulatory alleles to enhance yield and nutritional quality in modern wheat.Keywords: B3 transcription factor, nutrient allocation, seed growth, seed size, wild emmer wheat (Triticum dicoccoides)

Introduction

Seed weight (SW) and nutrient allocation are major determinants of grain yield, nutritional quality and environmental resilience in wheat (Avni et al. 2017). In modern durum wheat (Triticum turgidum ssp. durum) and bread wheat (T. aestivum L.), prolonged directional selection has favoured uniform grain morphology, a process that has simultaneously narrowed allelic diversity at key developmental loci (Luo et al. 2007; Nevo 2014). As a consequence, domesticated germplasm represents only a limited subset of the regulatory variation governing seed growth and carbon and nitrogen (N) partitioning. In contrast, wild emmer wheat (WEW; T. dicoccoides), the wild progenitor of modern tetraploid and hexaploid wheat, retains extensive genetic and phenotypic diversity shaped by long‐term natural selection across steep ecological gradients in the Fertile Crescent (Avni et al. 2017; Fadida‐Myers et al. 2022; Nevo 2014). WEW has proven to be a rich resource for gene discovery, exemplified by the cloning of the Gpc‐B1/NAM‐B1 locus, which increases seed protein, zinc (Zn) and iron (Fe) content (Uauy et al. 2006). This diversity encompasses substantial variation in seed morphology, nutrient composition and reproductive strategies, such as positional seed dormancy within the spikelet.

These traits are adaptive in nature and are associated with differences in SW and colour (Nave et al. 2016; Avni et al. 2017). Despite their importance for understanding wheat evolution and adaptive strategies, the molecular basis of SW variation in wild wheat populations and their relationship to local environmental differentiation remain poorly understood. Genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) in wild populations provide a powerful framework to address this gap, as wild panels capture deeper allelic variation, broader recombination histories and pronounced environmental structuring compared with elite germplasm (Avni et al. 2017; Luo et al. 2007; Prusty et al. 2021). While previous studies have identified quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with SW and dormancy, causal genes and their regulatory functions have rarely been resolved, particularly for transcription factors (TFs).

Plant‐specific B3‐domain TFs (B3TFs) are key regulators of seed development, functioning as core components of the LAFL network (LEAFY COTYLEDON 1 (LEC1), ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE 3 (ABI3), Fusca 3 (FUS3) and LEC2) to control seed‐specific gene expression, nutrient accumulation and developmental transitions that determine seed traits (Abraham et al. 2016; Carbonero et al. 2017; Fatihi et al. 2016; Lepiniec et al. 2018; McCarty et al. 1991; Moreno‐Risueno et al. 2008; To et al. 2006; Tsukagoshi et al. 2005). FUS3 and LEC2 directly bind seed storage protein gene promoters, while ABI3 acts as a transcriptional cofactor (Kagaya et al. 2005); LEC2 promotes oil accumulation and upregulates FUS3 (Ahmad et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2015; Santos Mendoza et al. 2005; Stone et al. 2008), whereas FUS3 represses ethylene signalling to regulate developmental transitions (Lumba et al. 2012). In Arabidopsis, RAV1 (B3/AP2 domain TF) negatively regulates SW by repressing MINISEED3 (MINI3), a WRKY TF and HAIKU2 (IKU2), a leucine‐rich repeat (LRR) kinase (Shin and Nam 2018), while Maternal Effect Embryo Arrest 45 (MEE45) controls cell proliferation in maternal integuments (Li et al. 2021). In maize, ZmAFL4 (LEC2 ortholog) regulates carbon metabolism and starch deposition during endosperm development (Grimault et al. 2015) and ZmABI19 modulates endosperm regulators including ZmbZIP22, NAC130, Opaque2 and Opaque11 (Yang et al. 2021). In sorghum, members of the B3TF SbLAV1 were identified as a regulator of starch biosynthesis in developing grains (Gong et al. 2025). Conserved B3TF function is also supported by tomato FUS3 overexpression, reducing SW via altered cell division and expansion. (Grant‐Grant et al. 2022). Beyond storage regulation, B3TFs contribute to Fe homoeostasis, with altered Fe distribution observed in abi3, lec2 and fus3 mutants (Ahmad et al. 2022).

However, the contribution of natural allelic variation in B3TFs to seed trait diversity and eco‐geographic differentiation in wheat remains largely unexplored. Here, we assembled an ecologically structured panel of approximately 460 WEW accessions spanning the major climatic zones of Israel (Tene et al. 2022) and quantified seed traits. GWAS using SW as the target trait identified significant loci on chromosome arm 2BL, along with additional loci across other chromosomes. A key SNP, S5_683245061, defined haplotypes associated with SW and exhibited pronounced differentiation across environmental gradients.

To dissect the genetic control of this locus, we used a combination of genetic and molecular approaches for SW and nutrient content in wheat. First, chromosome‐arm substitution lines (CASLs) in bread wheat confirmed the contribution of this locus to seed traits, while transcriptomic profiling of a TILLING‐derived EMS mutant in durum wheat identified a B3TF that we named B3‐Seed Growth (B3‐SG) as the candidate regulator. Metabolomic and lipidomic analyses further linked this gene to carbon‐N partitioning and seed composition. Independent validation using CRISPR/Cas9 knockout (KO) lines in bread wheat showed that loss of function consistently increased SW across genetic backgrounds, establishing this locus as a conserved negative regulator of seed growth. These findings highlight the value of wild germplasm in uncovering regulatory variation masked during domestication (Avni et al. 2017; Nevo 2014) and establish B3‐SG as a promising target for improving seed quality, yield stability and climate adaptation in modern wheat.

Materials and Methods

Wild Emmer Wheat Collection

A diversity panel of approximately 460 WEW accessions was collected from ecologically diverse regions across Israel (Figure 1a; Supporting Information S1: Figure 1e). The collection is maintained at the Harold and Adele Lieberman Germplasm Bank, Institute for Cereal Crop Research (ICCR), Tel Aviv University (TAU). Plants were grown at Aaronson Farm, Haifa (32.7106° N, 34.9628° E), during 2019–2020 cropping season (November–May) under drip irrigation in loam soil (40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay). For haplotype validation experiments (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1a–d), 38 representative accessions from both SNP S5_683245061 haplotype groups were grown in an open field mini‐plot at TAU (32.1117° N, 34.8013° E) during the 2021–2022 cropping season (November–May) under drip irrigation in Hamra soil (90% sand and 10% clay). Seeds were harvested from all spikes of each accession. Dormant (D) and non‐dormant (ND) seeds were separated based on spikelet position and consistent differences in SW and colour and used for subsequent analyses.

Genome‐Wide Association Study

GWAS was performed on the WEW accessions following the framework as described previously (Tene et al. 2022). Mean SW values per accession were used as the quantitative trait (Data S1). Genomic positions of significant SNPs were determined using the Zavitan WEWSeq v1.0 reference genome (2017) accessed through the GrainGenes genome browser (Data S2; https://graingenes.org/GG3/Zavitan_info). Association analysis was conducted using the BLINK model implemented in GAPIT (Data S3 and S4). Candidate genes within associated genomic intervals were retrieved using BioMart in Ensembl Plants (https://plants.ensembl.org/biomart). Gene annotations included genomic coordinates and homology information across plant species.

Chromosome Arm Substitution Lines

Two sets of CASLs were generated by replacing the long arm of chromosome 2B in the wheat cultivar Chinese Spring (CS) and Bethlehem (BL) with the corresponding arm from the WEW accession TTD140, as described previously (Millet et al. 2013; Yu et al. 2020). TTD140 carries the haplotype 1 allele at the S5_683245061 locus on chromosome 2B. CASLs and their recurrent parents were grown in 5‐L pots under semi‐controlled greenhouse conditions with drip irrigation. Seeds from the first and second spikes were harvested for phenotypic analysis.

Ethyl Methanesulfonate Line

The EMS line Kronos3240, derived from the EMS‐mutagenized TILLING population of tetraploid durum wheat cv. Kronos was obtained from the John Innes Centre (https://plants.ensembl.org/Triticum_aestivum/Variation/Mappings?db=core;g=TraesCS2B02G487800;r=2B:683758420−683760224;t=TraesCS2B02G487800.1;v=Kronos3240.chr2B.683759518;vdb=variation;vf=31138118). The original seed stock was propagated for another generation and verified for homozygosity of the mutation (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2). EMS line and the recurrent parent Kronos were grown in 5‐L pots under semi‐controlled greenhouse conditions with drip irrigation. Seeds from the first and second spikes were harvested for phenotypic analysis.

RNA Extraction, Sequencing and Differential Expression Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from developing spikes at 7 days post‐anthesis (DPA) using the dodecyl sulphate and guanidine thiocyanate (DSGT) method (Sharma et al. 2019, 2024). RNA quality and quantity were assessed using Qubit fluorometry and TapeStation analysis. RNA‐seq libraries were prepared using a full‐length mRNA library preparation protocol at the Crown Genomics Institute of the Nancy and Stephen Grand Israel National Center for Personalized Medicine (G‐INCPM), Weizmann Institute of Science and sequenced as single‐end reads on an Illumina NextSeq platform, generating approximately 20 million reads per sample. Raw reads were processed using cutadapt to remove adapters and poly(A/T) sequences, and reads shorter than 30 bp were discarded. Differential gene expression (DGE) analysis was performed using DESeq. 2, with betaPrior, cooksCutoff and independentFiltering set to FALSE. p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction. All analyses were executed using a reproducible Snakemake‐based workflow. Gene annotation was performed using T. turgidum cv. Svevo reference genome, from Ensembl Plants (https://plants.ensembl.org/Triticum_turgidum/Info/Index).

CRISPR/Cas9 Construct Assembly and Wheat Transformation

A single guide RNA (sgRNA; 5′‐GGCTAGGCGAGTGCCGAGGC‐3′) specifically targeting Exon 1 of TaB3‐SG (TraesFLD2B01G526400; CS homologue TraesCS2B02G487800) was designed using the WheatCRISPR tool (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3). The selected sgRNA was synthesized and ligated into PaqCI‐digested pJD633 vector (Addgene #160393). The resulting construct was verified by colony PCR and full‐plasmid sequencing and introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain AGL‐1. Wheat (cv. Fielder) transformation was performed following standard protocol (Hayta et al. 2021; Prusty et al. 2025). Immature embryos (18–22 DPA) were isolated, inoculated with A. tumefaciens in wheat inoculation medium, centrifuged and co‐cultivated for 2 days in the dark. Embryos were sequentially transferred through resting, selection, regeneration and rooting media. Regenerated plantlets were acclimatized and grown to maturity under controlled greenhouse conditions (21 ± 1°C, 16‐h photoperiod). Two independent lines were verified for intended mutation and homozygosity (Supporting Information S1: Figure 4) and confirmed to be transgene‐free. The CRISPR/Cas KO lines and the recurrent parent Fielder were grown in 5‐L pots under semi‐controlled greenhouse conditions with drip irrigation. Seeds from the first and second spikes were harvested for phenotypic analysis.

Seed Traits Phenotyping and Metabolite Analysis

SW and SA were quantified across the WEW diversity panel and additional genetic materials. For WEW, these traits were measured from a representative set of 30 fully mature seeds per accession. SA was determined from high‐resolution digital images using ImageJ software (Schneider et al. 2012) with consistent threshold and particle analysis parameters. For CASLs, EMS mutants and CRISPR/Cas KO lines, all seeds harvested from individual spikes were used for SW and SA measurements. Ground seed samples were extracted with methyl tert‐butyl ether (MTBE) and methanol:water (1:3, v/v), producing an organic phase for lipidomics, while the polar metabolites were analysed using a GC‐MS platform (Lisec et al. 2006). Data processing was performed using Xcalibur Software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Seed N content was quantified using an elemental analyser (FlashSmart Thermo Fisher) or by near‐infrared (NIR) spectrophotometry (Tan et al. 2022). For seed micro‐elements analysis, 200 mg of seed powder was digested in 65% nitric acid and 30% hydrogen peroxide, heated to 210°C, diluted to 50 mL and analysed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS) following standard protocol.

Results

Positional Dormancy Is Linked to Seed Weight and Genotype‐Specific Variation

In WEW, positional dormancy within spikelets represents an adaptive strategy shaped by environmental heterogeneity (Cook 1913). D seeds are smaller and darker, whereas ND seeds are larger and brighter, indicating coordinated regulation of dormancy and early seed development. Consistent with earlier studies, these traits suggest a shared genetic basis linking SS and dormancy (Nave et al. 2016).

Across the WEW population, SW distributions formed two distinct normal distributions corresponding to D and ND seeds. D seeds were significantly lighter (mean = 0.025 g) than ND seeds (mean = 0.035 g; Figure 1a), confirming an inverse relationship between dormancy and SS (Nave et al. 2016). GWAS conducted separately on D and ND seed sets identified multiple loci exceeding the genome‐wide significance threshold (LOD > 7.0) on Chromosomes 2A, 2B, 3B and 7B (Figure 1b; Supporting Information S1: Figures 5a and 6a). Two SNPs, S5_683245061 (Chr 2B) and S4_43269369 (Chr 2A), showed reproducible associations with SW across both seed types (Figure 1b; Supporting Information S1: Figures 5a and 6a). Haplotype analysis revealed that these loci also influenced SA and N content. For S5_683245061 (C/T), Haplotype 1 (‘C’) was consistently associated with higher SW, larger SA and increased N content relative to Haplotype 2 (‘T’) (Figure 1d–f and Supporting Information S1: Figure 5c–e). Similarly, at S4_43269369 (G/A), Haplotype 2 (‘A’) conferred more favourable seed traits than Haplotype 1 (‘G’) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 6b–g). Allele frequency distributions and permutation analyses confirmed that these associations were not driven by population structure. Moreover, 438 lines carried the ‘C’ allele and 27 the ‘T’ allele at S5_683245061, whereas 422 lines carried the ‘G’ allele and 41 the ‘A’ allele at S4_43269369. Permutation analysis excluding Haplotype 2 lines resulted in the loss of corresponding GWAS signals (Supporting Information S1: Figure 7b,e,h,k), whereas random exclusion of an equivalent number of lines had no effect (Supporting Information S1: Figure 7c,f,i,l). These results support the contribution of specific allelic variants to seed trait variation, independent of seed position within the spikelet. Independent field‐based validation using 38 WEW accessions confirmed the robustness of the S5_683245061 effect. Haplotype 1 (n = 17) lines exhibited significantly greater SW and SA than Haplotype 2 lines (n = 21) in both D and ND seeds. In ND seeds, Haplotype 1 lines showed significantly higher SW (p = 6.4 × 10−8; Supporting Information S1: Figure 1a) and larger SA (p = 1.3 × 10−5; Supporting Information S1: Figure 1b) compared to Haplotype 2. These differences were maintained in D seeds, with Haplotype 1 exhibiting greater SW (p = 1.1 × 10−7; Supporting Information S1: Figure 1c) and SA (p = 3.4 × 10−4; Supporting Information S1: Figure 1d). The consistency of these effects across positional seed classes establishes S5_683245061 haplotype 1 as a stable genetic determinant of increased SW.

Chromosome 2BL Substitution Confirm the Contribution of the S5_683245061 Locus to Seed Traits

The SNP S5_683245061 maps to the long arm of chromosome 2B in T. dicoccoides. To assess the phenotypic contribution of this region, we analysed two sets of CASLs in which the 2BL arm from the WEW accession TTD140 (haplotype 1 at S5_683245061) was introgressed into the hexaploid wheat backgrounds CS and BL (BL676). In both genetic backgrounds, CASLs showed significantly higher SW (CS: p = 8.6 × 10−4; BL: p = 5.8 × 10−3 Figure 1g,i) and SA (CS: p = 0.02; BL: p = 7 × 10−5; Figure 1h,j) relative to their respective recurrent parents. These results confirm that the 2BL region harbouring S5_683245061 promotes increased SW.

TdB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190) Encodes a Seed‐Expressed B3TF at the 2BL Seed Trait Locus

To identify candidate genes underlying the association at SNP S5_683245061, we integrated genomic position with RNA‐seq expression data from WEW (Avni et al. 2017; Data [Link], [Link], [Link]). A confidence interval of ±1 Mb around the lead SNP was defined. All genes within this interval were retrieved based on the T. dicoccoides genome annotation. Within this confidence region, a total of six genes were identified (Data S7). Among these, two genes, TdB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190) and TRIDC2BG071150, showed expression in both flower tissues and developing seed based on expression datasets (Data [Link], [Link], [Link]). Functional annotation further indicated that TdB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190) encodes a predicted B3TF containing a conserved B3 DNA‐binding domain (amino acids [AAs] 233–338; Figure 2a), characteristic of TFs involved in seed development across multiple plant species, whereas the other expressed gene is of unknown function and is expressed in root, leaf, flower and developing seed (Figure 2b; Data S7). Additionally, enriched expression in reproductive tissue was shown in the TaB3‐SG (TraesCS2B02G487800; homologue of bread wheat ‘Azhurnaya’ and ‘CS’), TdurB3‐SG (TRITD2Bv1G223390; homologue of durum wheat ‘Svevo’), TdB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190; homologue of WEW ‘Zavitan’ (Data S8; Maccaferri et al. 2019; https://bar.utoronto.ca/efp_wheat/cgi-bin/efpWeb.cgi), further corroborating the B3TF reproductive tissue‐specific expression pattern across wheat species. Co‐expression analysis in T. dicoccoides ‘Zavitan’ showed strong correlations with MADS‐box TFs and genes involved in ubiquitination and transcriptional regulation, consistent with participation in a regulatory network controlling seed development and composition (Figure 2c; Data S9). Hence, TdB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190) was selected as the most likely candidate gene underlying the observed association. The second major SNP, S4_43269369, mapped within the confidence interval in close proximity to TRIDC2AG009610, which is annotated as an AMP deaminase and has been previously implicated in early embryo development (Xu et al. 2005). Functional characterization of this locus will be addressed in future studies.

To investigate natural variation at the TdB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190), we used NGS to sequence the full coding region and 470 bp of promoter in 21 representative accessions from both haplotypes. A single conserved SNP was detected in Exon 3 at Position 343 (Figure 2d). This G‐to‐A base substitution in Haplotype 2 results in an alanine‐to‐threonine (A‐to‐T) AA change outside the conserved B3 domain, suggesting a potential effect on protein function rather than gene regulation. qPCR analysis of developing seeds at seven DPA detected no significant difference between haplotypes (Figure 2e), indicating natural phenotypic variation is unlikely to arise from transcriptional regulation. Thus, this coding polymorphism represents a candidate functional variant underlying the seed trait association.

Functional Validation of TdurB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190) Using a Tetraploid EMS Mutant

To functionally validate TdurB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190), we analysed the tetraploid EMS‐mutant line Kronos3240, which carries a premature stop codon in the tetraploid durum wheat homologue TdurB3‐SG (TRITD2Bv1G223390) (Figure 3a; Supporting Information S1: Figure 2). Compared with wild‐type Kronos, the homozygous EMS mutants significantly increased SA (p = 1.2 × 10−15; Figure 3b) and SW (p = 1.5 × 10−11; Figure 3c).

RNA‐seq of developing spikelets at seven DPA revealed extensive transcriptomic reprogramming. Principal component analysis (PCA) clearly separated mutant and wild type samples (Figure 3e). Differential expression analysis identified 1386 upregulated and 1188 downregulated genes (p < 0.05; fold change > 2) (Figure 3g,h; Data S10). Functional enrichment analyses showed upregulation of pathways related to fatty acid metabolism, N metabolism and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, whereas genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism, transcriptional regulation and sugar transport were predominantly downregulated (Figure 3g,h; Data S11). These results indicate that loss of B3TF activity broadly reprograms metabolic and developmental pathways during grain filling.

Consistent with these transcriptomic shifts, metabolomic profiling revealed elevated levels of essential AAs (lysine, threonine, methionine) and simple sugars (glucose, arabinose) alongside reduced TCA cycle intermediates (malate, succinate, fumarate and aconitate) in mutant seeds (Figure 4a; Data S12). Lipidomic analysis showed increased diacylglycerols (+26%) and fatty acids (+13%), with concomitant reduction in phosphatidylethanolamine and digalactosyldiacylglycerol (Figure 4b). Elemental analysis (ICP‐MS) further revealed higher accumulation of Zn, Fe and molybdenum (Mo) in mutant seeds relative to the wild type (Figure 4c–e; Data S13). Together, these findings support that TdurB3‐SG (TRITD2Bv1G223390) appears to influence SW, metabolic partitioning and micronutrient accumulation during wheat seed development.

CRISPR Disruption of TaB3‐SG (TraesFLD2B01G526400) Alters Seed Traits in T. aestivum cv. Fielder

To investigate the functional role of the hexaploid wheat homologue TaB3‐SG (TraesFLD2B01G526400), we analysed two independent CRISPR/Cas KO lines in T. aestivum cv. Fielder carrying early premature stop codons (Figure 5a; Supporting Information S1: Figure 4). Both KO lines produced significantly larger and heavier seeds than the wild type control, indicating that TaB3‐SG (TraesFLD2B01G526400) functions as a negative regulator of seed growth in bread wheat (Figure 5b,c). Furthermore, an increase in N content was observed in the KO line (Supporting Information S1: Figure 8), indicating an effect on seed quality and consistent with the EMS and haplotype analysis of seed quality.

Geographical and Climatic Differentiation of T. dicoccoides Haplotypes

Analysis of haplotypes at the S5_683245061 locus revealed clear geographic and climatic structuring. Haplotype 1 exhibited a broad distribution across multiple ecological zones, whereas Haplotype 2 was confined to a narrow geographic range (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1e). Accessions carrying Haplotype 1 were associated with significantly higher mean annual temperatures (p = 3.4 × 10−7), lower annual precipitation (p = 4.6 × 10−5) and lower elevations (p = 4.1 × 10−7) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1f–h). In contrast, Haplotype 2 predominated in cooler, wetter and higher‐altitude environments. This reciprocal environmental partitioning supports a role for local climatic adaptation in shaping haplotype distribution.

Discussion

Transcriptional regulation is central to coordinating seed growth with metabolic allocation during wheat seed development and natural variation in SW reflects the combined effects of multiple loci and genetic background interactions. B3TFs are established regulators of developmental transitions and resource partitioning in plants (Carbonero et al. 2017; Fatihi et al. 2016; Suzuki and McCarty 2008). Our identification of TdurB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190) extends this role to wheat seed development, with Haplotype 1 consistently associated with increased SW and SA.

Metabolomic profiling of EMS mutants revealed elevated essential AAs and simple sugars, reduced TCA intermediates and broad reprogramming of lipid and secondary metabolism, indicative of altered carbon‐N partitioning during seed filling. Strong co‐expression of TdurB3‐SG (TRITD2Bv1G223390) with multiple MADS‐box TFs places it within broader regulatory networks governing seed development and maturation. Together, these results support a model in which TdurB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190) appears to influence transcriptional, signalling and metabolic pathways to coordinate seed growth and nutrient allocation.

Background mutations may contribute to the overall phenotype observed in the EMS lines. However, evidence from the CRISPR lines indicates that the phenotype is primarily driven by the B‐genome mutation. Biochemical analysis of CRISPR line, which exhibited a strong phenotype, showed consistent positive effects on SW, SA (Figure 5) and grain quality traits such as N content (Supporting Information S1: Figure 8), in agreement with both the EMS lines (Figure 3) and the haplotype analysis (Figure 1). The similar phenotypic outcomes observed in EMS and CRISPR/Cas KO lines support the conclusion that modification of the B genome alone is sufficient to produce the phenotype. As the analysis focused on the B genome, genome‐edited lines for the A and D homeologs are not currently available. It remains possible that simultaneous perturbation of all three genomes could result in a stronger phenotype, consistent with additive effects among homeologs.

Haplotype analysis of TdB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190) revealed no differences in transcript abundance but identified coding variation predicted to alter the protein sequence associated with seed traits and geographic distribution. The more prevalent Haplotype 1 was associated with increased SW compared to Haplotype 2, which carries a nonsynonymous G‐to‐A at exon three, causing an A‐to‐T AA change outside the conserved B3 domain. Although DNA‐binding capacity is likely preserved, the introduction of a potential phosphorylation site may alter regulatory activity through post‐translational modulation. Similar functionally important AA substitutions outside the B3 domain have been reported in other B3TFs, including BnFUS3 of Brassica napus, where a single residue change produced loss‐of‐function phenotypes and reduced seed oil accumulation (Elahi et al. 2015; Moreno‐Risueno et al. 2008). Interestingly, analysis of the nonsynonymous G‐to‐A allele in the hexaploid wheat pangenome accessions (https://graingenes.org/blast/) revealed specific allele distribution (Data S14) that may be used for future breeding purposes. Further biochemical and allele‐specific analyses will be required to define the precise molecular impact of this variant.

The geographic distribution of WEW TdB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190) haplotypes suggests adaptive differentiation, with Haplotype 1 being enriched in warmer and drier environments, consistent with a potential advantage under heat and water‐limited conditions. These findings highlight the importance of natural allelic diversity at WEW TdB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190) and position this gene as a promising target for breeding strategies aimed at improving seed nutrient composition and yield stability across diverse agro‐climatic regions. In addition, the observed inter spikelet variation between D and ND seeds provides a genetic framework for future studies dissecting the regulation of seed dormancy in wheat.

Collectively, our results provide convergent genetic, biochemical and molecular evidence that B3‐SG is a key regulator of wheat seed development. Independent analyses across wild emmer GWAS, CASLs, EMS mutants and CRISPR/Cas KO lines consistently demonstrate that loss of B3TF function increases SW and SA. These phenotypic effects are accompanied by coordinated changes in AA metabolism, lipid composition, soluble sugars, micronutrients and N content, indicating broad reprogramming of carbon‐N partitioning. Integration of RNA‐seq data further suggests a regulation framework in which TdB3‐SG (TRIDC2BG071190) regulates genes involved in cell expansion, water transport and stress‐responsive signalling.

Although modern breeding has focused primarily on yield optimization, our findings highlight WEW as a valuable reservoir of regulatory variation for improving SS and nutritional composition. These results demonstrate both the promise and complexity of exploiting wild regulatory alleles to improve seed quality and yield in modern wheat.
