Alternative Surveillance Improves Outcomes by Detecting Early-Stage Hepatocellular Carcinoma in Chronic Hepatitis B Patients
Authors: Dong Ho Lee, Heejin Cho, Su Jong Yu, Min Kyung Park, Yun Bin Lee, Eun Ju Cho, Jeong-Hoon Lee, Yoon Jun Kim, Jung-Hwan Yoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsCurrent guidelines recommend biannual ultrasound for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) surveillance in chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients. However, computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may be used when ultrasound is inadequate. The clinical impact of these alternative modalities remains unclear.MethodsCHB patients undergoing regular HCC surveillance were classified into two groups ultrasound-only and alternative surveillance (CT/MRI). Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups using the Risk Estimation for HCC in CHB (REACH-B) score. Outcomes included 10-year overall survival (OS), HCC tumor size, Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage at diagnosis, and OS after HCC diagnosis. Propensity score matching was applied to balance baseline characteristics.ResultsA total of 2,024 patients were included after propensity score matching to ensure balanced baseline characteristics, with 1,012 patients in each group. OS was similar (ultrasound-only 96.0% vs alternative 96.8%; p=0.379). HCC occurred in 66 patients in each group. Alternative surveillance detected smaller HCC tumors (1.6 cm vs 2.1 cm; p<0.001) and more BCLC stage 0 cases (alternative 71.2% vs ultrasound-only 42.4%; p=0.003). The OS after HCC diagnosis was higher with alternative surveillance (83.0% vs 67.0%; p=0.025). In high-risk patients (n=970), alternative surveillance increased the OS (97.3% vs 93.6%; p=0.029) and the OS after HCC diagnosis (83.0% vs 60.6%; p=0.010). No significant differences were observed in low-risk patients.ConclusionsCT/MRI-based alternative surveillance led to earlier HCC detection and improved post-diagnosis survival, particularly in high-risk CHB patients, supporting its potential role as an alternative to ultrasound in selected populations.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Surveillance, Ultrasonography

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common malignant tumor and the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide.1 HCCs occur in the risk group, including liver cirrhosis and chronic hepatitis B (CHB), and international guidelines recommend surveillance of HCC by the biannual ultrasound (US) to improve overall survival (OS) through the early detection. Indeed, one randomized controlled trial proved that surveillance of HCC using biannual US and serum alpha fetoprotein (AFP) level reduced cancer-related mortality by 37% in CHB patients.2 However, the low sensitivity for detecting early-stage HCC is a major limitation of US-based surveillance. Previous studies reported that US had low sensitivity with 63% for detecting early stage and approximately 30% in very early-stage HCC.3-6

To overcome the limitation of US as HCC surveillance, recent updated international guidelines recommend computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as alternative surveillance for selected patients who had an inadequate US examination.7-10 Several studies using a Markov model reported that alternative surveillance using CT was more cost-effective than biannual US-based surveillance because of the higher sensitivity to detect early-stage HCC.11,12 Recent study by Lima et al.13 reported that CT for the surveillance and diagnosis of HCC and complete MRI for inadequate CT was the most cost-effective strategy in an optimal compliance scenario. Indeed, the effectiveness of imaging based surveillance for HCC vary according to the patients with different risk of developing HCC.14 However, most studies on imaging-based HCC surveillance relied on model-based estimations, such as the Markov model, which may not represent real patients. In addition, effectiveness of alternative surveillance using intermittent replacement of US with CT or MRI on clinical outcome including OS in a real-world practice has not been fully evaluated. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcome of alternative surveillance by CT or MRI for HCC compared to US in a retrospective CHB patient cohort, considering the risk of HCC development.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was designed as a single center retrospective cohort study and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB number: H-2208-023-1346) with the waiver of requirement of informed consent.

1. Selection of study population

We included adult patients (>19 years old) with CHB who underwent US for HCC surveillance from January to April 2011. For the eligibility, only patients undergoing regular HCC surveillance based on the current HCC management guideline were evaluated.9 The exclusion criteria were: (1) prior history of malignancy including HCC; (2) history of solid organ transplantation; (3) lack of complete laboratory test results; and (4) less than 2-year follow-up period. The enrolled patients were classified into two groups according to the imaging modalities for HCC surveillance: (1) US only group included patients who underwent HCC surveillance using only liver US and serum AFP level check with 3- to 6-month interval; and (2) alternative surveillance group which patients underwent at least one contrast-enhanced CT/MRI for HCC surveillance instead of scheduled US. The risk estimation for HCC in CHB (REACH-B) score was calculated for each patient to estimate the risk of HCC development, and score 8 was set as a cutoff value to categorize enrolled patients into low- and high-risk groups.15

2. Method of HCC surveillance

All included patients underwent HCC surveillance with laboratory tests and imaging studies including US, CT or MRI every 3 to 6 months. At our medical center, biannual liver US with serum AFP measurement is the standard for HCC surveillance, in accordance with current guidelines.7-10 All liver US examinations were performed or supervised by one of attending radiologists who had more than 5-year experience in liver imaging. In addition to US, contrast-enhanced CT or MRI were considered as alternative for scheduled US when US examination was inadequate because of poor sonic window. For contrast-enhanced CT, a 4-phase dynamic liver CT protocol was used, including precontrast, arterial phase, portal venous phase, and delayed phase imaging. For contrast-enhanced MRI, gadoxetic acid-enhanced liver MRI was utilized, incorporating precontrast T2-weighted imaging, dynamic sequences, hepatobiliary phase, and diffusion-weighted imaging. When contrast-enhanced CT/MRI was performed to evaluate suspicious nodules detected on US or an elevated AFP level regardless of US findings (i.e., when the intent was diagnostic rather than for surveillance), that CT or MRI exam was excluded from patient group classification. Surveillance imaging tools were chosen by discussion between attending physicians and radiologists in consideration of patient’s risk of HCC development and quality of US examination.16,17 The representative surveillance strategy for CHB patients is shown in Fig. 1. Surveillance imaging method and total number of surveillance imaging tests during the follow-up period were recorded and compared by the group. Surveillance adherence rate was also evaluated and compared between two patient groups. In addition, the quality of the baseline US examination was evaluated using the US Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System visualization score. The scores were categorized as follows: A (no or minimal limitations), B (moderate limitations that may obscure small masses), and C (severe limitations that significantly reduce the sensitivity for detecting focal liver lesions). A visualization score of B or C was classified as suboptimal visualization.16

3. Diagnosis of the HCC occurrence and outcome measurement

HCC was diagnosed by either histopathology or noninvasive imaging criteria. When surgery or liver biopsy was performed, the final diagnosis was made by histopathology. In case of noninvasive imaging diagnosis of HCC, the guidelines of both the European Association for the Study of the Liver and the Korean Liver Cancer Association-National Cancer Center were used.7,10 Diagnosed HCCs were also recorded with the size, number of HCCs, and Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage. It also included the detection method for HCC in both groups. The index date was set at the first US examination for HCC surveillance during the study period. The primary outcomes were OS in each group. The secondary outcomes included the size and BCLC stage of HCC, and OS after HCC diagnosis. OS was defined as the time interval between the date of index and the death from any cause. Similarly, OS after HCC diagnosis was defined as the time interval from the HCC diagnosis to the date of death from any cause.

4. Statistical analysis

All continuous variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. All categorical variables were presented as number with percentage and compared using the chi-square test. To reduce the potential biases originating from the difference in baseline characteristics between two patient groups and to set up the final study population, propensity score analysis was performed using a multivariate logistic regression model including the following baseline characteristics: age, sex, Child-Pugh score, presence of ascites, history of antiviral therapy for CHB, serum albumin level, total bilirubin level, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, platelet count, prothrombin activity, fibrosis-4 and score of Model for End-Stage Liver Disease. Thereafter, the propensity score model was utilized to create a one-to-one matched patient cohort by using the nearest neighbor matching method with the caliper width of 0.10.18,19 OS was estimated by using the Kaplan-Meier method, and compared with the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using a commercially available software program (SPSS version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

1. Patient selection

A total of 3,075 CHB patients eligible for this study underwent liver US for HCC surveillance between January and April 2011. After applying the exclusion criteria described in the Materials and Methods section, 566 patients were excluded, and 2,509 patients were initially included: 1,200 in the US only group and 1,309 in the alternative surveillance group. The baseline characteristics of the two groups are summarized in Table 1. Significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of sex, body mass index, Child-Pugh score, presence of ascites, history of antiviral therapy, albumin levels, total bilirubin levels, and platelet counts. Notably, the alternative surveillance group had significantly worse liver function compared to the US only group. Additionally, fibrosis-4 and Model for End-Stage Liver Disease scores were significantly higher in the alternative surveillance group. The rate of suboptimal visualization on US was also significantly higher in the alternative surveillance group than in the US only group. After propensity score analysis, a total of 2,024 patients were selected for the final study population, with 1,012 patients allocated to each group. Following propensity score matching, there were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups, except for body mass index and the rate of suboptimal visualization on US, both of which remained significantly higher in the alternative surveillance group compared to the US only group (Table 1). 2. Number of surveillance imaging tests and surveillance adherence

During the median follow-up period of 135 months (IQR, 127 to 137 months), the median number of surveillance imaging tests was 18.0 (IQR, 9.5 to 22.0; range, 4 to 24) in US only group, and 18.0 (IQR, 10 to 22.5; range, 4 to 24) in alternative surveillance group. There was no significant difference in median number of surveillance imaging tests between two groups (p=0.598). The surveillance adherence rate was 72.9% (738/1,012) in the US only group, and was not significantly different from that of 73.5% (744/1,012) in alternative surveillance group (p=0.763).

3. Overall survival

During the follow-up period, 42 of 1,012 patients in US only group died: 16 from progression of HCC; seven from malignancies other than HCC (3 lung cancer, 1 breast cancer, 1 cholangiocarcinoma, 1 leukemia, and 1 malignant melanoma); one from liver failure; one from heart failure; and 17 from unknown causes. Among the 1,012 patients in alternative surveillance group, 40 patients died: seven from progression of HCC; 11 from malignancy other than HCC (4 pancreatic cancer, 3 lung cancer, 1 cholangiocarcinoma, 1 stomach cancer, 1 ovarian cancer, and 1 urothelial cancer); five from liver failure; one from brain hemorrhage; one from interstitial lung disease; and 15 from unknown causes. The proportion of deaths attributed to HCC progression was significantly higher in the US only group compared to the alternative surveillance group (38.1% vs 17.5%, p=0.038). The estimated 1-, 5-, and 10-year OS in US only group was 100%, 98.6%, and 96.0%, respectively, and these were not significant differences from those of 100%, 99.6%, and 96.8%, respectively, in alternative surveillance group (p=0.379) (Fig. 3A).

4. Development of HCC and OS after diagnosis

During the follow-up period, HCC occurred in 66 patients in US only group, and this was diagnosed by histopathology (n=25) and noninvasive imaging criteria (n=41). The group had 0.6%, 3.8%, and 6.7% of the 1-, 5-, and 10-year cumulative incidence of HCC development, respectively. Additionally, HCC was found on surveillance US examination in 52 patients. In the remaining 14 patients, surveillance US did not show any suspicious lesion in the liver. However, these remained patients showed elevated levels of serum AFP, and thus, contrast-enhanced CT (n=13) or MRI (n=1) was performed. HCC was subsequently detected by CT or MRI. The treatment modalities for HCC in the US only group included liver transplantation (n=1), surgical resection (n=24), local ablation (n=15), and transarterial chemoembolization (n=26). The rate of curative intent treatment in this group was 60.6% (40/66).

In alternative surveillance group, all of 1,012 patients underwent at least one contrast-enhanced CT (median, 3; IQR, 1 to 6; range, 1 to 24) instead of scheduled US for HCC surveillance. Contrast-enhanced MRI was done in 222 patients (median, 1; IQR, 1 to 2; range, 1 to 10). In this group, HCC developed in 66 patients, diagnosed by histopathology (n=31) or noninvasive imaging criteria (n=35). HCC was found on surveillance US (n=14), CT (n=37) or MRI (n=15). The treatment modalities for HCC in the alternative surveillance group included liver transplantation (n=1), surgical resection (n=30), local ablation (n=19), and transarterial chemoembolization (n=16). The rate of curative intent treatment in this group was 75.8% (50/66). The 1-, 5-, and 10-year cumulative incidence of HCC development in alternative surveillance group were 0.6%, 3.1%, and 6.6%, respectively.

The median size of HCCs in US only group was 2.1 cm (IQR, 1.6 to 3.0 cm), which was significantly larger than the alternative surveillance group with 1.6 cm (IQR, 1.2 to 2.0 cm) (p<0.001) (Fig. 3B). The distribution of BCLC stage at the time of HCC diagnosis in US only group was: BCLC 0 (n=28, 42.4%), BCLC A (n=30, 45.5%), BCLC B (n=2, 3.0%), and BCLC C (n=6, 9.1%). The alternative surveillance group was: BCLC 0 (n=47, 71.2%), BCLC A (n=18, 27.3%), and BCLC B (n=1, 1.5%). This distribution was significantly different between groups (p=0.003) (Fig. 3C). Regarding the tumor location, there was no significant difference between two groups (p=0.200). The estimated 1-, 5-, and 10-year OS after HCC diagnosis in 66 patients of US only group was 95.1%, 70.6%, and 67.0%, respectively, which were significantly lower than 66 patients of alternative surveillance group with 98.4%, 90.0%, and 83.0%, respectively (p=0.025) (Fig. 3D).

5. Outcome according to the risk of HCC development

Among the 2,024 analyzed patients, 970 with a REACH-B score ≥8 were classified into the high-risk group. This group included 503 patients with US only surveillance and 467 patients with alternative surveillance. The estimated 1-, 5-, and 10-year OS in 503 patients of US only group was 100%, 97.6%, and 93.6%, respectively, and these were significantly higher in the alternative surveillance group with 467 patients by 100%, 99.6%, and 97.3%, respectively (p=0.029) (Fig. 4A). Among the 503 patients in US only group, HCC developed in 45 patients (8.9%), whereas 40 of 467 patients (8.6%) in the alternative-surveillance group developed HCC. The median tumor size was significantly smaller in 40 patients of alternative surveillance group than in 45 US only group (median 1.7 cm [IQR, 1.2 to 2.3 cm] vs median 2.1 cm [IQR, 1.8 to 2.9 cm]; p=0.008) (Fig. 4B). The distribution of BCLC stage at the time of HCC diagnosis was also significantly different between the groups (p=0.041) (Fig. 4C), and the rate of BCLC stage 0 was higher in alternative surveillance group than US only (62.5% [25/40] vs 35.6% [16/45]). The estimated 1-, 5-, and 10-year OS after HCC diagnosis was 94.9%, 60.6%, and 60.6% in 45 US only group patients, and which were significantly lower than alternative surveillance group with 40 patients by 97.3%, 93.7%, and 83.0% (p=0.010) (Fig. 4D).

Among the 2,024 enrolled patients, 1,054 patients with REACH-B score <8 were classified as low-risk group. This group included 509 patients with US only surveillance and 545 patients with alternative surveillance. In contrast to the high-risk group, there was no significant difference in OS between the group of US only and alternative surveillance (p=0.202) (Fig. 5A). HCC occurred in 21 of 509 patients in the US only group (4.1%) and in 26 of 545 patients in the alternative-surveillance group (4.8%). Although median size of HCC in alternative surveillance group with 26 patients was significantly smaller than US only group with 21 patients (1.5 cm [IQR, 1.2 to 1.9 cm] vs 2.0 cm [IQR, 1.5 to 3.0 cm]; p=0.018) (Fig. 5B), there was no significant difference in the distribution of BCLC stage (p=0.071) (Fig. 5C) and OS after HCC diagnosis (p=0.937) between groups (Fig. 5D).

DISCUSSION

In our study, the median size of HCC in alternative surveillance group was significantly smaller than US only group (1.6 cm vs 2.1 cm, p<0.001), and the proportion of BCLC stage 0 HCC was significantly higher in alternative surveillance group than in US only group (71.2% vs 42.4%, p=0.003). Additionally, the proportion of curative intent treatment was higher in the alternative surveillance group (75.8%) than in the US only group (60.6%), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (p=0.062). Regarding the cause of death, the proportion of deaths attributed to HCC progression was significantly higher in the US only group compared to the alternative surveillance group (38.1% vs 17.5%, p=0.038). Based on these results, we carefully suggest that alternative surveillance using CT/MRI as intermittent replacement of scheduled US can lead to the detection of earlier stage HCC with smaller size compared to US only surveillance. Furthermore, OS after HCC diagnosis was significantly higher in the alternative surveillance group than in the US only group (p=0.025), possibly due to the earlier detection of HCC, which increases the likelihood of curative treatment and may reduce mortality related to HCC progression. However, there was no significant difference in OS in the overall study population between the two groups (p=0.379). Since the first report by Zhang et al.2 demonstrated mortality reduction by using biannual US and serum AFP level check for HCC surveillance in CHB patients, the current international guidelines recommend HCC surveillance for risk population including CHB patients to improve the prognosis.7-10 Among the various imaging modalities, liver US at 6-month intervals has been the standard method for HCC surveillance because it is easy to perform, low in cost, and requires neither contrast injection nor radiation exposure. However, there have been several important limitations in liver US for HCC surveillance including subjective nature, operator dependency, and limited accuracy for early-stage HCC. Recent prospective studies assessing the diagnostic capability of imaging studies for HCC surveillance in high-risk population reported that the sensitivity of biannual US was only ranged from 27.9% to 29.2% for all stage of HCC, and from 17.7% to 27.3% for BCLC 0 stage HCC.4,6 Given that, biannual US might be suboptimal for HCC surveillance, especially in high-risk population who potentially have more advanced stage of liver cirrhosis. In this regard, surveillance strategies including other imaging modalities such as contrast-enhanced CT/MRI as alternative to US have been suggested, and a number of recent updated guidelines for HCC management adopt the alternative surveillance using CT/MRI for selected patients with inadequate quality of liver US.7-10 In our study, more than half of the patients in the initial cohort underwent alternative surveillance using contrast-enhanced CT or MRI, reflecting current clinical practice. Before propensity score matching, the alternative surveillance group had significantly worse liver function and higher fibrosis-4 and Model for End-Stage Liver Disease scores compared to the US only group. After propensity score matching to balance baseline characteristics, our study showed that alternative surveillance by using CT/MRI as intermittent replacement for US enabled the detection of HCC in earlier stage with smaller size than US only with serum AFP based surveillance, potentially supporting the current practice guidelines.

Since the effectiveness of HCC surveillance based on imaging studies would be varied among the patients with different risk of HCC occurrence as reported by Goossens et al.,14 we performed additional subgroup analyses in the patient groups based on the estimated risk of HCC development calculated by REACH-B score. Similar to the results of total study population, alternative surveillance lead to the detection of HCC in earlier stage with smaller size than US only surveillance in high-risk group with REACH-B score ≥8. In addition, high-risk patients showed significantly higher OS and post OS after HCC diagnosis in alternative surveillance group than US only group (p=0.029 for OS and p=0.010 for OS after HCC diagnosis, respectively). However, the low-risk patients with REACH-B score <8, did not have a significant difference in both OS (p=0.202) and post OS after HCC diagnosis (p=0.937) between US only and alternative surveillance group. Compared to US, a key advantage of CT/MRI is its higher sensitivity in detecting small HCCs measuring 2 cm or less.4,6 Given that the likelihood of HCC development was significantly higher in the high-risk group (REACH-B score ≥8) than in the low-risk group, the benefit of alternative surveillance utilizing CT/MRI in detecting small HCCs may be more pronounced in high-risk patients. This finding may help explain the results of our study. Given that, we would cautiously conclude that alternative surveillance using intermittent replacement of US with CT/MRI would be more beneficial for patients with high risk of HCC development with REACH-B score ≥8.

Our study had several limitations. First, due to the retrospective study design, there would be a risk for selection bias. To reduce the potential bias from the difference in baseline characteristics between two groups, we used propensity score analysis for the selection of final study population and no significant difference between two groups after propensity score analysis were observed. In addition, there was no significant difference in median number of surveillance imaging tests between two groups, and the surveillance adherence rate was also quite similar, indicating that there was no significant difference in the intensity of surveillance between two groups. Adding to the selection bias, lead-time bias could not be entirely eliminated due to the retrospective nature of the study. Second, the included patients with CHB represent one of the most important etiologies of liver disease. Thus, our results need to be validated in other population including various etiology of liver disease. Third, because of the retrospective study design and the inclusion of patients who underwent varying numbers and frequencies of alternative imaging studies (CT and MRI), there was no uniform protocol for the alternative surveillance group. Given that, further studies with uniform protocol for alternative surveillance are required. Fourth, we did not analyze the cost-effectiveness of alternative surveillance compared with US-only and AFP-based surveillance. The cost of contrast-enhanced CT or MRI is generally higher than US. In addition, radiation exposure from the CT for HCC surveillance would be another potential hazard to the patients. Regarding the MRI, the availability should be considered since surveillance needs repeated examinations with regular interval. Adverse effect of contrast agents is another limitation of contrast-enhanced CT or MRI for HCC surveillance. Given that, further studies are needed to evaluate the cost-effectiveness or risk-benefit of alternative HCC surveillance compared to US only surveillance.

In conclusion, alternative surveillance using CT/MRI as intermittent replacement for scheduled US enabled the detection of HCC in earlier stage with smaller size than US only and serum AFP based surveillance, and it had a potential to improve the clinical outcome including OS and post OS after HCC diagnosis, especially for patients with high risk having REACH-B score ≥8.
