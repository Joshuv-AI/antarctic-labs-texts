The SOS Response: A Double-Edged Sword between the Persister and Resister Formation
Authors: Kushala Ravikumar, Krishna Kurthkoti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial agents have revolutionized the treatment of infectious diseases; however, their widespread and extensive use has accelerated the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). While resistance driven by mutation and gene transfer poses a major threat, the transiently dormant subpopulation, called persisters, poses an equally significant challenge. The bacterial SOS response, triggered by extensive DNA damage, promotes resistance by inducing mutations in the target protein, activating drug efflux systems and horizontally transferring genetic material. Furthermore, SOS has been implicated in controlling toxin-antitoxin modules, as observed in the tisB/istR system in Escherichia coli, which further facilitates the emergence of persister populations. Although persistence and resistance are often studied separately, their shared regulation by the SOS system remains unexplored. Targeting SOS regulators, such as RecA and LexA, and DNA damage responders, including error-prone polymerases, can significantly help control the emergence and spread of AMR, although such approaches are in their early translational stages. This review explores the role of the SOS response as a unifying mechanism linking resistance and persistence, while evaluating emerging therapeutic strategies targeting SOS regulators and associated stress response pathways as antievolution approaches to combat AMR effectively.

Introduction

Antibiotics have greatly benefitted the treatment of infectious diseases; however, their widespread and inappropriate use has led to a global crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). A report from the Lancet 2022 suggests an estimated 1.27 million deaths worldwide that are directly linked to AMR. , AMR is the ability of a microorganism to survive and proliferate under the influence of an antimicrobial agent, a phenomenon accelerated by the prolonged and excessive use of antibiotics in both agricultural and clinical settings. Bacterial intrinsic mechanisms to gain resistance depend particularly on two bacterial traits: antibiotic tolerance and persistence, which play an important role in the development and acquisition of antibiotic resistance. − The emergence of bacterial resistance occurs through various mechanisms, including enzymatic degradation of drugs, modification of drug targets, reduced drug uptake, and active efflux of antibiotics through efflux pumps. Biofilm formation further enhances resistance by inhibiting antibiotic penetration and promoting persistence. , Various mechanisms act as key contributors to resistance with the SOS response acting as the mediator of resistance.

Persisters are a subpopulation of nongrowing or slow-growing bacteria that continue to survive under antibiotic treatment and other stressful conditions. Persisters can tolerate antibiotics and are a source of recurrent infections, making them clinically relevant. , The heterogeneous population of persisters comprises different cell populations, including triggered persisters, which arise in response to stress, and spontaneous persisters, which arise stochastically during growth. Bartell et al. reported that bacterial lineages of high-persister variants produce isolates with antibiotic resistance and slow growth rates, indicating that they are evolutionary precursors to resistance. This challenge between persistence and resistance is intricately modulated by the stress response in bacteria, particularly the SOS system, which is multifaceted. ,

Exposure to antibiotics at lethal or sublethal doses has been shown to result in redox imbalances, resulting in an increased burden of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) in bacteria. ROS damages DNA, resulting in strand breaks, cross-links, and the chemical modification of bases. Bacterial cells exposed to ROS or other DNA damaging agents generate single stranded DNA (ssDNA) which triggers the SOS response, a highly conserved regulatory stress response governed by two major proteins, RecA and LexA. RecA binds to ssDNA, facilitating the autocleavage and degradation of LexA. The removal of LexA derepresses the SOS regulon, thereby activating it, along with a set of genes involved in DNA repair and stress response. The SOS regulon encompasses a variety of proteins, such as DNA polymerases that enhance mutagenesis, recombinases that mobilize antibiotic resistance genes, and proteins involved in persistence and biofilm formation. , Given its extensive role in promoting bacterial evolvability and increasing tolerant and resistant bacterial populations, a better understanding of the molecular regulation of the SOS response presents a valuable opportunity to combat AMR. Emerging strategies focus on inhibiting RecA, LexA, or SOS-associated polymerases, with compounds acting as RecA inhibitors (e.g., OXF-077) and repurposed anticancer agents that interfere with DNA-damaging signals. However, these compounds remain in the preclinical stages because of problems associated with their specificity, delivery, and toxicity. In this Perspective, we first discuss the regulation of SOS and its interplay with the formation of persisters and antibiotic resistance. In the following sections, we examine the integration of the SOS response with other bacterial stress responses, highlighting its dual role in resistance and persistence, and critically evaluate emerging SOS-targeted antievolution strategies as potential approaches to combat AMR. These observations support the framework of the bacterial SOS response as the central hub linking persistence and evolution of antibiotic resistance. Framing the SOS pathway as a key contributor to bacterial evolvability, we also focus on therapeutic interventions aimed at targeting persistence and resistance.

SOS Response in Bacteria: A Central Regulator of Persistence and Resistance in Bacteria

Rec A, an Inducer of SOS

The recA gene is involved in homologous recombination and DNA repair pathways, demonstrating its pleiotropic nature. RecX negatively regulates RecA, and both genes are expressed within the same cistron under the transcriptional control of LexA. The lexA gene not only represses recA but also inhibits the expression of several other genes, including uvrA, uvrB, umuC, and sfiB genes. The protease activity of RecA facilitates the derepression of these genes, which are involved in DNA repair, mutagenesis, and cell division. Furthermore, RecA functions as a DNA-dependent ATPase that plays a central role in homologous recombination and the SOS response. Upon binding to single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) in the presence of ATP, RecA undergoes a conformational change to its active form, which facilitates the nucleation and extension of helical filaments. Filament assembly occurs in discrete nucleation and elongation phases, with nucleation being rate-limiting and significantly enhanced by the regulatory proteins. Predominantly, extension proceeds in the 5′ to 3′ direction with RecA-ATP monomers associating reversibly. Filament disassembly from the 5′-proximal end is driven by ATP hydrolysis. These dynamic filament properties are crucial not only for strand exchange during homologous recombination but also for inducing the SOS response through LexA autocleavage, thereby linking RecA filament activity directly to DNA damage signaling and repair. ,

LexA, a Repressor of SOS

LexA regulates the expression of SOS response genes by acting as a transcriptional repressor of SOS genes. LexA protein comprises two domains joined by a peptide linker: the N-terminal DNA-binding domain (NTD) and the C-terminal catalytic domain (CTD), which contains a serine–lysine catalytic dyad. The CTD has autoproteolytic activity that is activated by RecA-ssDNA-ATP, and subsequently CTD is also responsible for LexA homodimerization. LexA undergoes self-cleavage by breaking the peptide bond between Ala74-Gly85, mediated by the serine–lysine CTD of the LexA CTD domain. , In a recA ‑ strain, the SOS response is not induced; however, a recA ‑ strain carrying an additional LexA (Ts) exhibits constitutive SOS gene expression, suggesting that while RecA is not a direct regulator, it is required for LexA inactivation. The self-cleaved LexA protein cannot bind to the SOS operator, as uncleaved LexA exhibits high-affinity binding to the putative SOS box located downstream of Escherichia coli pyrC. Disruption of a single base in the 20 bp SOS operator impairs LexA binding, highlighting the critical role of sequence integrity in LexA-mediated suppression. The homodimerization that activates the transcriptional repressor of the SOS regulon is regulated to self-cleave in the presence of activated RecA* along with other related proteins, such as phage repressors, UmuD protein, and its homologues.

Activation of SOS Response and Its Role in Bacterial Physiology

SOS is a conserved bacterial stress response to DNA damage that is observed across different bacterial species. It coordinates DNA repair pathways, halts bacterial cell division, and induces latent bacteriophages. The SOS system can be triggered by several agents, including physical agents such as UV and ionizing radiation and chemical agents such as alkylating and cross-linking agents. In addition to exogenous stressors, endogenous metabolic byproducts such as ROS-producing hydroxyl radicals can damage DNA and activate the SOS response. , Miroslav Radman used the term “SOS” to define the distress signal in the bacterial cells as a sign of genomic instability caused by DNA damage and replication blockage in E. coli. Induction of the SOS regulon is triggered by the accumulation of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), which serves as an activation signal. The SOS response regulatory system centers on two key proteins: LexA, a transcriptional repressor, and RecA, a recombinase and coprotease. The early phases of SOS activation involve the induction of uvr genes for nucleotide excision repair, followed by the activation of recA and other homologous recombination genes, such as ruvAB and recN. Subsequent induction includes polB and dinB, which encode DNA polymerases II and IV. The sulA gene, which inhibits cell division, is activated to allow time for DNA repair. If damage persists, error-prone polymerase V, encoded by umuC and umuD, is expressed. , RecA is activated upon sensing DNA breaks and binds to ssDNA, forming a nucleoprotein filament (RecA*). This active form promotes LexA self-cleavage, thereby derepressing SOS genes. Interestingly, LexA also regulates its own expression, creating a negative feedback loop that re-establishes the repression of the SOS regulon once the induction signal ceases. The regulation of the SOS response by LexA is further modulated by RecA-ssDNA nucleoprotein filaments, which influence both the timing of SOS induction and the rate of recovery by promoting LexA self-cleavage. Additionally, low concentrations of RecX negatively regulate RecA activity by inhibiting filament polymerization and promoting disassembly.

Although the core components of the SOS response are conserved, its regulation of this response varies across bacterial species. RecA in E. coli and Bacillus subtilis is under a single promoter, whereas in Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), recA is regulated by two promoters in response to DNA damage. In Pseudomonas aeruginosa, the level of RecA-LexA regulation resembles that of E. coli, but it significantly undergoes error-prone DNA damage stimulated by the addition of antibiotics, such as ciprofloxacin, facilitating the autocatalytic cleavage of LexA. The SOS response is tightly regulated by the binding affinity of LexA to specific consensus sequences (SOS boxes). Sequence variability in the SOS box influences LexA binding strength, which can be measured using the heterology index (HI); a higher HI denotes weaker LexA binding. The clinical relevance of SOS activation became evident in a study conducted by Barrett et al., who demonstrated that treatment with fluoroquinolones (FQs), including ofloxacin (OFL), induced the SOS response in 17 OFL persister isolates. These persisters exhibited increased resistance to OFL, rifampicin (RIF), carbenicillin, d-cycloserine, and fosfomycin. Interestingly, resistance to RIF is heritable and RecA-dependent, suggesting that RecA-mediated SOS induction facilitates survival and adaptation following FQ treatment. Pribis et al. discussed the gambler phenotype in bacteria, a transiently differentiated subpopulation of bacterial cells that have high ROS and σS general stress response developed due to the subinhibitory exposure to ciprofloxacin. In 10–25% of the cipro-induced ROS cell subpopulations, the σS activity was observed to be high. Ciprofloxacin induced increased mutation rates by 26- and 18- fold in rifampicin (RifR) and ampicillin (AmpR) resistant mutants, contrary to the noninduced ciprofloxacin bacterial population. Strikingly, ciprofloxacin-induced mutagenesis requires mutagenic break repair (MBR) proteins, such as RecA, RecB, RuvC, SOS, and σS- response activators, and SOS-upregulated DNA polymerases IV, V, and II. Alternatively, the mutation rates were reduced by 87% and 70% in the strains of SOS noninducible (lexAInd), rpoS (S- deficient), and double SOS-S-defective mutants, implying that the cipro-induced ROS mutagenesis occurs through the S- dependent MBR pathway.

SOS Role in Persistence and Resistance: An Interplay with Stress Response

Beyond antibiotic-induced DNA damage, other environmental stressors, such as nutrient limitation and oxidative and acid stress, can activate responses involving RpoS, TA systems, efflux pumps, and SOS, collectively contributing to the emergence of resistant and persistent bacterial populations. The RpoS and TA systems in E. coli are tightly regulated in response to stress. Under normal conditions, the antitoxins MqsA (of the MqsR/MqsA TA system) and DinJ (of the YafQ/DinJ TA system) repress RpoS transcription and translation. Upon stress, antitoxins are degraded, which releases repression, thereby promoting persistence. Nan Wu and team have demonstrated the relative importance of the genes involved in the different pathways that include the antioxidative defense pathway (oxyR), global regulators that include (dnaK, clpB and rpoS), TA module (mqsR), stringent response (SR) (relA), and SOS response (recA) in the process of persister formation. They used four different antibiotics to assess the relative importance of the persister genes: gentamicin, ampicillin, norfloxacin, and trimethoprim. The persister gene mutants showed defective growth in the presence of antibiotics at different time points, suggesting that different genes are preferentially important at different time intervals to maintain persister survival. Furthermore, oxyR, dnaK, clpB, rpoS, relA, recA, and mqsR mutants showed a significant reduction in persistence at almost all time points, indicating their importance for persister survival. Interestingly, the rpoS mutant showed defective persistence to gentamicin, while it displayed higher persistence than the wild type against ampicillin or norfloxacin, suggesting that the loss of rpoS leads to ROS generation, thereby enhancing oxidative stress, whereas in the RpoS mutant induced by ampicillin or norfloxacin, compensatory mutations might enable their higher persistence. The SR is mediated by RelA and SpoT, which synthesize (p)­ppGpp, which in turn upregulates SOS-related genes, including recA, ruvA, sbmC, sulA, umuD, and yebG. The SOS response plays a key role in the generation of bacterial persister cells and in population-wide tolerance. Understanding the mechanisms and interactions of this response with stress responses could provide insights into persistent or resistant bacterial infections.

Antibiotic exposure induces ROS stress, leading to DNA damage and mutagenesis. This, in turn, drives the selection and counterselection of bacteria with high mutation rates, often referred to as mutator phenotypes, which significantly contribute to bacterial adaptation and evolutionary success in the presence of antibiotics. These phenotypes typically result from defective mismatch repair systems, particularly due to the inactivation of mutS or mutL, which are responsible for maintaining DNA replication fidelity by correcting biosynthetic errors in the DNA. Additionally, antibiotic-induced stress activates efflux systems, such as the AcrAB-TolC pump, which recognizes and transports beta-lactams, tetracyclines, and fluoroquinolone antibiotics. Bergmiller et al. showed that the biased partition of drug efflux between mother and daughter cells in the AcrAB-TolC pole in E. coli has significant implications for the emergence of multidrug resistance, resulting in phenotypic heterogeneity. Furthermore, El Meouche and Dunlop used E. coli to study the crosstalk between AcrAB and mutS and showed that cells with higher AcrAB expression were associated with lower mutS expression, consequently causing a higher mutation frequency. In addition to mutation-driven resistance, bacterial populations consist of nonmutant cells known as persisters, which exhibit transient tolerance to antibiotics. The SOS response, primarily triggered by DNA damage, not only activates DNA repair but also induces toxin-antitoxin (TA) modules that transiently disrupt processes such as translation, aiding persister formation. In E. coli, high levels of SOS induction are observed upon the expression of the toxin gene tisB, which encodes a small membrane-acting peptide that disrupts membrane potential and lowers ATP levels; overexpression of this toxin gene can ultimately cause cell death. Dòrr et al. showed that tisB deletion markedly reduced persister formation. A similar phenotype was observed in ΔpistR mutants lacking the antitoxin istR promoter. Stepwise exposure to increasing ciprofloxacin concentrations led to 10 to 100-fold higher persister levels than high-dose treatment with antibiotics, suggesting an adaptive induction of tisB. Notably, among the SOS-regulated TA modules, tisAB showed the highest induction under ciprofloxacin stress, underscoring its central role in persistence. The same study demonstrated that tisB overexpression conferred tolerance to unrelated antibiotics such as ampicillin, streptomycin, and colistin. Similar persistence patterns were observed in ΔrecA mutants treated with bactericidal agents. Activation of the SOS response enhances DNA repair mechanisms, which are closely linked to the formation of bacterial persisters. Unoson and Wagner reported that the SOS-mediated induction of tisB leads to membrane damage, resulting in slowed or arrested bacterial growth. This membrane disruption impairs ATP synthesis, subsequently reducing DNA, RNA, and protein synthesis. In E. coli, a similar observation was made with an increase in multidrug-tolerant persister cells, followed by the overexpression of other TA toxins, such as RelE and HipA. Deletion of the hipA gene led to a reduction in the number of persister cells.

Crane et al. showed the role of SOS in the emergence of AMR using zidovudine and ciprofloxacin, which are strong inducers of SOS which further triggered the development of resistance to rifampin, minocycline, and fosfomycin. In an in vivo model using the rabbit EPEC E22 strain, prolonged exposure (20 h) to SOS-inducing agents led to a greater degree of hypermutation than shorter in vitro exposures (3–4 h). Handel et al. further demonstrated that the SOS response is essential for resistance acquisition in E. coli against enrofloxacin and amoxicillin. Using ΔrecA mutants, they showed that resistance to amoxicillin was delayed, indicating that a functional SOS system promotes mutations through the upregulation of β-lactamase expression. The inability of ΔrecA mutants to adapt to enrofloxacin further highlights the role of the SOS pathway in facilitating resistance to fluoroquinolones. The delayed appearance of mutations adapting to enrofloxacin under nonoptimized conditions, mirroring the ΔrecA phenotype, reinforces the central role of the SOS system in the evolution of antibiotic resistance. The SOS response does not operate as an isolated event; instead, it integrates with other stress pathways that collectively enable the transition of bacteria into a transient tolerant persistent state or acquire stable genetic resistance. The following sections discuss each of these mechanisms in detail, focusing on their mechanistic contributions to persister formation and the enabling antibiotic resistance.

Stress Endurance by Bacterial Persisters

RpoS as a General Stress Response Regulator Influencing Persister Formation

The sigma factor RpoS (σ70, RNA polymerase of Stationary phase) is found in E. coli and other bacteria. It is a 38 kDa protein that belongs to the sigma 70-factor family. Under nutrient deprivation or stress conditions, stationary phase cells accumulate RpoS, which functions as a master regulator of stress response Small RNAs (sRNAs) such as DsrA, RprA, and ArcZ intricately regulate the post-transcriptional regulation of RpoS, but this translation requires the inhibition of secondary structures in the rpoS mRNA. The function of sRNAs is controlled by the RNA chaperone Hfq for stability and efficient pairing. RpoS induces stress-related responses in E. coli and other bacteria.

In P. aeruginosa, RpoS fine-tunes cellular metabolism and modulates the production of secondary metabolites to optimize stationary phase adaptation. The entry of bacteria into the stationary phase induces the RpoS regulon, which causes global bacterial stress resistance, and RpoS interacts with TA systems in E. coli to combat stress. In Salmonella typhimurium, RpoS contributes to biofilm formation, aiding persistence. Additionally, RpoS induces SOS-regulated error-prone polymerases, thereby elevating the mutation rate, which ultimately facilitates natural bacterial evolution. Genomic screening of E. coli isolates revealed mutational signatures dominated by specific base substitutions and indels, attributed to mutagenesis caused by the sigma-S (RpoS) stress response. A coordinated regulatory network involving the RpoS with other sigma factors such as σE (envelope stress) and σH (heat shock) and the transcription factors such as DksA results in persister formation, signifying a much complex network rather than a single pathway. RpoS serves as a central hub to promote persistence, mutagenesis, biofilm formation, and specialization through SR signaling and toxin-antitoxin activation.

Stringent Response Mediated by (p)­ppGpp

The SR is triggered by various environmental stresses, including amino acid starvation, iron limitation, nitrogen deprivation, heat shock, and other unfavorable conditions. , The key effectors of this response are derivatives of GDP/GTP, guanosine tetraphosphate (ppGpp), and guanosine pentaphosphate (pppGpp), collectively termed (p)­ppGpp or alarmones. These molecules are synthesized and hydrolyzed by members of the RSH (RelA/SpoT homologue) superfamily. In E. coli, RelA functions exclusively as a (p)­ppGpp synthetase, possessing a synthetase domain and a catalytically inactive pseudohydrolase. In contrast, SpoT is a bifunctional enzyme with weak synthetase activity but strong hydrolase activity, making it essential for the degradation of (p)­ppGpp. The accumulation of (p)­ppGpp is observed upon RelA activation, which is caused by uncharged tRNA occupying the ribosomal A site during amino acid starvation. SpoT is encoded by the spoT gene, which maintains basal levels of (p)­ppGpp during normal growth and responds to other stress signals, such as carbon, phosphate, or fatty acid starvation. Deletion of both relA and spoT results in (p)­ppGpp accumulation, highlighting their significant roles in regulating intracellular (p) ppGpp levels. , During nutritional deprivation, the transcription of stable RNAs, such as tRNA and rRNA, is downregulated, initiating a SR. (p)­ppGpp and the transcription factor DksA bind to RNA polymerase (RNAP) and alter promoter selectivity, repressing genes involved in growth and activating those required for stress adaptation. RpoS integrates with the SR through RNAP-DksA-(p)­ppGpp signaling, which establishes a network grouping general stress survival with SOS-mediated persistence. Transcriptional bubbles formed during RNAP activity expose single-stranded DNA, which is more prone to spontaneous damage than double-stranded DNA. (p)­ppGpp inhibits DNA replication and may facilitate the replication restart via error-prone polymerases, potentially increasing mutagenesis. Furthermore, (p)­ppGpp downregulates ribosome activity, elevating ppGpp levels and transcription of the Ribosome Modulation Factor (RMF), which, along with the Hibernation Promoting Factor (Hpf), inactivates ribosomes; this mechanism allows cells to conserve energy and survive harsh environments. Song and Wood proposed that E. coli persister formation occurs through a large fraction of dimerized 100S ribosomes mediated by the RMF and Hpf proteins. Ribosomal reactivation causes persister resuscitation, providing a direct connection between ppGpp and persistence. ,

The Classification of a Toxin-Antitoxin System and Its Role in Persistence

Toxin-Antitoxin (TA) systems in bacteria are often regarded as “selfish addiction modules”, composed of an operon encoding a toxin protein that is neutralized by an antitoxin made of either a protein or a small RNA (sRNA). The toxin in the TA system is harmful to the cells, as it can impair essential cellular processes such as replication, transcription, mRNA stability, and translation, while the antitoxin serves as the antidote protecting the cell. The expression of TA systems can trigger persister formation and has been shown to be expressed when bacteria are subjected to antibiotics. TA systems are classified into five classes based on their genetic structures and regulatory mechanisms. Type I TA systems inhibit the expression of toxins by interaction between antitoxin sRNA and the stable mRNA of the toxin. The symR/symE system of E. coli is a well-known example that regulates the LexA protein of the SOS system. Other examples of type I TA systems include tisB/istR-1, ibs/sib, and hok/sok genes. In the type II TA system, both the toxin and antitoxin are proteins. Neutralization of antitoxins by toxins occurs through the protein–protein complex formation. Examples include relB/relE, phd-doc from the E. coli bacteriophage P1, and vap/vapC from Salmonella enterica. The type III TA system resembles the type I TA system in that it uses an RNA antitoxin; however, the antitoxin binds to the toxin protein. The type IV TA system was characterized in the yeeU/yeeV TA module of E. coli; this TA class, in contrast to the other classes, does not involve direct interaction of the RNA or the protein. The YeeV toxin inhibits bacterial growth by interacting with MreB and FtsZ, preventing their polymerization and inhibiting cytoskeleton assembly. Simultaneously, the YeeU antitoxin counteracts this effect by stabilizing MreB and FtsZ polymers. In the type V TA system, the antitoxin protein has a sequence-specific endoribonuclease activity that cleaves the mRNA toxin, thereby preventing its translation; this mechanism can be observed in the ghoS/ghoT TA system of E. coli. , Together, these findings support the role of TA systems in modulating persister cell formation, likely through their influence on cellular stress responses and drug efflux regulation. Collectively, these findings support the role of TA systems in modulating persister cell formation through their influence on cellular stress and the regulation of drug efflux.

Bacterial Biofilm and Persister Formation

Bacterial biofilms consist of an ecosystem of one or more microbes enclosed within an extracellular matrix composed of polysaccharides, proteins, and DNA. Biofilms establish an environment that promotes antibiotic tolerance and resistance. Bacterial biofilms can survive antibiotics owing to impaired antibiotic diffusion, antibiotic efflux, nutrient and oxygen limitations, and the expression of biofilm-specific genetic mechanisms. Starvation contributes to antibiotic tolerance in bacteria, and starvation-induced tolerance has been observed in vivo during biofilm growth. The nutrient consumption by the cells located at the periphery of biofilm clusters shows tolerance to almost all classes of antibiotics. To explore the underlying regulatory mechanisms, Nguyen et al. disrupted relA and spoT, which are key genes regulating the SR. Inactivating SR sensitized P. aeruginosa biofilms to oxidative stress, and this increased sensitivity was linked to the production of 4-hydroxy-2-alkylquinoline molecules (HAQs). Using a pqsA mutant that lacks HAQs, alongside relA and spoT mutants, they showed that SR-mediated antibiotic tolerance might be driven by HAQ signaling and the subsequent induction of antioxidant defenses. Bernier et al. demonstrated that starvation stress causes variations in the bacterial population within biofilms, particularly influencing their tolerance to the antibiotic ofloxacin. They found that when nutrients such as glucose, leucine, or lysine were deprived, some bacterial subpopulations within the biofilm developed a significantly higher tolerance to ofloxacin. This increased tolerance is linked to the activation of the SOS response, which requires the RecA protein. In their study, bacteria lacking key SOS components, such as the ΔrecA and lexAind3 mutant (which expresses a noncleavable form of LexA, blocking SOS gene activation), showed lower ofloxacin tolerance in biofilms, similar to bacteria that were not starved. This suggests that both RecA and LexA activities are essential for starvation-induced tolerance. Additionally, when a wild-type copy of recA was introduced into a leucine-auxotrophic strain, the tolerance to ofloxacin was fully restored. However, overexpression of RecA alone did not restore tolerance in the lexAind3 mutant, indicating that both RecA and LexA cleavages during the SOS response are crucial for developing a tolerant phenotype under starvation conditions. These findings show that biofilm-associated tolerance is not linked to structural barriers alone but appears to be an integration of stress signaling pathways. The SR mediates metabolic adaptation and HAQ-dependent oxidative defense, whereas the SOS pathway contributes to stress-induced tolerance. This crosstalk reinforces resistance in biofilms and underscores the SOS pathway as the central mediator of environmental stress, persistence, and resistance within biofilms.

Stress Endurance by Antibiotic Resistance

Mechanism of Gaining Antimicrobial Resistance

Bacterial resistance can be classified into intrinsic, acquired, and adaptive types (Chart ). Intrinsic resistance is innate to bacterial species and is not dependent on prior antibiotic exposure. For example, vancomycin resistance in E. coli is due to its outer membrane barrier, efflux pumps that restrict the entry of antimicrobial compounds, and low permeability of the membrane which limits the molecules that can pass through the bacterial outer layer, often contributing to the intrinsic resistance of bacteria. Acquired resistance arises through genetic changes, either via spontaneous mutations or horizontal gene transfer mechanisms such as transformation, transduction, or conjugation. Adaptive resistance, or phenotypic resistance, refers to transient, reversible phenotypes that arise in response to environmental stressors, including subinhibitory antibiotic exposure, nutrient limitation, pH changes, and ion concentrations. This resistance is typically lost once the stress is removed, and the resistance-related mechanism can be activated upon stress, which includes the temporary upregulation of efflux pumps, biofilm formation, and stress-response pathways. Although adaptive resistance does not involve permanent genetic changes, prolonged exposure may select for mutations that lead to stable and acquired resistances.

Categorization of Anti-Microbial Resistance

Antimicrobial agents act through various mechanisms, including inhibition of DNA replication (e.g., fluoroquinolones targeting DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV), protein biosynthesis (e.g., drugs binding to the 30S or 50S ribosomal subunits), cell wall synthesis (e.g., β-lactams and glycopeptides), and folate metabolism. Fluoroquinolones that inhibit DNA replication include ciprofloxacin, which acts at the target site of DNA gyrase/topoisomerase. Beta-lactam antibiotics that target cell wall synthesis include penicillin, ampicillin, and cephalosporins. Doxycycline, erythromycin, and chloramphenicol are antibiotics that target the protein synthesis. Bacteria can resist these drugs through multiple mechanisms, such as enzymatic inactivation (e.g., acetylation of aminoglycosides), target modification, efflux pumps, and decreased membrane permeability. , Together, these diverse mechanisms enable bacteria to survive antibiotic treatment and contribute to the persistent challenges posed by AMR.

Efflux Pump Contributing to Antibiotic Resistance

Drug efflux is a key mechanism contributing to antimicrobial resistance and is largely observed in Gram-negative bacteria. Efflux pumps are membrane proteins that extrude toxic compounds, such as antibiotics, from bacterial cells. Efflux pumps are classified into six major families: the Small Multidrug Resistance (SMR) superfamily, Proteobacterial Antimicrobial Compound Efflux (PACE) superfamily, Major Facilitator Superfamily (MFS), Multidrug and Toxic Compound Extrusion (MATE) superfamily, Resistance Nodulation Division (RND) family, and adenosine triphosphate ATP-Binding Cassette (ABC) superfamily. ABC efflux pumps serve as the primary active transporters that use ATP hydrolysis as the energy source for extrusion, while the SMR, PACE, MATE, MFS, and RND pumps act as secondary active transporters as they use the proton motive force or sodium ion gradient as their energy source. A study conducted by Zwama and Nishino showed that a mutation in the proximal binding pocket of Arg717 in AcrB of S. enterica elicited increased resistance levels. The same R717 (Q/L) mutation of AcrB expressed in E. coli showed a 4-to 8-fold increase in minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for azithromycin and ciprofloxacin, while reducing resistance to cloxacillin and novobiocin by up to 2-fold. This substrate-specific gain and loss of resistance reveals the emergence of resistant phenotypes, where a single point mutation in the substrate recognition site of AcrB shifts the efflux profile by enhancing the export of some drugs while impairing that of others. Antibiotic-induced stress responses can promote mutations that block drug activity or transiently upregulate multidrug efflux pumps, contributing to the emergence of tolerance. For instance, in E. coli, high expression of the AcrAB-TolC efflux system is associated with reduced expression of the DNA mismatch repair gene mutS, thereby promoting spontaneous mutation and the emergence of a mutator phenotype. This suggests a possible role of the SOS response in promoting antibiotic survival by enhancing efflux-mediated drug extrusion.

Inhibiting SOS and Other Associated Stress Pathways to Suppress Bacterial Evolvability

Inhibitors Specific to the SOS Mechanism

The use of antibiotics contributes to the emergence of AMR; therefore, understanding the mechanism of resistance evolution can help counter it. In addition to chromosomally acquired AMR, reducing the mutation rate in pathogens could be a promising strategy to impede AMR development. This approach requires the identification and inhibition of factors that elevate mutation rates, referred to as “evolvability factors”. Key regulators of the SOS response, such as RecA and LexA proteins, are among these factors. Suramin is a potent inhibitor of Mtb RecA, inhibiting its ATPase, DNA strand exchange, and LexA cleavage. Moreover, suramin inhibited ciprofloxacin-induced recA expression and suppressed the SOS response, thereby enhancing the efficacy of ciprofloxacin. Salini et al. further demonstrated that using suramin as an inhibitor against RecA reduced the rate of conversion of persisters to resisters and this was observed in diverse group of bacteria, which reiterated the importance of suramin as a novel therapeutic application in combating the development of drug resistance (Table ).

Inhibitors of Associated Stress Pathways as Complementary Strategies

Beyond the SOS response, other stress adaptation mechanisms also appear to be promising therapeutic targets for combating the formation of resistant and persistent bacterial populations. In E. coli, double-knockout mutants for relA and spoT, which lack (p)­ppGpp, displayed significantly reduced antibiotic persistence. This suggests that (p)­ppGpp synthesis is a potential target for eliminating persister cells. Wexselblatt et al. used Relacin to inhibit Rel-mediated (p)­ppGpp synthesis, leading to B. subtilis cell death at an IC50 of 200 μM, while also preventing spore and biofilm formation. Syal et al. tested a modified version of Relacin in Mycobacterium smegmatis, which resulted in elongated, biofilm-deficient cells resembling rel mutants. The compound also showed good permeability into human lung epithelial cells, suggesting its potential utility in treating persistent Mtb infections. Edaravone behaved as an antievolvability drug against the transiently differentiated subpopulation of bacterial cells that follow the “bet-hedging strategy”, in which a subpopulation of cells risks a phenotype that may or may not be advantageous, depending on the environment. This subpopulation of cells, called gamblers, is at risk of acquiring mutations that can lead to heritable resistance against antibiotics that have not been previously encountered. Edaravone works by removing ROS and σS- from the high gambler population without reducing the antibiotic power of ciprofloxacin (Table ).

In S. aureus, mutations in the norA gene lead to the overexpression of the NorA efflux pump, which is frequently observed in MRSA strains and is associated with enhanced biofilm formation. Therefore, using an inhibitor against NorA could facilitate the reversal of multidrug resistance and the inhibition of biofilm formation. Zimmermann and team attempted to screen the already approved drugs for possible inhibitory effects on the NorA pump. Combined treatment with ciprofloxacin and nilotinib effectively inhibited NorA activity. Although nilotinib does not directly affect biofilms, its inhibition of NorA leads to reduced biofilm formation.

Future Perspectives

Over the past few decades, AMR has emerged as a major public health concern, with projected mortality reaching millions by 2050 and potentially exceeding that of cancer-related deaths. The unrestricted use of antibiotics and improper disposal have resulted in antibiotic contamination in environments such as soil and water, leading to accelerated dissemination of drug resistance in the bacterial population in these ecosystems. While the discovery of new antibiotics with new mechanisms of action seems to be the best solution for tackling AMR, approaches that reduce the rate of emergence of AMR are gaining traction as an alternative strategy. These “anti-evolution” strategies are based on the principle of inhibiting molecular mechanisms that confer drug resistance by promoting mutagenesis or the exchange of resistance-conferring elements between the bacterial community.

Studies over the past decade have shown that the bacterial SOS response occupies a central stage, driving the de novo emergence of AMR by increasing mutation frequency and promoting the transfer of resistance genes between bacterial communities through conjugation. Targeting the bacterial SOS response has emerged as an attractive strategy to impede the emergence of drug resistance in bacteria. Interestingly, RecA and LexA proteins, which are key regulators of the SOS response, are present in all bacterial members, providing an opportunity to develop broad-spectrum SOS inhibitors. Molecules targeting RecA and LexA have already been tested for their ability to reduce the rate of emergence of AMR in bacteria. Leveraging in silico methods facilitated by artificial intelligence or machine learning (AI/ML) tools in drug discovery can further accelerate the discovery of new molecules that are more potent, have a broader spectrum of activity, and lower cytotoxicity.
