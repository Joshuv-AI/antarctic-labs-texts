Multiscale Studies on the Transport Behavior of Nitrogen Foam for Improving Oil Recovery in Homogeneous and Heterogeneous Carbonate Reservoirs
Authors: Qianhui Wu, Lun Zhao, Wenqi Zhao, Baoguang Jin, Xuelin Wu, Zifei Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper presents the viability of utilizing foam to enhance oil recovery in carbonate reservoirs, characterized by medium temperatures (55 °C) and formation salinity. The foam tests first examined the effects of foam quality, injection velocity, surfactant concentration, and permeability on foam strength and incremental oil recovery on Indiana Limestone on a laboratory scale. Over 8% of the water–gas-flooded residual oil was extracted following the coinjection of a 2.5 total pore volume of nitrogen and 0.3 wt % APG surfactant (in synthetic formation brine) at a foam quality of 70% (4 ft./d). The modeling parameters for the foam dry-out effect, surfactant-dependent function, flow rate-dependent function, and oil destabilization effect were derived from history matching of gas/surfactant coinjection tests conducted at different foam qualities, surfactant concentrations, and injection rates. The viability of foam for EOR is evaluated by using two-dimensional synthetic homogeneous and heterogeneous models at the field scale. Enhancing the surfactant concentration in the surfactant-alternating-gas (SAG) slugs can significantly improve the foam efficiency, effectively redirecting gas injection into unswept regions. The numerical modeling results indicate that the APG foam possesses significant potential to improve oil recovery in both homogeneous and heterogeneous oil-wet carbonate reservoirs. It is also found that the water-top-gas-bottom (WTGB) injection strategy can successfully alleviate gravity segregation in a multizone completion wellbore by leveraging the buoyant force of the gas and the gravitational force of the aqueous phase.

Introduction

Foam has been widely utilized in the oil and gas sectors for various applications, including enhanced oil recovery (EOR), − matrix acid stimulation, aquifer remediation, gas leakage prevention, and drilling. The foam can successfully mitigate issues related to viscosity fingering, gravity segregation, and gas channeling by significantly reducing gas mobility. − Nonetheless, surfactant retention, the stringent requirement for foam generation, and long-term stability represent three primary challenges that must be adequately addressed. , The transport behavior of foam in porous media is a dynamic process involving the unremitting generation and coalescence of lamellae, which ultimately influences the foam microstructure and gas mobility within the porous medium. ,

The interplay between foam generation and stability is defined by foam strength, which depends on the gas type and reservoir characteristics (oil saturation, oil composition, mineralogy, wettability, the existence of fractures, pore size distribution, permeability, heterogeneity, brine salinity and hardness, pressure, temperature, local capillary pressure, water saturation, etc.), − surfactant chemistry (chemical additives, chemical structures, surfactant type and concentration, addition of polymers, thermal/chemical stability), operational methods (surfactant concentration, flow rate, injection scheme, foam quality, etc.), − and foam texture. − Two regimes were identified in the strong foam state, namely, the low-quality and high-quality regimes. −

The permeability of the reservoir significantly influences the foam behavior in porous media. It is generally recognized that foam strength increases with increasing rock permeability, − rendering foam advantageous for conformance in heterogeneous reservoirs. Furthermore, it has been indicated that the limiting capillary pressure rises as the permeability decreases. The limiting water saturation is generally elevated in rocks with poorer permeability. , Experimental evidence demonstrates that foam enhances oil recovery in low-permeability strata within heterogeneous porous media models. − The limiting capillary pressure is primarily determined by the type of surfactant, its concentration, and the permeability of the porous medium. Foam is thermodynamically unstable. The elevated surface free energy induces mechanisms that result in foam coalescence. Disjoining and capillary pressure are typically employed to denote the stability of lamellae. The stability of lamellae in porous media is influenced by various parameters, including gas type, permeability, local capillary pressure, water saturation, and gas velocity. −

Evaluating the foam behavior in oil-wet porous media has recently attracted great interest. ,, It is typically difficult to extract oil from carbonate reservoirs due to adverse wettability, detrimental reservoir heterogeneity, and elevated brine salinity. The presence of oil is widely recognized to adversely impact foam stability, particularly in the presence of light oil components. ,, The entering coefficient, spreading coefficient, bridging coefficient, and the stability of the “pseudoemulsion film” have been utilized to elucidate and describe foam stability in the presence of crude oil. , It is widely acknowledged that oil-wet conditions are detrimental to foam production and stability. , The stability of the wetting film is contingent upon rock wettability. − In oil-wet rocks, the films will fracture due to the dewetting of the aqueous films from the substrate. Recent studies indicate that foam formation in oil-wet rocks can occur if the surfactant modifies the wettability of the rock to water-wet conditions or generates a metastable water-wet state via a metastable pseudoemulsion film. ,,

This study examined the efficacy of foam-enhanced oil recovery (EOR) in oil-wet homogeneous and heterogeneous carbonate reservoirs with medium temperature and salinity, using laboratory experiments using Indiana Limestone outcrop core plugs and field-scale simulations. The influence of foam dry-out, surfactant concentration, flow rates, and permeability on foam transport behavior is methodically examined in the absence of oil. The foam EOR tests were conducted under oil-wet conditions. Following the history-matching of the benchmark experimental results, the essential parameters for foam modeling were determined. Thereafter, the foam transport behavior was analyzed using numerical simulation on two-dimensional synthetic homogeneous and heterogeneous models.

Materials and Methods

Reservoir Properties

The foam behavior is assessed using Indiana Limestone outcrops as the model porous medium. The average reservoir temperature and pressure are 55 °C and 1500 psi, respectively. Furthermore, the formation brine contains 110,000 ppm of total dissolved substances (TDS).

Chemicals and Instruments

The surfactant is C8–16 alkyl polyglucoside (APG) from BASF (Lot No.: Aspiro S2410X). The APG surfactant demonstrates superior solubility in synthetic formation brine (SFB) and remains stable for a minimum of six months under reservoir conditions. The dynamic adsorption of the APG surfactant at reservoir conditions is approximately 0.12 mg/g. , The partition coefficient of APG between oil and brine under reservoir conditions is around 0.03. ,, The interfacial tension between oil and surfactant solution is roughly 1.0 mN/m, as measured by a Ramé-Hart Model 500 Goniometer. The APG surfactant exhibits excellent solubility in high-salinity brine and maintains thermal stability below 100 °C.

Procedures

On Indiana Limestone, a sequence of core flooding tests was conducted in the absence and presence of crude oil. The protocols for conducting foam tests without crude oil are outlined as follows (55 °C, 2000 psi confining pressure, and 1500 psi back-pressure): (1) inject approximately 4 pore volumes (PV) of the SFB; (2) thereafter, presaturate the core sample with 4 PV of the surfactant solution; (3) coinject N2 and the surfactant solution at a specified foam quality and flow rate until steady state is achieved; (4) restore the core sample by flushing with about 50 PV of 2.0 wt % NaCl until the disparity between the recovered permeability and the original permeability is less than 5%; (5) repeat the foam coinjection process outlined previously at a different foam quality. Foam tests were conducted on rocks with varying permeabilities, and the modeling parameters were derived following history matching.

The foam EOR core flooding tests were performed under oil-wet conditions. ,, The protocols for executing foam testing in oil-wet conditions are outlined below (55 °C, 2000 psi confining pressure, and 1500 psi back-pressure): (1) presaturate the core plug with SFB under vacuum and measure the PV; (2) conduct an oil flood at a rate of 8 ft./d for 2 PV; (3) subsequently, age the core at 75 °C for 21 days while maintaining a minimal oil injection flow rate of 0.01 ft./d; (4) inject an additional 2 PV of crude oil; (5) coinject 10 PV of synthetic formation brine and N2; (6) coinject 10 PV of 0.3 wt % APG surfactant in SFB and N2 at a rate of 4 ft./d with 70% foam quality. The contact angle tests revealed that the core was altered to oil-wet after aging. The dynamic adsorption level of the APG surfactant under oil-wet conditions is 0.16 mg/g.3

Effect of Injection Location

The conventional injection approach may be unfavorable as water typically flows underride, while gas flows override due to the density disparity. For the water-top-gas-bottom (WTGB) injection strategy in a multizone completion wellbore, the water can be injected from the top perforated layers − and the gas is injected from the bottom perforated layers, − leveraging the gravitational and buoyant forces. The WTGB technology can effectively address gravity segregation issues, improving the sweep efficiency of water and gas.

WAG and SAG

In field applications, water-alternating-gas (WAG) and surfactant-alternating-gas (SAG) are the predominant injection techniques employed to ameliorate potential injectivity challenges. , For WAG and SAG injection strategies in this study, 10 days of surfactant/water injection are followed by 20 days of gas injection. Surfactant is normally injected for a restricted duration owing to its substantial expense. In the SAG injection scenario, 660 days of WAG was initially conducted, succeeded by 180 days of SAG, and subsequently followed by another 660 days of WAG. The injection rates for water and gas are 6 m3/d. An insight into the gas flow vectors presented in Figures and indicated that buoyancy predominantly governs gas flooding in the absence of foam. Conversely, gas mobility is markedly diminished due to the increased viscous forces associated with foam. Consequently, gas can flow both upward and downward subsequent to 6 slugs of SAG followed by WAG.

Conclusions

This paper presents the viability of foam-enhanced oil recovery (EOR) for homogeneous and heterogeneous carbonate reservoirs with a medium temperature of 55 °C and a high formation salinity of 11% TDS. The feasibility of foam EOR was examined using core flooding experiments in the laboratory, numerical simulations at the laboratory scale, and reservoir simulations at the field scale. The modeling parameters for the foam dry-out effect, surfactant-dependent function, flow rate-dependent function, and oil destabilization effect were derived from history matching of gas/surfactant coinjection tests conducted at different foam qualities, surfactant concentrations, and injection rates:(1)Over 8% OOIP of the water–gas-flooded residual oil was extracted following the coinjection of 2.5 total PV of nitrogen and 0.3 wt.% APG surfactant in SFB brine at a foam quality of 70% and 4 ft./d, with model parameters derived via history matching.(2)The WTGB injection strategy can efficiently alleviate gravity segregation in a multizone completion wellbore by utilizing the buoyant force of the gas and the gravitational force of the aqueous phase.(3)Enhancing the surfactant content in the SAG slugs can improve foam efficiency, effectively redirecting gas injection into unswept areas.(4)The simulation results at the field scale indicated the viability of APG foam-assisted EOR, if properly designed, for both homogeneous and heterogeneous carbonate reservoirs characterized by medium temperatures and elevated formation salinity.
