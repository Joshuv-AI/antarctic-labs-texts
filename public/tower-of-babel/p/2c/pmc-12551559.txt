Scaling success: parental perceptions of the benefits of a 20-week football living-lab approach for children living with cerebral palsy
Authors: Ricardo Martins, Will Pattison, Lara Vella, Ruth Postlethwaite, David Broom, Eileen Africa, Michael Duncan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiving-lab approaches have effectively connected academic research with community needs but have not yet been applied to promote physical activity for children with cerebral palsy (CP). This study evaluated a 20-week football-based living-lab programme for children aged 5–10 years with CP, grounded in the principles of Football is Medicine and physical literacy.MethodsA qualitative design was employed, involving seven one-to-one semistructured interviews with parents or legal guardians of programme participants. A deductive thematic analysis was conducted, and pen profile diagrams were developed to represent key themes related to physical literacy outcomes.ResultsParents reported benefits across multiple dimensions of their children’s physical literacy, including improved competence, confidence, motivation and enjoyment. All parents observed enhanced social interaction. Six described the sessions as inclusive and community-oriented, while five felt the programme was personalised to their child’s needs. Three parents perceived the football activities as complementary to physical therapy, supporting physical rehabilitation. Participation also enabled parents to exchange strategies and engage more meaningfully in their child’s well-being.ConclusionThe football-based living-lab supported the holistic development of children with CP, enhancing physical, psychological and social domains of physical literacy. Parental involvement further reinforced perceived therapeutic and community benefits.Keywords: Cerebral Palsy, Child Health, Rehabilitation, Qualitative research

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Football living labs offer recognised physical and psychological benefits for children with cerebral palsy (CP), yet most interventions are clinical, lack social elements and rarely consider holistic outcomes like physical literacy. Living Lab approaches have not previously been applied to support physical activity in this population and have proven potential for physical literacy development of children living with CP.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study shows that a 20-week football-based living-lab approach was perceived by parents to improve multiple domains of physical literacy, including confidence, motivation and social interaction, in children with CP. Parents also reported therapeutic and community benefits from participation.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) refers to a group of neurological conditions caused by damage to the brain’s motor control centres before, during or shortly after birth, resulting in motor impairments.1 These impairments may present as spasticity, dyskinesia, hyper-reflexia, excessive contraction of opposing muscles, involuntary movements and difficulties with motor planning. Such challenges can have a significant impact on daily activities and restrict participation in physical activity (PA) and exercise.2 Additionally, psychological challenges are prevalent and may be directly related to the cerebral damage itself (eg, intellectual disability, attentional difficulties), or they may emerge secondarily as a result of motor impairments and their implications for social participation and daily life.3 4 Regardless of severity, children living with CP face considerable limitations and barriers in getting involved in organised PA opportunities that fit their needs. Besides this, regular participation in PA has been shown to help manage symptoms, providing psychological (ie, self-efficacy and confidence) and social benefits (ie, becoming more sociable), while also creating positive attitudes towards PA.5,7 However, despite strong evidence for physical benefits, there is a lack of evidence for the psychosocial outcomes or longer-term impacts of PA interventions in people with CP, particularly in real-world settings.8

Despite the physical and psychological benefits of PA, individuals with CP often face challenges in finding organised PA opportunities.9 Recent research suggests there are many barriers to their participation (eg, ground not adapted to their frames or different skill levels).9 These challenges stem from factors such as impaired selective motor control, abnormal muscle tone, muscle weakness and sensory, communication and behavioural difficulties associated with CP.10 11 Additionally, parents of children with CP frequently report challenges that extend beyond the child’s physical impairments, encompassing emotional, social and logistical concerns.9 These include navigating complex healthcare systems, managing daily caregiving responsibilities and dealing with feelings of stress, isolation and uncertainty about their child’s future opportunities.1 12 Families often highlight barriers to inclusion in community activities and sport, citing a lack of suitable opportunities and concerns about their child’s confidence, social acceptance and long-term independence.10 Given this, various interventions have been designed to enhance PA and exercise participation for children with CP. However, most of these strategies have centred on therapeutic and rehabilitative approaches,13 often delivered in one-on-one sessions with physiotherapists.14 Although effective in improving isolated motor outcomes, such approaches often lack a focus on enjoyment, confidence-building and sustainability, key elements shown to facilitate long-term engagement. Besides this, it is known that PA and exercise tend to be more effective when conducted in social environments, where external factors such as support from coaches and peers encourage participation, leading to confidence and enjoyment.8 This is particularly relevant for children with CP, given the difficulties they face when engaging in PA. Therefore, parents’ perceptions of confidence, inclusion and enjoyment might help build up from previous international sport-based interventions, where adaptable and playful approaches were adopted and transferred to new contexts.14 15 These insights underline the critical role of parents as both advocates and informants of their children’s needs and experiences, reinforcing the importance of capturing parental perceptions when evaluating interventions.

Recently, a new approach using living-labs was attempted to design PA opportunities for children with CP.9 This approach incorporated an interactive feedback process to refine the programme and promote sustainability.16 Pattison et al developed the living-labs using non-linear pedagogy principles, underpinned by ecological dynamics, to create environments that encouraged children to develop movement solutions tailored to their own abilities and the challenges set by coaches.1 9 17 In their intervention, football was used as the primary activity, incorporating gamification and storytelling to enhance social interaction, confidence and enjoyment, while also offering additional health benefits across different populations.18,20 Football was selected not only because of its popularity but also due to its flexibility as a fun and social game that can be adapted to participants of all ages, skill levels and socioeconomic backgrounds.21 22 Research has demonstrated its positive impact on physical fitness, motor skills, psychosocial well-being, cognitive functioning and learning.15 23 24 Within clinical populations, football has further been described as a form of ‘medicine’, helping individuals sustain lifestyle changes through motivating and socially engaging activities.18,20 A further rationale for selecting football lies in its strong cultural role in promoting social integration. Football is widely accessible and familiar, providing a shared context that facilitates inclusion and peer interaction. Football-based interventions have been shown to enhance social capital and integration across age groups,22 making it particularly relevant for children with CP, who often face exclusion from mainstream activities. Consequently, football can act as an ideal vehicle for developing physical, psychological and social skills. However, the effectiveness of implementing this approach in a long-term capacity with children living with CP remains underexplored. In the context of our living-lab approach, the values of play, enjoyment and status emerged strongly in children’s reported experiences, while parental perspectives reflected values of comfort and fear.25 These findings align with previous research in adult CP populations, which has identified a lack of evidence on how PA programmes affect daily functioning, social confidence and emotional wellbeing.8 This highlights intergenerational differences in how inclusive participation is understood and needs to be further explored following a long-term intervention.

As established in the research by Pattison et al,9 parents play a significant role in the lives of children living with CP, gaining a unique insight into their children’s lives. However, to the authors’ knowledge, no previous research has specifically explored parents’ perceptions of the benefits of a living-lab approach for children living with CP from a physical literacy perspective. In line with the Physical Literacy Consensus Statement for England,26 we refer to physical literacy as ‘our relationship with movement and PA throughout life’, which is shaped by how an individual moves, connects, thinks and feels, and by their environment, culture and experiences. Within this multidimensional framework, motor competence and technical skills are considered fundamental elements of physical literacy, but the concept is broader and includes psychological, social and affective domains. This distinction is particularly important as it is difficult to measure reliably and validly the impact of living-labs on physical aspects, since motor competence assessments and technical skills are often not tailored to children living with CP, making it challenging to capture these fundamental elements of physical literacy. Therefore, parents (ie, those who see everything their child has engaged in in terms of PA) are vital to understanding the success of an intervention. This study, therefore, addresses a significant gap in the literature and offers a novel contribution to the field, since the impact of football living-labs for children with CP has not been explored and how it could affect the various domains of physical literacy.

Understanding parents’ perspectives is crucial for assessing the effects of living-labs on children’s daily lives from a physical literacy approach. Given this, living-labs may serve as an effective platform for promoting PA opportunities that are appropriately tailored to the unique needs of children with CP. The primary aim of this study was to explore parental perceptions of the benefits of a 20-week football living-lab approach for children with CP in various physical literacy domains.

Methods

Participants

Seven parents from the West Midlands, UK, agreed to take part in the semistructured interviews (two males and five females, aged 30–45 years). The children of the interviewees (mean±SD age: 7.3 ± 1.83 years) included six diagnosed with CP classified as GMFCS (Gross Motor Function Classification System) levels I–III, and one child diagnosed with spina bifida, a condition in which the spine and spinal cord do not develop properly during pregnancy, resulting in a gap in the spine. The diagnoses are different, the two conditions are congenital and reflect similar neuromotor limitations;27 hence, we felt it appropriate to include the parent of said participant in the study. Although our primary focus was on motor function classification, it is important to note that children with CP may also experience associated psychological or cognitive challenges, either directly linked to cerebral damage or indirectly arising from the impact of motor impairments.

Living-lab protocol

The living-lab programme consisted of one session per week, each lasting 1 hour, over a 20-week period, delivered at an indoor sports hall in Coventry, West Midlands, UK. The location was organised to account for bad weather and to create an even surface suitable for walking frames. The living-labs were informed by Football is Medicine19 and by non-linear pedagogy principles.28 The non-linear pedagogy principles helped design a more inclusive environment where children could work with their siblings and other children with CP or independently to achieve certain game-based tasks. The games were designed to incorporate football-related games following recommendations to facilitate children’s fundamental movement skills and physical literacy.29 30 The programme was designed on the theoretical concepts of Constraints-Led Approach,31 Managed Chaos,31 Ecological Dynamics,17 Analogy Learning,30 Affordances32 and Teaching Games for Understanding.33 The activities were designed to be football-related, alongside developing fundamental movement skills and physical literacy, and often had a (non-football related) theme to further engage children.9 By adopting this non-linear approach to coaching and learning, it enabled children to contribute to the development and progression of the games, capturing the essence of the collaborative living-lab approach.9 28

Patient and public involvement

Parents and siblings of children with CP were actively involved in the design and delivery of the living-lab programme, contributing to the co-development of session content, themes and feedback processes. Their insights were used iteratively to refine the football activities and ensure they were inclusive, engaging and tailored to the needs of participants. While parents were not involved in formulating the original research questions, they played a central role in the intervention’s development and its contextual adaptation.

Qualitative procedures

To examine parental perceptions of the benefits of the programme on their child’s development, we conducted semistructured interviews, one-to-one and face-to-face. The interviews lasted between 13 and 32 min, with an average interview time of 24 min. The interview guide was developed and piloted to explore differences in children’s physical literacy domains34 35 (interview guide in online supplemental Appendix 1), aiming at capturing noticeable psychological, physical and cognitive changes in their development as well as their PA habits and enjoyment of the programme. The interviews were conducted (by LV and RP, trained female PhD students) in a calm room in the university where the activities were taking place. Field notes were taken, and previous views were noted before the interviews, so the interviewers could reflect on potential bias.

The interviews were recorded using a Dictaphone (Olympus DS-2400, digital voice recorder) and transcribed verbatim into Microsoft Word. Data saturation was achieved when there was enough information to replicate the study, no new data emerged/no further coding was feasible or new themes could be identified and the final sample size of n=7 was in line with the sample size guidelines (5–25) for phenological36 studies.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was conducted according to the guidelines,37 and pen profile diagrams were created to explore parental perceptions on the 20-week football programme.37,41 The lead author transcribed all interviews verbatim and became familiar with the data (data was coded and anonymised and stored at the institutional cloud). Following this, initial codes were generated, and a deductive approach was used to develop the themes and create a pen profile diagram. After the coding and the themes were defined, the team met as a whole and (1) did a group sense check of the data to ensure trustworthiness;42 (2) reflected on the data and generated additional insight through critical discussions to debate and redefine themes; (3) to ensure trustworthiness, quality and credibility, analyst triangulation was used; and (4) reflexivity, including friendly discussions and field notes (predata collection, during and post data collection), was employed to increase the study’s rigour.37 This facilitated the debate and redefinition of the themes during critical discussions.

To ensure clear and detailed reporting, we followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research and Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research.43

Results

Findings were summarised into one pen profile diagram (figure 1). Within the pen profiles, all parents involved in the study recognised the benefits of the living labs for their children’s physical literacy development. Figure 1 demonstrates that the programme was deemed inclusive (n=6) and had an adaptable environment (n=6). Parents highlighted the adaptable nature of the programme (n=6), which allowed children with varying levels of mobility to participate. One parent mentioned, ‘There are children in walkers, sticks, you know, but everyone joins in’ (P7), reinforcing how the environment was designed to be inclusive regardless of each child’s physical characteristics. Parents suggested that the programme was beneficial for children to be involved and have fun with other children living with CP. They also highlighted the advantage of having a game situation that differs from mainstream activities, where children often struggle to be included and are left behind.

Pen profile diagram representing the parental perceptions of the living-labs, including themes and quotes. CP, cerebral palsy.

It was also mentioned that the programme complemented their physiotherapy (n=3). Parents noticed that children were so engaged in the activities that they did not realise they were doing physical work. This highlighted that the programme was more enjoyable and motivating compared with their one-to-one physiotherapy sessions. Additionally, parents noted improvements in their children’s competence (n=3), with some expressing that their child now had the confidence to try new activities. One parent mentioned, ‘He’s got the confidence now to try things’ (P2), highlighting the programme’s role in improving not only skill development but also confidence. Parents expressed a range of benefits, many of which aligned with the concept of physical literacy. In terms of confidence (n=7), parents also reported visible improvements in their children’s shyness and their ability to play freely in the living-labs. They mentioned seeing their children feeling more confident and less afraid of trying new things, as expressed by one child: ‘…nothing is stopping me now, Mum’ (P6).

Following the programme, parents observed an improvement in their children’s social interaction (n=7), suggesting that it was important for them to engage with other children living with CP and to make new friends while playing games they enjoyed. Overall, the living-lab approach was deemed successful and enjoyable (n=7) by all parents, who also suggested a potential expansion of the programme to other parts of the country.

Discussion

In the present study, we explored whether a football-based living-lab approach, delivered weekly for 20 weeks, was perceived as beneficial by parents of children with CP from a physical literacy perspective. Parents are often the primary facilitators and gatekeepers of activity participation, particularly for children with physical disabilities.9 12 As such, understanding their perspectives is essential for evaluating both the perceived value and sustainability of novel PA approaches. The findings suggest that this living-lab model was perceived by parents as offering a range of benefits that go beyond the physical. Parents described improvements in their children’s confidence, motivation, physical competence and social interaction. Importantly, many of the reported outcomes were described holistically, encompassing psychological well-being (eg, reduced shyness), physical development (eg, posture, movement confidence) and social inclusion (eg, playing with peers with similar abilities). While parents did not explicitly use the term physical literacy, the themes that emerged closely align with its core domains,26 reflecting their children’s relationship with movement and PA throughout life, including how they move, connect, think and feel, as well as the influence of their environment, culture and experiences.

The football-based living-labs were designed using game-based and inclusive pedagogical strategies (ie, storytelling), as outlined in Pattison et al.9 Unlike traditional physiotherapy, the living-lab approach offered a playful (ie, gamified) and socially supportive space where children could explore movement in ways that were enjoyable and relevant to them, while engaging with siblings and peers. This resonates with broader literature on ecological dynamics and non-linear pedagogy, which suggests that learning and development are best supported in environments that encourage individual exploration, social interaction and adaptability.17 The features of exploration and interaction were supported through the implementation of analogy learning,30 through the creation of storyline components to games, affording children the opportunity to externalise their attentional focus instead of concentrating on the conscious control of their body.28 30 44 Creating an external focus of attention (eg, a competitive game or a themed game, such as pirate ships) which engages and motivates children to participate can help to develop their intrinsic motivation for being active.9 Additionally, the value of intrinsic motivation shared by parents aligns with the message from Krustrup et al,18,20 suggesting it is ‘time for patients to play’, with motivating and social activity making it easier for patients to make lifestyle changes. Our previous findings also support emerging evidence that a one-off living-lab approach can help initiate PA engagement among children with CP. However, our study expands on this by providing insights into the longer-term (ie, 20 weeks) perceptions of a weekly living-lab programme. This sustained involvement appears to promote broader benefits over time, and parents expressed strong support for expanding the model to a longer duration and other areas of the country. The sustained nature of the programme may be particularly important for promoting physical literacy, as repeated exposure enables children to build confidence and competence progressively. Our findings also raise important considerations for the scalability of inclusive community interventions. Scaling often emphasises that interventions often fail to replicate impact beyond pilot stages when contextual adaptability and core success factors are not maintained.45 46 The living-lab model, by embedding co-design, adaptability and community responsiveness, reflects several of these success factors. While our study examined a single programme, the parental emphasis on inclusivity, enjoyment and sustainability highlights conditions that may inform how such approaches could be responsibly scaled in other contexts.

A further consideration is that several parents noted that their children were unaware they were doing physical work, a reflection of how intrinsic motivation and engagement can be promoted through gamified activities. This aligns with Self-Determination Theory, which emphasises autonomy, competence and relatedness as key drivers of motivation and long-term behavioural engagement.47 The value of PA interventions for children living with CP is well established, with research highlighting benefits across physical, social and emotional domains.8 13 14 However, existing intervention research tends to resemble physiotherapy, often delivered in one-to-one clinical formats that prioritise rehabilitation over enjoyment or self-directed exploration.9 While these approaches have clinical value, they may fall short in promoting autonomy, social engagement and intrinsic motivation, critical components of long-term PA participation and holistic development.48 Our findings suggest that gamified, group-based approaches not only support physical improvements but also promote meaningful social connections and sustained engagement, which could contribute to the development of lifelong PA habits. Furthermore, by involving parents and children in the design and delivery of the programme, the intervention was tailored to better meet participants’ preferences and abilities, enhancing inclusivity and relevance and providing practical recommendations for designing and delivering living-labs for children living with CP.

Strengths

A key strength of this study lies in its focus on parental perceptions, which are vital given the challenges in obtaining reliable and valid motor competence or technical assessments for children with diverse needs, including those with CP. Children were not included in the interviews due to the extended duration of the intervention, which may have made it difficult for them to accurately recall their experiences. Also, younger children might struggle to articulate their thoughts and feelings in a way that provides meaningful insight for qualitative analysis. Additionally, parents highlighted crucial aspects that define a successful intervention, offering practical guidance that coaches, policymakers and practitioners in disability sport can use to improve future programmes. Moreover, the study’s findings extend beyond football-specific outcomes, revealing broader benefits related to confidence, motivation, social inclusion and psychological well-being. This suggests that the pedagogical and philosophical approaches underpinning the living-lab model, such as gamification, inclusive design and non-linear pedagogy, could be effectively adapted for other settings, including school physical education and multisport interventions.

Lastly, the long-term nature of the 20-week programme allowed exploration of sustained engagement and evolving benefits over time, which builds on previous research focused on shorter interventions. This duration provides valuable insights into how repeated exposure can progressively enhance physical literacy and intrinsic motivation in children living with CP.

Limitations

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study’s reliance on parent reports means the children’s perspectives were not directly captured. While this was a pragmatic choice given the participants’ young age and potential communication challenges, future research should prioritise child-centred qualitative methods, such as draw-and-tell or photo elicitation, to ensure the voices of children with CP are heard and represented.

A further consideration is that the reliance on parent reports does raise the potential for bias, including social desirability and selective recall. Furthermore, the researchers’ positionality, as advocates for inclusive PA, may have influenced both the interpretation of data and the facilitation of interviews. We sought to mitigate these risks through reflexivity, field notes and analyst triangulation, but they remain important considerations for transparency.

Practical application

The flexible and inclusive environment created through this games-based living-lab approach, informed by non-linear pedagogical perspectives, may help other coaches and practitioners create environments that promote the facets of physical literacy, which are essential for supporting long-term participation in PA. In addition, we produced ‘A Practical Guide for Coaches and Clubs’, which can be found in online supplemental Appendix 2, to support wider application of the programme.

Conclusion

This study contributes novel insights into how a football-based living-lab approach is perceived by parents of children with CP. It highlights the potential of this model to support a more holistic and meaningful form of PA that aligns with the principles of physical literacy, with parents particularly noting improvements in competence, confidence, motivation, enjoyment and social interaction, thereby promoting long-term engagement. Further research is warranted to examine the perspectives of children themselves and to assess the impact of such interventions across different settings, across different domains of physical literacy and across different age groups.
