Global trends and inequalities in hypertensive and ischemic heart disease attributable to high body mass index: A systematic analysis from 1990 to 2021 with projections to 2035
Authors: Xinyu Liu, Xu Zhang, Cheng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertensive heart disease (HHD) and ischemic heart disease (IHD) are major global burdens. High body mass index (BMI) is a key modifiable risk factor, but the global burden, trends, and inequalities of HHD and IHD attributable to high BMI remain poorly defined.MethodsWe analyzed Global Burden of Disease data from 1990 to 2021 across 204 countries, estimating age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs), disability-adjusted life-year rates (ASDRs), and annual average percentage changes (AAPCs). Additional analyses included age- and sex-specific distributions, socio-demographic index (SDI) patterns, frontier and decomposition analyses, inequality metrics, and projections to 2035 using Bayesian age–period–cohort and ARIMA models.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, global ASMR and ASDR for HHD attributable to high BMI slightly increased (AAPC: 0.014 and 0.099), whereas those for IHD declined (AAPC: −0.067 and −0.751). The highest HHD burden occurred in Southern Sub-Saharan Africa, and the greatest IHD burden in Eastern Europe. Elderly females had higher HHD mortality and DALYs, while middle-aged males bore greater IHD burden. HHD inversely correlated with SDI (r = −0.5524, P < 0.001), whereas IHD showed a unimodal relationship. Decomposition highlighted aging and population growth as major contributors. Inequalities worsened for HHD but improved for IHD. Forecasts suggest continued rises in HHD and declines in IHD by 2035.ConclusionsHHD and IHD attributable to high BMI show divergent global trends and inequality patterns, underscoring the urgent need for targeted weight management and cardiovascular risk reduction, particularly in low- and middle-SDI regions.Keywords: High BMI, Hypertensive heart disease, Ischemic heart disease, Global burden of disease, Health inequalities

Introduction

The dual pandemic of obesity and cardiovascular disease (CVD) presents a significant challenge for health systems worldwide. Currently, more than 2.1 billion adults are classified as overweight or obese [1], leading to a concerning rise in CVDs such as hypertensive heart disease (HHD) and ischemic heart disease (IHD), especially in low- and middle-income countries that are experiencing rapid changes in nutrition [2]. Excess body fat contributes to various health issues, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation, all of which can worsen the progression of HHD and IHD [3,4]. Despite improvements in the prevention and management of CVDs, the prevalence of BMI-related cardiovascular issues continues to increase, revealing significant disparities across different socio-demographic groups [5]. Nevertheless, there is a lack of comprehensive global studies that evaluate the long-term effects of high BMI on HHD and IHD, particularly regarding future projections.

With the global rise in obesity rates, HHD and IHD has become major challenges for healthcare systems worldwide. Hypertension, a common comorbidity of obesity, independently increases the risk of cardiovascular events [6]. In 2019, an estimated 1.1567 million deaths were attributable to HHD, with the majority concentrated in underdeveloped regions [7]. At the same time, from 1990 to 2021, age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) for IHD decreased by an average of 1.3 % per year, with the improvement largely concentrated in resource-abundant regions [8]. Previous studies have reported that global DALYs and deaths from high BMI-related CVDs more than doubled from 1990 to 2019, while age-standardized rates (ASRs) declined only slightly (EAPC: −0.18 for DALYs, −0.43 for deaths) [6]. IHD, stroke, HHD, and atrial fibrillation/flutter were the main contributors, with IHD and stroke leading DALYs and deaths [6]. However, these analyses covered trends only up to 2019 and grouped all high BMI-related CVDs, limiting insights into HHD and IHD specifically. Given the variation in BMI-related cardiovascular risk across age, sex, and geographical regions, and the influence of socio-economic development, healthcare access, dietary patterns, physical activity, and urbanization [8,9], obtaining comprehensive and up-to-date insights into the burden, epidemiological characteristics, and future trends of HHD and IHD is critical. To address these gaps, our study uses GBD 2021, focusing on HHD and IHD to reveal subtype-specific patterns across age, sex, and regions, and includes projections to 2035 to identify subtype-specific patterns and inform targeted prevention strategies. Such insights are essential for public health experts and policymakers to develop effective interventions to mitigate the burden of HHD and IHD linked to high BMI.

To gain an up-to-date and comprehensive understanding of the HHD and IHD burden attributable to high BMI, we utilized the latest data from the GBD study. This analysis examined the burden of HHD and IHD across different regions, age groups, sexes, and socio-demographic index (SDI) levels from 1990 to 2021. We systematically analyzed ASRs for 204 countries or 21 regions, along with the average annual percent change (AAPC). Additionally, we projected the future burden of BMI-related HHD and IHD through 2035, considering demographic shifts and evolving disease patterns. By identifying high-risk populations and forecasting future trends, this study aims to inform more targeted global health policies and intervention strategies to mitigate the growing cardiovascular impacts of obesity.

Methods

Data sources

This study utilized data from the GBD 2021 dataset, accessible via the GBD Results tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). The dataset covers the burden of 371 diseases and injuries and 88 risk factors across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021 [10], providing a comprehensive overview of global mortality, disability, and risk factor impacts.

The GBD divides the world into 21 geographic regions based on shared characteristics to facilitate regional analysis [11,12]. The SDI serves as a robust predictor, reflecting the socio-demographic status of a nation, and exhibit a strong correlation with human health development outcomes [11]. Following the classification of these countries and territories by their SDI, five distinct regions were delineated: high, high-middle, middle, low-middle, and low. We extracted data from 204 countries and territories on mortality, sex, age, and ASRs for HHD and IHD attributable to high BMI from 1990 to 2021. Our analysis focused on quantifying the impact of high BMI on HHD and IHD burden across countries, regions, and SDI levels using mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and ASRs.

Statistical analysis

This study analyzed estimates from the GBD 2021 dataset, covering global, regional, and national prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of HHD and IHD attributable to high BMI, stratified by sex, location, 5-year age groups, and year. DALYs integrate years lived with disability and years of life lost, reflecting both premature mortality and reduced quality of life [13].

The ASRs for HHD and IHD, including ASMRs and ASDRs, were calculated using the GBD 2021 global population standard per 100,000. Joinpoint regression analysis (Joinpoint Regression Program, National Cancer Institute, USA) was used to assess annual trends in ASMRs and ASDRs from 1990 to 2019. The model fitted log-linear segments allowing for significant changes in trend (joinpoints), with annual percent changes (APCs) and their 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) estimated for each segment. The AAPC summarized the overall trend. The time interval between data points was one year, and trends were considered significant when the 95 % CI did not include zero [14].

The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the association between the SDI and ASMRs or ASDRs, employing Gaussian process regression for modeling data from 1990 to 2021 [15].

Predictive analysis utilized the Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model to project future patterns of high BMI-related HHD and IHD over the forthcoming decade, effectively incorporating age, period, and cohort effects to address demographic transitions [16] and providing a comprehensive framework adept at managing data uncertainty and complexity [17,18], with predictions derived through integrated nested Laplace approximation packages. Sensitivity analyses were performed with the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model to validate the robustness of projections. The BAPC model assumes additive and linear age, period, and cohort effects, and that past trends reasonably inform near-future projections, while the ARIMA model assumes stationarity and does not account for sudden external shocks. All analyses were performed in R software (version 4.4.1).

Frontier analysis assessed the potential for reducing disease burden relative to socio-demographic progress by benchmarking the lowest achievable ASDRs for HHD and IHD against SDI, identifying underperforming regions [19].

Decomposition analysis [20] elucidated the impact of demographic changes, such as shifts in age structure and population growth, on the burden of HHD and IHD attributable to high BMI, allowing for the identification of independent contributions from these factors.

Cross-country inequality analysis evaluated health disparities using the SII and concentration index, as delineated by the WHO, quantifying disparities in the distribution of high BMI-related HHD and IHD burden across countries and territories, with the SII obtained by performing robust regression analysis of DALY rates against the SDI using the midpoint of the cumulative population distribution arranged by SDI, and the concentration index computed by evaluating the area beneath the Lorenz curve, juxtaposing cumulative DALYs against the cumulative population distribution ranked by SDI.

Results

Global burden analysis of HHD and IHD attributable to high BMI from 1990 to 2021

From 1990 to 2021, the global burden of HHD attributable to high BMI increased, with ASMR rising from 6.83 to 7.21 per 100,000 and ASDR from 144.72 to 147.33 per 100,000 (Fig. 1A–B, Table 1). The AAPCs for ASMR and ASDR were 0.014 and 0.099, respectively (Fig. 1C–D). In contrast, the burden of IHD declined during the same period. ASMR decreased from 13.78 to 11.71 per 100,000, and ASDR from 300.78 to 277.65 per 100,000 (Fig. 1A–B, Table 2), with AAPCs of −0.067 and − 0.751, respectively (Fig. 1E–F).

Differences in burden of HHD and IHD attributable to high BMI cross age and sex

HHD deaths attributable to high BMI peaked in females aged 85–89 (51,252 cases) and males aged 70–74 (31,447 cases) (Fig. 3A). The ASMR of HHD increased with age, with females exhibiting higher rates than males from age 75 onwards (Fig. 3B, Table S1). Regionally, Central Sub-Saharan Africa had the highest female ASMR and ASDR, while in males, the highest ASMR was in North Africa and the Middle East and the highest ASDR in Southern Sub-Saharan Africa (Fig. 3E–F, Table S2).

Eight countries showed particularly high ASDRs: Bulgaria, Lesotho, Eswatini, Cook Islands, Bahamas, Egypt, Guyana, and Afghanistan (Fig. 4B).

Decomposition analysis of HHD and IHD burden attributable to high BMI

Globally, deaths and DALYs from HHD attributable to high BMI increased markedly over the past 30 years, with the largest rise in deaths seen in low-middle SDI countries and DALYs in middle SDI countries (Fig. 6A–B). Aging, population growth, and epidemiological changes contributed 38.13 %, 58.42 %, and 3.45 % to the global increase in HHD deaths, respectively (Fig. 6A, Table S5). In low-middle SDI countries, these contributions were − 291.49 %, 114.97 %, and 276.52 %, respectively. For global HHD DALYs, aging, population growth, and epidemiological changes accounted for 32.39 %, 66.88 %, and 0.73 %, respectively, with the largest effects observed in middle SDI countries (58.61 %, 70.58 %, and − 29.19 %) (Fig. 6B, Table S6).

During the same period, deaths and DALYs from IHD attributable to high BMI also rose significantly, with the greatest increases in middle SDI countries (Fig. 6C–D). Aging, population growth, and epidemiological changes contributed 45.46 %, 76.91 %, and − 22.37 % to global IHD deaths, respectively (Fig. 6C, Table S7). Health inequality analysis revealed significant absolute and relative income disparities in the DALYs burden of HHD attributable to high BMI (Fig. 7A–B). The SII for HHD ASDR was −190.28(95 % CI: −260.60, −119.96) in 1990 and − 230.80(95 % CI: −302.40, −159.20) in 2021 (Fig. 7A), while the DALYs concentration index changed from −0.16 (95 % CI: −0.21 to −0.09) to −0.17 (95 % CI: −0.23 to −0.11) over the same period, indicating widening inequality.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the global burden, trends, sociodemographic disparities, and future projections of HHD and IHD attributable to high BMI from 1990 to 2021. We found that age-standardized mortality and DALY rates for HHD increased, while those for IHD declined slightly over time. The burden varied markedly by age, sex, region, and SDI level: HHD burden was highest among older females in low-SDI regions, whereas IHD burden remained greater among middle-aged males in high-middle SDI settings. BAPC and ARIMA models projected a future decline in HHD but a rise in IHD. Decomposition and frontier analyses indicated that population aging and growth are key drivers of the observed burden, particularly for HHD, while improvements in epidemiological factors helped offset IHD trends. Health inequality metrics further revealed widening disparities in HHD and narrowing gaps in IHD.

Overweight and obesity have become major global health concerns, with their prevalence rising across all regions from 1990 to 2021. By 2021, an estimated 1 billion adult men and 1.11 billion adult women were affected worldwide [21]. The ASDR attributable to excess body weight has increased by over 15 % in the past two decades [22], contributing significantly to the growing burden of CVDs. Among these, HHD and IHD remain leading causes. In 2019, HHD accounted for 1.15 million deaths and 21.5 million DALYs globally, with the highest burden in middle-SDI countries—nearly triple that of high-SDI regions [7]. HHD burden rises with age and is more pronounced in females. In contrast, the burden of IHD has declined in terms of both mortality and DALYs [8], though this trend is mainly seen in high-SDI regions. IHD burden also increases with age but remains consistently higher in males. These patterns align with our findings.

Regional and sex disparities underscore the complexity of HHD burden attributable to high BMI. Central and Southern Sub-Saharan Africa, and North Africa and the Middle East, carry a disproportionately high burden, with females more affected than males. This is likely driven by a sharp rise in obesity in the North Africa and Middle East super-region—the largest increase among all GBD regions. From 1990 to 2021, male obesity prevalence in this region rose from 9.5 % to 36.2 %, and female prevalence from 23.7 % to 51.1 % [21]. In contrast, high-income regions, including High-income Asia Pacific and Australasia, exhibit lower HHD burden and smaller sex disparities, likely reflecting more effective obesity management and narrower sex gaps in obesity prevalence [21], as well as improved hypertension treatment and control [23]. Consistent with our findings, Xu et al. reported that in 2021, low-SDI regions had an age-standardized HHD mortality rate of 11.27 per 100,000—2.5 times higher than high-SDI regions (ASMR = 4.38 per 100,000)—with a continuing upward trend from 2014 to 2021 (APC = 0.47) [24]. Moreover, another study indicated that certain middle-to-high SDI countries in Eastern Europe (such as Ukraine, Belarus) exhibited markedly higher IHD ASMRs (252.89 per 100,000) than other regions with similar SDI, highlighting internal heterogeneity within SDI strata [25]. Sex-specific differences further underscore the need for targeted interventions. Liang et al. demonstrated that HHD prevalence peaks among women aged 75–79 years (RR = 3.44) compared with males of the same age group (RR = 2.95), and postmenopausal estrogen deficiency increases IHD risk by 98 % [26]. These biological insights support our results prioritizing elderly women for metabolic and cardiovascular interventions in regions with high HHD burden.

Although the global burden of IHD attributable to high BMI has declined, regions such as Central Asia, North Africa and the Middle East, and Eastern Europe continue to experience a relatively high burden, likely due to sustained increases in obesity prevalence [21]. Consistent with prior studies [8], low- and middle-SDI regions have shown rising ASDRs for IHD, reflecting persistent growth in obesity-related risk. In contrast, high-income regions, including High-income Asia Pacific and Western Europe, exhibit lower IHD burden, likely owing to effective prevention strategies, early intervention, and robust healthcare systems [27,28]. Previous research confirms this disparity, highlighting the benefits of timely metabolic risk management in resource-rich settings, while resource-limited regions face constraints in healthcare capacity and economic development [29,30]. Globally and regionally, males consistently show higher IHD-related deaths and ASDRs than females, a gap projected to widen over time [30]. These sex differences may result from both biological and behavioral factors, including higher smoking rates and psychological stress among men [31,32]. Targeted interventions addressing male-specific risk factors are essential to reduce IHD mortality and improve outcomes.

Age- and sex-specific analyses reveal distinct patterns in disease burden. HHD-related deaths attributable to high BMI are most common among individuals aged 70 years and older, while IHD-related deaths peak in those aged 60 years and above. The global trend toward population aging, driven by improvements in healthcare and increased life expectancy, highlights older adults as a key target for prevention and management strategies [33]. HHD requires particular attention in elderly females, whereas IHD prevention should focus more on middle-aged and older males.

Our analysis reveals distinct associations between SDI and the burden of HHD and IHD attributable to high BMI. A strong inverse correlation was observed between SDI and the ASDR of HHD, suggesting that higher SDI levels are linked to lower HHD burden. This may reflect improved hypertension control in higher-SDI settings [34]. In contrast, low hypertension control in certain populations may be driven by high sodium intake, physical inactivity, increasing obesity, poor medication adherence, and weak health systems. Contributing factors include limited primary care infrastructure, inadequate insurance coverage, high treatment costs, and insufficient blood pressure screening [35]. For IHD, the relationship with SDI followed a unimodal pattern: ASDR increased with SDI up to approximately 0.70, then declined in higher-SDI settings. This pattern may reflect the global spread of obesogenic environments, particularly in middle- and low-SDI regions, where processed foods rich in fat and sugar have replaced healthier dietary [36]. In contrast, high-SDI regions have achieved significant reductions in IHD burden through robust healthcare systems, comprehensive prevention efforts, and effective management of risk factors [27,28]. Notably, countries such as Nauru exhibit extremely high IHD ASDRs, likely due to genetic predisposition to obesity combined with rapid dietary transitions toward Westernized patterns [37]. Consistent with our findings, a recent study reported that in 2021, IHD ASDR continued to rise in middle-SDI regions (EAPC = 0.218) while declining in high-SDI regions (annual decrease 1.3 %) [26]. Zhou et al. showed that the age-specific mortality peak occurs 15 years earlier in low-SDI regions (62.5 years) than in high-SDI regions (77.5 years), reinforcing the need for earlier IHD interventions in low-SDI settings and highlighting middle-aged men as a priority group [25]. Collectively, these results underscore the critical importance of SDI-tailored strategies, including population-based interventions and early, targeted cardiovascular risk management, to reduce high BMI-related HHD and IHD burdens.

Frontier analysis revealed notable disparities in efficiency across countries with different SDI levels. For example, Bulgaria, despite having a relatively high SDI, showed elevated ASDR and inefficiency for HHD attributable to high BMI. Similarly, Nauru, a middle-high SDI country, exhibited high ASDR and effective difference for IHD. In contrast, several low-SDI countries demonstrated significant progress in reducing disease burden despite limited resources. These findings support the GBD theme that development level alone does not determine health outcomes [38]. With well-designed policies and efficient resource use, countries at any development stage can achieve meaningful reductions in disease burden.

Decomposition analysis highlights the significant impact of population aging and growth on the global burden of HHD and IHD attributable to high BMI. As life expectancy increases, the proportion of older adults rises, contributing to higher rates of obesity, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease [39,40]. The growing elderly population will continue to elevate the burden of HHD and IHD in the coming years [40,41]. These findings underscore the need to integrate demographic trends into long-term health system planning. Effective management of chronic diseases requires proactive strategies, strengthened prevention, and sustainable resource allocation to meet the evolving demands of aging populations.

In terms of inter-country inequality, the disease distribution map and cross-regional comparisons reveal that the burden of HHD and IHD attributable to high BMI is closely associated with the SDI, consistent with previous reports [7,42]. Regarding the SDI index, the ASDR for HHD are highest in regions with low SDI and lowest in regions with high SDI, while the ASDR for IHD are highest in regions with high SDI and lowest in regions with low SDI.

Projections to 2035 suggest a decline in HHD ASMR and ASDR, likely reflecting improvements in hypertension management. However, the continued rise in absolute deaths and DALYs underscores the counteracting effects of population growth and aging. In contrast, the projected increase in IHD ASMR and ASDR marks a reversal of prior declines and signals a potential public health concern. This trend may be driven by aging populations, demographic expansion, and rising obesity prevalence.

Our findings reveal marked regional and sex-specific disparities in high BMI-related HHD and IHD, highlighting the need for tailored interventions. In low-SDI regions, HHD burden is highest among elderly women, suggesting community-based waist circumference screening, postpartum weight management, dietary guidance, and aerobic exercise as priorities [43]. Middle-to-high SDI regions face rising HHD/IHD burden, requiring integrated metabolic surgery, pharmacotherapy, and multidisciplinary team management [43,44]. High-SDI regions illustrate the value of precision stratification to guide digital health interventions and combined pharmacologic therapy for high-risk patients [43,45]. Sex-specific strategies are crucial: women benefit from early intervention for gestational diabetes and PCOS, while men require focused management of central obesity and behavioral risks [43]. Globally, standardized evaluation metrics, equitable drug access, and AI-assisted risk prediction can enhance intervention effectiveness, address emerging comorbidities, and ultimately reduce health disparities.

This study has several limitations. First, data quality and availability varied across countries, potentially affecting the accuracy of estimates. In some low- and middle-income regions, limited epidemiological data and underreporting may have led to underestimation of HHD and IHD burden. Second, GBD estimates are based on modeling assumptions that may introduce uncertainty. Although robust statistical methods were applied, the results should be interpreted as the best estimates based on available evidence. Finally, the use of multiple analytical approaches was essential to enhance the robustness of our findings.

Conclusion

From 1990 to 2021, the global burden of HHD attributable to high BMI increased, while that of IHD declined. Significant disparities were observed by age, sex, and region—HHD burden was highest among elderly females in low-SDI countries, whereas IHD burden rose most in middle-SDI regions. Projections to 2035 suggest a modest decline in HHD but a resurgence of IHD, particularly in lower-income settings. Frontier, decomposition, and inequality analyses highlight the roles of demographic shifts and health system limitations. These findings underscore the urgent need for equity-focused, region-specific strategies to mitigate the cardiometabolic impact of high BMI, and for continued monitoring and evaluation of interventions to ensure their effectiveness and guide future public health policy.
