Protocol for somatic cell nuclear transfer in the common marmoset
Authors: Yoko Kurotaki, Yuko Yamada, Narumi Ogonuki, Atsuo Ogura, Erika Sasaki, Shogo Matoba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySomatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) in non-human primates is highly inefficient, as many reconstructed embryos arrest during preimplantation development. Here, we provide a protocol for performing SCNT in the common marmoset. We describe steps for oocyte collection, in vitro maturation, enucleation, and nuclear transfer using fibroblast or cumulus donors. We then detail procedures for oocyte activation, triple epigenetic enhancement, and embryo culture up to the blastocyst stage.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Matoba et al.1Subject areas: Developmental biology, Stem Cells, Biotechnology and bioengineering

Innovation

Generating cloned embryos in non-human primates remains technically challenging and inefficient,2,3,4 particularly in the common marmoset. Previous SCNT attempts in this species rarely produced embryos beyond the morula stage,5 and reproducible blastocyst formation has only recently been achieved.1,6 This protocol integrates several key innovations optimized for marmoset oocytes and somatic donors.

First, we describe a workflow that begins with the collection of GV-stage oocytes via ovum pick-up, followed by a reliable method for in vitro maturation to MII oocytes under low-oxygen culture conditions. Second, we provide procedures for enucleation and nuclear transfer including two complementary strategies: fusion-based SCNT using fibroblast donors and piezo-assisted direct nuclear injection using cumulus cell nuclei. Finally, we outline a strategy for triple epigenetic enhancement that combines TSA treatment, Kdm4d mRNA injection, and G9a/EHMT2 inhibition to reduce H3K9 methylation and improve nuclear reprogramming.

Together, these steps enable the reproducible production of cloned marmoset blastocysts suitable for downstream applications, such as nuclear transfer embryonic stem cell (ntESC) derivation or post-implantation developmental analysis. This protocol has been written to facilitate adoption by laboratories with prior experience in rodent SCNT that aim to extend their work to primate models.

Expected outcomes

This protocol enables the generation of cloned common marmoset embryos and blastocysts via SCNT using either fibroblast or cumulus cell donors. From a single hormonally stimulated donor female, several dozen GV-stage oocytes (typically 20–40) can be collected per ovary pair, although the yield varies depending on individual hormone responsiveness. Under optimized IVM conditions, approximately 30%–70% of GV oocytes mature to the MII stage, reflecting variation in hormonal responsiveness, follicular development, and oocyte quality among individual donors. When cytoplasm removal is minimized, survival after enucleation is typically very high (>90% and often approaching 100%).

Most enucleated oocytes can be successfully reconstructed using either fibroblast donors through HVJ-E-mediated fusion or cumulus cell nuclei by piezo-assisted injection. Under optimized conditions, fusion efficiency with fibroblast donors is typically very high and often approaches 100%. Following reconstruction and activation, the typical cleavage rate is approximately 70%. Blastocyst formation rates are calculated based on the total number of embryos subjected to in vitro culture. With triple epigenetic enhancement using TSA, Kdm4d mRNA, and a G9a/EHMT2 inhibitor, fibroblast-derived SCNT embryos reproducibly develop to the blastocyst stage at rates of approximately 10%–15%, consistent with our previous report. Cumulus-derived SCNT embryos generally exhibit slightly lower blastocyst formation rates of around 10%.

High-quality cloned blastocysts typically exhibit a clear blastocoel cavity and a compact inner cell mass comparable to fertilization-derived controls, although subtle differences in morphology may occasionally be observed. In contrast, reconstructed embryos that fail to undergo efficient epigenetic reprogramming often develop more slowly and arrest during the early cleavage stages, most commonly between the 2-cell and 8-cell stages.

As an optional validation step, the genetic origin of SCNT embryos can be confirmed using donor- and recipient-informative nuclear and mitochondrial DNA markers. SCNT embryos should carry the donor nuclear genotype without detectable recipient nuclear contribution, while their mitochondrial DNA is expected to be predominantly derived from the recipient oocyte. PCR- or sequencing-based analyses can be used to confirm donor nuclear origin and evaluate donor mitochondrial DNA carryover.

Limitations

Limited oocyte availability

This protocol requires ovaries from hormonally stimulated common marmoset donor females. As the number of obtainable oocytes per animal is inherently constrained by physiology and ethical considerations, experimental throughput is lower than in rodent SCNT systems.

High technical demand

Enucleation, nuclear transfer, and piezo-assisted injection in marmoset oocytes are technically challenging procedures that require extensive micromanipulation expertise. Laboratories without prior SCNT expertise may require a substantial training period before achieving consistent outcomes.

Incomplete reprogramming

Triple epigenetic enhancement with TSA, Kdm4d mRNA, and G9a/EHMT2 inhibition significantly improves blastocyst formation; however, it does not fully restore the developmental potential to that of fertilized embryos. Epigenetic and transcriptomic abnormalities may be present in cloned blastocysts, as suggested by the analysis of ntESCs.1

Species specificity

Culture conditions, gas composition, and treatment timings are optimized specifically for the common marmoset and may not directly translate to other primate species without further optimization.

Downstream steps not covered

This protocol ends at the blastocyst stage. Subsequent procedures, including embryo transfer, establishment of pregnancy, and derivation or long-term maintenance of ntESC lines, require additional protocols, specialized resources, and appropriate regulatory approvals.
