Exploring the relationship between psychological interventions and glycemic stability in youth with type 2 diabetes mellitus using machine learning methods
Authors: Jisun Park, Young Se Kwon, Ji-Eun Lee, Dong Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

To the editor,

Current guidelines from the American Diabetes Association and the International Society for Pediatric and Adolescent Diabetes strongly recommend psychiatric evaluation and appropriate interventions for children and adolescents with diabetes. Notably, psychiatric disorders often precede the onset of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and a large Korean cohort study showed that schizophrenia and bipolar disorder significantly increase the risk of T2DM in young adults [1]. Machine learning encompasses a range of statistical methods used to identify complex patterns and relationships within data, predict outcomes, and classify observations [2]. Therefore, we report findings from a machine learning-based analysis of the effects of psychiatric interventions in pediatric and adolescent patients with T2DM, demonstrating how computational approaches can reveal clinically meaningful patterns in glycemic variability that traditional statistical methods may overlook.

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using electronic health records from 111 pediatric patients aged <18 years with T2DM who were treated at Inha University Hospital from January 2010 to March 2024. Patients were divided into psychiatric intervention (n=15) and nonintervention (n=96) groups. The intervention group received comprehensive psychiatric evaluations, individual psychotherapy, and pharmacotherapy when indicated. Psychiatric interventions were tailored to individual patient needs following standardized screening. Patients received psychiatric evaluation and assessment for depression, with additional testing performed when necessary. Treatment typically involved weekly therapy sessions over 6 months, with varied approaches, including medication and family therapy, as appropriate. This diversity of treatment reflects the real-world clinical practice captured in our retrospective design. We excluded patients who were receiving psychiatric medication at the time of diabetes diagnosis or who had a prior history of diagnosed mental health conditions. Machine learning techniques, including linear regression, principal component analysis, and K-means clustering, were used to analyze intervention timing and glycemic outcomes. Linear regression was used to model the relationship between intervention timing and glycemic outcomes. The dataset was split 80/20 for training and testing, with cross-validation and feature selection applied. Model performance showed an R² of 0.78, indicating reasonable predictive capability [3,4].

Baseline characteristics were comparable between the intervention and nonintervention groups in terms of age at diagnosis (14.00±1.65 years vs. 13.80±2.19 years, P=0.684), sex distribution (P=0.851), initial hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels (9.92%± 2.93% vs. 10.11%±2.46%, P=0.812), and baseline body mass index-standard deviation score (2.34 vs. 2.44, P=0.83), minimizing concerns regarding selection bias. Although the overall difference in the latest HbA1c levels was not statistically significant (8.26%±2.26% vs. 7.76%±2.31%, P=0.434), Fig. 1 demonstrates a clinically meaningful pattern through violin plot visualization. The intervention group showed a more compact HbA1c distribution, with reduced glycemic variability compared with the nonintervention group.

The timing of psychiatric involvement also appeared meaningful. Patients who received psychiatric care within 180 days of diagnosis showed better glycemic trends (mean HbA1c, 7.29%±1.92%) than those with delayed intervention (9.37%±2.11%, P=0.094) (Table 1). Although this difference was not statistically significant, the observed trend suggests that earlier psychiatric evaluation after diabetes diagnosis may be more beneficial for subsequent diabetes management.

Patients with T2DM have heightened psychological risks due to multiple factors, including lifelong disease management, associations with obesity, complex treatment adherence challenges, and the possibility of serious complications [5]. Moreover, many pediatric patients with T2DM from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds face amplified mental health risks. Addressing psychological well-being in children and adolescents with T2DM is essential, as untreated mental health issues negatively affect diabetes management, treatment adherence, and quality of life [6].

Our study illustrated the distribution patterns of the latest HbA1c levels in the intervention and nonintervention groups using violin plots, revealing important insights beyond simple mean comparisons. Although the difference in mean HbA1c levels between groups was not statistically significant, the violin plot visualization showed that the intervention group had a more compact and stable distribution pattern. This finding suggests that psychiatric interventions may contribute to reduced glycemic variability, which is clinically relevant because HbA1c stability can be as important as absolute values in diabetes management. In addition, although our findings did not reach statistical significance, the observed trend toward improved outcomes with early intervention aligns with the broader literature emphasizing the importance of early psychosocial support in chronic disease management [7].

Although this study underscores the potential benefits of integrating psychiatric interventions into diabetes care, its small sample size, single-center design, and heterogeneous psychiatric interventions limit generalizability. In addition, the potential for overfitting in our machine learning models indicates the need for external validation. Nevertheless, our findings are hypothesis-generating and provide a compelling rationale for further investigation. We advocate large-scale, multi-institutional prospective studies using standardized psychiatric protocols to rigorously evaluate the impact of mental health integration on metabolic outcomes in pediatric T2DM.
