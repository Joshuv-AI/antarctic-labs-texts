Cold exposure increases aortic dissection risk through extracellular cold inducible RNA binding protein and toll like receptor 4 signaling
Authors: Hsiao-Ya Tsai, Wu-Chien Chien, Chi-Hsiang Chung, Chih-Yuan Lin, Yu-Juei Hsu, Min-Chien Tsai, Chin-Wang Hsu, Shih-Hung Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeveral observational studies have indicated a greater risk of cardiovascular diseases in cold weather. Cold-inducible RNA-binding protein (CIRP) is highly conserved and upregulated in response to various cellular stressors. Although CIRP provides protective effects against cold exposure, extracellular CIRP (eCIRP) is a danger-associated molecular pattern that can trigger inflammatory response. We aimed to explore the association between cold temperature and AD development, decipher the mechanistic links between cold stress and vascular cell injury, and provide a new therapeutic approach for cold stress-induced vascular emergencies. We used daily meteorological records obtained from the Taiwan Central Weather Administration and health insurance claims from the National Health Research Institute to examine the association between cold temperature and AD development. A murine AD model was established via treatment with the irreversible lysyl oxidase inhibitor 3-aminopropionitrile fumarate (BAPN). The mice were subjected to acute cold exposure (ACE) at 4 ± 1 °C. We found that cold stress and exogenous CIRP induced vascular inflammation and the overexpression of matrix metalloproteinase-2 through the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) pathway in endothelial cells in vitro. There was a greater risk of AD at cold temperatures. ACE increased the aortic arch diameter and circulating CIRP and interleukin-6 levels in BAPN-fed, AD-susceptible mice. Exogenous recombinant CIRP exacerbated AD in BAPN-treated mice. C23, a competitive CIRP antagonist, ameliorated ACE-exacerbated AD in BAPN-treated mice. In conclusion, cold temperatures are associated with AD development in a subtropical/tropical monsoon climate. Cold stress could exacerbate AD development through the eCIRP/TLR4 pathway. Introduction

The incidence of acute type A aortic dissection (AD) in the emergency department can reach 24.92/100,000 among different hospital types1. The mortality rate of nonsurgically treated acute type A AD patients can reach 47.3% in the first 24 h, with an hourly mortality rate of 2.6% after symptom onset; without immediate surgical intervention, the mortality rate of patients with ruptured AD can reach 80%2. Weakening of the media layers of the aorta leads to increased wall stress, induces aortic dilatation, and eventually results in intramural hemorrhage, dissection, and rupture3. Activated proinflammatory signaling pathways, the recruitment of inflammatory cells, matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) overexpression, the apoptosis of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), and endothelial dysfunction are proposed mechanisms for the development of AD.4

Global warming increases the frequency of extreme weather. Cold-related mortalities in cardiovascular emergencies are often worse in regions with warm climates than in those with cold climates5,6. Several observational studies have indicated that there is a greater risk of AD and ruptured thoracic and abdominal aortic aneurysms (TAAs/AAAs) in cold weather in continental climates.7,8 Lower temperatures are associated with increased sympathetic activity, acute-phase reactants, hypercoagulability, and decreased red cell deformability9. Increased inflammatory cells and plaque-associated neovascularization resulted in plaque instability in cold-acclimated hyperlipidemic mice10. Mechanistically, cold stress can lead to an increased metabolic rate and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production with oxidative stress injury, lipid peroxidation, and apoptosis11. In mice subjected to 4 °C cold, Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and bone marrow differentiation factor 88 (Myd88) are overexpressed, and nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-kB) is phosphorylated, which activates the apoptotic signaling pathway in the liver and increases the circulation of interleukin-1α (IL-1α), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)12.

Cold-inducible proteins upregulate the expression of antioxidant genes and alleviate oxidative stress13. Cold-inducible RNA-binding protein (CIRP) is a 172-residue nuclear protein comprising an N-terminal RNA recognition motif (RRM) domain and a C-terminal glycine-rich domain. CIRP is highly conserved, constitutively expressed in various tissues, and upregulated in response to various cellular stressors14. CIRP is consistently upregulated in various organs of different species in response to cold stress, hypoxia, ultraviolet radiation, glucose deprivation, superoxide, and even heat stress15. CIRP is rapidly induced during mild-to-moderate hypothermia16. Under stress, CIRP migrates from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, binds to its target RNAs, stabilizes mRNAs, and therefore facilitates their translation and preserves cell functions. Although CIRP provides protective effects against amyloid-induced apoptosis in neurons and cardiomyocytes via antioxidative effects and the suppression of mitochondrial apoptotic pathways, extracellular CIRP (eCIRP) is a danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) that can induce inflammatory cascades and oxidative stress.17–19 Accumulating evidence suggests that eCIRP could activate inflammatory signaling pathways involving TLR4, Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells‑1 (TREM‑1), and nuclear factor-kappaB (NF-kB). 20–22 eCIRP can also induce MMPs and promote inflammatory cell infiltration through the expression of intracellular adhesion molecules in a TLR4-dependent manner.23,24 TLR4 knockdown inhibited eCIRP-induced activation and inflammation of synoviocytes in patients with rheumatoid arthritis25. In contrast, the inhibition of CIRP with C23, a potent oligopeptide derived from CIRP, is protective in animal models of sepsis and acute lung injury26,27.

Previous studies have indicated the crucial roles of the endothelium in the pathogenesis and initiation of aortic aneurysm and dissection. Endothelial cells (ECs)-derived reactive oxygen species (ROS) and cyclophilin A secretion play a pivotal role in determining the susceptibility of the aortic wall to angiotensin II-induced effects28. As the vascular barrier, interactions involving ECs and inflammatory cells in the intima contribute to the initiation of AD.29 From the perspective of cold stress, endothelial dysfunction can be observed in an animal model of cold-induced hypertensive rats due to reduced endothelial nitric oxide synthase activity30. The released CIRP in shock can directly activate ECs and induce pyroptosis to cause lung injury31.

The association between cold temperature and AD has not been extensively studied in a subtropical/tropical monsoon climate setting, and the mechanistic links between endothelial dysfunction, cold temperature, and AD development have yet to be well established. The molecular mechanism involved in cold stress-exacerbated AD and the roles of cold response proteins in cold-induced vascular emergencies are still under investigation. We hypothesize that cold exposure could exacerbate TAA/AD via eCIRP-mediated endothelial dysfunction and vascular inflammation, and that an eCIRP blocker could ameliorate cold-stress-induced exacerbation of AD.

Materials and methods

Human data sources

The present study used daily meteorological records from the Taiwan Central Weather Administration (CWA) and health insurance claims from the National Health Research Institute (NHRI). The study period was from 2013/1/1 to 2018/12/31. Three major cities, namely, Taipei (including New Taipei, 25°2’51.94"N), Taichung (24°8’48.84"N), and Kaohsiung (22°36’58.54"N), which are located in the northern, middle and southern parts of Taiwan, respectively, were included in the study. A low-temperature alert is issued by the CWA when the temperature is expected to drop below 10 °C. More than 50% of the population of Taiwan resides in the study cities. The Taiwan National Health Insurance program has provided coverage for more than 98% of the 23 million people in Taiwan since 1996. The dataset contained scrambled identification numbers of beneficiaries, general information on gender, birth dates, health care received, diagnoses/claimable medical treatments for outpatient, emergency, and inpatient services (https://www.apre.mohw.gov.tw/). Disease diagnoses were coded according to the 9th revision of the International Classification of Diseases with Clinical Modification (ICD9 CM). The accuracy of the coding of several cardiovascular diseases in the NHIRD has been validated in several studies32. The annual extreme temperatures of the 0-10th percentile were the 10% of days with the lowest minimal temperature this year, and the 90-100th percentile was the 10% of days with the highest minimal temperature. The annual difference in temperatures in the 0-10th percentile was 10% of days, with a slight difference in temperature this year, and the 90-100th percentile was 10% of days, with the largest difference in temperature this year. The CWA provides 24-hour real-time weather data ( maximum temperature, minimum temperature, and average temperature) from 25 monitoring stations in Taiwan. The present study used daily weather data from the Taipei, Taichung, and Kaohsiung weather stations during the study period. This study evaluated the associations between the risk of AD and cold and fluctuating temperatures. All the data curation and analysis were conducted at the Health and Welfare Data Center, Taipei. This study was conducted in accordance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tri-Service General Hospital at the National Defense Medical Center in Taipei, Taiwan, which waived the need for individual informed consent because all the identified data were encrypted.

Gene expression omnibus (GEO) database and protein‒protein interaction (PPI) networks

We used publicly deposited microarray databases in the search. The National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GEO database was used to explore the expression of target mRNAs in clinical and experimental AD samples. The keyword “aortic dissection” was used in the search. Aortic tissues from healthy donors and AD patients in the same database were identified for further analysis. We analyzed the expression profile of CIRP mRNA in GSE84274 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE84274). The microarray platform for GSE84274 is GPL13534 (Illumina HumanHT-12 V4.0 expression bead chip), in which aorta samples from healthy donors and aorta samples from patients with AD were included33. The target mRNA expression profiles were analyzed via GEO2R. PPI networks were constructed via the STRING database (https://string-db.org/cgi/input.pl?sessionId=d57my53pHchw&input_page_show_search=on) on the basis of the protein-coding genes identified among the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The nodes represent individual proteins, and the edges represent known or predicted interactions. The interaction strength was indicated by the edge thickness and color intensity.

Cell cultures and reagents

Human telomerase-immortalized human aortic endothelial cells (TeloHAEC, ATCC, CRL-4052) were cultured in Vascular Cell Basal Medium (ATCC PCS-100-030), supplemented with Vascular Endothelial Cell Growth Kit-VEGF (ATCC PCS-100-041). Cold stress was induced by incubation at 22 °C for the indicated period34. Short interfering RNA targeting CIRP (siCIRP, Santa Cruz, s97329) and scramble were used for loss-of-function in vitro experiments. Transfection reagents were purchased from Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc. (siLentFect, #1703360), and Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc. (Lipofectamine 3000, L3000015). C23, a CIRP-derived peptide, and the recombinant mouse CIRP (rmCIRP) protein were synthesized and purchased from Leadgene Biomedical, Inc., Taiwan. Recombinant human CIRP (rhCIRP) protein was purchased from Abcam, Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA (ab106903).

Acute cold exposure (ACE)

ACE was performed as previously described with some modifications35. The mice were subjected to a cold room at 4 ± 1 °C and then placed at room temperature at 24 ± 2 °C for the remaining 12 h/12 h alternatively per day. The rectal temperature was measured using a thermometer (TM84D, Tenants, Taiwan).

Murine models of AD

All experimental protocols and procedures (IACUC-21-275) were approved by the institutional animal care committee of the National Defense Medical Center (Taipei, Taiwan) and complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health (8th edition, 2011). The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). The pathological basis for most AD is a dysplastic or mediolytic aorta, resulting in relative vulnerability to shear stress36. An AD mouse model was also established via treatment with the irreversible lysyl oxidase inhibitor 3-aminopropionitrile fumarate (BAPN, Sigma, A3134). BAPN treatment led to TAA/AD formation via the inhibition of lysyl oxidase activity, which is responsible for collagen and elastin crosslinking. Briefly, three-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (National Center for Biomodels, Taipei, Taiwan) were subjected to BAPN (1.0 g/kg/day) via gavage. Previous studies and our preliminary data indicate that three-week-old male C57BL/6 mice began to die during the second week when administered BAPN at 1 g/(kg/d) for four weeks. After 4 weeks of BAPN treatment, mice were randomly assigned to the different experimental settings. In this study, we use BAPN to precondition mice into AD-prone models and test whether acute cold stress could exacerbate AD. ACE began at the fourth week to evaluate the possible detrimental impact of ACE on TAA/AD formation in AD-susceptible mice. This experimental design aims to mimic acute cold stress, i.e., cold wave, which exacerbates cardiovascular emergencies in high-risk, susceptible individuals. For rmCIRP treatment, rmCIRP was given via retro-orbital injection at a dosage of 3.2 mg/kg/day for 7 days. For the CIRP antagonist treatment group, C23 (8 mg/kg/day) was injected into the mice retro-orbitally under adequate anesthesia for 7 days. A schema of the animal experiments is shown in Fig. 1. The sample size in each group was determined to be at least 30 according to the online sample size calculator (https://clincalc.com/stats/samplesize.aspx) and our previous studies. Given the high mortality before the experimental endpoint, those mice that survived to the end of the experiment could then be investigated for aortopathy and for the harvesting of fresh tissues; additional animals needed to be enrolled. The mice that died before the expected end of the experiment were autopsied immediately. Blood clots that were found in the thoracic cavities of these mice were considered dead due to AD37. Mice that survived at the end of the experiment were euthanized by exsanguination under anesthesia. The mice were considered adequately anesthetized (Zoletil 50 10 mg/kg)/xylazine 0.1 mg/kg, given intraperitoneally) when they did not attempt to withdraw a limb after applying pressure. Blood was then collected via cardiac puncture. After the periaortic soft tissue was carefully removed, the entire aorta was perfused with saline, digitally photographed in situ, and excised. The aorta was subjected to formalin fixation and paraffin embedding or freezing at −80 °C. The body weights of the animals were monitored during treatment to assess side effects. All experimental mice were fed ad libitum and maintained on a 12-hour/12-hour light/dark cycle.

Assessment of TAA/AD severity

After perfusion fixation with cold 4% paraformaldehyde, the aorta was exposed under a dissecting microscope, and the periadventitial tissue was removed from the aortic wall. The gross appearance of the aorta was photographed digitally, and the maximal external diameter of the suprarenal aorta was measured by imaging processing software (ImageJ). An outgrowth of > 50% indicated the development of an aortic aneurysm, as previously described38. The gross appearance of each aorta was assessed for severity of aneurysmal formation according to a scheme inspired by the classification system described by Daugherty and colleagues: normal aorta; type I indicates dilated aorta without dissection (between 1.25 and 1.49x average aortic diameter); type II indicates single aortic aneurysm (1.5x average aortic diameter or greater) without dissection; type III indicates AD, as indicated by intramural thrombus; multiple distinct aortic aneurysms (with or without dissection); and type IV indicates ruptured aorta. Two observers who were blinded to the animal groups independently evaluated the aneurysmal tissue. In cases of discrepancies, the observers discussed the cases and reached an agreement on the classification39.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

The perfusion-fixed aorta was embedded, cut into cross-sections (5 μm), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin and Verhoeff-Van Geisen (VVG) for elastin. The severity of elastin degradation was semi-quantified as previously described (grade 1, no degradation; grade 2, mild degradation; grade 3, severe degradation; and grade 4, presence of aortic rupture)40. The elastic degradation was reviewed and graded in a blinded fashion by 2 researchers. Once there was disagreement between the grades, the researchers reviewed and reached a consensus.

Immunoblotting

The whole-cell protein lysates were subjected to SDS‒PAGE followed by electroblotting onto a PVDF membrane. The membranes were probed with monoclonal antibodies against CIRP (Proteintech, 10209-2-AP), Myd88 (CST, #4283), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS, Abcam, ab136918), phosphorylation of p65 at Ser536 (pp65, CST, #3033), Caspase 1 (CST, #89332), MMP-2 (GeneTex, GTX104577), NLRP3 (GeneTex, GTX133569), and β-actin (GeneTex, GTX629630). Since the sample volumes and antibodies were limited, it was impractical to use a single antibody per membrane. The bands were visualized using chemiluminescent detection reagents on the cleaved membrane (Supplementary Material 1). Densitometric analysis was conducted using image-processing software (Multi Gauge, Fujifilm). Target protein expression measured by immunoblotting was quantified by densitometry and is expressed relative to an internal control or as phosphorylated protein relative to total protein. The data are expressed as the fold change relative to the control values.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Mouse serum was collected and analyzed by using a CIRP ELISA kit (MyBioSource, MBS7252333), IL-6 (BioLegend, 431307), and TNF-α (R&D Systems, MTA00B) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed independently at least three times, and all continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The F test for equal variance was performed before the group differences were analyzed. Comparisons between two groups were performed via Student’s t test. For multiple groups, the data were analyzed via either one-way or two-way ANOVA with interaction analysis. For post hoc analysis, a Tukey test was applied to correct for multiple comparisons, and a Fisher least significant difference test was used for planned comparisons. The associations between time-to-event outcomes were examined using the Kaplan‒Meier method with the log-rank test. Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05. Analyses were performed via a statistical software package (SPSS version 16.0 for Windows; SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA)41–43.

Results

Cold temperatures are associated with the occurrence of AD

Overall, the incidence of AD was 10.67 per 100,000. By matching the meteorological data from the CWA and the date of the diagnosis of AD from the NHIRD database, we found that compared with the remaining days, days in the lowest 10th percentile of daily minimum temperature (°C) had significantly increased rates of AD in the north (Taipei), middle (Taichung), and south (Kaohsiung) in Taiwan (Table 1). A discordant association could be observed between the temperature and the accumulated number of AD (Fig. 2A). Compared to normothermia at 25 °C, the relative risk of developing AD doubled when the temperature dropped to below 10 °C. (Fig. 2A and B). Days with lower temperature alerts (10 °C) had an AHR of 7.134 (CI 1.780-28.595, p = 0.006) in Taipei and an AHR of 1.028 (CI 1.021–1.035, p < 0.001) in Kaohsiung. Overall, we found acute cold stress, such as a cold wave, significantly increased the incidence of AD in a tropical/subtropical region.

Identification of target genes/pathways in the aortic tissues of mice subjected to ACE

The whole aorta was resected from mice subjected to ACE. RNA-seq was performed to identify potential molecular targets that mediate cold stress-induced vascular injury. A heatmap is shown in Fig. 3A. The CIRP expression and key molecules for aneurysmal formation and endothelial dysfunction, e.g., MMP-2 and iNOS were abundantly expressed in the aortic tissue in mice subjected to ACE (Fig. 3B). We also observed a trend toward increased CIRP expression in patients with AD compared to control subjects, but it did not reach statistical significance. (Fig. 3C). PPI network analysis revealed that TLR4 is the hub gene that mediates CIRP to activate several aneurysmal/dissection-prone and endothelial dysfunction molecules and pathways, i.e., MMP-2 and the NLRP3-caspase-1 inflammasome (Fig. 3D). Taken together, cold temperature is associated with the development of AD, and the eCIRP/TLR4 pathway could be one of the crucial mechanisms for AD development in cold weather.

CIRP mediates the cold stress-induced proinflammatory response and MMP overexpression in ECs in vitro

CIRP, Myd88, pp65, iNOS, MMP-2, NLRP3, and active caspase-1 levels were increased in TeloHAEC subjected to cold stress at 25 °C (n = 5, Fig. 4A). Exogenous rhCIRP, a surrogate of cold stress, also increased the phosphorylation of p65 and the expression of iNOS, MMP-2, NLRP3, and active caspase-1 in TeloHAEC in vitro (n = 5, Fig. 4B). C23, a CIRP competitive inhibitor, reduced the expression of pp65, iNOS, MMP-2, NLRP3, and active caspase-1 in ECs in vitro (n = 5, Fig. 4C). Taken together, these findings suggest that eCIRP mediated cold stress-induced endothelial dysfunction and inflammation and that blocking eCIRP with C23 can reduce cold stress-induced acute vascular injury.

ACE further exacerbates the severity of TAA/AD in BAPN-treated AD-prone mice

We then tested whether ACE could exacerbate AD development in BAPN-fed AD-susceptible mice. Consistent with a previous study, rectal temperature significantly decreased in mice subjected to ACE (Supplementary Fig. 1)35. As expected, the BAPN-fed mice were prone to develop TAA/AD than the vehicle-fed mice. ACE further increased the severity of TAA/AD in the BAPN-treated mice. Though ACE did not significantly increase the mortality rates in BAPN-treated mice (n = 40 in each group, Fig. 5A and B), ACE increased the ascending aorta and aortic arch diameter in the BAPN-treated mice (n = 10, Fig. 5C, D, and E). ACE also increased the incidence of and severity of TAA in the BAPN-treated mice (Fig. 5F and G). ACE induced increased levels of circulating eCIRP and IL-6 in the mice subjected to ACE (n = 5, Fig. 5H and I). Feeding with BAPN significantly increased the degradation of elastin (n = 10, Fig. 5J). Taken together, ACE exacerbated TAA/AD in AD-prone mice.

Exogenous RmCIRP exacerbates AD in BAPN-treated AD-prone mice

To further clarify the roles of eCIRP in cold-aggravated AD, we tested whether exogenous rmCIRP, a surrogate of cold stress, could exacerbate AD in BAPN-fed AD-susceptible mice. Compared with the vehicle, the administration of rmCIRP significantly increased the mortality rates of the BAPN-fed mice (n = 40) with increased ascending aorta and aortic arch diameters (n = 10, Fig. 6A–E). Exogenous rmCIRP increased the level of circulating IL-6 (n = 5, Fig. 6F). rmCIRP increased the incidence of and severity of TAA in the BAPN-treated mice (Fig. 6G and H). We observed a trend toward increased elastin degradation in BAPN-fed mice treated with rmCIRP, but the difference was not statistically significant. (n = 5, Fig. 6I). C23, a competitive CIRP inhibitor, ameliorates cold stress-exacerbated AD in BAPN-fed mice

To provide a rationale for the use of CIRP blockers as adjunctive medical treatments for AD, we then tested whether C23 could reduce the susceptibility of BAPN-fed mice subjected to ACE. C23 reduced mortality rates (n = 40) and the expansion of the aortic width (n = 10, Fig. 7A–E). Compared with those in the control group, the circulating TNF-α levels in the C23-treated group were lower (n = 6, Fig. 7F, G). We observed decreased elastolysis in the mice treated with C23 (n = 10, Fig. 7H). The expression levels of iNOS, NLRP3, pp65, MMP2, Myd88, and caspase-1, and phosphorylated p65 were reduced in the aortic tissues of mice treated with C23. (n = 5, Fig. 7I).

Discussion

In this study, we explored the association between cold stress and AD through big data analysis, identified the eCIRP/TLR4 pathway as a potential intervention target, and showed that a CIRP blocker could ameliorate cold stress-exacerbated AD in a murine model of AD. Specifically, we demonstrated that acute cold stress exacerbates AD in an AD-susceptible animal model, at least partly through eCIRP-mediated TLR4 activation.

We again revealed an association between cold temperature and AD development in a subtropical/tropical monsoon climate setting. With the increase in aging and climate change issues, timely preventive strategies or interventions for AD would benefit susceptible individuals. Decrease in temperature between neighboring days was proposed to be a modifiable risk factor that potentially eliminates a considerable proportion of AD onset44. Elucidating the chronological patterns of AD onset could potentially help the communities and health facilities provide susceptible individuals with preventive measures and preemptive treatment for AD. We observed a relationship between the temperature and the cumulative number of AD cases. The risk of AD development could be up to 2-fold greater when the temperature decreased to less than 10 °C than in normothermia (25 °C).

Our study demonstrated the roles of CIRP in the pathogenesis of AD and revealed that the eCIRP/TLR4 pathway is one of the molecular mechanisms for increased AD at cold temperatures. Here, we showed that exogenous recombinant CIRP could induce endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and MMP-2 overexpression and exacerbate AD in BAPN-treated AD-prone mice. Endothelial dysfunction is associated with aortic vascular inflammation in response to angiotensin II (AngII)-induced AD.28 Fibroblasts, ECs, and VSMCs are the sources of MMP-2 in AD.45 In the MMP-2-knockdown mouse model, reducing MMP-2 levels decreased the elastin degradation, leading to a reduced incidence and rupture of BAPN-induced AD46. In mice subjected to cold exposure, inflammatory cell infiltration and proinflammatory cytokines were significantly increased, MMPs were overexpressed, and collagen and VSMCs were depleted, resulting in increased atherosclerotic plaque size and plaque instability10,47.

Accumulating evidence suggests that the proinflammatory effects of eCIRP are mediated through TLR4. Consistent with previous studies, our results also revealed that the TLR4 pathway is crucial for TAA/AD. TLR4 is overexpressed in aortic tissues of patients with TAA/AD48. TLR4 levels were significantly higher in patients with AD than in healthy controls, with a significant positive correlation49. Nonetheless, MyD88 deficiency could also reduce AngII-induced AAAs and atherosclerosis independent of either TLR2 or TLR450. The cytotoxicity initiated by the eCIRP/TLR4 axis could lead to intestinal epithelial cell death and gut injury51. A murine model of acute pancreatitis had increased serum levels of CIRP52. In this study, exogenous recombinant CIRP induced a proinflammatory response both in vitro and in vivo. eCIRP can directly induce pyroptosis to cause acute lung injury in a murine model of hemorrhagic shock. Injection of CIRP can induce lung injury and local production of TNF-α and IL-1β in healthy mice31. eCIRP significantly increased neutrophil extracellular trap formation in bone marrow-derived neutrophils53.

The NLRP3 protein is highly expressed in the aortic tissues of patients with AD.54 IL-6 infusion can initiate macrophage accumulation and aortic dilation. Under elevated tension, IL-6 can be produced by aortic VSMCs55. In a murine model of AD, IL-6 produced by these neutrophils causes progressive destruction of the arterial wall structure. Blockade of IL-6 signaling can prevent post-dissection vascular remodeling56. Notably, we also detected elevated IL-6 in rmCIRP-challenged mice. IL-1β was also upregulated in both human AAD samples and a murine model of AAA. Administration of an anti-IL-1β antibody prevented elastin degradation and increased survival in a murine AAD model57.

We further provide new insight into adjunctive medical treatments for AD. Targeting therapies, small molecule inhibitors, and nucleic acid drugs are expected to be innovative therapeutic modalities for treating AA/AD41,58. C23 binds to TLR4 and competitively inhibits CIRP binding to the TLR4 receptor. The affinity of C23 for the TLR4/MD2 complex is one order of magnitude higher than that of CIRP.59. Therefore, CIRP is not expected to be downregulated by C23. Here, we demonstrated that C23 reduced AD development in AD-susceptible mice subjected to ACE. Inhibition of CIRP with C23 is protective in animal experiments in sepsis and acute lung injury26,27. Attenuation of NF-kB activation and ROS production can inhibit the progression of experimental AAA growth.58,60 Blocking CIRP by either CIRP gene knockout or systemic administration of C23 reduced ROS accumulation and pyroptosis52. Targeting eCIRP may provide a new preventive and therapeutic approach for AA/AD.

Limitations

The development of AD is multifactorial in origin. VSMCs, macrophages, and neutrophils also contribute to the pathogenesis of AD. Therefore, tissue-specific manipulation of CIRP could further elucidate its role in AD. CIRP is a nuclear protein that translocates to the cytoplasm in response to cold stress. In this study, we demonstrated that CIRP can be upregulated in aortic tissues and in ECs subjected to cold stress. Specifically, we measured circulating CIRP in the serum of mice subjected to ACE and demonstrated that eCIRP was upregulated. For this reason, previous studies on the roles of eCIRP in pathological conditions also measured whole-tissue, cell lysate, and serum levels of eCIRP. 22,24,61 In addition to TLR4, CIRP can trigger immune responses by interacting with triggering receptors expressed on myeloid cell-1 and IL-6 receptors, as shown in previous studies62. eCIRP can be internalized by ECs through a TLR4-mediated endocytosis pathway, leading to receptor-interacting protein kinase 3-dependent PANoptosis63. Other cold-response proteins and involved pathways should be further explored. Ultrasound is an ideal tool for evaluating the serial changes of the aorta in experimental aneurysmal formation. We believe that multiple approaches incorporating survival rates, external diameters, morphological changes of the aorta, and elastin degradation scales to assess the outcomes are crucial to represent the outcomes of the interventions. The determination of gelatinase activities could further elucidate the potential roles of cold stress on post-transcriptional regulation of MMPs. While our current data are limited to total MMP-2 protein levels, several prior studies have demonstrated that the total protein abundance of MMP-2 in human and experimental aneurysms correlates strongly with gelatinase activities.64,65.

Conclusion

Cold temperatures are associated with AD development in a subtropical/monsoon climate. Cold stress can exacerbate TAA/AD development through the eCIRP/TLR4 pathway. Blocking eCIRP prevents cold-induced exacerbation of TAA/AD in susceptible mice.
