Impact of anxiety, depression and online mindfulness on IBD patients’ quality of life: a web-based cross-sectional survey and randomized pilot study
Authors: Shurong Hu, Dingting Xu, Chunpeng Zhu, Yuqi Wang, Shuyan Li, Yan Chen, Caihua Wang, Yiping Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals with Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) often suffer from psychological comorbidities, including symptoms of anxiety and depression, which contribute to a substantial impairment in quality of life (QOL). The potential benefits of online mindfulness interventions for IBD remain unexplored.ObjectiveStudy 1 (cross-sectional survey): To establish correlations between the severity of depression and anxiety and the QOL in IBD patients. Study 2 (pilot study): To evaluate the feasibility and preliminary effects of a mobile-delivered mindfulness intervention on psychological symptoms and QOL/sleep quality.MethodsStudy 1: Anxiety, depression and QOL were measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), and Short Health Scale (SHS) respectively. They were assessed in 484 IBD patients. Regression analyses determined associations. Study 2: A randomized parallel design tested the Online MINdfulness-based stress reduction with Daily EXercise (MINDEX) program vs an active control (IBD education video) over 2 months. Sleep quality was measured using Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Feasibility was assessed via retention (30/36 intervention vs. 22/36 control completers) and all completion rates (25%); Preliminary effects via GAD-7/PHQ-9/SHS/PSQI changes.ResultsStudy 1: More than 80% of the patients had severe anxiety or depression. The study revealed a significant association between increased GAD-7 scores and SHS scores, with an R2 of 0.43 and coefficient of 12.6 (95% CI 9.4–15.7) for UC (Ulcerative Colitis) and R2 of 0.27 with a coefficient of 10.4 (95% CI 8.8–12.1) for CD (Crohn’s Disease). Similarly, higher PHQ-9 scores correlated significantly with higher SHS scores, with an R2 of 0.37 and a coefficient of 9.2 (95% CI 6.5–11.8) for UC, and an R2 of 0.22 with a coefficient of 7.2 (95% CI 5.9–8.6) for CD. Study 2: 30 MINDEX patients completed the intervention, and 22 control patients completed the control program. At two-month follow-up, no statistically significant differences were observed in GAD-7, PHQ-9, SHS or PSAI scores between the MINDEX group and the control group (0.052 < P < 0.908).ConclusionsStudy 1: Our findings indicate strong associations between psychological symptoms and QOL in IBD, while causal relationships require Longitudinal investigation. Study 2: While feasibility to implement this pilot trial found no significant short-term benefits of MINDEX on anxiety, depression, QOL, or PSQI over the 2-month study period. Larger trials with longer follow-up are needed to evaluate efficacy.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT04939948, Clinical Trial Registry June 17, 2021.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07426-7.

Introduction

Inflammatory Bowel Diseases (IBD), including Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), are chronic and recurrent inflammatory disorders characterized by significant morbidity and complications, such as bowel perforation or obstruction [1]. The global incidence and prevalence of IBD are on the rise, necessitating more interventions to manage this burden on patients’ QOL [2]. Patients with IBD commonly experience psychological symptoms, particularly depression and anxiety, at higher rates than the general population. Systematic reviews have reported pooled prevalence rates of 21-25.2% for depression and 19.1–35.1% for anxiety, with variations attributed to differences in assessment methods and cohort characteristics [3, 4]. A 2021 nested case-control study of 15,360 controls and 13,360 IBD patients revealed depression was more prevalent in pre-IBD populations up to five years before diagnosis, suggesting depressive symptoms with gastrointestinal manifestations may warrant earlier IBD screening [5]. The development of psychological symptoms in individuals with IBD may reduce QOL, complicate disease management, and amplify disease activity [6–8]. Nearly two-thirds of IBD patients with anxiety and approximately one-third with depression remain undiagnosed [9]. Untreated mental illness can have devastating consequences and may further worsen the course of IBD [9].

Numerous publications have explored the prevalence and risk factors linked to symptoms of anxiety and depression. These studies have highlighted that disease activity, new-onset disease, inpatient status, lower socioeconomic status, stressful life events, and medication side effects can significantly impact the psychological symptoms of patients with IBD [6, 7, 10]. While the importance of psychological comorbidity in IBD is increasingly recognized, and several studies have investigated the effects of depression and anxiety symptoms on patient outcomes, the causal relationship between symptom severity and QOL in IBD patients requires rigorous investigation [3, 4]. Specifically, quantitative evidence delineating how varying levels of depression or anxiety severity correlate with specific dimensions of QOL in this population remain relatively limited [3]. To address this gap, study 1 (cross-sectional survey) aimed to investigate the quantitative correlation between the severity of depression or anxiety and QOL using clinical data from a tertiary medical center.

Despite some patients having knowledge about anxiety and depression, they express serious concerns about medication treatment, particularly regarding adverse drug reactions and cross-reactions. Hence, the selection of a safe and effective approach to treat anxiety and depression becomes crucial. Several psychotherapy interventions, such as cognitive-behavioral techniques and mindfulness meditation, have been studied for patients with IBD [11]. These interventions targeting psychological distress have shown the potential to enhance QOL in individuals with IBD. Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) have been extensively investigated for their impact on chronic inflammatory conditions, including IBD [12, 13]. Offering online interventions allows more people with IBD to benefit from mindfulness training as it provides the flexibility to practice at home. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of MBIs through online programs and mobile applications, but evidence in IBD remains nascent [14, 15]. The Online Mindfulness-based Stress Reduction with Daily EXercise (MINDEX) program was based on the principles of Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) as described by Sipe and Eisendrath [16]. The intervention consisted of eight 30-minute video sessions, each focusing on key components of MBCT, including mindfulness meditation, cognitive restructuring, and stress reduction techniques [16]. The videos were developed by a team of clinical psychologists and mindfulness practitioners and delivered through a secure online platform. Therefore, Study 2 (pilot study) was designed to test the feasibility and preliminary effects of the MINDEX program on psychological symptoms and QOL/sleep quality.

Methods

Phase 1: cross-sectional survey

Design and setting

This study was a cross-sectional survey following the STROBE (STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology guideline [17]. The study was conducted at the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (SAHZU), spanning from June 2021 to December 2022 (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04939948, Clinical Trial Registry June 17, 2021).

Participants

All adult patients (at least 18 years old; had no prior experience with meditation) with a confirmed diagnosis of IBD from the SAHZU Crohn’s and Colitis Center were eligible to participate in the study. Participants who faced difficulties in understanding and executing instructions with illiteracy were excluded from the study.

Recruitment and screening

The participant flowchart outlines the instrument’s multistage development, validation, recruitment, and screening processes (Fig. 1). Data collection utilized an online questionnaire administered via Wenjuanxing, a survey platform; The online questionnaire was developed specifically for this study. To ensure content validity and cultural appropriateness, it underwent rigorous evaluation with five IBD specialists and 1 public health specialist. Experts independently assessed item relevance, clarity, and cultural sensitivity. Modifications included simplifying medical terminology, adjusting response anchors for local comprehension, and restructuring question sequence. Patients enrollment flow chat

To identify the required sample size, we performed a power analysis, with power greater than 0.8 and α < 0.05, a sample size of 450 participants per group was required. We anticipated a dropout rate of 10%, giving a total planned sample size of 500 participants.

A study information sheet was provided to all interested individuals that included details about the study objectives, procedures, risks, and benefits. To assess the participants’ ability to understand and execute instructions, we conducted a preliminary screening via a brief questionnaire. This questionnaire was designed to gauge the participants’ familiarity with the study procedures.

Baseline data collection

Disease activity: patients with IBD self-reported their disease activity using validated patient-based measures. For those with CD, the Harvey-Bradshaw Index (HBI) was utilized, and for patients with UC, the Patient-based Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index (P-SSCAI) was employed [18]. An HBI score greater than 4 or a P-SSCAI score of 5 or higher was classified as active disease, while an HBI score of 4 or lower or a P-SSCAI score below 5 indicated disease remission. Surgery for IBD referred to abdominal procedures (e.g., resection/colectomy) directly addressing IBD complications. Biologics included anti-TNFα agents, vedolizumab, or ustekinumab administered during the study.

QOL: participants completed the Short Health Scale (SHS), a 4-item self-management questionnaire validated for use in adult IBD patients [19, 20]. Each item corresponds to specific dimensions of QOL: symptoms, social function, disease-related worry, and general well-being. Responses were graded on a 100-mm visual analog scale, with scores ranging from 0 to 100, where higher scores represented worse QOL.

Anxiety and depression: participants’ anxiety and depression levels were evaluated using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [21, 22]. Each scale comprises 7 and 9 self-assessment items to gauge anxiety and depression, respectively. GAD-7 scores stratified as mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), and severe (≥ 15). PHQ-9 scores categorized as mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), and severe (≥ 15). All questionnaires were self-administered by participants through an online platform. A clinical researcher was available to provide assistance and clarify any questions regarding the items, if needed.

At baseline, we collected sociodemographic variables (age, gender, education level, marital status, and employment status) and clinical characteristics (IBD subtype [CD/UC], disease duration, current disease activity [HBI/P-SSCAI], history of IBD-related surgery, current biologic use, and concomitant medications) via self-report through the online survey platform concurrently with psychological and QOL measures.

Phase 2: randomized pilot study

Trial design

This study was conducted as a 2-arm, parallel-group, purely web-based randomized pilot study, following the CONSORT-EHEALTH (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials of Electronic and Mobile Health Applications and Online Telehealth) guideline [23, 24]. Recruitment occurred during routine clinical interactions at the SAHZU Crohn’s and Colitis Center. Eligible individuals received a study information sheet detailing objectives, procedures, and risks/benefits. Those who expressed interest underwent preliminary screening to confirm comprehension of instructions and study requirements, consistent with Study1 inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants underwent computer-generated randomization to ensure balanced 1:1 allocation between intervention and control groups. An independent researcher, uninvolved in study execution or participant contact, generated and managed the allocation sequence to minimize bias. The unblinded research team recruited eligible patients between October 2022 and February 2023, providing study information and addressing inquiries during enrollment.

As a pilot study primarily focused on feasibility parameters, a formal sample size calculation for efficacy was not performed. We anticipated a dropout rate of 10%, the target sample size of 90 participants (45 per group) was deemed sufficient to estimate feasibility metrics (recruitment rate, retention rate, adherence patterns, acceptability scores) with reasonable precision and to generate preliminary estimates of variability for clinical outcomes to inform future definitive trials.

Interventions

The mindfulness intervention consisted of standardized audio content adapted from evidence-based mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) programs, focusing on breath awareness, body scanning, and non-judgmental observation of thoughts/emotions. Content was developed by a clinical psychologist specializing in mindfulness for chronic illness and reviewed by two gastroenterologists for IBD relevance. Audios were delivered via a dedicated, password-protected section of the study platform, accessible anytime post-live stream. Participants received weekly reminder messages via WeChat and had access to technical support from a research assistant during business hours. The live-streamed sessions were facilitated by the clinical psychologist, providing introductory guidance. Participants were required to complete audio sessions lasting at least 30 min, three times per week, throughout the 8-week intervention period. The active control group received access to a single, 10-minute evidence-based educational video covering IBD pathophysiology, treatment options, and basic self-management (diet, medication adherence). The content was developed by the study gastroenterologists, reviewed for accuracy by an additional IBD nurse specialist, and hosted on the same platform as the intervention materials. Participants also completed questionnaires during the study, which took about 5 to 10 min to finish. Feasibility was assessed via three metrics: retention (proportion completing 8-week assessment), adherence (intervention group’s session completion tracked through self-reports/platform logs), and engagement (≥ 80% weekly questionnaire completion by both groups).

Compliance monitoring

To assess compliance and engagement with the MINDEX program, we employed multiple methods. Participants received personalized logins to access intervention materials on a dedicated online platform. Weekly self-reports documented listening days and session durations. Additionally, monthly support calls were provided exclusively to the intervention group to enhance engagement, address technical barriers, and answer program-related questions. These calls did not involve clinical advice or outcome assessments. Program completion included a final satisfaction and participation evaluation. This approach captured both quantitative and qualitative engagement metrics. Due to the nature of the mindfulness intervention, participants could not be blinded to their group assignment. However, all outcome assessments were conducted by assessors blinded to group allocation. Assessors analyzed data through an automated platform interface displaying only coded group identifiers. Data analysts were also blinded to group assignments to ensure objective interpretation of results.

Outcome measures

Sleep quality: the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scale (PSQI) was employed to assess sleep quality, encompassing subjective sleep quality, latency, duration, efficiency, persistence, use of sleep drugs, and daytime dysfunction [25]. The total scores on this scale range from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality.

The primary outcome of the study focused on patients’ QOL, as assessed electronically via self-report questionnaires. and reflected through SHS scores. Additionally, the secondary outcomes were targeted at improvements in anxiety, depression, and sleep quality, which were evaluated using the PHQ-9, GAD-7, and PSQI scores obtained from an online questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25.0). All analyses adhered to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, with participants analyzed according to their original randomization group irrespective of adherence. Missing data was handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), incorporating baseline characteristics and prior observations [26].

For Study 1: Between-group comparisons stratified by demographic/clinical factors were conducted for key outcomes (SHS, PHQ-9, GAD-7, PSQI) using independent-samples t-tests (normally distributed data) or Mann-Whitney U tests (non-normally distributed data). The association between GAD-7/PHQ-9 scores and SHS scores was evaluated using simple linear regression, with results presented as R² values. Multiple regression analysis was used to identify independent predictors of QOL, including variables demonstrating univariate associations (p ≤ 0.05) in preliminary analyses.

For Study 2: The pre-specified primary outcome for assessing preliminary effects was the change in SHS score from baseline to the 8-week follow-up. Secondary outcomes included changes in PHQ-9, GAD-7, and PSQI scores. Between-group comparisons (intervention vs. control) for key outcomes at baseline and post-intervention (8-week follow-up) were conducted using independent-samples t-tests (for normally distributed data) or Mann-Whitney U tests (for non-normally distributed data).Within-group changes from baseline to post-intervention in the intervention group were assessed using paired-samples t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate. To account for within-subject correlation arising from repeated measurements, the generalized estimating equation (GEE) approach was used with appropriate covariance structures. The time-by-group interaction effect, assessed via the Wald χ² test, was used to determine whether changes over time differed significantly between groups. Adjusted mean differences between groups at week 8, along with their 95% confidence intervals, are reported as the primary estimate of interventional effect.

Results for both studies are reported with point estimates (e.g., mean differences, regression coefficients), odds ratios (OR) where applicable, 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), effect sizes, and p-values. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

Study 1: cross-sectional survey

The primary goal of Study 1 was to examine the associations with and contributing factors to QOL in this IBD population. Among 521 individuals completing the questionnaire, 484 (92.9%) with confirmed IBD diagnoses were included (Fig. 1).

The cohort comprised 302 males (62.4%, 302/484) and 182 females (37.6%, 182/484), with Crohn’s disease (CD) predominating (82.2%, 398/484) over ulcerative colitis (UC) (17.8%, 86/484). Elevated anxiety symptoms (GAD-7 ≥ 10) affected 75.4% (365/484) of participants, while 81.6% (395/484) exhibited clinically significant depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 10) (Table 1).

Disease-specific analysis revealed CD patients had higher rates of surgical intervention (CD: 61.1% [243/398] vs. UC: 4.7% [4/86], p < 0.001) and biologic use (CD: 89.4% [356/398] vs. UC: 60.5% [52/86]; p < 0.001). Psychological symptoms and QOL in IBD

Among IBD patients (UC or CD), SHS scores differed significantly across depressive symptom severity levels: between mild vs. moderate (p < 0.001) and moderate vs. severe (p < 0.001) groups (Table 2). Similarly, patients with moderate anxiety symptoms demonstrated significantly higher SHS scores than those with mild symptoms (p < 0.001). Furthermore, a significant difference in SHS scores was observed between moderate and severe anxiety symptom groups (p < 0.001).

Correlation between depression/anxiety severity, and QOL in IBD

In UC patients, the PHQ-9 total scores showed a positive correlation with the SHS total scores, with an adjusted R2 of 0.37 and a coefficient of 9.2 (95% CI 6.5–11.8). For CD patients, the PHQ-9 total scores also exhibited a positive correlation with the SHS scores, with an adjusted R2 of 0.22 and a coefficient of 7.2 (95% CI 5.9–8.6) (Table 3). Similarly, the GAD-7 total scores positively correlated with the SHS scores in UC patients, with an adjusted R2 of 0.43 and a coefficient of 12.6 (95% CI 9.4–15.7). In CD patients, the GAD-7 total scores also showed a positive correlation with the SHS scores, with an adjusted R2 of 0.27 and a coefficient of 10.4 (95% CI 8.8–12.1) (Table 3). Among the variables studied, there were no other significant correlations with the SHS scores in UC patients. However, in CD patients, variables such as employment and disease status exhibited negative correlations with SHS scores (p-value ≤ 0.05), while SHS scores positively correlated with age (p-value ≤ 0.05) (Table 3).

Study2: randomized pilot study

The primary goal of Study 2 was to assess the feasibility and preliminary effects of MINDEX on QOL and psychological symptoms in IBD patients within a pilot randomized trial.

Baseline characteristics and outcomes

From 90 initially screened participants, 72 participants were randomized equally to control (n = 36) and intervention (n = 36) groups. Attrition resulted in 14 control (38.9%) and 6 intervention (16.7%) participants being excluded due to incomplete endpoint questionnaires, yielding an analytical sample of 52 participants (control: n = 22, 61.1%; intervention: n = 30, 83.3%) (Fig. 2). Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups: mean age 33 years, 53% female, with balanced distributions in education, employment, socioeconomic status, medications, and living arrangements (Table 5). No significant baseline differences existed in GAD-7, PHQ-9, SHS, or PSQI scores (Table 6). At 8-week follow-up, no statistically significant difference was observed in mean SHS scores between the MINDEX group (187.2 ± 83.3) and the control group (198.2 ± 94.5) in this pilot trial. In addition, there were no significant reductions in PHQ-9, GAD-7, or PSQI scores. Furthermore, time-by-group interaction was measured according to GAD-7, PHQ-9, SHS or PSQI scores (Table 6). Feasibility and engagement findings

Our findings revealed that only one-quarter (25%) of patients engaged in online mindfulness programs more than three times a week, with sessions lasting at least 30 min each (Fig. 3 A and 3B). Additionally, we assessed adverse reactions to mindfulness therapy and found that approximately half of the patients reported being able to avoid complex thinking, while fewer than 10% experienced false memory formation or discarded positive or critical thoughts (Fig. 3 C). Discussion

This study presents findings from two distinct but related studies involving patients with IBD. Study 1 (a cross-sectional survey) examined factors associated with QOL, and Study 2 (a pilot study) investigated the feasibility and preliminary effects of the digital mindfulness-based intervention (MINDEX). The findings and implications of each study are discussed below.

Study 1: cross-sectional survey findings on psychological symptoms and QOL

Our findings revealed strong associations between depression/anxiety symptoms and QOL in IBD patients, consistent with previous literature [27, 28]. Higher symptom severity was correlated with elevated SHS scores, indicating poorer QOL and highlighting the clinical relevance of mental health management. Patients with UC and CD at comparable symptom levels exhibited similar impairments in SHS, suggesting that psychological burden on QOL is consistent across IBD subtypes. This was further supported by multiple regression analyses. Although disease remission was correlated with better QOL [29], systematic reviews indicated that anxiety/depression often persisted during remission and continued to impair QOL [30]. Inconsistent findings across studies may stem from methodological differences, such as choice of adjustment variables or measurement tools, as well as complex interactions between disease activity, psychological symptoms, and contextual factors. Overall, these results emphasize the need to integrate mental health assessment and psychological care into routine IBD management.

Study 2: pilot intervention (MINDEX) outcomes and implementation

Study 2 primarily evaluated the feasibility and preliminary effects of the online MINDEX program. The intervention was technically feasibility and initially acceptable; However, no statistically significant improvements in anxiety, depression, QOL, or sleep quality were observed at 8weeks. The main limiting factor was low adherence, with only 25% of participants completing ≥ 3 sessions per week, corresponding with reduced engagement sustainability over time. Commonly reported barriers included lack of time and limited understanding of mindfulness. This findings suggested that although online delivery was viable, strategies to improve adherence and engagement were crucial before advancing to larger trials. Future versions of the intervention could integrate cognitive-behavioral techniques to facilitate comprehension and enhance efficacy. Previous studies of mindfulness and cognitive-behavioral interventions in IBD have also reported mixed outcomes [11, 31], indicating a need for further research informed by feasibility data.

Online mindfulness interventions, such as MINDEX, offer increased accessibility and flexibility, overcoming limitations of traditional formats such as fixed schedules and locations [32–34]. In our study, we adopted flexible intervention methods and timing through WeChat live streaming and audio transmission techniques [35]. This allowed for intervention without disrupting patients’ daily lives, making it convenient and accessible. Moreover, MINDEX proves to be cost-effective for both healthcare providers and patients; however, this hypothesis would need verification through appropriately designed studies and larger sample size.

Strengths and limitations

Study 1 provided detailed insights into the associations between psychological symptoms, disease activity, and QOL in a defined IBD cohort. However, the exploratory nature of the diagnosis-specific (UC vs. CD) findings regarding psychological symptom contributions to QOL requires confirmation in larger, longitudinal studies.

Study 2 was randomized design, which enhanced intergroup comparability. Online delivery increased accessibility, addressing a key barrier for IBD patients. The study also explicitly framed and conducted as a pilot study to assess feasibility and generate preliminary data. Regarding limitations, suboptimal adherence significantly attenuated potential for observing effects and limits conclusions about efficacy. The 8-week intervention window may have been insufficient for full benefit realization. As a pilot, the sample size was not powered to detect small-to-moderate effects on clinical outcomes; Future efficacy trials require larger samples. Extended observation periods are warranted in subsequent studies to assess durability of outcomes.

Conclusions and future directions

Study 1 confirmed strong associations between depression/anxiety symptoms and QOL in IBD patients. Critically, it demonstrated strong links between psychological symptom severity and QOL, independent of disease activity, and highlighted a significant gap in psychological care provision. In the future, we can investigate diagnosis-specific (UC vs. CD) links between psychological symptom profiles and QOL impairment in larger cohorts.

Study 2 demonstrated the technical feasibility and initial acceptability of delivering the MINDEX program online. However, primarily due to suboptimal adherence, no statistically significant improvements in anxiety, depression, QOL or sleep quality outcomes were observed at the 8-week assessment. The findings underscore the challenges of engagement and adherence in digital mindfulness interventions for this population. In the future, we can conduct a large-scale, adequately powered RCT based on the feasibility findings of this pilot study, incorporating enhanced strategies to boost participant retention, engagement, and protocol adherence. In addition, we can include longer follow-up periods in future trials to assess the sustainability of any intervention effects.
