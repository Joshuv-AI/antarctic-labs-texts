Interpretable radiomics-based machine learning model for differentiating glioblastoma from primary central nervous system lymphoma using contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging
Authors: Xueming Xia, Qiaoyue Tan, Yuxin Xie, Wenjun Wu, Qiheng Gou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to develop and validate an interpretable radiomics-based machine learning model using contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging (CE-T1WI) to differentiate glioblastoma (GB) from primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL), while comparing the performance of models using high-order versus low-order features. A retrospective analysis was conducted on 383 patients with histopathologically confirmed diagnoses (226 GB cases with 226 samples; 157 PCNSL cases with 232 samples). Radiomic features were extracted from CE-T1WI sequences using PyRadiomics, including both low-order and high-order features. A sequential feature selection pipeline combining variance thresholding, minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR), and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was used to identify the most informative and stable radiomic features for model building. Ten machine learning algorithms, including LightGBM, logistic regression, and random forests, were utilized to construct classifiers. Model performance was evaluated based on area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, negative predictive value (NPV) and positive predictive value (PPV). A comparison of the average performance metrics across all ten models was conducted between the high-order and low-order feature models. Interpretability was provided through SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP). Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS version 25.0 and Python 3.10.16. The sum of 1316 high-order features were extracted, and after feature reduction and selection, 17 optimal features were retained for machine learning models. Additionally, 107 low-order features were reduced to 20 discriminative features. The models, particularly those based on high-order features, demonstrated exceptional diagnostic performance, with AUC values exceeding 0.95 in 9 out of 10 models in the test sets. Among the ten classifiers evaluated, the LGBM model emerged as the most robust performer, achieving a test set AUC of 0.955 and demonstrating the smallest discrepancy (0.001) between the training and test AUC values. High-order features significantly outperformed low-order features, with improvements in AUC, sensitivity, and NPV (p < 0.05). The SHAP provided an in-depth interpretation of the LGBM model’s predictions, identifying key features such as original_firstorder_Kurtosis and exponential_GLDem_DependanceVariance as significant contributors, while offering both global and sample-specific perspectives. The study demonstrates the potential of using a CE-T1WI-derived radiomics approach combined with machine learning for distinguishing GB from PCNSL with high accuracy and interpretability. The model provides a practical, non-invasive diagnostic approach to support preoperative decision-making, particularly when biopsy or pathological sampling is challenging or uncertain. This approach has strong potential for clinical application in neuro-oncology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22391-7.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GB) and primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) are primary aggressive neoplasms in the brain, exhibiting distinctly different biological behaviors1,2. The treatment protocols and prognosis for GB and PCNSL are vastly different3,4. Current guidelines for GB recommend maximal surgical resection followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy with temozolomide5,6. In contrast, for PCNSL, non-invasive treatments are recommended, including high-dose methotrexate induction chemotherapy succeeded by consolidation therapy with whole-brain radiotherapy or autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation7. Therefore, accurate preoperative differential diagnosis of these two tumor types is crucial for developing appropriate treatment strategies in the field of neuro-oncology.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers fine-resolution imaging of brain soft tissues, exhibiting significant benefits in identifying brain tumors, with contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging (CE-T1WI) being the most commonly used diagnostic sequence8. Typical GB is often characterized by significant necrosis, hemorrhage, and peritumoral edema, whereas PCNSL typically presents as one or more uniformly enhancing masses with little or no apparent necrosis or hemorrhage, and are often associated with minimal edema9. Furthermore, GB typically exhibits irregular tumor borders with invasive growth patterns, whereas lymphomas often present with more well-defined boundaries. These characteristics contribute to the differential diagnosis between the two conditions. However, atypical lesions exhibit similar appearances on MRI, such as irregular, contrast-enhancing lesions with prominent enhancement on CE-T1WI in both tumors10. On T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), both types of tumors may present with extensive edema surrounding the mass, with generally high signal intensity in the lesion, often accompanied by abnormal changes in the white matter. High signal intensity surrounding the lesion, particularly in the tumor periphery and edema area, can also be detected on T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR). Additionally, both of these tumors can occur in the cerebral hemisphere, particularly in the subcortical regions, and are commonly found in the central areas of the brain, such as the internal capsule and basal ganglia11,12. Finally, both conditions share certain clinical similarities, including manifestations like headaches, seizures, and neurological impairments. These similar clinical presentations make preoperative differential diagnosis a challenging task in clinical practice.

Currently, obtaining tumor tissue samples through biopsy for microscopic analysis, immunohistochemical staining, and molecular testing is the most accurate diagnostic approach for determining the specific nature of the tumor13,14. However, brain tumor biopsy is an invasive procedure that typically requires tissue samples to be obtained through puncture or surgical intervention. For certain patients, particularly those who are elderly, frail, or have tumors located in critical areas such as the brainstem, this procedure may pose significant risks. At the same time, certain tumors may exhibit pathological heterogeneity, and biopsy results may not fully represent the characteristics of the entire tumor. The precision of tissue collection can also influence the dependability of pathological diagnoses. With the advancement of neuroimaging technologies, innovative imaging methods such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) have emerged15. DTI, by observing the diffusion direction of water molecules in white matter fibers, evaluates the integrity of the brain’s white matter and helps assess the impact of tumors on brain structure16. MRS enables the analysis of metabolic changes in brain tissues, aiding in the identification of tumor types17. These advanced imaging techniques enhance the ability to distinguish pathological and physiological differences in lesions. However, their diagnostic efficacy still requires improvement and additional confirmation in larger patient populations. Additionally, the substantial expenses and prolonged scanning durations hinder their broad clinical use, making traditional MRI methods indispensable in in the diagnosis of brain neoplasms.

The application of artificial intelligence (AI) in brain tumors is becoming increasingly widespread, particularly in the areas of diagnosis and differential diagnosis, where significant progress has been made18,19. AI technologies, especially machine learning and deep learning, are capable of extracting complex features from neuroimaging and train models based on image characteristics such as tumor morphology, borders, and enhancement patterns, thereby aiding in the differentiation of various types of brain tumors and assisting clinicians in improving diagnostic accuracy and efficiency20. Several studies have demonstrated that machine learning models, through the extraction of features from MRI, are capable of differentiating GB from PCNSL21,22. Based on the study conducted by Joo et al., fully automated radiomics-based machine learning models have demonstrated promising diagnostic performance for the classification of single enhancing brain tumors with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.87823. The study by Naser et al. developed and tested a deep learning model utilizing DenseNet169 for pre-surgical diagnosis of PCNSL and GB, demonstrating a precision of 80% and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.8, with a combined expert and automated diagnosis achieving 95% accuracy24. The meta-analysis by Guha et al. found that deep learning and machine learning algorithms, using MRI radiomic features, effectively differentiated PCNSL from GB, achieving up to 97.9% diagnostic accuracy and offering valuable assistance for preoperative decisions by neuro-oncologists25. However, current machine learning and deep learning models still exhibit significant limitations in addressing this diagnostic challenge. First, the relative scarcity of imaging data for GB and particularly PCNSL in clinical practice results in limited dataset sizes. Small sample volumes may constrain model generalizability, as they might fail to adequately capture the full spectrum of radiomic feature diversity between GB and PCNSL, potentially leading to overfitting risks. Second, the complexity of feature selection and extraction processes may cause models to over-rely on specific features, thereby reducing interpretability. Inconsistent feature selection approaches could further contribute to model performance variability across different institutions or imaging equipment. Consequently, intricate feature engineering may obscure the clinical rationale behind model decisions, and this lack of interpretability in AI models could hinder their clinical adoption. Third, while AI models often demonstrate good performance on training datasets, their efficacy frequently declines in validation sets, indicating limited generalizability. Finally, some studies report suboptimal model performance with relatively low AUC values, which substantially restricts their clinical utility.

Therefore, this study aims to develop and validate interpretable machine learning model based on radiomic features extracted from CE-T1WI to differentiate GB from PCNSL, while comparing the impact of high-order versus low-order features on model performance.

Materials and methods

Patient and MRI acquisition protocol

This retrospective study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of our institution, and the IRB approval number is 2,024,402. All individuals or their legal representatives provided documented consent after being fully informed. Inclusion criteria encompassed patients with histopathologically confirmed GB or PCNSL who had available pretreatment axial CE-T1WI. Patients were excluded if they had received antitumor therapy prior to MRI acquisition, had lesions smaller than 1 cm in maximum diameter, exhibited significant imaging artifacts affecting diagnostic quality, or had a history of extracranial malignancies including systemic lymphoma. Ultimately, the study cohort comprised 226 GB cases (226 samples) and 157 PCNSL cases (232 samples) managed at our hospital from January 2014 to December 2024. MRI examinations were conducted on all participants with a 3.0T Siemens Magnetom Prisma scanner, which was fitted with a 64-channel head/neck coil. A spin-echo (SE) sequence was used to perform CE-T1WI after the intravenous administration of gadopentetate dimeglumine at a typical dose of 0.1 mmol/kg body mass. The following imaging parameters were set for CE-T1WI: repetition time (TR) of 1900ms, echo time (TE) of 2.26ms, inversion time (TI) of 900ms, flip angle of 9°, slice thickness of 1 mm, axial field of view (FOV) of 25.6 × 25.6 cm², and acquisition matrix of 256 × 256.

Image preprocessing and tumor segmentation

To ensure data consistency and mitigate variations arising from diverse imaging devices or protocols, all medical images underwent standardized preprocessing. First, the voxel spacing was normalized to 1 × 1 × 1 mm³ through the use of fixed-resolution resampling, which addressed the spatial heterogeneity. Next, intensity normalization was performed using Z-score scaling (mean-centered with unit standard deviation) across the entire image volume. Furthermore, to remove intensity non-uniformities resulting from fluctuations in the magnetic field, N4ITK bias field correction was applied, reducing spurious gradients and enhancing the robustness of radiomic feature analysis.

Manual segmentation was performed on CE-T1WI using 3D Slicer (Version 4.11), an open-source platform for medical image analysis. Both radiologists with extensive experience were unaware of the histological information. A medical imaging specialist (Radiologist A) with > 10 years of specialized experience in neuro-oncologic imaging manually delineated tumor volumes of interests (VOIs) on sequential axial slices, adhering to the following criteria:1) inclusion of all contrast-enhancing tumor components, intratumoral vasculature, and necrotic regions;2) exclusion of peritumoral edema beyond the enhancing margin;3) strict adherence to tumor boundaries visible on CE-T1WI. All segmentations were independently validated by neuroradiologist B (>10 years of neuroimaging expertise). Discrepancies were resolved through consensus review to ensure accuracy. This single-reader approach with expert validation, though precluding assessment of observer agreement (e.g., intraclass correlation coefficient, dice coefficient), adheres to established radiomics methodologies when segmentation by multiple readers is impractical, prioritizing precision through rigorous expert verification.

Radiomic feature extraction

PyRadiomics (v3.0.1) was used to obtain radiomic characteristics of the tumor. Low-order features consist of 107 original features, including 14 shape descriptors that capture tumor morphology (e.g., volume, sphericity), 93 texture features comprising 18 first-order statistics, 24 Gy-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features, 16 Gy-level run-length matrix (GLRLM) features, 16 Gy-level size zone matrix (GLSZM) features, 14 Gy-level dependence matrix (GLDM) features, and 5 neighborhood gray-tone difference matrix (NGTDM) features derived from the initial image. High-order features comprise 1,316 radiomic features in total, including the 107 original features and an additional 1,209 filtered and transformed features generated from the original texture features. The 1,209 filtered and transformed features included wavelet decomposition across eight frequency sub-bands (LLL to HHH) for multi-angular texture analysis and intensity transformation methods (logarithm, exponential, square, square root, gradient) applied to amplify gray-level variations. Each filter configuration (8 wavelet + 5 transforms) independently yielded the same categories and number of texture features as those extracted from the original image (93 features per configuration). This multi-scale and transformation-based approach aimed to provide comprehensive feature sets for subsequent machine learning analysis.

Feature reduction and selection

The data was randomly partitioned into training (80%) and test (20%) portions for model construction and evaluation. To tackle high dimensionality and select the most pertinent radiomic characteristics, a sequential characteristics choosing pipeline was employed: first, features with very low variance (threshold > 0.01) were eliminated as they provided minimal information; subsequently, all remaining features underwent Z-score standardization using parameters computed solely from the training data to standardize scales and prevent data leakage to the test dataset; next, the Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) algorithm was applied to recognize promising subsets of features (sizes ranging from 10 to 50, chosen pragmatically based on an approximate feature-to-sample ratio guideline of 1:20 to 1:10); each of these mRMR-derived candidate subsets was then subjected to Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression with its penalty parameter (λ) meticulously tuned via 10-fold cross-validation to further shrink the feature set and enhance stability; finally, the forecasting ability of these LASSO-refined feature subsets was rigorously evaluated using a 10-fold cross-validated Random Forest model, assessing their discrimination ability via the receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC-AUC), and the subset achieving the highest performance while retaining model interpretability was chosen for the final predictive model building. The Venn diagram was used to intercept the high-order and low-order feature sets in order to extract the common and stable features.

Model construction

An extensive machine learning investigation was carried out, wherein GB-PCNSL classifiers were constructed using ten distinct algorithms—K-nearest neighbors (KNN), LGBM, XGBoost, GradientBoost, ExtraTrees, CatBoost, multilayer perceptron (MLP), logistic regression, RandomForest, and support vector machine (SVM)—applied to the preprocessed and feature-selected training dataset. These algorithms encompassed linear frameworks, tree-based approaches, neural networks, and ensemble strategies. Hyperparameters of the models were optimized through Bayesian optimization, enabling an efficient exploration of potential configurations. To ensure robustness, 10-fold cross-validation was used during this procedure. After identifying the best hyperparameters, the classifiers were retrained on the entire training set. Model evaluation

The effectiveness of the model was comprehensively assessed for both lower-order and higher-order radiomic feature sets employing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Key evaluation metrics included AUC with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, NPV, and positive predictive value (PPV). Classifiers were rigorously compared using these metrics derived from both cross-validation and an independent validation cohort. The optimal classifier was identified based on superior discriminative performance (maximized AUC, accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, PPV, and NPV) coupled with consistent AUC values between cross-validation and independent testing phases. Radar plots visually synthesized multi-metric performance. Additionally, the stability of feature sets was assessed by computing and comparing mean values (AUC, accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, PPV, NPV) across all ten algorithms for each feature set type.

Optimal model interpretation

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were used to enhance model interpretability by quantifying the contribution of every radiomic feature to the outcomes generated by the optimal classifier. SHAP values provide a reliable, game theory-based measure of feature importance by calculating marginal contributions across all feature combinations. We employed model-specific explainers (TreeExplainer, KernelExplainer, or LinearExplainer) to evaluate each model’s interpretability. Global feature significance and directional influence (positive SHAP values indicating GB classification, negative indicating PCNSL) were visualized via SHAP summary plots and swarm plots. Decision plots illustrated cumulative feature contributions for individual predictions, while force plots detailed the additive impact of features for selected samples, enabling both cohort-level and case-specific interpretation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 25.0 and Python 3.10.16. The normality of continuous variables was evaluated using Shapiro-Wilk tests and Q-Q plots. Data that followed a normal distribution (mean ± SD) were analyzed using ANOVA or independent t-tests, while data that were not normally distributed [median (Q1, Q3)] were assessed with Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests. The comparison of categorical variables (counts/proportions) was conducted using χ² or Fisher’s exact tests. Diagnostic performance was evaluated by ROC-derived AUC. Feature set stability was quantified by comparing mean metrics (AUC/accuracy/specificity/sensitivity/NPV/PPV) across all ten models using independent t-tests. A p-value of less than 0.05 was established as the threshold for significance.

Feature selection and model construction

The training group was made up of 181 GB and 185 PCNSL samples, ensuring balanced class representation. From an initial pool of 1,316 high-order features, variance threshold reduction retained 1,108 features for subsequent analysis. Feature selection was performed through a two-step process: mRMR identified 33 optimal features based on random forest model performance, followed by LASSO regression refinement, which further reduced the feature set to 17. Parallel analysis of 107 low-order features yielded 20 discriminative features. (provided in Supplementary material 1) Intersection analysis revealed four robust features common to both high- and low-order feature sets: original_firstorder_Kurtosis, original_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformity, original_shape_Flatness, and original_shape_Maximum2DDiameterSlice (visualized in Fig. 3). The high-order and low-order characteristics were separately used as inputs for model development. To identify the optimal classifier, we systematically implemented and evaluated ten machine learning algorithms using standardized performance metrics. All models were subjected to Bayesian hyperparameter optimization, and the optimized hyperparameters were subsequently applied to the testing set provided in Supplementary Material 2.

With respect to other evaluation metrics, all models further displayed robust outcomes in test data. The models consistently attained high accuracy, with values reaching 87% and above. The Test NPV ranged from 0.87 for KNeighbors to 0.93 for RandomForest. The Test PPV varied between 0.8 for XGBoost and 0.911 for MLP. Discussion

In this research, we constructed and tested a radiomics-based machine learning framework using high-order and low-order features from a single CE-T1WI sequence to differentiate GB from PCNSL. Our findings show that radiomic models, especially those based on high-order features, exhibit exceptional diagnostic performance and generalizability, with all ten models achieving AUC values exceeding 0.90 on both training and test sets. Among the ten classifiers evaluated, the LGBM model emerged as the most robust performer, achieving a test set AUC of 0.955 and demonstrating the smallest discrepancy (0.001) across the training and test AUC values. Notably, classifiers built on high-order features consistently outperformed their low-order counterparts in key metrics such as AUC, sensitivity, and NPV (p < 0.05), suggesting that high-order features capture more discriminative tumor characteristics. The SHAP offers a thorough explanation of the LGBM model’s predictions, providing both broad perspectives and case-specific analysis. These results collectively underscore the feasibility of using CE-T1WI-derived radiomic features within an interpretable machine learning framework to non-invasively distinguish between GB and PCNSL, supporting its potential role in clinical decision-making when pathological confirmation is challenging.

Over the past few years, AI has gained more widespread application in the differential diagnosis of brain tumors, demonstrating notable progress and effectiveness in classifying various tumor types26–30. For instance, Bathla et al. revealed the robust diagnostic capability of radiomics models in a cohort of 94 patients (34 with PCNSL and 60 with GB), where the best model combining CE-T1WI, T2-FLAIR, and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) reached an AUC of 0.977 for distinguishing GB from PCNSL22. Moreover, meta-analyses have further confirmed the diagnostic potential of machine learning in this context. A study by Guha et al. evaluated 1159 patients and found that machine learning outperformed radiologists in diagnostic accuracy, with a pooled sensitivity of 0.89 and an AUC of 0.94 in comparison with 0.82 sensitivity and 0.90 AUC for radiologists26. This reinforces the promising role of machine learning models in supporting radiologists for accurate brain tumor classification. In line with this, Silva et al.‘s systematic review and meta-analysis reported a pooled AUC of 0.99 for machine learning in distinguishing GB from PCNSL, further underscoring its potential in brain tumor diagnostics27. In contrast to these studies, our research leveraged more refined feature engineering and model optimization techniques, achieving a maximum AUC of 0.966 using only CE-T1WI, highlighting the practical and efficient application of single-sequence imaging. This approach not only demonstrated strong diagnostic performance but also offered enhanced clinical utility, making it applicable in routine clinical settings where multimodal imaging may not always be feasible.

Deep learning also shows promising potential in the differentiation of brain tumors31,32. For instance, in a study led by Jaruenpunyasak et al., axial CE-T1WI from 274 patients with corpus callosal tumors (94 with PCNSL and 180 with GB) were utilized to develop deep learning models, which achieved an optimal AUC of 0.8433. Similarly, in a study conducted by Naser et al., a convolutional neural network (CNN) founded on DenseNet169 was trained on MRI from 68 PCNSL and 69 GB patients, achieving an accuracy of 80% and an NPV of 0.80 on an independent test dataset (10 PCNSL, 10 GB), outperforming six experienced clinicians (73% accuracy, NPV 0.77)34. While these studies reported promising results, many were constrained by small sample sizes and limited interpretability. In contrast, our study included nine machine learning models, all achieving AUC values above 0.95 on the test set, and incorporated SHAP analysis for interpretability. SHAP quantitatively revealed every feature’s role in the classifier’s output, enhancing transparency, reliability, and clinical confidence in its decision-making process35. These findings align with those of Yang et al., who utilized SHAP analysis to interpret their radiomics and deep learning fusion classifier for pre-surgical distinction of PCNSL and GB, achieving an AUC of 0.9236. However, deep learning models typically require large datasets and complex architectures. In light of these challenges, our approach, which combines traditional machine learning methods with fine-tuned feature engineering and SHAP interpretability analysis, remains highly competitive even under conditions with limited modalities and sample sizes. It not only achieves diagnostic performance comparable to multimodal deep learning models, even surpassing them in AUC values, but also ensures model transparency and clinical applicability, while demonstrating the stable practicality of single-sequence radiomics. These findings collectively underscore the growing role of AI-driven models in the accurate differentiation of brain tumors.

In this study, we conducted an in-depth comparison of ten machine learning models constructed using high-order versus low-order features, with a particular focus on their performance in identifying tumor heterogeneity. The results demonstrated that high-order feature classifiers significantly outperformed low-order classifiers in key metrics such as AUC, sensitivity, and NPV, with a p-value less than 0.05. The marked improvement in the high-order models underscores their enhanced reliability and diagnostic utility, particularly in reducing false negatives and improving the accurate classification of tumors. This comparative analysis highlights the superior capacity of high-order features in capturing tumor heterogeneity. High-order features encompass texture, shape, and gray-level inhomogeneity, which are critical for detecting subtle spatial and structural differences within tumor tissues37. In contrast, low-order features primarily capture basic statistical measures, such as mean and variance. While these features can provide some diagnostic insight, their simplistic nature fails to account for the subtle variations within tumor microarchitecture. Furthermore, model stability emerged as another key finding of this study. High-order feature models demonstrated significantly greater robustness compared to their low-order counterparts, particularly in maintaining consistent performance across the training and test group. This indicates that high-order features not only enhance model accuracy but also support broad applicability across diverse clinical scenarios. Therefore, machine learning models based on high-order features hold substantial promise for clinical translation, offering efficient and reliable diagnostic support38.

In analyzing the discriminative power of higher-order features, we observed that several key features with significant weights have clear biological counterparts at the pathological level. Among these, original_firstorder_Kurtosis plays a critical role in distinguishing GB from PCNSL, as it reflects the tail characteristics of the intensity distribution and captures tumor tissue heterogeneity, which is particularly important for GB. GB typically exhibits necrotic and cystic areas, leading to higher kurtosis values, reflecting its aggressive growth, whereas PCNSL tends to be more homogeneous with little to no significant necrosis, resulting in lower kurtosis values. Similarly, exponential_GLDem_DependanceVariance, which measures the variance in pixel intensity dependency, serves as a crucial texture descriptor. GB, due to its irregular growth and necrosis, shows higher intensity variance, contrasting with the more uniform texture seen in PCNSL. Wavelet-HLH_firstorder_Mean, related to the mean tumor intensity derived from wavelet transformation, captures multi-scale features of the tumor. GB, with its necrotic regions, leads to higher mean values in wavelet-based features, reflecting the internal structural diversity of the tumor, while PCNSL presents a more uniform texture. Wavelet-HLH_firstorder_Skewness, which shows the asymmetrical nature of the signal intensity distribution, further differentiates the two, with GB showing more irregular signal distribution and extreme skewness, whereas PCNSL displays a more uniform, symmetrical distribution. These higher-order radiomic features provide crucial insights into tumor heterogeneity, which is closely linked to the pathological characteristics of cell density, necrosis, vascular structure, boundary morphology, and signal consistency, aligning with key indicators in neuro-oncological pathology. Within the SHAP interpretability framework, we can trace the role of every feature in the model’s decision-making procedure, connecting result-oriented outputs back to their biological foundations and bridging image features with tissue-level insights. This research paradigm, which integrates radiomic features with interpretable machine learning algorithms, not only enhances model credibility but also provides a robust biological foundation for future precision diagnostics.

SHAP is a powerful tool for model interpretability that explains how each feature contribute to the classifier’s outcome39,40. SHAP’s ability to break down sophisticated AI models, such as deep learning and ensemble methods, into interpretable explanations makes it highly suitable for healthcare applications. In this study, SHAP was applied to deeply analyze the LGBM model. By examining the distribution of SHAP values, we could visually identify which features had a positive or negative impact on predictions across different samples. For example, “original_firstorder_Kurtosis” and “exponential_gldm_DependenceVariance” emerged as the most important features, with wide ranges of SHAP values and high average SHAP scores, indicating their significant influence on the model’s predictions. With SHAP, clinicians can not only assess the accuracy of model predictions but also gain actionable insights into how specific features influence patient outcomes. For instance, by analyzing the performance of “original_firstorder_Kurtosis” across different patient groups, clinicians can gain insights into its potential role in disease classification or risk assessment, leading to more personalized treatment plans for patients. Moreover, SHAP’s application enhances the model’s transparency, making it easier for clinicians to comprehend AI decision-making processes, thus fostering trust and a higher willingness to adopt AI-driven tools41. Compared to traditional “black-box” deep learning models, such as DenseNet, SHAP offers a more interpretable “white-box” approach, reducing barriers to the integration of AI in clinical practice42,43.

Although CE-T1WI is widely used in brain tumor diagnosis due to its ability to visualize enhancement and indicate blood-brain barrier disruption, it has notable limitations in assessing tumor infiltration, peritumoral edema, and non-enhancing components. In comparison, T2-FLAIR offers distinct advantages by suppressing cerebrospinal fluid signals, thereby enhancing sensitivity to abnormalities in the white matter9. It is particularly effective in detecting tumor-associated edema, infiltrative margins, and atypical lesions. For instance, in high-grade gliomas, T2-FLAIR more clearly delineates invasive tumor borders, which is crucial for preoperative planning and radiotherapy target delineation. T2-FLAIR also shows strong potential in radiomics. Compared to CE-T1WI, it provides more informative texture, shape, and higher-order statistical features that better capture the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment. Incorporating T2-FLAIR into radiomic pipelines can enhance model sensitivity to subtle differences among tumor subtypes and reveal more biologically meaningful patterns. Future research should focus on integrating CE-T1WI with other imaging modalities such as T2-FLAIR, MRS, and DTI to comprehensively capture the spatial, metabolic, and functional characteristics of brain tumors. Multimodal fusion can overcome the limitations of individual sequences, improve diagnostic accuracy, and enhance model generalizability. This approach is especially valuable for differentiating tumors with overlapping imaging features and supporting more precise clinical decision-making.

Although this research has generated positive outcomes, a number of constraints still exist. First, the study is conducted at a single institution and based on retrospective analysis, which may carry a risk of selection bias, and the sample size is imbalanced, potentially affecting the generalizability of the model. Second, no segmentation reproducibility analysis was performed, which could impact the stability of the radiomic features, despite efforts to control for bias through expert consensus. Additionally, only a single CE-T1WI sequence was used in this study. While this sequence is widely applied in clinical practice, it may not fully capture all tumor characteristics, such as the infiltration boundary and non-enhanced areas. Further investigations should verify the classifier’s stability and generalizability in different clinical settings through multi-center, prospective cohort studies, especially by recruiting larger and more balanced samples to reduce bias and enhance the model’s applicability. Furthermore, developing user-friendly clinical interfaces will help improve the model’s widespread adoption and practical utility. From a technical perspective, future efforts could explore the integration of deep learning and traditional machine learning classifiers with clinical data to build hybrid models, balancing both model performance and interpretability. Such advancements would not only improve diagnostic accuracy but also make the model more adaptable to different tumor types and imaging protocols, ultimately advancing the development of precision medicine.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated an interpretable radiomics model using CE-T1WI sequences to differentiate GB from PCNSL. The model, leveraging machine learning and high-order features, achieved excellent diagnostic performance with AUC values over 0.90 in both training and validation sets, demonstrating strong generalizability. It outperformed low-order feature models in key metrics such as AUC, sensitivity, and NPV, while maintaining stability across various clinical scenarios. The results highlight the value of radiomics-based models in capturing tumor heterogeneity, particularly when pathological sampling is not feasible or results are uncertain, supporting preoperative neuro-oncological diagnosis. SHAP analysis clarified individual feature contributions, enhancing the model’s transparency. Overall, the approach shows significant potential for clinical brain tumor diagnosis, especially in complex, non-invasive settings, with promising prospects for translational application.
