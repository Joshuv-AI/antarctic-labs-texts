Introduction

Self-insertion of objects into the urinary tract is a rare presentation in emergency practice; however, it remains one of the potential causes of acute urinary retention (AUR) [1–3]. A retrospective study by Nagabhushana et al. on self-inflicted foreign bodies in the lower genitourinary tract among males reported that, among 17,978 inpatients over a six-year period, ten patients (0.055%) had inserted foreign objects ranging from seeds and twigs to electrical wires [4]. Various objects have been documented in the literature, including electrical wires, eyebrow pencils, tongue cleaners, nail scissors, and telephone cables inserted into the lower genitourinary tract [1–3, 5, 6]. Previous studies have identified psychiatric disorders, intoxication, social isolation, and autoerotic behaviors such as masturbation as the most common contributing factors for self-insertion of foreign bodies into the genitourinary tract [1, 2, 4]. Clinical presentation varies widely, ranging from asymptomatic cases to suprapubic pain, dribbling, penile ulceration with discharge, external genital swelling, hematuria, and acute urinary retention [2, 4, 5].

Khat (Catha edulis) is a psychoactive plant commonly chewed in East Africa and the Arabian Peninsula for its stimulant effects. Its active compounds, particularly cathinone and cathine, possess amphetamine-like properties and may induce euphoria, agitation, impulsivity, altered judgment, and transient psychotic symptoms in susceptible individuals. Although khat-associated neuropsychiatric manifestations have been described, reports linking khat use with self-inflicted genitourinary foreign body insertion are extremely rare.

We report a rare and regionally relevant case of a 40-year-old previously healthy male who presented with acute urinary retention following self-insertion of an intravenous infusion set tubing into the urethra after prolonged khat chewing. Physical examination revealed suprapubic fullness and tenderness consistent with bladder distension, which was further supported by bedside ultrasonography. Imaging demonstrated a foreign body within the urinary bladder. Subsequent cystoscopic evaluation confirmed extensive intravesical coiling of the intravenous infusion set tubing. Because of the complex configuration and the potential risk of urethral injury during transurethral extraction, endoscopic removal was considered unsafe, and open cystotomy was successfully performed for complete retrieval of the foreign body.

This case is unique because of the association with prolonged khat use, the unusual intravesical coiling configuration of the foreign body, and the requirement for open surgical management rather than endoscopic retrieval. The report highlights the importance of comprehensive imaging, careful procedural planning, and multidisciplinary management in patients presenting with self-inserted lower urinary tract foreign bodies.

Case report

A 40-year-old previously healthy male presented to the emergency department with acute urinary retention after self-inserting an intravenous infusion set tubing into the urethra. The patient reported prolonged khat consumption for approximately four hours prior to the event, after which he experienced transient loss of self-awareness and impaired judgment. He subsequently attempted to insert the intravenous infusion set tubing through the urethral meatus, believing it would relieve his urinary discomfort.

Upon arrival at the emergency department, the patient appeared anxious but was fully alert and oriented. His vital signs were within normal limits, with a blood pressure of 120/80 mmHg, heart rate of 78 beats/minute, respiratory rate of 16 breaths/minute, and body temperature of 37 °C. He complained of severe suprapubic pain and inability to void despite a persistent urge to urinate.

Physical examination revealed suprapubic fullness and tenderness consistent with bladder distension. Digital rectal examination demonstrated a normal-sized prostate without palpable abnormalities. No external genital trauma, urethral bleeding, or signs of local infection were identified. Bedside ultrasonography confirmed marked urinary retention and demonstrated a foreign body within the urinary bladder. Urethral catheterization was successfully performed, resulting in immediate decompression of the bladder and symptomatic relief. Urinalysis showed no evidence of hematuria or urinary tract infection.

To further characterize the configuration and extent of the foreign body, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the pelvis was performed. Reconstructed three-dimensional CT images demonstrated extensive intravesical coiling of the intravenous infusion set tubing conforming to the shape of the urinary bladder (Fig. 1A–C). Urological consultation was obtained, and cystoscopic examination confirmed a densely coiled foreign body occupying the bladder lumen (Fig. 2). Because of the complex intravesical configuration and concern for urethral injury during transurethral extraction, endoscopic retrieval was considered technically unsafe. Therefore, open cystotomy was performed for complete removal of the foreign body (Fig. 3A and B).

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient recovered without immediate complications. Postoperative management included intravenous fluids, analgesia, prophylactic antibiotics, and supportive care. The patient demonstrated good clinical improvement and was discharged in stable condition after careful monitoring. Psychiatric consultation and postoperative retrograde urethrography were recommended to evaluate potential underlying behavioral factors and possible urethral injury; however, the patient was lost to follow-up and did not return for further assessment.

Discussion

Foreign bodies within the lower urinary tract are uncommon but clinically significant urological emergencies. Reported etiologies include self-insertion through the urethra, iatrogenic causes, penetrating trauma, and migration from adjacent organs [4, 7]. Numerous unusual objects have been described in previous reports, including electrical wires, eyebrow pencils, tongue cleaners, nail scissors, and telephone cables inserted into the lower genitourinary tract [1–3, 5, 6]. The present case is particularly notable because the foreign body consisted of an intravenous infusion set tubing that formed extensive intravesical coiling conforming to the shape of the bladder, ultimately necessitating open surgical removal.

Self-insertion of urethral foreign bodies is commonly associated with psychiatric disorders, intoxication, autoerotic behavior, social isolation, or impaired judgment [1, 2, 4]. In the current case, the patient had no documented psychiatric illness but reported prolonged khat consumption prior to the incident. Khat (Catha edulis) contains cathinone and cathine, psychoactive compounds with amphetamine-like stimulant properties that may induce euphoria, agitation, impulsivity, altered perception, and impaired judgment. Although a direct causal relationship cannot be definitively established, the transient alteration in self-awareness following prolonged khat use may have contributed to the patient’s impulsive behavior and subsequent self-insertion of the foreign body. Reports describing khat-associated self-inflicted genitourinary foreign bodies remain exceedingly rare, which further highlights the uniqueness of this case.

The clinical presentation of intravesical foreign bodies varies considerably depending on the size, shape, and duration of retention of the object. Acute urinary retention and suprapubic pain, as observed in our patient, are among the most frequently reported presenting symptoms and are consistent with findings from previously published cases [4, 6, 8].

Imaging plays a crucial role in the diagnosis and management planning of lower urinary tract foreign bodies. Although plain radiography and ultrasonography are widely available and cost-effective initial investigations, contrast-enhanced computed tomography provides superior characterization of the location, configuration, and extent of foreign bodies, particularly in complex cases [9, 10]. In the present case, bedside ultrasonography initially confirmed urinary retention and the presence of an intravesical foreign body, while reconstructed three-dimensional CT images demonstrated extensive intravesical coiling of the intravenous infusion set tubing conforming to the bladder contour.

Definitive management of intravesical foreign bodies requires complete removal while minimizing complications. Endoscopic retrieval is generally preferred because it is minimally invasive and associated with shorter recovery time [4]. However, open surgical intervention may be necessary in selected cases involving large foreign bodies, extensive coiling, knotting, sharp objects, or impaction within the bladder wall. In our patient, cystoscopic examination demonstrated a densely coiled foreign body occupying the bladder lumen. Because of the length, extensive intravesical coiling, and potential risk of urethral injury during traction, transurethral extraction was considered technically unsafe. Therefore, open cystotomy was performed for safe and complete removal of the foreign body.

Psychiatric assessment is recommended in patients presenting with self-inflicted genitourinary foreign bodies to identify possible underlying behavioral or substance-related disorders and reduce the risk of recurrence. Additionally, evaluation for urethral injury should be considered because advancement of a foreign body from the urethral meatus into the bladder may result in occult urethral trauma.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, postoperative retrograde urethrography was recommended but could not be performed because the patient was lost to follow-up, limiting assessment for possible urethral injury. Second, although prolonged khat consumption likely contributed to impaired judgment and impulsive behavior, a definitive causal relationship cannot be established based on a single case report.

Why should an emergency physician be aware of this?

Self-inserted urethral foreign bodies should be considered among the differential diagnoses of acute urinary retention, particularly in patients presenting with atypical histories or altered mental status. Complex intravesical configurations may complicate endoscopic retrieval and necessitate open surgical intervention. Early recognition, appropriate imaging, prompt urological consultation, and multidisciplinary management are essential to prevent complications such as urethral injury, infection, and bladder damage. This case also highlights the potential influence of khat-associated behavioral impairment in unusual urological emergencies.
