Small defect detection in printed circuit boards based on the multiscale edge strengthening and an improved YOLOv10
Authors: Weixun Chen, Xuneng Ke, Siming Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The manufacturing processes of Printed Circuit Boards (PCBs) have become increasingly complex, and even minor defects can significantly impair product performance and yield. Accurate identification of PCB defects is therefore crucial but remains challenging. Given the low resolution, small target size, and diverse nature of PCB surface defects, this study proposes a novel YOLO MSES-SPDConv (MS-YOLO) network based on an improved YOLOv10 framework to achieve more accurate and efficient detection of minor PCB defects with a smaller model size. Firstly, to address the performance bottleneck of the C2F module in the head network of YOLOv10 when processing complex PCB features, we introduce the Multiscale Edge Strengthening (MSES) structure to replace the traditional C2F module. The MSES structure employs a multi-branch design for multidimensional learning of minor PCB features enhanced by edge information. During inference, it is simplified to a single branch to retain high-frequency information and reduce memory consumption. Secondly, in the neck of the network, we incorporate the Improved SPDConv module to minimize the loss of fine-grained information and enable multiscale feature extraction for PCB defect images. Lastly, we optimize the network structure using the Layer Adaptive Magnitude-based Pruning (LAMP) method and design a dual-distillation strategy to further refine the improved YOLOv10 algorithm. This strategy leverages two distinct network heads for knowledge transfer, enhancing the learning effectiveness of the student model and improving accuracy while reducing model size. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed MS-YOLO outperforms several state-of-the-art models, achieving a mean Average Precision (mAP@50) of 98. 9%. This validates the significant improvements of MS-YOLO in detection accuracy, operational efficiency, and practical applicability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20387-x.

Introduction

Printed Circuit Board (PCB), as a core component of the electronics information industry, is increasingly utilized in fields such as computing, autonomous driving, aerospace, and other high-end technologies, making their quality assurance critically important1. The quality of PCB directly determines the performance and reliability of electronic devices. Consequently, enhancing PCB quality holds critical significance for the entire electronic information industry. With the growing market demand for precise and complex electronic circuits, the trend toward miniaturization and higher density of electronic components has made PCB more susceptible to various factors during production. These factors include mechanical friction, electrostatic interference, and chemical corrosion, which may lead to defects such as missing holes, mouse bites, open circuits, short circuits, burrs, and false copper2.These issues not only affect the appearance of the PCB but may also degrade its performance and even lead to the failure of the circuit board. Defects may occur in different areas such as the substrate, copper surface, traces, and vias. Therefore, surface defect detection has become a basic requirement for quality control in PCB manufacturing.

Early detection of surface defects in printed circuit boards (PCBs) relied on manual inspection, functional testing3, and in-line testing methods. Manual inspection is susceptible to visual fatigue, resulting in low efficiency and high error rates. Functional testing is costly and time-consuming, making it difficult to rapidly identify a variety of defects. In-line testing is limited to detecting electrical functional defects, with restricted detection range and precision4. With the advancement of technology, traditional image processing algorithms utilized visual features such as texture, edges, and contrast to identify defects5,6. However, their reliance on local features (e. g. , Scale-Invariant Feature Transform [SIFT]7and Speeded-Up Robust Features [SURF]8) in complex backgrounds limited their application effectiveness.

Machine learning algorithms enabled the automated detection of PCB defects by extracting features from visual images and employing classifiers9. Techniques such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), decision trees, and random forests have been widely applied, significantly improving detection accuracy and efficiency10. However, these methods often depend on effective feature extraction algorithms and still face challenges in handling complex foreground–background segmentation and detecting small targets11.

In recent years, with the development and application of deep learning in various fields12–17, researchers have introduced deep learning methods to PCB surface defect detection. Techniques such as candidate box generation, feature pyramid networks18, and multiscale detection19 have been employed to address issues related to small target detection20 and complex texture interference to some extent. Deep learning-based object detection methods are primarily divided into one-stage and two-stage21 algorithms. Representative two-stage algorithms include Faster R-CNN22, DetectoRS23, and Mask R-CNN24. These algorithms are characterized by high precision but have complex training processes, slow speeds, and large model sizes, making them unsuitable for lightweight and high-speed detection in industrial scenarios 25. Representative one-stage algorithms include the YOLO series, SSD26, EfficientDet27, and RetinaNet28. These algorithms have simplified network structures, improved detection speed and accuracy29, Among single-stage networks, the YOLO series30–32 has been widely adopted in various downstream tasks, demonstrating advantages in detection accuracy, speed, and deployment compared to other models.

This study focuses on single-stage object detection algorithms, utilizing the YOLOv10 algorithm as the baseline model. In the global network architecture, we propose a Multiscale Edge Strengthening (MSES) structure, which employs a multi-branch design. During the training stage, the MSES structure enhances PCB features by incorporating edge information through multidimensional learning. In the inference stage, the structure is simplified to a single branch to retain high-frequency information and reduce memory consumption. In the neck network, we incorporate an improved SPDConv module33, which processes features across multiple scales to effectively enhance the neck network’s ability to extract features and resist noise in PCB defect images. Finally, to address the issue of increased model size after fusion and improvement, we apply the Learning-based Adaptive Model Pruning (LAMP)34 score pruning method to globally remove the lowest-scoring connections until the desired sparsity is achieved. After pruning optimization, the model size is reduced by 15% compared to the original YOLOv10 model35. Based on this, we design a dual-distillation strategy for YOLOv10, which further improves model accuracy while reducing model space. This ultimately resolves the challenges of high difficulty in distinguishing PCB surface defects from the background, as well as the misclassification and missed detection caused by small defect sizes. Related work

In this section, we first briefly review the related work of applying traditional visual algorithms, machine learning algorithms, and deep learning algorithms to PCB surface inspection, and analyze the advantages and disadvantages of each stage of PCB quality inspection. Subsequently, the detection performance of the model proposed in this paper is introduced.

In the research of traditional visual inspection algorithms for PCB detection, Zhu et al.36 leveraged the superior performance of the Canny operator to automatically generate PCB edge maps, thereby enhancing defect detection capabilities. Na Dong et al.37 proposed an improved particle swarm optimization algorithm to address template matching problems, enabling accurate determination of fault locations and types. However, these traditional methods fall short in robustness and real-time performance, which are critical for industrial applications.

In the realm of machine learning-based PCB inspection algorithms, Hao Wu et al.38 introduced a feature selection method and a two-stage classifier for solder joint inspection, utilizing tricolor hemispherical LED array illumination and a color digital camera to acquire solder joint images. Thanasis Vafeiadis et al.10 proposed a framework for detecting PCB surface defects based on Support Vector Machine (SVM). While these methods have improved robustness, they still face challenges such as large data volumes, information redundancy, high-dimensional feature spaces, and susceptibility to environmental factors.

In the context of two-stage object detection networks for PCB inspection, Hu et al.39 proposed an improved Faster RCNN algorithm to enhance the detection of small defects. Zhang et al.40 introduced a cost-sensitive residual convolutional neural network (CSResNet) that effectively balances sample imbalance and misclassification costs. Although these algorithms achieve high precision, they are characterized by complex training procedures, slow detection speeds, and large model sizes, making them unsuitable for lightweight and high-speed detection requirements in industrial settings.

To address these limitations, researchers have developed single-stage object detection algorithms. For example, Wujin Jiang et al.41 proposed the RAR-SSD algorithm, which enhances feature focusing through an attention mechanism to improve detection accuracy. Xia et al.42 introduced an improved YOLO model incorporating a global context attention module to further enhance feature fusion. Kong et al.43 developed the SMC-YOLO network based on YOLOv7-tiny, which significantly improves the detection accuracy of tiny defects but has a relatively large model size. Overall, these algorithms perform suboptimally in high-precision small target detection.

In summary, existing PCB quality inspection algorithms face three major challenges: (1) difficulty in accurately identifying tiny defects, leading to missed detections; (2) similarity between defect features and background colors, resulting in false detections; and (3) large model sizes, which are impractical for deployment on edge devices.

In light of the problems with existing PCB quality inspection algorithms, in order to improve the accuracy and detection speed of PCB surface defect recognition while meeting the requirements for lightweight deployment, this paper takes a one-stage object detection algorithm as the research direction and proposes an improved MS-YOLO algorithm with YOLOv10n as the baseline model. This algorithm deals with a large number of small targets and complex background noise interference on PCB surfaces. The algorithm achieves 98. 8% mAP@0. 5, 77. 9% mAP@0. 5:0. 95, 68. 0 FPS, and a model size of 4 Mb on the PCB dataset, verifying the effectiveness of the algorithm.

Methodology

YOLOv10 represents a significant milestone in the YOLO series, integrating the strengths of its predecessors and introducing numerous novel features and substantial enhancements. The series comprises six distinct model variants: YOLOv10n, YOLOv10s, YOLOv10m, YOLOv10b, YOLOv10l, and YOLOv10x. As the depth and width of these models increase, so too does the number of parameters and computational demands, catering to a wide range of applications from resource-constrained devices to high-performance computing platforms.In the context of PCB defect detection, where ease of deployment and real-time performance are critical, this study selects the YOLOv10n model as the baseline for PCB defect detection. The architecture of this model is primarily divided into three components: the backbone network responsible for feature extraction, the neck network for feature layer fusion, and the head network for generating classification and localization prediction outputs.As depicted in Fig. 2, we propose MS-YOLO by making global modifications to YOLOv10n. Specifically, we optimize all C2f. modules in the network to MSES modules and introduce our proposed the Improved SPDConv module into the neck network. Finally, we optimize the model using pruning and distillation techniques. These architectural modifications are designed to address the challenge of detecting small PCB defects against complex backgrounds, and will be detailed in subsequent sections.Fig. 2The architectural design diagram of the MS-YOLO network.

The multiscale edge strengthening (MSES) module

To address the limitations of the C2F module in the YOLOv10 head network, particularly its overly simplistic design and performance bottlenecks when processing complex PCB features, this paper proposes a redesigned module called MSES (Multiscale Edge Strengthening). The MSES module enhances the model’s ability to capture edge details at multiple scales and improves both the representation of complex features and detection accuracy through a combination of multiscale feature extraction and edge enhancement techniques. The MSES module consists of two key components: SPA (Single-Pass Aggregation) and MCEE (Multi-Channel Edge Enhancement). Figure 3 provides a schematic diagram of the MSES architecture.Fig. 3Schematic diagram of the MSES architecture.

Schematic diagram of the MSES architecture.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the PCB feature map is first input into the MSES module from the Input end, and then proceeds to the SPA module. The SPA module employs a three-path cascaded architecture to process the input features. This architecture is designed to capture features at different scales and levels through distinct pathways, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to perceive multiscale targets. The processing workflow of the three-path cascaded structure is as follows:The first branch: This branch directly employs the RepConv module to perform initial feature extraction and channel adjustment on the input feature map. RepConv is an efficient module that integrates convolution and identity mapping, enabling robust feature extraction with minimal parameters. By dynamically adjusting the convolutional kernel and channel numbers, RepConv effectively extracts local features and optimizes the distribution of feature channels.The second and third branches: These branches follow the same processing procedure, both utilizing stacked MCEE modules for feature extraction. The MCEE module is designed with residual connections, which enhance cross-channel information interaction between adjacent layers and enable the reuse of features across different levels.

Following processing by the three-path cascaded structure, the feature maps from each branch are integrated at the network’s end through a single-pass feature aggregation operation. This operation enhances the model’s ability to detect targets of varying sizes while reducing feature redundancy and storage requirements. During this process, channel shuffling is employed to recombine and rearrange all processed feature channels 44. Channel shuffling disrupts fixed channel associations, promotes cross-feature interaction, and establishes more effective feature connections. Finally, the output feature map (Output) preserves the same size as the input feature map while demonstrating enhanced multiscale feature representation capabilities.

The MCEE module is inspired by the architectural features of the RepVGG network45, employing distinct operations during training and inference to balance the richness of feature learning and the efficiency of inference. During training, the feature map is split through channel shuffling, and the feature map, enhanced by RepVGG processing and maintained in its original state, boosts the learning capability. During inference, structural reparameterization converts the dual-branch structure into a single convolutional kernel, reducing computational complexity and memory consumption to enable fast inference. As shown in Fig. 4, the feature dimension of the input tensor is first set to C × H × W. Following the channel splitting technique used in the ShuffleNet network, the input feature channels are divided into two separate channel dimensions, each sized C × H × W. The input feature channels are processed in two distinct stages: the training stage and the inference stage:Fig. 4Schematic diagram of the MCEE architecture. Training stage: The MCEE module during training is constructed using two 3 × 3 convolutions and an EdgeEnhancer module. The EdgeEnhancer module scales the input 3 × 3 convolution to multiple scales and extracts local feature information at each scale. It then uses residual computation to subtract the average pooled features from the original features, effectively removing low-frequency background components and enhancing high-frequency edge features. Finally, cross-scale edge feature aggregation is achieved via channel concatenation.Inference stage: By utilizing structural reparameterization techniques, the EdgeEnhancer module and the two 3 × 3 convolutions are converted into a single 3 × 3 RepConv. This approach reduces computational complexity and memory consumption during inference while preserving the feature representation capability acquired during training. The multi-branch topology learns rich feature representations during the training stage, while the simplified single-branch structure during inference saves memory consumption, enabling fast and efficient inference.

The improved spatial pyramid decomposition convolution (SPDConv)

Conventional PCB inspection algorithms frequently produce false detections when distinguishing between defect areas and non-defect zones, as exemplified in Fig. 5. This challenge primarily stems from the intrinsically minute scale of defect characteristics and their chromatic resemblance to background patterns, making them difficult to reliably identify with traditional inspection methods and leading to suboptimal accuracy46. To address this critical limitation, this research implements the Improved SPDConv module within the YOLOv10n framework. Comparative analyses reveal that the SPDConv-enhanced architecture demonstrates higher precision and superior robustness in small object detection relative to conventional convolution modules, particularly in recognizing subtle defects. The detailed architecture of this module is illustrated in Fig. 6.Fig. 5Mouse_bite detection results comparison.Fig. 6Schematic diagram of the SPDConv architecture.

Mouse_bite detection results comparison.

Schematic diagram of the SPDConv architecture.

The Improved SPDConv module is composed of a spatial partitioning module and a basic convolutional layer. The spatial partitioning module employs a specific downsampling strategy to divide the input feature map into two equally sized sub-feature maps, each with dimensions of H/2 × W/2 × C, where H, W, and C denote the height, width, and number of channels of the input feature map, respectively. These two sub-feature maps are subsequently concatenated along the channel dimension via a Concat module, resulting in a feature map of size H/2 × W/2 × 2C. A 3 × 3 convolution operation is then applied to reduce the dimensionality and establish connections between the feature maps, yielding a final feature map of size H/2 × W/2 × C. This process demonstrates that, compared to traditional convolutional operations, SPDConv can better preserve the information within the feature maps through its unique partitioning and combining approach, thereby enhancing the feature extraction capability for small-scale features.

When integrated into the YOLOv10n model, the Improved SPDConv module significantly improves the detection accuracy for low-resolution images and small targets. Specifically, the Backbone convolutional layers are retained, while the Neck layer is replaced with a downsampling convolutional module. Experimental results indicate that the model incorporating the Improved SPDConv module achieves a 0.9% improvement in the mAP@0.5–0.95 metric compared to the model using standard convolutional modules.

Dual distillation technique

Knowledge distillation is a model compression technique that leverages a complex and high-performing teacher model to guide the training of a simpler student model, thereby enhancing its performance while maintaining low parameter and computational requirements. In the training of YOLOv10, a dual-label assignment strategy is employed, which integrates both one-to-one and one-to-many label assignment methods. Building on this concept, this study proposes a dual-distillation strategy for YOLOv10, utilizing the BCKD (Bidirectional Channel Knowledge Distillation) algorithm to distill the improved YOLOv10 model. The unpruned model serves as the teacher model, providing knowledge in the form of output probability distributions to guide the training of the student model, which has been pruned using the LAMP (Layer-Adaptive Magnitude-based Pruning) method. This guidance helps the student model improve its predictive accuracy. The BCKD distillation loss comprises two components: classification distillation loss and localization distillation loss.

Experimental dataset

The PKU-Market-PCB47 is a synthetically generated PCB defect image dataset publicly released by Peking University. There are 1,386 images suitable for defect detection tasks, including 693 normally placed PCB images and 693 images that have undergone random rotation processing, with an average pixel size of 2777 × 2138. As shown in Fig. 8, there are six categories of PCB defects: missing hole, short, mouse bite, open circuit, spur, and spurious copper, and each defect image contains only one defect category.Fig. 8Illustration of defect categories in the PKU-Market-PCB dataset.

This study conducts experiments using a PCB image dataset comprising 1,386 images. To simulate the complex scenarios that may arise during PCB quality inspection, the original dataset is augmented using image augmentation techniques such as Gaussian blur, random scaling, cropping, random brightness adjustment, salt-and-pepper noise, and random rotation. After augmentation, the dataset is expanded to 4,158 images. The distribution of various defects and instances in the dataset is depicted in the Fig. 9, The dataset is randomly split into training, validation, and testing sets in a 7:1:2 ratio based on the six defect categories, with 2,910 images in the training set, 416 in the validation set, and 832 in the testing set.Fig. 9Chart showing the number and image distribution of each category in the PKU-Market-PCB dataset.

This paper employs the Mean Average Precision (mAP), Precision, and Recall as evaluation metrics to comprehensively assess the performance of the detection model. The calculation formulas for these evaluation criteria are presented as follows:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Pr ecision = \frac{TP}{{TP + FP}}$$\end{document}10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{Re}} call = \frac{TP}{{TP + FN}}$$\end{document}

In Eqs. (9) and (10), True Positives (TP) refer to the number of positive samples correctly identified, False Positives (FP) denote the number of negative samples incorrectly predicted as positive, and False Negatives (FN) represent the number of positive samples incorrectly predicted as negative. Precision and recall are complementary metrics for evaluating the performance of a classification model. Precision reflects the proportion of actual positive samples among those predicted as positive, while recall measures the proportion of actual positive samples that are successfully identified by the model. Relying solely on either metric is insufficient for a comprehensive evaluation of model performance.11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AP = \frac{{\sum\limits_{n = 1}^{N} {p(n)*gt(n)} }}{{N_{gt} }}$$\end{document}12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$mAP = \frac{{\sum\limits_{m = 1}^{Q} {p(n)*} AP(m)}}{Q}$$\end{document}

In Eqs. (11) and (12), AP (Average Precision) is calculated by determining the precision and recall values of the positive samples predicted by the model at different confidence thresholds, plotting the PR (Precision-Recall) curve, and then averaging the area under the PR curve. mAP (mean Average Precision) represents the average of the individual class AP values.

Conclusion

This study presents the MS-YOLO model, an enhanced YOLOv10n network structure tailored to address the challenges of small target detection on the PCB dataset. Initially, the traditional C2F module is replaced with the MSES structure, which employs a multi-branch design to enrich feature learning and simplifies to a single branch during inference to minimize memory usage. Subsequently, the Improved SPDConv module is integrated into the head network to reduce the loss of fine-grained information and bolster the extraction of multiscale features from PCB defect images. Finally, a score-based pruning method is applied to eliminate redundant information and compress the model size. Additionally, a dual-label distillation strategy for YOLOv10 is designed to further enhance model accuracy. Experiments demonstrate that MS-YOLO outperforms other mainstream object detection models in mitigating false positives and false negatives. It achieves higher detection accuracy and speed while satisfying the requirements for lightweight deployment, thus meeting the industrial demands for PCB surface defect detection.
