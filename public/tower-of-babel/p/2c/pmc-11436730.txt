Glycemic variability’s impact on painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy in type 2 diabetes patients
Authors: Kuo-Cheng Chang, Yen-Wei Pai, Ching-Heng Lin, I-Te Lee, Ming-Hong Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHyperglycemia in type 2 diabetes leads to diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) and neuropathic pain, yet the association between glycemic variability and painful DPN remains insufficiently evidenced. To address this, we conducted a prospective longitudinal cohort study involving adult type 2 diabetes patients at a medical center. DPN was identified using the Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument (MNSI), and neuropathic pain was assessed with the Taiwan version of the Douleur Neuropathique 4 (DN4-T) questionnaire. At baseline in 2013, all participants were free of DPN and were re-evaluated in 2019 for the development of painful DPN. We measured visit-to-visit glycemic fluctuations using the coefficient of variation (CV) of fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Patients were stratified into tertiles according to their FPG-CV and HbA1c-CV. Among the 622 participants, 267 developed DPN during the six-year follow-up. Following matching of age and sex, 210 patients without DPN and 210 with DPN (including 26 with neuropathic pain) were identified. Our findings revealed a significant association between high FPG-CV and painful DPN, with the highest tertile showing an adjusted odds ratio of 2.82 (95% confidence interval 1.04–7.64) compared to the lowest tertile. On the contrary, HbA1c-CV did not show a significant association with the risk of painful DPN. Our study indicates that higher FPG-CV is associated with an increased risk of painful DPN, supporting the role of glycemic variability in the development of painful DPN.Keywords: Peripheral neuropathic pain, Diabetic peripheral neuropathy, Glycemic variability, Coefficients of variation, Type 2 diabetesSubject terms: Diabetes complications, Type 2 diabetes, Neuropathic pain, Peripheral neuropathies

Introduction

In patients with type 2 diabetes, diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) is a prevalent microvascular complication that can lead to severe consequences, including foot ulceration and the need for lower extremity amputation1. Roughly half of individuals with diabetes mellitus receive a diagnosis of DPN, and 25–50% of DPN patients experience diabetic neuropathic pain2. It is important to note that the prevalence of painful DPN within the diabetic population may be underestimated, as many patients do not proactively report their pain symptoms to healthcare providers. The symptoms and signs of neuropathic pain, such as allodynia and paresthesia, result in disability, emotional distress, and social withdrawal and have a detrimental impact on their quality of life3.

While chronic hyperglycemia has traditionally been considered a significant risk factor for the development of DPN, numerous studies have shown that stringent blood glucose control significantly reduces the risk of DPN, primarily in patients with type 1 diabetes, with less favorable outcomes observed in patients with type 2 diabetes4. Furthermore, the significance of glycemic level control in managing diabetic neuropathic pain remains uncertain5. This suggests that, alongside maintaining target blood glucose levels, other glycemic variables may play a crucial role in the development of painful DPN. Glycemic variability, defined as the level of fluctuation in blood sugar, has been strongly associated with DPN in patients with type 2 diabetes6–8. Previous case series research indicated that maintaining consistent blood sugar levels with minimal episodes of hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia was associated with lower pain ratings in patients with painful DPN9. Another small-scale study reaffirmed that individuals with painful DPN in patients with type 1 diabetes exhibited worse glycemic stability and more prominent blood sugar variations compared to those with painless neuropathy10. Up to this point, research on the association between glycemic variability and painful DPN has been limited, with most studies consisting of case series or cross-sectional studies. Therefore, our study aimed to examine whether glycemic variability, assessed through the coefficient of variation (CV) of fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), was associated with an increased risk of painful DPN in a Han Chinese cohort of adult type 2 diabetes patients.

Materials and methods

This is a prospective hospital-based 6-year follow-up study conducted at a tertiary medical center in Taiwan, which handles approximately 6,500 outpatient visits and 1,600 inpatient admissions daily. As in our previously published study6, we had recruited a cohort of patients with prevalent or newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes and who were older than 20 years in 2013. For the present study, we identified patients from the initial cohort who did not have DPN in 2013 and established a follow-up cohort. In 2019, we reassessed this follow-up cohort to observe the development of DPN and neuropathic pain over the 6-year follow-up period. The diagnosis of type 2 diabetes was established for each participant by endocrinologists following the criteria outlined by the American Diabetes Association (ADA). All eligible patients were integrated into our diabetes care network, ensuring systematic recording of comprehensive demographic and clinical information. We excluded patients diagnosed with type 1 diabetes or gestational diabetes mellitus and those with fewer than two available plasma laboratory test results, preventing the calculation of the CV for FPG and HbA1c. Patients with unspecified types of diabetes mellitus were also excluded. This research received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (CG18082B-1). Informed consent was obtained from each participant during enrollment. All procedures adhered to the applicable guidelines and regulatory standards; the study is in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association.

Anthropometric measurements and biochemical data

In both the 2013 and 2019 enrollment phases, case-management nurses recorded anthropometric measurements for all participants. These recorded metrics included height, body weight, body mass index (BMI), as well as systolic and diastolic blood pressure obtained by static measurements. During outpatient visits, laboratory tests are performed every 3–6 months, including FPG, HbA1c, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and triglycerides (TG). These laboratory tests are obtained from the antecubital vein of the patient after fasting overnight. Detailed descriptions of the measurement methods and calculations can be found in our previously published articles11,12.

Assessment of DPN

The DPN was assessed using the Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument (MNSI). According to the latest ADA position statement on DPN13, MNSI has been recommended as a validated tool for the detection of neuropathy in clinical and research studies. Based on current diagnostic criteria for DPN14, the diagnosis of confirmed DPN requires evidence of electrophysiological abnormalities. However, as per our study methodology, the presence of neuropathy symptoms or signs alone, without electrophysiological validation, can only be diagnosed as probable or possible DPN. The MNSI examination (MNSIE) comprises structured assessments that include abnormalities of foot appearance, ulcers of the lower extremities, perception of vibratory sensation, perception of light touch sensation, and ankle tendon reflexes. Based on previous validated studies involving adult diabetic patients15, individuals with a score above two on the MNSIE are categorized into the DPN group.

Assessment of neuropathic pain

The evaluation of neuropathic pain was carried out using the Taiwan version of the Douleur Neuropathique 4 questionnaire (DN4-T). The DN4 questionnaire was originally developed by the French Neuropathic Pain Group and consists of 10 items designed to compare clinical manifestations in patients with chronic pain related to neurological or other somatic injuries16. In French speaking populations, the DN4 questionnaire has been shown to be effective in distinguishing neuropathic pain from nociceptive pain. However, considering cultural differences and the habitual terms used to describe pain, the DN4 questionnaire must be translated into the corresponding languages and validated when used in non-French speaking populations17. DN4-T has been previously validated in research, with a diagnostic threshold score of 3 or higher for neuropathic pain. Its sensitivity to identify neuropathic pain is 0.77, and its specificity is 0.78. The predictive power of the diagnosis of neuropathic pain is 0.8318. The DN4-T questionnaire has been used in several published studies for research focused on painful DPN19,20.

Comorbidities

Comorbidities were identified if any of the following conditions were documented in outpatient or inpatient claims from 2013 to 2019, including hypertension (ICD-9-CM codes 401–405; ICD-10 codes: I10-16), cardiovascular disease (ICD-9-CM codes 390–438; ICD-10 codes: I00-I69), ischemic heart disease (ICD-9-CM codes 410–414; ICD-10 codes: I20-I25), chronic kidney disease (ICD-9-CM codes 582–583; ICD-10 codes: N03-N05), chronic liver disease (ICD-9-CM codes 571–573; ICD-10 codes: K70-K76), and hyperlipidemia (ICD-9-CM codes 272; ICD-10 codes: E78.0-E78.5). As severe complications of diabetes, severe hypoglycemia and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state were also identified. Severe hypoglycemia was defined according to the 2017 ADA Position Statement: severe cognitive impairment secondary to hypoglycemic state that requires external help to recover. We used primary diagnostic codes from emergency department visits or hospitalizations to identify cases of severe hypoglycemia (ICD-9-CM codes 251.0, 251.1, 251.2; ICD-10 codes: E08.64, E11.64, E15, E16.0, E16.1, E16.2) and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (ICD-9-CM codes 250.20, 250.22; ICD-10 codes: E11.00, E11.01, E11.65).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented using mean values and standard deviation (SD), or numbers and corresponding percentages. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test or the chi-squared test, while continuous variables were analyzed using the Student’s t-test or ANOVA.

We collected all relevant measurements for each patient during the follow-up period from 2013 to 2019 to calculate FPG-CV and HbA1c-CV for each individual. CV is defined as the SD of FPG or HbA1c divided by the mean FPG or HbA1c, respectively. Since the frequency of outpatient visits can affect the calculated SD (and therefore the CV), we applied an adjustment to both the FPG-CV and the HbA1c-CV by multiplying the CVs by the square root of (n-1) divided by n, where n represents the number of measurements included in FPG and HbA1c. The mean ± SD for the number of included measurements of FPG and HbA1c was 32.66 ± 7.77 and 30.51 ± 5.08, respectively. The patients were classified into tertiles according to their FPG-CV or HbA1c-CV values.

We conducted multivariate logistic regression analyses to investigate the impact of each identified independent variable on non-painful DPN and painful DPN. Variables included in the multivariate regression models were those that exhibited significant differences between patients with and without DPN. Adjusted odds ratios (OR) along with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for intergroup comparisons. We selected a statistical significance threshold of a P-value less than 0.05 (P < 0.05), and all tests were two-tailed. Data analysis was performed using the statistical software package SAS version 9.4 for Windows.

Results

In the follow-up cohort collected in 2019, we initially considered a total of 1,005 patients with diabetes who did not exhibit DPN in 2013. We excluded 343 patients who either did not have type 2 diabetes or had uncertain diabetes classifications. As a result, 662 patients were ultimately included in this study. Among these patients, 395 remained free of DPN in 2019, while 267 developed DPN, comprising 234 with non-painful DPN and 33 with painful DPN. Furthermore, we conducted a 1:1 matching of patients with painful DPN and non-painful DPN based on age and sex. This matching process led to 210 patients being assigned to the “without DPN” group and 210 patients assigned to the “DPN” group, with 184 patients having non-painful DPN and only 26 patients having painful DPN. The association between FPG-CV and HbA1c-CV and non-painful DPN and painful DPN

We conducted multivariate logistic regression analyses to calculate the odds ratios (ORs) for non-painful DPN among patients in each tertile of FPG-CV and HbA1c-CV. Additionally, we adjusted for BMI, the only demographic variable with significant differences between patients with and without DPN. Subsequently, the adjusted ORs for non-painful DPN in the second and third tertiles of FPG-CV were 0.70 (95% CI, 0.43–1.15) and 1.09 (95% CI, 0.66–1.80), respectively. As for HbA1c-CV, the adjusted ORs in the second and third tertiles were 1.13 (95% CI, 0.70–1.85) and 1.14 (95% CI, 0.69–1.87), respectively.

As shown in the results presented in Table 3, our study population did not exhibit a significant increase in the risk of non-painful DPN among patients with type 2 diabetes associated with higher levels of FPG-CV or HbA1c-CV.

Discussion

As previously mentioned, while numerous cross-sectional studies have pointed to a link between glycemic variability and DPN6–8,21,22, there is a notable scarcity of research concerning the association between glycemic variability and painful DPN. To the best of our knowledge, our study represents the first prospective investigation into the relationship between the variability of both FPG and HbA1c and the development of painful DPN over a 6-year follow-up period. Our research has unveiled that among adult patients with type 2 diabetes, a higher long-term FPG-CV is correlated with an increased risk of painful DPN.

In our study, patients with painful DPN displayed significantly elevated levels of FPG-CV, whereas this significance was not observed with HbA1c-CV. One possible explanation for this inconsistency is that HbA1c reflects average blood glucose levels over the 8 to 12 weeks preceding the blood test. The assessment of blood glucose status through FPG is better equipped to capture acute fluctuations in blood glucose resulting from irregular dietary and lifestyle patterns in patients with type 2 diabetes. A previous study has found that HbA1c values are only associated with chronic hyperglycemia23. In contrast, HbA1c is not related to parameters of glycemic variability, such as the mean amplitude of glycemic excursions and the standard deviation scores. While both FPG and HbA1c can be employed to calculate the CV for assessing glycemic variability in patients with type 2 diabetes, HbA1c-CV could be more modest compared to FPG-CV due to the inherent nature of HbA1c as a measure of mean values and its weaker relationship with glycemic fluctuations24,25. Assuming that the association between glycemic variability and DPN is consistent, the use of FPG-CV, which yields higher variation values, would be more suitable to investigate the relationship between DPN and glycemic variability than HbA1c-CV.

There is currently no comprehensive understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying painful DPN. Previous research has suggested that elevated blood glucose levels and poor glycemic control can influence the development of painful DPN. However, the mechanisms by which glycemic fluctuation induces neuropathic pain in patients with DPN remain unclear. Below, we outline several potential mechanisms by which glucose flux may contribute to the development of painful DPN. Firstly, as early as 1992, abrupt changes in glycemic control were reported to cause acute painful neuropathy26, initially termed insulin neuritis and now referred to as treatment-induced neuropathy of diabetes5,27. Subsequent animal and human studies have demonstrated that rapid glucose reduction and fluctuation via insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents lead to microvascular changes in the epineurium, including neovascularization, vascular tortuosity, and arteriovenous shunt formation, resulting in nerve fiber hypoxia28,29. These structural and functional alterations in peripheral nerves trigger excessive electrical impulses, causing neuropathic pain30. Second, glycemic variability is likely associated with diabetic neuropathic pain through mitochondrial dysfunction. A prior study has revealed that excess glucose can lead to mitochondrial bioenergetic failure in unmyelinated C fibers, resulting in the accumulation of electron donors within mitochondria and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction5. Glycemic variability has also been reported to have a detrimental impact on intracellular mitochondrial metabolism31. C-type sensory fibers, which lack Schwann cell protection, are particularly vulnerable to bioenergetic failure caused by mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby contributing to the development of neuropathic pain. Third, there is growing evidence suggesting an association between DPN and diabetic neuropathic pain involving the protein kinase-related pathway. In patients with diabetes, neuropathic pain is linked to hyperactivity in sensory neurons. Recent research has proposed that diabetic neuropathic pain occurs due to the overexpression of specific receptor channels in sensory neurons, primarily regulated by protein kinases32. Previous studies have indicated that glycemic variability activates protein kinase C and its downstream responses33,34. Additionally, a study utilizing continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) revealed that as the time in range decreased and glycemic variability increased, the severity of diabetic neuropathic pain became more pronounced35. Therefore, although detailed pathophysiological mechanisms still require further investigation, our research findings, in conjunction with the evidence mentioned above, suggest that glycemic variability may contribute to the development of painful DPN.

Although numerous studies have demonstrated an association between glycemic variability and DPN, our study did not uncover any associations between non-painful DPN and either FPG-CV or HbA1c-CV. One possible explanation for this discrepancy is the significantly longer duration of diabetes in our study population compared to previous research, which predominantly focused on patients with diabetes durations of 5 to 10 years8,11,22. It is worth noting that diabetes duration is also recognized as an independent risk factor for DPN, alongside glycemic variability36. As a result, in our study, we observed a notably higher prevalence of DPN, reaching 40.33%, whereas the prevalence rates reported in the studies mentioned above were generally around 20%. Additionally, within our study cohort, we identified that painful DPN represented a relatively small proportion of all DPN cases, accounting for only 12.4%, whereas previous literature has reported its occurrence in up to 30% of all DPN cases5. However, our previous cross-sectional study found that only 21.2% of patients with DPN experienced painful DPN12. Other studies in Taiwan have reported a lower prevalence of painful DPN in patients with type 2 diabetes, around 7%19,37. Furthermore, research from other Asian populations has found that, compared to western patients, Asian diabetes patients have a lower prevalence of both small-fiber and large-fiber neuropathy38–40. These findings suggest that the prevalence of painful DPN in type 2 diabetes may vary by ethnicity. One possible explanation for this lower reporting rate of painful symptoms could be attributed to cultural factors. Based on prior research and our own experiences, many Asian patients may perceive chronic pain as an expected part of life or an inevitable consequence of aging, potentially leading to a reduced inclination to seek medical assistance41,42. Another explanation is the physiological differences between ethnicities. Previous literature has indicated that Asian patients have shorter stature, less adiposity, fewer smoking pack-years, and better skin microvascularization compared to Caucasian patients. These factors contribute to the lower prevalence of painful DPN in Asian populations, with differences in microcirculation having the greatest impact on small-fiber neuropathy38,39.

In our study, the final cohort included for analysis had a male predominance, with 62.9% of the patients being male in both the DPN and non-DPN groups. However, since we matched the DPN and non-DPN groups by age and sex prior to analysis, our study could not assess the impact of sex on the risk of painful DPN. In fact, previous research has often indicated that females are at higher risk for diabetic neuropathic pain or other chronic pain conditions43,44.

Our study boasts several strengths. Firstly, all patients with type 2 diabetes included in our study underwent initial testing for the absence of DPN in 2013 before enrollment. Subsequently, in 2019, we evaluated whether these patients had developed DPN. This approach allows for a more robust assessment of the relationship between glycemic variability and DPN during the follow-up period, in contrast to previous studies with cross-sectional designs. Secondly, our recruitment process, conducted by standardized trained care providers, ensured uniformity in data collection and enabled us to compile comprehensive information on patient demographics, comorbidities, laboratory data, lifestyle habits, and medication records. Additionally, we employed validated clinical screening tools, specifically the MNSI and DN4-T questionnaires, known for their sensitivity, specificity, non-invasiveness, and ease of administration.

However, our study has certain limitations. First, the diagnosis of DPN did not include electrophysiological tests. Instead, we employed the MNSI as a clinical diagnostic tool, which has lower sensitivity compared to electrophysiological assessments, particularly for detecting subclinical DPN. According to the latest ADA position statement on DPN, electrophysiological or other laboratory examinations are not considered mandatory for the screening of DPN, but are necessary for the diagnosis of confirmed DPN13,14. Therefore, as in our study, patients with symptoms or signs of neuropathy, without electrophysiological testing, can only be classified as probable or possible DPN, which is often employed in clinical settings. How glycemic variability affects the electrophysiological activity in patients with painful DPN remains to be elucidated by future research. Second, there are various indices available for assessing glycemic variability, and it remains unclear which one is the most appropriate. Nevertheless, CV has been widely employed in numerous studies to evaluate visit-to-visit glycemic variability6–8,45. CGM is often used to detect day-to-day glycemic variability, which has been shown to be associated with painful diabetic neuropathy in a previous study35. We did not use this measurement due to the numerous barriers associated with CGM, including higher costs, the burden of patient education, discomfort or foreign body sensation during wear, and the need for regular data uploads and processing46. Our study aims to provide a simple method for predicting high-risk populations for painful DPN using visit-to-visit glycemic variability in an outpatient clinical setting. Third, we did not analyze the impact of impaired glucose tolerance and postprandial glucose (PPG) on DPN primarily due to the lack of a gold standard for PPG measurement. Relevant issues include whether to use daily diets or standardized diet challenges, whether these challenges should involve carefully controlled meals or oral glucose tolerance tests, and the timing of PPG measurement after glucose or dietary challenges. However, based on our previous research11, using high HbA1c but normal FPG as a surrogate for high PPG, we found that high PPG indeed increases the risk of painful DPN. Fourth, a study from Qatar found that the severity and duration of hypoglycemia significantly impact small fiber damage47. However, our study only included events that required emergency resuscitation due to severe hypoglycemia, and therefore did not analyze the impact of the duration of mild to moderate hypoglycemia on painful DPN. Lastly, our experimental design did not entail a prospective randomized controlled trial, and our study population primarily consisted of Taiwanese individuals. Therefore, future research is warranted to address these limitations.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that greater glycemic variability, as evaluated through FPG-CV, was linked to an elevated risk of painful DPN in patients with type 2 diabetes over the 6-year follow-up period. Moreover, FPG-CV demonstrated a more pronounced correlation with the risk of painful DPN when compared to HbA1c-CV. This suggests that our study provides valuable insights into the importance of maintaining stable glycemic control to prevent the onset of painful DPN in individuals with type 2 diabetes.
