CD38 overexpression drives glioblastoma progression via L1CAM/ICAM1/JAK-STAT-Driven tumor microenvironment rewiring
Authors: Saeed Norouzi, Sonikpreet Aulakh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) isocitrate dehydrogenase 1/2 wild-type (IDH1/2 WT) WHO grade 4 is the most common primary malignant brain tumor in adults [1]. It arises de novo from neural stem or progenitor cells, including oligodendrocyte precursor cells [2]. Despite multimodal therapy including maximal safe surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy (temozolomide), the prognosis of GBM remains dismal, with a median overall survival ranging from 12–15 months [3]. GBM progression is characterized by extensive cellular and genomic heterogeneity, therapeutic resistance, and a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), all of which contribute to treatment failure and inevitable recurrence [4].

Among the various components of the TME, tumor-associated microglia and macrophages (TAMs) constitute up to 40% of the tumor mass and play a critical role in tumor progression [5]. While these myeloid-derived cells can elicit anti-tumor responses, growing evidence indicates that in GBM, TAMs adopt a tumor-supportive phenotype, promoting tumor invasion, proliferation, and immune evasion through the secretion of cytokines and extracellular matrix-remodeling enzymes [6]. Identifying the molecular factors that trigger and sustain this tumor-supportive immune response via TAMs may offer novel therapeutic avenues.

One such target of interest in GBM is CD38, a highly conserved type II transmembrane glycoprotein that serves as a key modulator of the immune landscape in several hematological and solid malignancies [7,8]. In multiple myeloma, overexpression of CD38 is associated with an aggressive disease phenotype and has been effectively targeted with anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies (e.g., daratumumab, Isatuximab), resulting in both direct cytotoxic and immune-modulatory effects [9]. Public datasets confirm CD38 in immunosuppressive myeloid cells across mouse models [5].

Prior preclinical studies have demonstrated that CD38 regulates microglial activation in the glioma TME, where deficiency of CD38 significantly attenuates tumor expansion, increases cell death, decreases metalloproteinase-12 expression, and prolongs survival in syngeneic mouse models by modulating tumor-associated microglia/macrophage properties [5]. Additionally, CD38 inhibition with small-molecule compounds like rhein and its water-soluble form K-rhein suppresses lipopolysaccharide/IFN-γ-induced microglial activation, cell death, and nitric oxide production, thereby inhibiting glioma invasion and proliferation in vivo [10]. Similarly, in lung cancer, preclinical studies demonstrate that CD38 overexpression promotes tumorigenesis, while its knockout enhances anti-tumor immunity through NAD⁺ restoration and increased CD8+ T cell infiltration [11,12]. In CD133⁺CXCR4⁺ lung cancer stem cells, CD38 overexpression fosters an immunosuppressive microenvironment, distant metastasis, and resistance to conventional chemotherapy [13,14]. Notably, tumor-expressed CD38 drives acquired resistance to PD-L1 blockade, highlighting its role in immune checkpoint therapy resistance [15].

Building on these prior studies and the promising outcomes from our single institutional investigator initiated non-randomized interventional clinical trial (NCT04922723) where we evaluated the safety and tolerability of the FDA approved fully humanized anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody daratumumab in combination with radiation and temozolomide for patients with newly diagnosed GBM, we hypothesized that in preclinical GBM models, CD38 overexpression (OE) would accelerate tumor growth and promote an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The murine GL261 glioma cell line was purchased from the Mayo Clinic, MN. Both T98G and LN229 human glioblastoma cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The HEK293T cell line was kindly provided by Professor Saravanan Kolandaivelu (Biochemistry and Molecular Medicine, West Virginia University). All cells were grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

Overexpression of CD38 in GL-261 cells

For the induction of CD38 expression in GL-261 cells, we used the CRISPRmod CRISPRa All-in-One Lentiviral sgRNA system (Horizon Discovery, USA), an adaptation of the classical CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology. This system utilizes a catalytically deactivated S. pyogenes Cas9 (dCas9) fused to transcriptional activators (dCas9-VPR). CD38-targeting single guide RNAs (sgRNAs), 5′-AAGGCTCTTGGGTTGTCAAC-3′, 5′-CGGAGGTGACTGGACTAATA-3′, 5′-CCTGCTCCAAAGTTCCACGG-3′, and 5′-AAGTGTCAAGGTTGTTGGTC-3′, were designed using CHOPCHOP (https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no) and the CRISPR Design Tool from MIT (https://www.zlab.bio/guide-design-resources). GL-261 cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 8 × 10⁵ cells per well in reduced-serum medium. The following day, cells were infected with lentiviruses carrying a blasticidin resistance marker (BlastR) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1.08. After 72 hours, the medium was replaced with blasticidin-containing medium to select for transduced cells, yielding a polyclonal population rather than single-cell–derived clonal lines. CD38-OE GL261 cells were therefore maintained as polyclonal populations following lentiviral transduction and blasticidin selection, while scramble control cells retained parental CD38 expression levels. Western blot was used to confirm the overexpression of CD38 in transduced cells (Fig. 1.A).

Evaluation of CD38 expression following human and mouse GBM cell lines (A) and (B) overexpression of CD38 in T98G and Ln229 (human GBM cell lines) using western blot and fluorescent microscope imaging. Fig. 1B Kaplan-Meier overall survival analysis indicated significantly decreased in CD38-OE GBM- bearing mice (26.45 days) versus CD38-WT GBM- bearing mice (35.73 days) (p < 0.0001; n = 11 per group).

Lentivirus packaging for CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing in human GBM cells (T98G, LN229)

The lentiviral plasmid pCDH-MSCV-hCD38-EF1α-GFP+Puro (Addgene: 134,936) was used to overexpress CD38 in human GBM cell lines T98G and LN229. A human CD38-targeting shRNA sequence (5′-GCATACCTTTATTGTGATCTA-3′) was synthesized by VectorBuilder (Chicago, USA) and cloned into the lentiviral vector pLV[shRNA]-EGFP/Puro-U6. A scramble shRNA sequence (5′-CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCG-3′) was used as a negative control and similarly cloned into pLV[shRNA]-EGFP/Puro-U6.

To generate lentiviruses, HEK293T cells were seeded in 10-cm culture plates at a density of 2 × 10⁶ cells in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, without antibiotics. The following day, once the cells reached 80–90% confluency, a plasmid mixture was prepared in 250 µL Opti-MEM, containing 1 µg PsPAX2 (Addgene: 12,260), 0.4 µg PMD2.G (Addgene: 12,259), and 1.4 µg of the transfer plasmid (encoding either CD38, CD38 shRNA, or scramble shRNA). Additionally, 8 µL TransIT-VirusGEN Transfection Reagent (Mirus Bio, USA) was added. The mixture was added dropwise to the cells. After a 72-hour incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, the culture medium containing viral particles was collected, pooled, and concentrated using Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugal filters (Millipore, Germany) at 5000 × g for 30 min to harvest the lentiviruses.

Overexpression and knockdown of CD38 in T98G and LN229 cells using lentiviral particles

T98G and LN229 cells were plated in six-well plates at a density of 8 × 10⁵ cells per well in reduced-serum medium. The cells were then transduced with lentiviral particles carrying either the CD38 overexpression plasmid, CD38 shRNA plasmid, or scramble control, using the same protocol as for GL-261 cell transduction. The multiplicities of infection (MOI) were 0.95 for T98G and 1.13 for LN229 cells. Puromycin-containing medium was used to select a polyclonal population of transduced cells, while scramble control cells retained parental CD38 expression levels.Fluorescent microscopy imaging and western blot were used to confirm transduction and evaluate CD38 expression, respectively (Fig. 1A).

Protein analysis by western blotting

Protein lysates were prepared from cell pellets and brain tissue using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail and 1% EDTA. After a 30-second probe sonication at 15 to 20% amplitude on ice, the lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected as the final protein lysates. Total protein concentration was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Proteins were separated on 4–12% Bis-Tris gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked in bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 60 min, washed three times (5 min each) with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (TBST), and incubated with primary antibodies. The following antibodies were used (all from Cell Signaling Technology, USA, unless specified): Phospho-Stat1 (Tyr701) (#58D6), Stat1 (#14,994), Stat3 (#12,640), Phospho-Stat3 (Tyr705) (#9145), Human CD38 (#51,000), Mouse CD38 (#68,336), p21 Waf1/Cip1 (#2947), p27 Kip1 (#3688), p16 INK4A (#29,971), NF-κB p65 (#8242), GAPDH Rabbit mAb (HRP Conjugate) (#3683), and L1CAM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #PA5–85,876). All bands were quantified using ImageJ software, normalized to GAPDH. Data were obtained from three independent experiments.

Proliferation and viability assay

The MTS assay was used to evaluate the effects of CD38 expression variation on the proliferation and viability of mouse GBM cells (GL-261) and human GBM cells (T98G and LN229). The MTS assay is a colorimetric method that measures cell viability based on the reduction of the MTS tetrazolium compound to a soluble formazan dye by metabolically active cells. The in vitro Cell Proliferation Kit (Abcam, ab197010) was employed.

CD38 wild-type (WT) GL261 cells refer to the unmodified GL261 parental cell line. CD38-WT GL-261, CD38-OE GL-261, CD38-WT T98G, CD38-OE T98G, CD38-knockdown T98G, CD38-WT LN229, CD38-OE LN229, and CD38-knockdown LN229 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in a 96-well plate. After 72 hours, 10 μl of the MTS reagent was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for 4 hours at 37 °C. The absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). Data were obtained from three independent experiments, each performed in triplicate.

Tumor implantation

Eleven-week-old female wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory, USA for implantation of GL-261 cells. Mice were maintained and treated in accordance with all applicable guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of West Virginia University. After a one-week acclimation period, when the mice reached 12 weeks of age, they were randomized to groups using a random number generator, and investigators were blinded to group allocation during injections and analyses. Mice were anesthetized via inhalation of 2% isoflurane using a stereotaxic alignment system. The incision site was shaved and sterilized using Betadine, followed by a 1-cm midline incision from anterior to posterior. A 2-mm hole was drilled at a stereotactically defined location (1 mm posterior to the bregma, 2 mm lateral to the midline, and 3 mm depth). A 10-μL Hamilton syringe equipped with a 32-gauge Hamilton needle was used to inject 1 × 10⁴ CD38-WT GL-261 or CD38-OE GL-261 cells suspended in 5 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a rate of 1 μL/min.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Tumor progression was assessed on days 10 and 17 post-injection using gadolinium (Gd)-diethylenetriamine pentaacetate (DTPA)-enhanced T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). MRI scans were performed under 1%–2% isoflurane anesthesia in 98% oxygen. Immediately before imaging, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 150 µL of 0.1 M Gd-DTPA. Scans were conducted using a M7™ Compact MRI System (Aspect Imaging, USA), with a spheroid field of view 120 × 120 × 70 mm³, 1 mm slice thickness, and 14 slices total. Tumor areas were quantified using VivoQuant 5.3 software.

Survival analysis

Following injection of GL-261 cells, mice were monitored and weighed daily. The experimental endpoint was defined as a lack of physical activity and a > 30% reduction in body weight. Survival probability was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and statistical significance was assessed using both the log-rank and Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon tests.

Brain tumor flow cytometry

Mice were perfused with 10 mL of cold PBS, after which the brains were harvested, and the right hemisphere was collected for analysis. Brain tissue was dissociated into a single-cell suspension using the Adult Brain Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were stained with Zombie R718 fixable viability dye and incubated for 25 min. Fc receptors were blocked using anti-CD16/CD32 (BD Biosciences, USA) for 10 min. Surface antigen staining was performed using the following fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies from BioLegend: APC/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD45, PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse/human CD11b, Brilliant Violet 421™ anti-mouse Ly6G, FITC anti-mouse Ly6C, PE anti-mouse P2RY12. Staining was carried out on ice, in the dark, for 20 min. After staining, cells were washed twice and fixed using Cytofix/Cytoperm solution (BD Biosciences, USA). A minimum of 25,000 events were acquired per sample using a 3-laser Cytek Aurora full spectrum analyzer. Data were analyzed using FCS Express 7. Gating strategy was applied based on differential CD45 expression, distinguishing CD45^low microglia from CD45^high infiltrating macrophages (Fig. S1). Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and T-regs were identified as follows: PMN-MDSCs: Ly6G⁺Ly6C⁺, M-MDSCs: Ly6G⁻Ly6C⁺, and Tregs:CD3+, CD4+, FoxP3+.

Brain cytokine array

Brains were harvested, and the right hemisphere was homogenized in PBS supplemented with Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Triton X-100 was added to a final concentration of 1%, and samples were frozen at ≤ –70 °C. After thawing, homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min to remove cellular debris. Total protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For cytokine profiling, the Mouse Cytokine Array Panel A (Catalog #ARY006, R&D Systems, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Supplementary Table). Signal detection was performed using the Amersham Imager System (GE Healthcare, USA), and signals were normalized to positive control spots on each membrane and background-subtracted using ImageJ software. Results were compared to the control antibody-treated group.

Statistical analysis

Data are mean ± SEM of biologically independent replicates. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test was used to analyze flow cytometry data, cytokine assay, and human GBM proliferation rate results; unpaired t-tests were used to compare tumor volumes and proliferation rates between groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, n = 3. The number of mice in each group for survival analysis was determined using G*Power 3.1 software.

Results

Mice bearing CD38-OE GBM show increased tumor progression and shortened survival

To investigate the effect of CD38 overexpression on glioblastoma progression and overall survival, we compared the median overall survival (mOS) of mice intracranially implanted with CD38-OE GL-261 cells to those bearing CD38-WT GBM tumors. The experimental endpoint was defined by profound physical inactivity accompanied by a body weight loss exceeding 30%. As shown in Fig. 1B, mice in the CD38-OE group demonstrated a significantly shorter mOS compared to the CD38-WT group (26.5 days vs. 35.7 days; p < 0.0001; n = 11 per group). This result revealed that elevated CD38 expression within GBM cells promotes more aggressive tumor behavior, leading to accelerated disease progression and reduced survival.

To further investigate tumor progression, Gd-DTPA-enhanced T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed on days 10 and 17 post-tumor implantation. As illustrated in Fig. 2A and B, the CD38-OE group exhibited significantly larger tumor volumes compared to the CD38-WT group at both time points: 8.44 mm³ vs. 2.16 mm³ on day 10 (p = 0.0004; n = 6) and 34.75 mm³ vs. 8.51 mm³ on day 17 (p = 0.0048; n = 6). These results showed a 3.9-fold and 4.1-fold increase in tumor volume on days 10 and 17, respectively, in the CD38-OE group versus the CD38-WT group, providing strong evidence for a positive association between elevated CD38 expression and enhanced tumor establishment and/or accelerated progression in vivo. A comparison of the linear regression slopes between the two time points indicated that the CD38-OE group exhibited a tumor growth rate and expansion capacity approximately 4.64 times greater than that of the CD38-WT group (Fig. 2C).

Alteration in CD38 expression has no substantial impact on doubling time or expression of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CKIs)

Given the marked differences in tumor volume between the CD38-OE and CD38-WT groups, we investigated whether CD38 overexpression influences the intrinsic proliferation rate of glioblastoma cells. We measured the doubling time in murine CD38-WT and CD38-OE GL-261 cells and human GBM cell lines, including CD38-WT, CD38-OE, and CD38-knockdown variants of T98G and LN229 cells. As shown in Fig. 3A, no significant differences in doubling time were observed among the various CD38 expression conditions in either murine or human cell lines. These in vitro findings suggest that CD38 expression does not directly affect the proliferation rate of GBM cells, indicating that the observed in vivo tumor growth acceleration is likely driven by CD38-mediated effects within the tumor microenvironment rather than intrinsic cellular proliferation. To understand the molecular features contributing to larger tumors in CD38-OE GBM mice, we evaluated the in vitro expression levels of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p16, p21, and p27. As shown in Fig. 3B, there were no significant differences in the expression of these CKIs between CD38-OE and CD38-WT GL-261 cells.

CD38 promotes GBM progression through upregulation of L1CAM, STAT1, and NF-κB, and enhanced STAT1/STAT3 phosphorylation

Based on the lack of significant changes in proliferation rates, we hypothesized that CD38 facilitates rapid tumor cell implantation and establishment within the brain microenvironment. We examined the expression of the glycoprotein L1 cell adhesion molecule (L1CAM) in brain tissues from CD38-OE GBM-bearing mice, CD38-WT GBM-bearing mice, and healthy control mice. L1CAM overexpression is associated with poor prognosis and reduced overall survival in multiple cancers due to its role in enhancing cell adhesion, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [16,17]. As shown in Fig. 4, CD38 overexpression resulted in a 1.62-fold (p < 0.0001, n = 3) and 3.85-fold (p < 0.0001, n = 3) increase in L1CAM expression compared to the CD38-WT and control groups, respectively. No significant difference in L1CAM expression was observed between the CD38-WT and healthy control groups. L1CAM activates fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1), triggering downstream phosphorylation of STAT3. While total both FGFR1 and STAT3 levels remained unchanged across all groups, phosphorylated STAT3 (Tyr705) was elevated 2.24-fold (p < 0.0001, n = 3) in the CD38-OE group compared to the CD38-WT group. Similarly, total STAT1 and phosphorylated STAT1 (Tyr701) were increased 2.41-fold (p < 0.0001, n = 3) and 2.48-fold (p < 0.0002, n = 3), respectively, in CD38-OE GBM-bearing mice relative to the CD38-WT group. We also assessed nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), a transcription factor promoting tumor immune evasion, apoptosis resistance, and angiogenesis. NF-κB levels were upregulated in the CD38-OE group 1.87-fold (p < 0.0001, n = 3) compared to CD38-WT and 2.38-fold (p < 0.0001, n = 3) compared to healthy controls, with no significant difference between CD38-WT and controls. These findings suggest that CD38 overexpression facilitates tumor progression by promoting early tumor cell implantation via increased L1CAM expression and activation of STAT3 and NF-κB signaling pathways, contributing to an immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic brain microenvironment.

CD38 overexpression promotes tumor-supportive immune remodeling within the tumor microenvironment

To investigate the impact of CD38 overexpression on the TME, we performed immunophenotypic profiling of brain tissues from CD38-OE GBM-bearing mice, CD38-WT GBM-bearing mice, and healthy control mice. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a marked increase in immunosuppressive cell infiltration in CD38-OE tumors compared to CD38-WT tumors: glioma-associated macrophages (GAMs) showed a 8.43-fold increase (p < 0.0001, n = 3), monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) a 12.53-fold increase (p < 0.0002, n = 3), polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) a 15.66-fold increase (p < 0.0001, n = 3), and T-regs a 7.22-fold increase (p < 0.0001, n = 3). Microglial populations showed no significant change between groups (Fig. 5). These findings suggest that CD38 overexpression promotes the recruitment of immunosuppressive cell types, including GAMs, M-MDSCs,PMN-MDSCs, and T-regs, contributing to immune evasion and tumor progression.

CD38 overexpression alters the cytokine profile toward an immunosuppressive milieu

We analyzed cytokine and chemokine profiles in brain tissues from CD38-OE GBM-bearing mice, CD38-WT GBM-bearing mice, and healthy control mice. Cytokine profiling revealed elevated levels of pro-tumorigenic and immunomodulatory factors in the CD38-OE group compared to the CD38-WT group: CXCL10/IP-10 showed a 1.37-fold increase (p < 0.0001, n = 3), TIMP-1 a 1.35-fold increase (p < 0.0001, n = 3), and ICAM-1 a 1.25-fold and 1.34-fold increase (p < 0.0002, n = 3) compared to the CD38-WT and control groups, respectively. No significant change was observed in SDF-1/CXCL12 expression (Fig. 6). These findings suggest that CD38 overexpression enhances the secretion of chemokines and growth factors that promote tumor-supportive immune cell recruitment, establishing an immunosuppressive milieu.

CD38 associations with survival and immune infiltration in human GBM datasets

To further validate the human relevance of CD38-mediated signaling pathways identified in our preclinical model, we performed pairwise correlation analysis of CD38 expression with L1CAM, ICAM1, TIMP1, CXCL10, STAT1, and STAT3 in the CGGA glioblastoma cohort using GlioVis. Spearman rank correlations revealed a strong positive association between CD38 and STAT3 (r = 0.597, p < 0.001), alongside weak but significant positive correlation with L1CAM (r = 0.107, p < 0.05) and negative correlation with TIMP1 (r = −0.111, p < 0.05). No significant correlations were found between CD38 and ICAM1 (r = 0.0613), CXCL10 (r = −0.0181), or STAT1 (r = −0.0788). Among other genes, notable strong positive correlations included ICAM1 with STAT3 (r = 0.724, p < 0.001), L1CAM with CXCL10 (r = 0.598, p < 0.001), and STAT1 with STAT3 (r = 0.778, p < 0.001) (Fig. S2). These patterns suggest interconnected roles for CD38 in JAK-STAT signaling and tumor microenvironment modulation in human GBM, aligning with our observations of upregulated STAT pathways and immunosuppressive myeloid infiltration in CD38-overexpressing tumors.

To evaluate the human relevance of our preclinical findings on tumoral CD38 overexpression, we analyzed public glioblastoma datasets via the GlioVis platform. In the CGGA primary IDH-wildtype GBM cohort (n = 180), no significant association was observed (median survival 13.1 vs. 14.4 months; HR = 1.02, 95% CI 0.74–1.4; log-rank p = 0.8998) (Fig. S3). These results reflect the heterogeneity of human GBM and suggest that CD38′s prognostic impact may be context-dependent (e.g., subtype-specific or influenced by immune infiltration), consistent with its role in modulating the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment as observed preclinically.

To investigate correlations between CD38 expression and myeloid/lymphoid infiltration in human GBM, we analyzed TCGA-GBM data (n = 287) using the TIMER2.0 platform with purity-adjusted partial Spearman's correlation. CD38 showed variable associations with macrophage subsets across methods: positive correlations with M1-like macrophages (e.g., CIBERSORT r = 0.224; CIBERSORT-ABS r = 0.281) and M2-like macrophages (e.g., CIBERSORT-ABS r = 0.283; MCP-COUNTER r = 0.29), but negative with M0 macrophages (e.g., CIBERSORT r = −0.185) and some M1/M2 estimates (e.g., QUANTISEQ M1 r = −0.218). For MDSCs (TIDE method), a strong negative correlation was observed (r = −0.412), suggesting potential inverse relationships in immunosuppressive contexts. CD8+ T cell correlations were mixed, with positive trends in some algorithms (e.g., EPIC r = 0.321; ImmuCellAI cytotoxic r = 0.282) and negative in others (e.g., CIBERSORT r = −0.222). Tregs displayed predominantly negative associations (e.g., CIBERSORT r = −0.412; XCELL r = −0.366). These heterogeneous patterns indicate CD38′s context-dependent role in shaping the GBM immune landscape, particularly in enhancing myeloid-mediated immunosuppression, which supports our preclinical observations of increased TAMs and MDSCs in CD38-overexpressing tumors (Fig.S4).

Discussion

Glioblastoma (GBM) is an aggressive, universally fatal primary brain tumor, with limited clinical progress due to an incomplete understanding of its TME [1]. Despite advances in tumor immunology in cancers like melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer [18], GBM shows minimal clinical benefits due to its heterogeneity, low immunogenicity, immunosuppressive TME, and the blood-brain barrier [4,19]. GBM cells coordinate TME components through signaling networks involving cytokines, chemokines, matrix-remodeling enzymes, and growth factors, supporting biomass synthesis, cellular maintenance, therapy resistance, and tumor survival [[20], [21], [22]] CD38 contributes by hydrolyzing NAD⁺, depleting extracellular NAD⁺, and promoting adenosine-mediated immunosuppression, impairing T, NK, and dendritic cell function while recruiting suppressive cells like MDSCs, regulatory T cells, and regulatory B cells [23,24]. Our findings show that CD38-OE in GBM-bearing mice enables tumor progression and reduces median lifespan.

The adhesion ability of cancer cells enables them to bind more effectively to the extracellular matrix (ECM), facilitating a more dynamic interplay between the cells and their microenvironment. Enhanced attachment capacity supports faster and more efficient colonization and tumor growth [16]. L1CAM, critical for adhesion, migration, signaling, and neural development, is aberrantly expressed in GBM and linked to poor prognosis through enhanced proliferation, invasiveness, and metastasis [17]. The associations between L1CAM expression, tumor progression, and poor clinical outcomes have also been reported across multiple other cancer types [[25], [26], [27], [28]]It has been shown that L1CAM-decorated extracellular vesicles can enhance the proliferation of glioma cells [17]. Interestingly, recent studies in ovarian cancer cell lines demonstrated that L1CAM also regulates anchorage-independent cell growth and knockout of L1CAM expression in these cells markedly reduced tumor cell proliferation and tumorigenicity [29]. In our study, comparison of L1CAM expression between CD38-OE and CD38-WT GBM-bearing mice revealed a positive correlation between CD38 and L1CAM expression. Giordano et al. reported that L1CAM activates fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1), leading to SRC-mediated STAT3 phosphorylation, and its inhibition reduces cancer stemness [30]. Our results indicated that total STAT3 and FGFR1 levels were comparable across groups, however, phosphorylated STAT3 (Tyr705) was significantly higher in the CD38-OE group. Accordingly, the enhanced tumor progression observed in CD38-OE GBM-bearing mice, characterized by elevated levels of both L1CAM and phosphorylated STAT3, suggests a potential mechanism by which CD38 may promote GBM progression. Nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) promotes inflammation-driven oncogenesis by enhancing proliferation, apoptosis inhibition, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, metastasis, angiogenesis, immune evasion, and resistance [31]. Our data showed increase in NF-κB levels in CD38-OE versus CD38-WT groups, thus, CD38-OE seems to be positively correlated with GBM progression. Additionally, CD38 overexpression depletes NAD⁺, which activates STAT3 and promotes EMT[32], drives L1CAM expression via the FGFR1/SRC/STAT3 axis[33], and modulates STAT1 signaling[34]. These mechanisms likely underlie the upregulation of L1CAM, STAT1/3 phosphorylation, and NF-κB observed in our CD38-OE tumors.

In our study, CXCL10/IP-10 secretion was elevated in CD38-OE compared to CD38-WT and controls, indicating microglial activation. This suggests that CD38-OE may promote CXCL10/IP-10 secretion through microglial activation and CXCL10/IP-10 plays a significant role in leukocyte homing to inflamed tissues and is involved in both tumorigenesis [35,36]. It has been demonstrated that CXCL10/IP-10 is produced in a STAT3-dependent manner [37] which aligns with the significantly elevated levels of phosphorylated STAT3 (Tyr705) observed following CD38-OE compared to CD38-WT GBM-bearing mice. Therefore, it appears that the association between CD38-OE and increased CXCL10/IP-10 production mediated by enhanced STAT3 activation may represent a potential mechanism by which the TME shifts from a tumor-suppressive to a tumor-supportive milieu.

GAMs infiltration via GBM-secreted ICAM-1, regulated by the fatty acid synthase (FASN) pathway, correlates with GAM density[38], and leukocyte recruitment to inflammatory sites. Our results showed a positive correlation between CD38-OE and ICAM-1 expression. The FASN pathway regulates STAT1and disrupting the FASN-STAT1 axis impairs macrophage recruitment [31]. Our results revealed that total STAT1 and phosphorylated STAT1 (Tyr701) were higher in CD38-OE than in CD38-WT. The consistent correlation between upregulated ICAM-1 expression and CD38-OE implicates the STAT1–ICAM-1–CD38 signaling pathway, potentially involving FASN, in CD38-mediated macrophage recruitment to the GBM TME.

Tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-1 (TIMP-1) regulates ECM remodeling and is linked to progression and poor outcomes in cancers [39]. TIMP-1 downregulation in GBM reduces proliferation and increases apoptosis [40]. We found that TIMP-1 was upregulated in CD38-OE versus parental and controls, with no change detected in SDF-1/CXCL12. These finding suggest a potential CD38-TIMP-1 crosstalk enhancing tumor progression through ECM remodeling.

We observed that the populations of T-regs, monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs), and polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) increased in CD38-OE mice compared to CD38-WT. MDSCs subsets are known to suppress tumor-specific effector T cell functions. Elevated STAT3 signaling is recognized as a hallmark feature of PMN-MDSCs in both humans and mice [41]. Moreover, PMN-MDSC-mediated immunosuppression has been shown to occur predominantly in an antigen-specific manner, largely driven by enhanced STAT3 activation, whereas M-MDSCs exert suppressive effects in both antigen-specific and non-specific contexts via STAT1 and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)-mediated mechanisms. Additionally, M-MDSCs are generally considered to possess stronger immunosuppressive potential than PMN-MDSCs [42,43]. Commensurate with these prior studies, we found significantly elevated levels of M-MDSCs and PMN-MDSCs accompanied by increased STAT1/P-STAT1, and STAT3 respectively, indicated that the immunosuppressive effects mediated by MDSCs through STAT1 and STAT3 activation.

CD38 may be a pivotal immunoregulatory molecule orchestrating the tumor microenvironment to propel glioma progression. CD38-OE elevates L1CAM, ICAM-1, TIMP-1, STAT1, phosphorylated STAT1/STAT3, NF-κB, and CXCL10/IP-10, coinciding with increased MDSCs and GAMs. CD38 drives macrophage recruitment and ECM remodeling via the STAT1-ICAM-1 axis, possibly involving FASN, and immunosuppression through JAK/STAT signaling. Targeting CD38 or its downstream effectors presents immediate opportunities for biomarker validation, clinical evaluation, and innovative therapeutic strategies to counteract immunosuppression and improve clinical outcomes in GBM.
