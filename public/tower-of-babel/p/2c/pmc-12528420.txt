Emotional and behavioral problems and its associated factors among pre-school children of Mekelle, Tigray, Ethiopia: community based cross sectional study
Authors: Kalayu Mebrahtu Kiros, Tesfalem Araya Amare, Kokob Gebru Kidanu, Solomon Gidey Welu, Dawit Bekuretsion Gerlase, Jemal Seid Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Early childhood is a vital period for emotional, social, and behavioral development. During this stage, children may begin to show signs of emotional and behavioral problems (EBDs), yet this age group is often underrepresented in mental health research, especially in low-resource settings. Early detection and intervention are critical to prevent persistence of these problems into later life stages. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and factors associated with emotional and behavioral problems among preschool-aged children in Mekelle, Tigray, Ethiopia. A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from April to May 2021, involving 633 preschool children selected through systematic random sampling. Emotional and behavioral problems were measured using the parent-reported Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ). Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify significant predictors. The total difficulties score indicated that 17.7% of children were at risk for emotional and behavioral problems. Among the SDQ subscales, conduct problems were the most prevalent (29.6%), followed by emotional problems (28.5%), pro-social difficulties (27.4%),hyperactivity (19.7%) and peer problems(11.6%).Factors significantly associated with emotional and behavioral difficulties included children aged 5–6 years (AOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.34–0.98), preterm birth (AOR 2.94, 95% CI 1.15–7.48), lack of breastfeeding (AOR 8.45, 95% CI 1.29–55.80), presence of health problems (AOR 3.51, 95% CI 1.31–9.42),caregivers without formal education (AOR 12.55, 95% CI 1.68–93.72), care by relatives other than parents (AOR 2.02, 95% CI 1.12–3.66),uneducated mothers (AOR 2.88, 95% CI 1.05–7.87), fathers employed by government or NGOs (AOR 0.42, 95% CI 0.20–0.85), widowed parents (AOR 4.34, 95% CI 1.21–15.67), caregivers with substance use history (AOR 3.82, 95% CI 1.08–13.51) and caregivers experiencing mental distress (AOR 3.48, 95% CI 1.99–6.06). Emotional and behavioral problems are common among preschool children in Mekelle. Early identification and provision of targeted support to caregivers, particularly addressing modifiable risk factors, could improve developmental outcomes for children in this setting.

Introduction

Emotional and behavioral problems (EBPs) are common among children and can significantly affect their cognitive, social and academic development. These challenges often begin in early childhood and, if left unaddressed, may persist into adolescence and adulthood, leading to long-term consequences such as poor academic performance, impaired social functioning and mental health issues1,2. In recent decades, studies across various countries and cultures have reported a rise in the prevalence of these problems, signaling growing concern about child and adolescent mental health globally3–5.

The pre-school period is a particularly sensitive stage for the emergence of EBPs. Children at this age may begin to exhibit symptoms that, while not always meeting full diagnostic criteria for a psychiatric disorder, still suggest developmental risk and warrant clinical attention. These problems often manifest as conduct issues, hyperactivity, emotional distress, or social withdrawal and can interfere with daily functioning, relationships and learning6. While some definitions such as those related to attention deficit hyperactivity disorder remain imprecise in this age group, behavior problems are generally characterized by persistent patterns that deviate from expected social norms and hinder positive interaction with others.

Studies have attempted to quantify the prevalence of EBPs among young children. Global estimates vary, with some literature suggesting rates between 9.5% and 14.2% among children under the age of five7,8. A study in Salvador, Brazil, using the Child Behavior Checklist, found that 23.5% of pre-school children exhibited behavioral issues, including internalizing (9.7%) and externalizing (25.2%) symptoms9. The World Health Organization estimates that 5% to 15% of children aged two to five in low-income countries experience some form of mental health problem10. Data from various countries also reflect substantial variation: for instance, 2.9% in Kosovo12, 13% in Kenya13, 17.7% in Ethiopia’s Ambo district14 and 43.6% among HIV-positive children and adolescents in West Gojjam zone, Ethiopia15.

Multiple factors have been associated with the development of EBPs in early childhood. These include socio-economic disadvantage16, maternal physical and mental health9, cesarean section delivery17, caregiver’s age and educational level5, maternal employment status, number of siblings and changes in caregiver arrangements during early life. Additional risk factors include child’s health condition, parental marital status, parental age, parental loss, caregiver distress and limited family support14,15,18.

Despite growing recognition of early childhood mental health, there is a noticeable gap in the literature regarding the prevalence and determinants of EBPs among pre-school children in Ethiopia particularly in the Tigray region. Understanding the mental health status of young children in this context is essential, as early emotional and behavioral difficulties not only affect the child’s wellbeing but also strain family systems, schools and health services. Timely identification of such problems and their associated risk factors can inform the design of culturally sensitive interventions, improve access to care and support optimal developmental outcomes.

Therefore, this study aims to assess the prevalence and associated factors of emotional and behavioral problems among pre-school children in Mekelle City, Tigray, Ethiopia. Given that children represent one of the most vulnerable segments of society, the findings will provide valuable insight for mental health professionals, social workers, educators and policymakers. The results are expected to inform preventive strategies, guide early intervention efforts and support future research focused on improving the psychological wellbeing of children in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study areas and period

This study was conducted in Mekelle, the capital city of the Tigray Regional State, located approximately 783 km north of Addis Ababa, the capital of the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia. Geographically, the city is situated at 13° 32′ N latitude and 39° 28′ E longitude. It lies at an average elevation of 2300 m above sea level, with an annual average temperature of 22 °C and a mean annual rainfall of 629 mm. According to recent estimates, the total population of Mekelle is approximately 528,897, of whom 42,312 are children aged between 2 and 6 years19. Administratively, the city is divided into seven sub-cities Ayder, Kedamay Weyane, Hadnet, Quiha, Adi-Haqi, Hawelti and Semien which are further subdivided into 35 tabias and 105 ketenas.

Mekelle is served by one public specialized hospital Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital as well as three general hospitals: Mekelle Hospital, Quiha Hospital and the Defense Hospital. In addition, there are eight government-owned health centers distributed across the city. Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital has been providing both referral and non-referral services since 2008 G.C. and serves an estimated 8 million people in its catchment areas across the Tigray, Afar and southeastern Amara regions20. The data collection for this study was carried out between April and May 2021.

Study design

This study employed a community-based, cross-sectional, quantitative design using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire.

Source population and study population

Source population: The source population was all children of Mekelle city.

Study population: All 2–6 years of age children of Mekelle city, during the study period.

Eligibility criteria:

Inclusion criteria: all children aged 2 to 6 years old who lived in the study area six months and above during data collection period were included in this study.

Exclusion criteria:

Children who are with severe illness and deformities which cause difficulty for measurement and communication problems or other severe conditions which interfere the interview.

House hold without pre-school children.

Refusal to participate and caretakers who are not available for interview during two visits.

Sampling technique and procedure

A simple random sampling technique was used to select four sub-cities from the total seven sub-cities in Mekelle. From each selected sub-city, three tabias were randomly chosen, making a total of twelve tabias included in the study (Fig. 1). The total sample size was then proportionally allocated to each tabia based on the population size of eligible children in that area.

A complete list of households within each selected tabia was obtained from the respective sub-city administration and used as the sampling frame. Within each household, only one child was selected for the study. In cases where more than one eligible child was present, one was randomly selected.

Systematic random sampling was applied to select study participants within each tabia. The sampling interval (k-value) for each tabia was calculated by dividing the total number of eligible households by the number of participants required from that tabia. The first household was selected randomly from the range of 1 to k and then every kth household was included in the study. The primary caregiver of the selected child in each household was interviewed.

In instances where the selected household declined to participate or no eligible child was present, the next immediate household was selected. If that was also not eligible, the team proceeded to the next-nearest household (± 1 or ± 2 from the originally selected number).

Data collection procedure and instruments/tools

Data collection procedure

Data were collected by eight trained data collectors, each holding a Bachelor of Science degree in psychiatric nursing. Prior to data collection, they underwent a one-day training to ensure consistency and quality. Using the sampling frames obtained from the tabia administrators, households with pre-school children were systematically selected from each chosen tabia in the selected sub-cities. Within each selected household, the primary caregiver was interviewed and provided information on behalf of the child. In cases where more than one eligible child lived in the household, the lottery method was applied to randomly select the child for the interview.

Data collection instruments/tools

Information was gathered through face-to-face interviews with the primary caregiver of each selected child. Trained data collectors administered a semi-structured questionnaire designed to capture relevant information for the study. The questionnaire was organized into four key sections:

To assess emotional and behavioral concerns, the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) was used. This tool is a brief, structured screening instrument developed to identify common emotional and behavioral issues in children. It includes 25 items grouped into five subscales: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention, peer relationship difficulties and prosocial behavior21. An additional section, the impact supplement, explores the extent to which any identified difficulties interfere with the child’s daily life. The SDQ has demonstrated good reliability and validity across different settings, with studies reporting a sensitivity of 85% and a specificity of 80% in detecting child mental health problems15.

The entire questionnaire, including the SDQ, was initially translated from English into Tigrigna, the local language spoken in Mekelle. A separate translator, not involved in the initial translation, then back-translated the tool into English. Differences between the original and back-translated versions were reviewed and resolved through consultation with professionals experienced in child mental health and bilingual translation, ensuring both clarity and cultural relevance.

Data quality assurance

To assure the data quality high emphasis was given in designing data collection instrument. Before data collection the questionnaire was checked its simplicity, clarity and understandability. The questionnaire which was initially prepared in English was translated in to Tigrigna version and then the Tigrigna version was again translated back to English to check for consistency. One day training was provided for data collectors and supervisors. The questionnaire was also pre tested on 5% two weeks prior to the main data collection time and necessary corrections and amendment was taken.

During data collection, supervisor was checking filled questionnaires for consistency, completeness and accuracy on daily base. For enhanced quality control, the supervisor was randomly select one surveyed community each day and revisits the houses to make sure the data were accurately collected. Supportive supervision by investigators was also provided for each data collectors.

After data collection, checking and re-checking of the data was employed to identify whether the data was completely filled or not by double data entry.

Data analysis procedure

The collected data were entered into Epi Info version 4.2.2 and later transferred to SPSS version 23.0 for analysis. Before analysis, the data underwent careful coding, cleaning and verification to ensure accuracy and completeness. During this process, 5 questionnaires out of 633 (about 0.79%) were removed due to missing key information. These missing data were assumed to be random, so only complete records were analyzed without any imputation. The small amount of missing data is unlikely to have affected the study’s results.

Descriptive statistics, such as means, standard deviations and percentages, were used to summarize the characteristics of the participants. First, bivariate logistic regression was performed to explore the relationship between each independent variable and emotional and behavioral problems. Variables with a p-value below 0.20 in this step were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Before running the multivariable analysis, we checked for multi collinearity among the independent variables by calculating Variance Inflation Factors (VIF). None of the variables showed VIF values above 10, indicating no serious multi collinearity, so all were kept in the final model.

Finally, multivariable logistic regression was carried out to identify factors independently linked to emotional and behavioral problems among pre-school children, adjusting for other variables. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The findings are presented through tables, figures and descriptive text to make the results clear and easy to understand.

Prevalence and gender based distribution of emotional and behavioral problems

A total of 628 children participated in the study, of whom 111 (17.7%) were identified as having emotional and behavioral problems (EBPs). Among the different areas assessed by the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire, conduct problems were the most frequently reported (29.6%), followed by emotional problems (28.5%), difficulties with pro-social behavior (27.4%), hyperactivity (19.7%) and peer problems (11.6%) (Fig. 2). The findings also indicated that EBPs were more prevalent among girls than boys (Fig. 3).

Bivariable and multivariable analysis of Emotional and Behavioral Problems among pre-school children of Mekelle, Tigray, Ethiopia 2021

Logistic regression analysis identified several factors significantly associated with emotional and behavioral disorders (EBD) among pre-school children. Children whose caregivers had no formal education were about 12 times more likely to have EBD compared to those whose caregivers had formal education (AOR 12.55; 95% CI 1.68–93.72). Similarly, children cared for by relatives other than their parents had twice the odds of EBD compared to those cared for by their parents (AOR 2.02; 95% CI 1.12–3.66). Children whose mothers had no formal education were nearly three times more likely to have EBD (AOR 2.88; 95% CI 1.05–7.87). Conversely, children whose fathers were employed by the government or NGOs had a lower likelihood of EBD (AOR 0.42; 95% CI 0.20–0.85).

Caregivers with a history of substance use were associated with nearly four times higher odds of EBD in children (AOR 3.82; 95% CI 1.08–13.51) and those caregivers experiencing mental distress were more than three times as likely to have children with EBD (AOR 3.48; 95% CI 1.99–6.06). Children aged 5–6 years were less likely to have EBD compared to those aged 2–4 years (AOR 0.58; 95% CI 0.34–0.98). Children born preterm had almost three times greater odds of EBD than those born at full term (AOR 2.94; 95% CI 1.15–7.48).

Discussion

This study explored the prevalence and associated factors of emotional and behavioral disorders (EBD) among pre-school children in Mekelle, Tigray. Based on the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) total difficulties score, the prevalence of EBD was found to be 17.7%. The most frequently reported difficulties were conduct problems (29.6%), emotional problems (28.5%), prosocial challenges (27.4%), hyperactivity (19.7%) and peer problems (11.6%).

The observed prevalence is consistent with a similar community-based study conducted in the Ambo district, Ethiopia, which reported the same rate (17.7%)14. However, the figure is higher than those reported in various other settings, including urban Ethiopia (10.9%)22, East China (13.6%)17, Kenya (13%)13, Butajira (6.5%)23 and India (2.9%)12. Differences in prevalence may reflect variations in the age groups studied, data collection methods and sociocultural contexts. Unlike many of the comparison studies that focused on school-aged children, our study targeted younger, pre-school-aged children a group particularly sensitive to developmental disruptions and psychosocial stressors.

In contrast, the prevalence found here is lower than that reported in West Gojjam, Amara Region (43.6%)15 and Salvador, Brazil (23.5%)9. These discrepancies could be due to differences in assessment tools; for example, the Amara study used the Pediatric Symptom Checklist (PSCL), which may capture a broader range of behaviors compared to the SDQ. Furthermore, institutional or clinic-based samples may report higher rates due to referral or selection bias, whereas our study employed a community-based approach.Notably, children whose caregivers had no formal education were significantly more likely to present with emotional or behavioral issues. Similarly, children raised by relatives rather than biological parents had increased likelihood of EBD. These findings are consistent with the idea that stable and responsive caregiving often provided by biological parents is essential for early emotional development15,24.

Caregiver-related factors played a central role. For instance, caregiver substance use (AOR 3.82; 95% CI 1.08–13.51) and caregiver mental distress (AOR 3.48; 95% CI 1.99–6.06) were both strongly associated with child EBD. While the cross-sectional design of this study prevents conclusions about causality, these results are in line with previous research indicating that caregiver well-being profoundly affects a child’s emotional health15,25.

Children who were never breastfed, born preterm, or whose mothers lacked formal education also showed higher odds of EBD symptoms. These findings reflect the importance of early-life biological and social environments in shaping a child’s developmental trajectory. On the other hand, having a father employed in the government or NGO sector appeared to be associated with lower odds of EBD. This may reflect greater household stability or access to resources and health-related knowledge, though further study is needed to clarify this relationship.

Family disruption also emerged as a relevant factor. Children who had experienced the loss of a parent or were living with a widowed caregiver were more likely to exhibit emotional or behavioral concerns, reinforcing the idea that early adverse experiences can leave lasting effects on children’s psychological well-being.

Although strong associations were identified, the findings should be interpreted as correlations, not evidence of direct cause-and-effect. Longitudinal research is needed to determine whether and how these risk factors contribute to the development of emotional and behavioral disorders over time.

Strength

One of the strengths of this study is its community-based approach, which allowed for the collection of data from a representative sample of pre-school children in Mekelle.

The use of a standardized and validated tool, the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), adds reliability to the assessment of emotional and behavioral problems. Additionally, the large sample size and high response rate (99.21%) strengthen the generalizability of the findings to similar settings.

Limitation

First, family monthly income was measured by asking participants to estimate their monthly earnings, which may not accurately reflect their true economic status. Using a more comprehensive measure such as a wealth index could have provided a better assessment of socioeconomic status.

Second, the cross-sectional design of this study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the variables. As a result, the findings represent associations rather than direct cause-and-effect links.

Conclusion and recommendation

Conclusion

This study provides meaningful insights into the prevalence and contributing factors of EBD among pre-school children in a community setting in Ethiopia. It is among the few studies in the country focusing specifically on early childhood a stage where early intervention can have lasting impacts. By identifying unique predictors such as caregiver mental health, substance use, breastfeeding history and non-parent caregiving, the study highlights several areas where community-level mental health interventions can be targeted. These findings can support the development of early childhood mental health policies and services tailored to the Ethiopian context.
