Feed Modification for Improved Energy and Protein Provision for Infants and Young Children in the Pediatric Critical Care Setting: A Scoping Review
Authors: Jacinta Winderlich, Bridget S Little, Cassandra Bendall, Alice Anderson, Felix Oberender, Andrew A Udy, Emma J Ridley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this review was to explore key definitions, concepts, methods, and evidence for feed modification for improved energy and protein provision for infants and young children in the pediatric critical care setting. Attaining nutrition targets in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) is challenging, with suboptimal nutrition associated with poor outcomes. Modification of oral and enteral feeds, via supplementation or substitution with concentrated formula, to increase energy and protein provision is a common strategy utilized to address these gaps. However, there is limited literature and no expert consensus to guide this practice. We conducted a scoping review following JBI (Joanna Briggs Institute) methodology of all English-language studies (excluding reviews) exploring feed modification in the PICU in children aged up to 5 years published between 2010 and January 4, 2025. Of 2696 studies screened, 14 were included, with 57% (n = 8) published since 2020. Ten (71%) studies were randomized controlled trials and 8 (80%) of these explored congenital heart disease. Interventions comprised concentrated infant formula powder (n = 3; 30%), ready-to-feed liquid formula (n = 3; 30%), and expressed breast milk (n = 2; 20%). Primary outcomes varied with nutritional intake-related (n = 5; 50%) measures most frequently utilized. No consistent terminology was used to refer to “feed modification". Tolerance, ease of preparation, and contamination risk were identified as important considerations. In conclusion, a variety of techniques were used, with no consistent definitions, and the randomized trials were inconsistent in methodology and did not measure patient-centered outcomes. Further research is required to reach consensus for practice guidelines following further establishment of methods of feed modification that address nutrition gaps and improve patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Role of Feed Modification

In pediatric practice, therapeutic nutrition intervention is required across a range of acute and chronic illnesses within a variety of settings, including inpatient, outpatient, community, and home.1 Therapeutic nutrition intervention is required when a prolonged nutrition deficit exists as a result of increased requirements, decreased intake, or both. Without such intervention, nutrition deficits among infants and children negatively impact recovery and function, and growth and development over the longer term.2–4

Therapeutic intervention for optimization of nutritional intake for infants and young children may be achieved via “feed modification.” We define feed modification as the addition of macronutrient components or supplements to a child’s “feed” or use of a specialized formula with an energy or protein density higher than standard. “Feeds” refer broadly to expressed breast milk (EBM), infant formula, and any formula consumed orally or administered via a tube for children who require enteral nutrition support. The intention of feed modification, whether achieved via additions to a child’s usual feed or the use of a specialized feed, is to increase energy and nutrient density for improved nutritional intake within the volume consumed or administered via a tube. Feed modification is utilized in many areas of pediatric clinical practice and has been most extensively researched in premature infants.5–7

Role of Feed Modification in the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit

Nutrition deficits are frequently experienced by children in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) due to the illness or condition requiring admission and the necessary critical care interventions.8,9 An array of feed-modification strategies are anecdotally known to be utilized in the PICU to address these gaps and there is probably variation depending on geographical location. Key published PICU nutrition practice guidelines state “how” we should feed in the PICU and the energy and protein targets we should aim for; however, these guidelines do not currently address recommended feed type or feed modification,10,11 reflecting the evidence gaps in this area of practice.

Why Is Research Into Feed Modification in the PICU Important?

While known gaps remain between energy and protein targets and actual provision,12,13 it is essential for interventions including feed modification in the PICU to be explored further. Our group has started to explore this area of practice by describing current feed-modification use in our region.14

Relative to other critical care interventions, nutrition therapy is generally easy and inexpensive to implement. This area of research also aligns with published research priorities, including restrictive fluid management,15 understanding if there is an optimal feed type,16 and supporting breastfeeding-related activities.17 The potential establishment of improved outcomes via improved nutrition provision with feed modification is therefore worthy of research attention.

Objectives of the Review

To our knowledge, there has been no comprehensive review of feed modification for improved energy and protein provision in the pediatric critical care setting. This was confirmed by a preliminary search for existing scoping and systematic reviews conducted as per JBI methodology,18 searching Medline via Ovid, CINAHL via EBSCO, PROSPERO, Cochrane Database for Systematic Reviews, and JBI Evidence Synthesis. A review is necessary to identify evidence gaps and guide future research to improve nutrition provision in the PICU. Given the many unknowns, anticipated variation in practice, and limited evidence, the current questions regarding feed modification in the PICU are best addressed via a scoping review.

The objective of this scoping review was to explore key definitions, concepts, methods, and evidence for feed modification for improved energy and protein provision for infants and young children in the pediatric critical care setting. METHODS

The review was undertaken following the methodology of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis.18 An expert group was established including pediatric critical care dietitians, a pediatric intensivist, an adult critical care dietitian and intensivist, and a health sciences librarian. The objectives, inclusion criteria, and methods for this scoping review were specified in advance and published online on October 30, 2022 (https://www.clinicalnutritionopenscience.com/article/S2667-2685(22)00051-1/pdf).19 The review is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) framework and checklist.20,21 The overall purpose of the scoping review was to map the key definitions, concepts, methods, and evidence for feed modification for improved energy and protein provision in infants and young children in the pediatric critical care setting. Eligibility Criteria and Information Sources

Participants

Studies enrolling infants and young children up to the age of 5 years were included. The scoping review was limited to this age group to focus on the range of feed-modification options available including EBM and infant formula. In the absence of an otherwise natural cutoff, a pragmatic approach was taken in the selection of an arbitrary upper age limit of 5 years (see Table 2 for scoping review study eligibility criteria).

Concept

Literature considered for inclusion was that focused on the use of “non-standard” feed options for the purpose of increased energy or protein provision (with “standard” feed options defined as EBM and infant formula providing ∼280 kJ/100 mL for those up to ∼12 months and nutritionally complete pediatric formula providing ∼420 kJ/100 mL [or 1.0 calorie/mL] for those older than 12 months).

Context

The literature covering all settings was initially included given the anticipated limited PICU-specific studies and due to anticipated learnings from other clinical areas. General ward- and home-based settings are relevant for children discharged from the PICU who require ongoing nutrition support. However, the PICU-specific studies only are presented and the additional non–PICU-specific studies are outlined in Appendix V in the Supplementary Material. All geographic locations were included.

Types of Sources of Evidence

Primary research studies of any design (excluding case reports and reviews) were included. Evidence was limited to literature published from 2010 to the present to ensure that data would be contemporary. Only English-language sources were included due to limited study resources and feasibility purposes.

Updates to Methods Since Protocol Publication

Following the iterative process of a scoping review, since publication the protocol was updated to exclude literature focused on premature infants, gray literature, and review publications. This was to ensure that this scoping review would be appropriately focused on the data that best address the review questions.

Search Strategy

Key words and subject terms were identified and refined through consultation within the team, including the health sciences librarian. The search strategy was optimized in Medline on the Ovid platform before translation to additional databases. A systematic search was then conducted in Medline, Emcare, and Embase via Ovid and CINAHL via EBSCO (see Appendix I in the Supplementary Material for the full search strategy for each database). In addition, reference lists and cited articles were added to the results from 10 studies selected for their quality and relevance. Initial database searches were completed on November 15, 2022, and were last updated on January 4, 2025, covering the years 2010 to early 2025. Conference papers that progressed to full-text screening were investigated for subsequent peer-reviewed publications, and the references of relevant reviews retrieved by the search were checked against the resulting set. Unpublished studies and ongoing trials were searched in the US National Institutes of Health Ongoing Trials Register (http://clinicaltrials.gov) and the World Health Organization (WHO) International Clinical Trials Registry (http://apps.who.int/trialsearch/).

Selection of Sources of Evidence

On completion of the search, records were collated in EndNote reference manager software (version x9; Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA) and an author (J.W.) removed duplicates and obviously irrelevant articles using pre-specified criteria.

Studies were uploaded to the Covidence review management system (https://www.covidence.org). Source selection pilot testing was undertaken on a random sample of 25 articles by 2 reviewers (J.W. and B.S.L.). Discrepancies were debated in order to identify any required eligibility criteria adjustments. Screening of the full search commenced when 75% agreement was achieved in pilot testing.

Title and abstract screening was completed independently by 2 reviewers (J.W. and B.S.L. or C.B.) in accordance with inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were discussed and, if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (E.J.R.) was introduced. Full-text screening was also completed independently by 2 reviewers (J.W. and B.S.L. or C.B.), with consensus achieved via discussion among 3 reviewers. Exclusions following full-text review are outlined in Appendix II in the Supplementary Material.

Data Charting Process

The data charting tool was pilot-tested on 2 sources by 2 reviewers (J.W. and B.S.L.). The tool was then updated following consensus among the authorship group (Appendix III in the Supplementary Material). Changes included the addition of further data categories deemed relevant to the review. Data charting was undertaken independently by 2 reviewers and consensus completed by a third reviewer (J.W. and B.S.L. or C.B.).

Data Items

Charted data included details regarding the participants (number, age, diagnostic group), concept (study design, aim, method of feed modification, definitions), context (setting), and concepts relevant to the review questions. Refer to Appendix III for information on the data charting tool.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

The results of the review are outlined in tabular and figure format, and categorized according to identified themes and trends. Frequency counts (n [%]) are provided in addition to the tabular data. Tables and figures are accompanied by a narrative summary and organized under main headings determined by the research questions.

RESULTS

The search generated 3133 citations, which were reduced to 2696 following removal of duplicates, with 405 undergoing full-text review. A total of 26 full-text articles were retrieved and assessed as eligible for inclusion (Figure 1). Fourteen (54%) of these were focused on the PICU. The 12 remaining studies, presented in Appendix V, were otherwise conducted in inpatient and outpatient community settings, or both, including remote telehealth interventions.

PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) Flow Chart

Among all 14 PICU studies, 8 studies (57%) were published since 2020. Thirteen (93%) were conducted in high- or upper-middle-income countries according to World Bank criteria (Figure 2).22 Six (43%) were conducted in China, and 2 studies (14%) each were conducted in Brazil and the United States. Interventional studies accounted for 10 (71%), with all being randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Observational studies comprised 4 studies (29%), and among these, 3 studies were prospective and 1 study was a retrospective cohort study. Informed consent was utilized in 12 (86%) of the included studies, and no publications reported on whether consumers were involved in study development.

Methods of Feed Modification

Among the 10 PICU-based RCTs, enteral nutrition–only feeding was the intervention route in 4 (40%) studies, and 5 studies (50%) included both oral and enteral feeding. The remaining study did not specify if oral nutrition provision in addition to enteral was included in the intervention.

Concentrated infant formula powder and ready-to-feed liquid formula were the interventions in 3 (30%) studies each. Additions to EBM comprised the intervention in 2 (18%) studies, and protein modules were utilized as the intervention in 2 (18%) studies.

Reported Outcome Measures

The primary or first reported outcome measure was in the nutritional intake–related domain in half of the studies (50%) or anthropometry domain for 3 (30%) studies. Nutritional intake outcome measures included average energy delivery,26 daily protein delivery,27 nutrient intake (energy, carbohydrate, protein, and fat),28 and caloric attainment rate (defined as >80% Schofield estimate).29 Anthropometry outcome measures included weight gain after 7 days,30 weight-for age z-score and weight-gain rate,31 and weight at discharge.32 One study utilized mortality as the primary outcome33 and another utilized nitrogen balance.34

Key Concepts Among All Included Studies—Reported Benefits, Risks, Logistical Issues

Valuable considerations related to feed modification were noted across all 14 included studies. Mechanisms to avoid harm associated with feed modification included an “early success” criteria for development of hyperproteinemia or hyperuremia in the absence of impaired renal function.34 This study also noted that 1 patient experienced obstruction of the feeding tube, which was attributed to the viscosity of the formula.

With regard to benefits of specific methods of feed modification, fortification using a single additive was highlighted as a simple method.23 Pasteurized ready-to-feed liquid formula was highlighted as preferable for reduced risk of contamination.23 Zhang et al35 required formula to be prepared every 3 hours to prevent bacterial growth. Protein-only supplementation was noted to be of benefit in those who would experience excess energy intake if feed modification otherwise provided additional energy.27 A gradual increase in feed modification was recommended to allow for assessment of tolerance,35 especially if a hyperosmolar formula was to be provided.25 Reported study limitations included sample size,23,31 inconsistent anthropometric measures,23 impact of diuretics and edema on weight,23,24,30 and recall bias due to parent-reported measures.31

DISCUSSION

This is the first scoping review, to our knowledge, to explore key definitions, concepts, methods, and evidence for feed modification for increased energy and protein provision for infants and young children in the pediatric critical care setting. Evidence-based nutrition strategies are required to address energy and protein deficits in critically ill infants and young children given the negative outcomes associated with undernutrition.

General Study Characteristics

According to the pre-specified review criteria, we identified 26 studies to broadly address the research questions and then presented findings related to the 14 PICU studies. Despite including studies published since 2010, over half (57%) of the studies were published in the most recent 5 years. This aligns with the overall increase in pediatric critical care nutrition research and potentially reflects feed modification as an increasing priority for study. All geographic regions are not yet well represented, with no studies conducted in the Middle East and Africa. Research to date has also not yet explored all settings evenly according to economy: high- and upper-middle-income countries are represented in 93% (n = 13) of the included studies. Evidence-based nutrition strategies must be established for all regions and levels of resourcing.

Interventional studies accounted for the majority (71%; n = 10) of all studies. This is positive progress for feed-modification research when compared with areas dominated by observational study designs. Across all studies, informed consent was involved in all except 2 studies. Beyond standard good research practice, it could be assumed that parents and carers would feel strongly about being informed and consenting to participation in a study that would determine the nutrition provided to their child.

With regard to the potentially controversial area of industry-sponsored nutrition research, only 2 studies (14%) were sponsored by commercial nutrition product companies. In the absence of alternative research funding, where no alternative products are available on the market, and where breastfeeding and human milk are not being limited or investigated, industry funding may remain a potential financial support for studies pending strict declaration of conflicts and delineation of roles for researchers and funders.

Feed-Modification Definitions

Although we have utilized the all-encompassing term “feed modification” for this review to refer to the area of nutrition practice that involves manipulating oral or enteral feeds to increase energy or protein density, as expected we found that there was no consistency in use of terms across the included studies. A key word visualization within a PICU nutrition research intelligence review has also demonstrated that there is currently no consistently used term.36 It is likely that language describing these therapeutic nutrition interventions will evolve and a consistent term will establish itself as research activity continues and this area of practice is incorporated into published practice recommendations. In the meantime, and until an alternative is proposed and broadly accepted, we suggest the use of the term “feed modification.” Other terms including “concentration,” “fortification,” and “enriched” do not appear to capture the full range of methods relevant for infants and young children—including fortified breast milk, powdered formulas, and ready-to-feed liquid formulas.

Study Populations

Research focused on children with congenital heart disease comprised the majority of the included studies (n = 9 [64% of all studies] and n = 8 [80% of RCTs]). This condition-specific group among the heterogeneous general PICU patient population is well known to experience nutrition challenges and require intervention.37,38 Limiting participants to those from a particular diagnostic group controls for some variability that is otherwise inherent to a general patient population.

Randomized Controlled Trials

Feed-Modification Methods

Both enteral and oral provision of modified feeds were been considered among the trials, which is reassuring given that a proportion of children receiving either mode of nutrition delivery are known to not meet their energy and protein targets. Our group found in a study of nutrition provision across Australia and New Zealand PICUs that, among children reliant on enteral nutrition only on PICU admission day 7 (and where targets were known), 64% received less than 75% of their estimated energy and protein requirements.13 This study also found that, among those receiving oral nutrition on PICU day 7, only 1 in 5 patients were meeting at least 50% of their estimated requirements. We also found among children discharged to the ward that, in those receiving oral nutrition only on the first full ward day, 36% received less than 50% of their estimated energy and protein requirements.12 In those provided enteral nutrition only, and where targets were known, on day 28 of their admission at least one-third did not meet their estimated energy and protein requirements.

Concentrated infant formula powder was found to be one of the most common interventions (30%; n = 3). This refers to adding less water per scoop of powder than as advised on the label or as per “standard” preparation. Potential benefits of this method include use of a readily available product, increased provision of protein and micronutrients in addition to energy, and the ability to continue this intervention beyond the PICU and hospital admission at home with careful instruction and monitoring. However, potential risks include bacterial contamination during the preparation of the feed and the proliferation of any present bacteria following reconstitution (particularly Cronobacter) throughout any duration of storage and if administration at room temperature is prolonged via a tube and pump over several hours.39,40 Increased osmolality of the concentrated feed also introduces potential risks, including vomiting and diarrhea and necrotizing enterocolitis in those with premature and poorly perfused gastrointestinal tracts.41–43

Ready-to-feed increased energy and protein density liquid infant formula was also an equally most common intervention (30%; n = 3). This product reverses the potential benefits and risks of concentrated infant formula powder; it possesses a more limited increase in micronutrients and electrolytes alongside increased energy and protein, and therefore may be better tolerated in some if this results in a lower osmolality. There is also less potential risk associated with preparation error and bacterial contamination; however, it may be more expensive and not readily available in all regions (including for use in outpatient settings following hospital discharge).

Fortified EBM comprised the intervention in 2 studies. Either human-milk fortifier only32 or human-milk fortifier and medium-chain triglyceride oil33 was added to the EBM for increased energy- and nutrient-dense feeds. Given the immunological benefits of EBM,44 this feed option should continue to be explored in PICU nutrition intervention studies. Excluding breastfed or EBM-fed children from PICU nutrition intervention studies otherwise contradicts health professional and public health initiatives to promote and support breastfeeding. Our group found that at least 50% of children aged up to 2 years and provided enteral nutrition received EBM during their PICU admission,14 and a study in Brazil found that over 50% of infants aged up to 6 months and admitted to the PICU were breastfed.45 As Hookway et al46,47 have described, there are numerous potential benefits and barriers to providing breast milk to medically complex children in the hospital setting, and including these children in PICU nutrition studies is a major demonstration of support.

Reported Outcome Measures

This review has reinforced the challenges of establishing robust evidence for nutrition interventions given the variation in outcome measures utilized. As illustrated by the primary (or first reported) outcome measures in the 10 RCTs, these were predominantly either nutritional intake–related, followed by anthropometry-related (including weight) outcomes. However, within those broad categories, the actual outcome measures varied significantly. A 2020 systematic literature review confirmed the considerable variation in and definitions of outcomes used in PICU nutrition trials and highlighted the requirement for a consensus core outcome set.48

Anthropometric and nutritional intake outcome measures can be considered surrogate outcomes for patient-centered outcomes. Research should seek to confirm which nutrition interventions lead to a meaningful and clinically significant improvement in established PICU Core Outcome Set domains.49

Evidence Gaps and Future Research Directions

Although this review has confirmed an increasing evidence base for feed modification in the PICU, further studies are necessary to add high-level evidence in the setting of numerous variables—oral vs enteral provision, method of feed modification (each with their potential benefits and risks), population and condition, phase of critical illness, and consistent patient-centered outcomes. Evidence is required to definitively establish one method as superior to the other, or rather in which settings and clinical scenarios either method may best be recommended. Determining appropriate use for several methods is more likely given that practice recommendations must account for factors specific to a clinical scenario, including availability, cost, and potential risks.

Future studies should continue to include breastfed children and explore fortified EBM as a feed option while sensitively navigating maternal and infant considerations. Cost-effectiveness and consumer involvement in the research should also be incorporated as these aspects do not appear to have been routinely considered to date. Carer and health professional views on feed modification also appear to have not yet been explored. Despite these gaps, a statement on feed modification could be considered for inclusion in the next iteration of clinical practice guidelines with expert consensus, noting the limited, although increasing, evidence base.

Limitations and Strengths

Primary strengths of this scoping review include methodology in line with gold-standard recommendations and a robust search process. Limitations include the consideration of only English-language publications and a restricted search time frame.

CONCLUSION

No consistent definition was identified for feed modification, and a variety of techniques were used including use of formula powder, liquid concentrated ready-to-feed formula, and EBM fortification. PICU-based RCTs were relatively inconsistent in all aspects of methodology. The most commonly studied population was congenital heart disease and studies did not measure patient-centered outcomes. These aspects require investigation in future definitive research to inform evidence-based guidelines.

Implications of the Findings for Research

This review has highlighted that a definition for feed modification is required. Potential populations of interest to benefit from feed modification also need to be established, ideally via multicenter research in varied healthcare settings. Greater stakeholder input is required to determine optimal interventions for future trials, especially in the exploration of EBM fortification. Furthermore, a core outcome set should be established for PICU nutrition research, which should include key patient-centered outcomes. See Figure 4. There is substantial heterogeneity in studies to date concerning the method, population, phase of critical illness, timing, and outcome of feed modification in the PICU.
