Stra8 links neuronal activity to inhibitory circuit protection in the adult mouse brain
Authors: Ming Huang, Shuwen Yue, Xiaoyu Zhang, Lan Wu, Jay L. Vivian, Zi-Jun Wang, Ning Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYWhile neuronal activity is essential for brain function, it also poses threats to neuronal integrity. Here, we show that activity-induced expression of stimulated by retinoic acid gene 8 (Stra8) protects neurons from degeneration. Previously considered germline specific, Stra8 is expressed in the adult mouse brain and is induced by neuronal activity via calcium influx and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor signaling. Neuron-specific Stra8 knockout mice display hallmark features of neurodegeneration, including DNA damage, impaired proteostasis, inflammation, nuclear envelope erosion, reduced dendritic plasticity, memory deficits, and heightened excitotoxic vulnerability. Electrophysiological studies reveal disrupted inhibitory circuit function. Mechanistically, Stra8 binds regulatory regions of neuromodulator genes and regulates their expression. Notably, Stra8 loss upregulates the expression of Npas4, an immediate-early gene critical for inhibitory circuits, but the aberrant protein is mislocalized to the nuclear periphery. These findings identify Stra8 as an activity-dependent transcriptional regulator that safeguards neuronal structure and function.

INTRODUCTION

Neural circuits, the essential units of cognitive processing, are primarily composed of glutamatergic excitatory and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic inhibitory neurons.1 In a healthy brain, these neurons interact based on the principle of excitation-inhibition balance.2,3 The dynamic modulation of neural circuits is crucial for responding to external stimuli, which ensures that inhibitory neurons effectively balance excitatory inputs. This balance allows the brain to adapt to external changes while mitigating the risks associated with excessive excitation.4,5 Inhibition deficits within neural circuits can lead to neuronal hyperactivity due to the activation of ionotropic glutamate receptors, and neuronal hyperactivity could result in deleterious effects, including DNA damage,6 mitochondrial dysfunction,7 production of toxic lipids,8 protein aggregation,9,10 and inflammatory response.11 These events are recognized as features of neurodegeneration.12 However, the identity and function of activity-dependent modulators in inhibitory neurons whose dysfunction leads to neuronal hyperactivity and contributes to neurodegeneration remain elusive.

To date, stimulated by retinoic acid gene 8 (Stra8) has been recognized as specific to germline cells, where it is required for their transition from mitotic to meiotic cell cycles.13,14 Here, we show that Stra8 is expressed in the adult mouse brain and required for the function and maturation of GABAergic inhibitory neurons. Our data revealed that activity-dependent Stra8 expression maintains the excitatory-inhibitory balance, and that neuron-specific loss of Stra8 is sufficient to induce neurodegenerative features. Stra8 emerges as a key modulator of neuronal circuits, where it plays a role in protecting neuronal integrity.

RESULTS

Activity-induced Stra8 expression in the mouse brain

Using in situ hybridization (ISH) data from the Allen Brain Atlas database (www.atlas.brain-map.org), we identified the presence of Stra8 mRNA in the adult mouse brain, especially in the hippocampal region. In addition, when analyzing a single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) dataset of adult mouse hippocampus,15 our data show that Stra8 expression was primarily detected in inhibitory neurons and, to a lesser extent, glial cells and excitatory neurons (Figure S1A). Our qPCR analysis validated Stra8 mRNA expression in the adult mouse brain and revealed rapid induction upon exposure to a novel environment (Figure S1B). This activity-dependent upregulation of Stra8 expression was confirmed by RNAscope ISH, which detected stronger signals in specific brain subregions, including the retrosplenial cortex and dentate gyrus (Figures 1A and S1C).

To examine Stra8 expression at the protein level, we conducted immunofluorescence staining for Stra8 in wild-type and systemic Stra8-knockout (Stra8−/−; referred hereafter as Stra8-KO) mice,14 both harboring a transgenic pStra8-GFP reporter.16 Consistent with the ISH results, Stra8 protein was detected in the adult brain and showed increased expression following novel environmental stimuli (Figures 1B and S1D). Stra8 expression mostly colocalized with NeuN (Figures 1B and S1D), suggesting that Stra8 is predominantly expressed in neurons. To examine the subcellular localization of Stra8, we conducted Immunogold staining for transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Interestingly, neuronal Stra8 was localized to the nuclear lamina, as well as in phagosome-like structures (Figure 1C).

To lineage-trace Stra8-expressing cells, we used the Stra8GFPCre knockin mouse line, in which a GFPCre sequence was inserted into the 3′ end of the endogenous Stra8 gene via a 2A sequence17 (Figure S2A). GFP expression closely recapitulated the endogenous Stra8 expression in the mouse brain and was upregulated following exposure to novel environmental stimuli (Figure S2B). Then, we crossed Stra8GFPCre mice with Rosa26-loxP-stop-loxP-tdTomato mice to genetically label Stra8-expressing cells with tdTomato expression. Upon exposure to a novel environment, GFP-expressing cells increased significantly, peaking 2 h post stimulus, followed by tdTomato-expressing cells, which peaked 48 h later. Notably, tdTomato+ cells declined 1 week after stimulation (Figures 1D, 1E, and S2C).

To study the function of Stra8 expression in neurons, we generated Stra8fl/fl mice (Figure S3A). The floxed Stra8 allele was first validated in the testis, where deletion of Stra8 in premeiotic germ cells resulted in a loss of Stra8 expression and failure of meiotic initiation, phenocopying Stra8-KO mice18,19 (Figures S3B and S3C). Given the substantial colocalization of Stra8 expression with NeuN, we used nestin-Cre20 to delete Stra8 in neurons (Stra8f/−;nestin-Cre; referred hereafter as Stra8-cKO). Compared with control mice (Stra8f/+;nestin-Cre), Stra8-cKO mice showed a marked reduction of Stra8 expression in the brain, while their testicular Stra8 expression remained intact (Figure S3D). Interestingly, Stra8 was detected at ~39 kDa in the brain versus ~55 kDa in the testis, likely due to alternative splicing of the Stra8 gene21 (Figures S3E and S3F).

Loss of Stra8 induces neurodegeneration

Given the prominent expression of Stra8 in the hippocampus, a region essential for learning and memory through synaptic plasticity,22 we first assessed dendritic spine morphology using Golgi staining. We observed a significant reduction in dendritic spine density in young adult Stra8-cKO mice at 3–4 months of age, and these spines failed to increase in response to novel environmental stimuli (Figures 1F and S4A). Similar deficits were observed in Stra8-KO mice (Figure S4B). As dendritic spine plasticity is a key mechanism underlying experience-driven memory formation,23 we next assessed the cognitive function of Stra8-cKO mice using the Barnes maze test. Stra8-cKO mice displayed significant impairments in spatial learning, memory formation, and retrieval, as shown by prolonged escape latency (Figures 1G and S5A). Meanwhile, their motor activity, assessed by average speed, remained unaffected (Figures S5B and S5C). Similar phenotypes were observed in Stra8-KO mice (Figures S5D and S5E). Together, these data suggest that neuronal Stra8 expression is critical for dendritic spine plasticity and neurocognitive processes.

Cognitive decline is often associated with neurodegenerative processes.24 Thus, we examined whether hallmarks of neurodegeneration25 had appeared in the hippocampus of young adult Stra8-cKO mice at 3–4 months of age. Our analysis revealed accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) as well as increased total protein aggregates, suggesting aberrant proteostasis (Figures 2A and 2B). Additionally, our data show an increased accumulation of lipofuscin pigment in the hippocampus of adult Stra8-cKO mice (Figure 2C). We detected an accumulation of pro-inflammatory matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) in Stra8-cKO mice (Figure 2D). We detected an increase of γH2AX, a marker of DNA double-stranded breaks (DSBs), in Stra8-cKO mice (Figure 2E). Moreover, we noted elevated heterochromatin in Stra8-cKO hippocampus, as shown by increased H3K9me3 (Figure 2F).

To examine whether Stra8 deficiency influences organelle morphology in neurons, our TEM analysis revealed that, in the hippocampal CA1 pyramidal region, Stra8-cKO mice exhibited accumulation of lipid droplets, altered Golgi apparatus and mitochondria, erosion of nuclear envelope, increased heterochromatin, and dense nucleolus (Figure 2G). Moreover, our TEM results revealed that Stra8-cKO mice exhibited heterochromatin disorganization around nuclear membrane, loss of nuclear membrane integrity, abnormal endoplasmic reticulum (ER) interaction with the nuclear envelope, as well as nuclear invagination (Figure 2H). Additionally, our TEM analysis revealed an increase in both the number and diameter of autophagosomes in the hippocampal region of Stra8-cKO mice (Figures 2I and S6), suggesting that the regulatory role of Stra8 in autophagy is conserved in both brain and testis.26 Together, our data revealed that neuronal Stra8 deficiency induces hallmarks of molecular, morphological, and behavioral neurodegeneration.

Neuronal Stra8 expression is induced by depolarization

Ionotropic glutamate receptors mediate fast synaptic transmission and are classified into three main subtypes: N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors,α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors, and kainate receptors.27 To investigate the rapid upregulation of Stra8 expression in response to environmental stimuli (Figures 1A and S1A-S1C), we subjected primary hippocampal neuronal cultures (10–14 days in vitro [DIV]) to chemical depolarization. Similar to immediate-early genes (IEGs), such as Npas4, Egr1, Fos, and FosB, Stra8 mRNA expression was significantly upregulated 1 h after stimulation (Figures S7A and S7B). At the protein level, Stra8 expression was induced within 2 h following NMDA treatment, peaked at 10 h, and returned to baseline by 24 h (Figure 3A). This upregulation of Stra8 coincided with the downregulation of Npas4 expression (Figure 3A). As a negative control, Stra8 expression was absent in NMDA-treated primary hippocampal neurons derived from Stra8-KO mice (Figure S7C).

Given that Stra8 expression is induced by NMDA treatment within 2 h (Figure 3A), we tested whether Stra8 could be classified as an IEG. A defining characteristic of IEGs is that their transcriptional activation does not depend on new protein synthesis.28,29 To test this, our data show that, similar to Npas4, Stra8 expression was induced within 30 min after NMDA treatment even in the presence of cycloheximide (CHX), a protein-synthesis inhibitor (Figure 3B), supporting its classification as an IEG. Interestingly, 3 h after NMDA stimulation, CHX treatment significantly increased Npas4 expression but had no effect on Stra8 expression (Figure 3B). One possible explanation is that, unlike Npas4 mRNA, Stra8 mRNA stability may require newly synthesized proteins.

To confirm NMDA-induced Stra8 expression, we performed live-cell imaging of GFP and tdTomato signals in primary hippocampal neurons established from Stra8GFPCre/+;Rosa26-loxP-stop-loxP-tdTomato mice. Upon NMDA treatment, GFP-positive cells rapidly appeared, peaking at approximately 1 h before declining over the next 3 h (Figure 3C). Notably, tdTomato-positive cells—representing neurons that had expressed Stra8—emerged following the peak of GFP expression (Figure 3C). Quantification revealed that the increase in the number of GFP-positive cells closely mirrored that of the subsequent increase in tdTomato-positive cells (Figure 3D).

Calcium (Ca2+ plays a key role in transmitting depolarizing signal following NMDA receptor activation.30 To determine whether Stra8 expression is triggered by Ca2+ influx, we treated neurons with Ca2+ channel blocker nimodipine and the chelator EGTA alongside NMDA stimulation. Both inhibitors effectively blocked NMDA-induced Stra8 expression (Figure 3E), suggesting that Stra8 induction in response to neuronal activity is mediated by Ca2+ influx. Moreover, Stra8 expression was induced by bicuculline, a GABAA-receptor antagonist (Figure 3F). Additionally, pre-treatment with the NMDA receptor antagonist D-(−)-2-amino-5-phosphonopentanoic acid (AP5) and AMPA receptor antagonist 6-cyano-7-nitroquinoxaline-2,3-dione (CNQX) abolished Stra8 induction, suggesting that Stra8 expression depends on glutamate-receptor-mediated signaling (Figure 3F).

To further investigate how Ca2+ influx induces Stra8 expression, we used inhibitors targeting key kinases and phosphatases involved in NMDA receptor signaling.31,32 Pre-incubation with PD98059, an extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) inhibitor; CN585, a calcineurin (CaN); and INCA6, a calcineurin-NFAT interaction inhibitor abolished NMDA-induced Stra8 expression, whereas inhibition of protein phosphatase 1 and 2a (PP1/2A) by okadaic acid, Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) by KN-62, and protein kinase A (PKA) by KT5720 had little effect (Figure 3G). Unlike Stra8, Npas4 induction was ERK independent (Figure 3G). These data suggest that ERK, CaN, and CaN-NFAT interaction mediate Stra8 induction downstream of Ca2+ influx induced by NMDA receptor activation.

Stra8 is required for GABAergic inhibitory neuron maturation

Kainic acid (KA), a central nervous system excitant, is a cyclic analog of the neurotransmitter L-glutamate. KA is known to stimulate neuronal activity and induce seizures by activating kainate receptors in neurons.33 3 h after KA treatment (15 mg/kg), we detected a profound increase in GFP expression in the pyramidal layer of the hippocampus from Stra8GFPCre;Rosa26-loxP-stop-loxP-tdTomato mice (Figure 4A). Our data show that most GFP- and tdTomato-expressing cells colocalize with NeuN, but not GFAP, a glial cell marker, suggesting that Stra8-expressing cells are predominantly neurons (Figure 4A).

Surprisingly, compared with wild-type mice, we observed that Stra8-cKO mice exhibited heightened seizures after KA treatment. Thus, we recorded their behavioral responses to KA (15 mg/kg) stimulation hourly over a 10-h period using a modified Racine scale.34 Following KA injection, most wild-type mice showed only mild symptoms such as head nodding or motionless staring, which resolved within 10 h (Figure 4B). In contrast, Stra8-cKO mice exhibited severe seizures, with 83% displaying forelimb clonus, rearing, and falling within 6 h, and all mice were dead within 10 h (Figure 4B). In all Stra8-cKO mice, blood was visible in their saliva, suggesting that they might have bitten their tongues or the inside of their cheeks due to seizure (Figure S8A).

KA is known to induce excitotoxicity that leads to neuronal apoptosis.35 To determine whether loss of Stra8 exacerbates KA-induced apoptosis, we conducted terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining. Our data show that Stra8-cKO mice exhibited earlier and more extensive hippocampal apoptosis following KA injection compared to wild-type mice (Figure 4C). Gad67-mediated GABA synthesis is critical for protecting neurons from apoptosis in response to excitotoxicity.36 Consistent with this, our results show that hippocampal Gad67 expression was induced in wild-type, but not Stra8-cKO, mice after KA injection (Figure 4C). Western blot analysis of hippocampal samples confirmed that KA treatment induced Stra8 expression in wild-type mice, along with Npas4, Gad67, and parvalbumin (PV). However, in Stra8-cKO mice, Npas4 expression was profoundly elevated compared to wild-type mice even before KA treatment, while Gad67 and PV expression were reduced both before and after KA treatment (Figure 4D). Gad67 mediates GABA synthesis and is involved in the inhibitory neuron maturation in neural circuits.36,37 Consistent with the reduced Gad67 expression, our data show a significant decrease in GABA levels in Stra8-cKO mouse brains (Figure 4E).

Gad67 is a marker for inhibitory neurons. These let us rationalize that Stra8-expressing cells are primarily inhibitory neurons, which would be consistent with our findings from the snRNA-seq analysis of the adult mouse hippocampus (Figure S1A). Our immunofluorescence staining results in Stra8GFPCre/+ mice show that Stra8 expression coincided with Gad67 expression after KA stimulation (Figures S8B-S8D). Stra8-expressing cells do not colocalize with vGlut1, a marker of excitatory neurons, under basal conditions or following KA stimulation (Figure S8B).

We further investigated the subtype of inhibitory neurons that express Stra8. Nearly all inhibitory neurons express one of the three non-overlapping markers: PV, somatostatin (SST), and serotonin receptor 5HT3α.38 snRNA-seq analysis of the adult mouse hippocampus reveals that Stra8 expression positively correlates with Pvalb (PV) and Gad2 (Gad65), but not with Sst (SST), Htr3a (5HT3α), or Slc17a7 (vGlut1) (Figure 4F), suggesting that Stra8 is preferentially expressed in PV+ interneurons compared to other interneuron subtypes or excitatory neurons. Our immunofluorescence staining results consistently reveal that, after KA stimulation in Stra8GFPCre/+ mice, GFP expression appeared in PV-expressing (PV+) cells (Figures S8F and S8G).

We next established PV-specific Stra8 knockout mice by using PV-Cre39 (Stra8f/−;PV-Cre; referred hereafter as Stra8-cKOPV). Intraperitoneal injections of KA at 15 mg/kg in Stra8-cKOPV mice recapitulates the heightened seizure (Figure 4G). Western blot analysis of hippocampal samples revealed that KA-induced Stra8 expression was largely abolished in these mice, suggesting that Stra8 expression is induced in PV+ neurons (Figure 4H). Additionally, Gad67 and PV expression was also reduced (Figure 4H). Consistently, Npas4 expression was markedly elevated following KA stimulation in Stra8-cKOPV mice (Figure 4H). However, unlike Stra8-cKO (Figure 4D), Npas4 expression was not markedly elevated in the Stra8-cKOPV hippocampus under basal conditions (Figure 4H), suggesting that inhibition from non-PV inhibitory neurons remained effective. Moreover, we observed increased apoptosis in PV+ inhibitory neurons (Figures 4I and 4J), which suggests that loss of Stra8 leads to increased PV+ inhibitory-neuron vulnerability in response to KA. Moreover, Stra8-cKOPV mice showed accumulation of lipofuscin in the brain (Figure 4K). Together, these data suggest that Stra8 expression in PV interneurons is crucial for Gad67 expression and the maturation of GABAergic inhibitory neurons.

Stra8 is required for inhibitory neuronal circuit integrity

Stra8 is predominantly expressed in inhibitory neurons, and loss of Stra8 causes reduced tolerance to KA stimulation (Figure 4B). To investigate whether Stra8 is required for proper function of inhibitory neurons, we performed electrophysiological assessments on young adult wild-type and Stra8-KO mice with GFP reporter driven by the Stra8 promoter to identify Stra8-expressing cells (described previously). Our data show that loss of Stra8 results in a diminished excitability of GFP-positive hippocampal neurons, as shown by lower frequency of spontaneous action potentials (sAPs) (Figures 5A and 5B). Additionally, these neurons fired fewer action potentials in response to somatic depolarizations, as shown by the lower frequency of evoked action potentials (eAPs) (Figures 5C and 5D). Furthermore, our data show that loss of Stra8 compromised the functional integrity of inhibitory circuits in the brain, as indicated by decreased frequency and amplitude of spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sIPSCs) in hippocampal pyramidal neurons of young adult Stra8-KO mice (Figures 5E-5G). Together, these data suggest that both presynaptic release of GABA and postsynaptic GABA sensitivity are compromised due to Stra8 loss, thereby inciting excessive excitation following KA stimulation.

Stra8 regulates neuromodulator genes

To date, Stra8 is considered a transcription factor.21 To investigate its mechanism of action in neurons, we first enriched Stra8-expressing (GFP-positive) cells by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) from the hippocampi of wild-type and Stra8-KO mice carrying the pStra8-GFP reporter and compared their transcriptomes by bulk mRNA-seq (Figure S9A). Moreover, to identify genes directly regulated by Stra8, we generated Stra8-Avi knockin mouse line, in which an Avidin tag was inserted into the Stra8 gene before the stop codon (Figure S3E). We then performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq). By integrating ChIP-seq and mRNA-seq data, we found that genes directly activated by Stra8 are enriched in Gene Ontology (GO) terms related to cell-fate determination, such as Runx1, Pax2, and Otx2, and regulation of nervous system process, such as Ada, Psap, and Kcna1 (Figures S9B, S10A). Genes directly repressed by Stra8 are enriched in GO terms related to behavior, such as Npas4 and Vgf (Figures S9B and S10B). For Npas4, Stra8 binds to the second intron of the Npas4 gene, which was confirmed by ChIP-qPCR (Figure S9C). The significant binding motif of Stra8 in neurons was enriched (Figure S11B). The expression of other neuronal genes was altered in Stra8-KO cells (e.g., Arc, Pvalb); however, these genes are not bound by Stra8 (Figures S10C and S11A).

To further investigate the mechanism of action of Stra8, we performed snRNA-seq on hippocampal nuclei. After normalization and filtering, the dataset included a total of 28,306 genes across 14,224 nuclei (Figures S11C-S11F). Clustering analysis identified 17 distinct cell populations (Figure S12A), which were annotated based on known marker genes40 (Figures 6A, 6B, S12B, and S12C). Notably, an initial observation revealed that, consistent with our western blot analysis (Figure 4D), the Pvalb expression is decreased in the Stra8-cKO hippocampus (Figures 6C and 6D).

We then examined the transcriptomic changes of Stra8 target genes across all cell clusters and found that Stra8 regulates the highest proportion of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in inhibitory neurons (InNs) (Figure S13A). Integrated analysis with ChIP-seq data revealed that, in InNs, Stra8 directly represses genes associated with excitatory postsynaptic potential (e.g., Ulk4 and Tsc2) and activates genes involved in microtubule-based processes (e.g., Fgf13 and Gnai1) (Figure 6E). Additionally, loss of Stra8 in InNs upregulates genes linked to neuronal apoptosis (e.g., Fgf1, Fgfr2) and DNA repair (e.g., Xrcc6, Ddb2) while downregulating genes related to cognitive function, such as Grm5 and Calb1 (Figure S13B). Since Stra8 does not bind to these genes based on our ChIP-seq analysis, their dysregulation may reflect secondary effects in response to Stra8 loss.

Notably, Npas4, targeted by Stra8 and identified as upregulated in our bulk RNA-seq analysis (Figure S11A), is also markedly elevated in Stra8-cKO InNs (Figure 6E). At the protein level, we observed upregulated Npas4 expression (Figure 6F), which is consistent with our western blot analysis (Figure 4D). Interestingly, Npas4 displayed abnormal perinuclear localization in the Stra8-cKO hippocampus, in contrast to its typical nuclear localization in wild-type mice (Figure 6F), which coincides with nuclear envelope abnormalities (Figures 2G and 2H). This altered localization was also seen in primary cultured hippocampal neurons from Stra8-cKO mice (Figure 6G).

Moreover, CellChat analysis revealed that Stra8 deficiency leads to an increase in both the number and strength of cell-cell interactions (Figure S13C). Consistent with the predominant role of Stra8 in regulating gene expression in InNs (Figure 6E), InNs accounted for approximately 80% of the total interaction changes and about 60% of the alterations in interaction strength (Figure 6H). Notably, Stra8 loss enhances InN-glia interactions (Figures 6I, S13D, and S13E), such as the fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling pathway (Figure 6I). Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed increased proliferation and accelerated maturation from pre-oligodendrocytes to mature oligodendrocytes (Figures 6J and S14A), accompanied by upregulation of Mbp and Plp1 (Figure S14B), suggesting a reactive glial response. Microglial state analysis revealed increased activated and pro-inflammatory microglia (MGs) (Figures 6K and S14C-S14E), consistent with the elevated inflammatory marker observed in Stra8-cKO hippocampus (Figure 2D). Together, these data demonstrate that Stra8 is required for the proper transcriptional program in InNs within mouse hippocampus.

Stra8 is required for Npas4 regulation and neuroprotection

Npas4 has been shown to interact with the NuA4 complex to couple neuronal activity with DNA damage repair.41 Given the aberrant localization of upregulated Npas4 (Figures 6F and 6G) and the increased genomic instability observed in the Stra8-cKO hippocampus (Figures 2E and 2F), we examined whether components of the NuA4 complex (e.g., Ep400, Etl4) are altered. While KA stimulation in wild-type mice led to increased expression and colocalization of Ep400 and Etl4 with Npas4, such colocalization was not observed in the unstimulated Stra8-cKO hippocampus (Figures 7A and S14F). These findings suggest that Stra8 is required for the functional link between Npas4 and the NuA4 complex.

We next asked whether adult-onset knockout of Stra8 in the mouse hippocampus would upregulate Npas4 expression. To test this, we performed stereotaxic injection of AAV9-STRA8p-Cre-P2A-GFP into the CA1 region of the hippocampus. Injection coordinates were validated using trypan blue (Figure S14G). Consistent with our integrated ChIP-seq and snRNA-seq analysis showing that Stra8 represses Npas4 expression (Figure 6E), AAV9-mediated loss of Stra8 expression resulted in an increased Npas4 expression (Figure 7B). Aberrant perinuclear localization of Npas4 was also observed (Figure 7B).

Since Stra8 expression is most strongly associated with Pvalb in the adult mouse hippocampus (Figure 4F), we then examined the effects of restoring STRA8 expression specifically in PV+ interneurons in Stra8-KO mice carrying doxycycline-inducible STRA8 alleles (Rosa26-loxP-stop-loxP-rtTA3;PV-Cre;TetO-STRA8). Stra8-KO littermates carrying only Rosa26-loxP-stop-loxP-rtTA3;TetO-STRA8 alleles but lacking PV-Cre served as controls. Ectopic STRA8 expression was induced by doxycycline treatment for 7 days. Our data showed that targeted restoration of STRA8 in PV+ interneurons ameliorated both the number of cells aberrantly expressing Npas4 under unstimulated condition and the proportion of cells exhibiting perinuclear localization (Figure 7C). In addition, this restoration alleviated inflammation and lipofuscin accumulation in Stra8-KO hippocampus (Figures 7D and 7E). These findings suggest that Stra8 expression in PV+ interneurons protects against neurodegeneration.

DISCUSSION

Neuronal activity is a double-edged sword—it is indispensable for brain function but can also trigger cellular stress and contribute to neurodegenerative processes.42 The ability of neurons to maintain structural and functional integrity in the face of repeated activity depends on precise molecular safeguards.4,5 In this study, we uncover a previously unrecognized role for the meiosis-associated gene Stra8 as an activity-dependent transcriptional regulator in the adult mouse brain. Our findings show that Stra8 is predominantly expressed in GABAergic InNs, particularly PV+ interneurons, where it is rapidly induced by neuronal activity and is essential for maintaining inhibitory circuit integrity, preventing excitotoxic vulnerability, and preserving cognitive function. These results expand the functional repertoire of Stra8 beyond the germline and reveal its roles in protecting the adult brain from activity-induced neurodegeneration.

Stra8 has two known isoforms: a long isoform (~56 kDa) and a short isoform (~39 kDa).21 The short isoform was the first variant identified, initially detected in the P19 embryonal carcinoma cell line.13,43 In mouse embryonic stem cells, both isoforms are detected at similar levels, while the long isoform is predominantly detected in the adult mouse testis.21 In contrast, our study shows that the short isoform is primarily expressed in the mouse hippocampus (Figure S3). Structurally, the long isoform retains both the basic-helix-loop-helix (bHLH) domain and the high-mobility group (HMG) box,21 which are evolutionarily conserved regions involved in DNA binding.44,45 The short isoform lacks the bHLH domain but retains the HMG box.21 Functionally, the HMG box contributes to in vitro transcriptional activity of Stra8, whereas the bHLH domain is essential for initiating meiosis.21 This explains why the short isoform, despite being transcriptionally active, cannot initiate meiosis. The bHLH domain also contains a nuclear localization signal (NLS), which facilitates active nuclear import of the long isoform.46 Nonetheless, both isoforms can be detected in both the nucleus and cytoplasm.21 Given that proteins smaller than ~40 kDa can passively diffuse through nuclear pores,47 the nuclear presence of the short isoform is likely due to passive diffusion. Interestingly, nucleocytoplasmic shuttling of Stra8 is tightly regulated during different stages of germ cell development and may involve phosphorylation of the NLS region.48,49 These differences in localization mechanisms suggest that the long and short isoforms of Stra8 may participate in distinct nuclear and cytoplasmic events.

Npas4 is an IEG rapidly induced by neuronal activity.50 Npas4 functions as a neuromodulator51 and interacts with the NuA4 complex to mitigate activity-induced DNA damage.41 However, its overexpression has been linked to inflammation and neuronal death in mouse models of intracerebral hemorrhage,52 suggesting that tight regulation of Npas4 expression is required for neuronal integrity. Notably, the upstream transcriptional regulation of Npas4, especially its repression, remains poorly understood. Notably, the upstream transcriptional regulation of Npas4, especially its repression, remains poorly understood. RE1-silencing transcription factor (REST/NRSF) has been reported to repress Npas4 in non-neuronal cells53; however, in neurons, REST facilitates Npas4 expression under neuronal hyperactivity.54 Neuronal transcriptional repressors of Npas4 remain elusive. Our data identify Stra8 as a neuronal transcriptional repressor of Npas4 in the mouse hippocampus, in that Stra8 binds and represses Npas4 expression (Figure 6E). Furthermore, our immunogold labeling reveals that Stra8 localizes to the nuclear periphery (Figure 1C), a region often implicated in gene regulation.55 Transcription factors may interact with DNA in this region to modulate the expression of specific target genes, including IEGs.56 Npas4 expression is constrained by chromatin topology under basal conditions.57 Its activity-dependent induction requires topoisomerase IIβ (Top2B)-mediated DSBs in its promoter region, which preferentially occur near the nuclear periphery58 and coincide with Stra8 localization.

To maintain neuronal homeostasis, brain depends on the precise function of inhibitory circuits, which suppress excessive excitation and stabilize network activity.59 GABAergic InNs play a central role in this process through Gad67-mediated GABA synthesis and signaling.3,36,60 Notably, dysfunction of InNs has been associated with neurodegenerative features such as impaired proteostasis and disrupted lipid metabolism,61-64 which highlights their important role in preserving neuronal integrity. Among the three major non-overlapping inhibitory neuronal subtypes, PV+ interneurons, but not somatostatin-positive (SST+) or 5HT3a+ interneurons, exhibit early vulnerability and contribute to network hyperexcitability in neurodegenerative disease models.65,66 Importantly, restoring PV+ interneuron activity is sufficient to prevent both network hyperexcitability and cognitive decline,67 suggesting the protective role of PV+ neurons in neuronal integrity. Stra8 is predominantly detected in GABAergic InNs in the adult mouse hippocampus, with the strongest correlation observed in PV+ interneurons15 (Figures 4F and S1A). Loss of Stra8 in neuronal cells leads to impaired inhibitory circuit function and hyperexcitability (Figures 4B and 5D-5F). Loss of Stra8 in PV+ neurons recapitulates network hyperexcitability in young adult mice (Figure 4G), whereas restoring STRA8 expression in these cells partially rescues upregulated Npas4 expression and neurodegenerative features (Figures 7C-7E). These findings suggest that Stra8 plays a critical role in GABAergic neurons, particularly PV+ interneurons, in safeguarding neuronal integrity. Behaviorally, these cellular changes are accompanied by deficits in spatial learning and memory (Figure 1G), suggesting that Stra8-mediated neuronal integrity protection is required for maintaining cognitive function.

In the hippocampus of Stra8-cKO mice, we observed Aβ accumulation in the absence of insoluble plaque formation (Figure 2A). Aβ isa proteolytic product of amyloid precursor protein (APP) cleavage,68 and amyloid plaque formation is closely associated with the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease (AD).69 Aged rodents such as mice and rats do not spontaneously develop amyloid plaques.70 This resistance is attributed to species-specific differences in the APP sequence. Human and murine APPs differ at three amino acid residues within the Aβ region, which reduces the aggregation propensity of murine Aβ.71 As a result, mice are less likely to develop endogenous Aβ plaques but can still accumulate soluble Aβ oligomers.72 Notably, soluble Aβ oligomers, rather than amyloid plaques, are considered the earliest measurable markers of neurodegeneration, appearing prior to tau pathology and cognitive decline, which suggests their involvement in early stage of AD pathogenesis.73,74 However, the upstream molecular mechanisms that trigger early soluble Aβ accumulation remain elusive.75 Interestingly, STRA8 expression has been reported to be downregulated in bulk RNA-seq analyses of brain tissue from AD patients compared to age-matched non-dementia controls (Figure S14H).76 This observation raises the possibility that loss of STRA8 may serve as an upstream event contributing to Aβ accumulation in neurodegenerative diseases, including AD.

Limitations of the study

A primary limitation of our study is its focus on the expression and function of Stra8 within the mouse hippocampus. Behavioral assessments were limited to hippocampus-dependent cognitive tasks (e.g., the Barnes maze), and electrophysiological recordings were conducted in the hippocampus, guided by publicly available gene-expression datasets showing that Stra8 mRNA is most abundant in this region (www.atlas.brain-map.org). Nevertheless, Stra8 transcripts are also detectable in the cortex, and the functional relevance of Stra8 in this region remains to be investigated. In addition, our study primarily examined the role of Stra8 in inhibitory neural circuits based on its enrichment in InNs as suggested by our snRNA-seq analysis (Figure S1A). Although Stra8 expression in excitatory neurons and glial cells appeared minimal (Figure S1A), its potential function in these cell types may require further investigation using sensitive methods or conditional models. Moreover, among InNs, our analysis focused on PV+ interneurons due to the strongest transcriptomic correlation of Stra8 expression in this subtype compared to SST+ and 5HT3a+ interneurons (Figure 4F). The potential roles of Stra8 in SST+ and 5HT3a+ neurons remains unclear. Finally, although STRA8 has been reported to be downregulated in bulk RNA-seq analyses of brain tissue from AD patients compared to age-matched non-dementia controls (Figure S14H), its potential roles in human cells and contribution to neurodegenerative disease require further investigation.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the regulatory and ethical guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Kansas Medical Center. Animals were housed in a specified pathogen-free facility, maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle, and had ad libitum access to food and water. Experimental groups included both male and female mice in approximately equal numbers. 3-month-old mice were used in this study, as this age does not exhibit spontaneous neurodegeneration.94 The hippocampus was selected for analysis based on the Stra8 expression pattern observed in the Allen Brain Atlas (www.atlas.brain-map.org).

Wild-type C57BL/6J mice (Jax stock number: 000664), RFP-GFP-LC3 (C57BL/6-Tg(CAG-RFP/EGFP/Map1lc3b)1Hill/J, Jax stock number: 027139), Rosa26-CAGs-rtTA3 (B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm1.1(CAG-rtTA3)Slowe/LdowJ, Jax stock number: 029627), PV-Cre (B6.129P2-Pvalbtm1(cre)Arbr/J, Jax stock number: 017320) and Stra8-KO (B6.Cg-Stra8tm1Dcp/J, Jax stock number: 023805) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Transgenic mice expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) under the control of the Stra8 promoter were previously reported.16 The Stra8-GFPCre knock-in mouse line was previously reported.17

Generation of mouse strain with Stra8 conditional allele. CRISPR target sites (GGTGGGGCACTATGACACGGGGG, GTAATAAGCACGCATCATTTAGG) within intronic sequences of the mouse Stra8 locus flanking exons 3 and 4 (Figure S3A) were identified and the synthesized CRISPR reagent was verified for activity. A plasmid donor DNA construct was generated (Gene Universal, Newark DE) incorporating loxP elements at the CRISPR target sites flanking a 1.1 kb genomic sequence with 702 bp and 373 bp of outside homology for homology directed repair. The donor DNA construct was excised from the plasmid backbone and the double stranded DNA was co-injected along with the CRISPR reagents as an RNP complex into F1 C57BL/6J/FVBnJ embryos and transferred to pseudopregnant females for delivery. Resulting founders were confirmed for appropriate targeting of the loxP elements via homology directed repair (‘Stra8f’) by PCR and sequencing. One selected targeted strain as initially expanded by breeding to C57BL/6J mice then used for subsequent crosses.

Generation of mouse strain with Stra8 AVI tagged allele. A CRISPR target site (TTGACGATCTGTAAAGCAGGAGG) was identified near the end of the coding sequence of the last coding exon of the mouse Stra8 locus and the synthesized CRISPR reagent was verified for activity.95 A donor DNA was generated via DNA synthesis (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville IA) incorporating a short linker and AVI tag sequence96 immediately before the translation termination codon (Figure S3E) and flanked by 199 bp and 235 bp of Stra8 homology for targeting via homology-directed repair. The CRISPR reagent as a Cas9/crRNA/tracrRNA ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex was co-injected with the donor DNA97 into F1 C57BL/6J/FVBnJ embryos and transferred to pseudopregnant females for delivery. Resulting founders were confirmed for appropriate targeting of the AVI tag sequence via homology directed repair (‘Stra8AVI’) by PCR and sequencing. One selected targeted strain as initially expanded by breeding to C57BL/6J mice then used for subsequent crosses.

Generation of mouse strain with Rosa26-tetO-STRA8GFP allele. A circular Rosa26-tetO-STRA8GFP targeting vector containing STRA8 coding sequence (ENST00000275764.3) and a Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex, composed of guide RNA targeting the Rosa26 locus and Alt-R S.p. Cas9 Nuclease V3 (Integrated DNA Technologies), were electroporated into V6.5 embryonic stem cells (ESCs) using the Neon Transfection System. Following electroporation, cells were selected with 15 μg/mL blasticidin S (Funakoshi). Blasticidin-resistant ESC colonies were manually picked and expanded to establish stable ESC lines. Correctly targeted clones were identified and used to generate mice via tetraploid embryo complementation.98

Mouse primary hippocampal neuron culture

Dissociated hippocampal neurons from E18-P0 mice were plated at a density of 0.25 million cells/cm2 on 10 cm culture dishes or 6-well plates pre-coated with poly-D-lysine (0.5 mg/mL) for 1 h at 37°C and subsequently washed twice with PBS. The neurons were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 14 days (14 DIV) in Neurobasal medium (GIBCO, Cat# 21103049) supplemented with L-glutamine, penicillin/streptomycin (GIBCO, Cat# 15140122), and B27 (GIBCO, Cat# 17504044).

METHOD DETAILS

RNA isolation and quantitative PCR analysis

RNA isolation was performed using the TRIzol-chloroform method (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 15596018). For cellular samples, RNA extraction was conducted with the NucleoSpin RNA XS Micro kit (Macherey Nagel, Cat# 740902.50) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was conducted using the SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen, Cat# 18080-051). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed with gene-specific primers detailed in the Supplementary Table. Amplified cDNAs were analyzed using Power SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Cat# 4367659) on a QuantStudio 7 system (Applied Biosystems, Cat# 4485701).

Immunoblotting

Total protein from mouse hippocampus was isolated using RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific, Cat# 89900) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma, Cat# P8340). Lysate precipitates were removed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. Protein concentrations in the supernatants were determined using the DC protein assay (Bio-Rad, Cat# 5000111). Equal amounts of protein from each sample were mixed with LDS sample buffer (Invitrogen, Cat# NP0007) and sample reducing agent (Invitrogen, Cat# NP0009), and denatured for 10 min at 70°C. Proteins were resolved on Tris-Glycine gels (Bio-Rad) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, Cat# 1620177). Blots were incubated with primary antibodies in 5% milk in PBST overnight at 4°C, washed three times for 5 min each, and incubated with secondary antibodies in 5% milk in PBST at room temperature for 1 h. Detection was performed using Clarity ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Bio-Rad, Cat# 1705061).

Immunofluorescence analysis

Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 240 mg/kg 2,2,2-Tribromoethanol (Avertin, Thermo Scientific Chemicals, Cat# 421430100) and transcardially perfused with ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Post-perfusion, brains were extracted and post-fixed in the same fixative at room temperature for two hours, followed by immersion in 15% and then 30% sucrose solutions in PBS for 24 h each. The brains were then frozen, and 20 μm sections were cut and stored at −80°C. Sections were washed in PBS and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer were applied and incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing in PBS, secondary antibodies in blocking buffer were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Next, sections were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each at room temperature, and then mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DNA Stain DAPI (Invitrogen, Cat# P36931). Imaging was conducted using a Nikon A1R confocal microscope, and images were processed with Nikon NIS Elements. Primary and secondary antibodies are listed in Supplementary Tables. In addition to manufacturers’ validation, all primary antibodies were further validated through comparison with negative control samples. For quantification, the integrated density, area size, and mean fluorescence of the background were measured using ImageJ software. Corrected fluorescence was calculated with the formula: Corrected Fluorescence=Integrated Density-(Area Size×Mean Fluorescence of Background).

Mouse primary hippocampal neuron treatments

Neurons (14 DIV) were pre-incubated with control substance or inhibitors of ion channels, kinases, and phosphatases at indicated concentrations and durations: PBS (pH 7.4), nimodipine (L-VGCCi, 5 μM, 1 h), EGTA (chelating agent, 5 mM, 5min), AP5 (NMDARi, 100 μM, 1 h), CNQX (AMPARi, 50 μM, 1 h), okadaic acid (PP1/PP2Ai, 25 μM, 40 min), KN-62 (CaMKIIi, 10 μM, 40 min), KT5720 (PKAi, 4 μM, 40 min), PD98059 (ERKi, 50 μM, 40 min), CN585 (CaNi, 25 μM, 40 min), INCA-6 (CaN-NFAT, 50 μM, 40min), and CHX (protein synthesis inhibitor, 10 μg/mL, 30 min). After pre-treatment, primary hippocampal neurons were stimulated with either 50 μM N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) for 10 min or 20 mM KCl for 30 min. Cells were then washed twice with PBS and allowed to recover in their culture medium. Cells were collected at the designated times for further analysis.

Golgi-Cox staining

The FD Rapid GolgiStain Kit (FD NeuroTechnologies, Cat# PK401A) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mouse brains were immersed in 3% potassium bichromate (w/v) for3 days in the dark, with the solution being refreshed daily. Following this, the brains were transferred to a 2% silver nitrate (w/v) solution and incubated for 7 days in the dark, with daily solution changes. Vibratome sections were then cut at 200 μm, air-dried for 10 min, dehydrated sequentially in 95% and 100% ethanol, cleared in xylene, and finally, coverslips were applied.

Aggresome staining

Frozen sections were initially washed with PBS to remove O.C.T., followed by permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 7 min. Sections were then incubated with Aggresome dye (Proteostat Protein Aggregation Assay, Enzo, Cat# ENZ-51023-KP002, 1:3000 dilution in PBS) for 3 min, and subsequently destained in 1% acetic acid for 30 min. Next, the sections were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each at room temperature, and finally mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DNA Stain DAPI (Invitrogen, Cat# P36931).

Lipofuscin fluorescence imaging

Frozen sections were initially washed with PBS to remove O.C.T., followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were washed three times with PBS, each for 5 min at room temperature. They were then mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DNA Stain DAPI (Invitrogen, Cat# P36931). Lipofuscin exhibits fluorescence between 440 nm and 700 nm, with a broad and flat peak ranging from 540 nm to 640 nm.99 Fluorescence imaging was conducted using a Nikon A1R confocal microscope under 568 nm excitation light.

Barnes maze test

Mice were first acclimated to the designated goal hole, referred to as the hidey-hole, for 2 min on the initial day of testing. Testing commenced with the setup of the Noldus software to ensure accurate tracking and video recording. Each mouse was placed at the center of the maze under a white LAR cup. Upon starting the Noldus program, the cup was removed, allowing the mouse to freely navigate the maze while the researcher retreated to the monitoring station. The automated video tracking commenced once the mouse was detected by the overhead camera, recording until the mouse located the goal hole or until a maximum duration of 3 min was reached. If the mouse failed to find the hole within the allotted time, it was gently guided into it and allowed to remain there for 1 min. Mice that successfully located the hole on their own were also permitted a 1-min rest in the hole before being returned to their home cage. Between trials, the maze was thoroughly cleaned and the table rotated to prevent spatial learning biases, ensuring the previously used hole was not directly above the goal hole for the subsequent test. Behavioral endpoints analyzed included the time to reach the goal hole and the 3-min maximum trial duration, with data processed using the Noldus software program.

Live cell imaging

Live cell imaging was conducted using the CytoSMART Lux3 FL. Cells were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2, with images captured every 5 min to monitor the dynamics of endogenous tdTomato and GFP fluorescence in real-time. The imaging data were continuously recorded and analyzed using the software provided by Axion BioSystems (https://cloud.cytosmart.com/Account/Login?ReturnUrl=%2F).

Kainic acid administration and seizure testing

Kainic acid (KA, Sigma, CAS Number: 58002-62-3) was dissolved in PBS (pH 7.4) to prepare a solution with a concentration of 15 mg/kg and administered via intraperitoneal injection to adult wild-type and Stra8-cKO mice. KA solutions were freshly prepared on the day of each experiment, with each animal receiving a single injection. Post-injection, mice were placed in clear plastic cages and observed every 15 min for 10 h to monitor the onset of locomotor activity and behavioral signs of limbic seizures. Seizure activity was assessed using a previously established seizure scoring scale,100,101 as follows: Stage 1 - immobility; Stage 2 - forelimb and/or tail extension, rigid posture; Stage 3 - repetitive movements, head bobbing; Stage 4 - rearing and falling; Stage 5 - continuous rearing and falling; Stage 6 - severe tonic-clonic seizures or death, with observed bleeding in Stra8-cKO mice. Only the highest seizure score within each hour is recorded, as all observed behaviors are paroxysmal. Mice that experience continuous seizures lasting 3–4 min typically succumb.

TUNEL staining

TUNEL (Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase dUTP Nick End Labeling) staining was conducted using a TUNEL assay kit (Trevigen, Cat# P193468) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Initially, sections were washed three times with PBS for5 min each at room temperature to ensure cleanliness. They were then incubated in a 1 μg/mL Proteinase K/10 mM Tris solution for 15 min at room temperature to enhance permeability. Afterward, the slides were washed twice with PBS, with additional rinsing for non-positive controls, while positive controls were treated with DNase I solution for 10 min. The surrounding tissue was wiped clean, and the TUNEL reaction mixture, comprising TdT enzyme and label solution, was applied to each slide. The slides were incubated in a humid chamber at 37°C for 60 min, followed by three PBS washes. The anti-FITC-AP conjugate was then applied for 30 min, followed by further washing. Color development involved applying a mixture of Tris buffer, Levamisole, and Vector substrate, followed by incubation in darkness. The reaction was terminated with a final rinse in dH2O, allowing for the visualization of apoptotic cells in the tissue samples.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)

For cell sorting, GFP-positive cells were isolated on a Cytek Aurora CS flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences, Fremont, CA) equipped with a 5-laser setup and a 100 μm nozzle. The instrument was operated using SpectroFlo software (https://cytekbio.com/pages/spectro-flo), and cells expressing GFP were selected based on their fluorescence characteristics compared to negative control. The sorting parameters were adjusted to optimize purity and recovery of the GFP-positive population.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and immunogold labeling

Brain tissue was prepared for TEM by initially fixing with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and sectioning into pieces less than 1 mm thick. These sections were rinsed in 0.1M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) and post-fixed using a reduced osmium fixative composed of 1% osmium tetroxide and 1.5% potassium ferrocyanide in 0.1M sodium cacodylate buffer. Subsequently, the samples were rinsed through three changes of distilled water and dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 80%, 95%, and 100%, repeated twice) for at least 15 min per step. This was followed by two 20-min propylene oxide clearings. For resin infiltration, tissues were initially immersed overnight in a mixture of one part EMbed 812 resin and one part propylene oxide, followed by a mixture of two parts EMbed 812 to one part propylene oxide for 24 h, and finally in 100% EMbed 812 resin for 4–6 h. The tissues were then positioned in molds filled with fresh 100% EMbed 812 resin and polymerized at 60°C for 24 h.

The polymerized blocks were face trimmed and sized using a Leica EMTrim2. Sectioning was carried out on a Leica UC-7 ultramicrotome, where initial thick sections were made using a DiATOME diamond Histo knife and stained with toluidine blue to identify areas of interest. After laboratory confirmation, necessary block trimming was performed, and thin sections (75 nm) were cut using a DiATOME Ultra diamond knife. These sections were collected on 200 square mesh copper grids, air dried, and contrasted with 3% uranyl acetate followed by lead citrate for enhanced visualization. Finally, the prepared grids were examined under a JEOL JEM-1400 TEM at an acceleration voltage of 100 kV.

For immunogold labeling, ultrathin hippocampal sections from 3-month-old wild-type or Stra8-KO mice were incubated with anti-Stra8 antibody (Abcam, Cat#ab49405; RRID: AB_945677) followed by secondary detection with Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (2 nm ImmunoGold, Aurion, Cat# 25106). Labeled sections were then contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate before imaging on a JEOL JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope at 100 kV.

Electrophysiology

Whole-cell patch-clamp recording technique was used to measure synaptic potential and inhibitory neurotransmission of hippocampal slices as previously described with modifications.102 Mice were anesthetized (100 mg/kg ketamine and 5.25 mg/kg xylazine) and transcardially perfused with ice-cold oxygenated (95% O2 and 5% CO2) recovery artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) containing (in mM) 92 NMDG, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 20 HEPES, 25 glucose, 0.5 CaCl2, 10 MgSO4, 2 thiourea, 5 Na-ascorbate and 3 Na-pyruvate (pH 7.40, 300 mOsm). Brain was rapidly removed and cut into 250 μm coronal slices using a compresstome (VF-510-0Z, Precisionary). Brain slices were allowed to recover in oxygenated recovery ACSF in 33°C water bath for 30 min and then kept at room temperature for at least 30 min prior to use in recording ACSF containing (in mM) 124 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 24 NaHCO3, 12.5 glucose, 5 HEPES, 2 CaCl2, and 2 MgSO4 (pH 7.40, 300 mOsm).

Slices were transferred to perfusion chamber and superfused with oxygenated recording ACSF at room temperature. Neurons were visualized using a 40 × water-immersed lens with an upright microscope (Scientifica, East Sussex). Recording of spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sIPSC) was conducted in voltage-clamp mode with recording ACSF supplemented with 20 μM 6,7-Dinitroquinoxaline-2,3(1H,4H)-dione (DNQX) and 25 μM DL-2-Amino-5-phosphonopentanoic acid (AP-5). CA1 hippocampal pyramidal neurons were recorded using patch pipette (3–6 MΩ) pulled from 1.5 mm borosilicate glass capillaries (P-97, Sutter Instrument) filled with cesium-based internal solution (in mM: 100 CsCl, 30 NMG, 10 HEPES, 4 NaCl, 1 MgCl2, 5 EGTA, 5 MgATP, 0.5 Na2GTP, 12 phosphocreatine and 2 QX314, pH 7.30, 270 mOsm). Stra8-KO interneurons were identified by GFP. Action potentials (APs) were recorded in current clamp mode with potassium-based internal solution containing (in mM) 124 K-gluconate, 1 MgCl2, 6 KCl, 5 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 0.5 CaCl2, 0.5 Na2GTP, 3 Na2ATP and 12 phosphocreatine. To elicit current-evoked APs (eAP), a series of 300-ms current (−20 to 200 pA, 20 pA increment) was injected.

To minimize experimental variations, only cells with comparable membrane capacitances and series resistance changed by less than 10% were included. Data were acquired using an Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices), and digitized with a DigiData 1550B data acquisition board (Molecular Device). The recorded signals were digitized at 5 kHz, filtered at 1 kHz, and collected using Clampex 11.0 data acquisition system (Molecular Devices).

Stereotaxic surgery

AAV9-STRA8p-Cre-P2A-GFP (1 × 1013 vg/mL) was produced by Vector Biolabs using the Stra8 promoter sequence with an additional 33 nucleotides.16 Mice were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg) and placed in a stereotaxic frame (David Kopf Instruments). Viral injection was performed using a Hamilton syringe (31-gauge needle) at a rate of 0.1 μL/min. Following injection, the needle was left in place for an additional 5 min to allow diffusion. A total volume of 700 nL of virus was delivered into the CA1 region of the hippocampus using the following coordinates relative to bregma: −1.94 mm posterior, 1.7 mm lateral, and 1.5 mm ventral. Coordinates were validated using trypan blue injections. After surgery, mice received sustained-release buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg) for analgesia. Incisions were closed with wound clips or sutures, and animals were returned to their home cages. Brain tissue was collected three weeks post-injection for downstream analysis.

In situ hybridization

In situ hybridization for Stra8 mRNA was performed on 3-month-old mouse hippocampal sections using the RNAscope Probe–Mm-Stra8-O1-C1 (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Cat# 1160571-C1) following a modified RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent v2 protocol using RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 (ACD, Cat. No. 323100). On the day of assay, slides were baked (60°C, 60 min), post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (4°C, 30 min), serially dehydrated in graded ethanol, and treated with RNAscope Hydrogen Peroxide (10 min, room temperature) followed by target retrieval and Protease III digestion. The Stra8 probe was hybridized for2 h at 40°C, and signal amplification was performed per manufacturer’s instructions. Dox administration

Doxycycline hyclate (MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MO) was dissolved in sterile 0.9% saline at a concentration of 10 mg/mL and administered via intraperitoneal injection at a volume of 10 mL/kg, yielding a final dose of 100 mg/kg, as previously described.103 Injections were performed once daily for seven consecutive days.

Bulk RNA-seq

Bulk mRNA sequencing was conducted at the University of Kansas Medical Center – Genomics Core, utilizing the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 Sequencing System. The process began with a quality control step, where RNA samples were assessed using the TapeStation 4200 equipped with RNA ScreenTape from Agilent Technologies. Library preparation involved 500 ng of total RNA, which was processed to isolate mRNA through oligo dT bead capture. This mRNA was then fragmented and reverse transcribed into cDNA. The cDNA underwent end repair, ligation with Unique Dual Index (UDI) adaptors, strand-specific selection, and PCR amplification using the Universal Plus mRNA-seq with UDI preparation kit from Tecan Genomics.

Following the preparation, library validation was conducted using DNA 1000 ScreenTape on the TapeStation 4200 to assess the quality and size of the libraries. The concentration of each library was quantified using the NuQuant method on a Qubit 4 Fluorometer, and libraries were normalized and pooled to a concentration of 6nM. This pool was further validated and adjusted to a final concentration of 1.9nM for sequencing, using the Roche LightCycler96 with FastStart Essential DNA Green Master and KAPA Library Quant DNA Standards. For sequencing, pooled libraries were denatured, neutralized, and diluted before being loaded onto the NovaSeq 6000. A sequencing cycle of 2x101, with additional cycles for indexing, was performed using the S1 Reagent Kit. Post-sequencing, data were converted to fastq format using bcl2fastq, de-multiplexed, and made available for downstream analysis via secure FTP or Illumina BaseSpace.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

For the Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay, fresh hippocampal tissue was cut in small pieces and fixed using 1% formaldehyde for 10 min. The fixed tissue was then quenched and homogenized, followed by two washes in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells were lysed using 1 mL of lysis buffer from the EZ-ChIP kit (Millipore, Cat# 17-371RF), and chromatin was sheared to approximately 1000 bp fragments using a Diagenode pico bioruptor. The sheared chromatin underwent a preclearing step with Protein G beads. A 10 μL aliquot of this precleared chromatin was set aside as the input sample. The remaining chromatin was incubated with either anti-AVI antibody or preimmune IgG overnight at 4°C, followed by a 2-h incubation with Protein Agarose G beads to capture the antibody-chromatin complexes. Post-incubation, the complexes were washed and eluted from the beads using 1% SDS and 0.1M NaHCO3 (pH 9.0) at 65°C. Crosslinks were then reversed by overnight incubation at the same temperature. DNA was deproteinized at 45°C for 2 h and subsequently purified.

ChIP-seq

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-Seq) was conducted at the University of Kansas Medical Center – Genomics Core using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 Sequencing System. The procedure commenced with 5 ng of both fragmented input and immunoprecipitated chromatin, prepared using the TruSeq ChIP Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, Cat# IP-202-1012). The chromatin samples underwent end repair and 3′ adenylation, followed by ligation with Illumina indexed adapters. Amplification of the library was performed using PCR with Illumina adapter-specific primers. The amplified libraries were then purified using KAPA Pure magnetic bead purification system (KAPA, Cat# KK8002).

Quality control and sizing of the prepared libraries were assessed using the Agilent TapeStation 4200 HS D1000 ScreenTape. Quantification was carried out on the Roche LightCycler96 using the FastStart Essential DNA Green Master qPCR kit (Roche, Cat# 06402712001), adjusting the ChIP-Seq libraries to a concentration of 1.9nM for pooling. Prior to sequencing, the libraries were denatured with NaOH, neutralized with 400mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), and diluted to 380 pM for optimal clustering. Clustering and sequencing were performed on a NovaSeq 6000 S1 flow cell using the S1 Reagent Kit v1.5–200 cycle (Illumina, Cat# 20028318). Post-sequencing, data were converted from.bcl to.fastq format using bcl2fastq software, followed by de-multiplexing to yield sample-specific sequences for downstream analysis.

Bulk RNA-seq analysis

In our study, the processing of sequencing data was performed using several bioinformatics tools. Initially, the quality of the reads was assessed using FastQC software. Subsequent mapping of the reads to the mouse genome (Mus musculus GRCm39; Ensembl release 105) was conducted with STAR software (version 2.6.1c).81 Transcript abundance was estimated using featureCounts software.82 For normalization of expression data and differential gene expression analysis, we employed DESeq2 software.83 The processed data were stored in tab-delimited text files containing raw read counts, adhering to the GRCm39 genome build.

ChIP-seq analysis

In our ChIP-seq analysis, data preprocessing began with adapter and low-quality sequence trimming using Cutadapt (https://doi.org/10.14806/ej.17.1.200), configured with specific parameters to ensure precision (–times 1, -e 0.1, -O 3, –quality-cutoff 25). Cleaned reads were then aligned to the mouse genome (Mus musculus GRCm39; Ensembl release 105) using Bowtie2,85 which excluded unaligned reads and limited insert sizes (–no-unal, –maxins 2000). Post-alignment, Samtools86 filtered the reads to retain only properly paired and primary alignments, enhancing processing speed with parallel threading (-f 3, -F 12, -F 256, -@ 10). Coverage visualization was performed by Deeptools,87 applying a scaling factor and setting high-resolution parameters (–scaleFactor 1, –binSize 1, -p 10). Peak calling was conducted using MACS2,88 which operated on BAM files and incorporated several features to refine the analysis (-f BAM, -g mm, –call-summits, -m 5 50, pp-value cutoff of 0.05). Memory management during these operations was optimized by Picard, which adjusted Java heap sizes and parallel garbage collection parameters (Xms40g, Xmx40g, -XX = 20). The software tools mentioned (Cutadapt, Bowtie2, Samtools, Deeptools, MACS2, and Picard) are run on Linux operating systems.

Single-nuclei preparation

Hippocampal tissues from 3-month-old male and female mice were acutely dissected on ice and homogenized in 2 mL of chilled Nuclei EZ Lysis Buffer (Sigma, #N3408) using a Teflon pestle homogenizer with 10 gentle strokes. Homogenates were incubated on ice for 5 min to lyse cellular membranes and release nuclei. Nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min at 4°C, washed once with fresh lysis buffer, and resuspended in 200 μL of Nuclei EZ Storage Buffer (Sigma, #S8933) supplemented with 1 U/μL RNase inhibitor (Invitrogen, #10777019). To reduce clumping and ensure uniformity, the nuclei suspension was gently triturated using a pipette. Nuclei concentration and integrity were assessed using a Countess II FL Automated Cell Counter (Life Technologies), typically yielding >94% intact nuclei. Samples containing debris or aggregates were filtered through a 40 μm FLOWMI Cell Strainer (Thermo Fisher, #50-136-7555) to obtain a homogeneous, single-nucleus suspension suitable for downstream single-nucleus RNA sequencing.

Single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq)

Single-nucleus suspensions were prepared by adjusting nuclear concentrations to approximately 1,000 nuclei/μL using low-speed centrifugation at 4°C and resuspending in calcium- and magnesium-free PBS (Thermo Fisher MT21040CV) containing 0.04% BSA. Emulsification was performed using the 10x Genomics Chromium X and the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ GEM Library & Gel Bead Kit v3.1, targeting 10,000 nuclei per sample. GEMs were generated on the Chromium Chip G and subjected to reverse transcription in an Eppendorf MasterCycler Pro thermal cycler. Post-GEM RT cleanup was conducted using 10x Recovery Agent and Dynabeads MyOne Silane beads to purify the cDNA. Amplification of second-strand cDNA was followed by library preparation, including fragmentation, end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and sample index PCR using the Dual Index Kit TT Set A. Libraries were validated using the Agilent 4200 TapeStation and quantified with Roche LightCycler96 and KAPA Library Quant standards. Sequencing was carried out on the NovaSeq X Plus platform using a 100-cycle reagent kit with an asymmetric read structure (Read 1: 28 cycles; i7 index: 10 cycles; i5 index: 10 cycles; Read 2: 90 cycles). Demultiplexing and gene expression quantification were performed using the 10x Genomics Cell Ranger pipeline and visualized with Loupe Browser.

Analysis of snRNA-seq data

snRNA-seq data from age-matched male and female wild-type78 and Stra8-cKO mouse hippocampi were jointly analyzed. Illumina base call files were demultiplexed and converted to FASTQ files using bcl2fastq (v2.20.0.422, Illumina). Reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome GRCm39 using the Cell Ranger pipeline (10x Genomics, v5.1.0), which also performed read deduplication, cell calling, and generation of cell-by-gene count matrices. The resulting matrices were processed using the Seurat R package89 (v5; https://satijalab.org/seurat/). Genes detected in fewer than three cells were excluded. Cells were filtered to retain those with 500–4000 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and mitochondrial gene content below 0.25%.104 Doublets were identified and removed using DropletUtils90 and DoubletFinder.91 Filtered matrices were merged and normalized to the median UMI count per cell, followed by log transformation.105 The top 2,000 highly variable genes were identified, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. Batch correction across individuals was conducted using Harmony v1.0,92 and the corrected principal components were used for dimensionality reduction with UMAP. Marker genes for each cluster were identified using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function,105 applying the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Markers were required to be (i) positively expressed in the target cluster, (ii) detected in at least 10% of cells within the cluster, and (iii) upregulated by ≥ 0.25 log2 fold change compared to other clusters. Cell-cell communication analysis was performed using CellChat.93 Seurat objects were converted into CellChat format, and the built-in mouse ligand-receptor database was used to infer significant interactions. Ligand/receptor expression thresholds were based on average log expression distributions across cell types. Predicted interaction pairs with p < 0.05 and average log expression >0.1 were considered significant and visualized via heatmaps and circle plots. Cell differentiation trajectories of oligodendrocyte subtypes were reconstructed using Monocle3 v1.4.26.79 The top 2,000 highly variable genes were used to construct trajectories, which were visualized using the plot_cells() function. Microglial state analysis was performed using established state-specific markers.106 Heatmaps and hierarchical clustering were generated using the pheatmap R package (v1.0.13; Kolde R, 2025. https://github.com/raivokolde/pheatmap). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler.80

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Data are presented as mean ± SEM for electrophysiological measurements and mean ± SD for all other data types. Sample sizes (n) refer to either the number of mice or the number of independent cellular experiments, as detailed in the figure legends. To mitigate litter effects, mice from the same litter were allocated to different experimental groups. Each cellular and molecular tests were assessed in at least three biological replicates, while all time-course experiments replicate at least twice. Behavioral outcomes were confirmed in two independent trials. Data normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, we used two-sided Student’s t-tests for two-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for comparisons among more than 2 groups, and two-way ANOVA followed by a Sidak’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For non-normal data, two independent groups were compared with the nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test. A pP-value threshold of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Behavioral assays and sequencing library preparations were conducted by core facilities blinded to genotype information to maintain objectivity. GraphPad Prism version 9.0 was used for graph generation and statistical analysis using the relevant tests for each data set.
