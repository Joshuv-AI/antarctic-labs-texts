Cortical network dysfunction in migraine: linking genes, metabolism and clinical disability
Authors: Liangfeng Kuang, Xiaopei Xu, Jinpeng Niu, Xiao Wu, Xiao Luo, Kaicheng Li, Qingze Zeng, Mengting Zhou, Jiahui He, Shuyue Wang, Chao Wang, Peiyu Huang, Jianzhong Sun, Kaiming Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMigraine is a multifactorial neurovascular disorder that involves both genetic predisposition and environmental influences. Despite extensive research, the precise mechanisms through which genetic factors contribute to brain dysfunction and vulnerability in migraine remain unclear.MethodsThis study included 139 migraine patients and 65 healthy controls. We constructed a Morphometric Inverse Divergence (MIND) network based on regional morphometric measurements to explore the relationship between brain structural alterations, cognitive function, metabolic distribution, and gene expression profiles. The MIND network was further used to predict individual functional disability in migraine.ResultsTranscriptomic analysis identified 124 genes involved in synaptic signaling and neurotransmitter transport, primarily expressed in endothelial cells, oligodendrocytes, and excitatory/inhibitory neurons. Metabolic dysregulation explained 5.61% of the variance, with the oxygen-glucose index (OGI) as the primary driver. Migraine patients showed significant disruptions in MIND networks associated with language, cingulo-opercular, and somatomotor systems, which were correlated with cognitive domains (e.g., memory, pain, language) and predictive of functional disability (r = 0.23, Pperm​ = 0.015).ConclusionThis study reveals a novel link between macroscopic brain structural abnormalities and microscopic transcriptional patterns in migraine. These findings enhance our understanding of migraine neurobiology and offer potential therapeutic targets for future interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-025-02182-1.

Introduction

Migraine is characterized by recurrent attacks of pulsating headache and sensory hypersensitivity [1]. Pathophysiological mechanisms such as cortical spreading depression and activation of the trigeminovascular system partially explain the disorder [2]; however, our understanding of this complex brain functional disorder remains quite limited [3]. Recent neuroimaging studies [4–6] have provided compelling evidence of gray matter alterations in migraine patients, including grey matter volume changes in temporal and frontal cortices [7, 8], as well as cortical thickness abnormalities in somatosensory regions [9]. However, these univariate approaches sometimes yield inconsistent findings as they often examine isolated structural metrics, failing to capture the complex multivariate patterns of cortical reorganization in migraine. This limitation obscures potential links between macroscale structural changes and their underlying neurobiological substrates [10].

Recent advances in connectome mapping provide new opportunities to bridge this gap [11, 12]. The Morphometric Inverse Divergence (MIND) method offers a transformative approach by quantifying vertex-level multivariate similarity across multiple cortical features such as thickness, curvature, and sulcal depth [13]. Unlike traditional morphometric similarity networks that rely on simplified regional summaries, MIND preserves the rich spatial information of cortical organization while demonstrating superior biological validity [13]. This is supported by its strong correlation with gold-standard axonal connectivity measurements, alignment with established cytoarchitectonic boundaries, and robust association with regional gene co-expression patterns [13, 14]. Within this framework, a higher MIND value indicates greater morphometric similarity between cortical regions, thought to reflect more synchronized cortical organization and stronger structural integration, whereas a lower MIND value signifies greater morphometric divergence and weaker integration [13–16].

The integration of such advanced connectomics with molecular profiling has become increasingly feasible through resources like the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) [17]. By providing high-resolution transcriptomic maps across the human cortex, AHBA enables researchers to interrogate how regional variations in gene expression may shape - or be shaped by - macroscale network alterations [18]. This approach has already yielded insights into neurological conditions ranging from multiple sclerosis to major depressive disorder, revealing cell-type-specific transcriptional signatures associated with network dysfunction [19–21]. Applied to migraine, combining MIND’s precise structural mapping with AHBA’s molecular data could help unravel why certain cortical networks show heightened vulnerability to migraine-related changes, how genetic risk variants influence this vulnerability through specific transcriptional programs.

Building on these methodological synergies, the present study uses a multiscale approach to investigate the neurobiological underpinnings of cortical network dysfunction in migraine. Our primary objectives are: (1) to comprehensively map the patterns of cortical structural network abnormalities in a large cohort of migraine patients relative to healthy controls using the MIND method; (2) to explore the biological basis of these structural alterations by testing the hypothesis that migraine-vulnerable regions exhibit distinct molecular and metabolic profiles. To achieve this, we integrate our structural findings with cortical gene expression data from the AHBA to identify associated transcriptional signatures and cell types, as well as with normative PET-derived maps of brain metabolism to assess the role of energy-related processes; and (3) to establish the clinical relevance of these network-level changes by examining their spatial overlap with meta-analytic maps of cognitive function and testing whether individual MIND patterns can predict patients’ headache-related functional disability. By linking macroscopic brain structure to microscopic biology and clinical outcomes, this work aims to provide a more integrated understanding of migraine pathophysiology.

Methods

Participants

The study protocol received formal approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study enrollment. Patient recruitment strictly adhered to the diagnostic criteria outlined in the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) for migraine. Inclusion criteria for migraine patients consisted of: [1] confirmed migraine diagnosis; [2] age range 18–70 years; [3] documented migraine history exceeding one year; [4] monthly headache days ≤ 14 days. Comprehensive exclusion criteria were applied to all participants, including: [1] coronary artery disease; [2] diabetes mellitus; [3] hypertension; [4] active infectious diseases; [5] chronic inflammatory or autoimmune conditions; [6] hypercholesterolemia; [7] severe systemic illnesses; [8] current tobacco use; [9] pregnancy or lactation status; [10] obesity (BMI ≥ 30); and [11] medication overuse headache or substance addiction.

Healthy control participants were recruited from the local community through targeted sampling. All controls satisfied the same exclusion criteria as patient participants while additionally confirming complete absence of headache disorders or psychiatric conditions through structured screening. The final study cohort comprised 204 individuals, including 139 migraine patients and 65 demographically matched (age- and sex-matched) healthy controls (HCs).

Clinical assessment

All participants completed comprehensive medical interviews documenting demographic characteristics and family medical histories. For patients with migraine, we quantitatively assessed headache-related functional impairment using the validated Migraine Disability Assessment (MIDAS) questionnaire.

MRI acquisition and pre-processing

All patients were confirmed to be in an attack-free (interictal) period at the time of scanning. All neuroimaging data were acquired using a United Imaging MR790 3.0T scanner. Routine clinical brain scans were first performed to exclude secondary causes of headache. High-resolution anatomical images were obtained using a three-dimensional T1-weighted fast spoiled gradient echo sequence with the following parameters: repetition time = 7.5 ms, echo time = 2.9 ms, field of view = 256 × 240 mm², flip angle = 8°, isotropic voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm³, and 208 contiguous axial slices.

All structural MRI data were preprocessed using FreeSurfer version 6.0. The cortical surface of each subject was reconstructed from the 3D T1-weighted images through an automated pipeline that included skull stripping, motion correction, cortical gray and white matter segmentation, coordinate transformation, hemispheric and subcortical structure separation, and generation of gray-white matter and pial surfaces [15, 22].

Construction of individual MIND network

We constructed individual MIND networks using five standardized vertex-wise morphometric features: cortical thickness, gray matter volume, surface area, mean curvature, and sulcal depth. For each participant, morphometric features were aggregated within each of the 360 cortical regions defined by the Human Connectome Project atlas [23], forming multivariate distributions of structural characteristics.

Pairwise regional similarity was computed using the K-nearest neighbor algorithm to estimate symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence between multivariate distributions. The resulting divergence values were then transformed into MIND similarity metrics scaled from 0 to 1, with higher values representing greater similarity [13]. For regional analyses, we computed node-level MIND values by averaging all connection weights (without thresholding) for each of the 360 cortical regions. This approach preserves the continuous nature of the similarity measures while providing localized indices of structural integration.

Statistical analysis

where Y represents regional MIND values, group is the categorical variable (migraine vs. HCs), 𝑓1 (age) is a non-parametric smoothing function accounting for potential nonlinear age effects, and gender and TIV (total intracranial volume) are included as covariates. This modeling approach provides flexible estimation of age-related effects without imposing parametric assumptions on the functional form. Effect sizes for group comparisons were quantified using Cohen’s d. To control for multiple comparisons across all 360 cortical regions, we applied false discovery rate (FDR) correction with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. For network-level analyses, we examined MIND differences within 12 canonical functional networks [24] by applying the same GAM framework to network-averaged MIND values. This complementary analysis allowed us to identify systems-level alterations in structural connectivity patterns associated with migraine.

Estimation of regional gene expressions

The AHBA dataset contains whole-brain microarray-based gene expression data from postmortem brains of six adult human donors [17]. We processed whole-brain gene expression data from the AHBA using the abagen toolbox [25]. The data were preprocessed as follows: [1] updating the probe-to-gene annotation; [2] selecting the probe with the most consistent regional variation pattern between donors; [3] assigning the sample to brain region; [4] using a robust sigmoid function [25] to normalize the tissue sample expression value and gene expression value. Because only two donors contained data from the right hemisphere, gene expression values were extracted from the left hemisphere in our analysis. In addition, only the brain expressed genes [26], identified by expression levels in the Human Protein Atlas (HPA; https://www.proteinatlas.org/), were selected for further analysis, and then a region-by-gene expression matrix (180 × 7411) was obtained.

Transcriptomic analysis

To investigate the link between regional MIND alterations and underlying genetic organization, the regional map of migraine-related MIND abnormality was first quantified using the Cohen’s d values obtained from the case-control comparison (Migraine vs. HCs). Subsequently, Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression was applied to determine the spatial relationship between the d-value map (180 cortical regions in the left hemisphere) and the regional gene expression profile matrix from the AHBA. This procedure identifies a gene expression signature spatially co-localized with the observed pattern of cortical network dysfunction in migraine. Statistical significance was determined through permutation testing with spatial autocorrelation correction. Gene weights were converted to z-scores via bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) and classified into PLS + and PLS- groups (FDR correction, P < 0.05).

Gene set enrichment analysis

We performed functional enrichment analysis for gene ontology (GO) gene sets with Metascape (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1)[27]. Pairwise similarity between any two enriched terms was measured by calculating a Kappa-test score. Next, hierarchical clustering of similarity matrix was performed, and constructed tree was trimmed into separate clusters. The significance threshold for enrichment pathways was 0.05, corrected by FDR.

In order to obtain cell type specific marker genes, previous study conducted a comprehensive transcriptomic and epigenomic single-cell analysis on the adult brain [28]. We extracted cell specific gene sets of 25 types of brain cell, primarily including astrocytes, endothelial cells, microglia, neuronal cells, oligodendrocyte precursors and oligodendrocytes. To correct the size of the gene set, we calculated the enrichment score for each gene set and further compared it with the scores estimated by the permutation test (n = 10000), thereby obtaining a normalized enrichment score (NES).

Metabolism profiles association

To investigate whether the spatial pattern of cortical abnormalities in migraine is associated with the underlying metabolic landscape of the brain, we performed a spatial correlation analysis using a normative dataset of PET-derived metabolic maps from 33 healthy controls (HCs) [29]. It is important to note that this analysis is an indirect inference, as PET data were not acquired from the migraine patients in our study. The normative metabolic data included five features: cerebral blood flow (CBF), cerebral metabolic rate for oxygen (CMRO2), cerebral metabolic rate for glucose (CMRGlu), oxygen-glucose index (OGI), and glycolytic index (GI). These maps were obtained from Vaishnavi et al. [29], which used [¹⁵O] water and [¹⁸F] fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET in young, healthy adults at rest. Metabolic measurement data were converted to the HCP_MMP atlas using the neuromaps toolbox [30]. Based on the HCP_MMP atlas, the five metabolic system maps were assigned to 360 regions, and then a 5 × 360 metabolism matrix was obtained. We examined associations between metabolic feature matrix and migraine-related MIND abnormalities (Cohen’s d values) through multiple linear regression [31–33]. Relative variable importance was quantified for each metabolic feature.

Cognitive association analysis

In the absence of dedicated cognitive testing in our cohort, we performed a functional decoding analysis to infer the cognitive relevance of our structural findings using the Neurosynth [34, 35] framework. This widely used approach leverages meta-analytic data from over 14,000 neuroimaging studies to generate maps of brain regions consistently associated with specific cognitive terms [34, 35]. For each of 23 cognitive domains, we calculated the proportion of overlapping voxels within affected regions relative to total voxel count. In addition, the correlation analyses between cognitive terms and migraine-related MIND were conducted, with FDR corrected.

Functional disability prediction

Migraine-related disability was quantified using MIDAS scores. We implemented a support vector regression model using the LIBSVM toolbox [36] to predict individual disability scores from whole-brain MIND patterns. Model performance was evaluated via leave-one-out cross-validation, with prediction accuracy assessed using Pearson correlation between predicted and actual MIDAS scores.

Internal validation analysis

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we performed internal replication using a randomly selected subset comprising 70 migraine patients and 33 HCs. This validation cohort represented approximately half of our total sample size. Using identical processing and analytical pipelines as applied to the discovery cohort, we computed regional MIND differences between groups in the replication dataset. The reproducibility of our primary findings was formally assessed by calculating spatial similarity between the Cohen’s d maps derived from both cohorts. This quantitative comparison evaluated the consistency of effect size distributions across cortical regions, providing empirical evidence for the generalizability of our observed MIND alterations in migraine.

Results

The study included 139 patients with migraine and 65 HCs in the discovery cohort, with a replication cohort comprising 70 migraine patients and 33 HCs. Demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table s1 in Supplement.

Global and regional MIND alterations in migraine

Patients with migraine demonstrated significant disruptions in both global and regional morphometric similarity networks compared to HCs. Global MIND values differed significantly between migraine patients and HCs (d = −0.47, P = 0.002). Regional analysis showed higher MIND in the medial temporal cortex and lower MIND in the inferior frontal cortex and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex in migraine patients compared to HCs (Fig. 2A, B, Table s2 in Supplement). At the network level, migraine patients exhibited decreased MIND in the language, cingulo-opercular, and somatomotor networks (Fig. 2C). Transcriptional and cellular signatures of MIND alterations

To identify the transcriptional signatures underlying these structural alterations, we used PLS regression to link the spatial pattern of MIND abnormalities across 180 cortical regions of the left hemisphere with the expression profiles of 7411 brain-expressed genes in those same regions. PLS regression analysis revealed significant associations between regional MIND differences and gene expression patterns (PLS1: 14.9% variance explained, Pspin < 0.01; PLS2: 16.8% variance explained, Pspin < 0.01). The expression pattern of PLS2 showed the strongest positive correlation with observed MIND differences (r(178) = 0.41, Pspin < 0.001, Fig. 3A-C). The analysis yielded 124 significantly associated genes, comprising 74 overexpressed (PLS2+) and 50 underexpressed (PLS2-) genes (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Table s3). Functional enrichment analysis of these genes revealed significant enrichment in biological processes related to synaptic signaling and neurotransmitter transport regulation (Fig. 3E). Discussion

Structural network abnormalities and symptom heterogeneity

Our findings demonstrated distinct patterns of cortical reorganization in migraine that provide novel insights into the disorder’s complex symptomatology. We observed increased MIND values within the medial temporal lobe, a region critical for memory consolidation and affective processing. While our study did not include direct cognitive or psychiatric assessments, the observed increase in MIND values within the medial temporal lobe, particularly in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, offers a neurobiological explanation for the cognitive and emotional symptoms frequently reported by migraine patients [37–39]. These medial temporal regions, known to be critical for memory consolidation and affective processing, exhibited hyper-synchronized structural changes that may represent a substrate for peri-ictal phenomena [40–42], and these findings offers a testable hypothesis for future research linking such structural alterations to peri-ictal phenomena like mood disturbances or memory-related symptoms. Complementing these findings, we observed decreased MIND values in prefrontal regions including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and inferior frontal gyrus, which correlate with known neural substrates of disrupted executive control and impaired descending pain modulation [2, 43–45]. The findings suggest a potential mechanism where compromised structural integrity in these executive and modulatory networks may impede a patient’s top-down capacity to manage incoming nociceptive signals, and that this effect might partially explain treatment resistance, as well as the prominent attentional and cognitive deficits observed in migraine patients. The involvement of prefrontal regions aligns with previous research demonstrating the role of the cingulo-opercular network in pain processing [46] and supports the growing recognition of migraine as a disorder affecting multiple functional networks [47–50]. Our results build upon and extend previous neuroimaging findings in migraine. Resting-state functional MRI studies have consistently shown functional alterations in subregions of the sensorimotor network (SMN) [51, 52], which serves as a critical hub integrating sensory, motor and cognitive processing [53–55]. These functional changes in SMN components, including primary somatosensory and motor cortices, premotor cortex and supplementary motor area [54], have been linked to disruptions in affective, cognitive and pain processing [55]. The convergence of our structural findings with these functional observations strengthens the evidence for widespread network dysfunction in migraine, particularly in pain-related and sensorimotor networks [56]. The language network emerged as an interesting system affected in our study, consistent with recent reports of language-related abnormalities in migraine [57]. The frequent observation of dysphasia during migraine attacks [57] finds its structural correlate in our findings of altered MIND values in language-related cortical regions. Importantly, the cognitive functions associated with the affected MIND network regions - including action, language, pain, and emotion processing - show certain consistency with the spectrum of cognitive abnormalities reported in migraine patients. The robustness of these findings is underscored by their replication in our internal validation cohort, demonstrating high spatial similarity of MIND alterations between discovery and validation samples. The association between specific MIND alterations and particular cognitive domains helps explain why standardized cognitive screening tools like the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) often yield inconsistent results across migraine patients. Since the cognitive deficits are not global but selective—driven by discrete network dysfunctions—a general screening instrument may fail to capture the subtle pathology. This aligns with clinical studies showing that objective MoCA scores are highly variable, often only revealing selective deficits in domains like executive function and memory, and are significantly influenced by factors such as age and attack duration [58–60]. More importantly, our results implement the traditional view of migraine as primarily a pain disorder and instead portray it as a condition involving widespread network dysfunction affecting multiple cognitive domains. This new understanding prompts us to routinely assess cognitive symptoms beyond headache characteristics and consider targeted interventions such as cognitive rehabilitation for patients with specific cognitive alteration patterns.

On the neuroimaging front, our findings are supported by and helps integrate observations from recent large-scale meta-analyses [61–63]. Our observed abnormalities in the frontal gyri and medial temporal lobe are consistent with regions identified in Activation Likelihood Estimation (ALE) meta-analyses [61]. Clustered structural alterations (gray matter volume reduction) in the frontal gyri and insula, as well as functional activation alterations in the thalamus of migraine patients were also reported [63]. The convergent focus on these areas reinforces our conclusion that these regions are central nodes in migraine pathology, rather than random findings. Furthermore, the recurrent identification of Default Mode Network (DMN) connectivity abnormalities in functional meta-analysis [62] aligns conceptually with our MIND alterations, which reflect intrinsic network dysfunction during the interictal state.

Evidence for abnormalities in energy metabolism and mitochondrial function in migraine

The high metabolic demands of neural activity have been well established, with numerous studies demonstrating the tight coupling between energy expenditure and glucose-oxygen metabolism in the brain [64–66]. Our findings provide compelling evidence that metabolic dysregulation plays a fundamental role in the structural network alterations observed in migraine. The strong association between migraine -related MIND abnormalities and specific metabolic patterns, particularly the OGI which accounted for 41% of the observed variance, offers new insights into the pathophysiology of migraine. These metabolic-structural relationships help explain several long-standing clinical observations in migraine. The well-documented phenomenon of acute glucose elevation during migraine attacks [67] may represent a compensatory mechanism to address the brain’s increased energy demands during these episodes. Glucose serves as the primary fuel for cerebral metabolism, not only generating ATP but also maintaining redox balance and supporting the synthesis of critical neurotransmitters and structural components [68]. During migraine attacks, the combination of increased energy requirements and potential glucose deficiency can lead to oxidative stress [69, 70], creating a vicious cycle that may contribute to the structural changes we observed in MIND networks. Our findings align with previous research demonstrating that the severity of migraine symptoms correlates with the degree of mitochondrial dysfunction and reduced glycolytic activity [71]. This connection is further supported by animal studies showing that glycolytic inhibition can alleviate migraine-like symptoms [72], suggesting that modulating metabolic pathways may represent a viable therapeutic strategy. The predominance of OGI suggests that interventions targeting aerobic glycolysis, such as ketogenic diets or mitochondrial support therapies [69], may help prevent network degradation. While PET imaging remains the gold standard, more accessible metabolic markers like serum lactate/pyruvate ratios could help identify patients who would benefit most from metabolic therapies. By demonstrating that metabolic abnormalities were related to structural changes, our results suggest early metabolic intervention may be beneficial for the treatment of migraine. This metabolic perspective offers new opportunities for developing neuroprotective strategies in migraine management.

Transcriptional signatures: rethinking therapeutic targets

Our transcriptomic analysis reveals critical molecular signatures underlying migraine-related cortical changes. The enrichment of synaptic genes alongside endothelial and oligodendrocyte markers in MIND-altered regions extends beyond neuronal hyperexcitability [73], supporting a multi-cellular disease model. These findings integrate with existing genetic evidence, confirming Genome-wide association studies identified pathways involving synaptic function [74] and myelin integrity [75]. The endothelial signature validates clinical links to blood-brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction [76, 77], potentially explaining variable CGRP inhibitor responses in vascular comorbidities [78–80]. As these biologics primarily target neuronal pathways, their reduced effectiveness in patients with vascular comorbidities [80, 81] or white matter lesions [82, 83] may reflect the important role of endothelial dysfunction in these cases [81]. The inflammatory processes that contribute to endothelial impairment [84] and BBB disruption [85–87] during migraine attacks appear to be captured by our transcriptional signatures, suggesting that vascular-targeted therapies might benefit specific patient subgroups. Equally noteworthy is the prominent role of oligodendrocytes in our findings, which converges with multiple lines of evidence from genetic and histological studies. GWAS have consistently implicated myelin integrity genes like MBP (myelin basic protein) gene in migraine susceptibility [75, 88], while recent neuropathological work has demonstrated myelin sheet disruption in the trigeminal pathways of migraine patients [75, 88]. The concurrent identification of excitatory and inhibitory neuronal signatures supports the cortical excitability imbalance hypothesis [2, 70], while inflammatory markers corroborate endothelial dysfunction mechanism [81, 89, 90]. These results provide a molecular framework for developing targeted therapies addressing synaptic, vascular and glial components of migraine pathophysiology. Our genetic findings align with recent advances in migraine genetics. Large-scale Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) meta-analyses [91, 92] have significantly expanded the number of known migraine risk loci. Pathway enrichment analyses from these studies consistently highlight shared biological mechanisms, including neuronal excitability, ion channels, vascular factors, and synaptic function. The gene sets associated with our MIND patterns show strong enrichment for similar neurological and cellular pathways, such as glutamatergic signaling and synaptic vesicle trafficking. This provides a functional neuroanatomical link to these genetic risks. By showing that genetically-informed functional changes correlate with clinical disability (as measured by MIDAS scores), our research bridges the gap between population-level genetic data and individual-level brain network dysfunction, offering valuable insights for clinical applications.

Significance and limitations

Our findings have several translational implications that could promote both clinical understanding and treatment approaches for migraine. First, the localization of MIND abnormalities to the medial temporal lobe (memory and emotion processing) and prefrontal cortex (executive control) provides a direct neuroanatomical link to common, yet often perplexing, clinical symptoms like cognitive fog and emotional lability during attacks. Second, the strong relationship we observed between MIND patterns and functional disability (MIDAS scores) suggests that MIND could serve as a much-needed objective biomarker. This would be invaluable for quantifying disease burden beyond subjective patient reporting, especially in populations where self-reporting is difficult, such as pediatric or geriatric patients. Third, the identification of OGI as a key pathological driver offers a compelling mechanistic target. This finding rationalizes the use of non-pharmacological interventions, such as ketogenic diets or other strategies aimed at stabilizing brain energy homeostasis, as a potential treatment pathway. In summary, by grounding migraine pathophysiology in disrupted multiscale networks (genes, metabolism, function), this work advocates for a crucial shift in clinical focus: moving away from purely symptomatic management toward a pathology-informed stratification model. Essentially, this approach might pave the way toward truly biologically targeted therapies.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the gene expression analysis was restricted to the left hemisphere and excluded subcortical regions, potentially introducing lateralization bias and incomplete characterization of subcortical contributions. Future studies should investigate gene-structure relationships in the right hemisphere and subcortical regions to provide a more comprehensive understanding. Second, the cross-sectional design and relatively modest sample size limit our ability to establish causal relationships. Larger-scale longitudinal studies would help clarify the temporal dynamics between MIND abnormalities and molecular changes. Third, the clinical characterization focused primarily on core migraine symptoms; incorporating more detailed phenotypic data, including headache characteristics and associated symptoms, would enable more nuanced structure-clinical correlations. Fourth, our metabolic and cognitive analyses relied on indirect inferences from a normative PET dataset and the Neurosynth meta-analytic database, respectively, rather than from direct patient measurements. While this “spatial correlation” approach is powerful for generating hypotheses, our findings can only suggest that migraine-affected regions have specific normative characteristics; they do not confirm the actual metabolic or cognitive status of our cohort. Future studies incorporating these comprehensive patient measurements are required to validate these findings and establish direct structure-function-symptom relationships in individual patients. Fifth, the precise timing of MRI scans relative to the migraine cycle was not systematically controlled, although all patients were scanned during an attack-free period. The potential impact of this is likely minimal, as our study focuses on cortical morphometry, a structural measure that is relatively stable over short intervals compared to more volatile functional measures [93, 94]. Therefore, while the lack of precise timing data is a limitation, its potential impact on our structural findings is likely minimal. Another consideration is the heterogeneity within the migraine population. Our cohort included patients with and without aura. We combined these groups to maintain statistical power, a decision supported by a sensitivity analysis showing our primary findings were highly robust (r(358) = 0.99 (Pspin​<0.001), see Figure.s2 in Supplement).

Conclusion

MIND network alterations obtained from conventional MRI are sensitive to migraine-related clinical disability and cognitive dysfunction, and are underpinned by dysregulated aerobic glycolysis and transcriptional signatures enriched in synaptic signaling, neurotransmitter transport, and specific neurovascular cell types. Our findings bridge macroscopic cortical morphometry to microscopic molecular mechanisms in migraine, providing a novel perspective on its pathophysiology and identifying potential therapeutic targets.
