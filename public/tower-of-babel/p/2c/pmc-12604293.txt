Low dietary fiber diet does not reduce risk for adhesive small bowel obstruction development: self-controlled study
Authors: Masataka Kudo, Ken-ei Sada, Sho Sasaki, Shigemi Morishita, Naofumi Dobashi, Hiroyuki Oda, Mizuki Ninomiya, Masaru Kubokawa, Hideyuki Kashiwagi, Shunichi Fukuhara, Narufumi Suganuma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdhesive small bowel obstruction (ASBO) is a major postoperative complication with no established methods of prevention. ASBO is a form of mechanical obstruction caused by intra-abdominal adhesions that lead to kinking or twisting of the small intestine. Although low-fiber diets are occasionally recommended for post-abdominal surgery patients, their efficacy in preventing ASBO remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate whether a low-fiber diet reduces the risk of ASBO.MethodsA self-controlled case-crossover study was conducted on patients with ASBO admitted to an acute care hospital in Japan between April 2017 and March 2022. Conditional logistic regression modeling was used to compare fiber intake between 24 h before ASBO onset (case period) and 5–8 weeks earlier (control periods).ResultsThe mean age of the 40 enrolled patients (160 control period cases) was 82 years and 19 were women. Median dietary fiber intake during the case period was 12.3 g/day (interquartile range [IQR] 10.0–16.5 g/day), compared to 12.9 g/day (IQR 9.5–16.5 g/day) during control periods (p = 0.78). Among the patients, 25% were on a low-fiber diet (< 10 g/day) during both case and control periods. Multivariable conditional logistic regression adjusting for potential time-dependent confounders likewise found no association between dietary fiber intake and ASBO development (odds ratio 1.00, 95% confidence interval 0.90–1.11). Potential high-fiber ingredients intake showed no significant differences between case and control periods.ConclusionThis study found no evidence to support low-fiber diets for preventing ASBO in post-abdominal surgery patients. Restricting dietary fiber content may not be needed to prevent ASBO especially in older patients following abdominal surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-025-01195-y.Keywords: Intestinal atresia, Postoperative complications, Adhesive small bowel obstruction, Low-fiber diet

Background

Small bowel obstruction (SBO) accounts for 15% of hospitalizations due to acute abdomen [1]. Adhesions represent the most common cause, responsible for approximately 70% of patients with SBO [2]. The incidence of adhesive SBO (ASBO) varies by type of abdominal surgery; a large population-based study reported an overall incidence of 4.6% following abdominal surgery, with the highest rate (19.3%) observed after ileal pouch-anal anastomosis [3]. Open surgical approaches are associated with a higher risk of ASBO compared to laparoscopic techniques. In a study of patients who developed ASBO after colorectal cancer surgery, the recurrence rates at 3, 5, and 7 years were 13.9%, 27.2%, and 38.5%, respectively [4]. Prevention of ASBO is crucial, as 20–30% of patients require emergency surgical intervention due to complications like strangulation, which significantly increases the risk of mortality [5, 6]. Although providing proper treatment at the time of surgery can reduce the risk of adhesion formation and subsequent ASBO [7], no consensus has been reached on postoperative methods for preventing ASBO.

According to dietary guidelines for general adult population, recommended fiber intake is typically 20–30 g/day [8–10]. In contrast, several online sources recommend low-fiber diets —generally defined as containing less than 10 g/day of fiber [11–13] — in patients with a history of abdominal surgery, advising them to choose foods containing no more than 1–2 g of fiber per serving, specifically to reduce the risk of bowel obstruction, although the recommended duration of such restriction is not specified [14, 15]. The relationship between gastrointestinal diseases and low-fiber diets has been studied to some extent, with ambiguous results. Low-fiber diets may help reduce diarrhea, gas production, and bloating by slowing bowel movements and reducing fermentation in the colon [13]. Low-fiber diets have been associated with improvement of symptoms in patients with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) [12], while another study found no association between a low-fiber diet and the development of diverticulosis [16]. High-fiber diets are ineffective for preventing diverticulosis or ulcerative colitis and may worsen IBS symptoms when derived from wheat bran, indicating that certain conditions may benefit from fiber avoidance [16–18]. Nevertheless, high-fiber intake may reduce the risk of diverticulitis, Crohn’s disease, and postoperative complications after colorectal cancer surgery [13, 17, 19, 20]. A high-fiber diet could also reduce mortality, death from coronary artery disease, and the development of cancer [21]. Furthermore, consuming approximately 20 to 30 g of dietary fiber per day may help prevent and treat constipation [8]. Therefore, restricting dietary fiber intake may instead be harmful if this measure is ineffective for preventing ASBO.

The avoidance of high-fiber ingredients is also recommended for patients with intestinal stenosis after abdominal surgery [8]. One study reported that inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients with intestinal stenosis tended to avoid consuming high-fiber ingredients, such as beans and raw vegetables [22]. The relationship between consuming high-fiber ingredients and the development of ASBO does not appear to have been evaluated previously.

Methods

Aim

The main purpose of this study was therefore to determine the association between the development of ASBO and a low-fiber diet. The study also aimed to ascertain whether avoiding high-fiber ingredients is related to the development of ASBO.

Study design and setting

This study was conducted as a self-controlled study using the case-crossover method, first developed by Maclure et al. to study the effects of short-term exposures on transient risk [23]. Case-control studies, which are particularly suited for studying rare outcomes, typically need to account for several confounding factors related to patient background. Both unmeasured and unmeasurable confounders must also be considered, such as patient background characteristics (e.g., age, comorbidities) and surgical procedures. In case-crossover studies, each participant acts as their own control, allowing the analysis to determine if an association is present between exposures and outcomes within individuals. Therefore, this study design does not need to consider confounding factors related to patient background.

Data were collected from Iizuka Hospital, an acute care teaching hospital.

Measurements

Participants and definition of ASBO

Among patients admitted from April 2017 to March 2022, we identified those patients who had potentially experienced an episode of SBO, using code K56.5 of the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) [24]. Among patients selected by the ICD-10 code, we enrolled those patients fulfilling the following eligibility criteria: (a) diagnosis of ASBO confirmed by computed tomography (CT), including both simple and strangulating forms caused by adhesions such as closed-loop obstruction [25]; and (b) absence of non-adhesive etiologies such as hernias or obstructive lesions (malignant and benign) [26]. To ensure accurate dietary assessment, we limited the study to older patients who had been admitted from a nursing home or hospital. Dietary intake was recorded by nurses or caregivers. The selection of patients with dietary records was determined by inquiries at the facility to which they were admitted. Patients were excluded if they lacked dietary records, consumed food outside the facility, had a more than one-week fasting period during the observational period, or had a diet consisting of tube feeding, jelly, or other supplemental foods. Additional exclusion criteria were also established to ensure an accurate diagnosis of ASBO: (a) absence of a history of abdominal surgery, intra-abdominal infection, abdominal trauma, or radiation therapy [26]; (b) history of abdominal surgery within the past month [24]; (c) history of ASBO within the past two months; or (d) unknown date of ASBO development [27].

The onset of ASBO was defined as the occurrence of vomiting or abdominal pain. If the patient had experienced decreased appetite prior to those symptoms, the disease onset was defined as the occurrence of decreased appetite. However, if the decreased appetite had lasted more than 5 days without the onset of vomiting or abdominal pain, the disease onset was defined as ‘unknown’, and the patient was excluded [27].

Amount offiber ingested andpossible ingredients

Considering that the transit time for a meal to pass through the small intestine in healthy individuals is between 2.5 h and 15.5 h [28], the total amount of dietary fiber ingested in 24 h was used as a main exposure. The amount of dietary fiber (in grams) assessed five discrete points: 24-h immediately before the onset of ASBO and one 24-h period each 5, 6, 7, and 8 weeks before the onset of ASBO [29] (Figure 1). Information on meal menus and dietary intake was also obtained from the records of the nursing homes and hospitals in which the enrolled patients had stayed. In addition to assessing total dietary fiber intake, we specifically calculated the amount of fiber ingested by each patient by multiplying the fiber content determined from the menu by the amount of food consumed using the STANDARD TABLES OF FOOD COMPOSITION IN JAPAN - 2015 - Seventh Revised Version (Supplementary table). Based on their high-fiber content and common use in diets in East Asia, mushrooms, raw vegetables, seaweed, root vegetables, and beans were considered as possible ingredients that may trigger the development of ASBO, and ingested quantities were measured (in grams) [30–32]. Although no standardized definition of high-fiber content exists in previous studies, we selected food items that have been treated as high-fiber in earlier research [30–32]. Using the STANDARD TABLES OF FOOD COMPOSITION IN JAPAN – 2015 – Seventh Revised Version, the average dietary fiber content per 100 g of edible portion for each selected ingredient was as follows: mushrooms, 9.16 g; raw vegetables, 2.90 g; seaweed, 22.15 g; root vegetables, 4.15 g; and beans, 13.62 g. Given that these values are approximately, or more than, 3 g of dietary fiber per 100 g, each item was regarded as a high-fiber content in this study.

Other variables

The following data were collected from electronic medical records at the time of admission: sex, age, height, weight, laxative use, presence of strangulated small bowel obstruction on admission, past surgical procedures (gastrointestinal, gynecological, abdominal vascular, and retroperitoneal), history of abdominal radiation therapy, history of intra-abdominal infection, and history of abdominal trauma.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe categorical variables in terms of frequency and percentage, and continuous variables in terms of mean and standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, and median and interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. The 24 h before the onset of ASBO were designated as “case period”, and the 24-h periods at 5, 6, 7, and 8 weeks before onset were designated as “control periods”. Median fiber intakes (g/day) in the case period and control periods were calculated and compared using a corresponding t-test. In addition, a t-test comparison of potential obstruction ingredient intakes consumed during the case period and control periods was also conducted. We subsequently conducted multivariable analyses adjusting for the total amount of food consumed and the amounts of foods identified as potential time-dependent confounders. As in Model 1, amounts of dietary fiber intake during the case and control periods were compared using conditional logistic regression. As confounding factors, Model 2 included the total amount of food consumed, and Model 3 included the amounts of potential obstruction ingredients in addition to Model 2. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for developing ASBO were calculated for each model. As a sensitivity analysis, the fiber intake of just one meal in the case period and control periods were also compared. For subgroup analysis, we further examined potential effect modification by age.

In evaluating the association between low-fiber diet and development of ASBO, a diet containing less than 10 g/day of fiber was categorized as a low-fiber diet [11–13]. Odds ratios and 95%CIs were calculated for the development of ASBO in patients with low-fiber diets compared to those without. Complete-case analysis was performed, excluding patients with missing data.

Stata version 17.0 software (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA) was used for all analyses. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 177 patients had a diagnosis of ASBO confirmed by CT, with no non-adhesive etiologies. Among these patients, 60 patients met the inclusion criteria. After applying the exclusion criteria, a final 40 patients (160 control periods) were enrolled in this study (Fig. 2). None of the participants were subject to dietary restrictions concerning fiber intake. The mean age of enrolled patients was 82 years (SD 10 years; range 58–98 years) and 19 (48%) were female (Table 1). Median dietary fiber intake during the case period was 12.3 g/day (IQR 10.0–16.5 g) while that during the control periods was 12.9 g/day (IQR 9.5–16.5 g; p = 0.78) (Fig. 3). Few patients left food, even before ASBO onset (median intake rate: 100%, IQR 92–100% for the case period and 100%, IQR 98–100% during the control periods). In a sensitivity analysis of 31 patients, excluding patients missing single meal information and comparing fiber intake at a single meal between the period immediately before ASBO onset and control period, median dietary fiber intake was 4.1 g (IQR 3.9–4.4 g), compared to 4.2 g (IQR 3.9–4.4 g) during the control periods (p = 0.46).

Conditional logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate associations between dietary fiber intake and development of ASBO. The odds ratio calculated for dietary fiber intake and the development of ASBO in Model 1 was 1.00 (95%CI 0.90–1.11). The odds ratios for Model 2, which added dietary intake proportion to Model 1, and Model 3, which added the amounts of potential obstruction ingredients intake to Model 2, were 1.00 (95%CI 0.90–1.11) and 0.92 (95%CI 0.80–1.06), respectively.

No significant difference in the proportion of low-fiber diets (< 10 g/day) was seen between the case period and control periods (25.0% [10 of 40] in the case period; 25.6% [41 of 160] in control periods) (Table 2). The odds ratio for developing ASBO for those with < 10 g of dietary fiber was 1.18 (95%CI 0.34–4).

Analyses stratified by age using an ≥ 80-year cutoff showed that the OR per 1-g increase in fiber intake was 1.01 (95% CI 0.80–1.28; p = 0.91) for participants < 80 years (n = 16) and 0.99 (95% CI 0.87–1.13; p = 0.88) for those ≥ 80 years (n = 24), with no evidence of interaction (p for interaction = 0.86).

Discussion

In this study, a case-crossover design was employed to compare the daily dietary fiber intake of 40 older patients with ASBO during onset and non-onset phases of the disease. No significant association was found between daily dietary fiber intake and the development of ASBO. Sensitivity analysis, using the median dietary fiber content of a single meal as the exposure, showed similar results. Further, comparison of the amounts of potential obstruction ingredients that could trigger ASBO also showed no significant differences.

Low-fiber diets did not reduce the risk for ASBO development in this self-controlled study. While some opinions suggest that a low-fiber diet or avoidance of high-fiber ingredients could prevent the development of ASBO, no evidence in support of either approach has been identified. High-fiber diets have been hypothesized to increase the risk of ASBO by increasing intraluminal bulk, potentially predisposing the intestine to torsion or obstruction in the presence of postoperative adhesions. In the case of the sigmoid colon, high-fiber intake was previously suspected to contribute to volvulus [33, 34], although this association has been questioned by later studies [35]. A similar mechanism has been proposed for the small intestine. The present study did not demonstrate an increased risk of ASBO associated with high-fiber diets, suggesting that this mechanism may not be clinically relevant. No association was found between dietary fiber intake and the development of ASBO, nor was any preventive effect of a low-fiber diet confirmed. The present results do not support recommendation of a low-fiber diet for preventing ASBO.

Older patients with a history of abdominal surgery may benefit from increasing fiber intake slightly. Consuming fiber has numerous benefits. Each 1-g increase in fiber intake has been associated with a 1% reduction in all-cause mortality, a 1.9% reduction in the incidence of coronary artery disease, a 1.5% reduction in the incidence of type 2 diabetes, and a 0.8% reduction in the incidence of colorectal cancer [21]. Other systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported a 5% reduction in the risk of depression for every 5-g increase in dietary fiber intake [36]. The Food and Nutrition Board of the Institute of Medicine of the National Academies suggests that the target amount should be at least 14 g/1,000 kcal for adults, and ideally 24 g/day [37]. The dietary fiber intake of the general Japanese population has tended to be low, with a median daily intake of 13.7 g [38], and the median daily fiber intake in the present study population was 12.6 g. The lower dietary fiber intake in the present study might be linked to restrictions due to their post-abdominal surgery status. Considering the results of this study, avoiding restrictions on fiber intake may be feasible, even after abdominal surgery.

This study has two strengths over previous investigations. First, to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have accurately calculated dietary fiber content based on food ingredients and intake. Typically, food frequency questionnaires are used to study habitual dietary intake [16, 17, 20]. This short, inexpensive method has low patient burden and enables the collection of long-term data. However, this method suffers from limitations such as recall bias, over- or underestimation of intake, and restricted food lists [39–41]. In the present study, dietary fiber content was calculated exclusively for patients admitted to nursing homes and hospitals, allowing accurate measurement. Dietary intake was recorded by healthcare professionals, such as nurses and caregivers, which likely contributed to the accuracy of the measurements. Second, this study controlled confounding factors by employing a case-crossover approach. In a typical case-control study, numerous potential confounders need to be accounted for, such as age, sex, surgical site [42], differences in surgical approaches [43], and the use of adhesion barriers [44]. Intra-individual comparisons could limit confounders that require adjustment.

This study showed several limitations. First, dietary fiber content was estimated from the total amount of food, not from each ingredient. Bias in the consumption of certain ingredients could have affected the results. However, since nearly 100% of food was consumed, the impact of measurement error could be negligible. Second, snacks and meals brought in from outside of the nursing home or hospital could not be evaluated and might have contributed to an underestimation of the relationship between dietary fiber and ASBO. Third, because of the relatively low fiber intake of the patients enrolled in this study, the effect of the high-fiber diet on the development of ASBO could not be evaluated. Fourth, this study was conducted at a single center and all participants were older postoperative patients residing in long-term care facility, where dietary intake was relatively standardized and closely monitored. As a result, the potential influence of more diverse dietary patterns could not be adequately assessed. Further research is needed to examine whether similar associations are observed in more diverse, community-based cohorts. These settings of the present study may have affected the generalizability of the results. Fifth, the study included only 40 matched cases (160 control periods), which limits the statistical power to establish non-inferiority of low-fiber intake within a clinically acceptable margin. However, we believe that our multiple analysis, including sensitivity analysis, support the robustness of our main results. Finally, our dataset did not include reliable information on daily water intake, which may influence the effects of dietary fiber on bowel function. However, as this was a self-controlled design comparing individuals to themselves, we believe that within-individual variations in water intake are unlikely to be a critical confounder.

Conclusion

The present study found no significant differences in the development of ASBO regardless of dietary fiber intake. Dietary fiber content may not need to be restricted to prevent ASBO especially in older individuals following abdominal surgery.
