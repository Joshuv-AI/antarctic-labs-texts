Global bibliometric analysis of TP53-mutated acute myeloid leukemia: research hotspots and evolution
Authors: Xiaomin Wang, Lan Zhang, Xuhang Fan, Miaohui Wang, Haixia Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) with TP53 mutations represents a high-risk molecular subtype distinguished by genomic instability, resistance to therapy, and unfavorable clinical prognosis. This investigation sought to enhance comprehension of the worldwide research domain concerning TP53-mutated AML via bibliometric analysis.MethodsArticles and reviews concerning TP53 mutations in AML were gathered from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) and Scopus databases spanning from 1990 to 2025. A bibliometric analysis was conducted utilizing Microsoft Excel, CiteSpace, VOSviewer, and the bibliometrix R package was used to evaluate publication patterns, the roles of countries and institutions, authorship and collaborative networks, journals, co-cited references, and keyword evolution.ResultsA total of 683 publications were analyzed in this study, consisting of 626 original articles and 57 reviews, revealing a continuous rise in research productivity concerning TP53 mutation-associated AML, notably post-2010. The United States ranked first in publication volume, citation impact, and international collaboration, followed by Japan, China, and several European countries. Washington University School of Medicine, The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, and Harvard University were identified as the most productive institutions, while Kantarjian HM, Garcia-Manero G, and Fenaux P were identified as the most influential authors. TP53-mutated AML research was predominantly published in high-impact hematology and oncology journals, notably Blood and Leukemia. Keyword and co-citation analyses indicated a progressive shift in research focus from early cytogenetic and TP53 biological studies toward integrated molecular classification, next-generation sequencing (NGS), clonal evolution, prognostic stratification, and therapeutic resistance.ConclusionsThis analysis demonstrated that research on TP53-mutated AML has progressively shifted toward molecularly driven and translational approaches, while unmet clinical needs persist. Future studies should focus on multi-omics integration and mechanism-based therapeutic strategies to support refined risk stratification and improve patient outcomes.Keywords: TP53 mutation, acute myeloid leukemia (AML), bibliometric analysis, genomic instability, prognosis

Highlight box

Key findings

• TP53-mutated acute myeloid leukemia (AML) research shows a sustained increase in global scientific output since 2010, reflecting its persistent clinical relevance and unmet therapeutic need. The research landscape is highly concentrated in a limited number of countries and institutions, particularly the United States and leading academic centers such as MD Anderson Cancer Center. The field is undergoing a clear transition from cytogenetic characterization toward next-generation sequencing-based molecular profiling, clonal evolution analysis, and measurable residual disease-guided disease monitoring.

What is known and what is new?

• TP53-mutated AML is a biologically aggressive leukemia subtype characterized by genomic instability, complex karyotypes, therapy resistance, and extremely poor prognosis, with previous studies mainly focusing on molecular mechanisms, prognostic stratification, and limited therapeutic responses.

• This study provides a comprehensive bibliometric and scientometric analysis of TP53-mutated AML, revealing global research output, collaboration networks, and knowledge structure evolution, and demonstrating a clear transition toward precision oncology driven by next-generation sequencing, measurable residual disease monitoring, and clonal evolution research.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Future research should move from static risk classification toward dynamic longitudinal molecular monitoring to better capture clonal evolution and relapse mechanisms, while clinical management should integrate TP53 status into precision risk stratification frameworks and adaptive treatment strategies, and there is an urgent need to develop effective TP53-targeted therapies and improve the translation of molecular insights into clinical applications for high-risk AML.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly heterogeneous hematological malignancy characterized by the clonal proliferation of myeloid progenitor cells in the bone marrow and peripheral blood, resulting in impaired normal hematopoiesis (1,2). Epidemiologically, AML has an incidence of approximately 4.3 cases per 100,000 individuals per year, with a markedly higher prevalence among older adults, particularly those aged over 65 years, and accounts for approximately 1.2% of all newly diagnosed cancers in the United States (3). AML exhibits substantial genetic and cytogenetic heterogeneity (1,2,4). Among the recurrent genetic alterations identified in AML, TP53 mutations have attracted sustained attention due to their relatively high frequency in specific AML subgroups and their critical role in clinical risk stratification (2,5,6). TP53 mutations disrupt cell cycle regulation and promote genomic instability, thereby contributing to leukemogenesis and disease progression (5-7). Previous studies have demonstrated that TP53 mutations are closely associated with adverse clinical outcomes in AML, significantly influencing disease progression and survival (8), and are frequently correlated with more aggressive disease phenotypes and reduced overall survival (9). Further evidence indicates that TP53 mutations are particularly prevalent in high-risk AML subsets and are strongly associated with poor prognosis and inferior survival outcomes (10-12). Notably, biallelic TP53 mutations play a pivotal role in leukemic transformation by exacerbating genomic instability, underscoring their biological and clinical significance in AML (7,13).

Although the molecular characteristics and prognostic implications of TP53 mutations in AML have been extensively investigated, existing studies have largely focused on biological mechanisms, prognostic evaluation, or treatment outcomes. To date, however, systematic bibliometric analyses addressing TP53 mutations in AML remain scarce. The absence of such analyses limits a comprehensive understanding of publication trends, research hotspots, and thematic evolution within this rapidly expanding research field (12,14). Therefore, our study conducts a systematic bibliometric analysis of the literature on TP53 mutations in AML. By quantitatively evaluating publication outputs, keyword co-occurrence patterns, and thematic structures, this study aims to delineate the overall research landscape, identify major research trends, and highlight emerging topics, thereby providing a macro-level reference for future basic and clinical investigations. We present this article in accordance with the BIBLIO reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0422/rc).

Methods

Search strategy

As shown in Figure 1, data were retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) and Scopus databases on October 28, 2025. A preliminary search was conducted using the title, abstract, and keyword fields (15-17). The selection of search terms was informed by MeSH and prior relevant studies (Table S1). Using the combined search strategy, 357 records were retrieved from WoSCC and 534 from Scopus.

The flowchart for literature search, selection and analysis. WoSCC, Web of Science Core Collection.

Proceeding papers, corrections, early access articles, news items, book chapters, retractions, reprints, biographical items, book reviews, meeting abstracts, editorial materials, and letters were excluded. Only English-language articles and reviews published between 1990 and 2025 were considered. After filtration, 323 records remained in WoSCC and 360 in Scopus. The two datasets were merged using the bibliometrix R package. Duplicate records were identified based on titles, authors, publication years, and DOIs; when DOIs were unavailable, a combination of title and first author was used. All potential duplicates detected through automated matching were manually verified. Ultimately, 683 unique studies were included for analysis, comprising 626 articles and 57 reviews. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts and conducted full-text assessments according to eligibility criteria, with discrepancies resolved through discussion.

Data extraction

For the bibliometric analysis, bibliographic metadata were extracted from each eligible publication, including publication year, document type, authors, affiliations, countries/regions, journals, keywords, and citation counts. In addition, content related to TP53 gene mutations in AML was summarized at a descriptive level to support thematic analysis. To ensure data accuracy and minimize extraction bias, two reviewers independently performed data extraction, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Bibliometric and scientometric analysis

Bibliometric and scientometric analyses were performed utilizing various complementary tools. CiteSpace (version 6.2.4R, 64-bit Advanced Edition) generated knowledge maps of authors, institutions, and keywords, employing a one-year time slice from 1990 to 2025 (18). The analysis limited author and institution nodes to the top 25 per slice, while keyword networks underwent pruning through pathfinder and merged pruning techniques to delineate major thematic clusters. VOSviewer (version 1.6.20) was employed with full counting to visualize collaboration networks, encompassing co-authorship and institutional partnerships (19). The bibliometrix R package (https://www.bibliometrix.org) facilitated historiographic mapping and the calculation of bibliometric indicators, including the g-index, h-index, number of publications (NP), and number of citations (NC) (20,21).

Discussion

TP53-mutated AML is a highly challenging, high-risk subtype characterized by poor survival outcomes and a substantial disease burden (10-12). Its pathogenesis is primarily driven by TP53 loss-of-function-induced genomic instability. This process is accompanied by epigenetic alterations, clonal evolution, RNA reprogramming, and adaptation to the bone marrow microenvironment (13,24,25). This subtype exhibits marked primary and acquired drug resistance, with TP53-mutant clones persisting under therapeutic pressure and dominating relapse, posing a major barrier to durable remission (14,24). Therapeutically, current approaches—including chemotherapy, hypomethylating agents (HMAs), targeted therapies, immunotherapies, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)—remain largely ineffective, with a persistently high risk of relapse (24,26,27). Our study presents the first bibliometric analysis specifically focused on research concerning TP53 gene mutations in AML. It aims to integrate quantitative bibliometric insights with TP53-specific biological and clinical issues to provide a more comprehensive perspective on the field.

Despite increasing research, several unresolved issues remain in TP53-mutated AML. These include the molecular heterogeneity of TP53 mutations, mechanisms of clonal evolution under therapeutic pressure, intrinsic and acquired resistance to HMAs and venetoclax-based regimens, dysregulation of the bone marrow immune microenvironment, and potential strategies to restore p53 function or target TP53-specific vulnerabilities (28-31). Existing bibliometric studies have largely focused on publication trends and keyword hotspots, with limited integration of these findings with disease-specific clinical challenges or cross-disciplinary contributions.

Recent bibliometric trends indicate that research on TP53-mutated AML has progressively shifted from early cytogenetic studies and basic TP53 biology toward comprehensive molecular classification, next-generation sequencing (NGS), and clonal evolution analyses (32,33). This shift is driven by multiple internal factors. Advances in high-throughput sequencing and single-cell multi-omics approaches have enabled the detection of low-frequency subclonal TP53 mutations and the simultaneous profiling of co-occurring genomic alterations, which were previously undetectable (34). Clinically, increasing demand for precise risk stratification and personalized therapeutic strategies requires molecular-level characterization to guide treatment decisions (35). In addition, growing recognition of TP53-driven clonal dynamics and therapy resistance has highlighted the need to monitor clonal evolution longitudinally (35,36). Collectively, these technological, clinical, and mechanistic drivers have refocused research toward molecularly informed and translational approaches. The clinical significance of this evolution is substantial. Molecular classification and NGS-based profiling allow for refined prognostic assessment, identification of high-risk TP53 subtypes, and monitoring of measurable residual disease (MRD), directly informing adaptive treatment strategies (35,37,38). Integration of longitudinal and single-cell analyses enables researchers to track clonal persistence and emergent resistant subclones, connecting baseline molecular landscapes to treatment response and relapse risk (38,39). This convergence of technological capability, clinical necessity, and mechanistic understanding demonstrates that the evolution of research hotspots not only reflects academic trends but also translates into tangible improvements in patient stratification and therapeutic decision-making.

Interdisciplinary research also plays a critical role in advancing TP53-mutated AML investigation. Dual-map overlay analyses reveal extensive citation flows between clinical hematology/oncology journals and molecular biology/genomics journals. This pattern reflects the integration of mechanistic, translational, and clinical research, facilitating translation of molecular discoveries into potential therapeutic strategies and highlighting the importance of multi-domain approaches (29,32). This quantitative evidence indicates that discoveries in basic molecular mechanisms are frequently translated into clinical research, accelerating the identification of high-risk TP53 subtypes, refining prognostic stratification, and informing novel therapeutic strategies (36). Cross-disciplinary collaborations also facilitate methodological innovation, such as the integration of single-cell multi-omics with clinical trial data, which enhances understanding of clonal evolution, therapy resistance, and disease heterogeneity (34). Collectively, these interactions demonstrate that interdisciplinary research not only shapes the evolution of research hotspots but also directly supports the translation of mechanistic insights into actionable clinical advances for TP53 mutated AML (40).

To systematically examine research trends in TP53-mutated AML, we conducted keyword frequency, clustering, and co-occurrence analyses. These bibliometric approaches allowed us to capture the evolution of the field’s intellectual structure. Based on keyword clustering, we identified four major and interconnected research domains: molecular pathogenesis, disease spectrum and comorbid or precursor conditions, diagnostic technologies, and treatment outcomes.

TP53 functions as a central tumor suppressor maintaining genomic integrity through coordinated regulation of cell-cycle checkpoints, DNA damage responses, cellular senescence, and apoptosis. In AML, TP53 loss results in defective DNA repair, uncontrolled proliferation, and accumulation of genomic instability, collectively driving leukemogenesis (7,41,42). Accordingly, TP53 mutations are enriched in therapy-related AML and cases with complex karyotypes, reflecting heightened susceptibility to chromosomal damage, aberrant repair, and clonal diversification. Integrative analysis of bibliometric and mechanistic data further indicates that multi-hit or biallelic TP53 alterations are particularly potent drivers of leukemic transformation. These alterations sustain marked chromosomal instability, a hallmark overrepresented in complex karyotype AML as well as erythroid and megakaryocytic differentiation subtypes, consolidating TP53 mutation status as a defining molecular feature of biologically aggressive, high-risk AML (10,43,44). Beyond structural genomic damage, TP53 mutations are closely linked to epigenetic dysregulation, particularly aberrant DNA methylation. Co-occurring mutations in DNMT3A and TET family genes synergistically disrupt transcriptional programs, impair normal hematopoietic differentiation, and stabilize leukemic cell identity (45,46). Perturbation of epigenetic homeostasis also contributes to intrinsic resistance to cytotoxic therapies, highlighting the role of epigenetic remodeling in disease persistence and treatment failure (47,48). A defining biological feature of TP53-mutated AML is pronounced clonal evolution under therapeutic pressure. TP53 mutations often arise early during clonal hematopoiesis, conferring strong selective advantages that allow resistant subclones to persist and expand despite intensive treatment (36). Longitudinal and single-cell genomic studies consistently demonstrate that relapse predominantly originates from pre-existing TP53-mutant clones rather than newly acquired mutations, identifying clonal persistence as a central obstacle to durable remission (49,50). Recent evidence further indicates that TP53 dysfunction extends beyond DNA-centric processes to influence RNA metabolism and translational control. Dysregulation of RNA-processing factors, including DEAD-box helicases, alters RNA splicing, ribosome biogenesis, and stress-adaptive translation, thereby promoting leukemic cell survival and plasticity under adverse conditions (51-54). In parallel, TP53 mutations modulate leukemic interactions with the bone marrow microenvironment by regulating membrane-associated molecules, including glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored proteins (GPI-APs), that govern cell adhesion, immune evasion, and niche interactions, collectively facilitating disease persistence and therapeutic resistance (55-58). Together, this integrated mechanistic framework provides a coherent biological explanation for the aggressive clinical phenotype and therapeutic refractoriness of TP53-mutated AML and establishes a foundation for precision strategies targeting core molecular drivers and downstream vulnerabilities (7,10,12).

Furthermore, increasing attention to disease heterogeneity in TP53-mutated AML has led to the emergence of comorbidity-related keywords, including MDS, CLL, Li-Fraumeni syndrome (LFS), and other hematologic malignancies. These conditions do not arise from random coexistence but are systematically connected through TP53 as a recurrent molecular hub within co-occurrence and co-citation networks, highlighting TP53 dysfunction as a shared driver that shapes both AML pathogenesis and its comorbidity spectrum (7,12). Among these, MDS stands out as the most biologically coherent antecedent condition. TP53 mutations are highly enriched in MDS with complex karyotypes and are strongly associated with adverse outcomes and accelerated disease progression. Longitudinal studies show that TP53 mutation status independently predicts the transformation of MDS to AML, thus supporting a cumulative pathogenic trajectory that extends from MDS to overt leukemia (7,13,59-61). Additionally, inherited cancer predisposition syndromes further substantiate the centrality of TP53. The persistent co-citation of LFS and AML underscores the role of germline TP53 mutations in systemic cancer susceptibility. AML in LFS patients is predominantly therapy-related and exhibits molecular and cytogenetic features closely resembling those of sporadic TP53-mutated AML. These findings indicate that TP53 dysfunction, whether germline or somatic, drives leukemogenesis through convergent biological mechanisms (13,62). Moreover, TP53 aberrations in other hematologic malignancies reinforce this shared vulnerability. In CLL, TP53 mutations or 17p deletions are linked to disease progression and therapeutic resistance. Although direct coexistence of CLL and AML is rare, consistent bibliometric associations emphasize common TP53-driven evolutionary pressures across hematologic malignancies rather than incidental overlap (63-65). Collectively, TP53 abnormalities underlie non-random comorbidity patterns in AML, underscoring the need for integrated molecular risk stratification and cross-disease surveillance in high-risk populations (7,12,13).

Bibliometric analyses identify NGS as a highly prominent diagnostic keyword in TP53-mutated AML research, with increasing emphasis on its clinical applicability and prognostic relevance. At initial diagnosis, NGS enables comprehensive and simultaneous detection of clinically actionable mutations, including TP53, FLT3, and NPM1, which are closely associated with survival outcomes and therapeutic response. Large-scale genomic studies consistently show that incorporation of NGS-derived mutational profiles enhances prognostic stratification beyond traditional risk classification systems, particularly by identifying TP53-driven high-risk disease that may be underestimated when relying solely on cytogenetic criteria (10,12). A key diagnostic advantage of NGS is its high analytical sensitivity for detecting TP53 mutations, allowing reliable identification of low-variant allele frequency events, subclonal alterations, and biallelic TP53 inactivation that are frequently missed by conventional cytogenetics or fluorescence in situ hybridization. These abnormalities are tightly associated with genomic instability, complex karyotypes, therapy-related AML, and poor clinical outcomes, reinforcing TP53 mutation status as a defining molecular marker of adverse-risk AML (10,13). Beyond single-gene profiling, NGS has expanded the diagnostic spectrum of AML by enabling detection of previously unrecognized genomic rearrangements and molecularly distinct disease subsets. Rare but aggressive fusion events, including NUTM1 fusions and ENOX2::RUNX1 rearrangements, illustrate the molecular heterogeneity of AML and underscore the value of NGS in refining disease classification and identifying high-risk subtypes not readily detectable by conventional approaches (66,67). Importantly, the utility of NGS extends beyond baseline diagnosis. TP53-oriented NGS has demonstrated substantial value in MRD assessment and longitudinal disease monitoring. NGS-based MRD analysis provides highly sensitive detection of persistent or re-emerging leukemic clones and strongly predicts relapse risk and post-transplant outcomes. In TP53-mutated AML, continued detection of TP53-mutant clones after therapy is consistently associated with early relapse and inferior survival, highlighting the importance of NGS in dynamic risk reassessment throughout the disease course (50,68). Overall, our bibliometric and mechanistic analysis highlights that TP53-mutated AML research has progressed from early cytogenetic and biological studies to molecularly driven, translational, and interdisciplinary approaches. Significant unmet needs remain, including therapy resistance, clonal evolution, and translation of TP53-targeted strategies into clinical practice (29,31,69). Addressing these challenges will require multi-domain collaboration, longitudinal multi-omics integration, and adaptive clinical trials prioritizing TP53 status to inform personalized treatment. Taken together, TP53-focused NGS enables dynamic tracking of clonal evolution under therapeutic pressure, linking baseline TP53-centered genomic landscapes to treatment resistance and disease progression. By facilitating precise molecular stratification and sensitive MRD detection, NGS has fundamentally reshaped AML diagnostics and provides a critical foundation for individualized management of TP53-mutated high-risk AML, despite ongoing challenges related to standardization and data integration (10,12,50,66,68).

To date, TP53-mutated AML is characterized by very poor clinical outcomes, with patients deriving limited benefit from currently available standard therapies. Intensive chemotherapy and HMAs, with or without venetoclax, yield low response rates, short-lived remissions, and rapid relapse, resulting in significantly inferior survival compared with TP53-wild-type disease (10-12). Even in the frontline setting, treatment responses remain suboptimal, and in relapsed or refractory disease, response rates may decline to approximately 14%, underscoring the limitations of conventional therapeutic approaches in this subgroup (14). Allogeneic HSCT, a cornerstone of high-risk AML therapy, shows limited efficacy in the presence of TP53 mutations. Although some patients may experience modest survival prolongation, relapse rates remain high, and TP53 mutation status consistently predicts post-transplant relapse and treatment failure (24). In contrast, HSCT confers clearer survival benefits in selected molecular subtypes such as NPM1-mutated AML, highlighting the context-dependent efficacy of transplantation (70). Accordingly, in TP53-mutated AML, HSCT is more appropriately viewed as a disease-control or remission-prolonging strategy rather than a reliably curative intervention.

Given the limited efficacy of standard therapies, several novel treatment approaches have been explored. TP53-targeting agents such as eprenetapopt (APR-246), when combined with HMAs, have shown encouraging early activity, including higher response rates, molecular remission signals, and improved overall and event-free survival in early-phase studies (71,72). However, subsequent phase III trials failed to demonstrate a significant survival benefit, indicating the need for further optimization (27). Immunotherapeutic strategies have also attracted substantial interest. The anti-CD47 monoclonal antibody magrolimab has demonstrated anti-leukemic activity in TP53-mutated AML, particularly in combination with HMAs, achieving promising response rates in early studies (12,73). Nevertheless, randomized trials have not consistently shown meaningful overall survival benefits, and some were terminated early due to futility, reflecting uncertainty regarding its clinical efficacy (10,26). Similarly, PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint inhibitors combined with HMAs have shown immune activation and clinical responses, but the durability of benefit and impact on long-term survival remain unclear (74,75). Bispecific antibody-based therapies, particularly CD3-CD123-targeting agents such as flotetuzumab, have demonstrated comparatively promising activity, with reported complete remission rates of approximately 47% in relapsed or refractory TP53-mutated AML and survival prolongation in a subset of patients (76,77). However, substantial inter-patient heterogeneity persists, and durability of benefit requires confirmation in larger prospective trials. Cellular immunotherapies, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy and natural killer (NK) cell-based approaches, are also under investigation. While these modalities show theoretical promise and encouraging early signals, their clinical application remains limited by challenges related to antigen selection, toxicity, immune escape, and response durability, and they remain largely investigational (78-80). Overall, TP53-mutated AML remains a profoundly high-risk disease refractory to both conventional and emerging therapies, underscoring the need for biology-driven, adaptive treatment strategies that integrate TP53-based stratification, rational combination approaches, and dynamic disease monitoring to achieve durable disease control.

Our study presents significant strengths as one of the pioneering bibliometric analyses concentrating on TP53-mutated AML. It furnishes a quantitative overview of the field’s progression, covering a wide range of literature to comprehensively evaluate research trends encompassing molecular mechanisms, comorbidities, diagnostics, and therapeutic approaches. The utilization of visualization tools like VOSviewer and CiteSpace facilitates an unbiased delineation of research focal points, principal contributors, and knowledge voids, thereby augmenting the analysis’s reliability and reproducibility. Nevertheless, it is imperative to acknowledge several limitations. The restriction to English-language publications may lead to an underrepresentation of research from non-English-speaking regions. Inconsistencies in author names and institutional affiliations could potentially impact data precision. Furthermore, the inherent time delay between literature retrieval and publication may lead to a partial exclusion of the latest advancements, especially in swiftly evolving areas of TP53-mutated AML research.

In summary, this study comprehensively delineates the evolving research landscape of TP53-mutated AML, underscoring the central role of TP53-driven genomic instability in shaping disease biology, risk stratification, comorbidity patterns, and therapeutic resistance. Building on these insights, the findings emphasize the critical need for sustained efforts toward integrated molecular risk assessment, longitudinal disease monitoring, and the development of adaptive, biology-driven therapeutic strategies. Given the complexity of TP53-associated molecular networks and their profound influence on treatment response and disease evolution, continued multidisciplinary research will be essential to refine precision management approaches and ultimately improve clinical outcomes for patients with this exceptionally high-risk form of AML. Importantly, this study is among the first bibliometric analyses to integrate quantitative mapping of research trends with TP53-specific biological and clinical insights. Compared with previous bibliometric reports, our approach uniquely combines keyword evolution, cross-disciplinary interactions, and clinical relevance, providing a more comprehensive and actionable perspective for guiding future TP53-mutated AML research.

Conclusions

This analysis synthesizes nearly three decades of research on TP53-mutated AML, highlighting the evolving landscape from cytogenetic studies to molecularly driven, interdisciplinary approaches. Future progress requires adaptive, biology-guided precision management tailored to the unique challenges of TP53-mutated AML, including pronounced therapy resistance, early clonal persistence, and high relapse risk. Key priorities include: (I) tracking clonal evolution with single-cell multi-omics to monitor resistant subclones; (II) developing TP53-focused MRD assays for dynamic risk assessment; (III) designing combination and sequential therapies guided by mutation subtype and clonal architecture; and (IV) integrating molecular, clinical, and immune data to refine prognostic stratification and optimize individualized treatment. Implementing these strategies will support targeted clinical trials, enable precision therapy, and ultimately improve outcomes in this high-risk AML subset.
