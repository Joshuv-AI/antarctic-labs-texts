Effect of Education Based on the Common-sense Model of Self-regulation on Illness Perception Among Older Patients With Hypertension in Iran: A Randomized Controlled Clinical Trial
Authors: Saeed Hamidizadeh, Mohammad-Rafi Bazrafshan, Omid Barghi, Saleh Vadipeima, Mohammadreza Razavi, Ali Mohammad Parviniannasab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEffective blood pressure management requires sustained medication adherence and health-related behavior change. However, limited research has examined hypertension management from the patient’s perspective. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of an educational program based on the Common-Sense Model (CSM) on illness perception among older adults with hypertension.MethodsThis randomized controlled clinical trial included 62 patients with hypertension who were referred to Khalil-Azad Health Center in Larestan, Iran, from March 2024 to July 2024. Participants were selected and randomly assigned to the intervention group (n=31) or control group (n=31). The intervention group participated in a CSM-based educational program consisting of five weekly 45–60-minute sessions, whereas the control group received routine care only. Participants completed the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire before the intervention and 8 weeks after the start of the intervention.ResultsBefore the intervention, illness perception subscale scores did not differ significantly between the intervention and control groups (p>0.05). After the intervention, illness perception subscale scores improved in the intervention group compared with the control group, except for illness consequences, illness concern, and emotional representation (p<0.05 for the remaining subscales).ConclusionsA CSM-based educational program may help nurses and health care providers improve illness perception among patients with primary hypertension.Keywords: Aged, Hypertension, Health education, Patients, Self-regulation, Clinical trial

INTRODUCTION

Hypertension (HTN) is a major global health problem because of its association with cardiovascular disease [1]. Stage 1 HTN is defined as systolic blood pressure of 130–139 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of 80–89 mmHg, whereas stage 2 HTN is defined as systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or higher or diastolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or higher [2]. HTN increases the risk of heart disease, stroke, kidney failure, and other medical complications [3]. According to 2018 World Health Organization data, approximately 26.4% of the global population has HTN, and almost 60% of patients with HTN live in developing countries, including Iran [4]. A previous meta-analysis estimated the overall prevalence of primary HTN among Iranian adults to be 31.6% [5]. To reduce HTN-related mortality and complications and improve blood pressure control, interventions are needed to improve patients’ perceptions and attitudes and support adherence to recommended treatment regimens [6]. Patients’ perceptions and preventive behaviors can inform the design of appropriate programs and interventions [7]. Misperceptions about illness can adversely affect patient behaviors, including treatment adherence, self-diagnosis, help-seeking, and disease outcomes [8].

Kleinman et al. [9]’s model proposes that patients’ perceptions of disease and treatment may differ substantially from those of their health care providers. The model uses key questions to elicit patients’ health beliefs and understanding of their condition in multicultural care settings [10]. Duman and Yildiz [11] reported that higher illness perception (IP) scores were significantly associated with lower educational attainment, a shorter duration of antihypertensive therapy, and attribution of HTN to stress, genetic predisposition, diet, and occupational factors. Patients’ beliefs and perceptions of their condition shape how they understand the disease, which may affect both psychological well-being and coping strategies [12]. Leventhal [13]’s self-regulatory model, also known as the Common-Sense Model (CSM), is one framework for understanding IP. This model, introduced in 1980, originally categorized IP into five dimensions: identity, timeline, consequences, cause, and control/cure, including treatment control and personal control. Subsequent work added coherence, concern, and emotional representation to these five dimensions. According to Leventhal et al. [1] model, patients’ adherence to disease-management advice and education is expected to support clinical improvement. Leventhal et al. [14] reported that understanding disease perception helps identify factors that affect adaptation to and acceptance of the disease process, thereby informing patient support programs. This understanding may also guide the development of evidence-based nursing instructions and more effective support for patients’ needs [14]. Rakhshan et al. [15] found that IP-related interventions had a beneficial effect on lifestyle among individuals with metabolic syndrome. In a study by Rajabloo et al. [16], CSM-based education improved treatment adherence and reduced blood pressure in patients with HTN. Another CSM-based educational intervention improved self-management and reduced blood pressure in patients with HTN [17].

Blood pressure management is essential for slowing disease progression and preventing related complications. Given the complexity of chronic disease, the multiple influences on health behaviors, and the persistent challenge of blood pressure control in the community, comprehensive strategies are needed to improve patients’ perceptions and knowledge of HTN. In this context, inadequate blood pressure control and its consequences may differ from those of other chronic diseases. Therefore, identifying the perceptions of patients with HTN and evaluating the effect of CSM-based educational interventions may support the development of nursing care that is more closely aligned with the individual needs of these patients and may improve its effectiveness.

METHODS

Study Design

This randomized controlled clinical trial compared intervention and control groups before and after the intervention. The study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines, and a completed CONSORT checklist was included as a Supplemental Material 1.

Study setting and participants

Participants were patients older than 60 years with primary HTN confirmed by a cardiologist who had been referred to Khalil-Azad Health Center in Larestan, Iran, from March 2024 to July 2024. An attrition rate of 10% was anticipated based on estimates and dropout rates from interventional studies. Therefore, the current study included 62 participants, with 31 patients in each group.

Randomization

Patients were recruited at Khalil-Azad Health Center using convenience sampling. They were then randomly assigned to the intervention group (n=31) or control group (n=31) using randomized block allocation. Block randomization with a block size of 4 and equal allocation probability was used to assign eligible patients to the intervention group (group B) or control group (group A). With a block size of 4, six possible group sequences were available: ABAB, AABB, BBAA, BABA, BAAB, and ABBA. One sequence was randomly selected, and four eligible patients were assigned in order within each block. This process was repeated until all eligible participants had been allocated. Blinding was implemented during both data collection and data analysis. Inclusion criteria were a confirmed diagnosis of primary stage 1 HTN or stage 2 HTN, age older than 60 years, a minimum HTN duration of 6 months, and provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria were refusal to participate and the presence of other chronic diseases or severe complications related to HTN.

Interventions

The intervention framework was based on the CSM [14], and the educational content was approved after a literature review and consultation with the resident physician at the health center. The intervention was delivered by one trained researcher, AMP, in the conference room of Khalil-Azad Community Health Center. The intervention addressed five dimensions of IP: identity, defined as beliefs about how the disease is perceived; timeline, including acute, chronic, and cyclical perceptions; consequences, including effects on quality of life, interpersonal relationships, and professional responsibilities; cause; and control/cure, including treatment control and personal control [19].

Participants in the intervention group were divided into two subgroups. The intervention consisted of five weekly sessions (one session per week). The core educational content was the same for all participants, but the emphasis placed on specific concepts varied according to participants’ responses to the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ). The control group received only routine care at the center, including blood pressure measurement, blood pressure medication adjustment, and instructions on medication use. To comply with research ethics, participants in the control group received the intervention program as a pamphlet after completing the questionnaires. Measures

Participants’ demographic characteristics

The demographic questionnaire assessed age, sex, marital status, occupation, education level, and family history of primary HTN.

BIPQ

The BIPQ was developed by Broadbent et al. [20]. It includes nine items that assess different aspects of IP. Five dimensions reflect patients’ cognitive responses: consequences (item 1), timeline (acute/chronic; item 2), personal control (item 3), symptom identity (item 4), and treatment control or treatability (item 5). Two dimensions assess emotional responses: illness concern (item 6) and emotional representation (item 8). The questionnaire also assesses illness coherence (item 7) and cause (item 9, an open-ended question). Except for the cause dimension, all dimensions are evaluated on a Likert scale ranging from 0 to 10, yielding a total possible score of 0–80. For items 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8, higher scores indicate a more serious perception of the health threat posed by the illness. For items 3, 5, and 7, higher scores indicate greater perceived personal control, treatment control, and illness coherence, respectively. The criterion validity of this questionnaire was reported to range from 0.75 to 0.82, and its internal reliability, assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, ranged from 0.79 to 0.89. In Iran, the content validity index was reported to range from 0.83 to 0.91, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.68. The construct validity of the Persian version of the BIPQ was evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis, which showed acceptable goodness-of-fit indices [15].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess data normality. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for between-group comparisons, and the Wilcoxon test was used for within-group comparisons. The chi-square test was used to assess associations between nominal variables. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics Statement

All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol and activities complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of Larestan University of Medical Sciences, Larestan, Iran (IR.LARUMS.REC.1402.032). All participants were informed of the study aims and methods and were assured of anonymity and data confidentiality. Participants were informed that they were free to withdraw from the study at any time. Participants in both groups completed the IP questionnaire before the intervention and at the end of the eighth week after the intervention began. After the study was completed, the researcher distributed a training booklet to the control group.

This study was registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/) on March 24, 2024, under registration ID IRCT20240122060771N2.

RESULTS

Seventy-six participants with HTN were assessed for eligibility. Fourteen participants were excluded before randomization, and the remaining 62 participants were randomized. All 62 randomized participants completed the study and were included in the final analysis, as shown in the trial profile (Figure 1). Table 2 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the study participants. The mean ages of participants in the intervention and control groups were 67.67±6.02 years and 67.03±6.08 years, respectively. As shown in Table 2, the two groups did not differ significantly in age, education level, sex, or family history of HTN (p>0.05). Before the intervention, mean IP subscale scores did not differ significantly between the intervention and control groups (p>0.05). After the intervention, the intervention group showed significant improvements in IP subscale scores compared with the control group (p<0.05), except for illness consequences (p=0.403), illness concern (p=0.197), and emotional representation (p=0.742). Within the intervention group, all IP subscale scores except illness consequences changed significantly after the intervention (all p<0.05), except for the illness consequences subscale. In the control group, no significant differences were observed in any subscale at the end of the eighth week compared with before the intervention (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, no previous study has evaluated the effect of a CSM-based educational program on older adults with primary HTN. This study was conducted to address that gap. The findings indicated that the CSM-based educational program improved IP in the treatment control, personal control, coherence, timeline, and identity subscales. However, the intervention did not have a significant effect on illness consequences, illness concern, or emotional representation.

The current findings show a significant improvement in illness coherence after the intervention, which is consistent with studies by Eam and Surit [21], Khodaparast et al. [22], Karampour et al. [23], Rakhshan et al. [15], and Saranjam et al. [12]. In contrast, Cossette et al. [24] found that rehabilitation interventions for patients with acute coronary syndrome did not improve coherence, perceived acute or chronic disease status, or treatment control. This difference may be related to the intervention program used in the present study, which was designed according to the CSM and therefore may have provided a more comprehensive educational structure. The current results also indicate that perceived identity improved after the intervention, suggesting that participants better understood the signs and symptoms of HTN as well as its timeline and acute or chronic nature. Consistent with the current findings, Beaune et al. [6] demonstrated that improving patients’ understanding of the disease, including its duration, symptoms, complications, management methods, and available social support, could strengthen self-control. Similarly, Zakerimoghadam et al. [25] found that using the CSM to improve disease understanding improved self-care among patients with heart failure. Taken together, these comparative findings suggest that CSM-based interventions may provide an important framework for improving patients’ understanding of disease signs and symptoms.

The current findings indicate that treatment control and personal control improved after the intervention. Similar improvements in treatment control and self-control have been reported in studies of patients with diabetes [21], trauma [26], and heart failure [27]. Rajabloo et al. [16] applied the principles of the CSM in patients and found that treatment adherence was strongest among those who recognized the seriousness of their condition and its potential complications and used self-regulatory strategies to manage aggravating factors. These findings are consistent with those of the current study. The results suggest that education based on Leventhal et al. [1]’s model can improve treatment adherence and may influence the beliefs that guide patients’ behaviors related to HTN. Similarly, Richardson et al. [28] found that the treatment control subscale improved after a self-regulatory model-based intervention among patients with cancer in the intervention group.

In trauma patients, however, a 6-month follow-up showed no significant improvement in treatment control, possibly because the nature of traumatic injury created uncertainty about adherence to care instructions [29]. Because of differences in disease scope and clinical context, a precise comparison between trauma patients and patients with HTN may not be possible. Finally, the present study showed no significant between-group improvement in illness consequences, illness concern, or emotional representation, which is consistent with findings from previous studies [12,15]. The findings are also consistent with those of Rakhshan et al. [30], who investigated IPs among patients with cardiac pacemakers using a self-regulation framework. After a CSM-based educational intervention, patients expected fewer consequences and less severe illness, viewed their illness as chronic, controllable, and treatable, showed greater illness coherence, and developed more positive cognitive and emotional representations of their illness and related conditions. Emotional responses, concern, and disease-related anxiety may require psychological interventions. In addition, because the target population in the present study was older adults, age-related loneliness and social isolation may have contributed to these outcomes. Future studies should therefore include interventions that address patients’ mood and emotional state to support better outcomes.

This study was limited by its single-center design. Therefore, the study should be replicated in other health care facilities and with more diverse samples. Virtual learning approaches may also improve patients’ access to educational resources. In the current study, patients’ attendance outside the clinic’s designated time frame prevented documentation of blood pressure, which was an important background variable. Future studies should account for this variable. Another limitation was the short follow-up period, which prevented assessment of possible long-term effects and confirmation of the sustained effectiveness of the educational program. The main strength of the present study was that the CSM-based educational program addressed all aspects of IP, including nature, cause, personal control, treatment control, coherence, and consequences, across the broader cognitive and emotional dimensions.
