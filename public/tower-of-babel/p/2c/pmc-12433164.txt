Targeted prostate cancer screening using polygenic risk scores: lessons from the BARCODE1 trial
Authors: David Hennes, Hyerin Park, Niranjan Sathianathen, David C Chen, Declan G Murphy, Marlon L Perera
Published: 2025

Screening for prostate cancer (PCa) remains a controversial topic, largely due to concerns regarding overdiagnosis and overtreatment. However, these concerns must be balanced against the risk of delaying the diagnosis of clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa). The BARCODE1 study, recently published in the New England Journal of Medicine, represents a landmark effort to address this issue by operationalising polygenic risk scoring for population-level PCa screening (1). Patients were invited for screening at a population level, with 8,953 (22%) expressing interest and polygenic risk scores (PRS) ultimately calculated for 6,393 (16%) men for 130 PCa-associated variants using DNA extracted from saliva. A total of 745 participants (12%) had a PRS in the top 10th percentile, and of these, 468 (7% of the total cohort) subsequently underwent further investigation with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and prostate biopsy.

Of these men, 187 (40%) were found to have PCa, with 55% of these being intermediate risk or higher according to the 2024 National Comprehensive Cancer Network criteria. Only a minority (n=30) of these met standard biopsy criteria of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) >3 and Prostate Imaging-Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) score of ≥3. Of these 30 patients, 2 had low or very low risk disease, 6 had favourable intermediate, and 22 had unfavourable intermediate or above. One patient was unable to undergo MRI and was found to have high-risk disease. Of the remaining 156 additional cancer diagnoses, 82 were low or very low risk, 57 were favourable intermediate, and 17 were unfavourable intermediate risk or above.

For an asymptomatic man concerned about his risk of PCa, should assessment with a PRS be considered as the initial step in a screening pathway? The BARCODE1 trial represents a significant step forward in precision screening for PCa, but its findings must be contextualized within the broader landscape of PCa diagnostics. While the study demonstrates that polygenic risk-based screening can increase detection of clinically significant disease, the utility of the test and the stage at which it should be implemented in the diagnostic algorithm for PCa remains unclear. Currently in the UK, a prostate biopsy is offered if a man has a raised PSA level of >3 ng/mL or an abnormal digital rectal examination (DRE), and multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) demonstrating a PI-RADS 4 or 5 lesion (2). For PI-RADS 3 lesions, biopsy is considered based on additional risk factors such as PSA density and clinical assessment, with the decision made through shared decision-making between clinician and patient (2). While the findings indicate that 72% of men diagnosed via PRS-guided screening would have been missed by the UK’s traditional screening pathway, this value may be overstated in other countries like Australia, where MRI is commonly obtained for PSA >2 ng/mL with family history or other red flags (e.g., abnormal DRE, elevated PSA density), and biopsy is still considered even with a negative or equivocal MRI in the presence of red flags. The UK guidelines are also relatively restrictive compared to the European Association of Urology (EAU) and American Urological Association (AUA) guidelines, which recommend biopsy consideration even with negative MRI findings if clinical suspicion remains high due to risk factors such as significant family history or abnormal PSA density (3,4). As a result, the UK pathway may underdetect cancers that would be identified in other countries where lower PSA thresholds, broader use of mpMRI, and more liberal biopsy criteria are employed, thereby limiting the generalisability of the findings from the study to other clinical settings. In contrast to the findings from BARCODE1, a comprehensive review of data from the Polygenic Score Catalog found a much lower median PCa detection rate of 11% with a 5% false positive rate, indicating that this screening method may have more modest predictive power when applied to population screening (5). Furthermore, there was a high attrition rate in the study, with only 16% of those invited undergoing genetic testing, and 41% of high-risk men declining biopsy—often due to concerns about the procedure. The study population was also enriched for men with a family history of PCa (21%), limited to European ancestry, and permitted inclusion of participants who had undergone prior PSA testing and biopsies. This high rate of attrition, sources of potential bias and methodological limitations may impact the generalisability of the study’s findings.

In parallel, advances in PCa screening are shifting the diagnostic landscape. The accuracy of PSA based screening for PCa can now be enhanced by incorporating additional blood- and urine-based biomarkers, which have demonstrated improved sensitivity and specificity in detecting csPCa (6,7). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) have been studied as potential biomarkers to refine PCa risk assessment and could theoretically inform clinical decisions in cases where conventional screening tests are negative but suspicion remains high, or to guide selection for and intensity of active surveillance (AS) (8). However, current evidence indicates that SNP-based risk models alone are less accurate than established markers like PSA, and their addition does not significantly improve decision-making regarding biopsy or management in AS (9).

The advent of mpMRI has significantly improved the specificity of screening and reduced unnecessary biopsies, as clearly demonstrated in the PRECISION trial, which showed that MRI-targeted biopsy detects more csPCa and fewer indolent cancers compared to standard biopsy (10). The PRIMARY trial, a prospective multicenter phase II study, demonstrated that combining prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PSMA PET/CT) with mpMRI improved the negative predictive value for csPCa compared to mpMRI alone, suggesting that this dual-modality approach could further reduce unnecessary biopsies and enhance detection of significant disease (11). The ongoing PRIMARY 2 trial is expected to provide additional insights into the clinical utility of PSMA PET/CT in biopsy-naïve men with suspected PCa, potentially further refining risk stratification (12). AS protocols have also been refined to minimize overtreatment of low-risk disease, as supported by long-term data from the ProtecT trial (13). Furthermore, recent meta-analyses demonstrate that, for carefully selected men with favourable intermediate-risk PCa—particularly those with low-volume Gleason grade group 2 tumours—AS can yield metastasis-free and cancer-specific survival rates comparable to those seen in low-risk cohorts (14).

Routine germline testing for PCa is not currently accessible or recommended in UK guidelines for population screening, with testing generally reserved for men with high-risk, metastatic, or familial disease (15). The UK National Screening Committee continues to advise against systematic population screening for PCa, citing the limitations of PSA as a screening tool and the potential harms of overdiagnosis and overtreatment (16). If future evidence-based publications demonstrate that PRS offer significant clinical utility and cost-effectiveness for risk stratification or early detection, PRS could be considered for integration into UK screening protocols. However, this would require robust validation in diverse populations, demonstration of added value over current risk models, and careful consideration of ethical and economic implications. The ongoing TRANSFORM trial may assist this effort with its aim to directly compare PRS-based screening with standard PSA testing in a large UK population, in order to determine whether risk-stratified approaches can improve detection of csPCa while reducing unnecessary interventions (17). At present, PRS remains investigational, and its routine adoption in the UK would depend on further high-quality evidence and subsequent guideline updates.

Thus, while BARCODE1 offers proof-of-concept evidence supporting polygenic risk-stratified screening, further validation in diverse populations and demonstration of long-term cost-utility and clinical benefit is required before widespread implementation. If incorporated as part of a multimodal pathway alongside age, family history, PSA, and MRI, PRS could significantly enhance risk stratification and clinical decision-making in PCa diagnostics.
