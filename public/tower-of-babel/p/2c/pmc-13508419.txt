Streptococcus anginosus group brain abscesses and subdural empyemas: exploratory compartment-specific phenotypes and discharge outcomes in a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jin Feng, Bin Zhang, Wenchao Lu, Haibo Liu, Yang Liu, Yourui Zou, Hui Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntracranial suppuration caused by the Streptococcus anginosus group (SAG) includes brain abscess and subdural empyema, but compartment-specific phenotypes and short-term outcomes remain incompletely described. We characterized clinical, radiological, and microbiological features and explored factors associated with poor discharge outcome.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of patients with imaging-confirmed intracranial suppuration and SAG identified by culture and/or metagenomic next-generation sequencing from October 2020 to October 2025. Cases were classified as intracerebral (n = 39), subdural (n = 6), or mixed (n = 7). The primary outcome was Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) at discharge; GOS 1–3 defined poor discharge outcome. Analyses were descriptive, with exploratory univariable logistic regression.ResultsA total of 52 patients were included. Patients with subdural empyema were younger and had numerically lower admission functional status than those with intracerebral infection, whereas subdural and mixed-compartment infections showed higher inflammatory-marker levels. Ventricular involvement was uncommon (5/52, 9.6%) and was observed more frequently among patients with poor discharge outcomes in unadjusted comparisons (5/12 vs. 0/40). Complete ring enhancement was confined to intracerebral cases, and S. intermedius was the predominant species. At discharge, 12 patients (23.1%) had poor outcomes, including six in-hospital deaths (11.5%). Lower admission KPS and fever were exploratory univariable associations with poor discharge outcome.ConclusionsThis study describes compartment-specific clinical, imaging, and microbiological features of Streptococcus anginosus group brain abscesses and subdural empyemas. Ventricular involvement may be a marker of severe disease, but all findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require validation in larger multicenter studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13998-w.

Background

Brain abscess and subdural empyema remain high-stakes intracranial suppurative infections with substantial mortality and long-term neurological morbidity despite modern imaging, neurosurgical techniques, and critical care [1, 2]. Contemporary practice still shows marked heterogeneity in diagnostic and treatment strategies, reflecting persisting uncertainty regarding optimal source investigation, drainage strategy, and antimicrobial duration/route [3]. Large contemporary cohorts and international data indicate that survival remains nontrivial and is influenced by host factors and disease severity at presentation [4].

The microbiological landscape of intracranial abscesses continues to evolve, with streptococci dominating many community-acquired cases, while Staphylococcus aureus remains prominent in postsurgical settings [1, 5]. Within streptococci, the Streptococcus anginosus group (SAG)—S. intermedius, S. constellatus, and S. anginosus—is increasingly recognized as a leading cause of brain abscess in modern series [1, 6]. Notably, S. intermedius has emerged as a dominant pathogen in multiple cohorts, supporting the clinical impression that SAG members are particularly associated with deep-seated suppurative disease [5]. Alongside conventional culture, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has improved pathogen detection for central nervous system infections and can provide actionable results in selected scenarios, especially when cultures are negative or prior antibiotics have been administered [7, 8].

Anatomically, intracranial suppurative infections can be conceptualized by compartment involvement—intracerebral (parenchymal), subdural, or mixed/multicompartment disease—which may reflect different routes of spread, inflammatory burden, and surgical decision-making [9]. Empyema-predominant disease can present with more prominent systemic inflammatory features compared with parenchymal abscess in contemporary cohorts [10, 11]. Meanwhile, outcome studies in brain abscess have focused mainly on baseline illness severity and comorbidity, while pathogen- and compartment-stratified descriptions remain limited [12]. Therefore, in a tertiary neurosurgical referral hospital in China, we conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of microbiologically confirmed SAG-associated brain abscesses and subdural empyemas, stratified by compartment, to characterize clinical-radiological-microbiological phenotypes and to explore factors associated with poor discharge outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study conducted at the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University, Yinchuan, Ningxia, China, a tertiary neurosurgical referral hospital. The study aimed to characterize the clinical, radiological, microbiological, and discharge-outcome features of Streptococcus anginosus group (SAG)-associated brain abscesses and subdural empyemas. The cohort comprised eligible admissions occurring between October 2020 and October 2025. Following ethics approval, the historical medical records were retrospectively reviewed, and the extracted data were de-identified before analysis. This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE statement. The retrospective review of historical clinical data was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Review Committee of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University, Yinchuan, China (approval No. KYLL-2025-2576; approval date: 18 November 2025). The requirement for informed consent to participate was waived by the Medical Research Ethics Review Committee of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University.

Patient population and inclusion/exclusion criteria

Patients were identified through a systematic query of the hospital electronic health record (EHR) system using ICD-10 codes for intracranial suppurative infections (G06.x; e.g., G06.0 [intracranial abscess] and G06.2 [subdural/extradural abscess]). Potential cases were cross-referenced with microbiology laboratory records for cultures and/or metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) positive for Streptococcus anginosus group (SAG) species (S. intermedius, S. constellatus, or S. anginosus). When multiple admissions occurred for the same patient, only the first episode during the study period was included.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients of any age; (2) imaging-confirmed brain abscess and/or subdural empyema on CT and/or MRI, supported by compatible clinical presentation; (3) microbiological confirmation of SAG by culture and/or mNGS from a sterile-site specimen, including abscess/subdural pus aspirate obtained during neurosurgical drainage, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and/or blood; and (4) availability of discharge outcome data.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) healthcare-associated or acute post-traumatic infections, defined as onset ≥ 48 h after hospital admission or infection related to a recent neurosurgical procedure, intracranial instrumentation, or recent cranial trauma preceding symptom onset (e.g., craniotomy, ventricular drainage/shunt, intracranial monitoring, or stereotactic aspiration); (2) polymicrobial infections in which SAG was not considered the predominant pathogen, operationalized as (i) culture showing multiple organisms without SAG as the dominant isolate, or (ii) mNGS detecting multiple organisms without SAG as the highest-abundance clinically plausible bacterial taxon; and (3) incomplete medical records precluding key variable extraction or discharge outcome assessment. Remote craniofacial trauma without evidence that the current episode was an acute post-traumatic infection was retained as a potential predisposing condition. Potential mNGS contaminants or low-abundance organisms inconsistent with the clinical syndrome, specimen type, and conventional microbiology were not used to define eligibility.

Data collection and variables

Data were extracted from electronic health records (EHRs), radiology reports, microbiology laboratory records, and operative notes. Two investigators (Jin Feng and Wenchao Lu) independently abstracted data using a standardized case report form and a prespecified data dictionary. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus; unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by the senior author (Hui Ma). Data were compiled into a standardized database (Microsoft Excel) and cleaned prior to analysis. Symptom variables were coded as binary (present/absent) based on admission documentation. When documented, the symptom-onset-to-admission interval, admission-to-surgery interval, repeat surgical procedures, antimicrobial regimens, total planned antimicrobial duration, infectious diseases consultation, and echocardiographic evaluation were reviewed; however, these items were not consistently available in standardized patient-level form and could not be summarized or compared reliably. Echocardiography was not systematically performed or reliably documented; therefore, the proportion of patients evaluated and the number of infective endocarditis cases could not be determined. These variables were not used for multivariable or comparative modeling.

Variables were collected and categorized as follows:

Demographics and baseline status. Age and sex were recorded at admission. Functional status was assessed using the Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS; 0–100) documented on admission.

Clinical presentation. Presenting symptoms included headache, fever, seizure, vomiting, altered mental status/coma, and focal neurological deficits (e.g., hemiparesis, aphasia, facial palsy). Neurological deficit subtypes were not mutually exclusive and could co-occur in the same patient; detailed deficit subtypes were summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Predisposing conditions and comorbidities. Pre-existing conditions and potential risk factors were extracted from problem lists and admission notes, including diabetes mellitus, structural heart disease, chronic liver disease/cirrhosis, chronic kidney disease/uremia, immunocompromised status, pregnancy, and craniofacial trauma history (e.g., frontal sinus fracture). Immunocompromised status was defined a priori as active chemotherapy, chronic systemic corticosteroid therapy equivalent to prednisone ≥ 20 mg/day, or known HIV/AIDS.

Radiological features. Imaging variables were extracted primarily from formal CT/MRI reports; when report descriptions were ambiguous, source imaging was reviewed by the two investigators to reach consensus. Cases were stratified by anatomical compartment into: (1) intracerebral (parenchymal abscess without subdural collection), (2) subdural empyema (subdural purulent collection without parenchymal abscess), and (3) mixed/multicompartment (concurrent intracerebral abscess and subdural empyema).

Lesion location was categorized as lobar (frontal/temporal/parietal/occipital/cerebellar hemisphere) or deep parenchymal (basal ganglia, thalamus, or brainstem). Multilobar involvement was defined as involvement of ≥ 2 lobes and was summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Ventricular involvement was recorded as present if CT/MRI reports described any of the following: intraventricular rupture/communication, intraventricular debris or pus, ventriculitis, or ependymal enhancement.

Lesion burden was captured using (i) abscess number for patients with a parenchymal component, (ii) maximum parenchymal diameter (mm) defined as the largest reported cross-sectional diameter of the parenchymal abscess on CT/MRI (applicable to intracerebral and mixed groups), and (iii) maximum subdural thickness (mm) defined as the maximal reported thickness of subdural collection (applicable to subdural and mixed groups).

MRI enhancement pattern was categorized as complete ring enhancement, partial ring enhancement, or absent/non-ring enhancement, based on radiology report descriptors.

Laboratory parameters. Admission inflammatory markers included white blood cell count (WBC, ×10^9/L), C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/L), and procalcitonin (PCT, ng/mL). When multiple measurements were available, the first result within 24 h of admission was used.

Microbiological data and suspected source. SAG species (S. intermedius, S. constellatus, S. anginosus) and detection method (culture, mNGS, or both) were recorded from laboratory reports. For culture-positive cases, species-level identification followed the routine clinical microbiology workflow of the hospital laboratory; the specific identification platform was not consistently retrievable from retrospective records and was therefore not analyzed separately. mNGS-only attribution required a sterile-site specimen, compatible clinical and radiological syndrome, SAG as the dominant clinically plausible bacterial taxon, and no alternative pathogen explaining the presentation. Discordant culture/mNGS findings and possible contaminants were adjudicated by two investigators using specimen source, organism abundance, prior antibiotic exposure, and clinical plausibility. Blood culture positivity was recorded as yes/no. Co-infection was defined as detection of additional clinically plausible organisms from sterile-site specimens by culture and/or mNGS. The presumed primary source of infection was categorized as sinonasal, otogenic, pulmonary, dental/oral, or unknown based on clinical documentation, extracranial imaging, specialist assessment, operative findings, and anatomic plausibility during the index admission; when multiple possible sources were present, the source judged most consistent with the clinical course and available investigations was recorded as suspected rather than definitive.

Management variables. Surgical management during index admission was categorized as burr-hole irrigation and drainage (BHID) alone, BHID plus decompressive craniectomy (BHID + DC), BHID plus endoscopic sinus surgery (BHID + ESS), or combined BHID + DC+ESS, based on operative records. BHID was generally used for drainable intracranial purulent collections, DC for severe mass effect, brain swelling, or raised intracranial pressure requiring decompression, and ESS for suspected sinonasal source control when indicated by otolaryngology assessment. Repeat procedures and completeness of source control were reviewed when documented, but standardized patient-level source-control adjudication was not available. Antimicrobial treatment was summarized by intravenous antibiotic duration (days) and discharge route strategy (IV only vs. IV-to-oral switch). Exact antimicrobial initiation times, detailed regimen changes, total planned treatment duration, oral-switch criteria, and infectious diseases consultation status were heterogeneous or incompletely documented in structured data; treatment was therefore interpreted descriptively rather than compared as a prognostic exposure.

Outcomes. The primary outcome was Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) at discharge (1–5). Poor discharge outcome was defined a priori as GOS 1–3 (death, vegetative state, or severe disability). Secondary outcomes included in-hospital mortality (GOS 1). No standardized post-discharge follow-up dataset was available; therefore, delayed neurological recovery, cognitive impairment, epilepsy, relapse, readmission, and repeat surgery after discharge could not be evaluated.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.0). This was a retrospective analysis of all eligible SAG-associated intracranial suppurative infections identified during the study period; therefore, no formal a priori sample-size calculation was performed. Continuous variables are summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables as counts with percentages. Comparisons across anatomical compartments (intracerebral, subdural, and mixed) were conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and Fisher exact test for categorical variables because of small subgroup sizes and sparse cells. P values for compartment comparisons were considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

The primary outcome was poor discharge outcome, defined as GOS 1–3 at discharge. Associations between candidate factors and poor discharge outcome were assessed using univariable logistic regression, reporting odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For factors with zero-cell counts, corrected estimates were calculated using the Haldane-Anscombe method. However, because ventricular involvement occurred in only five patients and one outcome stratum contained no exposed patients, its corrected OR was considered too unstable for meaningful interpretation and was not reported; this variable was presented descriptively. Given the limited number of poor-outcome events (n = 12), multivariable modeling was not performed to avoid overfitting. Accordingly, all reported ORs should be interpreted as exploratory univariable associations, not independent prognostic effects. Analyses used complete cases for each comparison, and no imputation was performed. NA in descriptive tables indicates structural non-applicability rather than missingness.

Results

Baseline characteristics and clinical presentation

A total of 52 patients with Streptococcus anginosus group (SAG)-associated intracranial suppurative infections were included and stratified by anatomical compartment into intracerebral (n = 39), subdural (n = 6), and mixed (n = 7) groups (Table 1). Overall, the median age was 47.0 years (IQR 20.5–58.0). The median age was lower in the subdural (17.0 years [IQR 6.0–38.0]) and mixed (23.0 years [IQR 14.0–54.5]) groups than in the intracerebral group (49.0 years [IQR 28.0–62.0]; P = 0.040), although this finding should be interpreted descriptively because of small subgroup sizes. Males comprised 73.1% (n = 38) of the cohort. The median admission Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) was 55.0 (IQR 40.0–80.0); KPS tended to be lower in the subdural group (25.0 [IQR 10.0–50.0]) compared with intracerebral and mixed infections (P = 0.062); this small-sample comparison is descriptive.

The most common presenting symptom was headache (65.4%, n = 34), followed by fever (53.8%, n = 28) and seizures (36.5%, n = 19). Altered mental status or coma was documented in 15.4% (n = 8), vomiting in 7.7% (n = 4), and focal neurological deficits in 40.4% (n = 21). Altered mental status/coma was more frequent in subdural empyema (50.0%) than in intracerebral (10.3%) and mixed infections (14.3%; Fisher exact P = 0.053), although this finding should be interpreted descriptively because of small subgroup sizes. Differences in headache, fever, seizures, and focal deficits showed no clear statistical separation across compartments.

Predisposing conditions were uncommon, including diabetes mellitus (7.7%, n = 4), structural heart disease (3.8%, n = 2), hepatitis B virus-related decompensated cirrhosis (3.8%, n = 2), chronic kidney disease/uremia (3.8%, n = 2), immunocompromised status (3.8%, n = 2), pregnancy (1.9%, n = 1), and prior open frontal sinus fracture (3.8%, n = 2). Pregnancy occurred in only one patient and was not emphasized as a compartment-related finding despite the nominal P value.

Detailed subtypes of focal neurological deficits are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Hemiparesis was the most frequent deficit (28.8%, n = 15), followed by aphasia (9.6%, n = 5) and facial palsy (1.9%, n = 1); gait disturbance was reported in 5.8% (n = 3). Neurological deficit subtypes were not mutually exclusive.

Taken together, subdural and mixed infections occurred in younger patients. Impaired consciousness and lower admission KPS were most apparent in the subdural group, whereas inflammatory markers were higher in the subdural and mixed groups. These observations are descriptive because of the small subgroup sizes.

Radiological and laboratory characteristics

Radiological and laboratory characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Deep parenchymal location was observed in 17.3% of patients (9/52) and occurred only in the intracerebral group (23.1%, 9/39). Ventricular involvement was identified in 9.6% (5/52) and was also observed only in intracerebral infections (12.8%, 5/39). These compartment patterns should be interpreted cautiously because of limited precision in the small subdural and mixed groups.

Among cases with a parenchymal component (intracerebral and mixed; n = 46), the median maximum parenchymal diameter was 33.0 mm (IQR 24.0–41.0): 33.0 mm (IQR 23.5–40.5) in intracerebral infections and 30.5 mm (IQR 24.0–50.0) in mixed infections (P = 0.701). Among cases with a subdural component (subdural and mixed; n = 13), the median maximum subdural thickness was 9.0 mm (IQR 6.0–12.0): 10.5 mm (IQR 9.0–13.0) in subdural empyema and 6.0 mm (IQR 5.0–10.0) in mixed infections (P = 0.098).

MRI enhancement patterns differed by compartment. Complete ring enhancement was observed in 50.0% overall (26/52) and occurred exclusively in intracerebral infections (66.7%, 26/39). In contrast, absent enhancement was common in subdural empyema (66.7%, 4/6) and was also observed in mixed infections (28.6%, 2/7), compared with 2.6% (1/39) in intracerebral infections.

Blood cultures were positive in 15.4% (8/52) without clear separation across compartments. Admission inflammatory markers showed a descriptive compartment-related gradient: median WBC was highest in mixed infections (24.3 × 10^9/L [IQR 17.5–27.5]), followed by subdural empyema (15.3 × 10^9/L [IQR 14.8–19.1]) and intracerebral infections (11.2 × 10^9/L [IQR 9.4–13.9]). Median CRP and PCT showed similar gradients, with higher values in subdural and mixed infections than in intracerebral infections.

For cases with a parenchymal component (n = 46), anatomical distribution is detailed in Supplementary Table S2. The frontal lobe was most frequently involved (43.5%, 20/46), followed by the parietal lobe (21.7%, 10/46). Periventricular/intraventricular location occurred in 10.9% (5/46), and multilobar involvement (≥ 2 lobes) occurred in 6.5% (3/46); both were confined to intracerebral infections.

Microbiological confirmation relied mainly on conventional culture (86.5%, 45/52). Culture plus mNGS was used in 7.7% (4/52), and mNGS alone in 5.8% (3/52). Culture was most frequently used in intracerebral infections (92.3%, 36/39), whereas adjunct mNGS approaches were proportionally more common in subdural and mixed infections.

Polymicrobial/co-infection was rare: Porphyromonas endodontalis was detected in one subdural case (1.9% overall; 16.7% in subdural), and this patient had a confirmed maxillofacial space infection as the extracranial source. Sinusitis appeared numerically more frequent in subdural (66.7%) and mixed infections (71.4%) than in intracerebral infections (28.2%), but this pattern is descriptive and imprecise because of small subgroup sizes and partially presumptive source attribution.

Management strategies and antibiotic course

Management strategies and antibiotic course are summarized in Table 4. Burr-hole irrigation and drainage (BHID) was the predominant surgical approach (80.8%, 42/52). Combined procedures (BHID + DC, BHID + ESS, or BHID + DC+ESS) were more common in subdural and mixed infections, reflecting selected use of decompression and extracranial source-control procedures rather than a standardized treatment comparison. All patients received systemic antimicrobial therapy. The median intravenous antibiotic duration was 28.0 days (IQR 21.0–42.0) overall and was numerically longer in subdural (39.0 days [IQR 35.0–49.0]) and mixed infections (40.0 days [IQR 31.0–43.5]) than in intracerebral infections (26.0 days [IQR 21.0–35.0]). At hospital discharge, 38.5% of patients (20/52) remained on IV-only therapy, whereas 61.5% (32/52) transitioned to oral step-down therapy (IV-to-PO). Exact antibiotic regimens, timing of initiation, total planned duration, oral-switch criteria, and infectious diseases consultation status were heterogeneous or incompletely documented in retrospective records; therefore, treatment variables are presented descriptively and were not used to infer effectiveness.

Exploratory factors associated with poor discharge outcome

Univariable logistic regression analyses comparing patients with poor discharge outcome (GOS 1–3; n = 12) versus non-poor discharge outcome (GOS 4–5; n = 40) are summarized in Table 6. Admission functional status differed between groups: median KPS was 40.0 (IQR 15.0–45.0) in the poor-outcome group versus 60.0 (IQR 50.0–80.0) in the non-poor group, and higher KPS was associated with lower odds of poor discharge outcome (OR 0.95 per 1-point increase, 95% CI 0.92–0.98). Fever at presentation was more common among patients with poor discharge outcome (83.3% vs. 45.0%; OR 6.11, 95% CI 1.18–31.54). Ventricular involvement was rare and was observed in 5 of 12 patients with poor discharge outcome and in none of the 40 patients with non-poor outcome (41.7% vs. 0%; Fisher exact P < 0.001). Because of the small number of exposed patients and the zero cell, this variable is presented descriptively and no OR is reported. Age, suspected primary source category, SAG species, seizures, and infection compartment were not clearly associated with poor discharge outcome in this small cohort.

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cohort of 52 patients with SAG-associated intracranial suppuration, we identified descriptive compartment-specific patterns in presentation, imaging, microbiology, management, and discharge outcomes. S. intermedius was the predominant species. Subdural and mixed-compartment infections showed greater inflammatory burden, whereas imaging phenotypes differed by anatomical compartment. The subdural (n = 6) and mixed (n = 7) groups were small, and all subgroup and outcome findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Microbiology and source attribution are clinically important in SAG-associated intracranial infections [13]. SAG organisms are frequently linked to suppurative foci arising from the oropharyngeal and upper aerodigestive mucosa, including sinonasal and odontogenic/maxillofacial disease [6, 14]. In our cohort, S. intermedius was the most frequent species [15–18]. Sinusitis was the most common suspected source, but source attribution was partly presumptive and sometimes based on clinical and imaging correlation rather than direct microbiological proof. Therefore, source patterns should be regarded as descriptive rather than causal evidence of route of spread.

The apparent sinonasal predominance among subdural and mixed infections is biologically plausible because contiguous spread from paranasal sinus disease can involve extra-axial spaces [19, 20], but the small subgroup sizes preclude reliable statistical separation. These findings support careful extracranial source evaluation in clinical practice, particularly sinonasal, otogenic, dental/oral, and maxillofacial assessment [21–24], while acknowledging that this cohort cannot define an optimal source-control algorithm.

Microbiological confirmation relied mainly on conventional culture, with mNGS used in a minority of cases. The retrospective design limited our ability to evaluate diagnostic yield, low-abundance organisms, platform-specific species identification, or the effect of prior antibiotics. We therefore treated mNGS as a supportive clinical diagnostic method rather than as an exposure for outcome analysis.

Compartment-based stratification provided useful descriptive organization. Subdural and mixed infections tended to occur in younger patients and showed higher inflammatory markers, whereas lower functional status and impaired consciousness were most apparent in the subdural group [25–27]. These patterns are consistent with a more acute inflammatory presentation in extra-axial or multicompartment disease, but they may also reflect referral timing, pre-hospital antibiotic exposure, symptom duration, and small-number instability.

Radiological findings also varied by compartment. Complete ring enhancement was characteristic of intracerebral abscesses, whereas subdural empyema often lacked a classic ring-enhancing capsule because pus is located in an extra-axial space. Thus, absence of complete ring enhancement should not be used to reduce suspicion for subdural or mixed infection when clinical and diffusion-weighted imaging findings are concerning [28].

Ventricular involvement deserves attention because it may reflect intraventricular rupture, ventriculitis, or ependymal spread [29, 30]. Ventricular involvement appeared to be associated with more severe disease and poorer discharge outcomes; however, this observation was based on only five cases, all in the poor-outcome group. Because the sparse distribution and zero cell precluded a stable effect estimate, the finding should be regarded as a descriptive warning signal rather than a validated independent predictor.

Management data were consistent with contemporary multidisciplinary care but were not suitable for treatment-effect inference. BHID was the most common neurosurgical approach, while DC and ESS were used selectively for mass effect, brain swelling, or suspected sinonasal source control [31]. Oral step-down therapy has been described in retrospective brain abscess cohorts [32]. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, antimicrobial regimens, timing of initiation, treatment duration, oral-switch practices, and surgical decision-making were heterogeneous and not standardized, precluding formal comparative analysis of treatment effects.

The discharge outcome focus is another central limitation. GOS at discharge captures early survival and severe disability but does not capture delayed cognitive recovery, epilepsy, relapse, readmission, repeat surgery, or long-term functional trajectory. Therefore, our conclusions are limited to short-term discharge outcomes [27, 33].

Strengths of this study include a SAG-focused cohort with species-level reporting, compartment-based stratification, and prespecified radiological definitions for deep location and ventricular involvement. The 2020–2025 timeframe reflects contemporary diagnostic and treatment practices in a tertiary neurosurgical referral hospital.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design introduces selection bias. Second, the subdural (n = 6) and mixed (n = 7) groups were small, limiting the reliability of compartment comparisons and making P values descriptive only. Third, poor discharge outcome occurred in only 12 patients, so multivariable modeling was not performed and all reported ORs represent exploratory univariable associations rather than independent prognostic effects. In particular, subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating only. Fourth, outcomes were assessed only at discharge using GOS, without standardized mid- or long-term follow-up for neurological recovery, cognition, epilepsy, relapse, readmission, or repeat surgery. Fifth, source attribution was partly presumptive and may be misclassified. Sixth, detailed antimicrobial regimens, timing of initiation, total planned duration, oral-switch criteria, infectious diseases consultation status, repeat surgery, and completeness of source control were not consistently available in structured retrospective records. Finally, echocardiography was not systematically performed or reliably documented, so neither the proportion evaluated nor the number of infective endocarditis cases could be determined. The generalisability of these findings is limited by the single-center referral setting, local diagnostic and management practices, and the small number of subdural and mixed-compartment cases. Future multicenter studies with standardized microbiological adjudication, management variables, echocardiographic assessment when clinically indicated, and longer-term follow-up are needed to validate these exploratory signals.

Conclusions

This study provides a compartment-based characterization of SAG-associated brain abscesses and subdural empyemas. Ventricular involvement may represent a marker of severe disease, but this observation and all other subgroup findings remain exploratory and require validation in larger multicenter studies with standardized longer-term follow-up.
