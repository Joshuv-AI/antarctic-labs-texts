SOCS3 suppresses glioblastoma growth via JAK-STAT inhibition and mitochondrial unfolded protein response activation
Authors: Gaolei Hou, Zhaofei Song, Jingtao Wang, Junmei Wang, Hongbin Wang, Tao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGlioblastoma (GBM) represents one of the most aggressive brain malignancies, characterized by rapid proliferation and pronounced resistance to apoptosis, resulting in poor therapeutic outcomes. Suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3), a crucial negative regulator of the JAK-STAT pathway, frequently undergoes epigenetic silencing in GBM. However, the tumor-suppressive functions of SOCS3 remain inadequately defined. The present study explores the role of SOCS3 in regulating mitochondrial stress responses and evaluates its therapeutic potential in GBM.MethodsSOCS3 was overexpressed in GBM cell lines, and its effects on cell viability and apoptosis were assessed using MTT assays, TUNEL staining, and Western blotting. Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is malignant tumor arising from glial cells and represents the most prevalent form of primary brain cancer in adults [1]. Classified by malignancy grade, Grade IV GBM exhibits the highest level of aggressiveness [2]. Tumor progression is driven by diverse molecular abnormalities, and the pronounced heterogeneity and biological complexity of GBM present substantial challenges to effective treatment [3–5]. Clinically, despite treatments such as surgical resection, radiation, and chemotherapy, the five-year survival rate remains low due to the tumor’s invasiveness and recurrence [6]. GBM cells exhibit immune evasion, apoptosis resistance, and multidrug resistance, significantly limiting the efficacy of traditional therapies [7–10]. Therefore, investigating the molecular mechanisms of GBM and exploring new therapeutic targets have become key areas of focus in current biomedical research.

Suppressor of Cytokine Signaling 3 (SOCS3), a member of the SOCS protein family, plays a pivotal role in regulating key physiological functions, including immune responses, cell proliferation, apoptosis, and metabolic homeostasis [11]. Under normal physiological conditions, SOCS3 negatively regulates the Janus Kinase-Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (JAK-STAT) signaling pathway, thereby suppressing excessive immune responses and cell proliferation [12–14]. In various cancers, SOCS3 expression is frequently suppressed or functionally silenced, resulting in heightened cellular responsiveness to growth factors and promoting tumor initiation and progression [11]. Studies have shown that SOCS3 expression is significantly downregulated in GBM cells, and its inactivation is closely associated with the high malignancy, chemoresistance, and recurrence of GBM [15–18]. The loss of SOCS3 may enhance tumor cell proliferation and resistance to apoptosis by releasing the negative regulation of the JAK-STAT axis [11, 15, 19]. Therefore, SOCS3 holds significant research value as a potential tumor suppressor.

The JAK-STAT pathway has been extensively characterized for its involvement in immune regulation, cellular proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [20–22]. Activation of the pathway occurs upon cytokine binding to specific cell surface receptors, leading to phosphorylation of STAT transcription factors by JAK kinases and subsequent transcriptional activation [22–24]. Dysregulation of JAK-STAT signaling has been implicated in tumorigenesis, with constitutive activation observed in multiple malignancies. In GBM, persistent JAK-STAT signaling is closely associated with enhanced tumor cell proliferation and resistance to apoptosis [25].

The mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt) functions as a protective mechanism activated in response to mitochondrial stress [26, 27]. UPRmt maintains mitochondrial function by activating a series of molecular pathways to prevent cell death induced by stress [28]. Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of UPRmt in cancer development, particularly in modulating cellular survival and death under various intrinsic and extrinsic stress conditions [29, 30]. Studies have demonstrated that the JAK-STAT pathway and UPRmt are mutually regulated and collectively influence tumor cell survival and apoptosis [24, 31].

The present study aims to elucidate the functional role of SOCS3 in GBM by examining its regulatory effect on mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis through inhibition of the JAK-STAT axis and activation of the UPRmt. Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blotting (WB) were employed to assess SOCS3 expression in normal human astrocytes (NHA) and GBM cell lines. SOCS3 overexpression (oe-SOCS3) and knockdown (sh-SOCS3) models were established to investigate its influence on GBM cell proliferation and apoptosis. Proteomic profiling was conducted to identify SOCS3-associated differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) and enriched signaling pathways, thereby delineating its mechanistic role in promoting apoptosis via JAK-STAT inhibition and UPRmt activation. Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and cytochrome C (Cyt C) release were evaluated through JC-1 staining and immunofluorescence assays, further confirming SOCS3-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis. In vivo xenograft experiments demonstrated that SOCS3 overexpression significantly suppressed GBM tumor growth, with enhanced apoptosis driven by UPRmt activation. Collectively, these findings establish SOCS3 as a promising therapeutic target and provide mechanistic and experimental rationale for combinatorial strategies that simultaneously modulate JAK-STAT signaling and UPRmt in GBM treatment.

Results

SOCS3 promotes apoptosis in GBM cells

Expression levels of SOCS3 were examined in NHA (SVG p12) and GBM cell lines (A172, U251) using RT-qPCR and WB analysis. The results (Fig. 1A) revealed that the mRNA and protein levels of SOCS3 were significantly lower in A172 and U251 cells compared to NHA cells, with A172 cells showing a more pronounced decrease. Therefore, A172 cells were selected for further studies. We transfected A172 GBM cells with oe-NC, oe-SOCS3, sh-NC, and sh-SOCS3 vectors (Fig. 1B). RT-qPCR and WB analyses confirmed successful modulation of SOCS3 expression in each group. As shown in Figure S1, SOCS3 mRNA and protein levels were markedly elevated in the oe-SOCS3 group relative to the oe-NC group. In contrast, significant reductions in SOCS3 expression were observed in all three knockdown groups compared to sh-NC, with sh-SOCS3-1 achieving the most efficient silencing. Consequently, sh-SOCS3-1 was chosen for further functional assays.

We further investigated the effects of SOCS3 on GBM cell proliferation and apoptosis. WB analysis (Fig. 1C) revealed that overexpression of SOCS3 led to significant upregulation of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax, Cleaved Caspase-3 (Cl-Casp3), and Cleaved Caspase-9 (Cl-Casp9), alongside marked downregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, compared to the oe-NC group. Conversely, SOCS3 knockdown resulted in decreased expression of Bax, Cl-Casp3, and Cl-Casp9, and increased expression of Bcl-2 relative to the sh-NC group. Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase-Mediated dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) staining (Fig. 1D) demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of apoptotic cells in the oe-SOCS3 group compared to oe-NC, while the sh-SOCS3 group exhibited a lower apoptosis rate than sh-NC. Consistently, 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay results (Fig. 1E) showed that cell proliferation was significantly suppressed in the oe-SOCS3 group and enhanced in the sh-SOCS3 group. These findings were consistent with the WB results (Fig. 1F), which showed reduced expression of Ki67 and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) in the oe-SOCS3 group, and elevated levels of both proteins in the sh-SOCS3 group.

In summary, our results demonstrate that overexpression of SOCS3 significantly promotes apoptosis and inhibits proliferation in GBM cells, while SOCS3 knockdown has the opposite effect.

Bioinformatics analysis reveals the role of SOCS3 in UPRmt-dependent apoptosis in GBM cells

Previous work identified microRNA-19b (miR-19b) as an oncogenic regulator that suppresses SOCS3 expression and modulates the JAK-STAT axis to influence apoptosis in GBM cells [32]. To further explore the potential mechanism by which SOCS3 affects GBM cell apoptosis, we performed proteomic analysis on A172 cells transfected with either oe-NC or oe-SOCS3. Compared to the oe-NC group, the oe-SOCS3 group altered the expression of 52 proteins, with 33 proteins upregulated and 19 proteins downregulated (Fig. 2A, B). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed that these DEPs were associated with apoptosis, UPR, mitochondrial autophagy, and the JAK-STAT pathway (Fig. 2C, D). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further demonstrated that pro-apoptotic and UPR-related pathways were predominantly enriched in the oe-SOCS3 group, while pathways associated with mitochondrial autophagy and JAK-STAT signaling were enriched in the oe-NC group (Fig. 2E).

Next, we analyzed the correlation between SOCS3 expression and genes related to apoptosis, UPRmt, and mitochondrial autophagy using TCGA-GBM data. The results revealed a positive correlation between SOCS3 expression and the cell senescence gene CDKN1A (P21), the pro-apoptotic gene BAX, and the UPRmt genes ATF5 and DDIT3 (CHOP). Conversely, SOCS3 expression showed a negative correlation with cell cycle-related genes CCNA2 and CDK2, the anti-apoptotic gene BCL2, and mitochondrial autophagy genes PINK1 and PRKN (Fig. 3). Collectively, these findings indicate that high SOCS3 expression is closely associated with elevated UPRmt markers and an enhanced pro-apoptotic phenotype. Moreover, previous studies have suggested that activation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway inhibits UPRmt-induced apoptosis [33]. These observations suggest that SOCS3 may promote UPRmt-dependent apoptosis in GBM cells by inhibiting JAK-STAT signaling.

Meanwhile, we further evaluated the association between SOCS3 expression levels and clinical prognosis in GBM patients. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients with low SOCS3 expression had significantly prolonged overall survival (OS) compared with those with high SOCS3 expression (HR = 4.37, 95% CI: 3.31–5.76, P < 0.001), indicating that elevated SOCS3 is strongly associated with shorter survival. This trend was similarly observed in disease-specific survival (DSS), where the SOCS3 high-expression group exhibited markedly worse DSS (HR = 4.51, 95% CI: 3.37–6.04, P < 0.001). In addition, in the progression-free interval (PFI) analysis, patients with high SOCS3 expression consistently showed significantly poorer prognosis (HR = 2.89, 95% CI: 2.31–3.61, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Taken together, these findings indicate that high SOCS3 expression is not only closely associated with molecular features such as UPRmt activation, upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes, and suppression of mitophagy, but also predicts significantly worse clinical outcomes in GBM patients. These results further support the potential pro-tumorigenic role of SOCS3 in GBM progression.

SOCS3 significantly promotes GBM cell apoptosis via the mitochondrial pathway

To investigate the specific mechanisms by which SOCS3 promotes GBM cell apoptosis, we examined three major apoptotic pathways: the Death Receptor (DR) pathway, the endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway, and the mitochondrial-dependent pathway (Fig. 4A). WB analysis revealed no significant differences in the expression levels of DR-related genes (Fas, FasL, TNF-α, and Cleaved Caspase-8) or endoplasmic reticulum stress-related genes (p-PERK, GRP78, Cleaved Caspase-7, and Cleaved Caspase-12) across the A172 cell groups (Fig. 4B). These results suggest that SOCS3 does not induce apoptosis via the DR or endoplasmic reticulum stress pathways.

MMP depolarization and the release of Cyt C are key events in the mitochondrial-dependent apoptotic pathway. To evaluate mitochondrial integrity, JC-1 staining was used to measure changes in MMP across different experimental groups. The results (Fig. 4C and E) demonstrated that the oe-SOCS3 group exhibited a significant reduction in MMP compared to oe-NC, whereas the sh-SOCS3 group displayed a marked increase relative to sh-NC, indicating that SOCS3 facilitates mitochondrial membrane depolarization. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 4D and F) revealed a significant increase in Cyt C release from mitochondria into the cytoplasm in the oe-SOCS3 group compared to the oe-NC group. In contrast, Cyt C release was significantly diminished in the sh-SOCS3 group relative to the sh-NC group.

The Caspase-9 activity assay showed that Caspase-9 activity was significantly increased in the oe-SOCS3 group compared with the oe-NC group, whereas it was significantly reduced in the sh-SOCS3 group compared with the sh-NC group (Fig. 4G).

These results collectively indicate that SOCS3 induces GBM cell apoptosis via the mitochondrial-dependent pathway.

SOCS3 significantly promotes UPRmt-dependent apoptosis in GBM cells by inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 pathway

We further investigated the molecular mechanisms by which SOCS3 promotes GBM cell apoptosis through the mitochondrial pathway. After transfection, cells were treated with a JAK2/STAT3 pathway inhibitor (Ruxolitinib) and a JAK2/STAT3 agonist (IL-6) as illustrated in Fig. 5A. qRT-PCR and WB analysis (Figs. 5B, C) showed that SOCS3 overexpression significantly elevated mRNA and protein expression of HSP60, ClpP, mtHSP70, LONP1, and CHOP while reducing phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3 compared to the oe-NC group. IL-6 treatment reversed these effects, indicating that activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway can suppress SOCS3-induced mitochondrial stress responses. Conversely, SOCS3 knockdown markedly decreased mRNA and protein expression of HSP60, ClpP, mtHSP70, LONP1, and CHOP while increasing JAK2 and STAT3 phosphorylation, which was counteracted by Ruxolitinib treatment. Additionally, TUNEL staining results (Fig. 5D) revealed that apoptosis was significantly reduced in the oe-SOCS3 + IL-6 group compared to oe-SOCS3 alone, while the sh-SOCS3 + Ruxolitinib group showed higher apoptotic rates than the sh-SOCS3 group.

Next, we treated the oe-SOCS3 + IL-6 group with 1 mM NR for 12 h to trigger the UPRmt-dependent apoptosis model (Fig. 5E). TUNEL staining results (Fig. 5F) showed a significant increase in the apoptosis rate in the oe-SOCS3 + IL-6 + NR group compared to the oe-SOCS3 + IL-6 group.

The results demonstrate that SOCS3 significantly promotes GBM cell apoptosis through a mitochondrial-dependent pathway by inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway and activating the UPRmt. Furthermore, IL-6 can reverse the pro-apoptotic effects of SOCS3, suggesting that SOCS3’s inhibitory effect on the JAK2/STAT3 pathway is limited (Fig. 5G), while NR restores UPRmt and enhances the apoptotic response. These results underscore the pivotal role of SOCS3 in modulating mitochondrial stress responses and apoptosis in GBM.

SOCS3 inhibits GBM progression in nude mice by suppressing JAK2/STAT3 and promoting UPRmt

To further investigate the impact of SOCS3 in vivo, we subcutaneously injected A172 cells and U251 cells carrying oe-NC or oe-SOCS3 into the right flank of nude mice to establish a GBM model. In parallel, intracranial injections were performed to establish orthotopic GBM models. Ten days later, the mice were treated with IL-6 or NR (Figs. 6A and 7A; Figure S2A; Figure S3A). Tumor size was monitored every two days, and tumor weight was recorded at the study endpoint. Mice in the oe-SOCS3 group exhibited significantly smaller tumors in both volume and weight compared to the oe-NC group (Figs. 6B-D and 7B, D; Figure S2B, D; Figure S3B, D). IL-6 treatment reversed this suppression, increasing tumor burden in the oe-SOCS3 + IL-6 group, while NR effectively counteracted the tumor-promoting effect of IL-6. WB analysis (Figs. 6 and 7E, H; Figure S2E, H; Figure S3E, H) demonstrated that SOCS3 overexpression markedly upregulated UPRmt-associated proteins (HSP60, ClpP) and apoptosis-related proteins (Caspase-3 and Caspase-9), while downregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, compared to the oe-NC group. Conversely, IL-6 treatment diminished the expression of HSP60, ClpP, Bax, Caspase-3, and Caspase-9, while elevating Bcl-2 levels in the oe-SOCS3 + IL-6 group. Notably, co-treatment with NR and IL-6 restored HSP60 and ClpP expression and re-elevated Caspase-3 and Caspase-9 levels, indicating reactivation of UPRmt signaling and enhanced apoptotic activity. Further assessment of JAK2/STAT3 phosphorylation revealed that SOCS3 significantly inhibited STAT3 activation by suppressing JAK2-mediated phosphorylation, supporting the involvement of this pathway in downstream UPRmt regulation. TUNEL staining (Figs. 6I, J and 7I, J; Figure S2I, J; Figure S3I, J) showed a substantial increase in apoptotic cells in the oe-SOCS3 group relative to oe-NC. This effect was mitigated by IL-6 but was restored upon NR administration, confirming that SOCS3-induced apoptosis is mediated via UPRmt activation and JAK2/STAT3 inhibition.

Immunohistochemical staining (Figs. 6 and 7K, L; Figure S2K, L; Figure S3K-L) revealed that SOCS3 overexpression significantly reduced the expression of proliferation markers Ki67 and PCNA in tumor tissues compared to the oe-NC group (p < 0.05). In the oe-SOCS3 + IL-6 group, IL-6 treatment markedly elevated Ki67 and PCNA levels, whereas co-administration of NR effectively reversed this increase.

Collectively, these findings indicate that SOCS3 suppresses GBM tumor growth by promoting UPRmt-dependent apoptosis through inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Although IL-6 activation of JAK2/STAT3 counteracts these effects, NR treatment reactivates UPRmt signaling, thereby enhancing SOCS3-driven apoptosis and proliferation inhibition.

Discussion

The role of SOCS3 in GBM has attracted considerable attention, and multiple studies have shown that its inactivation is closely associated with tumor progression and therapeutic resistance [34, 35]. In addition to confirming the downregulation of SOCS3 expression in GBM cells, the present study identifies a novel mechanism in which SOCS3 regulates the UPRmt through the JAK–STAT signaling pathway. As a negative regulator of JAK–STAT signaling, SOCS3 plays a critical role in immune regulation; however, its specific functions in GBM cells remain largely unexplored. This study is the first to reveal that SOCS3 induces apoptosis in GBM cells through the UPRmt pathway by regulating intracellular environmental stress, providing new insights into its potential as a therapeutic target in GBM.

The JAK–STAT signaling pathway serves as a critical regulator of cell proliferation, survival, and apoptosis, and extensive research has investigated its role in tumor biology [19, 36]. Persistent activation of the JAK-STAT axis in GBM cells is typically associated with tumor proliferation, invasiveness, and resistance [37–39]. Our findings demonstrate that SOCS3, by inhibiting the JAK-STAT axis, significantly promotes apoptosis in GBM cells, which is consistent with previous studies. Most existing studies have emphasized the tumor-promoting effects of JAK–STAT signaling on cell proliferation, while limited attention has been given to the negative regulatory role of SOCS3 within this pathway. The present work experimentally confirms the influence of SOCS3 on GBM cell proliferation and apoptosis, thereby reinforcing its function as a modulator of JAK–STAT signaling and underscoring its dual role in tumor progression.

The UPRmt plays a pivotal role in tumor cells, particularly under conditions of environmental stress. By activating multiple molecular pathways, UPRmt enables cells to adapt to stress and preserve essential functions [27]. Most studies have investigated its involvement in cellular survival, aging, and apoptosis [30, 40, 41]. UPRmt promotes tumor cell survival and proliferation by maintaining mitochondrial function and metabolic homeostasis. Its activation enhances antioxidant capacity, regulates mitophagy, and induces the expression of molecular chaperones (e.g., HSP60) and proteases (e.g., LONP1) through transcription factors such as ATF5 and CHOP [42], thereby alleviating cellular stress. However, under conditions of prolonged or excessive activation, UPRmt may also trigger apoptotic pathways, such as CHOP-mediated cell death signaling, thereby counteracting tumor progression [43]. Thus, UPRmt serves both as an adaptive mechanism for tumor survival and a potential therapeutic target. Moreover, the JAK-STAT axis and UPRmt cooperatively regulate stress adaptation, apoptosis inhibition, and immune evasion in various cancers. In hepatocellular carcinoma, phosphorylated STAT3 translocates into mitochondria, where it enhances electron transport chain efficiency and collaborates with UPRmt to sustain mitochondrial function, supporting tumor survival under metabolic stress [44]. In pancreatic cancer, chemotherapy-induced stress triggers JAK1 phosphorylation of Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) at the S155 site, leading to its nuclear translocation and the formation of a PRMT5/c-Myc complex. This complex activates pro-metastatic pathways while suppressing apoptosis, a process dependent on the JAK1/TLR3/STAT3 axis and overlapping with the c-Myc–mediated activation of UPRmt [45]. Limited attention has been given to the regulatory role of SOCS3 in the UPRmt pathway. This study is the first to propose that SOCS3 activates UPRmt through the JAK-STAT axis, thereby promoting mitochondria-dependent apoptosis in GBM cells. Compared to previous research, the findings demonstrate that SOCS3 regulates immune responses and modulates mitochondrial function to drive tumor cell death, offering new insight into the role of UPRmt in GBM.

SOCS3 functions extend beyond regulation of the JAK–STAT signaling pathway and may influence tumor progression through additional molecular interactions. For example, SOCS3 has been shown to suppress the NF-κB signaling pathway [46]. NF-κB acts as a central mediator linking inflammation and cancer, enabling immune molecules (such as microRNAs, IL-6, IL-12, IFNs, and LPS) to exert tumor-promoting effects [47]. Therefore, SOCS3 may regulate IL-6 secretion and expression through NF-κB signaling, thereby indirectly modulating the JAK-STAT/UPRmt axis. Studies have further shown that SOCS3 expression is higher in GBM than in normal samples. Elevated SOCS3 mRNA expression correlates with WHO grade, histological subtype, treatment response, and prognosis, with higher grades associated with worse survival outcomes [48]. Additionally, upregulation of SOCS3 has been linked to increased radiosensitivity in the otherwise radioresistant GB39 cell line, further supporting its role in GBM radioresistance [49]. Studies have shown that the expression of SOCS3 in various immune cells can impact immune responses and immune escape mechanisms, highlighting the importance of SOCS3 in the tumor microenvironment [14, 50]. The present study focuses on the role of SOCS3 in GBM cells, particularly in the regulation of apoptosis. Loss of SOCS3 in GBM cells directly promotes malignant phenotypes while simultaneously remodeling the tumor microenvironment through soluble mediators (e.g., IL-6, CCL2), thereby indirectly strengthening immunosuppression. Therefore, SOCS3 exerts dual functions in both tumor and immune cells, forming a synergistic network that accelerates GBM progression. Interaction between SOCS3 and the UPRmt pathway not only influences tumor cell growth but also modulates immune cell activity, contributing to immune evasion. Future research should further define the role of SOCS3 in the tumor immune microenvironment and assess whether combining SOCS3 regulation with immunotherapeutic strategies could enhance GBM treatment outcomes.

In vivo experiments are crucial for validating the potential of new therapeutic targets. This study investigates the inhibitory effect of SOCS3 overexpression on GBM growth in a nude mouse model and further explores the modulation of SOCS3 activity by IL-6 and NR. Previous studies have primarily emphasized the role of IL-6 in regulating immune responses in tumors [51, 52], with fewer addressing its interaction with SOCS3 within tumor cells. Our findings indicate that IL-6 reverses the activation of UPRmt by SOCS3, while NR further restores UPRmt, enhancing SOCS3-induced apoptosis. This discovery not only confirms the therapeutic potential of SOCS3 in cancer treatment but also provides new experimental evidence for future combination therapies for GBM. Investigation of the synergistic effects of SOCS3, IL-6, and NR may open new avenues for therapeutic development.

Currently, GBM treatment relies primarily on surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. However, due to tumor heterogeneity, recurrence, and resistance, the efficacy of existing therapies is often suboptimal [53, 54]. SOCS3 inhibits the JAK-STAT axis and activates UPRmt, thereby promoting apoptosis in GBM cells and providing significant therapeutic potential [17, 55]. Compared to conventional therapies, SOCS3’s regulatory mechanism not only suppresses tumor proliferation but also inhibits tumor growth by inducing apoptosis. Thus, targeting SOCS3 or its related pathways could offer new directions for GBM treatment. Future approaches may combine SOCS3 targeting with existing therapies to enhance antitumor effects, improve treatment outcomes, and reduce drug resistance. Potential therapeutic strategies include SOCS3 gene therapy, in which viral or nanoparticle-based vectors are used to enhance SOCS3 expression in tumor tissues, thereby strengthening its negative regulation of the JAK-STAT axis. Alternatively, compounds may be screened or designed to promote SOCS3 expression or stabilize the SOCS3 protein. However, these approaches face challenges, including tissue-specific expression and the short half-life of SOCS3, potential disruption of normal immune functions, and limitations in delivery efficiency and specificity. Thus, careful consideration must be given to balancing antitumor efficacy with potential side effects.

In multiple solid tumors, including GBM, CAR-T cell therapy has not achieved the level of efficacy observed in hematologic malignancies. Key barriers include tumor cell resistance to single-antigen CAR-T targeting and the highly immunosuppressive GBM microenvironment. The SOCS3–UPRmt axis regulates GBM cell survival and exhibits potential immunomodulatory effects, suggesting that combining inhibitors or agonists of this pathway with CAR-T therapy may help overcome microenvironmental resistance. Such a strategy could promote CAR-T targeting of tumor cells while enhancing the release of cytokines and chemokines, thereby alleviating immunosuppression. In addition, recent studies have shown that deletion of Kdm6b enhances antigen presentation, interferon responses, and phagocytosis in myeloid cells by inhibiting the immunosuppressive mediator SOCS3, ultimately improving anti-PD1 efficacy [56]. These findings represent another potential strategy to improve the efficacy of CAR-T and anti-PD1 therapies in the future.

The present study provides an in-depth investigation of SOCS3 in GBM, demonstrating that SOCS3 promotes mitochondria-dependent apoptosis by suppressing the JAK-STAT axis and activating UPRmt. These findings establish a novel theoretical framework for the therapeutic application of SOCS3 in GBM and offer experimental support for combination strategies targeting both JAK-STAT and UPRmt. Clinically, targeting SOCS3 and its associated pathways may represent a new therapeutic approach for GBM. Nevertheless, this study has certain limitations. Although the in vivo effects of SOCS3 were confirmed in A172 and U251 xenograft models in nude mice, clinical samples and patient data were not included for validation. Moreover, the focus of this study was primarily on the JAK-STAT and UPRmt pathways, while SOCS3 may also influence GBM cell biology through other signaling networks, which warrants further investigation. In addition, the potential synergistic effects of SOCS3 with other therapeutic strategies require further exploration. Expanding sample sizes and conducting clinical trials will help to further validate the therapeutic potential of SOCS3 in GBM and provide stronger support for its clinical application.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study provides robust experimental evidence supporting SOCS3 as a promising molecular target for GBM therapy. Future research should integrate clinical samples, patient cohorts, and investigations of additional signaling pathways to advance the development and clinical translation of SOCS3-based therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

SVG p12 human normal astrocyte cells (SNL-650, SUNNCELL), A172 GBM cells (STM-CL-5066, Stemrecell, China), and U251 GBM cells (STM-CL-5251, Stemrecell, China) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (11965092, Gibco, USA) enriched with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (10270-106, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15140122, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Cell treatment and grouping

Lentiviral constructs carrying sh-NC, sh-SOCS3, oe-NC, or oe-SOCS3 were generated using pLenti6/V5-DEST™ (V49610, Thermo Fisher, USA) for overexpression and pLenti6/BLOCK-iT™-DEST (K494300, Thermo Fisher, USA) for knockdown. HEK-293T cells (CRL-11268, ATCC, China) were used for packaging. Viral supernatants were harvested after 48 h and concentrated by Genechem (Shanghai, China). GBM cells (A172) were cultured to approximately 50% confluence and transfected with the lentivirus (MOI = 5). After 48 h of transfection, cells were selected using 10 µg/mL puromycin (HY-K1057, MCE) and further cultured for 1 week to obtain stable transfected cell lines [57–59]. Targeting sequences for shRNAs are detailed in Table S1. Transfection efficiency was assessed by RT-qPCR and WB 48 h post-transfection.

To modulate the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, oe-SOCS3 cells were treated with IL-6 (10 ng/mL; 200-06, PeproTech, USA), while sh-SOCS3 cells received Ruxolitinib (5 µM; S1378, Selleck, USA). All treatments were applied for 24 h [59–61]. To establish the UPRmt-dependent apoptosis model, cells were treated with 1 mM Nicotinamide Riboside (NR) (SMB00907, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 12 h [62].

Cell groups: oe-NC (A172 cells transfected with lentivirus carrying oe-NC), oe-SOCS3 (A172 cells transfected with lentivirus carrying oe-SOCS3), sh-NC (A172 cells transfected with lentivirus carrying sh-NC); sh-SOCS3 (A172 cells transfected with lentivirus carrying sh-SOCS3), oe-SOCS3 + IL-6 (A172 cells transfected with lentivirus carrying oe-SOCS3 and treated with IL-6), sh-SOCS3 + Ruxolitinib (A172 cells transfected with lentivirus carrying sh-SOCS3 and treated with Ruxolitinib), oe-SOCS3 + IL-6 + NR (A172 cells transfected with lentivirus carrying oe-SOCS3 and treated with both IL-6 and NR).

Proteomics

Total proteins were extracted from three biological replicates of A172 cells transfected with either SOCS3 overexpression (oe-SOCS3) or control (oe-NC) plasmids. Cell lysis was performed using high-intensity sonication, followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA assay (Beyotime, China). Proteins were reduced, alkylated with iodoacetamide, and digested with trypsin in a two-step protocol: the first digestion used a 1:50 trypsin-to-protein ratio overnight at 37 °C, followed by a second digestion (1:100) for 4 h. Peptides were desalted using Strata X C18 SPE columns (Phenomenex), lyophilized, and subsequently labeled with TMT reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Peptide separation was performed on an EASY-nLC 1000 UPLC system using a linear gradient of mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid, 90% acetonitrile), starting from 8% to 16% over 30 min, then to 30% over 25 min, rising to 80% within 2 min, and held for 3 min. Mass spectrometric analysis was conducted using an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode for MS/MS fragmentation. Data were analyzed using MaxQuant software, and protein annotation was performed via the UniProt and KEGG databases. Differential expression analysis was identified utilizing the “edgeR” R package (v4.0.16), with a threshold of |log2FoldChange| > 1 and p < 0.05.

Functional enrichment analysis

DEPs were analyzed for GO enrichment, covering Molecular Function (MF), Cellular Component (CC), and Biological Process (BP), and KEGG pathway enrichment using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.10.1). For GSEA, reference gene sets were retrieved from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB: http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp). Each analysis was run with 1000 permutations, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Public data analysis

RNA-seq data for GBM patients were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://gdc.cancer.gov/). Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate associations between SOCS3 expression and genes involved in cellular senescence, apoptosis, UPRmt, and mitophagy.

Cell proliferation assay (MTT assay)

The proliferative activity of GBM cells was evaluated using a colorimetric MTT assay. Cells from each experimental group were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well. Treatments were administered at 4, 24, 48, and 72 h after seeding. At the designated time points, 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL; Roche, Switzerland) was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 4 h to allow for formazan crystal formation. After incubation, 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added to each well to solubilize the formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA) to quantify cell viability.

TUNEL staining for apoptosis detection in cell and tissue samples

To assess apoptosis levels in cell and tissue samples, TUNEL staining was performed. For cell samples, the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (11684795910, Roche, Switzerland) was applied, while the TUNEL Assay Kit (C1091, Beyotime) was employed for tissue samples. Cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), washed with Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS), and then subjected to TUNEL staining. Tumor tissue samples were fixed for 24 h, dehydrated through an ethanol gradient, cleared in xylene, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min before TUNEL staining.

For cellular assays, the TUNEL reaction mixture was directly added, followed by DAPI staining to visualize nuclei. Apoptotic cells showed intense green fluorescence under a fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Apoptotic rates were quantified by analyzing five randomly selected fields. In tissue sections, permeability was enhanced by treating with 20 µg/mL proteinase K for 15 min at room temperature (RT), followed by PBS rinsing and incubation with a peroxidase inhibitor (P0100A, Beyotime) for 20 min. Sections were then incubated with 50 µL biotin at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by 0.3 mL of stop solution for 10 min, and then treated with 50 µL of Streptavidin-HRP for 30 min at RT. DAB was applied for 5 min to develop the chromogenic signal.

Tissue samples were imaged under a light microscope, and the number of DAB-positive apoptotic cells (brown/red) and total hematoxylin-stained nuclei (blue) was analyzed using ImageJ software to calculate the apoptosis percentage.

Assessment of MMP using JC-1 staining

To assess changes in mitochondrial membrane potential, the JC-1 staining kit (C2006, Beyotime, China) was used. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h before incubation with JC-1 staining solution. Fluorescence signals corresponding to JC-1 aggregates (red) and monomers (green) were detected using a fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). A decline in MMP was reflected by diminished red fluorescence and enhanced green fluorescence. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining for Cyt C localization

To investigate the effect of SOCS3 overexpression on Cyt C localization, we performed immunofluorescence staining. First, cells from each group were fixed in 4% PFA (1.00496, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), washed with PBS, and post-fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min. After permeabilization with PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 for 5 min, the cells were blocked in PBS with 2% serum. Subsequently, cells were incubated overnight with an anti-Cyt C antibody (556432, BD PharMingen, USA), followed by incubation with a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody (B40912, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 1 h. Fluorescence images were acquired with a Leica fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) to assess Cyt C release from mitochondria into the cytoplasm. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Caspase-9 activity assay

Cells from each group were collected and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was carefully discarded. Cell pellets (2–10 × 10⁶ cells) were lysed in 50–100 µL of lysis buffer, followed by gentle vortexing and incubation on ice for 10 min, with an additional vortex step afterward. Caspase-9 activity in each group was then measured using a Caspase-9 Activity Assay Kit (BC3890, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Experimental animals and ethics

Six- to eight-week-old male Balb/c nude mice of SPF grade (401, Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were maintained under SPF conditions at 22–25 °C with 60–65% humidity on a 12-hour light/dark cycle. After one week of acclimatization, the health status was confirmed prior to experiments. All animal procedures followed institutional and national ethical guidelines and were approved by the Affiliated Hospital of Hebei Engineering University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) . Mice were housed under humane conditions, and efforts were made to minimize distress throughout the study. At the endpoint, animals were euthanized under ether anesthesia in accordance with approved protocols.

Construction of GBM nude mouse model

Male Balb/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old, n = 10 per group) were used for xenograft establishment. After one week of acclimatization, tumor cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells in 100 µL sterile PBS per mouse. Both A172 and U251 cells, as well as their SOCS3-overexpressing counterparts, were employed. Tumor growth was monitored daily, and dimensions were measured every other day with a caliper (160 − 153, Mitutoyo, Japan), including the longest diameter (L), perpendicular diameter (W), and height (H). Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = π/6 × L × W × H. When tumors reached 1.5–2 mm³, drug administration was initiated. IL-6 (200 µg/kg; 200-06, PeproTech, USA) was injected intraperitoneally every two days for a total of seven doses. NR (200 mg/kg, N3376, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was administered daily via gavage for 14 days. The control group received an equivalent volume of sterile PBS. After 4 weeks, the mice were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation, and tumor tissues were immediately harvested for further analysis [63, 64].

Nude Mouse Grouping (n = 10): Model: Mice injected with A172 cells/U251 cells; oe-NC: Mice injected with A172 cells/U251 cells transfected with empty vector; oe-SOCS3: Mice injected with A172 cells/U251 cells overexpressing SOCS3; oe-SOCS3 + IL-6: Mice injected with A172 cells/U251 cells overexpressing SOCS3, treated with IL-6 (intraperitoneally); oe-SOCS3 + IL-6 + NR: Mice injected with A172 cells/U251 cells overexpressing SOCS3, treated with IL-6 and NR.

Orthotopic xenograft model in nude mice

A172-luc, U251-luc, and their SOCS3-overexpressing derivatives were harvested under optimal conditions. After discarding the culture medium, cells were washed twice with PBS, digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, and neutralized with complete DMEM. Following centrifugation, cells were resuspended in PBS, washed, counted, and adjusted to the desired concentration. The suspensions were kept on ice until implantation. Male Balb/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old) were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneally). After shaving the scalp, animals were fixed on a stereotaxic frame. Following disinfection and incision, a burr hole was drilled 3 mm posterior to the bregma and 2 mm lateral to the midline (left hemisphere). A microsyringe (10 µL) was used to deliver 5 µL of cell suspension (5 × 10⁵ cells) vertically into the brain at a depth of 3 mm, then retracted by 0.5 mm. Injection was performed slowly (1 µL/min), with 1-minute pauses between increments. The syringe was kept in place for 3 min before withdrawal. The burr hole was sealed with bone wax, and the incision was closed by layered suturing.

Animal grouping (n = 10): Model: Nude mice injected with A172 or U251 cells.oe-NC: Nude mice injected with A172 or U251 cells transfected with empty vector.oe-SOCS3: Nude mice injected with SOCS3-overexpressing A172 or U251 cells.oe-SOCS3 + IL-6: Nude mice injected with SOCS3-overexpressing A172 or U251 cells and treated with IL-6 (intraperitoneal injection).oe-SOCS3 + IL-6 + NR: Nude mice injected with SOCS3-overexpressing A172 or U251 cells, treated with IL-6 (intraperitoneal injection) and Nicotinamide Riboside (NR) [65].

In vivo bioluminescence imaging (BLI): Nude mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) and then injected intraperitoneally with 150 mg/kg D-luciferin. After 5 min, bioluminescence signals were captured using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS).

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded tumor tissues were sectioned, deparaffinized, and subjected to antigen retrieval in a 100 °C water bath. Sections were blocked with 5% goat serum (C0265, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 20 min at RT and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Ki67 (ab15580, 1:100, rabbit, Abcam) and PCNA (E607250, 1:50, rabbit, Sangon Biotech). The next day, sections were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ab6721, Abcam, 1:1000) for 20 min at 37 °C, followed by streptavidin-HRP (A0303, Beyotime) for 20 min at 37 °C. Visualization was achieved using DAB (P0203, Beyotime), and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin (C0107, Beyotime) for 1 min, then blued in 1% ammonia water. Slides were dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared in xylene, and mounted in neutral resin. Stained sections were examined using an Olympus BX63 microscope. For each sample, five random high-power fields were selected, and 100 cells were counted per field to determine the percentage of positive cells. The staining results were evaluated semi-quantitatively based on both the proportion of positive cells and the staining intensity.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from each group of cells and tumor tissues using TRIzol reagent (16096020, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The quality and concentration of RNA were measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (ND-1000, Nanodrop, USA). Approximately 1 µg of RNA from each sample was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (RR047A, Takara, Japan). Quantitative PCR was conducted with the SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II kit (DRR081, Takara, Japan) on a real-time PCR system (ABI 7500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). GAPDH served as the internal control. Primer sequences, synthesized by GENERAL BIOL, are listed in Table S2. The relative expression of the target gene was calculated utilizing the 2-ΔΔCt method.

WB

Mitochondrial and cytosolic proteins were extracted using the Mitochondrial Protein Isolation Kit (78840, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Total proteins from tumor tissues were isolated with RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (40 µg) were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk (A600669, Sangon Biotech, China) containing 0.1% Tween-20 (A100777, Sangon Biotech, China) for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies (listed in Table S3) were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG, ab205718, 1:5000; goat anti-mouse IgG, ab205719, 1:5000; Abcam, UK) for 2 h at RT. Protein bands were visualized using ECL reagents (RPN2232, Amersham, UK) and imaged with the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH (ab9485, 1:2500, Abcam, UK) served as the loading control.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted independently at least three times, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group differences were analyzed using independent sample t-tests or one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s HSD test when appropriate. For non-normally distributed data or unequal variances, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
