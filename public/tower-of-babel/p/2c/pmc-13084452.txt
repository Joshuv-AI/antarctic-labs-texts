Chemical Investigation of an Oxy-steam MILD Combustion Process Utilizing Hydrogen Peroxide
Authors: Jordan A. C. Kildare, Michael J. Evans, Muhammad A. Chishty, Paul R. Medwell, Alfonso Chinnici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adaptation of hydrogen fuel for heating applications presents several challenges, particularly thermal NO x production and reduced radiative heat transfer. Oxy-steam combustion in the MILD (moderate or intense low-oxygen dilution) regime addresses both challenges by removing the nitrogen from the oxidizer and introducing steam as a diluent that can increase thermal radiation. However, both steam and pure oxygen are required to achieve these conditions. This study investigates the feasibility of using hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as an oxygen and steam carrier for hydrogen-fuelled heating applications, as when heated, H2O2 decomposes into water and oxygen. Batch reactor, perfectly stirred reactor, and opposed flow laminar flamelet simulations are conducted over a range of representative conditions to investigate the combustion characteristics of this system. Reactor ignition is found to be enhanced with an increase in H2O2 mass fraction within the oxidizer mixture. However, even at a high H2O2 content, preheating does not allow significant decomposition of the H2O2 into an appropriate radical pool to promote ignition within furnace-relevant residence times, suggesting that external preheating or cracking may be required for practical applications. Fundamentally, H2O2-steam flames without precracking show unique flame structures when compared to the equilibrium/cracked mixtures in MILD conditions, similar to those observed in conventional combustion of H2O2. A double-peak structure of heat release is observed in the H2O2-steam cases, where one peak correlates with the exothermic decomposition of H2O2, and the second peak corresponds to the primary combustion process with the fuel. That finding suggests that there is a significant heat addition into the flow that comes from the decomposition process that cannot be neglected in the reactor analysis, and can contribute to low temperature ignition in these conditions.

Introduction

Combustion is likely to remain a key tool for heating in industrial furnace applications in the coming years, despite advances being made toward electrification, and alternative heating options. A major concern with the continued use of combustion is the production harmful carbon-based emissions. To alleviate this concern, alternative noncarbonaceous fuels are being explored, in particular, hydrogen. However, hydrogen burns hotter than many hydrocarbon fuels at stoichiometric conditions, leading to potential formation of thermal NO x , another undesirable emission. − The high temperatures produced by hydrogen combustion also result in materials challenges due to excessive heat load, along with embrittlement from reactions with hydrogen. Furthermore, combustion of hydrogen instead of hydrocarbons tends to reduce the overall radiative heat transfer in the combustion system, the predominant heat transfer mode in many industrial applications. Hence, three main challenges arise from hydrogen adaptation in place of hydrocarbons for heating applications, namely (i) the potential for higher NO x emissions via the thermal route, (ii) reduction in radiative heat transfer, and (iii) material limits/compatibility.

Thermal NO x production can be effectively countered by reducing the peak temperatures present in a combustion system. The use of moderate or intense low-oxygen dilution (MILD) combustion technology has been shown to reduce the production of NO x by developing a distributed reaction system that suppresses local hotspots. ,, The main characteristics of MILD combustion are the highly diluted oxidizer, as well as high temperature reactant conditions, usually above fuel autoignition. , MILD is typically achieved in furnaces by recirculation of hot exhaust gases and subsequently mixing with the inflowing fuel and oxidizer to vitiate the mixture. − As a result, the average temperature is raised, and the peak temperatures are lowered, thus suppressing the formation of thermal NO x . − NO x reducing MILD conditions can also be achieved with external heating methods, such as concentrated solar, rather than relying on hot combustion products. Management of NO x has been addressed through a combination of MILD combustion technology with oxy-fuel combustion systems, i.e. MILD-oxy systems. , In oxy-fuel combustion, the oxidizer consists of pure oxygen, potentially diluted with a nitrogen-free combustion product such as carbon dioxide (CO2). Dilution of the oxidizer in this form directly reduces the source of NO x , however, a source of oxygen is required, usually needing an air-separation unit or other technology to provide the oxidizer. Combining the reduced peak temperatures of MILD combustion with the low nitrogen content of oxy-fuel combustion results in a significant reduction in the production of NO x from all routes. In the case of hydrogen as the fuel rather than a carbon-based fuel, there is no nitrogen carried via the fuel, leading to negligible NO x production.

Reduction of overall temperature for thermal NO x suppression can be achieved through dilution of the fuel or oxidizer with an inert diluent, such as water (as steam). − Variation of syngas composition (H2/CO) has resulted in reductions of NO x concentrations in MILD conditions when CO content is increased, with CO predominantly acting as a diluent rather than a fuel. Some reduction in the overall radiative transfer relative to pure H2 flames was noted with an increase in CO dilution, owing mainly to the reduced gas temperature. The introduction of steam into the oxidizer has been shown to reduce NO x formation in exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) furnaces, as well as in canonical burners. Steam has a large heat capacity and so is able to absorb significant amounts of energy. It also acts to suppress reactions as it is a stable combustion product, along with reducing the oxidizer fraction via dilution. , Addition of steam also assists in radiative heat transfer, as H2O is a highly radiative species. , However, soot production in steam-diluted low-oxygen combustion has been shown to be reduced with increasing steam content, thus reducing the radiative heat transfer from soot.

Dilution via nitrogen addition to either the oxidizer or fuel results in the reduction of reaction rates due to the oxidizer fraction reduction, however, does not provide the radiative or heat absorption properties of steam. Additionally, increased nitrogen may counter the NO x reduction benefits of MILD combustion. Steam dilution does not have that issue, however, has a major disadvantage in that the energy required to evaporate liquid water is large. As such, it is difficult to effectively recover the required energy from heat exchangers within the industrial process. For that reason, the development of steam-based diluted combustion processes, either in air- or oxy-environments, are required to further fundamental understanding while improving the overall process effectiveness. To this end, the overarching aims of the present study are to develop a MILD oxy-steam combustion process utilizing an alternative steam carrier and compare its combustion characteristics to conventional oxy-steam combustion processes. The addition of steam carrier provides an alternative to address this issue while potentially achieving low NO x and enhanced radiation heat transfer rates. ,

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is both an oxygen and water carrying molecule and may serve as an alternative steam carrier. H2O2 can be considered a safe and readily transportable form of steam, oxygen, and heat in a liquid form when compared to cryogenic or ammonia based transport of H2. − H2O2 also has the capacity to reduce parasitic energy losses and lower both capital and operating costs by minimizing the need for air separation units in conventional oxy-fuel systems. , When heated above 150 °C, H2O2 begins to dissociate through the exothermic reaction 2·H2O2 ⇌ 2·H2O + O2 + 2884.6 kJ/kg (at 25 °C). Typically, H2O2 is in solution with water, added to the steam content introduced. It offers an attractive method of introducing both steam and oxygen into a combustion system. The use of H2O2 within combustion systems is not a new concept. Fundamental combustion analysis of the addition of prevaporized H2O2 as an oxidizer to fuels including methane, − ammonia, aluminum, and n-decane have all shown enhanced flame speeds and burning rates. Premixed flame calculations were used for methane flame analysis, where the oxidizer was a combination of air and H2O2. , Opposed-flow diffusion flames were used to investigate extinction limits of several fuels including methane, with H2O2 as the oxidizer, showing significant increases to limits in all cases with H2O2 addition to the oxidizer. Comparatively, closed zero-dimensional homogeneous batch reactors were used to simulate n-decane combustion, in addition to premixed flame calculations, again with air as the primary oxidant. The variation in available radicals, particularly the increase in available HO2 and OH as a result of H2O2 addition, was noted as the primary means of flame speed enhancement for both methane and n-decane combustion. ,, A computational fluid dynamics analysis was conducted in the case of H2O2 addition to air for combustion of ammonia, under the assumption of prevaporization of both H2O2 and ammonia. That study also noted that the introduction of H2O2 changes the controlling reaction pathways to enhance combustion characteristics. Similarly, accompanying equilibrium calculations to experimental burning rate measurements for aluminum in water/H2O2 mixtures indicated the radical pool developed from H2O2 decomposition (OH, HO2, H) improved burning significantly. Applications to diesel reforming also highlight the significance of the radical pool produced from H2O2 decomposition, specifically resulting in higher hydrogen yield as a result. MILD combustion conditions have been shown to be affected significantly by available radicals, more so than in air, especially for flame stabilization, lift-off height in jet flames, reaction zone development, , and heat release characteristics. However, despite the evidence that H2O2 addition to air combustion strongly affects the radical pool, application in a low-oxygen furnace conducive to MILD combustion has yet to be explored.

From a combustion process perspective, two major challenges in the application of hydrogen to industrial furnace processes still remain largely unsolved, namely, thermal NO x production and radiative heat transfer loss. The first challenge can be primarily addressed through the consideration of an oxy-steam configuration, significantly reducing NO x production by the reduction of N2 input directly. The use of H2O2 in solution with water as the oxidizer source for a hydrogen furnace directly addresses the first challenge by removing the N2 source from the oxidizer. That eliminates the air separation unit for oxygen extraction and thus the residual N2 present in the oxidizer stream. The use of H2O2 may also alleviate the second challenge of radiative heat transfer loss. The decomposition of H2O2 results in available oxygen for combustion, and provides water as a diluent to suppress minor NO x formation from air ingress and provide a MILD-like reactant mixture. In addition, the higher steam content improves radiative heat transfer. As the decomposition is exothermic, it is hypothesized that less preheating would be required to achieve the elevated temperature conditions required to sustain combustion with a diluted oxidizer. That concept is explored as part of the presented work, identifying the conditions in which, chemically, the use of H2O2 for MILD oxy-steam combustion is applicable. This paper will present numerical analyses of applying aqueous H2O2 as an oxidizer in the context of a MILD oxy-steam generating system, via batch reactor, perfectly stirred reactor, and opposed flow laminar flame simulations. The aim is to investigate the feasibility of applying H2O2 in this context from a chemical kinetics standpoint, and as such, the techno-economical considerations involved in the lifecycle of H2O2 are not addressed in this work.

Method and Theory

Operating Principle of the MILD H2O2-Steam Furnace

In a typical oxy-steam furnace, oxygen is provided via an air-separation unit to remove nitrogen, while water is preheated and introduced into the furnace as a way to dilute the combustion process to reduce peak temperatures and improve radiative heat transfer. However, this involves significant amounts of heat energy to raise the temperature of water to a level where it does not suppress combustion as a whole. Figure presents a schematic of the operational workflow for the proposed H2O2-steam furnace as well as the conventional oxy-steam process. Instead of the traditional water inflow and separate oxygen stream that is separated from air, a solution of H2O2 in water is used as the oxygen and water source. It can be preheated using excess heat from the furnace process and does not require the level of preheating that a traditional water stream would, as the H2O2 will decompose exothermically to increase the flow temperature. Thus, compared to traditional oxy-steam furnaces, the air separation process is removed entirely, and the water inflow heating requirements are significantly reduced. This technology, however, has not been applied in practice, and requires evaluation of efficacy first from a chemical standpoint. It is noted that H2O2 is usually produced via a hydroquinone process, where H2 is the feedstock through steam reforming; however, alternative production processes are under development. In particular, substantial research is underway to develop alternative H2O2 production pathways with reduced embedded emissions; consequently, the associated cost and emissions intensity will be strongly dependent on the specific production route adopted.

Simplified schematics for the oxy-steam MILD combustion process using H2O2 (top) and a conventional one using an air separation unit (bottom).

The energy cost of the proposed system can be estimated based on the heat released during decomposition, compared to the latent heat required for vaporization of aqueous H2O2. The heat required to raise the temperature and subsequently vaporize an aqueous solution of H2O2, Δh, can be calculated as Δh=YH2O2(Δhvap,H2O2+ΔTCp,H2O2)+YH2O(Δhvap,H2O+ΔTCp,H2O) where Y i is the mass fraction of species i, Δh vap,i is the heat of vaporization of species i, ΔT is the increase in temperature to boiling, and C p,i is the specific heat capacity of species i. For H2O2, the heat of vaporization is 1.26 MJ/kg, and the specific heat capacity is 2.64 kJ/kg·K. For water, heat of vaporization is 2.26 MJ/kg and specific heat capacity is 4.184 kJ/kg·K. As such, the approximate heat input for vaporization for an aqueous mixture at 15% H2O2 by weight with initial temperature of 300 K and boiling temperature of approximately 400 K (molality ∼ 11%, ebullioscopic constant of ∼0.5) is Δh=0.15×(1.26+100×2.26×103)+0.85×(2.26+100×4.184×103)=0.390⁡MJ

The decomposition of peroxide releases approximately 2.88 MJ/kg of heat, resulting in a net heat balance for 0.15 kg of decomposed peroxide as Δhnet=390−0.15×2880=−43⁡kJ which is an increase of enthalpy in the flow. Comparing the above to the energy expenditure of an air separation unit and the heat of vaporization of the equivalent water, where in both cases there is no return of energy into the flow, the energy benefit of using H2O2 is notable. The benefit becomes more significant at higher concentrations due to the reduction of water content which is more energy intensive to heat and vaporize.

Perfectly Stirred Reactor Simulations

ANSYS Chemkin was used to simulate combustion in a perfectly stirred reactor (PSR) of hydrogen in solutions of H2O2 and water. In all simulations, consistent with literature, − the solutions of H2O2 and water are assumed to be fully vaporized for simulation purposes. Reactor-based simulations have been shown to effectively characterize low-oxygen elevated-temperature combustion in the past. Three oxidizer configurations were considered in this study: H2O2-steam, dH2O2-steam, and oxy-steam. H2O2-steam refers to where the solution is assumed to be preheated rapidly before injection and hence has not decomposed into oxygen and steam. dH2O2-steam refers to where the H2O2-steam mixture decomposes and chemical equilibrium is allowed to be reached before injection. Oxy-steam refers to where the oxidizer is an equivalent oxygen-steam system (i.e., fully decomposed H2O2-steam). For each condition, nonreacting cases were also run to estimate the amount of heat energy carried by the flow before decomposition. For all simulations, the chemical kinetic mechanism used was the NUIGMech1.1 mechanism, with the carbon submechanisms omitted. That mechanism has been validated for H2/O2 combustion including H2O2 reactions in high-temperature conditions during its development. − Validation of ignition delays of kerosene fuel pyrolysis products, and laminar flame speed from methane/air/H2O2 flames have been conducted as part of this work, with the results presented in Supporting Information SM.1 and SM.2, respectively. Closed Zero-Dimensional Batch Reactor Simulations

Constant pressure (1 atm) closed 0D batch reactor simulations were carried out to investigate hydrogen combustion using the same set of oxidizer mixtures discussed in Section . The purpose of these simulations was to assess the ignition delay time of the different mixtures, as well as the temporal evolution of certain important radicals under the different oxidizer configurations, as opposed to the PSR simulations, which aimed to assess the heat produced, equilibrium temperatures and radical species for a given residence time. Ignition delay time was defined by the peak rate of change of temperature occurring in less than 0.5 s, consistent with other studies in literature. ,,−

Opposed-Flow Laminar Flame Simulations

Chemkin was applied to simulate opposed flow laminar flames for the different hydrogen and oxidizer mixtures (refer to Section ). The global mean strain rate was varied between 10/s and 1500/s. Here, the strain rate is defined as the sum of the two opposed flow speeds divided by the domain length. The velocities are specified such that the momentum of the opposed streams is equal, leading to a stagnation zone around the central plane of the domain.

Results and Discussion

Closed 0D Batch Reactors

Closed 0D homogeneous batch reactor simulations were used to assess ignition delays and rates of production of key radical species. The decomposition pathway of H2O2 is results in significant production of the perhydroxyl (HO2) radical as an intermediate before equilibrium is reached. Additionally, HO2 is an important radical in the oxidation of hydrogen, making it an ideal marker for analyzing the combustion process between H2O2 and hydrogen. Figure shows profiles of HO2 mole fraction, heat release rate (HRR), and temperature with time for three different H2O2 mass fractions. In all cases, the preheat temperature of the initial oxidizer mixture is 673 K, and the fuel-oxidizer equivalence ratio is unity. Decomposition of H2O2 increases the initial temperature of the mixture significantly compared to the assumption of no decomposition, or considering the equivalent water-oxygen case, exceeding the autoignition temperature of hydrogen even with 5% H2O2 by mass. For all H2O2 fractions in the dH2O2-steam cases, HO2 increases in mole fraction as a precursor to combustion occurring, before being rapidly consumed during the combustion process. However, in the 15% H2O2 case (Figure (c)), upstream equilibrium decomposition of H2O2 leads to higher initial fractions of the HO2 radical, allowing for more rapid ignition characteristics but a lower peak fraction. The peak occurs primarily due to the associated HO2 production pathways from H2 oxidation. A similar trend in the HO2 fraction is observed for the H2O2-steam profiles, although it is less prominent than in the dH2O2-steam cases. A sharp increase in the HO2 mole fraction precedes the combustion in the 15% H2O2 case, signifying further decomposition of the H2O2 into available radicals for the combustion process to occur. At lower H2O2 fractions (≤10%) in Figure , however, the time for the H2O2-steam mixture to ignite exceeds 2–3 s, which is impractical when considering applications in a furnace. In contrast, equilibrium decomposition (dH2O2-steam) considered upstream of the furnace entry results in an oxidizer that is sufficiently hot to achieve autoignitive conditions with hydrogen, an outcome that could be achieved with an appropriate catalyst.

MILD Combustion Characteristics of Decomposed H2O2

Based on the canonical method for defining MILD combustion in PSRs, a temperature index was adopted in this work as a metric to compare different oxidation conditions. The domain of analysis is specifically the reactor, which represents the furnace in the proposed system, and hence, the analysis is limited to the PSR inlet and outlet streams. Consideration of the system as a whole, including any upstream decomposition for the dH2O2-steam cases, reduces the index to that of the H2O2-steam cases. Due to the heat adding role of H2O2, the canonical autoignition temperature formulation (ΔT/T ai ) cannot be readily applied. As such, the temperature index is instead defined by the inlet and outlet stream temperatures as TI=(Tout−Tin)/Tin where T out is the PSR outflowing gas temperature and T in is the PSR inflowing gas temperature. In the dH2O2-steam cases, the inflow temperature is the temperature of the mixture achieved after equilibrium decomposition of the H2O2/H2O solution, whereas in the H2O2-steam cases, the inflow temperature is simply the preheat temperature.

At the lower H2O2 fractions by weight (<5%) in Figure , no ignition is observed in any oxidizer configuration. Despite some decomposition occurring under these conditions, the heat release is not enough to result in thermal runaway and subsequent ignition. In addition, at H2O2 fractions of 5%, the presence of radical species is not significant enough to promote ignition with the hydrogen in lean conditions, as these conditions are lower than the standard hydrogen-oxygen-diluent flammability limits of 4–5% for hydrogen and oxygen each. In stoichiometric and rich conditions, however, ignition at 5% H2O2 occurs, despite little decomposition occurring. In these conditions, both H2O2 based oxidizer configurations result in ignition in equilibrium conditions, with no ignition occurring in the oxy-steam case. Interestingly, however, no ignition occurs within 0.5 s in the closed 0D batch reactor cases discussed previously for the H2O2-steam cases, as seen in Figure . In Figure , above 10% H2O2 for all conditions with H2O2 in the oxidizer, the equilibrium temperature increases by more than 300 K, suggestive of combustion occurring in these conditions. Both the H2O2- and dH2O2-steam cases show similar features for equilibrium temperature as H2O2 fraction is increased; however, variation in the heat release rate is significant. At those conditions, the amount of decomposition in the dH2O2-steam is significant, leading to greater input temperature but reduced overall heat release rate. The heat released for the dH2O2-steam cases is predominantly from the combustion with hydrogen, rather than through H2O2 decomposition. In the equivalent H2O2-steam case, there is no ignition until an equivalent mass fraction of 20% H2O2, which corresponds to 10% oxygen by mole.

The temperature index for each oxidizer condition is shown in Figure (g–i). Up to a H2O2 fraction of 15% inclusive, the oxy-steam configuration does not ignite, even with the highest preheat temperature tested, up to 673 K (400 o C). Comparatively, the H2O2- and dH2O2-steam cases show very similar final temperature profiles; however, they show significantly different temperature index behavior. As the normalizing temperature is higher in the dH2O2-steam cases in the higher H2O2 conditions, the temperature index is significantly reduced despite both cases igniting. The lack of additional heat from decomposition in the equivalent oxy-steam case results in a lower temperature rise at equilibrium, which also decreases the temperature index. Only the dH2O2-steam cases are consistently below a temperature index of unity, which is suggestive of MILD combustion.

Above values of 15% H2O2 in Figure , the dH2O2- and H2O2-steam configurations diverge both in heat release rate and in temperature index. At this point, the upstream decomposition of H2O2 in the dH2O2-steam case is significant enough to cause an initial heat rise of the oxidizer before it enters the reactor (typically >100 K), thus reducing the overall heat release rate within the reactor. Comparatively, in the equivalent oxy-steam configuration, there is no additional heat release due to decomposition, so once the oxygen fraction is high enough, typical combustion with hydrogen occurs. The result is a higher heat release rate than the dH2O2-steam cases but lower equilibrium temperature. The corresponding TI values of the oxy-steam conditions are lower than those of the H2O2-steam conditions, which is a similar effect to what would be observed with nonadiabatic conditions due to the reduced equilibrium temperatures (refer Figure ).

However, because the outflowing temperatures are the same, if the temperature index of the dH2O2-steam configuration is defined using the preheat temperature, then the regime map is identical with that of the H2O2-steam configuration. Increasing the H2O2 fraction significantly changes the HRR profile when comparing the H2O2-steam and dH2O2-steam configurations. At 10% H2O2, Figure indicates almost identical HRR and temperature profiles between the H2O2-steam and dH2O2-steam oxidizer configurations, which is in agreement with both the closed 0D batch reactor and the PSR results. Minor decomposition of H2O2 occurs at those conditions, leading to a very similar oxidizer at equilibrium as at initial conditions. However, increasing to 15% H2O2 by weight to 15% results in a double peak structure for the HRR profile in Figure . That structure corresponds to the non-negligible decomposition in the H2O2-steam configuration, which does not occur in the dH2O2-steam configuration. The first peak observed corresponds to the exothermic decomposition of H2O2, while the second peak corresponds to combustion with hydrogen. That prediction is supported by the temperature profiles, where the H2O2-steam temperature rises to meet the dH2O2-steam temperature profile close to the first peak mixture fraction location before reaching the final peak near the stoichiometric mixture fraction. Similar features are noted with a two-stage ignition in the closed batch reactor simulations presented in Figure , especially at a higher H2O2 content. Interestingly, the conditions that reflect MILD combustion for the H2O2-steam configuration in Figure do not display a double peak structure in any scenario. That suggests that in the case of the MILD criterion, only H2O2 fractions of below 15% will meet the criteria as decomposition will occur simultaneously with H2 ignition if no upstream decomposition is assumed.

An increase in the strain rate also influences the flame structure. At a strain rate of 100/s in Figure , the double peak structure collapses into a combined mode at 15% H2O2 due to the lower concentration of H2O2 as compared with the 20–30% cases. A further increase to the strain shows similar behavior for the higher peroxide cases, as the flammable region is reduced and H2O2 decomposition heat is higher than the combustion heat from hydrogen. The result is that the decomposition of H2O2 dominates the flame zone, as seen in Figure for the 25% and 30% H2O2 cases. At lower strain rates, the flammable region is broader, leading to a very lean decomposition of H2O2 at higher oxidizer fractions and subsequent heat release being primarily from hydrogen ignition. These features are explored in the following sensitivity analysis.

Conclusions

A series of chemical simulations were conducted to analyze the effects of using H2O2 in water as an alternative oxidizer and steam source for industrial furnaces. Closed homogeneous batch reactor simulations, perfectly stirred reactor simulations, and opposed-flow laminar flame simulations were performed across a range of conditions to determine combustion features and flame structure in furnace relevant conditions. This series of 0D and 1D reactors represents a first-order layout of a MILD H2O2-steam/hydrogen furnace system.

Across the different simulation configurations, HO2 was identified as the key precursor developed throughout the H2O2 decomposition process to ensure ignition occurs, consistent with the literature. Closed homogeneous batch reactor simulations indicated that decomposition provided the needed radicals to initiate combustion that are not present in an equivalent H2/O2/diluent system. Practically, the temperature of ignition is lowered significantly by the decomposition and increasing radical pool, suggesting that the introduction of H2O2 can effectively promote ignition characteristics under these burner conditions. However, the ignition delay calculated in the closed batch reactors suggests that a catalyst or external preheating is required to adequately decompose the H2O2 before use in a furnace. The result of that decomposition will be reduced relative increase in temperature, as well as reduced ignition delay in the furnace.

Perfectly stirred reactor simulations showed agreement with the homogeneous batch reactor simulations in regards to initiation of combustion in an equivalent H2/oxy-steam system. Ignition was only observed in the equivalent oxy-system with an effective 20% w/w% of H2O2, as opposed to 10% in the dH2O2-steam system, and resulted in a lower equilibrium temperature and higher temperature index. The perfectly stirred reactor conditions indicate that in order to achieve reasonable equilibrium temperatures and low temperature index, an equilibrium decomposed mixture of H2O2 and water is needed as the oxidizer.

The effects of reactor conditions for the perfectly stirred reactor simulations were investigated. Wall heat loss and external air intrusion simulations showed substantial effects on equilibrium temperature, however, only in the more extreme cases. For wall heat losses above 10% of the thermal input, equilibrium temperature reduces by 20%, whereas at an air intrusion fraction of 10%, there is an increase in equilibrium temperature by <10%. Increases to residence time result in reduced heat release rates, but conserved equilibrium temperature. Changes to reactor volume showed zero impact on the calculated rates, mole fractions, and equilibrium temperatures. However, increases in reactor pressure resulted in large increases in heat release rate due to the increased number density and effective concentration, also reducing the proportion of radicals at the PSR exit. Minimal effects on temperature were observed. Overall, the influence of the reactor conditions were negligible within the expected range of operation that this study has focused on.

The use of preheated H2O2-steam mixtures for an oxidizer results in unique flame structures in opposed-flow diffusion flames, with a double peak of heat release rate observed. The double peak structure is consistent with those observed in literature for conventional oxidizer conditions, however, is observed here in a low-oxygen MILD flame. One peak corresponds to the decomposition of H2O2, while the other is driven by combustion with hydrogen. The decomposition process was found to contribute toward the a shift in peak heat release rate toward the lean side of the flame. The heat release peak associated with H2O2 decomposition is accompanied by a significant production of HO2, as in the case of the closed batch reactor cases, providing the needed radical pool for ignition.
