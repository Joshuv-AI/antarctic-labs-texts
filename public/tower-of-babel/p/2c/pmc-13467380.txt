Resolution of Refractory Vertigo Following Endovascular Treatment of Right Subclavian Steal Syndrome
Authors: Muhammed Emre Gülesir, İsa Sincer, Halil Ibrahim Gökce, Umut Ata Ugras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Subclavian steal syndrome (SSS) is characterized by retrograde blood flow in the vertebral artery caused by proximal stenosis or occlusion of the subclavian artery. Although SSS most often involves the left subclavian artery because it originates directly from the aortic arch, right-sided SSS is anatomically rare. This study reviews the current literature on the diagnostic challenges and therapeutic management of right-sided SSS, emphasizing the safety and efficacy of percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and stenting. Furthermore, we present an illustrative case of a 68-year-old male with medically refractory vertigo and right arm claudication. Clinical examination revealed a 40 mmHg inter-arm systolic blood pressure gradient. Angiography confirmed severe proximal right subclavian artery stenosis with retrograde steal. The patient was successfully treated with a balloon-expandable stent, resulting in complete anatomical resolution and immediate cessation of vertebrobasilar symptoms. Our clinical case underscores the necessity of routine bilateral blood pressure monitoring in patients with refractory vertigo and suggests that endovascular stenting appears to be a safe and effective, low-morbidity treatment strategy in such cases.

Introduction

Subclavian steal syndrome (SSS) is a condition in which severe stenosis of a segment of the subclavian artery or brachiocephalic trunk, proximal to the origin of the vertebral artery, reverses ipsilateral vertebral artery flow (retrograde flow), thereby supplying the upper extremity [1,2]. Atherosclerosis is the most common etiology [1]. Anatomically, the direct origin of the left subclavian artery from the aortic arch makes this region more prone to plaque formation; however, because the right subclavian artery branches from the brachiocephalic trunk, right-sided SSS is relatively rare in the literature [1,2]. Today, percutaneous endovascular interventions have largely replaced traditional surgery for symptomatic patients, offering low morbidity and high success rates [1-3]. In this study, we review the management of this rare entity and present a case of symptomatic right SSS successfully treated with endovascular stenting. This case is uniquely instructive because it highlights the anatomical rarity of right-sided SSS and demonstrates the dramatic, immediate resolution of chronic, medically refractory vertigo with modern endovascular techniques.

Case presentation

A 68-year-old male with a history of essential hypertension, hyperlipidemia, hyperthyroidism, and benign prostatic hyperplasia presented to our outpatient clinic with exertional fatigue, exertion-related right arm pain (claudication), and dizziness. His medical history included coronary angiography approximately 10 years prior, with medical follow-up, and there was no history of diabetes mellitus. Notably, his dizziness (vertigo) had persisted for years and was resistant to medical therapy (betahistine). A recent emergency department visit documented a significant difference in blood pressure between his upper extremities (left arm: 140/80 mmHg, right arm: 100/80 mmHg).

During the current visit, his arterial blood pressure was 147/54 mmHg, and his pulse was 70 beats/min. Based on his medical records, his optimized medications included perindopril/indapamide/amlodipine (10/2.5/5 mg), atorvastatin 40 mg, acetylsalicylic acid 100 mg, clopidogrel 75 mg, pantoprazole 40 mg, methimazole 5 mg, tamsulosin 0.4 mg, and betahistine. Baseline laboratory tests were unremarkable (Table 1). Preoperative evaluation revealed a normal sinus rhythm on electrocardiography with a heart rate of 70 beats/min and no acute ischemic changes. Echocardiography demonstrated normal left ventricular systolic function with an ejection fraction of 60%. Detailed objective echocardiographic measurements were as follows: left atrium 3.4 cm, left ventricle 4.2 cm, interventricular septum 1.2 cm, posterior wall 1.2 cm, and ascending aorta 3.4 cm.

To evaluate his chronic vertebrobasilar symptoms, carotid color Doppler ultrasonography was performed, revealing patent bilateral carotid arteries but a continuous retrograde (reversed) flow pattern in the right vertebral artery. These findings were highly suggestive of a rare right subclavian steal syndrome. The patient was admitted for definitive diagnosis and percutaneous transluminal angioplasty.

Initial coronary angiography showed non-critical plaques in the coronary arteries. Subsequent right brachiocephalic artery angiography revealed severe stenosis of the proximal right subclavian artery just before the origin of the vertebral artery (Figure 1, panel A, Video 1). In some views, the vertebral artery did not fill, appearing stump-like. Retrograde filling from the right vertebral artery into the distal subclavian artery, the pathophysiological hallmark of the steal phenomenon, was strikingly captured and confirmed angiographically (Figure 1, panel B).

The sequence begins with baseline right brachiocephalic angiography showing severe proximal right subclavian stenosis and retrograde flow from the right vertebral artery (subclavian steal). This is followed by fluoroscopic predilatation using a 7x60 mm balloon. Subsequently, a 9x31 mm balloon-expandable stent is positioned and successfully deployed across the target lesion. The video concludes with final control angiography confirming complete anatomical resolution of the stenosis and the restoration of normal antegrade hemodynamics.

A decision was made to perform percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) and stenting in the same session. Under standard intraprocedural anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin, the right subclavian artery was selectively engaged using a 7F SIM1 catheter. After predilatation of the lesion with a 7x60 mm peripheral balloon, a 9x31 mm balloon-expandable stent was successfully implanted in the target segment without any technical difficulties (Figure 1, panel C). Final control angiography showed complete resolution of the stenosis, full restoration of antegrade flow to the right arm, and correction of retrograde "steal" flow (Figure 1, panel D). No periprocedural complications occurred, and the patient was discharged symptom-free on an optimized dual antiplatelet regimen of acetylsalicylic acid 100 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg daily.

Discussion

While subclavian artery stenoses are often asymptomatic, when symptomatic, the clinical presentation typically includes signs of upper-extremity hypoperfusion and vertebrobasilar insufficiency [1,2]. Our patient exhibited right arm claudication triggered by exertion due to ipsilateral ischemia, along with chronic vertigo resistant to medical treatment. The most common physical examination finding in subclavian artery stenosis is a systolic blood pressure difference of >15-20 mmHg between the arms [1,2]. In our patient, this difference was 40 mmHg, underscoring the crucial role of routine bilateral blood pressure measurement in diagnosis [4].

In the diagnostic approach, carotid color Doppler ultrasonography is a non-invasive, highly reliable first-line test [1,2]. The gold standard for clarifying the degree and localization of stenosis while offering simultaneous treatment is digital subtraction angiography (DSA) [1,2]. In the past, treatment of SSS relied on surgical bypass, but percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and stenting have become the first-line options today [1-3]. Furthermore, recent multicenter data specifically evaluating right subclavian artery occlusive lesions have confirmed that endovascular treatment with balloon-expandable stents is safe and yields excellent long-term patency [5].

Recent literature, including cases published in Cureus, increasingly highlights the efficacy of endovascular approaches, even in anatomically challenging or recurrent presentations of subclavian steal syndrome, emphasizing a significant reduction in periprocedural morbidity compared with open surgery [6,7]. Beyond anatomical and procedural considerations, holistic management of cardiovascular diseases such as SSS requires an understanding of the patient's broader risk profile. Recent comprehensive reviews highlight that socioeconomic and racial determinants, including systemic barriers and lower socioeconomic status, are crucial independent risk factors that can exacerbate traditional elements like hypertension and atherosclerosis, leading to poorer cardiovascular outcomes [8]. Integrating these socioeconomic insights into cardiovascular risk assessments is essential for developing tailored secondary prevention strategies following interventional procedures. In our case, a balloon-expandable stent was successfully implanted, resolving the anatomical stenosis, restoring antegrade flow, and eliminating the pathophysiological steal phenomenon, as definitively demonstrated in our angiographic series. Conclusions

Routine bilateral blood pressure measurements must be obtained in patients with vertebrobasilar symptoms that are resistant to medical treatment and in those with upper-extremity claudication. Right SSS should be considered in the differential diagnosis. In these cases, endovascular stenting of appropriate lesions appears to be a safe and effective therapeutic approach that successfully restores anatomical patency and immediately resolves debilitating symptoms.
