Targeted degradation of c-Myc through the midnolin–proteasome pathway
Authors: Jingyuan Zhao, Huanhuan Wu, Han Yu, Chenyu Li, Yuling Mao, Hong Yuan, Shuai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAlthough PROTACs have revolutionized drug discovery, they face limitations regarding E3 ligase reliance and resistance, while emerging lysosomal strategies are often unsuitable for intranuclear targets. Addressing the challenge of degrading nuclear proteins, we developed “midnolin-based targeting chimeras (MbTACs),” a ubiquitin-independent platform that hijacks the midnolin–proteasome pathway. We validated this strategy by designing a series of degraders, exemplified by molecules such as midnolin-based c-Myc degrader (MbMD) and midnolin-based AR degrader (MbAD), which effectively degrade the nuclear transcription factors c-Myc and androgen receptor (AR). As a validated midnolin-based degradation system, MbTACs provide a crucial dimension for targeting undruggable nuclear proteins, offering a distinct and complementary approach to current degradation technologies.

Targeted protein degradation (TPD) has emerged as a transformative chemical biology tool and therapeutic strategy. By leveraging the principle of chemically induced proximity to engage endogenous cellular degradation machinery for the specific elimination of disease-causing proteins, TPD holds immense potential for drug discovery and development (1–3). Among various TPD modalities, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) have demonstrated particularly significant progress by hijacking the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) for protein degradation (4). Despite breakthroughs achieved with PROTACs and related technologies against various targets (5), significant challenges remain for degrading nuclear proteins, particularly transcription factors (TFs) (6). In addition, PROTACs have the disadvantage of relying on specific E3 ligases, which may be underexpressed in certain cancer cells (7), and downregulation or mutation of E3 ligase complexes can lead to acquired resistance (8, 9). Beyond proteasome-mediated approaches, autophagy-based degradation strategies, including autophagy-targeting chimeras (AUTACs) (10, 11), autophagosome tethering compounds (ATTECs) (12, 13), and related targeting chimeras, have also been explored. However, these platforms predominantly operate through autophagic pathways and have been mainly applied to aggregated proteins or cytosolic targets, with more limited demonstration of direct nuclear protein degradation. Concurrently, while emerging lysosome-based technologies like lysosome-targeting chimeras (LYTACs) (14) have successfully expanded the degradation scope to membrane and extracellular proteins-addressing some UPS-independent degradation needs-they are not suitable for intracellular nuclear targets. Therefore, developing novel ubiquitin-independent TPD technologies capable of specifically targeting nuclear proteins represents a central and still unresolved challenge in the TPD field.

Recent studies have reported a novel ubiquitin-independent midnolin–proteasome pathway as a key mechanism for nuclear protein degradation (15). Midnolin binds the proteasome via an α-helical structure, and its “Catch” domain specifically engages β-strand-forming regions of substrate proteins, facilitating their capture and degradation. The core mechanism enabling substrate selectivity lies in the formation of a biochemically compatible β-sheet interaction between the hydrophobic core of the Catch domain and a hydrophobic β-strand on the substrate. Overexpression of immediate early genes (IEGs) is closely linked to tumor progression, and research confirms that midnolin can specifically degrade certain IEG-encoded proteins (e.g., EGR1, FosB, IRF4) via this ubiquitin-independent proteasomal pathway (15). The proto-oncoprotein c-Myc, a transcription factor overexpressed in most cancers and often deemed “undruggable” (16–18), features a helix–loop–helix structure (19) but lacks the β-sheet structure recognizable by midnolin (15); consequently, it does not appear to be a natural substrate for midnolin.

Here, to achieve the targeted degradation of specific nuclear proteins mediated by midnolin, we developed a protein degradation system, termed MbTACs, by hijacking the midnolin–proteasome pathway for targeted degradation of nuclear proteins. We designed and synthesized a multimodule peptide MbTAC molecule. The design of MbTACs incorporates two core elements: a ligand module that specifically binds the target protein, and a hydrophobic KLVFF peptide derived from amyloid-β (20). We expect the hydrophobic KLVFF module to be able to interact with the midnolin Catch domain via hydrophobic forces, and the targeting peptide module binds the substrate proteins, thereby chemically inducing proximity between midnolin and protein of interest (POI) to trigger the degradation of target proteins. Furthermore, we incorporated enzyme-responsive motifs and cell-penetrating peptides to enhance tumor selectivity and cell-penetrating ability of these degraders.

In this study, we constructed a series of peptide-based MbTACs, systematically evaluated their efficacy in degrading nuclear proteins, and elucidated their mechanism of action. Our results demonstrate that MbTACs effectively induce the degradation of nuclear proteins such as c-Myc and the androgen receptor (AR), a process dependent on midnolin but not the ubiquitin system. We also demonstrated that MbTACs can degrade c-Myc to inhibit tumor cell proliferation in vitro and inhibit tumor growth in vivo. The MbTACs molecules developed through this work represent a validated midnolin-based TPD.

Results

Designing the Peptide MbTACs to Hijack Midnolin.

In this study, based on the structure and function of midnolin, we hypothesized that molecules consisting of a β-strand and targeting peptide modules could bind to the Catch domain of midnolin as well as the POI, respectively, thereby hijacking midnolin and degrading the target protein. The MbTAC molecule consists of several functional components; the midnolin-based AR degrader (MbAD) peptide (Fig. 1A) contains a targeting module that binds to AR (21) and a KLVFF module that binds to midnolin. The cell-penetrating peptide PKKKRKV (22) was added to the C-terminus of the peptide to enhance cellular uptake. The midnolin-based c-Myc degrader (MbMD) peptide (Fig. 2A) contains a targeting module that binds to c-Myc (23) and a KLVFF module that binds to midnolin, cell-penetrating peptide RKKRRQRRR (24), and a widely adopted legumain-responsive module AAN (25, 26) for specific responsive release in tumor cells. All peptides were synthesized using standard solid-phase synthesis methods and further purified by reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) and validated by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) (SI Appendix, Figs. Targeted Degradation of AR by MbAD.

We first investigated the degradation of proteins and the effects of MbAD on cells to analyze whether MbAD molecules can degrade AR and thus exert an antiproliferative effect. As shown in Fig. 1B, AR in prostate cancer C4-2 cells was successfully degraded by treatment with different concentrations of MbAD (100 μM, 50 μM, and 25 μM) for 24 h. Moreover, MbAD (100 μM) inhibited the proliferation of C4-2 cells in vitro (Fig. 1C), increased cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation (Fig. 1D) and caused cell death (Fig. 1E). However, the protein degradation and antiproliferative effects exhibited by MbAD were achieved only at a high concentration, which may be due to the weak membrane penetration ability of PKKKRKV or the steric hindrance affecting the binding of MbAD to midnolin.

MbMD Hijacks Midnolin to Degrade c-Myc.

PROTACs and other protein degradation chimeras commonly suffer from poor tissue selectivity and low passive membrane permeability (4, 27–30). To enhance the degradation potential and selectivity of MbTACs, we added legumain enzyme-responsive modules to the MbMD molecule and incorporated membrane-penetrating peptides with a stronger membrane-penetrating capacity (Fig. 2A). MbTACs can enter the cell more efficiently and are then specifically cleaved by the overexpressed legumain in the tumor (26) (Fig. 2B). In vitro studies of legumain activity confirmed the cleavage of MbMD by legumain using HPLC. Chromatographic analysis compared the increase in cleavage products after legumain treatment with those in the corresponding control groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). To verify that the midnolin-based MbTACs molecule has a universal degradation ability, we designed the degradation molecule MbMD with a c-Myc targeting module. We used HiBiT tags to label the N terminus of c-Myc in HCT116 cells. Upon the addition of LgBiT protein, they complement to form luciferase. The luminescence was used to quantify endogenous c-Myc levels and to investigate the effect of MbMD on c-Myc protein degradation. The effects of the c-Myc blockers CH1 and MbMD on the proteins were first analyzed (Fig. 2C). The concentration-dependent assessment using HiBiT protein detection revealed that CH1 and GFR (in which the binding sequence of c-Myc replaced with three glycines) showed negligible degradation of c-Myc in colorectal cancer HCT116 cells, whereas MbMD demonstrated potent c-Myc degradation; the DC50 value of MbMD was 5.483 μM (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, MbMD also exhibited potent degradation efficacy in PC-3 and SK-OV-3 cells, with DC50 values of 4.451 μM and 12.35 μM, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S11).

The MbMD mutant MbMD-MT1 (in which the c-Myc binding sequence was replaced by three glycines), and the MbMD mutant MbMD-MT2 (in which KLVFF was replaced by GGGG) also could not effectively degrade c-Myc (Fig. 2C). To further test whether the peptide can induce c-Myc degradation, we treated the cells with MbMD and MbMD mutants for 24 h and then measured total c-Myc levels by immunoblotting (Fig. 2D). The results of the immunoblotting (Fig. 2D) also support this finding; among these equimolar peptides (10 μM), MbMD showed stronger activity in degrading c-Myc. Moreover, none of CH1, GFR, or MbMD mutants caused c-Myc degradation (Fig. 2D).

To confirm the binding capability of MbMD with midnolin, we used AlphaFold3 for binding prediction. AlphaFold3 prediction produced models of interactions between midnolin and several peptides. The results of AlphaFold3 predicting midnolin binding with peptides indicate that the KLVFF region of MbMD forms a β-strand after binding with the midnolin Catch domain (Fig. 2E), completing a five-stranded antiparallel β-sheet tertiary structure, while MbMD-MT2 lacking KLVFF lacked the ability to bind to the midnolin Catch domain. GFR containing KLVFF and MbMD-MT1 containing KLVFF can also bind to the midnolin Catch domain to form a β-strand (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). To further confirm the binding capability of MbMD with midnolin, we measured the binding parameters of MbMD to midnolin using biolayer interferometry (BLI) technology. We determined the dissociation constant (KD) of the peptides for immobilized midnolin (Fig. 2F). We found that MbMD exhibited a significant binding affinity for recombinant midnolin protein, with a KD value of 390 nM, while the binding affinity of the MbMD-MT2 was significantly lower, with a KD value of 48.11 μM (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, we investigated the binding affinity of MbMD-MT4, a peptide containing only the c-Myc-recognition sequence and the midnolin-binding motif, which yielded a KD of 208 nM (Fig. 2F). These findings are consistent with the results obtained using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) technology (MbMD showed a KD value of 156 nM, while MbMD-MT2 showed a KD value of 4.94 μM) (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Collectively, the data indicate that the degradation of the target protein c-Myc by MbMD is closely associated with the interaction between its KLVFF domain and the Catch domain of midnolin, and that this interaction is not compromised by the presence of the enzyme-responsive module or the cell-penetrating peptide.

To confirm that the degradation of c-Myc by MbMD is dependent on midnolin, we conducted a series of studies to analyze the formation of the MbMD, c-Myc, and midnolin ternary complex, as well as the effect of knocking out midnolin on the degradation of c-Myc by MbMD. Since MbMD targets the degradation of c-Myc, we investigated the localization of this molecule in the cells. First, we studied the colocalization of MbMD with the nucleus in the cells and found that the red fluorescent molecule colocalized well with the blue nucleus (Fig. 3A). To further validate MbTACs’ nuclear protein degradation capability, we treated HCT116 cells with 10 μM MbMD and isolated nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins. Western blotting of cytoplasmic (Cyto) and nuclear (Nuc) fractions revealed that c-Myc was predominantly localized in the nucleus, with cytoplasmic components showing only weak signals. The 10 μM MbMD treatment effectively degraded nuclear c-Myc (Fig. 3B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MbMD enters the nucleus and achieves targeted c-Myc degradation therein. Subsequently, we sought to further validate that MbMD forms a ternary complex with c-Myc and midnolin to trigger the degradation process. We used a coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay to determine that MbMD promoted the interaction between midnolin and c-Myc. The co-IP assay first tested the effect of adding MbMD to cell lysate protein on the formation of the c-Myc and midnolin ternary complex. The presence of c-Myc was detected by Western blot after the antimidnolin antibody was added to the cell lysate and immunoprecipitated. The results showed that MbMD induced coprecipitation of c-Myc with midnolin in the MbMD-treated group (Fig. 3C), indicating that MbMD, midnolin, and c-Myc interacted with each other (Fig. 3C), and that a ternary complex was formed between MbMD, c-Myc, and midnolin. We then added MbMD to the cells, cultured them for 24 h, and performed co-IP to detect the formation of the MbMD-induced live cell protein ternary complex (Fig. 3D). The results also confirmed the conclusion above, and that CH1 could not exert this effect, proving that MbMD binding to c-Myc and midnolin promotes the formation of a ternary complex. To further examine MbMD induced proximity in intact cells, we employed the NanoLuc bioluminescent resonance energy transfer (NanoBRET) system comprised of transiently coexpressed c-Myc-NanoLuc (donor) and HaloTag-midnolin labeled with a HaloTag NanoBRET 618 ligand (acceptor). In this system, MbMD caused an increase in BRET signal in cells (Fig. 3E), indicating that MbMD promotes ternary complex formation with midnolin and c-Myc, which supports the co-IP data.

To verify that the degradation of the target protein by MbMD is mediated by midnolin, experiments were performed to confirm the dependence of MbMD-induced protein degradation on the midnolin–proteasome pathway. The results showed that the degradation of c-Myc by MbMD weakened after treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG-132 (Fig. 3F). In contrast, treatment with the ubiquitin-activating enzyme E1 inhibitor TAK-243 could not block the degradation of c-Myc by MbMD (Fig. 3F). The proteasome inhibitor MG-132 inhibited MbMD-mediated degradation, suggesting that MbMD induces the degradation of target proteins through the proteasome mechanism but does not depend on the ubiquitination pathway. In order to study the necessity of midnolin in the degradation of c-Myc by MbMD, we used CRISPR-Cas9 to knock out the midnolin gene MIDN to investigate its effect on the degradation of c-Myc by MbMD (Fig. 3G). Immunoblotting results showed that MbMD could not effectively degrade c-Myc in midnolin knockout cells (Fig. 3G). In conclusion, the data suggest that MbMD induces proximity between c-Myc and midnolin, facilitating c-Myc degradation by the midnolin–proteasome pathway.

To comprehensively evaluate the specificity of MbMD-mediated c-Myc degradation, we performed quantitative proteomic analysis. Using a label-free quantification approach, we analyzed HCT116 cells treated with 10 μM MbMD or GFR for 24 h. The results suggest that MbMD induces efficient c-Myc degradation with limited off-target activity in HCT116 cells (Fig. 3H). More importantly, compared with the vehicle control, the control peptide GFR caused only minimal perturbation to the global proteome (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). This indicates that merely occupying the Catch domain of midnolin does not elicit significant proteomic instability. These results suggest that binding to the Catch domain of midnolin is unlikely to markedly impair the function of the midnolin pathway, and that this binding strategy is therefore unlikely to induce severe mechanism-based toxicity.

MbMD Demonstrates Antiproliferative Effects In vitro.

The transcription factor c-Myc is widely involved in key processes such as the proliferation and apoptosis of a variety of normal and tumor cells and is a key protein in the regulation of cell fate (31). Given that c-Myc is often abnormally overexpressed in a variety of tumors, to analyze whether MbMD induces c-Myc degradation and exerts antiproliferative effects, we performed a series of activity analyses. The HCT116 cells were treated with 10 μM MbMD for different periods (12 h, 24 h, 48 h). it was observed that MbMD-induced degradation of c-Myc was also dependent on the duration of treatment (Fig. 4A). These findings demonstrate that MbMD efficiently induces time-dependent degradation of c-Myc in HCT116 cells.

Further research on the antiproliferative effect of MbMD at the cellular level was conducted by detecting cell viability. The results indicated that MbMD significantly inhibited the cell viability of HCT116 cells, compared to GFR and the c-Myc binding blocker CH1 (Fig. 4B). We also tested MbMD-MT1 and MbMD-MT2, which do not degrade c-Myc; the results showed that they did not affect the viability of HCT116 cells either (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Furthermore, the CCK-8 analysis of cell viability in after midnolin-knockout cells showed that midnolin depletion also eliminated the inhibitory effect of MbMD on cell viability (SI Appendix, Fig. S16). This result corresponds to the reduced ability of MbMD to degrade c-Myc in knockout cells. This suggests that MbMD may exert a stronger antiproliferative effect compared to the individual binding peptide, by inducing the degradation of c-Myc to inhibit the activity of tumor cells. To further evaluate the broad applicability of MbTACs, we assessed the efficacy of MbMD across SNU119, 769-P, A2780, MDA-MB-231, PC-3, and SK-OV-3 cell lines, and the results demonstrated that MbMD exhibited strong antiproliferative activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). We profiled the basal protein levels of c-Myc and midnolin across a panel of cancer cell lines, Western blot analysis revealed varying expression levels of these proteins among the different cell lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). This variation suggests that the sensitivity of different cell lines to MbMD may be intrinsically linked to the cellular abundance of midnolin and c-Myc. As the target protein degradation mediated by MbTACs relies on the formation of a ternary complex, and the expression of c-Myc and midnolin may affect the responsiveness to MbTACs. Crucially, we treated these cell lines with the control peptide GFR to rule out off-target toxicity arising from midnolin occupancy. The results showed that GFR exhibited no antiproliferative activity (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S19). These data indicate that binding to midnolin is not sufficient to impair cell viability, and that the observed antiproliferative effects of MbTACs require productive recruitment of the target protein.

The antiproliferative effects of MbMD were further verified using colony formation assays (Fig. 4C) and EdU staining assays (Fig. 4D). The results showed that CH1 only slightly inhibited the proliferation of HCT116 cells, while MbMD significantly inhibited cell proliferation. In addition, live-dead cell staining (Fig. 4E) indicated that MbMD was able to cause tumor cell death, and ROS analysis showed that MbMD significantly promoted cellular ROS production (Fig. 4F). In conclusion, these results demonstrate that MbMD exerts a potent antiproliferative effect in vitro by degrading c-Myc and blocking the biological function of c-Myc.

MbMD Inhibits Tumor Growth In vivo.

Based on the antiproliferative efficacy of MbMD observed in vitro, we proceeded to evaluate its in vivo activity using a xenograft mouse model. Briefly, immunodeficient mice were implanted subcutaneously with HCT116 cells. When the tumor volume reached approximately 60 mm3, the mice were randomly allocated into three treatment groups. Saline, CH1, and MbMD were injected into the tail vein of tumor-bearing mice every 2 d (Fig. 5A). Tumor volume and body weight were measured. MbMD significantly inhibited tumor growth, whereas CH1 only weakly inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 5A). Analysis of dissected tumors confirmed that the size of tumors treated with MbMD was significantly smaller than tumors treated with saline and CH1 (Fig. 5B). Analysis of the dissected tumors confirmed that the average weight of tumors treated with MbMD was significantly lower than that of tumors treated with saline and CH1 (Fig. 5C). Next, we verified the degradation of c-Myc in tumors by Western blotting; c-Myc expression in tumors was reduced by MbMD (Fig. 5D). Immunofluorescence staining of tumor tissue sections further confirmed these observations, and confocal images showed that c-Myc was highly expressed in the tumors, whereas treatment with MbMD reduced c-Myc (Fig. 5E). H&E staining confirmed that MbMD induced significant necrosis of the tumors (Fig. 5E). In addition, the TUNEL assay showed that MbMD had a greater apoptotic effect on tumor tissues (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis of Ki-67 expression levels in tumor tissues showed that MbMD treatment led to a reduction of Ki-67 in tumor tissues (Fig. 5E). In conclusion, MbMD demonstrated significant antitumor activity in vivo.

During the study period, neither CH1 nor MbMD significantly changed the body weights of mice (Fig. 5F). Major organs of mice were collected for H&E staining to analyze the effects of MbMD on the tissues. H&E staining images of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys showed that MbMD did not cause significant damage (SI Appendix, Fig. S20). Immunogenicity studies have always been an important part of drug development because of the wide-ranging impact of the immune response to drugs, which can alter the efficacy of the drug or produce side effects (32, 33). MbMD did not significantly alter the levels of cytokines IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in peripheral blood (SI Appendix, Fig. S21), suggesting the absence of an acute systemic inflammatory response in this model. These findings suggest that MbMD can be used to degrade c-Myc in tumors and exert antitumor effects without significant side effects. Current results indicate that no significant tissue damage or systemic inflammatory response was observed in the MbMD-treated group in single-dose experiments, but further safety studies are needed to validate the safety profile through more comprehensive immunogenicity studies.

Validation of the Generality of MbTAC-Mediated Protein Degradation.

To further validate the broad applicability and modular design potential of the MbTACs platform, we selected mouse double minute two homolog (MDM2, a key oncoprotein in the p53 signaling pathway) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) as targets and constructed degrader molecules, MDM2D and GPX4D (Fig. 6 A and B and SI Appendix, Figs. S22 and S23). Experimental results demonstrated that MDM2D effectively induces robust degradation of the MDM2 protein in multiple cell lines (Fig. 6C). In p53 wild-type HCT116 and 769-P cells, MDM2 degradation was accompanied by an increase in p53 protein levels, whereas p53 abundance remained largely unchanged in p53-mutant cell lines (MDA-MB-231 and SK-OV-3). This differential response is consistent with the established role of MDM2 in regulating p53 protein turnover (34, 35). Furthermore, both cell viability assays (Fig. 6D) and colony formation experiments (SI Appendix, Fig. S24) confirmed that MDM2D effectively inhibits the proliferation of various cancer cells. Likewise, GPX4D exhibited comparable potency, effectively inducing target degradation and antiproliferative activity (Fig. 6 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S25). These results not only successfully extend the MbTACs strategy to diverse targets but also strongly demonstrate its broad applicability.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a midnolin-based MbTACs platform for the degradation of nuclear proteins. We found that MbMD promoted significant degradation of c-Myc and exhibited enhanced antitumor activities compared to CH1, which functions by binding alone without recruiting midnolin. We also verified that the control peptide GFR (MbMD analogue lacking the c-Myc targeting module) did not affect cell viability. The results suggest that MbMD degradation of c-Myc proteins is dependent on the c-Myc binding motif and the β-strand. Furthermore, we demonstrated that MbMD can form a ternary complex with c-Myc and midnolin, confirming that the degradation is mediated by the midnolin–proteasome pathway rather than the canonical ubiquitin system.

The MbTACs platform effectively induces the degradation of therapeutically relevant cellular proteins, with AR and c-Myc serving as representative examples, highlighting the potential of this strategy for targeting intracellular proteins. TFs represent one of the most intensively pursued yet challenging classes of targets for TPD. In contrast to existing strategies, the mechanism of MbTACs exhibits distinct characteristics and potential advantages. Most established strategies for degrading TFs are derived from UPS-based paradigms, particularly PROTACs and molecular glues (17, 36–41). While these approaches have demonstrated notable successes, their efficacy often depends on several factors, including the availability and nuclear localization of suitable E3 ligases (9, 42), the formation of stable ternary complexes, and efficient ubiquitin chain assembly (43). However, MbTACs mediate proteasomal degradation through a noncanonical pathway that does not rely on direct E3 ligase recruitment. This distinction may be particularly relevant for TFs, which frequently possess intrinsically disordered regions and transient protein–protein interactions that can complicate efficient ubiquitination and ternary complex formation (44). From a spatial perspective, many TFs dynamically shuttle between the nucleus and cytoplasm (45), and midnolin is not strictly restricted to a single subcellular compartment (46); this broadens the range of transcriptional regulators that can be effectively targeted. Autophagy-based targeting chimeras provide an alternative route for protein degradation. However, these approaches are generally biased toward cytoplasmic substrates, aggregated proteins, or organelles (10, 47), and their application to the selective and efficient degradation of soluble TFs remains limited. Collectively, these mechanistic differences position MbTACs as a complementary TPD strategy that expands the toolkit for targeting TFs and other regulatory proteins.

MbTACs technology offers a strategy for the targeted degradation of nuclear proteins. Its unique design concept is embodied in two core features: 1) it represents a degradation system constructed based on midnolin; and 2) its modular architecture facilitates efficient design and functional tuning, while also enabling straightforward synthesis, underscoring its considerable promise for translational applications. A cell-penetrating motif was incorporated into the MbMD peptide, to improve the low efficiency of escaping endocytic vesicles during the delivery process of cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) (48); specific legumain cleavage sites were introduced to promote the separation of peptide molecules from the vesicle membrane and increase the endosomal escape efficiency (48). Furthermore, by incorporating enzyme-responsive sequences, MbMD can act more effectively and specifically in tumor cells. This conclusion was further corroborated by the analysis of target protein degradation for the AAN-deficient mutant MbMD-MT3 and the dual-deficient mutant MbMD-MT4 (lacking both AAN and the cell-penetrating motif). Specifically, the DC50 value for MbMD-MT3 increased to 10.39 μM compared to MbMD (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). Moreover, MbMD-MT4 exhibited significantly reduced antiproliferative activity relative to MbMD (SI Appendix, Fig. S27), collectively underscoring the critical role and value of the enzyme-responsive module and CPPs.

The degradation efficiency of MbTACs is likely co-regulated by multiple factors, including binding affinity to target proteins, the interaction between the β-strand and Catch domains, and the membrane penetration efficiency. Theoretically, optimizing hydrophobic peptides combined with capture structures could improve the recruitment and selection capabilities of MbTACs for midnolin. Notably, however, we observed a significant discrepancy between in vitro binding and cellular activity. While BLI and SPR analyses demonstrated that MbMD exhibits nanomolar (nM) affinity for recombinant midnolin protein, the concentration required to induce effective degradation in cellular assays typically falls within the micromolar (μM) range. This orders-of-magnitude difference represents a common challenge in the development of peptide-based TPD (49–51). To investigate this, we treated HCT116 and MDA-MB–231 cells with MbMD in the presence of varying concentrations of the membrane-permeabilizing agent digitonin. The results (SI Appendix, Fig. S28) revealed a markedly enhanced cellular response to MbMD when the permeabilizer was present, indicating that membrane penetration efficiency is one of the limiting factors for the intracellular effectiveness of peptide MbTACs.

Furthermore, regarding the structural design of chimeric molecules, the linker is typically considered a critical determinant of the activity of bifunctional molecules. Our AlphaFold3 predictions indicated that the C-terminal fused KLVFF Green Fluoxrescent Protein (GFP) cannot directly bind to the midnolin Catch domain, but the introduction of a GSGSGSGS linker suggested improved structural complementarity between KLVFF and the Catch domain (SI Appendix, Fig. S29). This suggests that optimization of the MbTAC linker may enhance degradation efficiency for certain specific target proteins. However, in the specific design of MbMD, we adopted a “linker-free” compact architecture as the optimal configuration. To validate the applicability of this design within MbMD, we synthesized and evaluated an optimized molecule containing a linker, designated MbMD-MT5. Experimental results revealed that MbMD-MT5 did not exhibit superior activity over the original linker-free MbMD molecule in inducing c-Myc degradation or inhibiting tumor cell proliferation (SI Appendix, Fig. S30). Therefore, MbTAC design should be guided by the intrinsic spatial compatibility between the target protein and the midnolin Catch domain. For targets such as c-Myc, where direct engagement enables efficient ternary complex formation, a linker-free architecture can achieve robust degradation, whereas other targets may still benefit from rational linker engineering.

In summary, we verified that the mechanism of MbTACs-mediated degradation is through the midnolin–proteasome system and is independent of the canonical ubiquitin-dependent degradation. Further research on midnolin-driven degradation will improve the design of degradable chimeras and increase the degradation efficiency of target proteins. Further structure–function optimization combined with mechanism studies will determine the optimal structure of MbTACs. In addition, further studies on the properties of MbTACs, such as metabolic stability, tolerability, and pharmacokinetic profile, are needed for potential clinical translation. We expect that the favorable pharmacological and biological properties will enable the adoption of MbTACs in a variety of diseases.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis and Purification of Peptide MbTACs.

All peptides used in the experiments were synthesized by solid-phase synthesis (GenScript). The synthesized peptides were purified by HPLC. Finally, MS detection was performed. For use, the peptides were initially dissolved in DMSO, and diluted with sterile double-distilled water.

Enzymatic Reaction of Legumain and HPLC Analysis.

To validate the recognition and cleavage of AAN in the MbMD with active legumain, dilute recombinant human legumain (MedChemExpress, MCE, HY-P70897A) to 100 μg/mL with activation buffer and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h in a mixture of 50 mM NaAc and 0.1 M NaCl at pH 5.0. Legumain was then diluted to 5 μg/mL with analysis buffer, and MbMD was added (with a final concentration of 300 μM), followed by continued incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. After ultrafiltration, 100 μL of the sample was collected for HPLC analysis (the chromatographic column was an Inertsil ODS-SP 4.6 × 250 mm, and the detection wavelength was set to 220 nm). The mobile phase was composed of equal volumes of aqueous solution containing 0.065% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and acetonitrile solution containing 0.05% TFA, with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min.

AlphaFold Prediction of Peptide Binding to Midnolin.

The peptide sequences were paired individually with the MIDN sequence (UniProtKB: Q504T8) and input as a dual sequence FASTA file into AlphaFold3 for multimer prediction, using the default reference database. Molecular graphics were generated in ChimeraX.

BLI Assay.

BLI was performed using an Octet-R8 biosensor. The midnolin recombinant protein (Purity > 85%, determined by SDS-PAGE, CSB-YP013822HU, Cusabio, USA) was solubilized in sodium acetate buffer (NaAc, pH 4.0) and diluted to 20 μg/mL in 10 mM NaAc for immobilization. AR2G sensors were activated with a 1:1 mixture of 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) and N-Hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), followed by the immobilization of recombinant midnolin. Residual active sites on the sensor surface were subsequently blocked with ethanolamine hydrochloride. Peptides at varying concentrations, prepared in PBS+ buffer, were associated with the midnolin-immobilized sensors for 240 s, followed by a 300-s dissociation step at 25 °C. All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure precision and reproducibility. The data were analyzed using Octet Analysis Studio 12.2 software. Reference and blank subtraction were applied to all sensorgrams.

SPR Assay.

SPR experiments were performed using a Biacore 8 K instrument. The midnolin recombinant protein (Purity > 85%, determined by SDS-PAGE, CSB-YP013822HU, Cusabio, USA) was solubilized in sodium acetate (NaAc) solution and diluted to 40 μg/mL in 10 mM NaAc buffer (pH 4.0) for immobilization. It was covalently immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip (Cytiva, USA) after activating the chip surface with EDC/NHS (1:1) coupling chemistry and blocking residual reactive sites with ethanolamine-HCl. Peptides at five different concentrations in HBS-EP+ buffer were allowed to interact with the midnolin protein wells on the sensor chip for 60 s, followed by a 60-s dissociation period at 25 °C. Each experiment was repeated three times per sample to ensure precision and reproducibility of the SPR data. Data analysis was conducted using Biacore Insight Evaluation software, applying blank and reference subtraction.

Cell Culture.

HCT116, A2780, PC-3, SNU119, MDA-MB-231, 769-P, C4-2, and SK-OV-3 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 and DMEM medium, respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotics (streptomycin and penicillin). These cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Colocalization Assay.

HCT116 cells were seeded on glass coverslips at a density of 10,000 cells/well, treated with 10 μM Cy3-labeled MbMD and incubated for 8 h, followed by nuclear staining with Hoechst 33342 and monitoring colocalization using a Nikon fluorescence microscope.

Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Fractionation and Immunoblotting.

The nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction kit (Beyotime, P0027) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24 h treatment with 10 μM MbMD, HCT116 cells were collected and processed to isolate cytoplasmic and nuclear-enriched components. Finally, the proteins were boiled at 95 °C for 5 min in 1× loading buffer and then used for immunoblotting analysis.

HiBiT Protein Quantification.

An expression plasmid was constructed by fusing the HiBiT tag sequence VSGWRLFKKIS to the N terminus of c-Myc with a GSSG linker and cloning it into the pcDNA3.1(+) plasmid vector. The plasmid was transfected into cells to express HiBiT-tagged c-Myc proteins. Cells expressing HiBiT-tagged c-Myc proteins were plated at 5,000 cells per well in a 96-well format. The next day, peptides were added, and cells were treated for 24 h. HiBiT protein levels were quantified using the Nano-Glo HiBiT lytic detection system (Promega, N3050). Nano-Glo HiBiT lytic reagent, consisting of lytic buffer, lytic substrate, and LgBiT, was added and luminescence was measured with a plate reader. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism by fitting nonlinear regression curves to extract DC50 values.

NanoBRET Cellular Ternary Complex Formation Assay.

The NanoLuc sequence was fused to the C-terminus of the c-Myc sequence and cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector. Similarly, the HaloTag sequence was fused to the N terminus of the midnolin sequence and cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector, which was purchased from GenScript. HCT116 cells (2 × 105) were cotransfected with HaloTag-midnolin plasmids (pcDNA3.1) and c-Myc-NanoLuc (pcDNA3.1). Twenty four hour posttransfection, a final concentration of 100 nM HaloTag NanoBRET 618 Ligand (Promega, N1661) was added to cells. The cells were treated with 500 nM MG-132, and DMSO or 10 μM MbMD for 24 h. The BRET signals were measured 10 min after adding NanoBRET Nano-Glo Substrate. The NanoBRET ratio was quantified in milliBRET units (mBU), calculated as the ratio of the receptor signal (618 nm) to the donor signal (460 nm) multiplied by 1,000.

Midnolin Knockout.

The sequence of the CRISPR-Cas9 plasmid vector containing the target sgRNA is as follows (15): nontargeting sgControl, GTATTACTGATATTGGTGGG; human sgMIDN #1, GAAGCTGCAGGAGTTCGGCG; human sgMIDN #2, GCTGACCTTGGTACCCACCG. The predesigned CRISPR-Cas9 guide RNA was cloned into the eSpCas9-2A-Puro vector purchased from GenScript. The plasmids were transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, L3000015), followed by selection with 2 μg/mL puromycin. Cells were collected for immunoblotting to verify MIDN knockout.

Quantitative Proteomics Analysis.

To perform proteomic analysis on the degradation of cellular proteins by MbMD, HCT116 cells were treated with 10 μM MbMD for 24 h, and then the cells were collected and processed for proteomic analysis. For cell lysis and protein digestion, the cells were lysed by vigorous shaking them in 0.5 mL lysis buffer (8 M urea, 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate; supplemented with Benzonase 24 U/100 mL), and the extracted proteins were centrifuged at 14,000× g for 10 min to remove cell debris. Mass spectrometry and bioinformatics analyses were performed (PTM Biolabs Inc, China). For bioinformatics analysis, differentially expressed proteins were identified based on the fold change (FC) values and P values of the identified proteins, with cutoff criteria of |log2FC| > 1 and P < 0.01.

Cell Viability Assay.

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were seeded in 96‐well microplates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well in 100 μL of medium. After seeding, different concentrations of peptide were added to the cells. Following a 24 h treatment period, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (MCE, HY-K0301) was added to each well, and the plates were further incubated for 3 h. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. The absorbance of the samples was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Cell-ATP Viability Assay.

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. Following adherence, cells were treated with indicated concentrations of compounds for 24 h. Cell viability was then assessed using the Cell-ATP Viability Detection Kit (MCE, HY-K0302) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 μL of the detection reagent was added to each well. The plates were shaken on an orbital shaker for 2 min at room temperature to induce cell lysis, followed by a 10-min incubation to stabilize the luminescent signal. Luminescence was measured using a multimode microplate reader. Relative cell viability was calculated by normalizing the luminescence intensity to that of the solvent control group (set as 100%).

Colony Formation Assay.

HCT116 cell lines were seeded and cultured in a 6-well plate at a density of 1 × 103 cells/well. Subsequently, the cells were treated with a fresh medium containing peptide at the concentration of 20 μM. Every 3 to 4 d, fresh medium with the compound was replenished. The culture was continued until the cells reached confluency. Colonies were stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min at room temperature. The number of colonies was calculated.

Living and Dead Staining.

To evaluate the survival status of the cells, living and dead cells were labeled using calcein-AM and PI (Beyotime, C2015M). After the peptide treatment, the cells were washed three times with 1× PBS. Subsequently, staining solution was added to the cell culture dish, followed by an incubation period of 30 min at 37 °C. Finally, a fluorescence microscope (Leica) was utilized to visualize the cell fluorescence, and pictures were captured for analysis.

EdU Assay.

EdU staining was used to analyze the proliferation of cancer cells according to the following protocol. HCT116 cells were treated with the indicated concentration of MbMD for 24 h and incubated with EdU (Beyotime, C0078) for 2 h. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. EdU‐positive cells were analyzed in different treatment groups under a fluorescence microscope.

ROS Staining.

DCFH-DA (MCE, HY-D0940) was diluted in serum-free medium at a ratio of 1:1,000. After the peptide treatment, the cell culture medium was discarded, and the diluted DCFH-DA was added to each well. The cells were then incubated for 20 min at 37 °C; subsequently, the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium to thoroughly remove any DCFH-DA that did not enter the cells. Finally, the fluorescence microscope was used to directly observe the presence of ROS.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) Analysis.

To determine if MbMD promotes the formation of the ternary complex, immunoprecipitation analysis was performed by adding MbMD to the cell lysate. HCT116 cells were washed twice with PBS, and lysed in prepared RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) containing protease inhibitor cocktail for 30 min. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was collected to obtain the protein sample and divided into two groups: negative control and MbMD treated group. MbMD was added and the samples were incubated at room temperature for 2 h, then midnolin antibody (Proteintech, 18939-1-AP) was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The Protein A/G Magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0202) were added and then incubated at room temperature for 2 h, then the beads were washed three times with RIPA buffer. Finally, the proteins were eluted by boiling at 95 °C for 5 min in 1× loading buffer and then used for Western blotting.

Cells were cultured with the addition of MbMD, and immunoprecipitation analysis was performed to investigate if MbMD facilitates the formation of the ternary complex. HCT116 cells were treated with 500 nM MG-132 and DMSO or 10 μM MbMD for 24 h. The protein samples were extracted from the HCT116 cell lysate, and midnolin antibody (Proteintech, 18939-1-AP) was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Protein A/G Magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0202) were added and incubated at room temperature for 2 h, followed by three washes with RIPA buffer. Finally, proteins were eluted using 1× loading buffer by boiling at 95 °C for 5 min for Western blotting.

Western Blotting assay.

RIPA buffer containing PMSF was used to extract total protein from the treated cells and tissues. SDS-PAGE was performed to separate protein bands, which were then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. Following a 1 h blocking step with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), the PVDF membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with midnolin antibody (Proteintech, 18939-1-AP), c-Myc antibody (Cohesion, CQA8035), and GAPDH antibody (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig). After three washes, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, enhanced chemiluminescence was employed as directed to observe the immunoblot.

Tumorigenesis Assays in Nude Mice.

The experimental method for the nude mouse tumor xenograft study described below was conducted after obtaining ethical approval from the Central Hospital of Dalian University of Technology. Immunodeficient male nude mice (Hufukang Bioscience, Beijing, China) were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 106 HCT116 cells. MbMD (10 mg/kg), CH1 (8.2 mg/kg), and saline control were injected into the tail vein of tumor-bearing mice (n = 5 per group) every 2 d. The growth of the tumor was monitored daily with a caliper and calculated as follows: tumor volume (mm3) = (width2 × length)/2. On day 16, mice were euthanized by isoflurane inhalation following AVMA guidelines and IACUC-approved procedures. Following euthanasia, blood and tumor tissues were collected for further analysis.

Histology and Immunofluorescence.

To further confirm the in vivo antitumor activity and safety of MbMD, histological analysis of xenografts was performed, and tumor tissues were collected from euthanized mice, this procedure followed the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 Edition) and was approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Tumors and other tissues were cut into 3 μm sections for H&E staining. Tumor tissues were stained with c-Myc (Cohesion, CQA8035), Ki-67 (Cohesion, CPA9691), and TUNEL; nuclei were labeled with DAPI. Finally, the slices were visualized under a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).

Analysis of MbTACs Immunogenicity.

To assess the potential immunogenic risk of the peptide MbTACs, mouse blood was harvested, and the cytokines interleukin-2 (IL-2), IL-6, IL-10, IFN-γ, and TNF-α were detected by ELISA in peripheral blood.

Statistical Analysis.

The data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 in this paper. Data were presented as the mean ± SD and analyzed using an unpaired t test or one-way ANOVA. Statistically significant differences were defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, and ns represents no significant difference.
