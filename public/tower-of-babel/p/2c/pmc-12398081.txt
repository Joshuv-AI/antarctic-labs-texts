Integrative metabolomic and transcriptomic analysis provides insight into the mechanism of pigmentation in potato tuber eyes
Authors: Yan Yang, Wei Jiang, Renxing Wei, Lili Lu, Lei Zhang, Xiu Wang, Binquan Huang, Xianping Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe pigmentation of tuber eyes serves as a critical morphological marker for distinguishing potato varieties, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying localized pigmentation in tuber eyes remain largely unexplored. In this study, localized pigmentation around the tuber eyes of a potato variety named M4, which produces tubers with yellow and red eyes, was studied via targeted and untargeted metabolomics and transcriptome profiling.ResultsA comparison of the yellow and red tuber eyes revealed that the main differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were pelargonidin, cyanidin, and peonidin, leading to the red pigmentation surrounding the tuber eyes. Other metabolites, such as flavonoids (e.g., kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside), lonicerin, turanose, isomaltulose, alpha, alpha-trehalose, galactinol, and 2’-hydroxyacetophenone, also contribute to the accumulation of anthocyanins in red tuber eyes. Transcriptomic analysis revealed 22 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in M4, with 18 upregulated genes and 4 downregulated genes, including a novel gene, MSTRG.13,281, which is predicted to encode a long noncoding RNA (lncRNA). Among these genes, ten were associated with the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway, whereas seven were putative defence-related genes. Integrative analyses of metabolic and transcriptomic data revealed strong network-level associations between 21 DEGs and both 10 targeted and 7 untargeted DAMs.ConclusionsThese findings enhance our understanding of pigmentation patterning in potatoes and provide valuable genetic and metabolic insights for breeding programs aiming to manipulate tuber colour for improved varietal identification and nutritional quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07206-1.Keywords: Potato, Colour of tuber eyes, Anthocyanin accumulation, Metabolomics, Transcriptome

Background

Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) is the most important tuber crop and a staple food for 1.3 billion people worldwide [1]. It is highly popular because of its high contents of carbohydrates, dietary fibre, vitamins, minerals (such as potassium and zinc), and amino acids, all of which provide nutritional benefits to humans [2–5]. The potato ancestors originated in the Andes of southern Peru 8,000–10,000 years ago, and since then, more than 5000 cultivars of potato have been developed through multiple domestication events and according to the needs of consumers [6, 7]. The tuber, which is an edible and reproductive organ, also has distinct characteristics, including shape, skin colour, flesh colour, and eye colour.

Tuber eye colour is an important characteristic that can vary among potato cultivars and may influence marketability and consumer preference. The colour of tuber eyes, which are the small indentations on the surface of the potato where sprouts emerge, can range from white to yellow, red, or even deep purple. It can correspond to the primary skin colour of the tuber or be a secondary colour, often localized around the eyes, eyebrows, or surrounding areas, exhibiting a stippled or scattered pattern. Although tuber eye colour has little commercial value, it plays a crucial role in the identification of potato varieties [8]. Tuber eye colour is a trait that is often considered by breeders and growers when selecting cultivars for specific markets. For example, potatoes with lighter eye colours are often preferred for processing, whereas those with darker or more vibrant colours may be favoured for fresh markets.

In recent decades, the identification of chemicals associated with tuber pigmentation, including of tuber skin and flesh, has attracted increasing attention because coloured tubers are appealing and have potential health benefits. Anthocyanins and carotenoids impart colours, such as red, purple, and yellow colours, to tubers. The accumulation of anthocyanins contributes to the red and purple skin and flesh exhibited by some tubers. There are more than 600 naturally occurring anthocyanins, which are classified as flavonoids and are characterized by the presence of chromophores [9]. Anthocyanidins, which form the core structure of anthocyanins, are found in potato and include pelargonidin, peonidin, cyanidin, malvidin, petunidin, and delphinidin [10, 11]. Derivatives of delphinidin, petunidin, and malvidin are mainly responsible for purple pigmentation in potato, whereas derivatives of cyanidin, pelargonidin, and peonidin are the predominant contributors to red pigmentation in potato [12–15]. However, the main compounds contributing to differences in tuber eye pigmentation are largely unknown. Metabolite profiling has been adopted as a high-throughput method to investigate the specific tissue profiles of anthocyanins and other polyphenols involved in the pigmentation of potato and other plants, revealing variations in the composition and concentrations of these compounds [16–22].

With the recognition of the contribution of anthocyanins to tuber colour, several genetic factors influencing tuber pigmentation, especially in tuber skin, have been identified in recent years. Some well-known genes involved in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway of coloured potato varieties are the R gene, encoding dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (dfr) [23, 24]; the P gene, encoding flavonoid 3’,5’-hydroxylase (F3’ 5’H) [25]; and the D gene, encoding an R2R3 MYB transcription factor [26, 27]. Additionally, several transcription factors involved in the regulation of anthocyanin synthesis, such as StAN1, StbHLH1, StWD40 [28], StAN11 [29], StMYBA1, StMYB113, and StJAF13 [30], have been identified through gene cloning and functional verification. Other studies have used transcriptome profiling to investigate the role of specific factors, such as StMYB44, which represses anthocyanin accumulation under high temperatures [31]. The transcript levels of StMYBATV and StMYB3 were found to be significantly positively correlated with altitude and flavonoid accumulation [32].

The mechanism underlying pigmentation in the skin and flesh of potato tubers has been a notable focus of potato research. However, relatively little attention has been given to the occurrence of contrasting pigmentation exclusively in tuber eyes, as this feature does not directly influence the visual appeal or quality of potatoes. Nonetheless, tuber eye colour is an important characteristic for distinguishing among potato varieties. For example, farmers use the name ‘red eyes’ to refer to a variety with red tuber eyes, as this is a typical trait. There have been few studies on the partial accumulation of anthocyanins in tubers, and the mechanisms underlying anthocyanin accumulation remain unclear.

In this study, a potato variety named M4 that produces tubers with two different eye colours, namely, a uniform colour matching the skin (yellow) and a secondary colour (red), was discovered. We conducted a comprehensive analysis of M4 by targeted metabolomic, untargeted metabolomic, and transcriptomic analyses of samples of both types of tuber eyes. The main objectives of this study were (i) to compare the accumulation of different metabolites between yellow and red tuber eyes, (ii) to examine the expression of different genes associated with the two eye colours, and (iii) to identify the key regulatory genes involved in tuber eye pigmentation. The aim of this study was to gain insights into tuber eye pigmentation, particularly in relation to specific tissues.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and sampling

Plant materials

M4 tubers have yellow skin and flesh, are oval in shape, and have yellow or red eyes and eyebrows. Interestingly, tubers harvested from the same plant can present different pigmentation features, with red and yellow (Fig. 1A) or only yellow (Fig. 1B) eyes and eyebrows. To investigate the formation and heritability of tuber eye colour, statistical analyses of phenotypic traits, including the ratios of plants producing red-eye tubers and yellow-eye tubers, and only yellow-eye tubers, as well as the frequency of red-eye tubers and yellow-eye tubers, were conducted on the basis of data from two seasons of investigation.

For the first season, M4 tissue culture plants were transplanted into individual pots on 23 March 2023. Three replicates of 30 pots each were established and harvested on 5 August 2023. For the second season, seed tubers selected on the basis of stable red-eye or yellow-eye phenotypes were planted on 7 March 2025. Each eye colour group (red/yellow) consisted of 30 pots arranged in three completely randomized blocks (n = 90 pots per treatment). Harvest was performed on 28 June 2025. For each season, the proportions of plants producing red-eye tubers and yellow-eye tubers, and only yellow-eye tubers, along with the frequency of red-eye and yellow-eye tubers, were recorded and analysed. Both experiments were performed under solar greenhouse conditions at Songming Research Station, Yunnan Academy of Agricultural Sciences. All the plant materials were cultivated in 21 × 18 cm pots. Conventional agronomic practices, including scheduled irrigation, balanced fertilization, and integrated disease/pest management, were uniformly maintained throughout the growth cycle.

Sampling

M4 was cultivated in a greenhouse at the Songming Research Station, Yunnan Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Kunming, China), during the usual sowing season (March to August 2022). At the mature stage, fresh tubers with diameters of 4–5 cm and varying eye colours (red or yellow) from the same plant were harvested and cleaned with sterilized water. For each biological replicate, 10 plants bearing yellow-eye tubers and red-eye tubers were selected. Using sterile blades, eye and eyebrow tissues (approximately 1 mm in depth) were excised from both red-eye and yellow-eye tubers. The samples from red-eye tubers were pooled to create composite red-eye samples, whereas those from yellow-eye tubers were combined to form composite yellow-eye samples. The tuber eyes were collected in triplicate, quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until further use for metabolomic and transcriptomic profiling analyses.

Metabolite identification and quantification

Sample Preparation for targeted metabolomic profiling

Anthocyanins, which are major metabolites found in red and purple tubers, were detected using a high-throughput platform. The frozen samples were lyophilized over 48 h (Alpha 1–2 LD plus; Martin Christ GmbH, Osterode amHarz, Germany), crushed and mixed using a mixer mill (A 11B S025, IKA, Germany). Fifty milligrams of the lyophilized powder was weighed out and then subjected to extraction using 0.5 mL of a mixture of methanol, water, and hydrochloric acid (500:500:1, V/V/V). The extract was vortexed thoroughly for 5 min and then shaken for 5 min using an ultrasonic oscillator. After the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g and 4 °C for 3 min, the supernatant was collected. The residue was re-extracted by repeating the above steps under the same conditions. The supernatants from two separate extractions were combined and filtered through a membrane filter (Anpel, with a pore size of 0.22 μm). The filtered solution was examined by MetWare (http://www.metware.cn/) on the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC‒MS/MS platform.

The quantitative analysis of the anthocyanins was performed by multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) via triple quadrupole mass spectrometry. Standard solutions with different concentrations (0.01 ng/mL, 0.05 ng/mL, 0.1 ng/mL, 0.5 ng/mL, 1 ng/mL, 5 ng/mL, 10 ng/mL, 50 ng/mL, 100 ng/mL, 500 ng/mL, 1000 ng/mL, 2000 ng/mL, and 5000 ng/mL) were prepared to obtain the mass peak intensity data for the corresponding quantitative signals of each standard concentration. A standard curve of different substances was drawn with the standard concentration as the abscissa and peak area as the ordinate. The integral peak areas of all the detected samples were substituted into the standard curve linear equation for calculation, and finally, the absolute content of the substance in the actual sample was determined. The formula was as follows: the content of the metabolite in the sample (µg/g) = C*V/1,000,000/M.

Where C is the concentration value (ng/ml) obtained by substituting the integral peak area of the sample into the standard curve; V is the volume of solution used for resolution (µL); and M is the weight of the weighed sample (g).

Sample Preparation for untargeted metabolomic profiling

An untargeted metabolomic strategy was used to identify metabolites present in tuber eyes. Freeze-dried tuber eyes were crushed using a mixer mill (MM 400, Retsch, Haan, Germany) with zirconia beads for 1.5 min at 30 Hz. One hundred milligrams of the powder was weighed out and then subjected to overnight extraction at 4 °C in 1.0 ml of 70% methanol. The extract was subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min, after which the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter (SLGPR33RB, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). The filtered extract was then analysed via an LC‒ESI‒MS/MS system (Vanquish UHPLC and Q Exactive™ HF-X, Thermo Fisher, Germany).

The raw data files generated by UHPLC‒MS/MS were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.1 (CD3.1, Thermo Fisher) to perform peak alignment, peak picking, and quantitation for each metabolite. The main parameters were set as follows: retention time tolerance, 0.2 min; actual mass tolerance, 5 ppm; signal intensity tolerance, 30%; signal/noise ratio, 3; and minimum intensity, 100,000. Subsequently, the peak intensities were normalized to the total spectral intensity. The normalized data were used to predict the molecular formula on the basis of additive ions, molecular ion peaks and fragment ions. The peaks were subsequently matched with the mz Cloud (https://www.mzcloud.org/), mz Vault and Mass List databases to obtain the accurate qualitative and relative quantitative results. The multivariate OPLS-DA model was used to calculate the variable importance in projection (VIP) values. The screening criteria for differentially abundant metabolites were metabolites with products whose abundances differed by more than two-fold or less than 0.5-fold between the Y (yellow tuber eyes) and R (red tuber eyes) groups and VIP ≥ 1.

RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The enriched mRNA was fragmented into shorter fragments using a fragmentation buffer and then reverse transcribed to cDNA using the NEB Next Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB #7530, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) for Illumina. The cDNA library was sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Transcript profiles and annotation

Raw paired-end reads were quality-trimmed and filtered using fastp with default parameters [33]. Clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome of the diploid potato S. phurejia DM 1–3 516 R44 - v6.1 (https://spuddb.uga.edu/dm_v6_1_download.shtml) in orientation mode using HISAT2 [34]. Transcript assembly was performed with StringTie [35] following a reference-guided approach. The analysis of the differential expression of RNAs between the two groups (Y, referring to yellow tuber eyes, and R, referring to red tuber eyes) was conducted using DESeq2 software [36]. The transcript abundance of each gene was estimated by reads per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (RPKM). The criteria for identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) less than 0.05 and an absolute fold change of at least 2. Gene Ontology [37] (GO) terms were analysed, and significantly enriched GO terms were identified on the basis of an FDR threshold of 0.05. KEGG analysis [38] was performed to identify significantly enriched metabolic pathways or signal transduction pathways, using an FDR threshold of 0.05, in comparison with the entire genome background. Pathways that met these conditions were considered significantly enriched pathways among the DEGs.

qRT‒PCR analysis

To validate the expression levels of the DEGs in the RNA sequencing data, 16 DEGs were analysed via quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‒PCR). The primers for these genes were designed using Primer Premier5 (Table S1). qRT‒PCR was performed in a 96-well plate using a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Each reaction included 10 µL of SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Bio-Rad), 2 µL of 1:10 diluted cDNA, 1 µL of each primer, and 6 µL of double distilled (dd) H2O, resulting in a final volume of 20 µL. The PCRs were carried out as follows: initial activation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was performed with a temperature range of 65 °C to 95 °C, with an increase of 0.5 °C every 5 s. We performed qRT‒PCR analysis on three biological replicates, with each replicate having three technical replicates. StActin was used as a reference gene. Relative gene expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Integrative analyses of metabolites and genes

The Pearson correlation coefficient was applied to measure the strength of covariation between significant DEGs and differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) via the R package ‘Hmisc’ using the ‘rcorr’ function [39]. The correlation network analysis was conducted with Cytoscape 3.10.1 [40].

Novel gene prediction

First, to identify novel genes as significant DEGs, their sequences were used as queries for a BLAST search against the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). CPC version 2.0 [41] was subsequently used to predict novel genes.

Results

The formation of tuber eye colour in M4

To investigate whether the formation of red-eye and yellow-eye tubers in M4 occurs due to environmental influences or is a varietal-specific characteristic, tissue-cultured seedlings and tubers with different eye colours were planted. Tuber eye colour formation was observed, and the proportions of plants producing both eye colours tubers or only yellow eyes tubers, as well as the ratio of red-eye and yellow-eye tubers, were analysed. Regardless of whether the propagules were tissue-cultured seedlings or tubers with red or yellow eyes, approximately 80% of the plants produced tubers with both eye colours (Fig. 1C). Moreover, the proportion of red-eye tubers was significantly lower than that of yellow-eye tubers (Fig. 1D).

Analysis of anthocyanin profiles

To determine the mechanism underlying differences in tuber eye colour, targeted metabolomic profiling of anthocyanins was conducted to investigate the differences between yellow and red tuber eyes. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that samples the yellow tuber eye (Y) and red tuber eye (R) groups were clearly separated in the PC1 × PC2 score chart (Fig. 2A). A total of 28 anthocyanins and their precursors were identified from a database including 108 compounds related to anthocyanins (Fig. 2B, Table S2). A comparison of yellow and red tuber eyes revealed that ten anthocyanins were significantly upregulated in red tuber eyes, with variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 and |log2FC| >1. Four pelargonidins (pelargonidin-3-O-(6-O-p-coumaroyl)-glucoside (Anthocyanidin_62), pelargonidin-3-O-(6-O-malonyl-beta-D-glucoside) (Anthocyanidin_61), pelargonidin-3-O-glucoside (Anthocyanidin_68), and pelargonidin-3-O-rutinoside (Anthocyanidin_69)), two cyanidins (cyanidin-3-O-rutinoside (Anthocyanidin_12) and cyanidin-3-O-rutinoside-5-O-glucoside (Anthocyanidin_13)), two peonidins (peonidin-3-O-(6-O-p-coumaroyl)-glucoside (Anthocyanidin_80) and peonidin-3-O-rutinoside (Anthocyanidin_88)), malvidin-3-O-(6-O-p-coumaroyl)-glucoside (Anthocyanidin_47), and kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside (Anthocyanidin_38) were accumulated at higher levels in red tuber eyes (Fig. 2B and C).

Comparisons of metabolites between yellow and red tuber eyes via an untargeted metabolomic strategy

To further uncover potential differences in compounds between yellow and red potato tuber eyes, we analysed the samples using an untargeted metabolomic strategy. A total of 950 metabolites, which were clustered into 13 categories and 59 subcategories, were detected in the two differently coloured tuber eyes (Table S3). Among these categories, the one with the greatest number of metabolites was others, including various types of compounds (20.21%), followed by amino acids and their derivatives (19.26%), lipids (10.00%), nucleotides and their derivatives (8.84%), etc. Additionally, flavonoids, including anthocyanins, were abundant and accounted for 7.58% of the total (Fig. 3A). Seventeen DAMs were identified on the basis of VIP > 1 and |log2FC| > 1. Among these, eight compounds were upregulated and nine were downregulated in red tuber eyes compared with yellow tuber eyes (Fig. 3B). The volcano plots also revealed significant differences (p < 0.05) in the levels of seven metabolites, of which the levels of alpha, alpha-trehalose (Com_181), lonicerin (Com_241), kaempferol 3-O-robinobioside (Com_307), isomaltulose (Com_390), galactinol (Com_423), and turanose (Com_709) increased, and the level of 2’-hydroxyacetophenone (Com_221) decreased (Fig. 3B and C).

Transcriptomic analyses of the tuber eyes

Brief information on the sequencing results

RNA-seq-based transcriptomic profiling was carried out to compare gene expression patterns between yellow and red eye tissues in potato tubers systematically. The raw data from the two groups of samples were assembled and filtered to acquire a total of 41.37 Gb of sequencing reads, with GC percentage ranging from 43.05% to 43.61%. The percentage of bases with a quality score above the Q30 exceeded 93%. Furthermore, an average of 48,588,242 clean reads (accounting for 99.47% of the total reads) from the yellow tuber eye libraries and an average of 49,603,515 clean reads (accounting for 99.50% of the total reads) from the red tuber eye libraries were mapped to the S. tuberosum Group Phureja DM 1–3 v6.1 genome. These results suggested that the sequencing quality of each sample was high and met the requirements for library construction (Table S4).

DEG identification revealed anthocyanin pathway activation

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified through comparative analysis of transcriptome data between yellow and red potato tuber eyes. In total, 27,057 genes, accounting for 79.23% of the total, were identified as being expressed in at least one library. Among these, 1,234 genes were identified as novel genes, and 25,823 genes (78.45%) were mapped to the reference genome (Table S5). Genes with FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| >1 were considered significant DEGs on the basis of various analysis results. Comparative analysis revealed 22 significant DEGs (P < 0.05) in the yellow group versus the red group, with 18 genes (81.82%) upregulated and 4 genes (18.18%) downregulated in the red group. Among these DEGs, ten genes encoded enzymes or transcription factors that participate in various stages of anthocyanin biosynthesis. This set included both structural genes encoding biosynthetic enzymes and regulatory genes controlling the pathway. Chalcone isomerase and flavanone-3-hydroxylase, encoded by Soltu.DM.05G022280 and Soltu.DM.02G023850, respectively, play a role in the early stage of the flavonoid pathway. The dihydroflavonol reductase gene (StDFR; Soltu.DM.02G024900) and the flavonoid 3’ 5’-hydroxylase gene (StF3’5’H; Soltu.DM.11G020990) encode enzymes that catalyse the conversion of the precursor of anthocyanins to red anthocyanin pigments, including peonidin and pelargonidin, as well as purple anthocyanins, such as delphinidin. An anthocyanin synthase (StANS, Soltu.DM.08G026700), an anthocyanin O-methyltransferase (StFAOMT, Soltu.DM.09G025040), and a UDP-glycosyltransferase (StUGT, Soltu.DM.12G002520) are involved in converting colourless leucoanthocyanidins to colourful anthocyanins in the late stages of anthocyanin production. Two genes, a glutathione S-transferase (StGST, Soltu.DM.02G020850) and a multidrug and toxic compound extrusion gene (StMATE, Soltu.DM.03G018250), are involved in anthocyanin transport to the vacuole. A transcription factor named StbHLH1 (Soltu.DM.09G019660) was identified in our study. Furthermore, additional genes associated with flavonoid biosynthesis, such as a dihydrofolate reductase-like gene (Soltu.DM.06G017710), ATPase-like phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase [ATP]-like genes (Soltu.DM.12G006400 and Soltu.DM.12G006430), and a cadmium/zinc-transporting ATPase gene (HMA2, Soltu.DM.07G005000), presented increased expression. Additionally, a gene with unknown function, MSTRG.13,281, and three other genes encoding uncharacterized proteins were downregulated (Fig. 4A). These findings highlight the coordinated upregulation of key structural and regulatory genes in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway as a primary determinant of red pigmentation in potato tuber eyes, with significant differential expression observed between yellow and red eyes.

GO enrichment implicated metabolic and stress-response processes

The GO enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs identified were associated with 28 GO terms, which were divided into three categories: biological process, molecular function, and cellular component. Among these, 15 GO terms fell under the biological process category, including response to stimulus, metabolic process, single-organism process, and cellular process, with more than 10 genes implicated in each. The GO terms related to molecular function were further categorized into five groups, including catalytic activity, binding, and transporter activity. In the cellular component category, the enriched genes were associated with the membrane, organelle part, and cell part (Fig. 4B). These GO enrichment results indicate that the genes differentially expressed between yellow and red potato tuber eyes are predominantly involved in metabolic and stimulus-responsive biological processes, with significant roles in catalytic and binding activities, as well as cellular membrane and organelle functions.

KEGG analysis linked pigmentation to phenylpropanoid and central metabolism

To further elucidate the metabolic networks underlying the observed differences in pigmentation, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). This revealed four key pathways directly involved in the metabolisms of phenylpropanoids and their derivatives, including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941), flavone and flavonol biosynthesis (ko00944), and stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid, and gingerol biosynthesis (ko00945). Additionally, six metabolic pathways were interconnected, namely, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (ko00010), the citrate cycle (TCA cycle, ko00020), pyruvate metabolism (ko00620), carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms (ko00710), terpenoid backbone biosynthesis (ko00900), and carbon metabolism (ko01200). A glutathione S-transferase was involved in glutathione metabolism (ko00480) (Fig. 4C). Together, these KEGG enrichment results demonstrate that the differential pigmentation in potato tuber eyes is associated with key pathways involved in phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis, as well as interconnected metabolic processes, including glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and glutathione metabolism.

qRT‒PCR validation confirmed the RNA-Seq data and expression trends

To verify the accuracy of the differences in the expression levels of genes screened via RNA sequencing, 16 out of 22 significant DEGs were analysed via qRT‒PCR. The gene expression levels indicated that ten genes associated with the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway were significantly upregulated, as demonstrated by the RNA-sequencing analysis of the tubers (Fig. 5). Additionally, four genes involved in other biological processes, such as cellular component organization and response to stimulus (StHMG; Soltu.DM.02G022190 and StCYT5B; Soltu.DM.04G032930), were significantly upregulated in red eyes. Furthermore, two genes, Soltu.DM.07G019130, which negatively regulates peptidase activity, and Soltu.DM.09G030430, which is related to a glycine-rich protein, were significantly downregulated in red tuber eyes. The qRT‒PCR validation confirmed the RNA-seq results, demonstrating consistent upregulation of key anthocyanin biosynthesis genes and other functionally relevant DEGs in red tuber eyes, while also identifying downregulated genes potentially involved in the negative regulation of pigmentation-related processes.

Gene‒metabolite correlations revealed anthocyanin network hubs

Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated for significant DEGs and targeted and untargeted DAMs. Network diagrams were plotted for significant DEGs and DAMs with absolute Pearson’s correlation coefficient values > 0.8 and p values < 0.05. A total of 21 DEGs were found to be significantly associated with ten metabolites from the anthocyanin-targeted metabolome and seven compounds from the untargeted metabolome, resulting in 259 correlated pairs. Among these, 173 pairs exhibited correlations with anthocyanins, and 86 pairs exhibited correlations with compounds from the untargeted metabolome (Table S6). Nineteen DEGs were correlated with 10 to 15 out of the 17 DAMs, with the exceptions of Soltu.DM.04G032930 (significantly correlated with 2 compounds) and Soltu.DM.12G002520 (significantly correlated with 1 compound). Fourteen DAMs were correlated with 12 to 19 out of the 21 DEGs, except 2’-hydroxyacetophenone (significantly correlated with 8 DEGs), malvidin-3-O-(6-O-p-coumaroyl)-glucoside (significantly correlated with 5 DEGs), and galactinol (significantly correlated with 2 DEGs). On average, each DEG was associated with 12.33 metabolites, and each metabolite was associated with 15.24 DEGs. For example, MSTRG.13,281 was significantly negatively and positively correlated with 14 DAMs and 2’-hydroxyacetophenone (Com_221), respectively. Kaempferol 3-O-robinobioside, labelled Anthocyanidin_38 in the targeted metabolome and Com_307 in the untargeted metabolome, was significantly correlated with 19 DEGs in each dataset (Fig. 6). These results demonstrate strong network-level associations between differentially expressed genes and both targeted and untargeted metabolites, with particularly extensive correlation patterns observed between anthocyanin-related genes and pigments, highlighting the complex molecular interplay underlying tuber eye pigmentation.

MSTRG.13,281 is predicted to be a long noncoding RNA

Integrative analysis of the transcriptome and metabolome revealed that MSTRG.13,281, a metabolic hub, is a novel gene located on chromosome 6. The alignment results revealed high percent identity (99.65%) and significant alignment coverage between the query sequence and the predicted long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) sequence XR_001472395.1, with an E value of 0.0. This result suggested that MSTRG.13,281 may be a lncRNA. Amino acid translation for the MSTRG.13,281 sequence was subsequently attempted using different translation initiation sites; however, none of the reading frames successfully produced a protein sequence, indicating that MSTRG.13,281 was unlikely to encode a protein (Fig. 7A). Moreover, an analysis with the coding potential calculation algorithm CPC2 indicated that the MSTRG.13,281 transcript had a low protein-coding probability (0.0185527), supporting its classification as a noncoding RNA (Fig. 7B). These findings suggest that MSTRG.13,281 is a lncRNA.

Discussion

Heritable colour polymorphism in the tuber eyes of M4

The colour of potato tuber eyes is typically a stable trait; however, in the M4, tubers with two distinct eye colours are consistently produced, with red-eye tubers occurring at a lower frequency than yellow-eye tubers (Fig. 1). A similar phenomenon has been reported in sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas), another clonally propagated crop. In the field-grown mutant line Zheshu 81, individual plants produced both red- and yellow-skinned storage roots, and this mutant phenotype was stably inherited [42]. Unlike aboveground fruits such as citrus, whose colouration is influenced by light exposure [43], the environmental factors that trigger colour variation in tubers and storage roots remain poorly understood.

Targeted metabolomics revealed the core pigmentation chemistry

A total of 108 anthocyanin metabolites were identified, and the contents of 28 metabolites were above the detection threshold in all the tuber eye samples. However, only ten metabolites were significantly differentially accumulated in red and yellow tuber eyes. The remaining metabolites presented higher contents in one replicate than in the other two replicates within a group (Fig. 2B). Although the two types of tuber eyes presented different colourations, both presented red pigmentation at the lenticels on the tuber surface. Notably, these red spots were unevenly distributed—present in some bud eyes and eyebrows but absent in others (Fig. 1A, B). This patchy distribution likely contributed to the observed variations in anthocyanin content among biological replicates within the same group.

A comparison of yellow and red tuber eyes via anthocyanin-targeted metabolomic profiling revealed that the accumulation of anthocyanins, especially pelargonidin, cyanidin, and peonidin, resulted in red pigmentation surrounding the tuber eyes. This result was consistent with the predominant anthocyanins found in potato varieties with red skin and flesh, in which the most common anthocyanidins are acylated pelargonidin derivatives, such as pelargonidin-3-coumaroylrutinoside-5-glucoside, and other acylated and nonacylated pelargonidin-3-rutinoside derivatives, along with low levels of peonidin and cyanidin derivatives [10, 15, 22]. In addition, the compound kaempferol 3-O-robinobioside exhibited significant accumulation in red tuber eyes, as determined by both metabolite profiling strategies. These findings suggest that subpathways of flavonoid biosynthesis, anthocyanin biosynthesis and nonanthocyanin biosynthesis, such as kaempferol 3-O-robinobioside biosynthesis, are coordinated in tuber eyes. This finding was supported by the observation that kaempferol 3-O-robinobioside was present at higher levels in pink petals than in white petals and presented high correlation coefficients with anthocyanins in Nelumbo nucifera [44]. Furthermore, it has been suggested that total synthesis of an acylated pelargonidin can be achieved via the corresponding kaempferol glucoside [45].

Expanding the metabolic horizon through untargeted profiling

Untargeted metabolomics provides an opportunity to discover new targets within the metabolome by comprehensively screening all measurable compounds in a sample [46]. Given this advantage, we adopted untargeted metabolomics to search for novel target compounds in addition to known metabolites related to pigmentation in tubers, such as anthocyanins. In this study, seven significant DAMs were identified on the basis of VIP > 1, |log2FC| > 1 and p < 0.05, among which carbohydrates and their derivatives, such as turanose, isomaltulose, alpha, alpha-trehalose and galactinol, were the most abundant DAMs in red tuber eyes (Fig. 3). Carbohydrates, including sucrose, maltose, glucose and turanose, have been shown to induce the accumulation of anthocyanins [47, 48]. Among them, sucrose (Suc) is the most effective inducer, and turanose has the same effect as glucose on anthocyanin synthesis. MYB75/PAP1 is essential for Suc-mediated expression of the dihydroflavonol reductase (DFR) gene in Arabidopsis seedlings [48]. In addition, exogenous glucose increases anthocyanin accumulation through PsMYB2-mediated signalling pathways in Paeonia suffruticosa ‘Tai Yang’ cut flowers [49]. In this study, turanose, a sucrose isomer, exhibited correlation with 13 DEGs, including StCHI, StF3H, StF3’5’H, StUGT, StGST, and StMATE. This finding suggested that further study is needed to identify regulators of non-Suc-mediated anthocyanin accumulation.

In addition, Lonicerin (Com_241) and anthocyanins, which belong to a group of polyphenols, i.e., flavonoids, share a common early biosynthetic stage that is separate from that of basic flavanones such as naringenin and form different compounds that undergo different catalysed reactions [50]. These findings suggest that pigmentation not only involves anthocyanins but also results in the accumulation of nonanthocyanin flavonoids in tuber eyes. 2’-Hydroxyacetophenones can be used to synthesize flavones through the Baker–Venkataraman rearrangement [51], indicating that the reduction in the level of 2’-hydroxyacetophenones (Com_221) could be attributed to their transformation to flavones in red tuber eyes. These results expand our understanding of tuber pigmentation beyond anthocyanins, revealing coordinated shifts in carbohydrate metabolism and nonanthocyanin flavonoids while identifying 2’-hydroxyacetophenones as potential precursors for flavone biosynthesis, suggesting that future studies should explore these metabolic interactions to fully elucidate the pigmentation network.

Core anthocyanin biosynthetic genes involved in tissue-specific pigmentation

In this study, well-known genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis were found to be differentially expressed between yellow and red tuber eyes. Four of the DEGs have been previously cloned and shown to be involved in the accumulation of anthocyanins in tuber skin and flesh, namely, Soltu.DM.02G024900 (StDFR) [23], Soltu.DM.11G020990 (StF3’5’H) [25], Soltu.DM.02G020850 (StGST) [52], and Soltu.DM.09G019660 (StbHLH1) [28]. Six DEGs related to anthocyanin biosynthesis in various cultivars or to various environmental factors, such as altitude and UV irradiation, were screened via transcriptomic analysis, namely, Soltu.DM.05G022280 (StCHI) [21, 53], Soltu.DM.02G023850 (StF3H) [32, 54, 55], Soltu.DM.08G026700 (StANS) [53, 55], Soltu.DM.09G025040 (StFAOMT) [32, 53, 56], Soltu.DM.12G002520 (StUGT) [32, 53], and Soltu.DM.03G018250 (StMATE) [32, 53, 56]. In addition, other genes that indirectly influence the biosynthesis of anthocyanins were identified. For example, the DEG Soltu.DM.04G032930 was predicted to be a cytochrome b5-like gene, which has been proven to be involved in the metabolism of flavonoid 3,5-hydroxylase (F3’5’H) [57], enriched in coloured fruits such as blueberry [58], and involved in anthocyanin accumulation in the tissues of poplar [59]. The 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme encoded by StHMG (Soltu.DM.02G022190) is involved in the steroidal glycoalkaloid biosynthetic pathway [60], and glycoalkaloids are involved in the accumulation of anthocyanins [61]. Another study revealed that inhibiting the expression of HMG promoted the accumulation of anthocyanins in grape fruit [62]. However, further research is needed to elucidate the role of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzymes in the accumulation of anthocyanins in potato.

Metabolic–immune crosstalk: pleiotropic regulation of anthocyanin-mediated pigmentation

Anthocyanins confer resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses, and their biosynthesis is also induced by environmental factors [63, 64]. Seven of the DEGs were putative genes encoding defence-related proteins, namely, a pathogen-related protein (Soltu.DM.04G026000), the proteinase inhibitor PSI-1.2-like (Soltu.DM.07G019130) [65], patatin-3-Kuras 1 (Soltu.DM.08G001550) [66, 67], a stress-related heavy metal transporting protein (Soltu.DM.07G005000) [68], a glycine-rich protein (Soltu.DM.09G030430) [69], and a phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (Soltu.DM.12G006400 and Soltu.DM.12G006430). Several studies have shown that anthocyanins increase plant resistance to disease. For example, coloured potato cultivars are more resistant to soft rot than white/yellow-fleshed potato cultivars are [70], and anthocyanins play an important role in protecting citrus fruits from green mould, a major postharvest disease [71]. Furthermore, anthocyanins react with metals/metalloids to alleviate metal stress [68] and play a role in the response to abiotic stresses, including drought, salt stress, high light intensity, ultraviolet radiation, and low temperature [72]. The expression levels of Soltu.DM.08G001550 and Soltu.DM.07G005000 were positively correlated with the accumulation of anthocyanins; however, the expression levels of Soltu.DM.04G026000, Soltu.DM.07G019130, and Soltu.DM.09G030430 were negatively correlated with the amount of anthocyanins in red tuber eyes. In addition, the enzyme phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) functions in anthocyanin synthesis when organic acids are present as substrates, as demonstrated in the flesh of ripening soft fruits [73]. Notably, potato tubers contain organic acids (0.4 ~ 1%) [74] that potentially support this pathway. The high expression of PEPCK-encoding genes in our study suggests that anthocyanin accumulation in red tuber eyes may be partially driven by the catalytic activity of PEPCK, which links organic acid metabolism to pigmentation.

The StbHLH1 paradox: challenging transcription factor dogma

Despite the prominence of MYB-bHLH-WD40 complexes in anthocyanin regulation [75], StbHLH1 (Soltu.DM.09G019660) was the only transcription factor identified as a significant DEG. However, comprehensive analysis combining transcriptomic and metabolomic data revealed no significant correlation between StbHLH1 expression levels and metabolite profiles (Fig. 6). Although StbHLH1 is highly expressed in red potato and purple potato, including commercial cultivars [76] and landraces [53], and has been shown to be upregulated or downregulated in response to environmental factors affecting anthocyanin accumulation, such as UV-B radiation [56] or high temperatures that reduce anthocyanin accumulation [31], few studies have revealed a weak correlation between leaf and tuber skin pigmentation and StbHLH1 expression levels. For example, StbHLH1 transcription was detected in the leaves of eight potato genotypes with varying levels of anthocyanin pigmentation, with the highest expression observed in a cultivar lacking anthocyanin accumulation [77]. Furthermore, no significant difference in the expression of StbHLH1 was observed between StWRKY13-overexpressing transgenic lines and wild-type Desiree tubers, and compared with control lines, transgenic lines presented a deeper colour and higher anthocyanin content [78]. In the present study, the weak correlation between the expression of StbHLH1 and the contents of anthocyanins and other metabolites suggested that additional molecular processes, such as posttranscriptional regulation, may influence anthocyanin biosynthesis in tuber eyes. Furthermore, epigenetic regulation has been reported to be involved in anthocyanin production in potato cell culture [79].

LncRNA emergence: compensatory regulation unveiled

Intriguingly, our multiomics approach revealed an alternative regulatory candidate that may compensate for the observed disparity between canonical TF activity and metabolic outputs. In this study, a novel gene, MSTRG.13,281, was strongly downregulated (log2FC = −10.29), and its expression was significantly correlated with the levels of most of the DAMs (Fig. 6 and Table S6), which implies that MSTRG.13,281 may play a regulatory role in anthocyanin accumulation in potato tuber eyes. MSTRG.13,281 was predicted to encode a lncRNA (Fig. 7). Several studies have revealed the involvement of lncRNAs in the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway [80–82]. The lncRNA LINC15957 was shown to regulate anthocyanin accumulation in radish [83]. In sea buckthorn fruit, anthocyanin accumulation is increased or decreased due to the silencing of two lncRNAs, LNC1 and LNC2, which act as target mimics of miR156a and miR828a, leading to a reduction in SPL9 expression and the induction of MYB114 expression, respectively [84]. The lncRNA MdLNC610 contributes to the regulation of high-light-induced anthocyanin production in apple by positively regulating MdACO1 expression and facilitating ethylene biosynthesis [85]. However, functional tests are needed to verify whether MSTRG.13,281 impacts anthocyanin biosynthesis, and in silico analysis is needed to identify the targets of this lncRNA in the future.

Conclusions

Our multilevel profiling suggested potato tuber eyes as a unique model system for investigating spatially constrained anthocyanin regulation. The compartmentalized accumulation of anthocyanins and stress-related metabolites indicated an evolutionary adaptation where pigmentation plays dual roles in photoprotection and pathogen deterrence—a hypothesis supported by the coordinated induction of defence-related transcripts. The decoupling of StbHLH1 expression from metabolic outputs, in contrast with MSTRG.13,281’s strong metabolite correlations (r = −1.00~−0.90, r = 0.88), challenges the prevailing transcription factor-centric paradigm of anthocyanin regulation. These findings enhance our understanding of pigmentation patterning in potatoes and provide valuable genetic and metabolic insights for breeding programs aiming to manipulate tuber colour for improved varietal identification and nutritional quality.
