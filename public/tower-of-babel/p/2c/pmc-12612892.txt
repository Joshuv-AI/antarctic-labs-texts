Thermal Hazard and Kinetic Study of Sodium Percarbonate Based on Fraser–Suzuki Deconvolution
Authors: Yike Wang, Zhiyu Wang, Suijun Yang, Shuliang Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sodium percarbonate (SPC), as a highly efficient oxidizing agent, is widely used in the fields of cleaning and environmental protection. However, its thermal decomposition process is accompanied by significant exothermic heat release, posing risks of thermal runaway and fire hazards. In this study, the thermal decomposition data of SPC were obtained by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). The overlapping reaction peaks were deconvoluted using the Fraser–Suzuki (FS) function, revealing two distinct reaction steps that occur consecutively. The kinetic parameters for each step were then determined separately. Kinetic analysis revealed that the activation energy for the first step is in the range of 137.22 to 151.80 kJ/mol, with the pre-exponential factor ranging from 9.62 × 1014 s–1 to 8.66 × 1016 s–1. For the second step, the activation energy ranges from 90.21 to 106.41 kJ/mol, and the pre-exponential factor ranges from 1.38 × 109 s–1 to 3.86 × 1011 s–1. The first subreaction was modeled by the Avrami–Erofe’ev (A4) model, while the second subreaction was modeled by the truncated Šesták–Berggren (SB) model. Based on the obtained kinetic parameters, the decomposition behavior at heating rates of 2°C/min and 20°C/min was predicted, which validated the accuracy of the kinetic parameters. Furthermore, the temperatures at which the time to the maximum reaction rate are 8 h (TD 8) and 24 h (T D24), and the self-accelerating decomposition temperatures (SADT) were determined to be 86.6°C, 78.8°C, and 71°C, respectively. The kinetic parameters and thermal hazard evaluation results obtained in this study provide data support for determining the safe storage temperature and optimizing transportation schemes for SPC.

Introduction

Sodium percarbonate (SPC) is an adduct formed from sodium carbonate and hydrogen peroxide. Due to its highly efficient oxidizing ability, it has been widely used in fields such as cleaning, papermaking, and environmental pollution control. − However, the decomposition process of SPC is accompanied by significant irreversible exothermic behavior, constituting a potential risk of thermal runaway. For example, on September 28, 2013, an explosion occurred in a 20 m3 epoxy reaction kettle at a chemical plant, leading to a fire at the exhaust gas water-seal absorption tank. The uncontrolled thermal decomposition of SPC inside the reactor was a key factor in this incident. Therefore, a detailed evaluation of the decomposition characteristics and thermal stability of SPC is essential to ensure its safe use across various fields.

Currently, some preliminary investigations have been conducted regarding the thermal decomposition behavior and kinetic characteristics of SPC. Early studies mainly focused on the macroscopic characterization of SPC decomposition, yielding basic thermodynamic parameters such as decomposition enthalpy change and apparent activation energy. − However, most of these studies were based on the single-step reaction assumption and did not fully consider the possible multistep nature of the decomposition process. In recent years, research has shown that the surface product layer formed during the thermal decomposition of SPC can impede gas diffusion, resulting in a nonlinear change in the reaction rate that first decreases and then increases. , This phenomenon reveals that the decomposition process of SPC involves an initial stage controlled by diffusion, followed by a rapid reaction stage after the product layer breaks down. This indicates that SPC undergoes a complex, multistep thermal decomposition process. Therefore, using traditional single-step reaction models to describe the thermal decomposition of SPC has limitations.

For the kinetic analysis of complex reaction systems, two main approaches are nonlinear regression and deconvolution. Nonlinear regression assumes the process consists of multiple substeps, sets kinetic parameters for each, and iteratively optimizes the fit between theoretical and experimental curves to deduce subreaction parameters. , Although widely applicable, its accuracy declines with highly overlapping steps, thermal gradients, or significant noise; model imperfections may also distort kinetic parameters. In contrast, deconvolution first uses mathematical functions to separate superimposed signals into individual reaction peaks, followed by independent kinetic analysis of each peak. While function fitting involves nonlinear optimization, this “separate-then-analyze” strategy effectively reduces parameter coupling and interference common in simultaneous multistep fitting. Numerous studies have shown that the deconvolution method exhibits excellent applicability and accuracy in the kinetic analysis of multistep reaction systems, providing strong technical support for in-depth understanding of reaction mechanisms and safety evaluation in complex decomposition systems. −

This study employed the deconvolution method to resolve the complex thermal decomposition process of SPC into two subreactions in order to deeply analyze its thermal decomposition characteristics. In this method, the isoconversional method was used to calculate the activation energy of each subreaction. Subsequently, the master plot method was applied to each subreaction: the first subreaction was identified to follow the Avrami–Erofe’ev (A4) model, while the second subreaction was not adequately described by any classical model and was fitted using the truncated Šesták–Berggren (SB) model. The corresponding pre-exponential factors were then determined accordingly. The accuracy of the obtained kinetic parameters was verified by predicting the reaction curves under conditions outside the experimental range. Finally, the kinetic parameters were applied to industrial packaging scenarios to obtain thermal hazard parameters: the temperatures at which the time to the maximum reaction rate are 8 h (T D8) and 24 h (T D24), and the self-accelerating decomposition temperature (SADT). The results are intended to guide the safe storage and transportation of SPC.

Friedman Method

The Friedman method is a model-free differential isoconversional method that is widely used for determining the activation energy (E). Since this method does not rely on a predetermined reaction mechanism model, it offers strong applicability and flexibility. The basic principle involves taking the natural logarithm of both sides of the reaction rate equation to derive a linear relationship, as shown in eq : 5 ln(dαdt)=ln[Af(α)]−ERT

For different heating rates, the above equation can be rewritten as eq : 6 ln⁡(βidαdT)α,i=ln[Aαf(a)]−EαRTα,i

Here, the subscript i denotes the index of the nonisothermal experiment conducted at a heating rate of β i , and the subscript α indicates that the relevant parameters are calculated at a specific conversion α. Based on this equation, for a given extent of reaction under different heating rates, there exists a linear relationship between ln­(βdα/dt) and 1/T, with the slope equal to that of −E/R. Therefore, the activation energy E can be determined without prior knowledge of the reaction model f(α).

Deconvolution Method

When the reaction process involves multiple overlapping steps, directly applying isoconversional methods to determine the activation energy may lead to inaccuracies. To address this issue, in the case of multistep reactions, each reaction step can be separated and individually analyzed for its kinetic behavior, thereby avoiding interference from overlapping steps and improving the accuracy and reliability of kinetic parameters.

Through deconvolution, overlapping reaction curves can be separated, allowing for a kinetic analysis of each distinct reaction step. The Fraser–Suzuki (FS) function, as an important tool in deconvolution methods, has been widely used due to its excellent adaptability to various reaction types and high separation accuracy. Therefore, the FS function is selected in this study as the deconvolution method. The expression for the FS function is as eq : 7 y=a0exp{−ln⁡2[ln(1+2a3(x−a1a2))a3]2} where a 0, a 1, a 2, and a 3 represent the amplitude, peak position, half-width, and asymmetry parameter of the curve, respectively.

Calculation of Thermal Hazard Parameters

In this study, the AKTS kinetic analysis software was used to calculate the self-accelerating decomposition temperature (SADT) and the time to maximum rate (TMR) for SPC. The thermal balance equation for the reaction system is given in eq : , 12 UA(Tenv−Ts)+Ms(−ΔHr)dαdt=MsCpsdTsdt+McCpcdTcdt where U, A, T, M, and C p are the heat transfer coefficient, the surface area, the temperature, the mass, and the specific heat capacity, respectively. The subscripts c, s, and env represent the container, sample, and environment, respectively. Under cooling failure conditions, sample and container temperatures are assumed to be the same (T s = T c), and heat exchange between the system and the environment is negligible. The thermal balance equation can then be simplified as shown in eq : 13 dTsdt=MsCpsMsCps+McCpc(−ΔHr)Cpsdαdt

By analyzing the rate of temperature change and the corresponding variation in reaction rate, the adiabatic temperature rise profile can be accurately predicted, thus determining the TMR. Closely related thermal runaway parameters include T D8 and T D24, which denote the temperatures at which the time to maximum rate is 8 and 24 h, respectively.

In the calculation of SADT, the process of heat transfer to the surrounding environment is taken into account. The temperature distribution within the system is influenced not only by the rate of heat generation from the reaction but also by the heat storage capacity and thermal conductivity. Based on Fourier’s law of heat conduction, temperature changes can be described in eq : , 14 ∂T∂t=λρCp∇2T+1ρCpqr where λ, ρ, C p, T, and q r are the thermal conductivity, density, specific heat capacity, temperature, and heat generation rate per unit volume due to the decomposition reaction, respectively. Considering specific geometric constraints and neglecting axial temperature gradients, the gradient term in the heat conduction equation can be simplified. The final heat conduction equation is expressed as eq : 15 ∂T∂t=λρCp(∂2T∂r2+Jr∂T∂r)+ΔTaddαdt

Here, the geometric factor J takes values of 0 (infinite slab), 1 (infinite cylinder), or 2 (sphere), corresponding to different heat transfer geometries. ΔT ad denotes the adiabatic temperature rise.

On the basis of the above models, the environmental temperature can be adjusted stepwise to simulate the temperature response of the sample under specific packaging conditions, thereby determining the SADT of the sample.

Experiment

SPC (Na2CO3·1.5 H2O2, 99.0%) was purchased from Aladdin Reagents Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The heat flow variation with temperature was measured and recorded using a DSC-40A differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) from Hangzhou Yangyi Technology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). A 5 mg sample was placed in an aluminum crucible and subjected to thermal decomposition experiments under an inert atmosphere (N2, flow rate of 50 mL/min). Experiments were carried out under five distinct heating rates (3, 5, 7, 10, and 14 °C/min) to obtain data under different thermal conditions, which were subsequently utilized for the determination of kinetic parameters. Additionally, experiments were conducted at heating rates of 2 and 20 °C/min to verify the validity of the obtained kinetic parameters.

As the heating rate increases, the entire heat flow curve shifts to higher temperature regions. Specifically, the onset temperature, termination temperature, and peak temperatures of the exothermic peaks all increase with the heating rate. Additionally, the exothermic peaks become sharper, and the integral area increases. This is related to the influence of the heating rate on the temperature difference between the sample and the environment. An increase in the heating rate intensifies the thermal hysteresis phenomenon, causing the thermal flow curve to shift toward the higher temperature region.

Deconvolution Analysis

To separate the individual subreactions within the overall reaction process, the FS function was employed to deconvolute the reaction rate curves obtained at different heating rates. Specifically, each reaction rate curve was modeled as the sum of two FS functions, with each function representing a kinetically distinct reaction step. The parameters (a 0 to a 3) for each Fraser–Suzuki function were determined using nonlinear least-squares curve fitting with the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm to minimize residuals between the fitted curve and experimental data.

Additionally, the L value was used to characterize the contribution of each subreaction. It is defined as the percentage of the integral area of each subreaction’s rate curve relative to the total area of the overall reaction rate curve and theoretically should be independent of the heating rate. The optimized results are summarized in Table . The analysis indicates that the L values for the two subreactions exhibit minimal fluctuation across different heating rates, with their respective contributions ranging from 35.18% to 39.88% and 60.12% to 64.82%. This demonstrates the rationality of the deconvolution results.

This behavior aligns with a reported model in which a solid product layer from the initial step temporarily inhibits the progression of the subsequent reaction.

Validation of Kinetic Parameters

Utilizing the kinetic parameters obtained in the previous section, the reaction curves of SPC at heating rates of 2 °C/min and 20 °C/min were reconstructed and compared with the corresponding experimental curves. The results are presented in Figure . At a heating rate of 2 °C/min, the model-predicted temperatures are consistently lower than the experimental values, with deviations ranging from −1.61 °C to −0.01 °C. Similarly, at a heating rate of 20 °C/min, the predicted temperatures are largely lower than the experimental values across most of the conversion range (0.1 < α < 0.9), with deviations from −0.94 °C to +0.04 °C. This indicates that the model predicts the reaction to occur earlier than observed during the initiation and progression stages of the reaction. However, when the conversion exceeds 0.93, the predicted temperatures become slightly higher than the experimental values, reflecting a minor lag in the model’s prediction of the reaction completion stage. Overall, the model demonstrates good fitting performance at different heating rates and is able to accurately describe the relationship between conversion and temperature during the SPC reaction process.

Calculation of Thermal Hazard Parameters

In the field of chemical process safety, the assessment of thermal hazards in reaction systems is a critical step in preventing thermal runaway accidents. As core thermal hazard parameters, the self-accelerating decomposition temperature (SADT) and time to maximum rate (TMR) provide essential criteria for evaluating the safety of a reaction system during storage and transportation. In this study, the TMR and SADT of SPC were calculated by using the AKTS software.

The calculation process involved the thermodynamic properties of the material and the heat dissipation conditions of the packaging. Among these, the decomposition enthalpy (ΔH d) was 141.1 J/g, the specific heat capacity (C p) was 1.3 J/(g·K), and the heat inertia factor (Φ) was set to 1. Under the 25 kg packaging condition, the thermal conductivity was 0.5 W/(m·K) and the heat transfer coefficient was 5W/(m2·K). Based on the above parameter settings, several important thermal safety indicators were obtained through software simulation. The results showed that the adiabatic temperature increase (ΔT ad) reached 109 °C, indicating that the decomposition reaction releases a significant amount of heat. Under conditions of cooling failure, the T D8 and T D24 of the reaction system were 86.6 and 78.8 °C, respectively, as shown in Figure . If the system temperature remains below 78.8 °C, the reaction risk is relatively controllable. Once it exceeds 86.6 °C, the time to thermal runaway will be shortened to less than 8 h, and the risk will significantly increase. Therefore, it is necessary to be highly vigilant and promptly take safety measures such as cooling and isolation. Furthermore, for the 25 kg specification SPC packaging, the calculation results predicted its SADT to be 71 °C, as shown in Figure . During storage and transportation, the environmental temperature must be strictly controlled, and temperature monitoring and heat dissipation measures must be strengthened to prevent thermal runaway accidents.

Conclusion

In this study, the thermal decomposition characteristics and thermal hazards of SPC were systematically investigated by using DSC combined with kinetic analysis methods. The main conclusions are as follows:

By employing deconvolution methods, the thermal decomposition process of SPC was separated into two distinct subreactions. Kinetic analysis showed that the first stage is characterized by a higher activation energy, ranging from 137.22 to 151.80 kJ/mol, resulting in a relatively slow reaction rate. In contrast, the second stage has a lower activation energy, ranging from 90.21 to 106.41 kJ/mol, allowing the reaction to proceed more readily and with greater heat release. The first subreaction was modeled by the A4 model, while the second subreaction was modeled by the truncated SB model. The kinetic parameters obtained can be used to predict the reaction behavior of SPC under different temperature conditions.

The thermal safety of SPC was evaluated based on the kinetic parameters. The results indicate that the T D8 and T D24 values for SPC are 86.6 and 78.8 °C, respectively; ΔT ad is 109 °C; and the SADT under the 25 kg packaging condition is 71 °C. It is recommended that the storage temperature of SPC be maintained below 60 °C, and that temperature monitoring and heat dissipation design be strengthened to prevent the occurrence of thermal safety accidents and ensure its safety during industrial storage and transportation processes.
