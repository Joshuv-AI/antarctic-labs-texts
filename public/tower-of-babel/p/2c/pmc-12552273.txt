Prime-time for LncRNA signatures in acute myeloid leukemia?
Authors: Zhiyao Ren, Barbara De Moerloose, Tim Lammens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong non-coding RNA (lncRNA) signatures have emerged as important prognostic biomarkers in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), stratifying patients into high-risk and low-risk groups and thus providing valuable insights for personalized treatment strategies. The development of these signatures often involves comprehensive bioinformatics analyses, employing various statistical methods to ensure robustness and accuracy. Nevertheless, many reports are flawed by the presence of specific biases and/or incompleteness. Here we performed a comprehensive review of recently identified prognostic lncRNA signatures in AML, including our own report on a 69-lncRNA signature predicting relapse-free survival in pediatric acute myeloid leukemia. Next to their predictive impact we provide insights into their strengths and shortcomings.Keywords: LncRNA signature, Acute myeloid leukemia, Survival, Prognostic biomarker

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogenous disease characterized by the abnormal proliferation of myeloid blasts not undergoing the usual process of differentiation [1]. In adults, AML is the most common type of acute leukemia, with an average five-year overall survival (OS) of 30% [2]. While this survival statistic can be 60% in younger patients (15–40 years), it drops to 5% in those over the age of 70 [3]. In contrast to adult AML, pediatric AML (pedAML) is relatively uncommon, representing only about 15–20% of childhood leukemia diagnoses [4]. Importantly, the prognosis for pediatric AML is markedly better, with an OS rate of approximately 70%. Nevertheless, pedAML patients still face a relapse rate of up to approximately 30% [5, 6].

Risk stratification has shown to be a cornerstone in the treatment of AML, impacting treatment decisions [7, 8]. As such, the allocation to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is usually limited to fit patients predicted to have a high risk of relapse with chemotherapy consolidation [9]. Currently, biological risk stratification mainly relies on conventional cytogenetics and the presence of specific genetic aberrations [10, 11]. Nevertheless, given the high relapse rate even in standard risk/intermediate risk AML, it is clear that there is an urgent need to identify new stratifying biomarkers to treatment.

Historically, cancer research has primarily focused on protein-coding Genes; however, only less than 2% of the genome is encoding proteins. The genome beyond this small fraction was earlier considered as dark matter, but has been shown during the past decade to hold crucial regulators that play significant roles in both physiological and pathological processes. Among these, long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), RNA molecules longer than 200 nucleotides, lacking apparent protein coding potential, were identified [12, 13]. LncRNAs, similar to mRNAs, are transcribed by RNA polymerase II, producing nascent RNA transcripts that subsequently often undergo 5’ capping, splicing, and polyadenylation (Fig. 1). However, lncRNAs are characterized by the absence of effective open reading frames (ORFs), a reduced number of longer exons, and lower conservation levels [12]. The functions of lncRNAs are mainly determined by their cellular localization. Nuclear-localized lncRNAs are, among other functions, known to regulate chromatin architecture and remodeling, transcriptional regulation, and are involved in generating nuclear bodies. Conversely, cytoplasmic lncRNAs can function as competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) by acting as miRNA sponges, competing for miRNA binding and thereby derepressing miRNA targets. Furthermore, they also participate in the regulation of translation and post-translational modifications (Fig. 1) [14]. Interestingly, the expression is even more restricted and controlled than that of protein-coding genes, resulting in greater cell-type and tissue specificity. This characteristic endows lncRNAs with the potential to serve as specific prognostic biomarker and even as therapeutic target [12].

Biogenesis and function of lncRNAs. After being synthesized by RNA polymerase II, nascent RNA transcripts are produced and often undergo post-transcriptional modifications, including 5’ capping and 3’ polyadenylation, to become mature lncRNAs (a). These mature lncRNAs perform their functions in either the nucleus or the cytoplasm. Nuclear lncRNAs regulate chromatin (b), transcription (c), and nuclear body formation (d), while cytoplasmic lncRNAs act as miRNA sponges (e) and regulate translation (f) and post-translational modifications (g)

LncRNAs in normal hematopoiesis

Although nowadays known to be more complex, one can state that normal hematopoiesis starts from hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), which differentiate into common myeloid progenitors (CMP) and common lymphoid progenitors (CLP). Among these, the CMP further differentiates into erythroid and myeloid blood cells, while the CLP differentiates into lymphoid blood cells [15]. Within this hierarchical differentiation system, lncRNAs play crucial roles [16, 17]. For example, Xist is essential for maintaining the normal maturation process of HSCs and preventing their age-dependent loss [18]. In addition, Hu and colleagues found that LincRNA-EPS is critical for erythroid differentiation and the inhibition of apoptosis of mature erythrocytes [19]. For a detailed overview of the role of lncRNAs throughout the hematopoietic hierarchy, readers are directed to the work of Alvarez-Dominguez et al. and Li et al. [20, 21].

LncRNAs in the pathogenesis of AML

A considerable number of lncRNAs has shown to act as oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes in both pediatric and adult AML [22, 23]. As the molecular landscape of pedAML exhibits significant differences with that of adult AML, it remains important to make this distinction as well for findings related to lncRNA research. The first hint that lncRNAs were involved in AML pathogenesis came from the study by Garzon et al., showing differential lncRNA expression between adult AML patients and healthy controls [24]. We have recently shown that leukemia stem cells, which are at least in part responsible for relapse, harbor a unique lncRNA expression profile compared to their normal HSC counterpart, underscoring their potential as therapeutic target [25].

Many individual lncRNAs have been further characterized in the context of AML or specific genetic subgroups [22, 23]. For example, Wang and colleagues have shown that lncRNA HOTAIR recruits DNMT3B, inducing methylation of the tumor suppressor gene HOXA5 promoter. Knockdown of HOTAIR could re-establish the expression of HOXA5, consequently inhibiting the growth of AML cells and promoting apoptosis both in vitro and in vivo [26]. Also impacting transcription, the lncRNA RUNXOR utilizes its 3′-terminal fragment to interact with the RUNX1 promoter and enhancers. This interaction facilitates the formation of intrachromosomal loops and long-range interchromosomal interactions, resulting in regulation of RUNX1 expression [27]. Cytoplasmic lncRNAs have been shown to scavenge miRNAs (e.g., CD27-AS1 pairing with miR-224-5p; MALAT1 with miR-96) or regulate translation and post-translational modifications [28–32]. A comprehensive overview thereof is out-of-scope, and we refer to the excellent overviews provided by Gourvest et al. and Neyazi et al. [22, 23].

Prognostic value of multi-lncRNA signatures in AML

LncRNAs are emerging as effective biomarkers for tumor diagnosis and prognostication [33, 34]. Importantly, their clinical utility does not necessarily depend on having a defined biological function. Rather, lncRNA expression often reflects active upstream regulatory events—such as chromatin remodeling and transcription factor binding—especially in genomic regions neighboring oncogenes, including various classes of lncRNAs such as enhancer RNAs [35], endogenous retroviral elements [36], and antisense transcripts [37]. In this way, lncRNA expression can serve as a sensitive indicator of local transcriptional activity and the broader regulatory environment that underlies tumor behavior and clinical outcomes, thereby supporting their value as prognostic biomarkers in cancer. Indeed, levels of the lncRNA PCA3 (Prostate Cancer Antigen 3) in urine can be utilized for the diagnosis of prostate cancer, and its diagnostic kit has received FDA approval [38]. Similarly, lncRNA SChLAP1, has been shown to predict metastasis and risk stratification based on its levels in prostate cancer tissues and urine [39]. In AML, several prognostic lncRNA expression signatures have been published, including a study from our research group, reviewed and summarized below.

We queried (November 13, 2024) the NCBI PubMed database (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) using the terms “(prognos*) AND (acute myeloid leukemia) AND ((Long Non Coding RNA) OR (lncRNA))”, and obtained a total of 243 studies. Subsequently, we excluded all papers that did not specifically focus on designing lncRNAs-based prognostic models (n = 212). We systematically examined the final selection of 27 reported lncRNAs-based prognostic signatures for adult AML and 4 signatures for pediatric AML with the aim of consolidating and comparing all reported models in this field (Table 1; Fig. 2). The majority of the models (21/31) adopted a strategy where lncRNAs associated with specific pathways or phenotypes were identified and subsequently evaluated their prognostic significance. Additionally, two articles tailored their analyses to specific subtypes or characteristics of AML patients, focusing on patients aged ≥ 60 years with cytogenetically normal acute myeloid leukemia (CN-AML) and young patients (aged < 60 years) with CN-AML, respectively [24, 60]. Overall, the number of lncRNAs defining a signature varied from 2 to 69 (Table 1), with hazard ratios (HRs) ranging from 1.036 to 4.596 (Fig. 2).

As highlighted in a recent paper by Markowetz, most of the currently published clinical models are incomplete and far from clinical application [70]. Bias mainly resulted from the lncRNAs inclusion strategy and the model construction process. Most of the studies (24/31) include candidate lncRNAs based on their association with specific pathways or phenotypes, or through differential gene expression analysis. However, selecting pathway-associated lncRNAs solely based on correlation coefficients, as employed in many studies (19/31), is unreliable. Furthermore, the pre-selection of specific candidate lncRNAs may overlook other lncRNAs with potentially greater prognostic significance. Additionally, biases also arose from the fact that several models (12/31) were constructed using multiple statistical methods simultaneously, univariate Cox + least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox + multivariate Cox, a strategy prone to overfitting. This tends to identify a signature encompassing only a very small number of lncRNAs, which demonstrates good performance in the training cohort, however reducing the model’s robustness. This finally limits its ability to be validated in independent datasets.

Next to bias, the incompleteness in the identified papers mainly stems from the lack of internal and/or external validation, as well as the absence or partial absence of model evaluation metrics. Internal validation is crucial for estimating a model’s generalizability, while external validation must be performed to assess a model’s reproducibility and generalizability to other cohorts [71, 72]. However, only a limited number of models (5/31) underwent both types of validation. Additionally, although three other pedAML-based models, aside from ours [69], have undergone external validation, their external validation was based on adult AML cohorts, which is not ideal. Furthermore, some reports (14/31) do not provide, or only partially provide, the performance of the model precisely, such as the area under the curve (AUC), and HR. Additionally, among the eleven papers that performed external validation, only our work [69] reported both the AUCs and HRs of the model in the external validation cohort. Moreover, four models lacked evaluation of the independent prognostic value. This is achieved by incorporating the model together with established clinical variables into a multivariable Cox analysis and assessing whether the model remains significant (p < 0.05). Without this, it remains unclear whether the model can provide additional prognostic information beyond established clinical variables, thereby undermining its clinical value. Figure 3 illustrates the presence of the aforementioned bias or incompleteness in the 31 articles. In addition to the above parameters, validation in a prospective, independent cohort was not performed for any of the studies included in this report. Finally, clinical implementation also requires careful evaluation of aspects such as pre-analytical variables (e.g. sampling tube, storage conditions) and cost-effectiveness. None of the studies included, neither our own work, reported on these.

In addition, it is important to note that all existing multi-lncRNA prognostic signatures, including our own, are currently based on total gene-level expression rather than individual transcript variants. However, a single lncRNA gene may give rise to multiple isoforms with distinct functions depending on physiological or pathological conditions [73]. Nevertheless, incorporating isoform-level information into prognostic models remains technically challenging. First, lncRNA transcript annotation is still incomplete, with many isoforms poorly characterized [74]. Second, conventional RNA sequencing approaches often lack the resolution to distinguish closely related isoforms, particularly for low-abundance or structurally complex lncRNAs [75, 76]. These limitations underscore the need for improved methods capable of accurately resolving lncRNA transcript isoforms—such as advanced computational algorithms and long-read sequencing technologies—as well as for more comprehensive and refined annotation of lncRNA transcripts.

Given the clinical significance of refractory and relapsed acute myeloid leukemia (R/R AML), representing approximately one-third of pedAML patients, including many standard risk/intermediate risk patients, models for predicting relapse risk need to be further explored. In addition, as treatments for pedAML relapse are accompanied by unavoidable side effects, selecting the correct population undergoing intensified treatments is of utmost importance [5, 77]. Currently, there is a lack of highly effective models for predicting relapse. Aiming to provide a model useful for early identification of patients with a high risk to relapse, we have embarked on defining a lncRNA-based signature to predict relapse-free survival (RFS) in pedAML [69]. Using Children’s Oncology Group studies AAML0531and AAML1031 retrieved from the public Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET) repository, we unbiasedly included all lncRNAs annotated in the transcriptome. We first applied univariate Cox regression to preliminarily screen for lncRNAs significantly associated with RFS, followed by LASSO-Cox regression to select 69 lncRNAs and construct a linear signature named AMLlnc69, with AUCs for predicting 1-, 2-, and 3-year RFS being 0.78, 0.77, and 0.77, respectively, and a c-index of 0.73. Additionally, internal validation using bootstrap analysis was employed to demonstrate the reliability of the model-building process. Furthermore, we selected an external cohort (AAML1031) of pedAML for validation, where the model also performed well, achieving a 3-year AUC of 0.7 despite the low number of patients. Finally, we developed a nomogram tool, which incorporates AMLlnc69, NPM1 mutation, and white blood cell count at the time of diagnosis, parameters that were selected based on their independent prognostic value in a multivariate Cox analysis. Relative to the application of each individual factor, the nomogram provides a more refined estimation of the cumulative relapse risk. With further prospective independent validation, this tool could offer valuable insights for prognostic assessment and therapeutic decision-making in the clinical context. Notably, several lncRNAs included in the AMLlnc69 signature—such as MIR100HG, MIR17HG, MALAT1, and ZFAS1—have been previously reported to play functional roles in AML. However, despite these mechanistic insights, their clinical utility as biomarkers remains limited [69].

Detection of LncRNAs in clinic

The techniques currently employed to quantify lncRNA/messenger RNA (mRNA) biomarkers in patient samplesprimarily include RNA sequencing, microarrays, and RT-qPCR. The FDA-approved PAM50-based breast cancer classification strategy, along with the mRNA-lncRNA signature recently developed by Jiang et al. to guide the treatment of triple-negative breast cancer, brings hope for the clinical application of risk scores based on mRNA and lncRNA expression levels detected through microarrays and RT-qPCR [78–80]. Interestingly, a novel nCounter analysis system from NanoString has been developed, which allows for the direct profiling of individual RNA molecules in a single reaction without amplification [81]. The system has been successfully applied to detect lncRNA expression levels and efficiently predicted the prognosis of older patients with cytogenetically normal AML [82]. In addition, the system has also been applied in the detection of mRNA expression levels in the PAM50 model [83]. This rapid, standardized, and clinically feasible tool has facilitated the translation of lncRNA profiling from research laboratories to clinical practice.

Conclusions

LncRNAs have emerged as novel molecular players in both normal hematopoiesis and AML. Due to the high specificity, they have demonstrated significant value in prognostic prediction. Although numerous prognostic lncRNA-based signatures have been developed, their shortcomings have severely hindered their translation into clinical practice. The AMLlnc69 model we recently constructed focuses on the urgently needed prediction of relapse in pedAML in clinical practice. We paid attention to use an appropriate construction strategy, conducted both internal and external validations, and reported the evaluation metrics of the model. These steps ensure that the model is well-positioned for successful clinical translation. Nevertheless, our model has some limitations, including the lack of prospective independent cohort validation, insufficient consideration of sample preservation effects on transcriptome sequencing, and the absence of cost-effective lncRNA expression detection methods. Future prospective validation will allow to consider also these points, and better prepare for clinical translational.
