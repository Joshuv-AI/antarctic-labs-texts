Efficacy and safety of spesolimab in resistant pyoderma gangrenosum: a case report and brief review of the literature
Authors: Kmar Turki, Emna Bahloul, Mariem Ennouri, Rim Chaabouni, Sonia Boudaya, Fatma Hammami, Khadija Sellami, Slaheddine Marrakchi, Noura Bougacha, Hamida Turki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare neutrophilic dermatosis characterized by rapidly progressive painful ulcers, and increasing evidence supports a role for the interleukin-36 pathway in its pathogenesis. We report the case of a 59-year-old woman with idiopathic refractory PG that evolved over 8 years. The patient had an insufficient response to prolonged systemic corticosteroids and azathioprine, with persistent steroid dependency. Spesolimab was initiated as a single 900-mg intravenous infusion in combination with low-dose prednisone (10 mg daily). This led to pain reduction, re-epithelialization and progressive ulcer healing, estimated at 30% after 3 months, 50% after 6 months and 80% after 8 months. No major adverse events were observed, apart from an erysipelas-like reaction around the ulcers 3 months later. Our review of published cases suggests that spesolimab may induce rapid and sustained improvement in refractory PG, although responses appear more heterogeneous in patients with underlying myeloid disorders. Overall, spesolimab appears to be a promising and well-tolerated therapeutic option for refractory PG, but further controlled studies are needed to better define its efficacy and safety, and place in the treatment algorithm.

Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a neutrophilic dermatosis characterized by rapidly progressive painful ulcers. Its pathogenesis is largely attributed to pathergy, with keratinocytes releasing pro­inflammatory mediators, including interleukin (IL)-36, which plays a role in neutrophil recruitment.1–3 We report a case of refractory PG that improved significantly under treatment with the IL-36 receptor antagonist spesolimab.

Case report

A 59-year-old woman with no relevant medical history presented with chronic leg ulcers of 8 years’ duration. A biopsy of the ulcerative lesions revealed a predominantly neutrophilic infiltrate with no evidence of infection [negative bacterial and fungal cultures, and the special stains (Gram and periodic acid–Schiff) on the histopathological specimen did not reveal any microorganisms] (Figure 1). This was consistent with PG. A comprehensive diagnostic workup for PG was performed, including screening for underlying neoplasms (especially haematological malignancies), ­inflammatory bowel disease and drug-induced causes. All results were negative, leading to a diagnosis of idiopathic PG. She received systemic corticosteroid therapy for 3 years (prednisone ranging from 60 mg to 10 mg daily). A relapse occurred when the dose was tapered to 10 mg daily, and healing failed to resume despite re-­escalation to 1 mg kg–1 daily. Combination therapy with corticosteroids and azathioprine (150 mg kg–1 daily) was maintained for 2 years, leading to only partial healing (approximately 30%) (Figure 2a). The addition of ciclosporin, anti-tumour necrosis factor-α agents or intravenous immunoglobulins was considered but could not be implemented due to lack of reimbursement coverage by the national health insurance system. Due to therapeutic resistance, steroid dependency and corticosteroid-induced complications, treatment with spesolimab was initiated, combined with low-dose prednisone (10 mg kg–1 daily). A single intravenous infusion of spesolimab (900 mg) induced re-epithelialization, pain reduction and partial ulcer healing (30% within 3 months, 50% within 6 months) (Figure 2b, c). Significant healing was achieved at 8 months (80%) (Figure 2d). No adverse events were noted, although an erysipelas-like reaction appeared around the ulcers 3 months later.

Discussion

PG is a rare neutrophilic dermatosis characterized by rapidly progressive necrotic ulcers associated with severe pain. Its exact aetiology remains unclear, but it involves neutrophil dysregulation and immune dysfunction. IL-36, part of the IL-1 cytokine family, is implicated in neutrophilic inflammation and PG pathogenesis by amplifying proinflammatory signalling and modulating T-cell differentiation, thereby sustaining a chronic inflammatory cycle.1–3

Spesolimab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the IL-36 receptor,3 has shown promising results in refractory PG. Several reported cases demonstrated rapid and sustained improvement, with some patients experiencing pain relief and significant ulcer healing within 48–72 h of infusion and complete re-­epithelialization after 1–4 doses.4–8 These include patients who had failed multiple lines of corticosteroids and immunosuppressants, highlighting spesolimab’s potential as a transformative therapy. The rapid epithelial regeneration observed further supports a central role of IL-36 in PG and other neutrophilic dermatoses. Across the published cases and our own experience, spesolimab has been administered as a single 900-mg intravenous ­infusion in some patients7,8 and our own, whereas others received 2–6 repeated doses every 2–4 weeks,4–7 with no significant difference in overall outcomes. Notably, all patients whose PG failed to respond had an underlying myeloid disorder.7 The absence of response to spesolimab in these patients may reflect that IL-36 is not a central cytokine of PG when it occurs in the context of an underlying myeloid neoplasm.7 Notably, spesolimab efficacy in generalized pustular psoriasis has been demonstrated in patients with and without a mutation in IL36RN, suggesting that IL-36 pathway activation occurs through multiple mechanisms.9 However, the distinct failure of spesolimab in patients with PG with myeloid disorders may indicate that in these cases, alternative or additional pathogenic pathways predominate, potentially involving myeloid-derived inflammatory mediators beyond the IL-36 axis. This heterogeneity underscores the necessity for further studies to elucidate the distinct pathophysiology of neutrophilic dermatoses associated with underlying systemic disorders.

Furthermore, the duration of disease prior to treatment does not appear to influence the therapeutic response. Neither the rapidity of clinical improvement nor the duration of remission correlates with how long the disease has been present before the initiation of spesolimab.

Limitations include the concurrent use of other immunosuppressants in reported cases and ours, yet most patients experience improvement within 72 h, suggesting a direct effect of ­spesolimab. Another limitation of our case is that although the outcome at 8 months was a near-complete response (≈80% healing), it did not reach full resolution. Importantly, no significant safety issues have been reported.

In conclusion, these cases highlight the efficacy and safety of spesolimab as a promising therapeutic option for refractory PG. Randomized controlled trials are underway (NCT06624670, NCT06092216) to further define the safety, efficacy and clinical role of spesolimab in PG, offering hope for more targeted and effective treatments for this debilitating disease.
