Analysis of junctional beats during slow pathway ablation: Illuminating the mechanism of typical and atypical AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia
Authors: Phuong Ngo Thanh Nguyen, Yumi Katsume, Akiko Ueda, Seiichiro Matsuo, Noriko Nonoguchi, Hirotsugu Ikewaki, Takato Mohri, Kyoko Hoshida, Mika Tashiro, Toshiaki Sato, Ikuko Togashi, Kyoko Soejima
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJunctional rhythm (JR) frequently occurs during radiofrequency (RF) ablation procedures targeting the slow pathway (SP) for atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT), signaling successful ablation. Two types of JR have been noticed: typical JR as His activation preceding atrial activation, and atypical JR as atrial activation preceding the His activation. Nevertheless, the origin and characteristics of JR remain incompletely defined.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate whether JR induced by RF ablation at the anatomical SP position could reveal preferential conduction in the antegrade vs the retrograde direction.MethodsConsecutive RF ablation procedures targeting the SP for AVNRT were performed in 40 patients. Using electroanatomic mapping, the coronary sinus ostium, His bundle, RF sites, and the distances between these sites and the RF sites (n = 216) were analyzed. We compared the H-A and A-H intervals during AVNRT and JR.ResultsIn typical AVNRT, the H-AJR resembled the H-AAVNRT with an identical atrial activation sequence, supporting JR conduction to the atrium via a fast pathway. The atypical AVNRT group displayed a significantly shorter A-HJR than the A-HAVNRT (P < .0001) with identical atrial activation. The JR incidence in patients with both typical and atypical AVNRT showed no correlation with the RF site location.ConclusionFor patients with typical AVNRT, JR induced by SP ablation preferentially followed the fast pathway. In patients with atypical AVNRT and with retrograde SP conduction, a shorter A-H interval during JR, with the same atrial sequence as that observed during atypical AVNRT, implies retrograde conduction from the SP to the atrium.Keywords: AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia, junctional rhythm, ablation, slow pathway, fast pathway

Introduction

Catheter ablation has become the cornerstone treatment for atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT), the most prevalent form of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia. Traditionally, AVNRT variants have been classified into slow/fast, fast/slow, and slow/slow AVNRT based on the site of the earliest retrograde atrial activation and the relative duration of H-A and A-H intervals. Slow/fast AVNRT is identified by earliest retrograde atrial activation at the His recording site, with long A-H intervals (usually ≥200 ms) and short H-A intervals (25–90 ms). Distinguishing fast/slow AVNRT from slow/slow AVNRT involves recognizing the earliest retrograde activation at the inferior triangle of Koch or the coronary sinus (CS) with a short A-H interval (<200 ms).1 Fast/slow AVNRT involves the use of the fast pathway (FP) for antegrade and the slow pathway (SP) for retrograde conduction, resulting in A-H intervals of 30 to 185 ms and H-A intervals of 135 to 435 ms.2

During SP ablation, the appearance of junctional rhythm (JR) reflects the thermal effect of RF ablation on the atrioventricular (AV) node or its transitional cells in the atrial muscle. However, the mechanism and characteristics of retrograde atrial activation during JR have not been well described.3

Therefore, we hypothesized that analyzing JR during SP ablation could indicate the ability of either the FP or SP to conduct in the retrograde direction. This study aimed to determine whether preferential conduction of the SP in the antegrade vs the retrograde direction could be revealed through an analysis of JR caused by heating at the anatomic SP position.

Methods

Patient population

Retrospectively, we enrolled a total of 40 consecutive patients who underwent RF ablation of AVNRT from September 2021 to October 2023, with an average age of 57.5 ± 16 years, including 19 men. All participants underwent RF ablation for AVNRT utilizing an electroanatomic mapping system, either CARTO version 3 (Biosense Webster) or EnSite X (Abbott). Patients were categorized into 3 groups based on the presence of typical and atypical AVNRT: typical AVNRT (n = 31), atypical AVNRT (n = 7), and both types of AVNRT (n = 2) induced during the electrophysiological study.

Electrophysiological study and ablation

The RF ablation procedure followed an anatomical approach conducted during sinus rhythm, with concurrent monitoring of ventriculoatrial conduction during JR. RF power, initially set approximately 25 W, was systematically adjusted to attain a temperature of 60 °C or a 5 to 10 Ω impedance fall. The initial RF lesions were placed anterior to the CS ostium at the tricuspid annulus. Subsequently, the lesions extended backward to the lips of the CS ostium. In cases in which AVNRT remained inducible, additional lesions were applied in a more cranial direction at the CS roof or below, and then into the proximal CS anterior wall. The RF ablation sites that induced JR were categorized as typical or atypical. Typical JR was characterized by His activation followed by atrial activation (Figure 1). In contrast, atypical JR exhibited atrial activation followed by His deflection (Figure 2). The distances from the RF ablation site to the His bundle and CS ostium were systematically measured. The atrial activation sequence was evaluated by using a diagnostic catheter.

Measurement of activation

H-A intervals during the AVNRT (H-AAVNRT) and JR (H-AJR) were determined by measuring the time from His deflection to atrial activation in the high right atrium (HRA) in typical JR, and the A-H interval during atypical AVNRT (A-HAVNRT) and atypical JR (A-HJR) were measured. This approach was adopted because distinguishing atrial activation from simultaneous ventricular activation at the earliest atrial activation during JR can be challenging, particularly in situations in which ventricular pacing cannot be reliably performed. To further understand the relationship between H-AAVNRT and H-AJR and to ensure consistency across measurements, a paired t test was performed between them to explore any potential difference between the intervals during AVNRT and JR, thereby providing insights into the dynamics of AV nodal conduction.

Electroanatomic mapping

Electroanatomic mapping was performed for RF ablation of the SP in 40 patients. The map included markings for the His bundle recording, CS ostium, and RF ablation sites (Figure 3). An analysis of the anatomical relationship between these designated sites and the location of the RF application was conducted to determine whether the types of JR were influenced by the RF application site.

A 3-dimensional electroanatomic map is shown. His recording sites are tagged yellow, while the coronary sinus ostium is tagged green. (Left) Left anterior oblique view of the patient; (right) right anterior oblique view of the patient.

Statistical analysis

Parametric data are presented as mean ± SD. Group comparisons were conducted using Fisher’s exact test or the paired t test, as applicable. For nonparametric data among the different groups, the chi-square test with Yates correction was used. Spearman’s rank correlation was used for correlation analysis. A significance level of P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Types of JR and AVNRT

The types of JR observed during RF application varied significantly among patient groups (P = .0005) (Figure 4). Specifically, all individuals diagnosed with typical AVNRT (n = 31) exhibited typical JR, and 7 of 31 patients displayed atypical JR as well. These 7 patients had both retrograde FP and SP conduction confirmed by right ventricle (RV) pacing. Patients with atypical AVNRT (n = 7) all manifested atypical JR, and 6 of 7 of patients also had typical JR. In these 6 patients, retrograde FP and SP conduction was confirmed by RV pacing. In patients with typical AVNRT, the atrial activation sequence observed during JR closely resembled that of the retrograde FP. In cases of atypical AVNRT, the atrial activation sequence recorded during atypical JR was similar to that observed during AVNRT. Types of JR and RF location

Among the 216 RF energy applications in the 40 patients, 75 (34.7%) had typical JR, 14 (6.5%) had atypical JR, 15 (6.9%) had both typical and atypical JR, and 112 (51.9%) had no JR. The distribution of the RF sites was based on their anatomical locations along the CS ostium and the His bundle. The distance between the CS ostium and the His region was subdivided into anterior, middle, and posterior regions, each further divided into 2 subsequent sections (Supplemental Figure 1A). We found that the majority of ablation sites were located in the posterior region. Notably, there was no observed correlation between the anatomical location of the RF site and the types of JR observed during RF ablation at that site across all patient groups (Supplemental Figure 1B).

Discussion

The main findings of our study were the following: (1) 2 types of JR were observed during SP ablation of AVNRT (typical JR with His activation preceding atrial activation, atypical JR with atrial activation preceding His activation); (2) the presence of atypical JR was dependent on the presence of retrograde SP conduction; (3) typical JR has a similar atrial activation sequence compared with typical AVNRT, and the H-A interval of typical JR is shorter than the H-A interval during typical AVNRT; and (4) atypical JR has a similar atrial activation sequence compared with atypical AVNRT, and the A-H interval during the atypical JR is shorter than A-H interval during atypical AVNRT.

The appearance of JR during RF application targeted at the SP has been demonstrated to be an indicator of successful catheter ablation in AVNRT. This phenomenon is often regarded as a reaction of the AV node to thermal injury affecting either the nodal or perinodal tissue.3 In our series, all patients had successful ablation, but program stimulation was not attempted following each RF application.

In patients with typical JR, all had retrograde FP conduction, and the atrial activation sequence was similar across cases, as judged by HRA, His, and CS recordings. In Supplemental Figure 2, if JR is caused by thermal injury to the SP and the activation follows the same pathway—the remaining SP to the retrograde FP—then H-AJR would be expected to be identical to H-AAVNRT. However, contrary to this expectation, H-AJR was significantly shorter than H-AAVNRT. As the cycle length of AVNRT is significantly shorter than JR, the decremental property of FP is possibly related to the longer H-A interval. Patients with atypical AVNRT predominantly display atypical JR during RF therapy, and also A-HJR was significantly shorter compared with A-HAVNRT. In atypical AVNRT, SP ablation prompted JR to conduct over the remaining SP of the AV node (Supplemental Figure 2). In this case, A-HJR was significantly shorter than A-HAVNRT with identical atrial activation, which could be explained by the longer cycle length of JR. In addition, the RF application at the SP causes the parasympathelysis, which might lead to the shorter conduction time.

Previously, several studies investigated the origin of JR. According to Yu and colleagues,4 the retrograde atrial activation sequence of JR during SP ablation showed its earliest occurrence in the anterior septum, away from the ablation site at posterior septum. Also, the pacing from the ablation site showed a different activation sequence compared with JR. The activation sequence resembled that observed during pacing from the RV apex. The authors proposed the presence of specialized tissue in the AV junction, suggesting a preference for transmitting the effects of RF energy to the anterior exit site and potentially matching the atrial exit site of the retrograde FP.4 In contrast, Boyle and colleagues5 used octapolar catheters at His recording site and CS and reported discordance in the earliest retrograde activation in more than half of the patients during AVNRT and JR during successful ablation of SP. They noted variation of the retrograde activation sequence and hypothesized that JR originating from multiple automatic foci in the posterior region resulted from transitional AV nodal fiber uncoupling and or injury.5 Anselme and colleagues6 indicated that the retrograde atrial activation sequence during typical AVNRT is more complex than previously understood. Multiple earliest atrial activation sites were observed in 21.7% of patients during RV pacing and 17.4% during AVNRT, with only 39.1% concordance in the site of earliest activation. They emphasized the anatomic heterogeneity and potentially dynamic properties of retrograde AV nodal conduction, rather than a strictly anatomically determined model. More recently, with the high-resolution mapping, Chua and colleagues7 mapped typical AVNRT and found anatomic variability and spatially heterogeneous activation. They suggested that alterations induced by ablation, such as cellular uncoupling and modification of electrotonic influences, may contribute to changes in JR activation patterns. Lee and colleagues8 evaluated JR in patients with typical AVNRT (n = 27) and atypical AVNRT (fast/slow AVNRT: n = 7; fast/intermediate AVNRT: n = 11) and found that JR during ablation of the SP conducted to the atrium through the FP or intermediate pathway but found no retrograde conduction in fast/slow AVNRT patients.

In contrast to these studies that looked into the typical JR, Tsuchiya and colleagues9 described atrial ectopy during SP ablation, originating from the posteroinferior atrium near CS ostium in some patients. The atrial ectopy was related to the presence or absence of retrograde SP conduction. In retrograde SP conduction, the activation conducts retrogradely to the atria from the RF site and activates the atria with a sequence of A-H-V as atypical JR. If there is no retrograde SP conduction, the activation has to go antegradely from the site of ablation and then retrogradely from the RF site to the atria via the anterior insertion with a sequence of H-A-V. However, their study showed that the site-specific atypical JR occurred more frequently at more posterior and inferior RF sites. Our result is in agreement with Tsuchiya and colleagues’ study that retrograde SP conduction determines atypical JR and not the site of ablation but not with the site specificity judged by the electroanatomic mapping system of RF sites with JR, based on their anatomical locations along the CS ostium and the His bundle, utilizing the historical metric (P1, P2, M1, M2, A1, A2).

Limitations

Our study had some limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, the activation sequences during JR and AVNRT were assessed based on the atrial activation sequence recorded by the multielectrode catheters in CS, HRA, and His bundle electrogram recording site, and no high-density map with currently available 3-dimensional mapping system was reconstructed. This suggests that while the activation sequences were compared, the level of detail provided by dense mapping was not achieved. Consequently, the possibility of subtle different activation sequences cannot be excluded. Second, there was no comparative analysis between successful ablation sites for typical and atypical JR. These limitations emphasize the importance of cautious interpretation of the study results and the identification of areas where future research may offer additional clarity.

Conclusion

In patients with typical AVNRT, JR induced by heating in the assumed SP region demonstrates a conduction preference for the atrium over the FP rather than retrograde over the SP. This observation aligns with the characteristic features of typical AVNRT. Additionally, in atypical AVNRT patients and patients with retrograde SP conduction, atypical JR, in which atrial activation is earlier than His activation, showed the similar activation as SP, suggesting the activation via the SP to the atrium. However, the H-A and A-H intervals were shorter compared with those observed during AVNRT. Detailed activation mapping of JR can be anticipated with high-density mapping.
