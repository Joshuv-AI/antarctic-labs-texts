Risky choice framing effects, doing vs. allowing harm, and fairness in uncertain prospects
Authors: Tomasz Żuradzki, Marta Maj, Paulina Szwed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In popular interpretations, risky choice framing effects are taken to reveal human irrationality. Even authors who argue that frames can sometimes highlight the reason-giving aspects of an outcome often treat the standard pattern of choices in canonical experiments, such as the Asian Disease Problem (ADP), as irrational. This paper proposes a new approach to studying risky choice framing effects, including those observed in the ADP, by interpreting them in terms of reasons: such effects occur when different frames highlight distinct reason-giving aspects of a situation and thereby shape how decision-makers represent and evaluate the available options, which in turn leads to systematic differences in choice. This reason-based approach has significant implications for debates about the apparent irrationality of choices in risky choice framing effects and about the supposed equivalence of the ADP options. It suggests that framing may influence not only how outcomes are valued, but also which considerations are taken to be relevant to the decision. Our central claim is that responsiveness to such reasons can, in some cases, be rational. To assess this proposal, we report a new experimental study examining the extent to which non-consequentialist moral reasons related to the doing/allowing distinction and fairness can explain choices in “sacrificial dilemmas” such as the ADP. Our findings suggest that participants’ choices are sensitive to these considerations, supporting the view that framing effects in moral contexts cannot be fully explained by standard outcome-based models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11229-026-05701-y.

Introduction

This paper examines risky choice framing effects through the Asian Disease Problem (ADP). While Prospect Theory (PT) has amassed substantial empirical support—primarily in contexts involving financial judgments and decisions—the ADP concerns life-saving choices and has a clear ethical dimension. The ADP shares similarities with “sacrificial dilemmas” in ethics, which probe both consequentialist and non-consequentialist reasons for judgment and decision (e.g., the Trolley Problem; Foot, 1967). Tversky and Kahneman (1986a) observed that framing can be “ethically significant” because “framing influences the experience of consequences” (p. 503). Yet, most research on the ADP in psychology and economics overlooks this ethical dimension, likely because experimental paradigms often focus on financial or other quantifiable incentives (such as the snow shovel cases by Kahneman et al., 1986). When outcomes are expressed only in numerical terms, subjects who do not maximize expected benefits are often seen as inconsistent or irrational.

While the dominant interpretation in psychology and behavioral economics is that risky choice framing effects reveal irrationality, a minority tradition has questioned this inference (e.g., Macdonald, 1986; Kühberger, 1998; Mandel, 2001, 2014; Sher & McKenzie, 2006). Nevertheless, even some critics of the standard approach continue to treat the canonical response pattern in the ADP as irrational. In a recent book and article, Bermúdez (2020, 2022) contrasts “the simplest” cases of framing effects, such as the ADP, where “frames prime responses,” with more complex cases in which “frames can function reflectively, by making salient particular reason-giving aspects of a thing, outcome, or action” (2022, p. 1). He concludes that the emphasis on the simplest cases “has blinded us to the existence of rational framing effects” (p. 3, italics original) in which the ability to frame something in multiple ways should be regarded as a powerful tool for understanding and decision-making. We agree with Bermúdez that, surprisingly, most research in moral psychology and behavioral economics on risky choice framing effects—particularly in the ADP paradigm—focuses on their existence and magnitude while neglecting their explanatory dimension. At the same time, we argue that the scope of rational risky choice framing effects may be even broader than Bermúdez assumes in his recent work, and “it is perfectly rational to be influenced by how things are framed” (p. 1) in certain canonical experiments, especially in contexts where human life is at stake. This is because different frames can make distinct reason-giving aspects of a situation salient, and sensitivity to such aspects can, in some cases, constitute a rational response rather than a cognitive error.

A natural question, then, is whether the mechanisms that explain framing effects in non-moral contexts—such as the well-known preference for “80% fat-free” over “20% fat”—might also account for the Asian Disease Problem. In such cases, framing effects are typically explained in non-normative terms by appeal to gain–loss asymmetries or other features captured by Prospect Theory. While such outcome-based mechanisms may account for part of the observed pattern, we argue that they do not explain fully how framing shapes which considerations are taken to be relevant in the first place.

The standard choice patterns in the ADP may stem from the interaction of multiple reasons that people recognize, which can reinforce or counterbalance each other. This paper advances the discussion by examining aspects that have received limited attention—specifically, morally valid reasons for choice reversals or shifts in life-saving dilemmas like the ADP. By “morally valid reasons,” we refer to considerations related to harm, agency, responsibility, or fairness that are salient in life-and-death contexts and supported by normative moral doctrines. Building on Dreisbach and Guevara (2019), we focus on two types of non-consequentialist reasons that may be central in explaining ADP choice patterns: fairness (egalitarian reasons) and the doing/allowing distinction. To evaluate this proposal, we conducted an experimental study investigating whether non-consequentialist considerations—fairness and the doing/allowing distinction—predict participants’ choices in the ADP. Rather than focusing solely on aggregate choice patterns, our design assesses how various evaluative dimensions influence implementation decisions, extending prior work on framing’s impact on the reasons agents consider when making decisions (e.g., Shafir et al., 1993). This approach enables us to identify which considerations actually drive choice, rather than simply describing shifts in aggregate preferences. Our results indicate that positively framed options are more likely to be interpreted as the distribution of benefits, yet we do not find strong evidence that negatively framed options are systematically interpreted as distributions of harms. Furthermore, evaluation of fairness emerge as an independent predictor of participants’ choices; probabilistic options—especially Program B—are judged as fairer and are chosen more frequently.

The structure of the paper is as follows. Section 2 introduces the Asian Disease Problem and its standard interpretation within Prospect Theory, examines the main charge of irrationality directed at the ADP, distinguishes different ways in which the alleged inconsistency can be understood, and discusses the principles of invariance and extensionality that are often taken to underwrite this charge. Section 3 presents our alternative, reason-based interpretation of the ADP. After reviewing several non-standard interpretations, we argue that different descriptions can highlight distinct reason-giving aspects, and that responsiveness to these reasons can be rational. We also consider how this approach relates to skepticism about moral intuitions, before exploring two families of non-consequentialist reasons relevant to the ADP: the doing/allowing distinction and fairness. Section 3.2.1 and 3.3.1 report the results of our experimental study on these dimensions, and Sect. 3.4 addresses the study’s main limitations. Section 4 concludes.

The ADP and its standard interpretation

Prospect Theory (PT), developed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in the late 1970s, aimed to explain human judgment and decision-making as an alternative to standard expected utility theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). The Asian Disease Problem (ADP) is among the most influential experiments illustrating how PT can predict and explain human choices more effectively than prior models. The ADP exemplifies a “risky choice framing effect,” where the outcomes of options with different risk levels are presented in alternative ways. Other major types of framing include “attribute framing effects” and “goal framing effects” (Levin et al., 1998).

In the ADP research, participants, under forced-choice conditions, are asked to imagine that the US is preparing for an unusual Asian disease, which is expected to kill many people (600 in the original experiment). Participants may choose one of two alternative programs that have been proposed to combat the disease and are informed that the exact scientific estimate of the consequences of the programs is as follows:

A)If Program A is adopted, 200 people will be savedB)If Program B is adopted, there is 1/3 probability that 600 people will be saved, and 2/3 probability that no people will be saved

If Program A is adopted, 200 people will be saved

B)If Program B is adopted, there is 1/3 probability that 600 people will be saved, and 2/3 probability that no people will be saved

If Program B is adopted, there is 1/3 probability that 600 people will be saved, and 2/3 probability that no people will be saved

For a second group of participants, all else was the same, but instead of Programs A and B, the following alternative Programs C and D were given:

C)If Program C is adopted, 400 people will dieD)If Program D is adopted, there is 1/3 probability that nobody will die, and 2/3 probability that 600 people will die

C)If Program C is adopted, 400 people will die

If Program C is adopted, 400 people will die

D)If Program D is adopted, there is 1/3 probability that nobody will die, and 2/3 probability that 600 people will die

If Program D is adopted, there is 1/3 probability that nobody will die, and 2/3 probability that 600 people will die

As one can see, the options seem to be equal in terms of their expected utility, but options A and C suggest outcomes with mixed favorable and unfavorable components (some lives will be saved, and some people will die). In contrast, options B and D offer a probabilistic outcome with a definite structure (a probability that either all lives will be saved or all people will die).

The original ADP experiment (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981) demonstrated a striking framing effect. When outcomes were presented in positive terms (“people will be saved”), most participants preferred the certain option: 72% chose Program A, while only 28% chose Program B. Conversely, when framed in negative terms (“people will die”), the majority favored the probabilistic option: just 22% selected Program C, and 78% chose Program D. Meta-analyses confirm that risky choice framing effects in the ADP are robust and reliable, though recent studies report somewhat smaller effect sizes (d = 1.13; Kühberger, 1998; Steiger & Kühberger, 2018; Klein et al., 2014). The ADP choice pattern has also been replicated across cultures, including a study of 49 countries during the COVID-19 pandemic (Im & Chen, 2022). Notably, the ADP’s design—contrasting a riskless with a risky alternative—produces particularly strong framing effects; Kühberger’s (1998) meta-analysis found the effect size much greater in such cases than when both options are risky. This indicates that the ADP exemplifies a particularly potent risky-choice framing effect, and its results may not directly generalize to other framing paradigms.

The standard interpretation—treating life-saving and money-saving decisions as structurally similar—explains the ADP pattern via a two-phase cognitive process (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981, p. 453). First, options are represented as certain or probabilistic gains or losses relative to a reference point: Programs A and B (“will be saved”) are seen as gains, while Programs C and D (“will die”) are seen as losses. These baseline differences are attributed to superficial variations in description. Second, outcomes are evaluated using value functions: concave for gains, convex for losses, and with losses weighted more heavily than gains. As a result, individuals tend to be risk-averse in the domain of gains and risk-seeking in the domain of losses. This framework explains the observed pattern: under positive framing, participants prefer the certainty of saving some lives (Program A), whereas under negative framing, they prefer the probabilistic option (Program D).

The scope of irrationality

The ADP effect is frequently cited as evidence of human irrationality or inconsistency, on the grounds that there is something “wrong” with agents who display these patterns of preferences. In this section, we critically analyze and challenge these claims, arguing that judgment reversals or shifts can be entirely rational—and, in some cases, grounded in morally valid reasons, particularly in life-saving choices like those in the ADP.

For many authors, it is not just irrational but “the flagship example of rational inconsistency” (Nichols et al., 2016, footnote 22). Tversky and Kahneman themselves emphasized the “inconsistent responses to problems 1 and 2” (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981, p. 453), and a recent meta-analysis of framing effects concluded that the reaction of the majority respondents in the ADP “is, on logical grounds, irrational” (Steiger & Kühberger, 2018, p. 46; cf. Gold & List, 2004: “framing effects are offensive to a logician’s account of rationality,” p. 254). For example, some authors reduce the ADP to a simple arithmetic task, insisting that if participants fail to recognize the numerical equivalence of options A and C or B and D, they should “take another look at the problem and do the arithmetic” (Hammond, 2000, p. 61). A similar perspective also appears in philosophical discussions: “The picture of human reasoning that emerges from this research is that we should maximize prospects, but in many cases, we do not do so. Human irrationality is rampant” (Rini & Bruni, 2017, p. 158).

If A = C and B = D (that is, they are equivalents), then it is straightforwardly irrational to prefer A(C) to B(D) and at the same time prefer B(D) to A(C). This follows not from any particular interpretation of the ADP but simply from the equivalence assumption itself. The equivalence thesis is widely accepted: Kahneman and Tversky described the two problems as “effectively identical” (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981, p. 453) and stated that “options C and D in Problem 2 are indistinguishable in real terms from options A and B in Problem 1, respectively” (Kahneman & Tversky, 1984, p. 343). Bermúdez (2020) maintains that even in ultra-intensional contexts—contexts where necessarily equivalent descriptions may nevertheless be treated differently because they highlight distinct reasons or explanatory perspectives (see Sect. 3)—the agent still recognizes that “the same action or outcome” is merely framed differently. On this view, the irrationality of the ADP consists in a failure of arithmetic or logical substitution: subjects do not “do the math” and therefore treat equivalent options as different.

Of course, such views are particularly prominent in economics, where the charge of irrationality is not tied to the complexity of practical reasons people may have in life-and-death contexts, but to a purely numerical mode of decision-making that equates rationality with maximizing expected lives saved, analogous to maximizing financial gain or loss. However, it is more surprising that this reasoning is applied to gambling with lives, which one might expect to be treated as more nuanced than gambling with dollars.

We identify two primary interpretations of this alleged inconsistency. The synchronic interpretation views the problem as simultaneous: if someone prefers A to B and D to C, while the equivalence between A and C (and B and D) is assumed, then those preferences jointly imply both A > B and B > A. The diachronic interpretation sees the issue as sequential: a person who initially prefers A to B may later reverse this preference when the options are reframed. Both interpretations also have counterfactual forms: on the synchronic counterfactual reading, a subject who prefers A to B would instead prefer the opposite option if the scenario were differently framed; on the diachronic counterfactual reading, a subject would change their preference if presented with the alternative framing at a later time.

Here is an example of standard (i.e., non-counterfactual) synchronic interpretation: “it is irrational to prefer A to B and at the same time prefer D to C” (Hausman et al., 2016, p. 50). However, most authors claim that this risky choice framing effect is irrational, not because of momentarily irrational preference structure but because of arbitrary diachronic changes in preferences and choices: “a framing effect is a special kind of choice reversal” (Dietrich & List, 2016, p. 201, italics added; see also Pettigrew, 2022, p. 36). One possible explanation of the diachronic version refers to risk-preference reversal: “it appears deeply irrational to flip between being risk-averse and being risk-seeking depending upon how the relevant prospects are framed” (Bermúdez, 2020, p. 38, italics added), even if both risk attitudes are independently rationally permitted. This diachronic approach assumes that it is irrational to change your preferences if you have no pro tanto reason for doing so. And the fact that the same option has been described differently cannot be such a reason.

However, there is a simple problem with empirically verifying both the standard synchronic and diachronic interpretations. The term “framing effect” is used in two different ways: sometimes to describe aggregate shifts in preferences across groups (as in the original ADP), and sometimes to describe within-subject reversals by the same individual under different frames. The charge of irrationality is strongest when individuals contradict themselves, and thus applies most forcefully to the latter. However, in the original version of the ADP, Tversky and Kahneman used a between-subjects design. Strictly speaking, no individual both preferred “A to B” and “D to C,” nor did any individual literally switch from risk-aversion to risk-seeking across frames. Still, the aggregate shifts observed in the ADP are typically taken to suggest that many individuals would have reversed their preferences had they been exposed to both frames, even though a between-subject design does not directly reveal individual reversals.

Therefore, it seems that the main charge of irrationality, if based on the original version of the ADP, must refer to a counterfactual account (either synchronic or diachronic). These two counterfactual versions rely on different empirical assumptions. The synchronic version treats preference reversals as latent dispositions that would manifest differently depending on how the choice is framed, whereas the diachronic version presupposes a hypothetical sequence of preference changes across time. While within-subject replications, in which participants received the two parts of the two framing questions either immediately after each other or with some delay (e.g., Stanovich & West, 1998; Aczel et al., 2018; Diederich et al., 2018), provide evidence that diachronic reversals can occur, synchronic susceptibility remains a mere inference from between-subject data. Mallon notices this shift (without approving it) from the between-subjects design to a synchronic counterfactual interpretation:While most framing effects are exhibited between subjects, the different responses in different conditions by different subjects are assumed to reflect how individuals would respond in considering either case alone, and such counterfactual individuals seem irrational for making different choices in the two cases (Mallon, 2016, p. 433; see also Rehren & Sinnott-Armstrong, 2021).

While most framing effects are exhibited between subjects, the different responses in different conditions by different subjects are assumed to reflect how individuals would respond in considering either case alone, and such counterfactual individuals seem irrational for making different choices in the two cases (Mallon, 2016, p. 433; see also Rehren & Sinnott-Armstrong, 2021).

However, the charge of irrationality or inconsistency carries less normative weight under counterfactual interpretation than under the standard approaches, because it relies only on hypothetical preference reversals rather than on actual, observable inconsistencies. Whereas the standard synchronic and diachronic readings purport to show that agents in fact contradict themselves, the counterfactual reading can only claim that they would have done so under a different description. Thus, the main discussions of the alleged irrationality of risky choice framing effects leave open the possibility that apparent reversals reflect responsiveness to different reasons rather than genuine irrationality.

Invariance and extensionality

Let’s go back to the question about which normative requirement is supposedly violated in ADP. Even if there is no numerical equivalence between options in the APD, the irrationality may consist in a violation of the principles of invariance and extensionality: if two descriptions pick out the same outcomes, then a rational agent must evaluate them in the same way. Therefore, before turning to our reasons-based alternative, it is important to examine these principles more closely, since whether A and C (or B and D) should be treated as equivalent is precisely what is at stake in deciding whether responses to the ADP are irrational. As several influential authors have emphasized, these principles are often treated as so basic that they are hardly articulated at all: Tversky and Kahneman (1986b, p. S253) describe invariance as “tacitly assumed,” while Arrow (1982) calls extensionality “a fundamental element of rationality, so elementary that we hardly notice it” (p. 6).

In logic, the principle of extensionality requires that two formulas with the same truth value under any truth assignment be mutually substitutable salva veritate in a sentence containing one of them. In Savage’s decision theory formulated within set theory, the axiom of extensionality is crucial: there cannot be two different sets with the same members. The set’s identity is fixed by its members and does not depend on how the set itself is described. This principle is closely connected with substitutivity, which states that if two names (or predicates) both refer to the same object (or property), they may be substituted for each other in a sentence without changing the truth value of that sentence.

Following (Grüne-Yanoff, 2016), we will understand extensionality as the principle based on logical equivalence, which is determined by the semantic characteristics of the explicit formulations only. In contrast, we will understand invariance as the principle based on a non-logical type of equivalence, that is, informational equivalence (Sher & McKenzie, 2006, p. 487).

Let’s take a sentence (1) Alvise Vivarini was a Venetian Renaissance painter. The painter used two different versions of his first name – sometimes, he was called Luigi Vivarini (and signed his different paintings with different versions of his first name). The principle of substitutivity assumes that in extensional contexts, the sentence cannot change its truth value, even if we substitute Luigi for Alvise. So, if (1) is true, this also must be true: (2) Luigi Vivarini was a Venetian Renaissance painter.

However, in intensional contexts, substitutivity may fail without implying any breakdown of the agent’s rationality. This occurs when the thinker/decision-maker is unaware of the relevant identities. Assume that (3) Anna believes that Alvise Vivarini was a Venetian Renaissance painter. However, she has no idea that the painter used a different version of his first name. Instead, she believes (4) that Luigi Vivarini was another person, say, a Florentine Renaissance painter. In this situation, Anna’s belief that Alvise Vivarini was a Venetian Renaissance painter does not commit her to believing that Luigi Vivarini was a Venetian Renaissance painter, even if the two names in fact refer to the same painter.

The same type of reasoning may be used for different psychological states than beliefs, particularly valuing or preferring. For example, after viewing some Renaissance paintings, it is true that (5) Anna values Luigi Vivarini’s paintings, but it is not true that (6) she values Alvise Vivarini’s paintings. Of course, it is not only possible, but it may be rational for Anna to value Luigi Vivarini’s paintings without valuing Alvise Vivarini’s paintings, provided that she does not know, and cannot deduce from the information she already has, that these two names refer to the same painter.

Let’s go back to the ADP. Suppose that Bernard belongs to a group of people who are susceptible to risky framing effects in the standard version of the ADP. On the one hand, it is true (7) that Bernard prefers the certain prospect of 200 being saved out of 600 to the uncertain prospect of a 1/3 probability of all 600 being saved and a 2/3 probability of none being saved. On the other hand, it is not true that (8) he prefers (or would have preferred, had he received a different frame) the certain prospect of 400 dying to the uncertain prospect of a 1/3 probability of none of the 600 people dying and a 2/3 probability of all 600 dying. Is this rationally acceptable, as it was in the case of Anna? Is he in the same position as Anna from the previous example, and may not know that having 200 people who may be saved (out of the population of 600) is the same as having 400 people who may die (out of the population of 600)? Of course, under the standard interpretation, the answer would be negative: it is rationally required for him to recognize that these two descriptions refer to the same expected events. Even Bermúdez (2020), who generally defends the view that there are many cases of rational risky choice framing effects, would not agree that the cases of Anna and Bernard are similar:A moment’s thought should be enough to convince him that the scenario in which 200 out of the 600 survive is exactly the same as the scenario in which 400 out of the 600 die (Bermúdez, 2020, p. 97).

A moment’s thought should be enough to convince him that the scenario in which 200 out of the 600 survive is exactly the same as the scenario in which 400 out of the 600 die (Bermúdez, 2020, p. 97).

Bermúdez assumes more generally that in this type of case, susceptibility to the experimental framing effects should disappear “when (rational) subjects have the relevant equivalence pointed out to them” (p. 98). Therefore, it is important to distinguish between (i) failure to recognize the alleged equivalence on one’s own and (ii) the persistence of framing effects even after the alleged equivalence has been explicitly pointed out. In the case of the ADP, the available evidence primarily supports only the former and weaker claim. As Kahneman and Tversky (1984) note, the failure to recognize that options A/C and B/D are two descriptions picking out the same outcomes is “pervasive and robust” (p. 343) even when respondents are presented with both problems in close succession:It is as common among sophisticated respondents as among naive ones, and it is not eliminated even when the same respondents answer both questions within a few minutes… In their stubborn appeal, framing effects resemble perceptual illusions more than computational errors… (Kahneman & Tversky, 1984, pp. 343–4).

It is as common among sophisticated respondents as among naive ones, and it is not eliminated even when the same respondents answer both questions within a few minutes… In their stubborn appeal, framing effects resemble perceptual illusions more than computational errors… (Kahneman & Tversky, 1984, pp. 343–4).

By contrast, the extent to which framing effects persist once equivalence is explicitly pointed out remains less clear, and some evidence suggests that making the relevant structure transparent can attenuate, and in some cases eliminate, the effect (e.g., Mandel, 2014). However, this does not undermine our central claim. Even when agents recognize that differently framed options are extensionally equivalent in terms of outcomes, this recognition does not, by itself, determine how the situation should be evaluated, since evaluation depends not only on outcomes but also on how morally relevant features of the situation are represented. Alternative descriptions may highlight distinct reason-giving aspects—for example, focusing attention on lives saved versus lives lost—which can guide moral assessment in different directions.

Bermúdez (2020) captures this possibility by appealing to “complex decision problem” cases in which subjects are well aware that there is a single outcome presented in two (or more) different ways and yet can rationally assign different values to that outcome depending on how it is framed. He describes ultra-intensional contexts as ones in which a rational agent might prefer an option under one description (X) to the same option under another description (Y), and prefer it under description Y to that same option under a third description (Z), even while recognizing that X and Z pick out the very same action or outcome. On this reading, the agent’s preference is not between numerically distinct outcomes but between ways of framing them, where different descriptions can make distinct reason-giving aspects salient. This raises the worry that if X and Z really are identical outcomes, it is unclear why an agent should ever distinguish them in preference rankings. Bermúdez’s point, however, is that rational sensitivity to frames can operate at the level of reasons made salient by descriptions, rather than only at the level of formally individuated outcomes. People see that X and Z are “different ways of framing the same action or outcome” (Bermúdez, 2022, p. 1). These situations are different from standard intensional contexts, in which the failure of substitutivity equivalent descriptions depends on a mere lack of knowledge. Although Bermúdez (2020, 2022) discusses ultra-intensional contexts in some highly stylized examples of Agamemnon and Macbeth, he mentions more morally relevant examples (i.e., abortion) in merely a few words. Instead, while discussing ultra-intensional contexts, he prefers to focus on “less dramatic types of rational framing effects” (Bermúdez, 2022, p. 1): mechanisms of self-control, game theory, and the resolution of interpersonal conflicts.

In contrast with his approach, we claim that the arguments Bermúdez (2020, 2022) put forward in favor of the rationality of some types of frame-dependent reasoning plausibly extend to ADP, which, in our interpretation, belongs to the class of “complex decision problems.” In particular, we will highlight that it is not true that in standard risky-choice paradigms like the ADP, “the alternative descriptions concern only a single dimension” (Fisher & Mandel, 2021, p. 7).

Non-standard interpretations of the ADP, reason-based choices, and morally valid reasons

Some non-standard interpretations of ADP aim to explain the characteristic pattern of choices in the original version of ADP by questioning the equivalence of options. For example, some argued that risky options B and D are equivalent, whereas safe options A and C are not because they were spelled out incompletely, e.g., Program A communicates that 200 people will be saved but not that 400 will not be saved (Kühberger & Tanner, 2010; Mandel, 2014). Moreover, some people may not “see” exact numbers in ADP: “They may think, e.g., that about 200 will be saved, or that 200 people or more will be saved, or that 200 will be saved immediately and more will be saved later” (Kühberger & Tanner, 2010, p. 316; Fisher, 2022). Moreover, some people may understand that Program A involves saving at least 200 people, and that means fewer than 400 might die. As Mandel (2014, p. 1193) puts it, “for most people, Options A and C… are descriptions of different events, and not merely re-descriptions of the same event.” Then, the fuzzy-trace (FT) model holds that thinking operates on intuitive, simplified information processing rather than exact numerical information. This approach assumes that some people may reduce quantitative information (i.e., 200 will be saved or 1/3 probability that 600 will be saved, and 2/3 probability that no one will be saved) into its qualitative gist. That is, they may understand that in program A, “some people will be saved,” whereas in Program B, “some people will be saved or no one will be saved” (Reyna & Brainerd, 1991). Even if one treats outcomes in ADP as logically or extensionally equivalent, they still may be interpreted differently by receivers because someone’s (e.g., speaker’s, researcher’s) decision to frame the information in one way rather than another provides additional information or nudges about communicative intent (Sher & McKenzie, 2006; other non-standard interpretations: explicated valence account (EVA; Tombu & Mandel, 2015) and query theory (Wall et al., 2020).

In contrast, we adopt a reason-based approach to risky choice framing. Contemporary philosophical discussions of reasons often distinguish between normative and motivating practical reasons (e.g., Alvarez, 2018). Explanations of intentional action are typically given in terms of the agent’s motivating reasons, and ascribing such reasons is usually sufficient to make the action intelligible. Our guiding question is how the characteristic pattern of choices in the ADP can be explained in terms of the reasons that count in favor of the available options. Building on earlier theoretical work by Schick (2003), Gold and List (2004), and especially Dietrich and List (2016), we treat options as bundles of properties and understand reasons as those properties that are motivationally salient in a given context. In this framework, choice is explained by a combination of a context-dependent salience function, which determines which properties an agent takes into account, and a ranking of these properties by importance. Framing effects arise because different descriptions of the same outcomes can highlight different properties—and thus different reason-giving aspects—thereby influencing which considerations guide an agent’s choice. We extend this framework to moral decision-making by focusing on two families of non-consequentialist reasons that are especially relevant in the ADP: considerations associated with the doing/allowing distinction and considerations of fairness (egalitarian reasons).

This perspective contrasts with standard explanations, which view decision-making as the maximization of value—typically by applying a value function to outcomes defined in terms of gains and losses relative to a reference point. While such accounts may help explain the aggregate pattern of responses in the ADP, they face a familiar explanatory limitation: even when a preference ordering can be ascribed that rationalizes observed choices, it remains unclear whether this constitutes a genuine explanation of those choices. Simply stating that an agent prefers one option to another does not explain why that option is chosen; a genuine explanation requires identifying the considerations in virtue of which it is preferred. From this perspective, the central question is why different descriptions of what is, in outcome terms, the same situation can make different considerations salient, thereby leading agents to favor one option in one frame but another in a different frame.

Our proposal is that descriptions can make distinct reasons salient, and that responsiveness to such reasons can sometimes be rational. Similarly to the path-dependence account of framing proposed by Gold and List (2004), which attributes such effects to underlying inconsistencies in the agent’s dispositions, and in line with Schick’s (2003) emphasis on the role of how agents represent a situation, our approach allows for context-sensitive but potentially rational variation in the considerations that guide choice. It thereby provides a unified way of understanding why agents may prefer A over B in one frame of the ADP but D over C in another, without treating such patterns as necessarily irrational.

This framework allows us to capture the idea that agents do not have fixed preferences over options but rather perceive them as bundles of motivationally salient properties (Dietrich & List, 2016). On this approach, framing effects—in particular effects observed in the ADP—arise when a decision-maker has multiple, and sometimes conflicting, reasons bearing on the available options. In effect, the decision process does not consist of searching for and then weighing all possible reasons but might rely on some heuristics, for example, acting on the most clearly or readily available reasons within some frame (Gigerenzer, 2021). However, this mechanism is not limited to simple heuristic processing, but may also involve sensitivity to morally relevant considerations. In the context of commodification arguments concerning the sale of sexual services, Gold (2019) emphasizes that framing can affect not only which reasons are salient but also which considerations agents treat as relevant for evaluation, thereby linking changes in choice to shifts in underlying valuation. Whether there is a violation of extensionality (and because of that of rationality) in the situations of the judgment reversals or shifts is not so obvious because different reasons under different frames co-define options, as they are seen by an agent. In other words, frames may influence how people “cut up the world” and individualize options. Whether different framings of some life-saving options are equivalent, including moral equivalency—that is, whether they include the same morally relevant factors—is a judgment that depends, among other things, on the agent’s moral views, which pick out some factors as relevant and other factors as irrelevant. In particular, whether two options are “effectively identical,” “indistinguishable in real terms,” or just “the same” depends on which moral factors someone treats as relevant. Thus, the criterion of extensionality as a guide to evaluating the agent’s rationality and the very “sameness” of options may only be applied after establishing what an agent cares about and for which reasons she is reactive. As stated by Bermúdez (2020): “decision problems are typically constructed, not given.” (p. 120).

A well-known illustration of this mechanism is provided by Shafir et al. (1993), who presented participants with a vignette concerning an only-child custody decision following a divorce. One group of participants was asked which parent should be awarded custody, while another group was asked which parent should be denied custody. The parents were described by short sets of characteristics intended to provide reasons for the decision (e.g., average income vs. above-average income; reasonable rapport with the child vs. a very close relationship with the child, etc.). According to the standard model, these two frames (“award” and “deny”) should, from the standpoint of rational choice, yield identical responses, since awarding custody to one parent is logically equivalent to denying it to the other. However, participants’ responses differed systematically across the two conditions. As the authors note:

These findings, which are puzzling from the perspective of value maximization, are easier to interpret if we assume that different frames and elicitation procedures highlight different aspects of the options and thus bring forth different reasons to guide decision-making (Shafir et al., 1993, p. 13).

This pattern extends to explicitly moral judgments. In a related line of research, Koralus and Alfano (2017) show that moral judgments about what is obligatory and what is impermissible exhibit a similar pattern consistent with reason-based choice. Participants were presented with scenarios involving two mutually exclusive and exhaustive courses of action and were asked which was morally obligatory or which was morally impermissible. According to the standard model, these two ways of framing the question should yield equivalent responses, under the assumption that the options are mutually exclusive and exhaustive and that no neutral alternative is available, so that judging one option obligatory amounts to rejecting the other as impermissible. However, the authors find systematic discrepancies between these conditions, suggesting that moral judgments, like ordinary choices, can vary depending on how the decision problem is framed.

These findings generalize beyond specific experimental paradigms. A broader body of empirical research shows that framing effects are sensitive to contextual and psychological factors, including how decision-makers interpret ambiguity and fairness-related aspects of a situation. In particular, Mai et al. (2020) show that fairness considerations can serve as social cues that shape how risky options are interpreted across different frames, thereby influencing choice. Similarly, other studies of the ADP indicate that framing effects are robust but modulated by task-related and contextual variables (e.g., Diederich et al., 2018), suggesting that they depend not only on outcome structure but also on how decision-makers construe the situation. Taken together, this line of work supports the view that framing effects are not merely driven by changes in outcome valuation, but also involve shifts in the salience of different evaluative considerations. This empirical picture aligns with a broader philosophical account of practical reasoning in terms of reasons.

Risky choice framing effects and skepticism about moral intuitions

Some scholars who discussed risky choice framing effects in the context of life-saving decisions drew far-reaching conclusions from the alleged irrationality of frame-based decision-making. They claimed that framing effects shape our moral intuitions and judgments, even when the differences in wording are “morally irrelevant.” This interpretation was supported by Kahneman in his Thinking Fast and Slow (2011), where he suggested that the framing effect may influence moral intuitions, which are frame-dependent:Your moral feelings are attached to frames, to descriptions of reality rather than to reality itself… Our preferences are about framed problems, and our moral intuitions are about descriptions, not about substance (Kahneman, 2011, p. 361).

Your moral feelings are attached to frames, to descriptions of reality rather than to reality itself… Our preferences are about framed problems, and our moral intuitions are about descriptions, not about substance (Kahneman, 2011, p. 361).

Under this interpretation, different framings evoke “moral feelings” that hinder access to “the reality” and thus distort our moral intuitions that concern frames, not “the reality” itself. Thus, under this framework, there is no place for any moral reasons that would evaluate the consequences of decisions (“the reality”) through how they have been realized. This is particularly evident when we consider Kahneman’s other remarks on moral issues. For example, when asked about two classic versions of the trolley problem (pushing the fat man in front of the trolley and diverting the trolley onto the man on the side track), he replied: “I find it hard to believe that the two cases differ in morally relevant ways” (Voorhoeve, 2009, p. 80). On this approach, any non-consequentialist reasons—such as the fact that some respondents evaluate ostensibly identical life-saving outcomes differently because they are sensitive to factors like intentions or fairness—are treated not as genuine reasons, but as manifestations of cognitive bias or irrationality (for critical discussion, see Kamm, 1998; Colombo, 2018).

Similarly, Horowitz (1998) argued that people’s different responses to doing harm and allowing harm to occur, which are justified by the Doctrine of Doing and Allowing (DDA), may be fully explained by agents’ sensitivity to framing, together with different risk attitudes toward losses and gains. The distinction between doing and allowing states that a common intuition that reasons against causing harm are more stringent than those against allowing harm is generally correct (Bennett, 1995; Foot, 1984; Quinn, 1989; Woollard, 2015). The reasons are more stringent in the sense that they constrain agents: it is more challenging to justify harming than allowing harm to occur, and they require more from agents after harm has occurred than from agents who merely allowed harm to occur (also in terms of expected compensation). Since doing harm is typically associated with harming, whereas allowing harm corresponds to not aiding, Horowitz (1998) suggests that the apparent moral significance of this distinction may be explained by the gain–loss asymmetry posited by Prospect Theory. Therefore, under this interpretation, the DDA is just a post hoc rationalization of “judgments that are driven by non-ethical psychological mechanisms, and manipulated by ethically arbitrary variations of framing” (Dreisbach & Guevara, 2019, p. 618). As we see, the PT has been used to question the authority and relevance of the most widely discussed non-consequentialist principles (like DDA) developed by philosophers to account for morally valid reasons. As such, PT added fuel to the new wave of skepticism coming from social psychology about the role of non-consequentialist moral judgments.

However, the harming/not-aiding distinction cannot be reduced to the loss/no-gain distinction characteristic of the PT (Kamm, 1998). The two distinctions track different features of a situation. The loss/no-gain distinction concerns only the outcome for the affected individual—namely, whether the individual ends up worse off or merely fails to benefit. By contrast, the harming/not-aiding distinction concerns not only what happens to the individual, but also the role of the agent, in particular, whether the agent actively brings about a negative outcome or merely fails to prevent it.

This difference has important implications. Losses that are brought about by an agent may be evaluated differently from losses that occur due to natural causes or inaction. Ignoring this distinction would lead the followers of the PT to predict that people should be as disturbed at deciding not to prevent a healthy person from losing their life as at causing her to lose a life (e.g., by directly killing her). However, this prediction is not borne out by ordinary moral judgments.

More generally, a growing body of work in moral psychology suggests that normative considerations can shape not only evaluative judgments but also more basic aspects of cognition. For example, Knobe (2010) argues that moral factors systematically influence judgments of intention, causation, and action individuation, as illustrated by his well-known findings that harmful side effects are more likely to be judged intentional than beneficial ones, even when the agent’s mental states are held constant. Such results challenge the view that these judgments are formed through purely value-neutral processes and instead support the idea that normative considerations can play a constitutive role in how situations are represented. This perspective provides further motivation for a reason-based approach to framing effects, according to which different descriptions may make different considerations salient and thereby guide choice.

In what follows, we adopt Bermúdez’s normative stance—that rational agents may respond to reasons made salient by descriptions despite outcome equivalence—and then instantiate it in the ADP using Dreisbach and Guevara’s (2019) reason catalog (the distinction between doing and allowing, as well as fairness in uncertain prospects).

Doing and allowing

Dreisbach and Guevara (2019) argue that the common pattern of responses to the Asian Disease Problem “fit nicely with the idea that they were influenced by consistent, principled, and fairly standard non-consequentialist ethical considerations, including considerations in the neighborhood of Doing and Allowing” (p. 614). They assume that changes in wording under different frames are not arbitrary or insignificant, as it is commonly taken, and this assumption is “a fundamental error of the prospect theoretic account” (p. 618).

How does DDA explain the characteristic pattern of choices in the ADP? Initially, Dreisbach and Guevara (2019) consider a step similar to that proposed by prospect theory: the reference point or baseline. The choice between A and B is formulated in terms of “people will be saved,” so the reference point is 600 people dead (although, precisely speaking, they are “almost dead”). The choice between C and D is formulated in terms of “people will die,” so the reference point is 600 people alive. However, the authors treat this assumption as provisional and later drop it (pp. 633–634), maintaining that baselines play no substantive role in explaining the ADP: “harm or benefit need not be a loss or gain from a baseline” (p. 643). Instead, they emphasize that the frames highlight distinct moral reasons, such as fairness and the distinction between doing and allowing. Our discussion below follows this emphasis.

Therefore, Dreisbach and Guevara (2019) argue that the positive frame (between A and B) resembles a choice about the distribution of benefits, since people assume the harm has already been done. Under this interpretation, choosing Program A over B stems from the desire to actively save 200 people (“doing something”), causing them to stay alive, while merely allowing the other 400 people to die. They expect that many (although Dreisbach and Guevara do not specify how many) respondents choosing A in the standard versions of the ADP who are motivated by non-consequentialist ethical considerations just want to manifest their agency and understand that this option makes them an important part of a causal process leading to saving many people. In turn, many such respondents avoid Program B because they want to avoid manifesting their agency in a gamble with these 200 people who can be actively saved by choosing Program A. Option B does not look very attractive from this perspective because the most probable result of choosing B is conceptualized under this approach as “doing” something that may result in 600 people being dead.

In contrast, the second choice between C and D, according to this interpretation, may be understood as the distribution of harms, because this frame suggests that nothing wrong has yet happened – people are still alive and fine. If implementing Program C involves a certain negative effect (“400 people will die”), many participants motivated by non-consequentialist ethical considerations may assume that this effect results from the program’s implementation itself, even if it involves using some “harmful means.” So, many people may think that death is not a result of the “Asian disease” itself, but as Dreisbach and Guevara (2019) suggest “innocents will die because of something lethal about the intervention itself” (p. 619). They even speculate that Program C may refer to “an experimental vaccine whose side-effects will immediately bring about the death of most, even while it works as hoped for on the rest” (p. 619), even though there is no reference to any vaccine in the original version of ADP. Additionally, unlike in the previous case, under the negative framing, the respondents may not want to manifest their own agency because they may understand that option C makes them an important part of a causal process leading to the certain death of many people. In contrast, option D involves merely putting the living people at risk of dying. Consequently, many respondents prefer avoiding Program C because they prefer gambling in such cases, that is, they prefer to avoid manifesting their agency, allowing a “chance to decide their fate.”

Before turning to the experimental results, it is important to clarify how we understand the relation between choice and preference in the present context. We do not assume that observed choices directly reveal stable, all-things-considered preferences. Rather, in line with reason-based and constructive accounts of decision-making, we treat choices as context-sensitive expressions of evaluative judgments that depend on which considerations are made salient in a given frame. Accordingly, our analysis does not aim to recover fixed underlying preferences, but to examine how different evaluative dimensions contribute to choice under alternative descriptions (and therefore this approach may be consistent with these approaches that refer to incomplete preferences to explain framing effects, see Pettigrew, 2022).

Doing and allowing: experimental study

Assuming a reason-based approach according to which respondents perceive options as bundles of motivationally salient properties, and drawing on Dreisbach and Guevara’s (2019) hypotheses about the relevance of moral reasons in the ADP, we conducted a new study to investigate how participants understand morally valid reasons for and against different choices in the ADP (for technical details, see the online Supplement; a preliminary version of some of these results was presented in Żuradzki (2022). Dreisbach and Guevara’s (2019) hypotheses predict that the positive frame (A and B) should be treated more frequently as the distribution of benefits than the negative frame (C and D). Conversely, the negative frame (C and D) should be treated more frequently than the positive frame (A and B) in terms of the distribution of harms (or at least risks of harm).

The study was conducted online with 158 participants in Poland who completed the study in Polish (after exclusions). We employed a within-subject design in which all participants evaluated all four ADP Programs (A–D). Participants were first presented with the full set of programs simultaneously, and subsequently evaluated each program separately. Participants evaluated each program on five dimensions, four of which were related to doing/allowing harm and one with fairness, using 7-point Likert scales (from “totally disagree” to “totally agree”), and whether each program should be implemented.

Participants in our study first read the ADP description, then were presented with all four programs simultaneously. Previous studies demonstrated that the framing effect replicates only in a limited scope when participants jointly evaluate all four options (in the study by Kühberger and Gradl (2013), the framing effect is primarily driven by changes in the evaluation of the riskless options (A and C), but not in the risky ones (B and D). Despite this, we assumed that this way of presentation is useful to make a first step toward empirical testing of the Dreisbach and Guevara’s (2019) hypotheses about non-consequentialist ethical reasons relevant in the context of the ADP, namely, that “will die” language disposes respondents to be guided by the ethics of harm, whereas “will be saved” language disposes them to be guided by the ethics of benefits (p. 643). In particular, we assumed that the presence of all options should not influence non-consequentialist considerations relative to the standard between-subjects design.

Therefore, in the next step, participants saw each program separately and were asked to evaluate whether “This program will benefit many people,” “This program will save many people,” “This program will harm many people,” and “This program will kill many people.” We treated agreement with the first two sentences as evidence that the program is interpreted as the distribution of benefits, and the positive answers to the last two sentences as evidence that the program is interpreted as the distribution of harms, as assumed by Dreisbach and Guevara’s (2019). To assess differences between programs, we conducted repeated-measures analyses of variance. In addition, to examine which evaluative dimensions predict implementation choices, we estimated mixed-effects regression models with evaluations as predictors and participant-level random intercepts.

First of all, the results of the study corroborate the Dreisbach and Guevara (2019) hypothesis that in the standard version of the ADP, the positive frame (i.e., options A and B in which “people will be saved”) is more likely to be interpreted as the distribution of benefits than the negative frame (i.e., options C and D in which “people will die”). A and B were treated as almost equally beneficial: the means for “This program will benefit many people” were very similar (respectively M = 3.89 and M = 3.67), and higher than C and D (respectively M = 2.86 and M = 3.22). Participants were more likely to evaluate Program A as benefiting many people than Programs C (p < .001) and D (p < .001). Program B revealed the same pattern (p < .001, p = .008, respectively). Moreover, A and B were evaluated as more life-saving than Programs C and D (respectively, A: M = 3.83, B: M = 3.77, C: M = 3.04, D: M = 3.34). Program A was evaluated as saving more lives than Program C (p < .001) and Program D (p = .006). Program B revealed the same pattern (p < .001, p = .018, respectively).

However, our data do not corroborate the other hypothesis by Dreisbach and Guevara (2019) that the negative frame (C and D) is more likely to be interpreted as a choice between distributions of harms than the positive frame (A and B). Although both C and D were evaluated as slightly more harmful than both A and B (respectively A: M = 4.20; B: M = 4.20; C: M = 4.63; D: M = 4.32), these differences were not significant.

Even more problematic were patterns observed in the case of ‘killing’. Although Program C was evaluated as involving more killing than Program B (p = .049), respondents did not evaluate Program D as involving more killing than either Program A or Program B (A: M = 4.51; B: M = 4.21; C: M = 4.68; D: M = 4.42), whereas, according to Dreisbach and Guevara’s (2019) hypothesis, Program D should be clearly treated as involving more killing than both positively framed options.

Fairness

The second non-consequentialist dimension to which Dreisbach and Guevara (2019) refer are egalitarian reasons, that is, fairness and equity. They consider which options in the ADP would show “equal respect” for everyone. Egalitarianism is a major theory of distributive justice, which, in standard contexts, assumes that inequalities are bad because they undermine individuals’ ability to live as equal citizens and may lead to domination. In the context of the ADP, egalitarian reasons would state that it is in itself bad if some people die but others do not (through no fault of their own). However, egalitarianism is a multidimensional theory, and the main distinction relevant from our perspective concerns the equality of chances (ex ante egalitarianism) and equality of outcomes (ex post egalitarianism). Programs B and D, which involve a 1/3 probability that all 600 people will be saved (i.e., no one will die) and a 2/3 probability that no one will be saved (i.e., all 600 people will die), are egalitarian in both senses. Not only does every individual have an ex ante equal chance of survival (1/3), but there is also full equality ex post: everyone is either alive or dead. In turn, Programs A and C (200 people will be saved [400 people will die]) are not fully egalitarian. Even if one understands these two options as cases of random distribution of survival (so again, every individual has ex ante equal chance for survival, i.e., 1/3), there is inevitable ex post inequality (some will be alive and some dead) (see Table 1).

Neglecting the multidimensional nature of egalitarianism may lead to some misunderstandings. For example, Dreisbach and Guevara (2019) assume that there are stronger egalitarian reasons that speak for option A (and against B) in positive framing and for option D (and against C) in negative framing. On the one hand, they claimed that “it is a questionable egalitarianism that opts for B over A since B makes it so likely that the benefit will fail to be bestowed at all,” adding that “there would be no unfairness or inequity in the treatment of the other 400” in option A (p. 622). On the other hand, in negative framing, Dreisbach and Guevara (2019) reverse their assumption and claim that “C would not… show everyone the same sort of equal respect as D. For the randomness does not change the fact that C’s implementation ensures the death of most of the innocents” (p. 620). Consequently, they argue that egalitarian reasons may explain the common pattern of choices in the ADP, but their explanation ignores the very distinction between ex ante and ex post egalitarianism. Moreover, contrary to what they claim, egalitarian reasons do not concern whether “the benefit will or will fail to be bestowed at all” (p. 622), but rather whether they will be or will fail to be “bestowed to all.”

Fairness: experimental study

As in the case of the distinction between doing and allowing, we checked whether participants who were presented with all four programs simultaneously would differ in their evaluation of the programs in terms of fairness, as predicted by Dreisbach and Guevara’s (2019) interpretation of the ADP. The details of the experiment was described above in Sect. 3.2.1. Notably, in addition to the four questions already mentioned, participants were asked whether “This program is fair to each of the 600 people” (for details, see the online Supplement). The means for consecutive programs were A: M = 2.87; B: M = 3.60; C: M = 2.77; D: M = 3.16. This results suggest that Program B was evaluated as the fairest to all 600 people, compared with Programs A (p < .001), C (p < .001), and D (p = .007). Thus, our data do not support Dreisbach and Guevara’s (2019) hypothesis that people treat Program A as more egalitarian than Program B, a hypothesis that overlooks the multidimensional nature of egalitarianism discussed above. In contrast, the results partially support our interpretation (specifically with respect to Programs A and B), according to which probabilistic options should be perceived as more egalitarian than certain options because they exhibit equality in both the ex ante and ex post dimensions.

Moreover, in answering a general question about which program should be implemented, Program B was evaluated as the one participants wanted to implement most, compared with Program C (p < .001) and D (p = .049). Program C was evaluated as the one participants wanted to implement less than Program A (p = .019). The means for consecutive Programs were: A: M = 3.29; B: M = 3.63; C: M = 2.90; D: M = 3.26.

Importantly, we explored what predicts the answer to the question of whether a given program should be implemented. The analyses were conducted in R environment (R Core Team, 2022), using package lme4 (Bates et al., 2015). In a multilevel model, in which responses were nested within participants, each evaluation of a program that is, as benefiting many people, harming many people, killing many people, saving many people, and being fair to each person, was treated as both a within-person and between-person predictor of implementation judgments. Controlling for program type, fairness evaluation predicted positively the answer that a program should be implemented (unstandardized bwithin = 0.35, t(474) = 10.79, p < .001; unstandardized bbetween = 0.38, t(158) = 7.12, p < .001). In standardized terms, obtained with the package parameters (Lüdecke et al., 2020), fairness was the strongest predictor of the belief that the program should be implemented in the conventional mixed-effects model, considering simultaneously within-person, β = 0.31, 95% CI [0.26, 0.37], and between-person effects, β = 0.51, 95% CI [0.37, 0.65]. The evaluation of programs as saving people was also strongly related to the outcome (βwithin = 0.30, 95% CI [0.22, 0.37] and βbetween = 0.28, 95% CI [0.02, 0.55]). In addition, the repeated-measures ANOVA showed that perceived fairness differed meaningfully across programs, partial η² = 0.07. These results suggest that participants’ judgments were not driven merely by whether a program was described in terms of gains or losses, but also by whether it was perceived as fair, which appear to have played an important role in differentiating the programs. In this respect, the results support the claim that fairness-related considerations play a substantively meaningful role in ADP-type dilemmas (the analyses are described in detail in the online Supplement).

Taken together, these findings clarify how the experimental results contribute to the paper’s overall argument. They provide empirical support for the claim that framing effects in the ADP are not driven solely by differences in outcome valuation, but also by the salience of distinct evaluative considerations that are not captured by standard gain–loss representations. In particular, the strong predictive role of fairness indicates that participants’ choices are systematically shaped by how different aspects of the situation are made salient under alternative descriptions. This supports a reason-based interpretation of framing effects, according to which shifts in choice reflect changes in the considerations agents take to be relevant, rather than merely changes in how outcomes are valued. Importantly, our aim is not to completely replace outcome-based accounts, but to show that they leave out a systematic and empirically detectable dimension of evaluation.

Limitations

One limitation of our experimental studies is that participants evaluated all four ADP options simultaneously, whereas in the standard between-subjects design, each participant is exposed to only one frame (A/B or C/D). In our study, option B was the most highly evaluated in terms of fairness; it was also perceived as relatively beneficial, and saving many people. It is therefore possible that considerations such as benefits and fairness become salient primarily in the simultaneous presentation format, and may play a less prominent role when only one frame is shown. While our results demonstrate that such reasons can influence choices when multiple frames are available, further research is needed to determine whether similar effects occur under the more restrictive conditions of the original ADP design. Future studies could address this limitation by directly comparing simultaneous and separate presentations within a unified modeling framework, for example, by jointly modeling outcome-based valuation and the salience of normatively structured considerations, and by assessing their relative explanatory and predictive power across different presentation formats.

Finally, the experimental part of our research has some limitations stemming from the generality of the Dreisbach and Guevara (2019) hypotheses. Although they worry “whether our own account will hold up under further empirical testing” (p. 615), they do not formulate clear predictions (for example, about the proportion of people who can be susceptible to non-consequentialist reasons in the ADP). Therefore, it is not clear which proportion of responses should be treated as evidence in favor of or against their hypotheses. However, given that they aim to present an alternative explanation to the prospect theory, one can assume that the non-consequentialist reasons they mention should explain the different evaluations of options that lead to the preference change in the ADP. Our study revealed important limitations of such an explanation.

Conclusions

Our paper introduces a novel approach to studying the Asian Disease Problem by examining the underlying reasons behind decision-making. We propose that framing effects, such as those observed in the ADP, arise when decision-makers face conflicting reasons for different options. Identifying the relevant reasons in a given choice situation is challenging. We explored the extent to which non-consequentialist moral reasons, particularly those related to the doing/allowing distinction and fairness, can explain and justify choices in “sacrificial dilemmas” like the ADP. Our findings provide a nuanced perspective on this issue.

Firstly, we confirmed that in the standard version of the ADP, choices framed positively (i.e., options A and B where ‘people will be saved’) are often perceived as decisions about the distribution of benefits. Secondly, we observed that fairness evaluations consistently and positively predicted which option was deemed most appropriate. This supports the hypothesis that non-consequentialist moral reasons are crucial in explaining the typical choice patterns in the ADP.

More generally, our findings can be understood as extending a broader line of criticism of the heuristics-and-biases program, according to which many alleged cognitive errors arise from “confusing intelligent inferences with logical errors” (Gigerenzer, 2018, p. 328). In particular, our results support the view that principles such as invariance and extensionality, while normatively appealing in formal models, provide a limited guide to how agents navigate complex and uncertain environments. Human cognition is not confined to the information explicitly given, but routinely goes beyond it, relying on context-sensitive inferences that are often adaptive rather than erroneous.

From this perspective, framing effects need not be understood solely as distortions. Instead, they can reflect the fact that different descriptions make different aspects of a situation salient, thereby activating distinct considerations relevant to decision-making. In particular, the assumption that logically equivalent frames are informationally equivalent is often unwarranted: different descriptions can highlight distinct aspects of a situation and thereby legitimately guide judgment and choice. This interpretation is supported by recent work on nudging, suggesting that contextual manipulations often operate by providing agents with additional reasons—whether or not these are explicitly recognized —and that framing can function as a form of implicit recommendation or communication (Levy, 2019). If setting defaults or framing options has a communicative component, there is not only nothing irrational about responding to framing, but this may be a sign of an intelligent social inference.

Finally, our account thus departs from standard economics models of decision-making that treat choice as the maximization of a single value function. Such models abstract away from the role of practical reasoning, in which agents deliberate about what to do in light of competing considerations. More broadly, these findings suggest that debates about the rationality of framing effects should move beyond questions of consistency and incorporate the role of normatively structured evaluation in shaping choice.
