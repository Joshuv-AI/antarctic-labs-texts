Long-term prognostic effect of serum albumin concentration in diabetic patients with stable coronary artery disease: A multicenter cohort study
Authors: Ting-Wei Lu, Shih-Chieh Chien, Hsin-Bang Leu, Wei-Hsian Yin, Wei-Kung Tseng, Yen-Wen Wu, Tsung-Hsien Lin, Kuan-Cheng Chang, Ji-Hung Wang, Chau-Chung Wu, Hung-I Yeh, Jaw-Wen Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Diabetes and insulin resistance alter the physiological state of serum albumin (SA), which is a prognostic marker for stable coronary artery disease (CAD). However, whether the SA concentration is associated with long-term cardiovascular (CV) outcomes in diabetic patients with stable CAD remains unclear.Methods:In total, 1148 patients were retrospectively identified from a nationwide multicenter cohort study on patients with stable CAD. They were categorized into four groups according to their diabetes mellitus (DM) status and SA concentration (cutoff: 4 g/dL).Results:The patients’ mean age was 62.5 years, and 83.5% were male. Of the total patients, 405 were included in group 1 (SA ≥4/non-DM), 322 in group 2 (SA <4/non-DM), 201 in group 3 (SA ≥4/DM), and 220 in group 4 (SA <4/DM). Group 4 had the oldest age and a higher prevalence of prior myocardial infarction and stroke. During the median 4.5-year follow-up (interquartile range: 1.5-6.7 years), the highest and lowest survival rates in terms of all-cause and CV mortality were found in groups 1 and 4, respectively. However, no prognostic differences were noted in nonfatal stroke and myocardial infarction among the groups. The data were consistent after covariate adjustment. Using group 1 as the reference, hazard ratio (HRs) (95% CIs) for all-cause mortality in groups 2, 3, and 4 were 3.64 (1.22-10.83), 3.26 (0.95-11.33), and 5.74 (1.92-16.95), respectively, and those for CV mortality were 2.8 (0.57-13.67), 2.62 (0.40-17.28), and 6.15 (1.32-28.58), respectively.Conclusion:In diabetic patients with stable CAD, a low SA concentration (<4 g/dL) was associated with increased long-term mortality regardless of all-cause or CV reasons but not nonfatal CV events.Keywords: Cardiovascular event, Coronary artery disease, Mortality, Serum albumin

1. INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus (DM) affects more than 400 million people worldwide and is significantly associated with cardiovascular (CV) disease and mortality.1–3 This chronic metabolic disorder is fundamentally caused by deficient insulin secretion and tissue insulin resistance, resulting in glucose and lipid dysregulation. Oxidative stress and inflammatory processes are triggered and promoted by the excessive accumulation of adipocytes and advanced glycation end products.4,5 Consequently, diabetic patients exhibit complex nutritional and inflammatory states.

Serum albumin (SA), a marker of nutritional and inflammatory states, is a major circulating protein that plays several vital physiological functions.6 Many studies, including our previous study on patients with stable coronary artery disease (CAD), have revealed that the SA concentration is inversely correlated with CV events and mortality in diverse populations.7–9 However, the long-term prognostic data of the diabetic population with stable CAD are insufficient. Moreover, the role of SA in diabetic patients remains unclear. A higher concentration of SA may reflect overnutrition. However, diabetes itself could decrease the innate synthesis of SA through the downregulation of mRNA expression and chronic inflammation.10–12 Furthermore, some studies have suggested that the SA concentration is positively correlated with insulin resistance.13,14 These findings challenge the notion regarding the detrimental effects of low SA concentrations.

We used the complete dataset of the Taiwan Biosignatures study to evaluate the long-term prognostic value of the SA concentration in diabetic patients with stable CAD.

2. METHODS

2.1. Study population

This study was a subanalysis of the Taiwan Biosignatures study.7,15 In brief, the Biosignatures study was a multicenter nationwide prospective cohort study conducted to identify risk factors among patients with CAD in a stable condition at baseline. Eventually, 10 medical centers participated in this study, and eligible patients were enrolled from October 2012 to September 2019. Patients with a history of significant CAD, as evidenced by a coronary angiogram; a history of myocardial infarction (MI), as evidenced by 12-lead electrocardiography or hospitalization; or a history of angina, as evidenced by ischemic electrocardiographic changes or a positive response to a stress test, were identified. Of these, patients who had received a successful percutaneous coronary intervention with either coronary stenting or balloon angioplasty at least once previously and had been stable on medical treatment for at least 1 month were enrolled. Patients were excluded if (1) they had been hospitalized for unstable angina, acute coronary syndrome, acute MI, acute cerebrovascular events, or other acute CV events within 3 months before enrollment; (2) they planned to receive further coronary revascularization or interventional procedures for other CV diseases during the subsequent 1-year period; (3) they had significant malignancy or tumors requiring advanced medical or surgical therapy or both in the subsequent year; (4) they had other major systemic diseases requiring hospitalization or surgery in the subsequent year; or (5) they were unable or were unwilling to be followed up during the subsequent 1-year period. Moreover, patients with a life expectancy of <6 months (such as those with malignant metastatic neoplasm) and those receiving treatment with immunosuppressive agents were excluded. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the appropriate health authorities, independent ethics committees, and independent review boards (IRBs) at each hospital, as well as the Joint IRB Ethics Committee Review Board in Taiwan.

2.2. SA concentration and baseline data collection

After enrollment, the patients’ baseline characteristics (including risk factors, such as a history of hypertension or DM and smoking habits) and medication history were recorded. Variable medication and dosage data were collected by chart review and using a structured questionnaire. Biochemical profiles, including blood glucose levels and lipid levels (total cholesterol, triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels), and kidney function were recorded. The patients’ left ventricular ejection fractions, measured by echocardiography, nuclear imaging, or angiography, were also recorded. SA concentrations were determined at enrollment. All patients were categorized according to their DM status (DM vs non-DM) and SA concentration (mean value as cutoff value: <4 g/dL vs ≥4 g/dL; interquartile range (IQR): 3.7-4.3 g/dL).

2.3. Clinical follow-up for adverse events

Each patient who was initially stable under medical treatment was prospectively and regularly followed up. After patient enrollment, their follow-up data were collected at outpatient clinic visits approximately every 3 months in the first year and approximately every 6 months from the second year after enrollment. Late outcomes were derived from the Health and Welfare Data Science Center after January 2020, when all patients were completely enrolled (project number: H108227).

The primary outcome of this study was all-cause mortality. The secondary outcome was the first occurrence of any CV event, nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, or CV death.

2.4. Statistical methods

Continuous variables are expressed as means ± SDs. One-way analysis of variance was performed to assess the differences in continuous variables among the four clinical categories (SA concentration of <4 vs ≥4 g/dL and DM vs non-DM; four groups) (Table 1). Categorical variables were assessed using chi-squared or Fisher exact tests and are expressed as counts and percentages. The primary and secondary outcomes were assessed as an overall percentage; they are expressed as proportions with 95% CIs. The event-free survival rate was calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with significance assessed using log-rank tests. In the Cox proportional hazards regression models, the hazard ratio (HR) was calculated along with the 95% CI, and p value. Multivariable analyses were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, glutamate pyruvate transaminase (GPT) level, total cholesterol level, triglyceride level, low-density lipoprotein level, heart failure, malignancy, and estimated glomerular filtration rate. During the review processes, the potential impacts of inflammation were raised. C-reactive protein (CRP) values were available in 450 subjects using storage blood samples. Thus, we did additional hazard regression models with adjustment of CRP and aforementioned factors to understand the potential impacts of inflammation. All p values are expressed up to three decimals, whereas those below 0.001 are expressed as p < 0.001. In all the tests, the two-tailed alpha significance level was 0.05.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Baseline patient characteristics

Of the total database of 2062 patients, 1148 patients had sufficient data for analysis. Stratification by the SA concentration and DM status revealed that 405 (35.3%) patients were nondiabetic and had SA concentrations of ≥4 g/dL (group 1), 322 (28%) were nondiabetic and had SA concentrations of <4 g/dL (group 2), 201 (17.5%) were diabetic and had SA concentrations of ≥4 g/dL (group 3), and 220 (19.2%) were diabetic and had SA concentrations of <4 g/dL (group 4). The patients’ mean age was 62.5 ± 12 years, and 83.5% were male. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the patients. The patients in group 4 were the oldest. Moreover, they had the highest white blood cell count, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level, and prevalence of previous MI and stroke but the lowest alcohol consumption, Hb level, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and SA concentration. No significant differences were noted in the patients’ sex, active smoking status, GPT level, and use of beta-blockers and statins as medications among the four groups.

3.2. Mortality and CV outcomes

During the median 4.5-year follow-up (IQR: 1.5-6.7 years), 54 (4.7%) all-cause deaths and 301 (26.2%) total CV events were noted, including 29 (2.5%) nonfatal acute MIs, 16 (1.4%) nonfatal strokes, and 26 (2.3%) deaths resulting from CV causes. Moreover, 28 deaths were categorized as resulting from non-CV causes (Table 2). Regarding all-cause mortality, total CV events, and CV mortality, the patients in group 1 had the lowest probability, followed by those in group 2 and group 3, whereas those in group 4 had the highest probability. The all-cause mortality, total CV event, and CV mortality rates were 1.0%, 20.3%, and 0.49% in group 1; 5.9%, 25.8%, and 2.5% in group 2; 4.5%, 29.4%, and 2.0% in group 3; and 10%, 35%, and 5.5% in group 4, respectively. No differences were noted in the outcomes of nonfatal MI and nonfatal stroke across the groups (p > 0.1). Kaplan-Meier estimates of the outcomes are shown in Fig. 1, with similar findings. The patients in group 4 consistently had significantly lower event-free survival rates in terms of total CV events (Fig. 1A), all-cause mortality (Fig. 1B), and CV mortality (Fig. 1C) (log-rank p < 0.001 for all). Full-size figures for each endpoint with numbers at risk were attached in Supplementary Figures 1-5, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A280. In the adjusted multivariate Cox proportional hazards models using group 1 as the reference (Table 3), the HRs (95% CIs) for all-cause mortality, total CV events, and CV mortality were 3.64 (1.22-10.83), 1.25 (0.91-1.72), and 2.8 (0.57-13.67) in group 2; 3.26 (0.95-11.33), 1.49 (1.04-2.13), and 2.62 (0.4-17.28) in group 3; and 5.74 (1.92-16.95), 1.62 (1.16-2.27), and 6.15 (1.32-28.58) in group 4, respectively. The intergroup p values for adjusted HRs are shown in Fig. 2. The HRs for nonfatal MI and stroke did not differ significantly among the four groups. In subgroup analysis of 450 patients with CRP value, data showed similar trend that diabetic patient with low SA concentration had highest mortality and CV mortality (Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A281).

4. DISCUSSION

In this multicenter study on a stable CAD cohort, patients with DM and low SA concentrations (<4 g/dL) were independently associated with an increased long-term risk of all-cause and CV mortality. However, the incidence rates of nonfatal CV outcomes did not differ among the groups, regardless of their SA concentration and DM status.

Albumin, synthesized in the liver, is the most abundant circulating protein that regulates several vital physiological functions. The SA concentration is influenced by various factors, such as the synthesis rate, metabolic catabolism, extravascular distribution, and systemic nutritional and inflammatory status.6,16 Low SA concentration has been implicated in diverse clinical situations, for example, physical function and delirium, apart from CV diseases.16–18 Although DM may reflect a state of overnutrition, insulin resistance, or deficiency, as the central pathophysiological hallmark of DM, can cause chronic inflammation and reduce the synthesis of proteins, including albumin. In a study on mice, insulin withdrawal resulted in the fivefold reduction of albumin mRNA levels relative to other total protein RNA levels. Diabetic mice exhibited a 60% reduction in total liver protein levels and a 24% reduction in albumin levels.10 Similarly, in this study, we noted lower SA concentrations in diabetic patients with CAD compared to nondiabetic individuals.

Reduced SA concentrations in patients with DM were considered to be associated with unfavorable metabolic profiles and adipose tissue inflammation.19 In vitro experiments revealed increased plasma protein glycation in patients with low SA concentrations. Moreover, consistent with our findings, in a previous study, the SA concentration was negatively correlated with HbA1c levels.20 This was considered the theoretical basis for the detrimental effects of low SA concentrations in diabetic patients. Many studies have revealed that low SA concentrations are independently associated with morbidity and mortality in diabetic patients. Zhang et al21 found that hypoalbuminemia (SA concentration: 3.5 g/dL) was independently associated with end-stage renal disease and mortality in patients with diabetic nephropathy. Moreover, Hu et al22 studied 523 Chinese patients with DM and noted an inverse correlation between the SA concentration and peripheral neuropathy (per g/L increment, odds ratio: 0.95 [95% CI, 0.92-0.98]). Our findings support this concept. Our study provided more comprehensive and long-term CV outcomes using a prospective, multicenter CAD cohort. Notably, we confirmed a close association of the SA concentration with mortality, but not with nonfatal events. This finding is consistent with that of our previous review, which indicated that low SA concentrations may play a direct causative role in incident CV events but may also be an indicator of an underlying condition.16 However, the underlying mechanism remains unclear and needs to be assessed in future studies.

Some results have challenged the detrimental role of low SA concentrations in diabetic patients. Bae et al. investigated 9029 nondiabetic individuals in Korea and found that individuals in the highest SA concentration tertile (≥4.6 g/dL) had a 1.5 to 3-fold higher risk of insulin resistance, fatty liver, and impaired fasting glucose than those in the lowest SA concentration tertile (<4.4 g/dL). However, the study did not reveal any relationship between the SA concentration and DM onset.13 During the 20-year follow-up of 1785 middle-aged men, Kunutsor et al14 found that the SA concentration showed a linear, positive, and independent association with DM (adjusted HR = 1.15, 95% CI, 1.02-1.29, p = 0.018). This could be partly explained by the overnutrition state of nondiabetic individuals who have a higher SA concentration at baseline, although the mechanism remains unclear. Furthermore, such metabolic dysregulation accompanied by high SA concentrations has not been reported in the population with established DM. This is not surprising because increased inflammatory processes and insulin resistance in the DM state would counter-regulate SA to a lower concentration. Thus, the state of reduced SA concentrations in diabetic patients indicates disease progression and the presence of comorbidity, which can result in a poor prognosis.

This study has some limitations. First, the Biosignatures study was conducted as a hospital-based study and not a community-based study. This design was potentially limited by selection bias. Second, baseline SA measurements were performed in separate laboratories of medical centers and not in a central laboratory. Data were measured as single-time-point, not available during follow-up. Third, the glycated albumin level was not available in the dataset. Fourth, we noted great heterogeneity among diabetic patients, which may affect the prognostic effect of the SA concentration. Finally, more precise estimation of individual’s nutritional status was not evaluated.

In conclusion, the results of our study on a stable CAD cohort followed up for the median 4.5 years demonstrated that a low SA concentration and DM were strongly associated with all-cause and CV mortality after adjustment for baseline factors. However, nonfatal CV events, including MI and stroke, were similar among groups stratified by the SA concentration and DM status.
