The Role of TRPV4 in Repeated Variate Stress-Induced Increases in Voiding Frequency and Somatic Sensitivity in Male and Female Mice
Authors: Beatrice M. Girard, Lydia Vadnal, Benjamin F. Stebbins McCaffrey, Susan E. Campbell, Margaret A. Vizzard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Psychological stress is a known precipitant of lower urinary tract (LUT) dysfunction and can exacerbate pelvic pain, yet the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4), a mechanosensitive ion channel expressed in LUT tissues and associated afferent circuits, is positioned to couple stress-related signaling to changes in voiding function. We tested the hypothesis that TRPV4 is required for repeated variate stress (RVS)-evoked bladder dysfunction and contributes to stress-associated somatic hypersensitivity and anxiety-like behavior. Male and female wild-type (WT) and Trpv4 knockout (KO) mice underwent RVS and were assessed using conscious cystometry, void spot assay (VSA), mechanical sensitivity testing (pelvic and hindpaw), and behavioral assays (open field, elevated plus maze). RVS produced sex- and tissue-specific regulation of Trpv4 transcripts LUT tissues. In WT mice, RVS induced bladder overactivity in both sexes, characterized by increased voiding frequency and reduced intermicturition interval. These stress-induced changes were absent in Trpv4 KO mice, demonstrating that TRPV4 is required for stress-evoked bladder dysfunction. Bladder pressure endpoints revealed additional sex-dependent effects in WT but not Trpv4 KO mice. VSA identified sex-specific spontaneous voiding changes that did not uniformly parallel cystometric measures, indicating complementary sensitivity of provoked and unprovoked assays. Despite protection from stress-associated bladder dysfunction, Trpv4 KO mice exhibited increased pelvic and hindpaw mechanical sensitivity following RVS. Behavioral testing revealed sex-dependent differences in stress resilience. These findings identify TRPV4 as a key mediator of stress-induced bladder overactivity and demonstrate a dissociation between referred mechanical pain outcomes in Trpv4 KO mice.

Introduction

Transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) is a polymodal, nonselective cation channel that contributes to mechanosensory and integrative sensory signaling in the lower urinary tract (LUT) (Girard et al. 2019; Merrill et al. 2016; Sidwell et al. 2023, 2024; Yoshimura et al. 2008). TRPV4 is expressed in the urothelium, suburothelial nerve plexus, detrusor smooth muscle, and dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons, positioning it to influence both peripheral sensory transduction and central processing of bladder signals (Everaerts et al. 2010b, c; Franken et al. 2014; Gevaert et al. 2007; Mochizuki et al. 2009; Sidwell et al. 2023). Mechanical stretch during bladder filling and chemical or thermal cues can activate TRPV4, producing Ca2+ influx and downstream signaling that includes ATP release from urothelial cells and modulation of afferent excitability, bladder sensation and detrusor contractility (Girard et al. 2019; Mochizuki et al. 2009; Perkins and Vizzard 2022; Wu et al. 2021). In the lamina propria, TRPV4-positive interstitial networks exhibit rhythmic Ca2+ activity that may coordinate local signaling during bladder filling and contribute to sensory integration (Heppner et al. 2017). Together, these findings support a urothelial–lamina propria “sensory web” in which TRPV4 participates in encoding mechanical information and relaying it to bladder afferents (Birder and Andersson 2013; Heppner et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2021).

TRPV4 signaling is highly plastic in response to inflammation, injury, growth factor expression and psychogenic stress. In cystitis-associated models and in mice with chronic urothelial NGF overexpression, TRPV4 expression in bladder tissues and lumbosacral DRG increases alongside urinary frequency and hypersensitivity, suggesting growth factor–dependent regulation of TRPV4 within LUT pathways (Girard et al. 2019; Merrill et al. 2012; Sidwell et al. 2023). Alternatively, in LPS-induced cystitis, bladder TRPV4 activation limits inflammation via M2-type macrophages and prevents bladder hypersensitivity, suggesting combined neural and immune mechanisms (Alpizar et al. 2020; Yoshizumi et al. 2022). Psychological stress is a recognized trigger for symptom exacerbation in functional bladder disorders, and repeated variate stress (RVS) paradigms in rodents recapitulate key features, including decreased bladder capacity and increased voiding frequency (Gao and Rodriguez 2022; Girard et al. 2021; Lackner et al. 2026; Mills et al. 2021; Sidwell et al. 2023). Importantly, RVS alters Trpv4 transcript expression in LUT pathways, including the urothelium, spinal cord, and DRG, and intravesical TRPV4 blockade mitigates stress-induced bladder dysfunction in male rats (Merrill and Vizzard 2014). These observations suggest that TRPV4 contributes to stress-related LUT dysfunction via effects on peripheral afferent signaling and/or central sensory processing. Stress-related bladder dysfunction frequently coincides with referred somatic sensitivity, reflecting convergence of visceral and somatic inputs at spinal levels (Girard et al. 2021, 2023; Merrill et al. 2013). TRPV4 expression in DRG and spinal circuits, together with its capacity to modulate afferent firing and ATP-mediated signaling, provides a mechanistic link between stress exposure and changes in both micturition function and pelvic and hindpaw mechanosensitivity (Girard et al. 2021; Janssen et al. 2016a; Merrill and Vizzard 2014; Sidwell et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2021).

Clinical observations further support translational relevance. TRPV4 expression is increased in patients with LUT dysfunction, and TRPV4 has been implicated in bladder cancer–associated signaling, underscoring broad roles in bladder pathophysiology (Andersson 2024; Cho et al. 2022; Janssen et al. 2016b; Katari et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2021). Sex as a biological variable is particularly relevant in stress-related disorders, which show female predominance clinically and sex-dependent neuroendocrine responses in rodents (Birder and Andersson 2013; Clemens et al. 2014; Merrill et al. 2016; Smith et al. 2016). TRPV4 is positioned to integrate stress-driven changes in urothelial signaling with spinal and DRG plasticity, potentially amplifying visceral input and referred somatic pain. Thus, defining whether TRPV4 drives both voiding and mechanosensitivity endpoints across sexes following RVS will support mechanistic models and sex-informed therapeutic targeting. We tested the hypothesis that TRPV4 contributes to RVS-induced increases in voiding frequency and referred somatic sensitivity in male and female mice. In adult wildtype and Trpv4 knockout (KO) male and female mice we evaluated and compared the following: voiding function (conscious cystometry, void spot assay), somatic sensitivity (hindpaw, pelvis), anxiety and exploratory behavior (open field and elevated plus maze), and Trpv4 mRNA expression in the lumbosacral spinal cord and DRG. These studies demonstrate a role for TRPV4 in RVS-induced bladder dysfunction in both male and female mice. Consistent with prior reports (Gao and Rodriguez 2022; Girard et al. 2021; Lackner et al. 2026; Mills et al. 2021; Sidwell et al. 2024), RVS increased somatic sensitivity (pelvic and hindpaw) in female wildtype mice; however, sensitivity was also elevated in Trpv4 KO mice, suggesting the involvement of other signaling pathways and/or engagement of compensatory mechanisms, potentially involving TRPV1 (Janssen et al. 2016a). In contrast, sex-specific differences emerged in anxiety-like behavior, as acute stress exposure (3 days of the RVS protocol) revealed an apparent resilience in female Trpv4 KO mice in the elevated plus maze. Together, these findings identify TRPV4 as a key mediator of RVS-induced bladder dysfunction and extend the potential utility of TRPV4-targeted therapies to other bladder disorders characterized by increased voiding frequency.

Methods

Animal Care and Use

All experimental protocols involving animal usage were approved by the University of Vermont (UVM) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC No. X9-020). The UVM Office of Animal Care Management (OACM) oversaw animal care in accordance with AAALAC and NIH guidelines. Adult wildtype (WT) male and female C57BL/6 mice (hereafter referred to as C57 or WT) (4–5 months old) were bred locally at the UVM Larner College of Medicine. C57 (RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664) were periodically introduced to maintain diversity. These experiments were also conducted in male and female global Trpv4 knockout (KO) mice (4–5 months of age) on a C57 background were bred locally at the Larner College of Medicine at the UVM (K. S. Thorneloe, GSK, Collegeville, PA). Previous studies using the global Trpv4 KO mice confirmed the absence of TRPV4 protein immunoreactivity (Girard et al. 2013), a lack of bladder functional responses to TRPV4 agonists or antagonists (Girard et al. 2019; Thorneloe et al. 2008), and the absence of cardiovascular effects following administration of the TRPV4 agonist GSK1016790A (Willette et al. 2008). Mice were group housed under controlled temperature and humidity with a 12-h light–dark cycle and ad libitum access to chow and water. WT (n = 103 female; 79 male) and Trpv4 KO (n = 123 female; 99 male) mice were used; subsets were assigned to experiments. Mice were randomly assigned to control and RVS cohorts. Estrous cycle status was not determined. All efforts were made to minimize pain, stress, or distress. All experiments and analyses were conducted by an investigator blinded to group assignment when possible. All experiments were performed using standardized protocols, reagents, equipment, and analysis pipelines. Animals were matched for strain, sex, age, and housing conditions, and procedures were conducted by the same personnel. Experiments were carried out across multiple experimental runs, with experimental groups balanced within each cohort when possible. Data collection and analysis parameters were held constant across cohorts to minimize variability.

Repeated Variate Stress

Mice were exposed to 14 consecutive days (2 wk) of a single stressor presented between 10 AM and 2 PM. The RVS battery consisted of oscillation stress, foot shock, pedestal stress, forced swim, and restraint stress presented in rotating order, as previously described (Girard et al. 2023; Gonzalez et al. 2014; Merrill et al. 2013; Merrill and Vizzard 2014; Sidwell et al. 2023, 2024). Control mice were handled daily and group housed; RVS mice were singly housed during the paradigm without enrichment. Researchers were blinded to group assignment where possible. For characterization of Trpv4 transcript expression in LUT pathways, we used both 2 wk and 4 wk RVS as previously described (Sidwell et al. 2023).

Behavioral Testing

Open-Field Testing

Open-field testing was performed at baseline (prior to RVS) and the day after the final stressor. Mice were videotaped for 10 min in a clear, unfamiliar Plexiglas box with an open top and an inner square demarcated in a quiet laboratory space as previously described (Sidwell et al. 2023, 2024). Videos were analyzed with ANY-maze (RRID: SCR_014289) to quantify locomotor activity (total distance, maximum speed), and anxiety-related exploratory behavior (freezing episodes in the outer zone and time mobile in the inner zone). The arena was sanitized between tests.

Elevated Plus Maze

Anxiety-like behavior was also assessed using the elevated plus maze (EPM) (Hogg 1996; Liu et al. 2025; Walf and Frye 2007) with two open and two closed arms around a center platform. Mice were acclimated to the testing room, placed on the center platform facing an open arm, and allowed to explore for 5 min under consistent ambient lighting. Behavior was recorded and analyzed with EthoVision XT (RRID: SCR_000441). Primary outcomes included time in open vs. closed arms vs. center. The maze was cleaned between trials. Behavioral exclusion criteria: sessions were excluded if video tracking failed (e.g., loss of detection). Behavioral testing was conducted at Pre-RVS and at 3, 7, 10, and 14 days of RVS in independent cohorts of male and female mice.

Somatic Mechanosensitivity

Referred somatic sensitivity was assessed in mice using calibrated von Frey monofilaments (Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL) applied to the plantar surface of the hindpaw and the lower abdominal/pelvic region as previously described (Girard et al. 2016b, 2021, 2023; Merrill et al. 2013; Schnegelsberg et al. 2010). Animals were placed individually in transparent chambers on an elevated wire-mesh platform and allowed to acclimate for 60 min before testing. Behavioral responses were determined using an up–down method. Filaments were applied perpendicularly to the skin until bending and maintained for approximately 1–2 s. Each filament was applied three times, with a minimum interstimulus interval of 5 s. Behavioral responses were scored as follows: 0, no response; 1, reaction of the animal; 2, reaction with a change in position; or 3, reaction with a change in position accompanied by licking and/or vocalization (Wolf-Johnston et al. 2024). A nociceptive score for each filament was calculated as a percentage of the maximal possible score (Wolf-Johnston et al. 2024). Baseline measurements were obtained before initiation of any experimental treatments.

Voiding Behavior

Video-Monitored Void Spot Assay

Voiding behavior was recorded in awake, freely moving mice using a video-monitored void spot assay as described (Dalghi et al. 2023). Mice were housed individually in acrylic cages with UV-transmitting bottoms over UV illumination to visualize urine spots on pure cellulose chromatography paper (0.35 mm thickness and 35 cm wet strength) (Fisher Scientific Company L.L.C., Waltham, MA), with access to complete nutrition and hydration gel during the recording (Bio-Serv, Flemington, NJ). In the VSA cages, control mice received enrichment whereas RVS mice did not (Girard et al. 2019). Voiding was recorded during a defined 4-hour (4 h) window using synchronized bottom-view cameras. Voiding events were identified by video review. Urine spot area was quantified from UV images using Fiji/ImageJ (RRID: SCR_002285; RRID: SCR_003070) and converted to estimated volume using a calibration curve.

To convert urine spot size to voided urine volume, a calibration curve was generated using known volumes of sodium phosphate buffer or pooled mouse urine applied to the same substrate used for behavioral testing. Filter paper (Whatman Grade 1), identical to that used in the void spot assay, was placed in the recording chamber. Known volumes ranging from 2 to 1000 µL were dispensed onto the paper using a calibrated micropipette. The paper was then covered with an absorbent pad and recorded for 1 h to allow complete spot development.

Screenshots of urine spots were captured after determining the time required for maximal spread and signal intensity for each volume range (small, < 25 µL; medium; large, > 50 µL). At least 3–5 replicate spots were generated per volume. Spot area was quantified using ImageJ software, and a calibration curve was constructed by plotting measured spot size against deposited volume. Linear or nonlinear regression was applied as appropriate, and the resulting best-fit equation was used to estimate voided urine volume from experimental samples. Calibration was repeated periodically and whenever changes were made to filter paper type, imaging conditions, or analysis parameters to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Recordings (4 h) were performed between 10 AM and 2 PM. Mice were placed directly into void spot assay cages and recording commenced immediately, without a behavioral acclimation period.

Conscious Open-outlet Continuous-infusion Cystometry

Two days after catheter implantation or the day after the final stressor, mice underwent conscious, open-outlet, continuous-infusion cystometry, as described (Sidwell et al. 2023, 2024). Under isoflurane anesthesia, PE-10 tubing was inserted through an abdominal incision to the bladder dome, heat flared and secured with 6 − 0 nylon; the distal end was sealed, tunneled subcutaneously, and externalized at the neck. Incisions were closed with nylon sutures. Postoperative analgesia (carprofen; 5.0 mg/kg, sc) was given at surgery and every 24 h for 2 days. After a 48 h recovery, unrestrained mice were placed in wire-bottomed Plexiglas cages for recording.

Before recording, the bladder was emptied and the catheter connected to a pressure transducer and microinjection pump via a T-tube. A Small Animal Cystometry Lab Station (Med Associates Inc, Fairfax, VT) was used. Room temperature saline was infused at 25 µL/min to elicit repetitive contractions for 60 min. For each mouse, minimum, threshold, average, and maximum bladder pressures were recorded, along with intermicturition interval (IMI) and infused volume (functional bladder capacity). Residual urine was gently aspirated through the intravesical catheter using a syringe, and the directly measured residual volume was consistently < 5 µL. Mice were euthanized at the conclusion and tissue harvested. Recordings were performed between 10 AM and 2 PM.

Cystometry exclusion criteria: mice were excluded for significant adverse postoperative events or if pain/distress was not relieved by the IACUC-approved analgesic regimen. Recording epochs contaminated by grooming, standing, walking, or defecation were excluded; ~5% of mice designated for cystometry were removed from analysis by predefined criteria.

Euthanasia and Tissue Harvest

For molecular analyses, tissues were harvested from separate cohorts of mice that did not undergo cystometric evaluation. Mice were deeply anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and euthanized via thoracotomy. The urinary bladder and lumbosacral spinal cord (L1, L2, and L5-S1) and associated dorsal root ganglia (DRG) were quickly dissected under RNase-free conditions. The bladder was opened along the midline and pinned to a Sylgard-coated dish; urothelium was separated from detrusor smooth muscle using fine forceps under a dissecting microscope (Girard et al. 2010, 2016a). Tissues were snap-frozen on dry ice prior to processing.

Real-time Quantitative RT-PCR

Real-time quantitative RT-PCR was performed as previously described (Girard et al. 2006, 2011; Sidwell et al. 2023). Total RNA was extracted with Stat-60. cDNA was synthesized using random hexamers and oligo(dT) with M-MLV reverse transcriptase. Quantitative PCR standards were generated by cloning amplicons into pCR2.1-TOPO vectors and verifying inserts by sequencing. qPCR used SYBR Green (Luna Universal qPCR Master Mix) on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast system with melt-curve analysis. Targets were quantified from standard curves and normalized to housekeeping genes (L32 or 18 S). In previous studies (Sidwell et al. 2023), we quantified TrpV4 mRNA expression in bladder urothelium and detrusor, lumbosacral spinal cord and DRG from female mice with and without RVS; in the present study, we assessed TrpV4 mRNA expression in urothelium and detrusor, lumbosacral spinal cord and DRG from male mice under the same conditions, and now directly compare these newly generated male data in the current study to the previously published female dataset (Sidwell et al. 2023). Female and male experiments were conducted in independent cohorts at different time points. All experimental conditions, including animal handling, assay protocols, and data acquisition parameters, were standardized across cohorts to minimize variability.

Statistical Analyses

Values are means ± SEM. P ≤ 0.05 was considered significant. Data were analyzed using ANOVA with strain, treatment, and sex as between-subject factors. When significant interactions were detected, simple effects were examined with Šídák’s post hoc multiple-comparisons testing (GraphPad Prism 10.0; RRID: SCR_002798). Raw cystometry data were analyzed with MED-CMG (Med Associates; SOF-551). Functional bladder parameters were averaged for each mouse strain in each condition based upon sex. Open-field data were analyzed with ANY-maze (RRID: SCR_014289). EPM behavior was analyzed with EthoVision XT (RRID: SCR_000441). Nociceptive scores expressed as a percentage of the maximal possible response were analyzed using non-parametric methods due to non-normal data distribution. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparisons post hoc test when appropriate.

Results

Sex and LUT tissue specific changes in TrpV4 mRNA expression in WT mice following RVS

In previous studies (Sidwell et al. 2023), we quantified TrpV4 mRNA expression in bladder urothelium and detrusor, lumbosacral spinal cord and DRG from female mice with and without RVS; in the present study, we assessed TrpV4 mRNA expression in urothelium and detrusor, lumbosacral spinal cord and DRG from male mice under the same conditions, and now directly compare these newly generated male data to the previously published female dataset (Sidwell et al. 2023). In bladder urothelium, TrpV4 mRNA expression was significantly (P ≤ 0.05) decreased in female WT mice after 2 wk and 4 wk RVS (Fig. 1 A), whereas no changes were observed in male WT urothelium (Fig. 1A). In detrusor, no changes in TrpV4 mRNA expression were observed at 2 wk or 4 wk RVS in female WT mice (Fig. 1B). In contrast, TrpV4 mRNA expression was significantly (P ≤ 0.05) increased in detrusor at 4 wk RVS in male WT mice, but not at 2 wk RVS (Fig. 1B).

For Q-PCR data analyses, we emphasized differences between control (no stress) mice and mice exposed to 2 wk or 4 wk RVS only, for clarity of presentation.

Baseline Sex- and Genotype-Dependent Differences in Open Field Behavior

WT male mice exhibited a significantly (P ≤ 0.05) greater number of freezing episodes in the outer zone (Fig. 3 C) and spent significantly (P ≤ 0.01) less time mobile in the inner zone of the open field test (Fig. 3D), although no differences were observed in total distance traveled or maximum speed (Fig. 3 A and B). In contrast, male or female Trpv4 KO mice exhibited no differences in these parameters (data not shown).

Female TrpV4 KO mice traveled significantly (P ≤ 0.001) less total distance than female WT mice but exhibited similar maximum speed (Fig. 3E). In addition, female TrpV4 KO mice displayed a significant (P ≤ 0.05) increase in freezing episodes in the outer zone (Fig. 3 J) and reduced time (P ≤ 0.01) mobile in the inner zone (Fig. 3H). Compared with male WT mice, male TrpV4 KO mice traveled significantly (P ≤ 0.001) less total distance (Fig. 3I) and achieved a lower maximum speed (P ≤ 0.01) (Fig. 3 J) but did not differ in the number of freezing episodes in the outer zone (Fig. 3 K) or time mobile in the inner zone (Fig. 3L). These data indicate that baseline locomotor- and anxiety-related behaviors differ by both sex and genotype, with WT males showing heightened anxiety-like behavior, TRPV4 deletion reducing locomotor activity in both sexes, and female TrpV4 KO mice exhibiting a combination of reduced mobility and increased freezing behavior.

RVS induces time-dependent and sex-specific changes in elevated plus maze behavior in Trpv4 KO mice

Given the slower movement observed in TrpV4 KO mice during the open field test (Fig. 3), we next turned to the elevated plus maze (Fig. 4) to better distinguish locomotor effects from true anxiety-related differences and to gain complementary insight into the behavioral consequences of TRPV4 deletion. Both male (Fig. 4D) and female (Fig. 4 A) Trpv4 KO mice exhibited a significant reduction in time spent in the open arm of the elevated plus maze at time points examined following RVS (days (d) 7, 10, 14) compared with pre-RVS baseline values, indicating increased anxiety-like behavior. Consistent with this pattern, both male (Fig. 4E) and female (Fig. 4B) Trpv4 KO mice showed a significant increase in time spent in the closed arm of the elevated plus maze at d7 and d10 compared with pre-RVS values. Female Trpv4 KO mice also exhibited a significant (P ≤ 0.001) reduction in time spent in the center of the elevated plus maze at d10 RVS (Fig. 4 C) compared with pre-RVS values, whereas male Trpv4 KO mice showed no differences (Fig. 4 F). Differences in time spent in open arm (Fig. 4 A), closed arm (Fig. 4B) and center (Fig. 4 C) were also demonstrated among the RVS time points examined (3d, 7D, 10 d, 14 d) for female Trpv4 KO mice and among the RVS time points examined for male Trpv4 KO mice in open and closed arm (Fig. 4D and E).

Sex differences were evident pre-RVS, with male Trpv4 KO mice exhibiting significantly (P ≤ 0.05) greater time spent in the open arm of the elevated plus maze (Fig. 5 A). Notably, sex-dependent effects also emerged during acute stress exposure: female Trpv4 KO mice displayed resilience at the 3 d RVS time point, showing no reduction in open-arm exploration relative to their pre-RVS baseline (Fig. 4 A). In contrast, male Trpv4 KO mice exhibited a significant decrease in open-arm time at d3 compared with pre-RVS (Fig. 4D). Time spent in the open arm at 3 d RVS was significantly (P ≤ 0.0001) different between female and male Trpv4 KO mice (Fig. 5B), and, as expected, time spent in the closed arm at this time point also differed significantly (P ≤ 0.0001) between sexes (Fig. 5B).

This early sex difference was transient; no sex-dependent differences were detected at later time points (days 7, 10, or 14), with male and female Trpv4 KO mice exhibiting comparable open-arm, closed-arm, and center exploration (Fig. 5 C, D and E). By 14 d of RVS, both male and female Trpv4 KO mice demonstrated a notable increase in time spent in the open arm relative to earlier time points, indicating a reduction in stress-induced avoidance of open spaces with prolonged stress exposure (Figs. 4 A and D and 5E).

TRPV4 is required for stress-induced alterations in cystometric parameters

Continuous-fill, open outlet cystometry was performed in male and female WT and Trpv4 KO mice to determine the effects of RVS on voiding function. Baseline cystometric parameters, including intermicturition interval (IMI), and infused volume (a measure of functional bladder capacity), did not differ significantly between sexes for Trpv4 KO or WT mice (Fig. 6 A and B). Male and female Trpv4 KO mice exhibited significantly (P ≤ 0.001 for male; P ≤ 0.01 for female) increased functional bladder capacity (Fig. 6 A) and IMI (Fig. 6B) compared to sex matched WT mice (Fig. 7A1, A2-Fig. 7E1, E2; Fig. 7C1, C2-Fig. 7G1, G2) as previously reported (Everaerts et al. 2010c; Gevaert et al. 2007).

Following RVS, WT mice of both sexes exhibited significant alterations in cystometric measures consistent with increased voiding frequency. Collectively, these cystometric data indicate that TRPV4 is required for the development of RVS-induced reductions in functional bladder capacity and IMI, and that these effects occur independently of sex in WT mice.

Similarly, threshold pressure in female WT mice was significantly (P ≤ 0.001) greater than that observed in female Trpv4 KO mice with or without RVS (Fig. 8 A). No significant differences were observed in minimum pressure in male or female WT or Trpv4 KO mice with or without RVS (Fig. 8B). A significant (P ≤ 0.05) difference in average pressure was noted between male WT mice and male Trpv4 KO mice (Fig. 8 C). Effects of RVS on voiding patterns assessed by void spot assay

Voiding behavior was further assessed using the void spot assay (VSA), a noninvasive method that measures spontaneous urine deposition without intravesical catheterization or saline infusion to evoke micturition (Bjorling et al. 2015; Dalghi et al. 2023; Sugino et al. 2008). As such, the VSA is considered to reflect more naturalistic voiding patterns under low-stress, freely moving conditions. Data presented below are restricted to primary void spots of at least 20 µL. Baseline VSA data demonstrated that male WT mice had fewer voids during the 4 h testing period that WT female mice (Fig. 9 A). Analysis of VSA data revealed sex-specific effects of RVS in WT mice (Fig. 9 A). Female WT mice exposed to RVS exhibited an increased number of primary void events over the 4 h testing period (10 AM-2 PM) (Fig. 9 A), whereas male WT mice did not show a corresponding increase in void number (Fig. 9 A). This pattern parallels cystometric findings demonstrating increased voiding frequency and reduced intermicturition interval in female WT mice following RVS (Figs. 6 and 7). In contrast, male WT mice did not exhibit a robust increase in void number in the VSA (Fig. 9 A) despite clear stress-induced changes detected during cystometry (Figs. 6 and 7).

Sex- and strain-dependent effects of RVS on pelvic and hindpaw somatic sensitivity

Referred somatic sensitivity was assessed using calibrated von Frey monofilaments applied to the lower abdominal/pelvic region and the hindpaw both before initiation of RVS protocol and after completion of the exposure (see Methods) (Girard et al. 2016b, 2021, 2023; Merrill et al. 2013; Schnegelsberg et al. 2010).

Baseline Pelvic Sensitivity

At baseline, pelvic mechanical sensitivity differed significantly between males and females in both WT (0.02–0.16 g) and Trpv4 KO (0.02–0.16 g) mice in response to lower-force von Frey monofilaments (P ≤ 0.001; Fig. 10 A, E, C and G) with females of both genotypes exhibiting increased pelvic mechanical sensitivity. However, no significant baseline differences were observed between WT and Trpv4 KO mice when compared within the same sex, demonstrating that genotype-related effects were not apparent under pre-stress conditions (Fig. 10 A, C, E and G).

Post-RVS Pelvic Sensitivity

Following RVS, female WT mice exhibited a significant (P ≤ 0.001) increase in pelvic sensitivity relative to baseline (0.07–6 g; Fig. 10E), whereas WT male mice showed no significant change (Fig. 10 A), indicating a sex-dependent effect of stress exposure on pelvic mechanosensitivity in WT animals. In Trpv4 KO mice, pelvic sensitivity significantly (P ≤ 0.001) increased after RVS in both sexes (0.07–6 g female Trpv4 KO; Fig. 10G) (0.04–6 g male Trpv4 KO; Fig. 10 C), demonstrating a strain-dependent phenotype in which TRPV4 loss does not prevent stress-induced pelvic hypersensitivity.

Baseline Hindpaw Sensitivity

Baseline maximum hindpaw sensitivity to the highest force monofilaments (2–4 g) was comparable between male and female WT mice (Fig. 10B and F). However, male WT mice were more responsive (P ≤ 0.01) to intermediate monofilament forces (0.07 g and 0.4–1.4 g) (Fig. 10B) compared to female WT mice (Fig. 10 F). In contrast, male and female Trpv4 KO mice displayed similar hindpaw responses at baseline (Fig. 10D, 10 H). Female WT and Trpv4 KO mice did not differ in baseline hindpaw sensitivity when compared within the same sex (Fig. 10 F, H); however, male WT mice exhibited greater (P ≤ 0.05) hindpaw sensitivity to intermediate filament forces (0.07–0.4 g) compared to male Trpv4 KO mice (Fig. 10B and D).

Post-RVS Hindpaw Sensitivity

Following RVS, WT male mice exhibited a significant decrease in hindpaw sensitivity relative to baseline (0.07 g and 0.4–1.4 g); P ≤ 0.001; Fig. 10B), whereas WT female mice showed no change (Fig. 10 F), demonstrating a sex-dependent effect of stress on hindpaw responsiveness in WT animals. In Trpv4 KO mice, hindpaw sensitivity increased after RVS in both sexes (0.07–1 g for males; 0.07–6 g for females; P ≤ 0.001; Fig. 10D, H), consistent with a strain-dependent phenotype in which TRPV4 deletion does not protect against stress-induced hindpaw hypersensitivity. Unlike pelvic responses, however, post-RVS hindpaw sensitivity differed by genotype, with Trpv4 KO mice exhibiting greater hypersensitivity after stress than WT controls (Fig. 10B, D and F, H).

Together, these results demonstrate that baseline mechanical sensitivity shows clear sex-dependent differences in both pelvic and hindpaw regions, regardless of genotype. Following RVS, WT mice exhibit sex-specific patterns of stress-evoked hypersensitivity, whereas Trpv4 KO mice show robust, sex-independent increases in both pelvic and hindpaw sensitivity. Importantly, Trpv4 deletion does not protect against RVS-induced hypersensitivity, rather, Trpv4 KO mice display equal or greater post stress mechanical sensitivity depending on the somatic region. These findings highlight both region-specific and genotype-specific contributions to RVS-evoked somatosensory changes.

Discussion

The present study identifies several novel and integrative roles for TRPV4 in stress-evoked LUT, somatic sensitivity and behavioral outcomes. We demonstrate that repeated variate stress (RVS) produces sex- and tissue-specific regulation of Trpv4 transcripts across the bladder, lumbosacral spinal cord, and dorsal root ganglia, revealing regionally organized plasticity within LUT sensory pathways. Functionally, we show that Trpv4 deletion prevents stress-induced bladder overactivity, eliminating reductions in intermicturition interval and increases in voiding frequency in both sexes, indicating that TRPV4 is required for the development of stress-evoked bladder dysfunction. In contrast, our findings reveal a dissociation in which Trpv4 deletion fails to protect against, and in some cases increases, stress-evoked pelvic and hindpaw mechanical hypersensitivity, demonstrating that TRPV4 differentially contributes to bladder versus somatic sensory outcomes. Finally, this study identifies a sex-dependent behavioral phenotype, with female Trpv4 KO mice showing acute stress resilience in the elevated plus maze, highlighting TRPV4 as a potential modulator of early stress-evoked anxiety-like behavior and central CNS role for TRPV4. Together, these findings demonstrated novel roles for TRPV4 across peripheral and central pathways engaged by psychogenic stress and establish TRPV4 as a key determinant of stress-induced bladder dysfunction, distinct from its influence on somatic pain and affective responses.

The sex- and tissue-specific regulation of Trpv4 transcripts following RVS suggests that psychological stress engages distinct, regionally organized TRPV4-related signaling nodes across the LUT and associated sensory pathways (Girard et al. 2019; Merrill et al. 2012, 2016; Sidwell et al. 2023, 2024). In females, the sustained reduction of Trpv4 mRNA in the urothelium after 2 wk and 4 wk RVS may reflect stress-driven remodeling of urothelial mechanosensory transduction. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that urothelial TRPV4 contributes to distension-evoked Ca²⁺ influx and ATP release, key inputs to afferent signaling during bladder filling (Mochizuki et al. 2009; Wu et al. 2021). More broadly, this interpretation aligns with a growing body of work demonstrating that psychogenic stress and disease states can alter urothelial receptor/channel expression and signaling mediator release, thereby shifting afferent excitability and bladder reflex function (Aizawa et al. 2012; De Rijk et al. 2022; Gao and Rodriguez 2022; Girard et al. 2023; Merrill et al. 2016; Mingin et al. 2015; Sidwell et al. 2024). Conversely, the male-specific increase in detrusor Trpv4 mRNA at 4 wk RVS suggests that prolonged stress may preferentially recruit TRPV4-related mechanisms in smooth muscle or its microenvironment to potentially influence the contractile functions (Andersson 2019; |2024; Wu et al. 2021). By including male and female WT mice, we found that the segmental pattern of Trpv4 transcript regulation in the spinal cord and DRG suggests that RVS drives sex-dependent plasticity along LUT sensory pathways. In female WT mice, increased Trpv4 mRNA in the lumbosacral spinal cord after 4 wk of RVS is consistent with stress-evoked reprogramming of sensory circuitry that integrates pelvic and visceral inputs (Aizawa et al. 2012; De Rijk et al. 2022; Gao and Rodriguez 2022; Girard et al. 2023; Merrill et al. 2016; Mingin et al. 2015; Sidwell et al. 2024). A limitation of this study is that female and male data were generated in independent cohorts at different time points. Although all experimental conditions were carefully standardized across cohorts to minimize temporal variability, we cannot fully exclude subtle time-related effects. Nonetheless, the consistency of experimental procedures and overall outcomes across cohorts supports the validity of the findings, but this factor should be considered when interpreting the results.

A key finding of the present study is that Trpv4 deletion prevented stress-induced increases in voiding frequency and reductions in intermicturition interval in both male and female mice, whereas wildtype animals exhibited robust cystometric evidence of bladder overactivity following repeated variate stress (RVS). Psychological stress is well recognized to induce bladder overactivity in rodents, typically reflected by reduced IMI and functional bladder capacity during cystometry (Girard et al. 2023; Merrill et al. 2013; Merrill and Vizzard 2014; Mingin et al. 2015; Sidwell et al. 2024; Smith et al. 2011; Tykocki et al. 2018; Westropp et al. 2024). Thus, the absence of stress-evoked changes in IMI and functional bladder capacity in Trpv4 KO mice indicates that TRPV4 is required for the development of stress-induced bladder dysfunction and that this requirement is largely independent of sex, supporting TRPV4 as a critical mediator linking stress exposure to altered bladder sensory and reflex function (Andersson 2019; Birder and Andersson 2013; Birder 2007). This protective phenotype aligns with prior work showing that TRPV4 expression and activity are increased in inflammatory cystitis models and that genetic or pharmacological disruption of TRPV4 attenuates bladder hyperreflexia and altered voiding patterns (Gevaert et al. 2007; Merrill and Vizzard 2014; Sidwell et al. 2024; Yoshiyama et al. 2015; Yoshizumi et al. 2022), and with evidence from NGF-driven models in which TRPV4 contributes to heightened afferent excitability and increased voiding frequency (Girard et al. 2019). The present findings extend this framework by demonstrating that TRPV4 is also requisite for bladder dysfunction driven by psychological stress, even in the absence of overt inflammation or structural pathology. Consistent with a mechanosensory role for TRPV4 in urothelial–afferent signaling, the selective reduction in threshold pressure after RVS in male WT mice, together with the absence of RVS-evoked threshold changes in Trpv4 KO mice, suggests that stress can shift the pressure “set point” for void initiation in a TRPV4-dependent manner (Gevaert et al. 2007; Merrill and Vizzard 2014). Collectively, the convergence of stress-based and inflammatory/NGF-driven evidence underscores the broad relevance of TRPV4 to bladder pathophysiology and supports continued development of TRPV4-directed interventions for LUT disorders characterized by increased voiding frequency and reduced bladder capacity (Gevaert et al. 2007; Junwei et al. 2025; Kawasaki et al. 2021; Merrill and Vizzard 2014). Future conditional or cell type-specific approaches will be important to define the relative contributions of urothelial, afferent, and stromal compartments to stress-induced bladder dysfunction. A key objective moving forward is to more precisely delineate the relative contributions of TRPV4-mediated peripheral versus central mechanisms underlying stress-induced bladder overactivity. In this context, the use of urothelial-specific Trpv4 KO mice will be essential to determine whether the phenotypes observed in global Trpv4 KO mice reflect summative effects of TRPV4 loss across multiple organ systems or arise from distinct tissue-specific mechanisms, beginning with targeted deletion in the urothelium.

Although both cystometry and the void spot assay (VSA) are widely used to assess urinary function in rodents, they probe different dimensions of micturition, which may explain the partial dissociation between assays observed here (Bjorling et al. 2015; Hill et al. 2018; Luo et al. 2023; Sartori et al. 2021; Sidler et al. 2018; Wegner et al. 2018). Cystometry evaluates reflex voiding during forced, continuous intravesical filling and may be particularly sensitive to stress-induced changes in mechanosensory/afferent signaling, compliance, and voiding thresholds that may remain subthreshold during natural behavior (Bjorling et al. 2015; Hill et al. 2018; Luo et al. 2023; Sartori et al. 2021; Sidler et al. 2018; Wegner et al. 2018). In contrast, the VSA captures spontaneous voiding without instrumentation and integrates peripheral bladder function with central micturition control and behavioral/metabolic influences (e.g., hydration status and circadian factors), which may buffer or mask modest peripheral sensitization (Hill et al. 2018; Luo et al. 2023; Wegner et al. 2018). This distinction is consistent with the present pattern in which female WT mice expressed stress-evoked dysfunction as increased voiding frequency in both assays, whereas males showed clear cystometric changes without a parallel increase in spontaneous voiding, suggesting provoked filling is sometimes required to reveal functional consequences of stress in males. It should be noted that the VSA was performed during the day (10AM-2PM) and this may have contributed to the current findings. Thus, it would be of interest to perform the VSA during the night when mice are more active and determine if RVS effects align better with cystometry results and are more prominent especially in male mice. A limitation of the present study is that mice were recorded immediately upon placement into VSA cages without a prior acclimation period. While this approach allows for detection of early voiding behavior, it may also capture stress-related responses associated with cage transfer rather than purely baseline micturition patterns. All behavioral assays were conducted within a consistent time window to minimize circadian effects; however, the potential contribution of acute contextual stress should be considered when interpreting the VSA data. Finally, because TRPV4 is strongly implicated in mechanotransduction during bladder filling, its contribution may be more robust under cystometric conditions and may be less apparent during spontaneous voiding assessed by VSA, reinforcing the need to use both assays to capture complementary, nonredundant aspects of stress-induced bladder dysfunction and its sex- and TRPV4-dependent modulation (Bjorling et al. 2015; Hill et al. 2018; Luo et al. 2023; Sartori et al. 2021; Sidler et al. 2018; Wegner et al. 2018).

These RVS experiments reveal a dissociation between LUT outcomes and somatic pain outcomes in the absence of TRPV4. In WT mice, stress effects on referred somatic sensitivity are sex- and region-dependent (female-predominant pelvic hypersensitivity; male-specific changes in hindpaw sensitivity), whereas Trpv4 KO mice show robust, largely sex-independent increases in both pelvic and hindpaw sensitivity following RVS. One interpretation is that TRPV4 primarily contributes to bladder-filling mechanosensory transduction (urothelial Ca²⁺ influx/ATP release and downstream afferent drive), such that loss of TRPV4 can be beneficial for bladder functional endpoints, consistent with evidence that TRPV4 is a key urothelial mechanosensor and that its deletion disrupts voiding physiology and stretch-evoked signaling (Everaerts et al. 2010a, c; Gevaert et al. 2007; Girard et al. 2019; Junwei et al. 2025; Merrill and Vizzard 2014; Mochizuki et al. 2009; Yoshizumi et al. 2022). However, Trpv4 deletion does not confer parallel protection against stress-evoked somatic hypersensitivity; instead, Trpv4 KO mice develop equal or greater mechanical hypersensitivity, indicating that loss of TRPV4 does not necessarily dampen stress-recruited pain processing (Janssen et al. 2016a).

Two complementary mechanisms may underlie this divergent phenotype: (1) TRPV4-dependent urothelial mechanotransduction may serve as a dominant “gate” for converting stress-driven changes into exaggerated bladder afferent output during filling, so TRPV4 loss can blunt stress-induced reductions in IMI and increases in frequency even if parallel nociceptive pathways remain active (Mochizuki et al. 2009; Wu et al. 2021); and (2) constitutive Trpv4 deletion may shift sensory signaling toward TRPV1-weighted high-threshold afferents that more strongly determine somatic von Frey outcomes, supported by evidence for TRPV1-related adaptations in Trpv4 KO mice during noxious bladder distention that are attenuated by TRPV1-fiber desensitization (Janssen et al. 2016a). In addition, psychogenic stress engages central micturition circuits (PAG–PMC/Barrington’s nucleus) and CRH/CRF signaling capable of bidirectionally shaping voiding behavior. As a result, central circuit state may exert a dominant influence over bladder output. In contrast, somatic hypersensitivity appears disproportionately sensitive to TRPV1-dependent increases in nociceptor gain. Together, these mechanisms may explain the dissociation observed in Trpv4 KO mice, in which bladder function is protected while pelvic and hindpaw sensitivity are enhanced (Janssen et al. 2016a; Mochizuki et al. 2009; Shimizu et al. 2021; Valentino 1988; Van Batavia et al. 2021).

The baseline open field phenotype indicates that loss of TRPV4 alters general activity levels, with both male and female Trpv4 knockout mice exhibiting reduced locomotor output (distance traveled and/or maximum speed) relative to WT controls. This reduction in locomotion represents an important caveat when interpreting anxiety-like measures, as decreased activity can limit center exploration and inflate apparent avoidance independent of affective state. Baseline behavioral effects of TRPV4 deletion also appear context dependent, as Trpv4 KO mice show normal exploratory/anxiety-related behavior in some settings (Gevaert et al. 2007) but reduced functional performance in others (Kashio et al. 2024). Against this background, a key finding from the elevated plus maze was that female Trpv4 KO mice showed resilience during acute stress (3 days of RVS), whereas male Trpv4 KO mice did not, consistent with known sex differences in stress neurobiology and CRH/LC–NE circuitry that shapes arousal and avoidance behavior (Bangasser and Valentino 2012; |2014; Mariscal et al. 2023; Valentino et al. 2013; Walf and Frye 2007). By contrast, both male and female Trpv4 KO mice exhibited reduced anxiety-like behavior after prolonged (14-day) RVS, suggesting altered stress adaptation/habituation or reduced persistence of avoidance when TRPV4 signaling is absent, while acknowledging that repeated EPM testing can introduce experience-dependent effects best interpreted by comparing patterns across groups and time points (Suarez-Pereira et al. 2022; Tucker and Mccabe 2017). These time- and sex-dependent behavioral effects support the broader possibility that TRPV4 influences central stress circuits via neuron–glia–vascular mechanisms, consistent with evidence that TRPV4 participates in CNS Ca²⁺-linked signaling and astrocytic homeostatic responses, including via an AQP4/TRPV4 functional complex (Benfenati et al. 2011; Kumar et al. 2018; Shibasaki 2024; Tureckova et al. 2023). Finally, these phenotypes may be clinically relevant given stress sensitivity in IC/BPS and related distress comorbidities (Clemens et al. 2014; Fischer-Grote et al. 2022; Lackner et al. 2026; Mohammadi et al. 2016; Naliboff et al. 2025). This relevance is further supported by evidence that RVS in female mice alters voiding-related measures and Trpv transcript expression (Sidwell et al. 2023). Together, these findings underscore the potential importance of TRPV4-dependent modulation of stress responses, particularly female-specific acute resilience, in shaping sex bias and heterogeneity in stress-exacerbated bladder dysfunction (Bangasser and Valentino 2012; |2014; Lackner et al. 2026; Mariscal et al. 2023; Mohammadi et al. 2016; Naliboff et al. 2025; Robbins and Ness 2008; Valentino et al. 2013; Walf and Frye 2007).

In summary, our studies identify TRPV4 as a critical mediator of stress-evoked bladder dysfunction, acting through sex- and tissue-specific pathways that shape LUT sensory and reflex responses. The dissociation between bladder protection and enhanced somatic sensitivity in Trpv4 KO mice underscores the complexity of TRP channel signaling under psychological stress and reveals distinct mechanisms governing visceral versus somatic outcomes. Importantly, demonstrating that TRPV4 is required for psychogenic stress–induced bladder overactivity in the absence of overt inflammation underscores its translational relevance as a therapeutic target for stress-exacerbated bladder disorders, including overactive bladder and IC/BPS (Girard et al. 2021; Janssen et al. 2016a; Merrill and Vizzard 2014; Sidwell et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2021).
