Selective COX-2 inhibitors for short-term musculoskeletal pain in inflammatory bowel disease in remission: a narrative review
Authors: Michiel A van Nieuwenhoven
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelective cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitors are often considered when anti-inflammatory analgesia is needed for musculoskeletal pain in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), but concerns remain about intestinal safety. The clinical evidence on short-term selective COX-2 inhibitor use in IBD, with focus on disease activity outcomes, was reviewed. A prespecified literature search was performed in PubMed and the Cochrane Library supplemented by reference list screening. Clinical trials and clinical studies evaluating celecoxib, etoricoxib or rofecoxib in patients with IBD and musculoskeletal/rheumatological symptoms were considered when gastrointestinal (GI) outcomes were reported. Two placebo-controlled randomised trials did not show higher rates of IBD relapse or symptom aggravation vs placebo during short-term treatment in selected patients, including ulcerative colitis in remission and IBD with rheumatic manifestations. Open-label studies and retrospective series reported variable GI adverse events and occasional symptom worsening, but study populations, baseline disease activity, relapse definitions and follow-up duration differed widely. Overall, the strongest available evidence supports cautious short-term use of selective COX-2 inhibitors in selected patients with IBD in remission when anti-inflammatory analgesia is required. Given the small evidence base and limited follow-up, treatment should be time-limited, use the lowest effective dose and include clinical monitoring.

Introduction

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are widely used for pain, fever and inflammatory musculoskeletal disorders. Their effect is linked to inhibition of cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes and reduced prostaglandin synthesis. Prostaglandins support gastrointestinal (GI) epithelial blood flow, mucus and bicarbonate secretion and mucosal repair. For this reason, NSAID exposure can cause GI injury, including dyspepsia, peptic ulcer disease, bleeding and small-bowel injury.1

Concern about NSAID use in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is based on two lines of clinical reasoning. First, NSAID-related mucosal injury may mimic or amplify GI symptoms in patients with IBD, which can be difficult to distinguish from an inflammatory relapse in routine care.1 Second, several clinical studies have reported symptom worsening shortly after conventional NSAID exposure in a subset of patients with IBD, while other studies have not shown a clear association.1 2 Interpretation is difficult because pain can precede a flare, so NSAID use may occur as a response to early disease activity.2 In addition, exposure definitions, relapse definitions and follow-up periods differ across studies, which contributes to variation in reported results.1 2

Selective COX-2 inhibitors were developed to reduce upper GI ulceration associated with conventional NSAIDs by preferentially inhibiting COX-2 while sparing COX-1.1 In IBD care, selective COX-2 inhibitors are often considered when short-term anti-inflammatory analgesia is needed.3 Even so, selective COX-2 inhibition still changes prostaglandin pathways, and concerns remain about intestinal safety in IBD.1 4 In addition, selective COX-2 inhibitors require routine safety assessment as other NSAIDs, with particular attention to cardiovascular risk.5

Musculoskeletal symptoms are among the most frequent extraintestinal manifestations in IBD. Patients may have peripheral arthralgia, peripheral arthritis or axial spondyloarthritis, and pain control is often required even when intestinal disease is in remission.3 Guidance on extraintestinal manifestations supports symptom-directed treatment while taking medication safety into account.3 In this setting, the clinically relevant and recurring question is whether short courses of selective COX-2 inhibitors can be used for musculoskeletal pain in patients with IBD in remission without increasing the risk of intestinal relapse.68

This narrative review therefore focuses on the short-term intestinal safety of selective COX-2 inhibitors used for musculoskeletal pain in IBD remission, summarising evidence from randomised trials and clinical studies and outlining practical considerations for use.4712

Search strategy and selection of evidence

This manuscript is a narrative review based on a prespecified search strategy focused on a defined clinical question and was not designed as a formal systematic review.

A targeted literature search was performed to identify clinical evidence on selective COX-2 inhibitors used for musculoskeletal pain or rheumatological manifestations in patients with IBD, with focus on intestinal safety during remission. The search was performed in PubMed and the Cochrane Library, and was complemented by reference list screening of key evidence summaries.1 4 The final search was run on 1 February 2026.

Search strategy

In PubMed, combined terms for IBD with terms for selective COX-2 inhibitors and musculoskeletal indications were used. The main query was:

(“Inflammatory Bowel Diseases”[MeSH] OR “Crohn Disease”[MeSH] OR “Colitis, Ulcerative”[MeSH] OR inflammatory bowel disease OR Crohn* OR ulcerative colitis OR IBD)

AND (“Cyclooxygenase 2 Inhibitors”[MeSH] OR COX-2 inhibitor* OR coxib* OR celecoxib OR etoricoxib OR rofecoxib OR valdecoxib OR lumiracoxib OR meloxicam) AND (arthralgia OR arthritis OR musculoskeletal OR spondyloarthritis OR rheumat* OR peripheral arthropathy OR extraintestinal)

To reduce the risk of missing relevant trials where musculoskeletal indications were not indexed in the abstract, a broader query without the musculoskeletal term block was also used:

(IBD terms as above) AND (COX-2 inhibitor terms as above).

A repeat search was performed with meloxicam because this NSAID has partial COX-2 selectivity and may therefore be considered clinically when selective COX-2 inhibitor options are reviewed. In the Cochrane Library, the search term was “cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitors” AND “inflammatory bowel disease” and the relevant review and its included studies were screened.6

Study selection and research question

Screening was done in two steps. First, titles and abstracts were screened to identify human clinical studies that assessed a selective COX-2 inhibitor in IBD and reported GI outcomes. Second, full texts were reviewed to determine whether the study matched the clinical question: short-term COX-2 inhibitor use for musculoskeletal pain or rheumatological manifestations in patients with IBD, with preference for populations described as in remission at baseline.712 Randomised placebo-controlled trials7 8 were discussed first and given greater weight in interpretation because they compare selective COX-2 inhibitor treatment directly with placebo.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) human clinical studies (randomised trials, prospective studies, retrospective clinical series) evaluating a selective COX-2 inhibitor (celecoxib, etoricoxib, rofecoxib and other selective COX-2 inhibitors if available); (2) participants with ulcerative colitis, Crohn disease or IBD unclassified; (3) a musculoskeletal or rheumatological indication or a population where anti-inflammatory analgesia was clinically indicated and (4) reported GI outcomes during follow-up (relapse, symptom aggravation, activity indices, withdrawal due to GI symptoms or need for rescue therapy).712

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies on conventional non-selective NSAIDs without a selective COX-2 inhibitor arm (used only for background discussion); (2) non-clinical studies (animal or in vitro); (3) studies without any intestinal disease activity outcome and (4) narrative papers without original clinical data (used only for background).1 4

Data extraction

From each included clinical study, the study design; setting; IBD type; baseline disease status when reported (remission, quiescent, active, mixed); indication for selective COX-2 inhibitor use; sample size (total and group sizes); drug and dosing; treatment duration; comparator (placebo or none); definition of GI worsening; and key findings relevant to GI safety were extracted. Evidence summary

Because study designs, populations, and outcome definitions varied, results were summarised descriptively rather than pooled. Randomised placebo-controlled trials are presented first because they provide the most direct comparative short-term safety data.7 8

Summary of clinical evidence

The PubMed search using the full strategy (IBD terms AND COX-2 inhibitor terms AND musculoskeletal/extraintestinal terms) retrieved 44 records. After limiting to human studies, 35 results were left for title/abstract screening, 26 records were selected for full-text assessment and 6 studies met inclusion criteria for this review.

The broader PubMed search (IBD terms AND COX-2 inhibitor terms) retrieved 213 records. After title/abstract screening, 29 records were selected for full-text assessment. This broader search did not identify additional eligible randomised trials beyond those already captured by the full strategy but did retrieve additional background and mechanistic papers outside the clinical eligibility focus. The search including meloxicam did not identify an additional full peer-reviewed clinical study of meloxicam for musculoskeletal or rheumatological symptoms in patients with IBD with reported intestinal disease-activity outcomes. A recent conference abstract compared COX-2 inhibitor NSAID exposure with no NSAID exposure, including a celecoxib versus meloxicam comparison, but was not included in the main evidence table because only limited abstract data were available. In the Cochrane Library, the search for “cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitors” AND “inflammatory bowel disease” retrieved 18 records. Screening identified one relevant Cochrane review and its included studies.

The search identified two placebo-controlled randomised trials of selective COX-2 inhibitors in IBD patients with rheumatic or musculoskeletal symptoms and several smaller clinical studies without placebo comparison. The evidence base therefore consists mainly of short-term controlled data for celecoxib and etoricoxib, and uncontrolled data for rofecoxib and mixed coxib use.712 Table 1 summarises the different studies.

Randomised placebo-controlled trials

Celecoxib in ulcerative colitis remission (14 days)

In a placebo-controlled randomised trial, 222 patients with ulcerative colitis in remission were assigned to celecoxib 200 mg two times per day or placebo for 14 days. Disease exacerbation through day 14 occurred in 3% of the celecoxib group and 4% of the placebo group (p=0.719). Bowel-related adverse events occurred in 11% of patients in each group.7

Etoricoxib in IBD with rheumatic manifestations (3 months)

In a multicentre double-blind placebo-controlled trial, 76 patients received etoricoxib (60–120 mg once daily) and 70 received placebo for 3 months. Aggravation of underlying IBD leading to stopping treatment occurred in 8/76 (10.53%) in the etoricoxib group and 8/70 (11.43%) in the placebo group. Adverse events leading to stopping occurred during the first month, and the symptoms were reversible.8

Prospective open-label studies

Rofecoxib in IBD-associated peripheral arthropathy/arthralgia (20 days)

A prospective open-label study enrolled 32 patients with clinically inactive to mild IBD and peripheral arthropathy and/or arthritis, treated with rofecoxib 12.5–25 mg/day for 20 days. Rofecoxib was withdrawn after a few days in 3/32 (9%) due to GI complaints, and no IBD flare was reported during treatment.9

Rofecoxib in inactive IBD with arthralgia (range 3 days to 3 months)

A prospective open-label trial enrolled 45 inactive IBD patients with associated arthralgia, treated with rofecoxib 12.5 mg/day (treatment range 3 days to 3 months). In the IBD group, 9/45 (20%) required withdrawal due to GI symptoms described as inducing clinical relapse; symptoms subsided after drug discontinuation. Arthralgia relief was reported by 32/45 (71%).10

Retrospective clinical series

Mixed celecoxib or rofecoxib use in IBD (median 9 months exposure)

A retrospective series identified 27 patients (11 celecoxib; 16 rofecoxib). The report describes 2/27 (7.4%) with aggravations of IBD and 3/27 (11%) with other adverse events (renal insufficiency, rash, asymptomatic colonic ulceration), all reversible.11

Celecoxib and/or rofecoxib use in IBD (up to 6 weeks)

A retrospective series identified 33 patients prescribed celecoxib and/or rofecoxib. Disease exacerbation was reported in 13/33 (39%), occurring within 6 weeks of initiation. Five of the 13 had symptom resolution after discontinuation, and the remainder required additional medical therapy; other GI adverse events were also reported.12 Drug-specific intestinal event counts were not sufficiently reported to draw separate conclusions for celecoxib and rofecoxib.

Across studies, GI safety findings differ mainly because the strongest evidence comes from placebo-controlled trials with short follow-up and selected participants, while the uncontrolled studies and retrospective series include more variable baseline disease activity, broader clinical indications, and less uniform definitions of relapse.712 In addition, in routine care, COX-2 inhibitors may be started in periods of evolving symptoms, and this can make it difficult to separate drug-related symptom worsening from early relapse that would have occurred regardless of treatment.1 2

Critical appraisal of the evidence

The two placebo-controlled trials provide the most direct short-term safety evidence, but both have limitations. The celecoxib trial was limited to ulcerative colitis in remission and lasted only 14 days, and it was not designed to assess repeated or long-term use.7 The etoricoxib trial included both Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis and had a longer treatment period, but the number of participants limits precision for less frequent GI outcomes.8 The open-label and retrospective studies are more vulnerable to selection bias, confounding by indication and variable relapse definitions.912 These limitations mean that the evidence supports cautious short-term use in selected patients, but does not allow broad use or a clear preference for one selective COX-2 inhibitor over another

Discussion

This review focused on a narrow clinical question: whether short courses of selective COX-2 inhibitors can be used for musculoskeletal pain in patients with IBD in remission without increasing the risk of disease relapse. The main comparative evidence comes from two placebo-controlled trials. In ulcerative colitis remission, celecoxib for 14 days was not associated with a higher relapse rate than placebo.7 In a mixed IBD population treated for rheumatic symptoms, etoricoxib for up to 3 months showed similar rates of IBD symptom aggravation compared with placebo.8 These controlled data support that, for selected patients and limited treatment duration, COX-2 inhibition does not appear to cause a large short-term increase in relapse risk.

In contrast, uncontrolled studies and retrospective series report variable tolerability and variable proportions of symptom worsening after COX-2 inhibitor initiation.912 A case report described an exacerbation of IBD temporally associated with celecoxib use.13 Differences between trials and routine-care studies are expected. Trials apply eligibility criteria, define baseline status and outcomes more precisely, and include placebo comparison, while retrospective series often include heterogeneous baseline intestinal activity, uncertain timing of exposure in relation to early relapse symptoms, and non-uniform definitions of relapse.12 912 Confounding by indication is a central issue: musculoskeletal pain may precede, accompany, or follow inflammatory activity, so COX-2 inhibitor initiation in routine care may coincide with early relapse rather than cause it.2 This limits causal inference from uncontrolled data.

Clinical implications

Musculoskeletal symptoms are common extraintestinal manifestations in IBD, and symptom control is often needed even when intestinal disease is quiescent.3 For mild pain, non-pharmacological measures and paracetamol can be considered, but these options may be insufficient for inflammatory pain.1 When an anti-inflammatory analgesic is clinically needed in a patient in remission, the controlled evidence supports that a short course of celecoxib or etoricoxib can be considered, with attention to dose and duration.7 8 A practical approach is to use the lowest effective dose for the shortest period and to advise prompt discontinuation and clinical review if bowel symptoms occur.3 6 Initiation during active intestinal disease should generally be avoided when alternatives exist, because baseline activity makes attribution difficult and may increase risk of symptom worsening regardless of drug choice.1 2

Non-IBD safety remains important. Selective COX-2 inhibitors are a subgroup of NSAIDs and require standard assessment of cardiovascular risk, renal function, and concomitant medicines.5 Proton pump inhibitors reduce upper GI complications for many NSAID users, but they may not prevent all lower GI injury, and they do not address cardiovascular risk.1 5

Comparison between selective COX-2 inhibitors

The available IBD data do not show that one selective COX-2 inhibitor is clearly safer for the intestine than another. Celecoxib has placebo-controlled evidence in ulcerative colitis remission for 14 days, while etoricoxib has placebo-controlled evidence in mixed IBD with rheumatic manifestations for 3 months.7 8 but the European Medicines Agency gives specific warnings on blood pressure and cardiovascular risk, and etoricoxib was not approved in the USA because of cardiovascular safety concerns. Rofecoxib data are open-label or retrospective, and the drug is no longer available in many countries due to cardiovascular event risk.1416 Mechanistically, all selective COX-2 inhibitors reduce COX-2-mediated prostaglandin synthesis; no clinical IBD study has shown a specific intestinal advantage of one drug based on this mechanism. Drug choice should therefore be based on availability, dose, treatment duration, patient cardiovascular and renal risk and previous patient response, rather than a proven intestinal safety difference between agents.

Relation to earlier reviews

Several evidence summaries and reviews have already examined NSAIDs and IBD activity, and COX-2 inhibitor safety has been addressed in a Cochrane review and a meta-analysis.2 6 17 A recent conference abstract based on routinely collected electronic healthcare records compared COX-2 inhibitor NSAID exposure with no NSAID exposure and also compared celecoxib with meloxicam. The abstract reported no increased risk of IBD activity in IBD cohorts, but it was not included in the main evidence table because a full peer-reviewed report was not available, and the abstract did not provide sufficient detail on remission status, indication, dose, duration or outcome assessment.18

The present paper adds value by restricting the scope to a clinically common scenario (short-term anti-inflammatory analgesia for musculoskeletal symptoms during remission) and by summarising the individual studies in a structured table with sample size, exposure duration and intestinal outcomes.712 However, the evidence base is small, and conclusions should remain limited to short-term use in selected patients.4 7 8

Limitations of the evidence

The main limitation is the limited number of placebo-controlled trials and the limited follow-up.4 7 8 Controlled data are strongest for short exposure (days to a few months), and do not address repeated courses or chronic use.7 8 Many observational reports use symptom-based definitions of relapse, which can misclassify drug-related dyspepsia or functional symptoms as IBD activity, and may miss subclinical inflammation.912 Finally, most available studies predate current biologic and small-molecule treatment patterns, which may change baseline relapse risk and concomitant medication use.1 2

Pediatric evidence

We did not identify placebo-controlled trials of celecoxib, etoricoxib, or other selective COX-2 inhibitors that specifically enrolled paediatric IBD populations with musculoskeletal pain. Published paediatric literature mainly discusses general NSAID and COX-2 inhibitor use patterns and safety concerns in children rather than intestinal relapse outcomes in paediatric IBD.19 20

Future research

Useful next steps include prospective pragmatic studies that enrol patients in documented remission, use a standard definition of relapse (symptoms plus objective markers such as faecal calprotectin and C-reactive protein) and record the exact timing of pain onset relative to relapse.2 Trials comparing a COX-2 inhibitor with a nonselective NSAID in carefully selected remission patients with inflammatory musculoskeletal pain would address the current gap while also monitoring cardiovascular and renal outcomes.5 Future trials should report Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis separately because disease location, relapse pattern, surgery and drug treatment may differ. In addition, future studies should compare available selective COX-2 inhibitors with each other and with nonselective NSAIDs when clinically appropriate while recording clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

In patients with IBD in remission who require short-term anti-inflammatory analgesia for musculoskeletal pain, selective COX-2 inhibitors have supportive short-term GI safety data from placebo-controlled trials. Because evidence is limited in time and individual susceptibility exists, cautious short-term use with monitoring seems appropriate. Current evidence does not establish a preferred selective COX-2 inhibitor for GI safety.
