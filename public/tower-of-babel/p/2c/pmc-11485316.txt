Distinct regional vulnerability to Aβ and iron accumulation in post mortem AD brains
Authors: Junye Yao, Zhenghao Li, Zihan Zhou, Aimin Bao, Zheng Wang, Hongjiang Wei, Hongjian He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe paramagnetic iron, diamagnetic amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and their interaction are crucial in Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathogenesis, complicating non‐invasive magnetic resonance imaging for prodromal AD detection.METHODSWe used a state‐of‐the‐art sub‐voxel quantitative susceptibility mapping method to simultaneously measure Aβ and iron levels in post mortem human brains, validated by histology. Further transcriptomic analysis using Allen Human Brain Atlas elucidated the underlying biological processes.RESULTSRegional increased paramagnetic and diamagnetic susceptibility were observed in medial prefrontal, medial parietal, and para‐hippocampal cortices associated with iron deposition (R = 0.836, p = 0.003) and Aβ accumulation (R = 0.853, p = 0.002) in AD brains. Higher levels of gene expression relating to cell cycle, post‐translational protein modifications, and cellular response to stress were observed.DISCUSSIONThese findings provide quantitative insights into the variable vulnerability of cortical regions to higher levels of Aβ aggregation, iron overload, and subsequent neurodegeneration, indicating changes preceding clinical symptoms.Highlights The vulnerability of distinct brain regions to amyloid beta (Aβ) and iron accumulation varies.Histological validation was performed on stained sections of ex‐vivo human brains.Regional variations in susceptibility were linked to gene expression profiles.Iron and Aβ levels in ex‐vivo brains were simultaneously quantified.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) represents a relentless neurodegenerative condition characterized by progressive brain pathologies, culminating in cognitive decline and dementia. 1 The challenge in diagnosing AD arises from the absence of a biomarker capable of identifying early pathological changes. Typically, diagnoses occur in the later stages, relying on clinical criteria supplemented by morphological indicators like hippocampal atrophy. 2 However, microscopic alterations which signal the onset of disease often occur prior to observable morphological and clinical manifestations. 3 This highlights the urgent need for a sensitive biomarker capable of detecting these early microscopic and pathological changes. 4 Among the defining features of AD is the formation of amyloid plaques resulting from the aggregation of amyloid beta (Aβ), marking a pivotal milestone in the disease's progression. 2 Additionally, abnormal iron homeostasis has emerged as a potential contributor to AD pathogenesis, 5 with growing evidence suggesting a complex interplay between iron and Aβ. 6

Positron emission tomography (PET) is commonly used to detect Aβ plaques in AD but is limited by factors such as ionizing radiation exposure, low resolution, and high cost. 7 , 8 Alternatively, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) presents a non‐invasive and cost‐effective alternative for Aβ plaque detection in clinical settings. MRI's ability to detect Aβ plaques is closely linked to the presence of focal iron deposition within the plaques, as evidenced by quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM). 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 This advanced MRI technique allows for the quantification of local magnetic susceptibility values and the mapping of iron distribution and alterations, primarily through the T2* shortening effects of paramagnetic iron. 13 , 14 , 15 However, interpreting MRI findings is complex due to the coexistence of paramagnetic and diamagnetic susceptibility sources within macro‐scale imaging voxels. 9 , 16 For instance, diamagnetic sources like myelin and neurodegeneration‐related protein accumulations exhibit negative susceptibility values, 17 , 18 potentially confounding the interpretation of iron‐related changes. 19 Thus, untangling the contributions of these contrasting susceptibility sources is crucial for understanding AD progression accurately.

Recent advancements in susceptibility imaging methods have been developed to differentiate the contributions from various susceptibility sources. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 One notable computational model, known as the iterative magnetic susceptibility sources separation (APART‐QSM), 21 incorporates frequency shift and T2* relaxation differences. We investigated regional Aβ aggregation and brain iron levels in four ex vivo right hemispheres from individuals with AD and healthy controls using a 7T human scanner. The imaging results were benchmarked against the gold standard of Aβ immunohistochemistry. To delve deeper into the molecular mechanisms underpinning these pathologies, we analyzed gene expression using transcriptomic data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas. 24 Using partial least squares (PLS) regression and gene ontological (GO) tools, 25 , 26 , 27 we identified biological processes linked to this transcriptomic profile, assessing whether patterns of gene expression correlate with cortical brain Aβ aggregation and iron accumulation in AD. This analysis sheds light on the gene expression profiles that may predispose certain cortical regions to increased Aβ aggregation and iron accumulation, potentially leading to neurodegeneration.

METHODS

In this study, four right hemispheres of the formalin‐fixed human brain (two AD, two healthy control [HC], see Table 1 for pathological and demographic information, Figure 1 for an overview of data processing) were obtained from the National Human Brain Bank for Health and Disease, China. Prior to death, the donors had been registered at the Zhejiang University School of Medicine and signed informed authorization for the use of their tissue and medical records for research purposes. This study was conducted with the prior approval of the ethics committee of Zhejiang University School of Medicine.

Post mortem brains were processed as in a previous study. 28 For the first 4 weeks, the hemisphere was immersed in 10% formalin. It was then rinsed with tap water for 24 hours to partially restore the relaxation parameters. 29 , 30 The sample was placed in a container full of Fomblin (Fomblin, YL VAC25/6, Solvay) after wiping the water off the surface. Prior to the MR scan, air bubbles were extracted from the containers using a vacuum pump, and the sample was kept at room temperature (22 ± 0.5°C) for 24 hours.

MRI data acquisition

The MRI data were acquired on a 7T MAGNETOM scanner using a NOVA head‐neck coil (Siemens Healthcare). The 3D multi‐echo gradient echo (GRE) scan parameters were: spatial resolution = 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.4 mm3; repetition time (TR) = 46 ms; echo times (TEs) = 5.62/16.48/27.34/38.2 ms; fractional anisotropy (FA) = 18°; bandwidth (BW) = 120 Hz/Px. The R2 mapping data were acquired using a 2D multi‐echo spin‐echo (MSE) sequence: spatial resolution = 1 × 1 × 2 mm3; TR = 2050 ms; TEs = 11/23/34/46/57/69 ms; FA = 180°; BW = 163 Hz/Px.

To address this, this study used MRI and pathological data from four post mortem human brain hemispheres, along with transcriptomic data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas, aiming to explore the regional vulnerability to iron and Aβ accumulation in AD brains, as well as the underlying biological mechanisms. Interpretation: The findings provide quantitative insights into the variable vulnerability of cortical regions to higher levels of Aβ aggregation, iron overload, and subsequent neurodegeneration in AD, indicating changes preceding clinical symptoms. MRI data analysis

For each hemisphere, the R2 map was generated by fitting the MSE data using a mono‐exponential function in qMRLab. 31 The acquired GRE images were processed using the STI‐Suite v3.0 toolbox, and phase images were unwrapped by a Laplacian‐based phase unwrapping method. 32 Background field removal was completed using the variable‐kernel sophisticated harmonic artifact reduction for phase data (V‐SHARP) method, 33 followed by the estimation of susceptibility maps using streaking artifacts reduction in QSM (STAR‐QSM). 34 , 35 ,

Finally, the phase data, GRE magnitude data, and R2 map were integrated into the APART‐QSM algorithm, 21 to separate paramagnetic (Xpara) and diamagnetic susceptibility (negative susceptibility values, absolute values |Xdia| are used in the following sections) on MATLAB R2019b (The MathWorks, Inc.). The magnitude image of the initial echo from GRE data underwent alignment to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using Advanced Normalization Tools with “SyN” parameters. 36 Subsequently, the inverse warping field from standard to native space was derived by reversing the warping field. This field was then implemented on the multi‐modal parcellation (MMP) atlas, 37 transitioning from MNI152 space to individual susceptibility map. Ultimately, mean susceptibility values within 180 regions of interest (ROIs) were calculated from all subjects, using the ROIs in native space. The difference between AD and HC group‐wise mean values was calculated. ROIs exhibiting low GRE signal due to B1 inhomogeneity were excluded, leaving a total of 168 ROIs for further analysis and resulting in three 168 × 1 vectors for Xpara, Xdia, and QSM difference. The exclude ROIs are listed in Table S1 in supporting information.

Due to the limited sample size, a two‐sample t test was performed to assess the voxel distribution within each ROI between AD and HC, combining voxels from AD1 and AD2, as well as HC1 and HC2.

Histological staining and statistical analysis

After MRI, the brain samples were sectioned in 5 mm thick coronal blocks. These tissue blocks were then prepared for embedding in paraffin and cut into sections with a thickness of 6 μm. Based on the standardized operational protocol for human brain banking in China, 38 six cortical regions were selected for immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining to detect Aβ aggregation of AD samples, including middle frontal gyrus, inferior parietal lobe, occipital pole, posterior hippocampus, para‐hippocampal and entorhinal cortex. In this study, IHC staining was performed using antibodies for Aβ (6F/3D; 1:10; Novocastra Vector Labs) and phosphorylated tau (AT8; 1:1000; Endogen), 39 to validate the accuracy of the estimated diamagnetic susceptibility for measuring Aβ aggregation. Despite the fact that cortical regions typically have minimal myelin content, 40 the tissue sections of these regions were also subjected to histochemical staining for myelin using the Luxol fast blue (LFB) technique (Figure S1 in supporting information). Additionally, Perls diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining was used to validate the accuracy of the estimated paramagnetic susceptibility for quantifying iron levels in the same regions.

Digitization of the stained sections was performed on a Leica DM6B upright microscope using a 10× magnifying objective, which led to a resolution of 0.69 μm/pixel. The Aβ, tau, and iron aggregation levels in cortical ROIs were quantified in stained sections using Image‐J and IHC‐toolbox. 41

The investigation began with the use of an initial IHC stained slice (Figure S2 in supporting information), where the training process commenced to develop a statistical color detection model using visually selected color pixels. The training process was initiated by selecting an ROI using a rectangular tool within ImageJ, specifically focusing on an IHC‐positive area. Subsequently, the model was automatically generated and computed to highlight the IHC‐positive pixels within the slice. After this, each slice underwent conversion to an 8‐bit format, and a slice‐specific threshold was determined using the default ISODATA algorithm to mitigate the presence of imperfections and cracks. The positive area within the cortical sub‐ROIs was quantified using the Analyze Particles Tool, with a minimum pixel area size threshold set to 200 to exclude insignificant background elements, ensuring exclusive counting of Aβ plaques. The “Show: Outlines” option was selected to exhibit a representation of the identified objects for visual inspection (Figure S3 in supporting information). The resulting area (%) represents the IHC‐positive area within the sub‐ROI. Finally, the values of all sub‐ROIs were tallied and averaged post‐removal of the maximum and minimum values, yielding the percentage of IHC‐positive area for the slice.

Accurately aligning MRI (slice thickness of 0.4 mm in this study) with histology‐stained slices (6 μm thickness) in post mortem human brain research poses a common challenge in neuroimaging studies. The manual selection of ROIs in this study introduced subjective bias due to the absence of a robust atlas registration method. To mitigate this bias, cortical anatomical landmarks were used for registration (Figure 1B), following previous methodologies. The delineation process was conducted by two dependent observers; only the intersecting regions identified by both observers were considered for further analysis. The correspondence between the statistical results of stained sections and susceptibility values are listed in Table 2. In addition, linear regression analysis was applied to mean susceptibility values and normalized staining values of five cortical regions in two AD hemispheres.

Relating gene expression and APART‐QSM maps

Gene expression data were obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas microarray transcriptomic data and projected onto the MMP Atlas using the “abagen” toolkit. 24 , 42 Only two donors for the right hemisphere were included to maintain consistency with the post mortem brains. Genes with expression levels above a background threshold of 50% were selected and gene expression data were normalized across the right cortex. Regional expression levels for each gene were compiled to form 168 × 15633 regional transcription matrices. The association between the different maps of |Xdia|, Xpara, and QSM in AD versus HC and gene expression level was investigated using PLS analysis, separately. 25

PLS regression is a multivariate statistical method, similar to principal component analysis, which combines dimension reduction and linear regression, producing components from the predictor matrix X (the 168 × 15633 matrix of 168 regional mRNA measurements for 15,633 genes) that have maximum covariance with Y (three 168 × 1 vector denoting regional susceptibility difference for |Xdia|, Xpara, and QSM). The primary PLS component (PLS1) was used to weigh and rank gene predictor variables. Furthermore, 10,000 permutations based on sphere‐projection‐rotations of the cortical map were examined to test the null hypothesis that PLS1 explained no more variance in Y than autocorrelation of the neuroimaging data. 43 Bootstrapping was implemented to estimate the variability of each gene's positive or negative weight on PLS1, and the ratio of a gene's weight to its bootstrapped standard error was used to determine its impact on PLS1. All PLS and bootstrapping analyses were performed using MATLAB. The hypothesis of zero weight for each gene was tested, and a false discovery rate inverse quantile transformation correction was applied to counteract the winner's curse bias, using R version 4.3.1. 44 Only genes that survived this correction at Q < 0.05 were included in the enrichment analyses.

Gene ontological analysis

To perform a GO enrichment analysis, the g:Profiler toolset was used on the significant positively and negatively weighted genes identified by PLS1. 26 To ensure the reliability of the results, we applied a filter to the resulting list of GO terms, retaining only those that showed significant enrichment at a corrected p value threshold of <0.01 using the g:SCS algorithm. Additionally, we excluded terms associated with > 2500 genes as they were considered too general. 25 To reduce and visualize the GO terms, we used the REViGO web page tool, which is based on semantic similarity. 45

RESULTS

Increased Aβ aggregation will increase the |Xdia| values in the corresponding regions. Although tau protein also has diamagnetic properties, it was observed in IHC analysis that the aggregation of tau protein in the listed ROIs is relatively minimal compared to the aggregation of Aβ, as indicated in Table 2. In addition, the region‐wise paramagnetic susceptibility was highly correlated (Figure 2E, R = 0.836, p = 0.003) with iron positive area computed from Perls/DAB–stained sections (Figure 2F–H).

Among the 168 MMP ROIs included in the statistics, we observed that the distribution of regional |Xdia| and regional Xpara difference maps (Figure 4A,B) have a significant degree of spatial consistency (Figure 4C, R = 0.385, P = b 2.59 × 10−7). This reflects the colocalization of Aβ and iron in the AD brains.

Beyond colocalization, we identified a distinct distribution pattern of Aβ and iron across the brain, which is difficult to discern with conventional QSM. Our findings indicate a higher |Xdia| difference than Xpara difference in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC; Figure 4A, arrows), suggesting a relatively high level of Aβ accumulation in these areas. Conversely, in the precentral, middle frontal, and temporal cortices (Figure 4B, arrows), we observed a higher Xpara difference than |Xdia| difference, indicating a comparatively more significant iron deposition in these regions.

Relating cortical Aβ in AD to variation in gene expression patterns

We used PLS regression to identify the pattern of gene expression that correlated with the anatomical distribution of brain Aβ, as measured using |Xdia|. The results revealed that PLS1 accounted for the most (27.8%) of the variance observed in the |Xdia| difference map. The spatial profile of |Xdia| difference map matched that of the PLS1 weightings (Figure 5A,B), particularly in the frontal cortex, parieto‐temporal‐occipital junction area. Furthermore, the gene expression weights associated with PLS1 exhibited a strong positive correlation (Figure 5C, R = 0.527, p = 2.11 × 10−13) with the differences in |Xdia| between AD and HC. This implies that genes assigned higher weights on PLS1 also exhibited higher expression levels in cortical brain regions characterized by increased diamagnetic susceptibilities. Similarly, genes with negative weights on PLS1 demonstrated relatively lower expression levels in cortical brain regions exhibiting higher |Xdia| differences. We therefore used PLS1 to rank and select significantly weighted genes, giving a set of 2408 significantly upweighted genes (Q < 0.01). The complete set of PLS1 gene weights and associated statistics are provided in Table S2 in supporting information.

Using GO analyses, we found sets of biological processes associated with upweighted genes (Figure 6). Genes more highly expressed in regions with higher |Xdia| values in AD were enriched for GO terms relating to cell cycle, cellular response to stress, post‐translational protein modification, macromolecule catabolic process, and chromatin organization. Full tables of the GO terms associated with upweighted genes are provided in Table S3 in supporting information. Our additional control analyses confirmed that these findings were specific to the |Xdia| values, rather than spuriously arising from gene–gene co‐expression. Specifically, the control analysis using a spatial spin of the cortical data revealed upweighted genes that were enriched for cytoplasmic translation. See Table S4 in supporting information for the full list of the GO terms associated with the upweighted genes from the control spatial‐spin null analyses. These control analyses provide additional support that the gene lists arising from our experimental analysis relating spatial variation in |Xdia| with gene expression are specific to this relationship and have not arisen spuriously due to gene–gene co‐expression or spatial autocorrelation.

Relating cortical brain iron in AD to variation in gene expression patterns

The same approach as |Xdia| was used for PLS regression for the purpose of discerning the correlation between gene expression patterns and the anatomical distribution of brain iron as measured by Xpara. The PLS1 explained the most (33.2%) variance in the Xpara difference map (Figure 5D) and the PLS1 gene expression weights (Figure 5E) showed a strong positive correlation with the Xpara difference between AD and HC (Figure 5F, R = 0.577, p = 2.90 × 10−16). To rank and select genes with substantial weights, we still used PLS1, resulting in the identification of 2886 genes with significant upweighting (Q < 0.01). The complete set of PLS1 gene weights and associated statistics are provided in Table S5 in supporting information.

Using GO analyses, we found sets of biological processes associated with upweighted genes (Figure 6A). Genes more highly expressed in regions with higher Xpara values in AD were enriched for GO terms relating to the establishment of localization in cell, post‐translational protein modification, cellular response to stress, synaptic signaling, cell death, and regulation of transport. Full tables of the GO terms associated with upweighted genes are provided in Table S6 in supporting information. Our additional control analyses also confirmed that these findings were specific to the Xpara values (Table S7 in supporting information), rather than spuriously arising from gene–gene co‐expression.

However, when relating traditional QSM in AD to variation in gene expression patterns, we generated an upweighted and downweighted gene list that were not significantly enriched for any GO biological processes (Table S8 in supporting information).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the coexistence and specific spatial pattern of paramagnetic iron and diamagnetic Aβ plaques, and their potential molecular mechanisms in post mortem human brain samples. The results support that AD progression is influenced by spatial colocalization of brain iron deposits with Aβ plaques. Meanwhile, Aβ plays distinct roles in the medial prefrontal cortex, while iron exerts its effects in the temporal cortex. The quantification of altered diamagnetic and paramagnetic susceptibility may provide insights into these processes, indicating preclinical brain changes prior to clinical symptoms and brain atrophy.

For clarity and consistency with previous research, alterations in paramagnetic susceptibility values will be denoted as variations in iron levels, given the established correlation between susceptibility values and tissue iron levels in brain gray matter. 13 , 19 , 46 Additionally, diamagnetic susceptibility values will be associated with Aβ aggregation levels, drawing from previous studies in brain cortical regions, 17 , 47 and our IHC experiment (Figure 2A). When interpreting the current results, it needs to be considered that myelin, 48 and phosphorylated tau within neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) also exhibit diamagnetism. The cortical gray matter regions under investigation in this study exhibit quite low myelin content based on LFB‐stained sections (Figure S1), making the myelin contribution to the susceptibility signal negligible. The neurofibrillary degeneration starts in the allocortex of the medial temporal lobe (entorhinal cortex and hippocampus) and spreads to the associative isocortex, relatively sparing the primary sensory, motor, and visual areas. 49 In our study, the NFT‐positive area in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus is relatively higher compared to other regions, but is still low compared to Aβ (Table 2). Unlike NFTs, Aβ plaques primarily accumulate in the isocortex, the cortical regions where the initial accumulation of Aβ occurs during the early pre‐clinical stage. 50 Therefore, in this study, the |Xdia| change in the cortical region mainly reflects the degree of Aβ aggregation.

The pathological validation has confirmed that the dia‐ and paramagnetic susceptibility values hold promise in quantifying the levels of Aβ and iron deposition in the brain tissue of patients. Post mortem brain tissue remains static during scans, allowing for longer scan times and higher resolutions. However, challenges are encountered in in vivo studies due to the inherently low spatial resolution of in vivo MRI scans, leading to partial volume effects and ghosting artifacts that can impact signal analysis. Additionally, factors such as blood flow and cerebrospinal fluid have the potential to influence magnetic susceptibility quantification. By incorporating black blood imaging or fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery techniques into a correction framework, the practicality and accuracy of the algorithm in in vivo imaging can be improved. Future research efforts will concentrate on refining the technology and broadening its applications in patient imaging.

The observation of a spatial colocalization of Aβ plaques and iron, as indicated by the diamagnetic and paramagnetic susceptibility map, aligns with previous findings from MRI and histology studies that have shown a strong association between iron accumulation and cortical Aβ distribution. 51 , 52 The depicted correlation (Figure 4C) between cortical iron levels and Aβ aggregation load in these specific brain regions implies that increased cerebral iron content is linked to the localized buildup of Aβ plaques in individuals with AD. Brain iron accumulation has been found to accelerate disease progression in Aβ‐positive AD patients. 53 , 54 , 55 The interaction between iron and Aβ may result in increased toxicity through the production of redox‐active iron species and oxidative stress. 56 , 57 Further, abnormal iron deposition has been detected in Aβ plaques in histologic evaluation of post mortem brains from AD patients. 58 Our findings support the interaction between Aβ aggregation and excess iron in the pathogenesis of AD. Therefore, iron‐targeted non‐pharmacological therapeutic strategies like physical exercise have become a new topic for research. 59

In addition to colocalization, we have also observed divergent spatial distribution profiles of Aβ and iron throughout the brain. The cortical pattern of increased diamagnetic susceptibility values implicates similar cortical regions to those where Aβ aggregation predominates in AD, 49 , 50 namely the medial prefrontal, medial parietal, para‐hippocampal, and insular cortices. Higher |Xdia| changes were detected in the inferior parietal cortex (Table 2), inconsistent with neuropathological studies indicating that Aβ accumulation occurs initially in cerebral regions with neuronal populations at high metabolic bio‐energetic activity rates (such as association cortices). 60 High levels of Aβ accumulation are also observed in the frontal, parietal, and temporal cortex in PET studies. 61 , 62 Noteworthy regions affected by Aβ deposition detected by PET also include the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus. 63 However, our study did not observe Aβ deposition in these regions, possibly due to individual variations. A previous study suggested that Aβ fibrils start to accumulate predominantly within certain parts of the DMN (default mode network) in preclinical AD. DMN is comprised but not limited to the mPFC, a key regulator of neurocognition. Impairment of DMN deactivation in AD often precedes the formation of the Aβ plaque and the onset of clinical symptoms, which merits further investigation. 64

In the meantime, the increased paramagnetic susceptibility values are predominantly present in precentral, middle frontal, and temporal cortices, aligning with previous studies demonstrating that the accumulation of iron increases with age mainly in the areas related to motor, cognitive, and visual functions. 65 Notably, the brain regions most often reported to show brain iron accumulation in published studies are the temporal lobe in AD, 66 a pattern also observed in the temporal cortex in this study. Notably, increases in regional iron concentration may also be attributed to the normal aging process, particularly given that the average age of the AD sample (80 years) in this study exceeds that of the healthy control sample (61 years). However, the patterns and implications of iron accumulation can exhibit notable distinctions between AD and the normal aging process. 67 Iron deposition with age typically occurs in the basal ganglia. The hippocampus and frontal cortex also show some increase in iron with age, but these changes are generally less pronounced. AD patients exhibit significantly higher levels of iron in cortical regions, particularly in the frontal and temporal lobes, 5 which correlates with cognitive decline and neuronal loss. 54 These studies align with our findings, suggesting that the iron deposition observed in AD in this study is largely driven by pathological changes. Our primary aim is to ensure that the healthy control sample remains free of abnormalities at the pathological level. Aligning the ages of the healthy control and AD samples could introduce additional variables like cerebral amyloid angiopathy due to the brain's challenge in maintaining a normal neurological state with age. In addition to age matching, the samples are sex matched, well fixed, and scanning parameters are consistently maintained for uniformity and sample preservation, thereby effectively reducing the influence of potential confounding variables.

Due to limitations in sample size, individual variations may have influenced these findings. Therefore, future investigations will aim to validate these results further through a large‐sample in vivo study.

We used PLS regression to identify the pattern of gene expression that best mapped to the spatial distribution of the dia‐ and paramagnetic difference between AD and HC, respectively (Figure 5). Due to the colocalization of Aβ and iron in some regions in AD brains, those genes with higher expression in regions with more Aβ aggregation in AD as measured by |Xdia| (upweighted on PLS1) and genes with higher expression in regions with more brain iron deposition in AD as measured by Xpara (upweighted on PLS1) were both significantly enriched for biological processes relating to cell cycle, post‐translational protein modifications, macromolecule catabolic process, ubiquitin‐dependent protein catabolic process, cellular response to stress, and intracellular transport (Figure 6). This is likely to be explained by the fact that AD is a multifactorial disease characterized by extracellular deposits of the misfolded Aβ protein, as well as NFTs composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. 49 Various pathological pathways are regulated by the intricate interactions and aberrant post‐translational modifications (PTMs) of a variety of proteins. 68 Previous studies using leverage proteomic techniques to identify communities of proteins linked to AD susceptibility have emphasized the crucial role of PTMs in disease progression. 69 Specifically, modules enriched for phosphorylation and ubiquitination have been associated with abnormal amyloid status. 70 , 71 In the meantime, the process of AD involves proteolytic disorders, as well as the intricate balance between anabolism and catabolism of Aβ peptides. 60

We also found different GO terms for the two ontological analyses. Those genes with higher expression in regions with more Aβ aggregation in AD were significantly enriched biological processes relating to chromatin organization and chromatin remodeling. Accumulating evidence during the last decades demonstrates that epigenetic mechanisms modulating dynamic changes in chromatin organization are critical components during the regulation of gene transcription in response to physiological cues. 72 Previous findings suggest that AD involves a reconfiguration of the epigenome by affecting disease pathways by dysregulating transcription‐ and chromatin–gene feedback loops. The identification of this process highlights potential epigenetic strategies for early‐stage disease treatment. 73

In addition, genes with higher expression in regions with more brain iron deposition in AD were significantly enriched for GO processes relating to synaptic signaling, cell death, and regulation of transport. Our findings reveal that regions showing increased iron deposition in AD have a functional abundance of cells and proteins involved in synaptic function and metal ion transport in the healthy brain. Any disturbance of such pathways could potentially render these regions vulnerable to excess iron accumulation, aberrant iron processing and subsequent downstream oxidative stress, proteinopathy, and cell death. 5 , 74 A large cohort study suggests that iron has an additional role downstream of proteinopathy, 75 by influencing the susceptibility of neurons to die (or cause synaptic damage) due to ferroptosis. While abnormally elevated iron levels do not directly trigger cell death through ferroptosis, the burden of high iron concentrations in tissues makes neurons more susceptible to ferroptotic stress. This observation offers insights into the upregulation of genes associated with cellular responses to stress and cell death.

LIMITATIONS

In our primary analysis, we used gene expression data obtained from individuals unaffected by neurological or psychiatric disorders. Consequently, the spatial expression profiles are based on unaffected individuals, rendering our findings applicable solely to this inherent diversity rather than to alterations in gene expression associated with AD. Given that the post mortem brain samples in this study exclusively consisted of right hemispheres, the analysis was restricted to data from the right hemisphere within the Allen Human Brain Atlas. However, it is important to note that right hemisphere data were only available from two brains. These factors could potentially contribute to a certain degree of inter‐individual variability.

The limited sample size (n = 4) in the study reduces statistical power, increasing the risk of Type II errors and hindering the generalizability of results. The variability in human anatomy and pathology, coupled with the heterogeneity in the AD population, necessitates larger sample sizes for robust findings representative of the disease spectrum. Post mortem studies encounter additional variability factors, complicating data interpretation. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable preliminary data, laying the groundwork for future research to confirm and expand upon the findings with larger, more diverse cohorts, enhancing the reliability and applicability of MRI‐based tools in AD research.

CONCLUSIONS

The spatial distribution pattern of paramagnetic iron and diamagnetic Aβ plaques plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of AD. We used a subvoxel QSM method to measure Aβ and iron levels concurrently in post mortem human brain hemisphere samples. Regional variations in diamagnetic and paramagnetic susceptibility are associated with a gene expression profile. In particular, increased Aβ aggregation was observed in the medial prefrontal cortex, which is linked to chromatin organization, while elevated iron levels were detected in the middle frontal and temporal cortices, associated with synaptic signaling and cell death. Aβ and iron were found to coexist in the medial parietal and para‐hippocampal cortices. These regions exhibited elevated gene expression associated with cellular stress response and post‐translational protein modifications. These findings suggest that the progression of AD may be influenced by spatial colocalization of brain iron deposits with Aβ plaques. However, the vulnerability of distinct brain regions to Aβ and iron accumulation varies; each region also exerts its unique mechanisms in the pathogenesis of AD. Our observation of altered diamagnetic and paramagnetic susceptibility may reflect these processes and thus indicate preclinical brain changes with the potential of causing progressive neuronal damage.

These findings suggest that the progression of AD may be influenced by spatial colocalization of brain iron deposits with Aβ plaques. However, the vulnerability of distinct brain regions to Aβ and iron accumulation varies, with each region contributing to the pathogenesis of AD. The observed changes in diamagnetic and paramagnetic susceptibility could reflect these processes, indicating prodromal brain changes with the potential of causing progressive neuronal damage. We propose that future studies could extend this work by including larger and more diverse samples, as well as patients at various clinical stages. This would help elucidate the correlation between Aβ and iron colocalization and disease progression.
