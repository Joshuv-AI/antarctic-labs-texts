Research advances in consistency and variability in ex vivo lung perfusion assessment metrics
Authors: Tuoyu Huang, Chenyue Wang, Bentong Yu, Guowen Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEx vivo lung perfusion (EVLP) has become an important platform for dynamic assessment and preservation of donor lungs, particularly for marginal grafts that might otherwise be declined for transplantation. Although EVLP has expanded the donor lung pool and improved opportunities for graft selection, the lack of standardized assessment criteria remains a major barrier to its broader clinical application. Considerable variability persists across centers, device platforms, perfusate compositions, perfusion flows, ventilation strategies, sampling time points, and thresholds used to determine transplant suitability. This review summarizes current evidence on the consistency and variability of EVLP assessment metrics, with emphasis on physiological parameters, ventilatory mechanics, hemodynamic indices, edema evaluation, biochemical and inflammatory biomarkers, microbiological assessment, imaging findings, and composite scoring systems. Core metrics such as the PaO2/FiO2 ratio, dynamic compliance, airway pressure, pulmonary vascular resistance, lung weight change, and inflammatory cytokine trends are widely used, but their interpretation remains highly protocol-dependent. Emerging biomarkers, radiological scores, lung ultrasound, and predictive models provide additional value, yet most remain limited by single-center validation and insufficient external reproducibility. We further discuss how device platforms, prolonged perfusion, therapeutic EVLP, and primary graft dysfunction (PGD) phenotypes influence metric interpretation. Overall, EVLP assessment should evolve from isolated static thresholds toward a hierarchical, multimodal, and dynamic framework that integrates physiological function, structural injury, inflammation, infection risk, and outcome-oriented prediction. Prospective multicenter studies with harmonized definitions, standardized data collection, and validated composite models are needed to improve donor lung selection, enable reliable cross-center comparison, and support safer expansion of lung transplantation.Keywords: Ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP), assessment metrics, consistency, variability, lung transplantation

Introduction

Ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP) has successfully transitioned from experimental research to clinical practice as a vital tool for expanding the donor pool and improving transplant outcomes (1-6). By perfusing and ventilating donor lungs normothermically ex vivo, EVLP provides a controlled period for functional assessment and preservation of marginal grafts that would otherwise be discarded, significantly increasing available lungs for transplantation (5,7-9). Although physiological improvement may be observed during EVLP, clinically established use remains centered on assessment and safe selection, whereas active reconditioning strategies are still being developed and validated. Retrospective studies show that EVLP introduction correlates with increased transplant volumes, mainly through greater use of donation after circulatory death and extended-criteria donors, while standard donor numbers remain stable (5).

The core challenge, however, is establishing objective, reproducible metrics that reliably predict post-transplant lung function. Currently, global EVLP protocols, such as Toronto and Lund, vary widely in perfusion strategies and assessment parameters, raising concerns about consistency and comparability of results (10). For example, one porcine study compared different perfusion flows (40%, 80% and 100% predicted cardiac output) in an open atrium cellular EVLP model. No flow rate enabled stable 8-hour perfusion, and groups showed no clear differences in lung function, inflammatory markers, or histopathology, highlighting the difficulty of standardizing perfusion parameters (10). Another study found that moderate-flow (30% predicted cardiac output) negative-pressure ventilation EVLP improved post-transplant oxygenation and reduced inflammation compared with low-flow (10%) protocols (11).

This variability stems not only from differences in technical protocols but also from diverse interpretations of what constitutes an acceptable donor lung. Current clinical practice relies largely on small, non-randomized, single-center studies, making decisions heavily dependent on clinical judgment and institutional experience (12). A North American expert consensus study using a modified Delphi process, for example, emphasized using EVLP to assess donor lungs of unclear quality or borderline status, including those with incomplete or concerning information from third-party organizations. The consensus noted that decisions to proceed with EVLP evaluation require considering multiple parameters such as lung compliance and deflation, the ratio of arterial oxygen partial pressure to inspired oxygen fraction (PaO2/FiO2, P/F ratio), peak airway pressure, radiographic edema and bronchoscopy findings (7). However, thresholds and weights assigned to these parameters may vary across centers.

Consistency in assessment metrics underpins multi-center data sharing and high-quality clinical research. Donor lung weight, for instance, has emerged as a potential objective metric. In cellular EVLP, lungs with lower procurement weight (<1,280 g) showed significantly higher suitability for transplantation than heavier ones (13). Conversely, variability across metrics may reveal complementary value from different assessment dimensions. Beyond traditional physiological parameters, inflammatory markers [such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-8 (IL-8)] (11,14-17), pulmonary vascular resistance (10,18) and information gained through innovative strategies, including extended perfusion time, positional changes and addition of anti-edema agents (19-23), may offer additional perspectives for comprehensive donor lung assessment. Here, we review EVLP assessment metrics, examining their consistency and variability across different applications and exploring pathways toward standardization and future development.

Core physiological and ventilatory mechanics metrics in EVLP

Gas exchange: consistency and variability

P/F ratio remains the central metric for assessing donor lung gas exchange during EVLP (24-29). Widely adopted in clinical protocols such as the Toronto approach, its threshold (for example, >400 mmHg) has achieved reasonable consensus (7). However, interpretation of this metric varies considerably. First, perfusate composition directly affects oxygen-carrying capacity (30-33). Blood-based cellular perfusates and acellular alternatives yield different P/F measurements because of their distinct oxygen transport properties (20). Second, perfusion flow rates introduce substantial variability (10,34,35). Studies show that although P/F ratios at the end of EVLP may not differ significantly between moderate-flow (30% predicted cardiac output) and low-flow (10%) protocols, the moderate-flow group demonstrates superior oxygenation after transplantation (11). Third, sampling timing matters: early EVLP measurements may better predict ultimate graft performance than pre-procurement donor P/F ratios (20,36). Together, these factors limit direct comparability across centers and protocols, underscoring the need for standardized operating procedures.

Ventilatory parameters, particularly dynamic lung compliance and peak airway pressure, serve as key indicators of lung recruitment and injury during EVLP (37-40). Major protocols monitor these metrics continuously and consider them critical for determining transplant suitability (7,41). Yet global consensus on acceptable compliance improvement or safe upper limits for plateau pressure remains elusive (2,42). For instance, a study of open atrium cellular EVLP found no significant differences in dynamic compliance or peak airway pressure across perfusion flow groups (40%, 80% and 100% cardiac output) during 8-hour perfusion, though no group achieved stable perfusion throughout, suggesting that single thresholds may prove insufficient (10). The Lund protocol may emphasize compliance trends rather than absolute values (43). Expert consensus emphasizes that decisions to initiate EVLP or determine graft suitability require integrating multiple parameters, such as compliance, deflation characteristics, P/F ratio, and peak airway pressure, through careful clinical judgment (7).

The dynamic nature of these metrics carries clinical significance: sustained improvement in gas exchange and ventilatory mechanics during EVLP generally signals reversible graft function (44-47). In portable normothermic EVLP, for example, marginal donor lungs perfused for up to 24 hours that maintain stable or improving P/F ratios without functional deterioration indicate satisfactory graft quality (20). However, protocols and centers differ in how they interpret the rate and magnitude of improvement and whether meeting an absolute threshold remains necessary for proceeding to transplantation (48,49). Some studies have successfully recovered donor lungs with initially low P/F ratios or evidence of edema through aggressive recruitment strategies [such as high positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP)], extended EVLP times or positional changes, suggesting that dynamic assessment of improvement may outweigh fixed thresholds (50). Hemodynamics and vascular resistance

Centers consistently monitor pulmonary artery pressure and calculate pulmonary vascular resistance to assess endothelial function and detect occult pulmonary hypertension (16,36,51-54). These parameters reflect the state of the pulmonary vascular bed and serve as key indicators of progressive lung injury during EVLP (54,55). However, differences in perfusion flow settings directly affect absolute pulmonary artery pressure values, limiting direct comparability across centers (10). In a porcine model, for instance, low-flow (10% cardiac output) perfusion yielded significantly higher pulmonary artery pressure and vascular resistance than moderate-flow (30%) protocols (11). Similarly, in an open atrium model, lungs perfused at 40% cardiac output showed marked increases in pulmonary vascular resistance during perfusion, whereas those in 80% and 100% groups did not (10). These findings demonstrate that fixed flow rates versus those based on body weight or predicted cardiac output produce fundamentally different hemodynamic readings (10,56,57), complicating cross-study comparisons and underscoring the need to specify perfusion protocol details when reporting data.

Monitoring perfusate weight gain or protein concentration changes to assess pulmonary edema represents another common metric (58-60). Edema formation remains a major limitation of EVLP, and quantifying its extent proves essential for determining transplant suitability (13,19,61-63). Yet quantitative standards remain elusive. Different protocols or centers may apply varying thresholds for acceptable weight gain during EVLP, reflecting differing perspectives on edema tolerance (7). Although weight gain provides an intuitive metric, varying thresholds for acceptable increase and differing assessment methods, such as weighing versus imaging, make vascular leakage a key target for EVLP standardization (64).

Biochemical and cellular biomarkers: similarities and differences

Perfusate biochemical markers

Perfusate biochemical markers during EVLP serve as key tools for assessing pulmonary inflammation, cellular injury, and endothelial-epithelial integrity (15,65). Centers widely monitor pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α (15,16,66) and IL-6 (15,45,67,68). Studies show that levels of these cytokines correlate closely with lung injury and inflammation. In a study comparing different perfusion flows, for instance, low-flow (10% cardiac output) perfusion resulted in significantly higher TNF-α and IL-6 levels throughout EVLP than moderate-flow (30%) protocols, with the moderate-flow group also demonstrating superior lung function and reduced inflammation (11). These findings suggest that dynamic changes in pro-inflammatory cytokines carry important negative predictive value. However, although elevated trends are widely accepted as indicators of poor graft function, no consensus exists across centers on specific cutoffs for determining transplant suitability (7). Beyond inflammatory markers, metabolites such as lactate dehydrogenase, glucose and lactate also serve as indicators of cellular injury and metabolic status, though their interpretation similarly suffers from protocol variability and individualized thresholds (30,69-75).

Specific markers of endothelial and epithelial injury, such as surfactant protein D, have shown promise for assessing barrier dysfunction in research settings (65,76-78). However, their application remains largely confined to exploratory studies. Whether to include these markers in routine clinical EVLP protocols and how much weight to assign them in decision-making varies considerably across centers (7,79). Thus, although such specific markers hold mechanistic value, they have not yet emerged as consistent, standardized tools for clinical decision-making. Their translation from bench to bedside will require additional prospective, multi-center studies to validate predictive performance and establish reliable reference ranges.

Cellular and microbiological assessment

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) analysis represents a core method for assessing local inflammation and infection during EVLP (74,80). However, protocols vary markedly across centers. Differences include timing of lavage (at initiation, specific time points during perfusion, or at termination), number of lavages performed, and thresholds for abnormal cell counts. Lack of standardized procedures limits direct comparability of data across centers and compromises consistency in decisions based on BAL findings. Expert consensus identifies bronchoscopy (typically including BAL) as an important parameter for lung quality assessment, but specific procedural details require further standardization (7).

Microbiological assessment, particularly bacterial and fungal cultures of perfusate or lung tissue, represents a critical step for excluding or confirming occult donor lung infection (81-84). This practice shows high consistency across clinical settings. Positive cultures generally indicate infection risk and serve as important negative predictors of transplant suitability (85). However, strategies for managing positive cultures differ considerably. Some centers attempt targeted antimicrobial therapy by adding antibiotics to the perfusate during EVLP following confirmed infection, aiming to clear or control infection and potentially salvage lungs that might otherwise be discarded (83,86). Others advise against using lungs showing Gram-negative bacterial growth (87-91). These differing approaches reflect the current lack of robust evidence-based guidelines for managing infectious complications during EVLP, with decisions relying heavily on center experience and clinical judgment. Establishing consensus on diagnostic criteria and management protocols for infection during EVLP would improve assessment consistency and expand the pool of safely usable donor lungs (Table 2).

Imaging assessment in EVLP

Imaging plays a critical role in assessing donor lung quality during EVLP (92-95). Centers widely use chest X-ray to evaluate the extent of consolidation, atelectasis and edema, with its intuitive nature gaining broad clinical acceptance (94). However, current assessment methods remain largely qualitative or semi-quantitative, such as modified radiological scoring, with no widely accepted, standardized quantitative system (96). This lack of standardization leads to significant interobserver variability and limits direct comparability of findings across transplant centers, compromising consistency and reliability of clinical decisions based on imaging results. Bronchoscopy, by contrast, represents a standard assessment component across all major EVLP protocols, enabling direct visualization of airway secretions, mucosal appearance, and potential injury. Its application shows high consistency. Expert consensus identifies bronchoscopic findings as important parameters for determining whether to transplant EVLP-treated lungs (7). However, no unified grading system exists for quantifying findings such as airway edema or mucosal hyperemia severity, limiting objectivity and comparability of these assessments (96).

Composite scoring systems and decision models: development and challenges

Common ground in existing composite scoring systems

Current expert consensus and clinical practice in EVLP clearly recognize that relying on any single metric to predict post-transplant outcomes proves unreliable; instead, integrating multiple parameters for comprehensive assessment remains essential (7). This trend toward multi-parameter integration forms the core philosophy of modern evaluation frameworks. Although centers differ in specific procedures and parameter weighting, the core dimensions constituting assessment systems show remarkable consistency. These shared dimensions include gas exchange efficacy (such as P/F ratio), lung mechanics (compliance, airway pressure), vascular integrity/edema (weight change, pulmonary artery pressure), and infection/inflammation status (cytokine levels) (11,13). This shared logic of assessing core pathophysiological dimensions provides the foundation for comparing and contextualizing different scoring systems.

Divergence in scoring systems and validation challenges

Despite consensus on assessment dimensions, individual research groups differ markedly in parameter selection and weight assignment when developing predictive models. This variability stems partly from technical diversity. Perfusion flow strategies during EVLP, for instance, directly influence measured parameters. Studies comparing moderate-flow (30% cardiac output) and low-flow (10%) perfusion found no significant differences in post-EVLP oxygenation, yet the moderate-flow group showed superior oxygenation after transplantation, whereas low-flow lungs exhibited higher pulmonary artery pressure, vascular resistance and inflammatory cytokine levels (11). These findings suggest that models developed under different perfusion protocols may prioritize entirely different key predictors, such as early hemodynamic metrics versus terminal oxygenation, and assign them varying importance. Furthermore, some models may emphasize dynamic changes in metabolic or biochemical markers, whereas others rely on imaging scores or static physiological thresholds. Such divergence poses substantial challenges for validation and generalizability. Most current scoring systems derive from single-center data. When applied to external donor populations, centers using different EVLP technical details or recipient matching strategies, predictive performance often declines (7). The absence of large-scale, prospective, multi-center validation studies represents the primary obstacle to establishing gold-standard assessment frameworks (96). A critical point of divergence lies in the tension between static thresholds and dynamic assessment trajectories. Some protocols emphasize achieving absolute thresholds, such as a terminal P/F ratio exceeding 400 mmHg, whereas others prioritize dynamic improvement trends during EVLP. For marginal donor lungs, studies show that final EVLP performance proves difficult to predict from pre-procurement donor P/F ratios but can be effectively predicted from early EVLP measurements (20). This distinction may lead to fundamentally different clinical decisions when assessing borderline grafts. Yet most existing scoring systems remain anchored to endpoint thresholds, failing to integrate dynamic trajectories throughout perfusion. This gap underscores the urgent need for unified, rigorously validated dynamic composite scoring systems (Table 3, Figure 1).

Device platforms, clinical trial evidence and operational models

Modern EVLP assessment must be interpreted in the context of device platform and clinical workflow. Stationary acellular systems, exemplified by the Toronto/XVIVO approach, are generally used after cold transport for in-house or regional reassessment, whereas portable blood-based systems such as the TransMedics Organ Care System (OCS) Lung allow perfusion, ventilation and functional assessment during donor-to-recipient transport. The INSPIRE randomized phase 3 study showed that portable normothermic OCS Lung preservation for standard-criteria bilateral lung transplantation was non-inferior to cold storage and reduced early severe primary graft dysfunction (PGD), while the EXPAND pivotal trial extended this concept to extended-criteria and donation-after-circulatory-death donor lungs, reporting 87% use of eligible lungs and 91% 1-year survival despite a higher-risk donor population (97,98).

Long-term and large-volume center experience further supports the safety of EVLP when used as a structured assessment platform rather than as an isolated metric. The Toronto Lung Transplant Program reported no significant difference in chronic lung allograft dysfunction-free survival or allograft survival between EVLP and non-EVLP recipients during long-term follow-up (99). Subsequent Toronto analyses showed that the indication for EVLP influences utilization and recipient outcomes, and a 1,000-case single-center experience highlighted the scalability of EVLP within a mature clinical program (100,101). These data indicate that assessment variability is partly a function of system design, case selection and center experience, not only of individual physiologic thresholds.

Operational models are also evolving. Remote EVLP at centralized evaluation facilities and mobile perfusion systems may broaden donor access, reduce unnecessary travel and standardize specialized assessment, but they introduce new variables related to transportation time, handoff protocols, data capture and final acceptance decisions (102). Prolonged EVLP and portable normothermic preservation can extend the observation window, yet longer support demands tighter control of edema, ventilation, perfusate composition, metabolism and infection surveillance. Therefore, future reporting should specify the device, perfusate type, flow strategy, ventilation settings, assessment time points, transport model and total support duration before metrics are compared across centers.

PGD phenotypes and outcome-oriented risk stratification

PGD should be considered not only as a binary post-transplant endpoint but also as a heterogeneous clinical phenotype that may differ according to donor risk profile, preservation platform and perioperative support. In the EXPAND trial, early PGD3 was frequent when baseline and early time points were included, but PGD3 at 72 hours was much lower, suggesting that transient early dysfunction after EVLP or OCS preservation may not carry the same prognostic meaning as persistent severe PGD (98). A recent Toronto analysis specifically comparing ISHLT PGD3 after EVLP with PGD3 after direct transplantation reported differential outcomes between these groups, supporting the concept that PGD after EVLP may represent a distinct phenotype rather than a uniform marker of irreversible graft injury (103).

This distinction has direct implications for EVLP assessment metrics. Oxygenation, compliance, pulmonary vascular resistance, edema and inflammatory biomarkers should be interpreted together with the pattern and trajectory of early graft dysfunction. For example, transient early oxygenation impairment that improves rapidly may reflect reperfusion physiology or recruitment dynamics, whereas persistent PGD with worsening mechanics, vascular resistance and inflammatory markers may indicate more severe endothelial-epithelial injury. Recent comparative biomarker data from standard-criteria and marginal donor lungs further support using biomarker patterns as contextual information rather than isolated acceptance thresholds (104). Incorporating PGD phenotype, timing and resolution into composite models may improve risk stratification and make outcome comparisons between EVLP and standard grafts more clinically meaningful.

Therapeutic and translational applications of EVLP

Although routine clinical EVLP is primarily an assessment and preservation platform, it also creates a uniquely accessible environment for targeted organ therapy and translational research. Antibiotic delivery during EVLP has reduced bacterial burden and improved donor lung function in infected human lungs rejected for transplantation (86). Gene-based and biologic strategies, including IL-10 gene delivery, recombinant IL-10 delivery and engineered mesenchymal stromal cell therapy, illustrate the potential and limitations of anti-inflammatory repair during human EVLP (105-107). Other approaches, including thrombolysis, perfusate purification, lavage/surfactant therapy, ultraviolet perfusate treatment and metabolic supplementation, are best viewed as emerging or experimental interventions until supported by larger clinical trials (75,108).

These translational applications reinforce rather than replace the need for standardized assessment. Therapeutic EVLP may alter the very metrics used for graft acceptance—including PaO2/FiO2, compliance, pulmonary vascular resistance, cytokines, microbiology, and edema—so studies must distinguish physiological stabilization from true organ repair. Extended normothermic perfusion, nutritional/metabolic regulation and extracorporeal cross-circulation models may further lengthen the therapeutic window and provide homeostatic support beyond conventional EVLP, but they also require rigorous endpoints, safety monitoring and reproducible protocols before broad clinical adoption (75,109).

Conclusions

EVLP serves as a bridge for expanding the donor pool and improving transplant outcomes. Standardizing and refining its assessment framework represents both a central challenge and an opportunity for the field. Current evaluation systems have achieved consensus on core physiological monitoring, establishing a foundation for basic safety and efficacy. However, substantial variability persists in operational thresholds, selection of secondary metrics and application of emerging biomarkers. This variability reflects genuine differences across centers in technical protocols (such as perfusion strategies and temperature control), donor lung quality spectra, and tolerance for acceptable risk. Such diversity is not entirely a weakness. It demonstrates the adaptability of EVLP across different clinical scenarios and exploratory phases, but it undoubtedly impedes effective comparison of multi-center data, dissemination of best practices and generation of large-scale clinical evidence.

Reconciling these different perspectives requires recognizing the hierarchical and dynamic nature of assessment metrics. Core physiological parameters, such as oxygenation, compliance and vascular resistance, form the cornerstone of evaluation. Their thresholds should be unified and validated against large-scale clinical outcome data. Biochemical markers, cellular biomarkers and imaging-pathological findings, by contrast, should serve complementary roles in mechanistic interpretation and risk stratification. Current variability in their translation from research to clinical application underscores the need to move beyond simple static cutoffs toward analyzing dynamic trends and their correlation with physiological function. Rising trends in specific inflammatory mediators, for example, combined with concurrent subtle declines in compliance, may carry greater warning value than absolute numbers alone.

Future directions should aim not to simply standardize every detail but to construct a hierarchical multi-modal integrated assessment framework. Such a framework would synergistically combine static thresholds for rapid screening and safety alerts with dynamic trend analysis for nuanced evaluation and prediction, while clearly defining the priority and decision weight of different metrics. Achieving this requires rigorously designed multi-center prospective studies that unify definitions and data collection standards for core metrics. Simultaneously, researchers should harness artificial intelligence and machine learning approaches to deeply integrate and uncover patterns within high-dimensional physiological, biochemical and imaging data. This would enable development of more generalizable, objective and highly predictive intelligent assessment models. Only through such collaboration and innovation can we overcome existing variability and advance EVLP from an individualized procedure to a standardized, generalizable clinical routine, ultimately benefiting more patients with end-stage lung disease.
