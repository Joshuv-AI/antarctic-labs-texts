Enhanced mRNA vaccine combined with immune checkpoint blockade efficiently suppresses tumor growth and metastasis
Authors: Yufeng Zhang, Xinghuan Ma, Danni Jin, Sujia Liu, Tianyu Song, Shubo Du, Zhili Liu, Jiaqi Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recently, mRNA vaccines have successfully prevented infectious diseases but remain only adjuvant therapies for cancer due to limited immune killing efficacy. The efficacy of tumor vaccines depends on both the immunogenicity of associated antigens and the delivery efficacy of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs). While next-generation sequencing has optimized neoantigen identification pipelines, most delivery systems still employ LNP formulations originally developed for infectious disease vaccines. Critically, most infectious disease vaccines primarily rely on humoral immunity for prophylaxis, whereas therapeutic tumor vaccines predominantly depend on cellular immunity for tumor eradication. Due to these mechanistic differences, developing delivery systems specifically designed for mRNA tumor vaccines is crucial. Herein, we constructed a mannose-functionalized LNP (MRLNP) that co-delivers tumor antigen-encoding mRNA and the TLR7/8 agonist resiquimod (R848). Mannose modification promotes lymph node drainage and dendritic cell (DC)-targeted uptake. R848 boosts antigen immunogenicity, driving DC maturation and antigen presentation to robustly activate cellular immunity. In the B16F10-OVA subcutaneous tumor model, MRLNP/mOVA vaccine combined with the anti-PD-1 antibody achieved a 93.5% tumor suppression rate. Furthermore, long-term immune memory generated by combination therapy effectively prevented lung metastasis formation in tumor rechallenge experiments. Collectively, this work establishes that MRLNP, as a novel enhanced delivery system, holds great potential for the development of highly effective mRNA tumor vaccines and combination therapies.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04358-6.

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of mRNA technology, mRNA vaccines have become clinically effective therapies for preventing infectious diseases [1]. Infectious disease vaccines primarily rely on B cells to produce specific antibodies to prevent pathogen infection [2]. In contrast, mRNA tumor vaccines predominantly depend on CD8⁺ T cell-mediated specific cytotoxic activity to eliminate tumors [3]. Thus, efficient priming of cellular immune responses constitutes a critical requirement for tumor vaccines. Currently marketed SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccines universally employ lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) as delivery vehicles [4]. Given distinct vaccine purposes (prophylactic versus therapeutic) and divergent immune biases (antibody-mediated immunity versus CD8⁺ T cell-mediated immunity), it is imperative to develop optimized nano-delivery systems specifically tailored for mRNA tumor vaccines.

The effective expression, processing, and presentation of mRNA-encoded antigens are absolute prerequisites for vaccine efficacy. Dendritic cells (DCs), as the most potent professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs), possess the capacity for efficient antigen processing and presentation, as well as the ability to initiate the activation and proliferation of naïve T cells [5]. Primary naïve T cells activation typically requires DCs to deliver three simultaneous signals: (1) MHC-peptide complexes, (2) costimulatory molecules (CD80/86 binding CD28; CD40 binding CD40L), and (3) cytokines (IL-12, IFN-α) [6]. Targeted delivery of antigens to these potent APCs therefore holds significant promise for enhancing antigen presentation and cellular immune activation [7].

The mannose receptor (MR), an essential pattern recognition receptor (PRR) of the innate immune system, is constitutively expressed on DCs and macrophages [8]. Through mannose receptor-mediated endocytosis, DCs efficiently internalize pathogens or nanovaccines for subsequent antigen processing and presentation [9, 10]. Recently, Ronit Satchi-Fainaro et al. developed mannosylated poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles for delivering melanoma antigen peptides in vivo [11]. Compared to non-mannosylated carriers, these mannose-modified nanoparticles demonstrated significantly enhanced uptake in DCs and upregulated surface maturation markers. Similarly, Chen’s group developed mannan-decorated polylactic acid-polyethylenimine (PLA-PEI) vaccine carriers, revealing that mannan conjugation markedly promotes nanoparticle drainage to lymph nodes [12]. Together, these studies underscore the potential of mannose and its derivatives as a surface-modifying ligand for enhancing vaccine delivery and immunogenicity.

In addition to MHC-peptide complex recognition, optimal T cell activation requires coordinated co-stimulatory signaling and cytokine secretion. To amplify these secondary signals, researchers commonly integrate immunostimulatory agonists into vaccine formulations, enabling more potent immune activation [13]. Emerging evidence indicates that Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists can enhance immune activation and promote antigen cross-presentation [14, 15]. However, the clinical translation of resiquimod (R848), a hydrophobic TLR7/8 agonist with potent immunostimulatory properties, has been hampered by its poor bioavailability and inadequate tissue accumulation following systemic administration [16, 17]. Notably, recent studies demonstrate that leveraging the inherent advantages of nanodelivery systems—including precise targeting and prolonged half-life—can effectively mitigate these limitations of R848, thereby revitalizing its therapeutic potential in cancer immunotherapy [18–20].

Building upon these findings, the ideal mRNA tumor vaccine carrier should simultaneously co-deliver mRNA-encoded antigens and immunostimulatory agonists to DCs, thereby enabling efficient antigen processing, presentation, and subsequent CD8+ T cell immune responses. To meet the aforementioned requirements, we have developed MRLNP, a novel multifunctional nano-delivery system. Building upon the classical LNP formulation (mRNA-1273), we introduce Lipid-PEG-Mannose and the TLR7/8 agonist R848 as novel components, enabling comprehensive functional upgrades. Surface mannosylation significantly improves the nanovaccine trafficking to lymph nodes and promotes DC uptake. Following internalization, the co-encapsulated R848 strongly activates endosomal TLR7/8 signaling pathways, which synergistically promotes DC maturation and antigen presentation. Through systematic optimization of key parameters including nanoparticle size, zeta potential, encapsulation efficiency, cellular uptake efficiency, and in vivo delivery performance, we established the optimal component ratios for MRLNP. Both in vitro and in vivo studies consistently demonstrated that MRLNP significantly enhanced immune activation compared to conventional LNP, thus providing a solid foundation for subsequent antitumor efficacy evaluation.

Although potent immune activation is essential for antitumor responses, tumors frequently develop multiple immunosuppressive mechanisms to inhibit cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) function [21, 22]. Among these, the PD-1/PD-L1 axis serves as a canonical immune checkpoint pathway, where ligand binding transmits potent inhibitory signals that induce profound immunosuppression [23–25]. To overcome this limitation, we developed a dual-modality therapy integrating an mRNA tumor vaccine with PD-1-blocking antibodies (Scheme 1). This combination therapy exhibited superior therapeutic efficacy in both the artificial OVA antigen-expressing tumor model and the native GP70 antigen-expressing tumor model, highlighting significant potential for clinical translation.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of MRLNP

Lipid-based nanocarriers represent one of the most advanced delivery platforms currently available. The delivery of mRNA is achieved using LNP composed of four components: ionizable cationic lipids, helper lipids, cholesterol, and PEG-lipids [26]. Critically, variations in composition and ratio directly influence the physicochemical properties of nanoparticles, thereby affecting their delivery efficiency and ultimate therapeutic efficacy [27, 28]. Therefore, it is essential to screen and optimize the incorporation ratio when introducing new components into LNP.

Owing to the high solubility of R848 in organic solvents and poor aqueous solubility, we directly dissolved it in the ethanol phase containing lipid components, enabling co-encapsulation with nucleic acids through microfluidic assembly (Fig. 1A). Figure 1B shows that the hydrodynamic diameter of RLNP (LNP/R848) slightly increased with higher R848 ratios but remained below 180 nm, which facilitates cellular uptake. The polydispersity index (PDI) values remained below 15%, demonstrating uniform particle size distribution and stable preparation process (Fig. 1C). R848 incorporation caused a slight increase in nanoparticle zeta potential, likely due to the amine groups of R848, though the overall charge remained low (Fig. 1D, S1A). Both drug loading and encapsulation efficiencies increased with higher R848 ratios, reaching a drug loading of 7.9% at a doping level equivalent to 5 times that of cholesterol (Fig. 1E, S2). Based on calculations and literature review, this doping ratio (R/C = 5) in LNP is sufficient for subsequent in vivo applications in mice [29–31]. Figure 1F shows the UV-Vis absorption spectra, where the characteristic peak of R848 at 250 nm in RLNP confirms successful loading. Furthermore, R848 incorporation shows no significant effect on mRNA encapsulation efficiency, demonstrating the feasibility of co-encapsulation (Fig. 1G).

To enhance the co-delivery efficiency of both adjuvant and antigen, we incorporated DSPE-PEG-Man into the formulation to generate DC-targeting MRLNP. Notably, the incorporation of DSPE-PEG-Man resulted in a slight increase in the hydrodynamic size of RLNP, and the final particle size of MRLNP was 182.7 ± 2.6 nm, while no significant changes were observed in zeta potential or mRNA encapsulation efficiency (Fig. 1H and I, S3). TEM imaging confirmed a morphological size of 150–200 nm for MRLNP, consistent with hydrodynamic size (Fig. 1J). The stability and release of mRNA in MRLNP were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. S4). Furthermore, stability monitoring at 4 °C demonstrated favorable storage stability of MRLNP, with its hydrodynamic size remaining unchanged for 14 days, indicating its suitability for vaccine storage and transportation (Fig. 1K).

Targeted delivery and maturation of BMDCs

Upon successful construction of MRLNP, we further evaluated its DC-targeting delivery performance using flow cytometry (FCM) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) (Fig. 2A). Figure 2B and C show flow cytometric quantification of nanoparticle uptake by bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) after 6-h incubation with different nanoformulations. Mannose-modified MLNP and MRLNP groups exhibited significantly higher Cy5 fluorescence intensity compared to the LNP group. Consistent with these findings, CLSM revealed significantly enhanced intracellular Cy5 fluorescence signal in mannose-modified MLNP and MRLNP groups (Fig. 2D). These results demonstrate that mannose-modified LNP can effectively enhance DC uptake through mannose receptor-mediated endocytosis.

Following cellular uptake of antigen and adjuvant, subsequent antigen processing and presentation critically determine immune activation efficiency. To evaluate this, we delivered ovalbumin-encoding mRNA (mOVA) using various nanocarriers and co-cultured these formulations with BMDCs for 48 h. We then evaluated DC activation and antigen peptide-MHC presentation by flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 2F (with representative flow cytometry plots in Fig. 2E) and 2G, the percentages of CD11c⁺ CD86⁺ and CD11c⁺ CD80⁺ DCs were significantly higher in the MRLNP group compared to the LNP group. Consistent with the upregulated co-stimulatory molecules, we observed markedly increased MHC-I-restricted presentation of the antigen peptide SIINFEKL, further demonstrating the efficient DC-activating capacity of MRLNP (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, the secretion levels of immune cytokines were measured by ELISA. As shown in Fig. S6, the levels of IFN-α and IL-12 were both significantly elevated in the MRLNP group compared with the LNP group, indicating that R848 in MRLNP fully exerted its adjuvant effect and promoted the secretion of immune cytokines.

Delivery efficiency of MRLNP in vivo

The in vivo delivery process of nanoformulations is significantly more complex than cellular delivery, as widely acknowledged in the field [32–34]. To address this, we systematically evaluated the in vivo delivery efficiency of mannose-modified LNP using an In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS). Fig. S7 and S8 show the luciferase-encoding mRNA (Luc-mRNA) delivery performance of LNP with varying DSPE-PEG-Mannose molar percentages. At the 0.25% molar ratio, luciferase expression at the injection site exhibited no statistically significant differences compared to conventional LNP (with 0% DSPE-PEG-Mannose) across all measured time points (6, 12, and 24 h). However, increasing the DSPE-PEG-Mannose ratio progressively reduced mRNA expression, suggesting that higher DSPE-PEG-Mannose content may negatively affect local mRNA delivery.

At 24 h post-injection, comprehensive imaging and analysis were performed on dissected major organs (including heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, muscle, and lymph nodes). Fig. S9 reveals that luciferase signals were primarily localized in the muscle tissue and ipsilateral lymph node. The 0.25% DSPE-PEG-Mannose formulation showed a 2.7-fold increase in luciferase expression in lymph nodes compared to unmodified LNP (Fig. S10). These results suggest that mannose modification at this ratio can significantly enhance the lymph node delivery efficiency.

Figure 3A shows the in vivo expression analysis of Luc-mRNA delivered by final optimized MRLNP. No statistically significant difference in intramuscular expression was found between the MRLNP and conventional LNP groups across all measured time points (Fig. 3B, S11A). However, quantitative analysis revealed significantly decreased hepatic expression in the MRLNP group at 4, 7, and 12 h (Fig. 3C, S11B). In line with this finding, q-PCR analysis demonstrated remarkably lower hepatic enrichment of the delivered Luc-mRNA in the MRLNP group (Fig. S12). Together, these results indicate that the introduction of the new component altered the systemic circulation kinetics of the formulation, thereby helping to reduce hepatotoxicity.

To further investigate the DC-targeting delivery capability of MRLNP in vivo, we evaluated the enrichment and expression of the formulation in lymph node DCs using the DiO fluorescent probe and near-infrared fluorescent protein-encoding mRNA (IRFP-mRNA) as reporters (Fig. 3D). As demonstrated in Fig. 3E and F, the MRLNP group showed significantly higher DiO signal intensity when compared with conventional LNP, confirming superior DC-targeting delivery efficiency. Moreover, enhanced IRFP expression was also observed in the MRLNP group (Fig. 3G and H). These results collectively demonstrate the critical role of mannose modification in DC-targeting delivery.

Activation of immune responses in vivo

After validating the efficient delivery capability of MRLNP, we next evaluated its immunostimulatory potential in vivo. Female C57BL/6 mice (4–6 weeks old) received two doses of MRLNP/mOVA vaccine (Fig. 4A). Single-cell suspensions from lymph nodes and spleens were analyzed by flow cytometry following antibody staining. As expected, lymph node DCs in the MRLNP group demonstrated statistically significant increases in expression of co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 compared to the conventional LNP (Fig. 4B, C and E). Furthermore, the MRLNP group showed enhanced SIINFEKL-MHC-I complex presentation, presumably due to mannose-mediated co-delivery of antigen and R848 to DCs, which potentiated DC maturation and antigen presentation (Fig. 4D). Consistently, enhanced DC maturation and antigen presentation were also observed in the spleen (Fig. 4F, S13).

Since potent DC maturation typically induces T cell activation and expansion, we analyzed T cell populations in lymph nodes. As shown in Fig. 4G and I, the proportions of both CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells showed statistically significant increases in the MRLNP group compared to the LNP group, demonstrating the superior immunostimulatory capacity of MRLNP.

In vivo anti-melanoma experiment and immune profiling of tumor microenvironment

Based on the demonstrated immunostimulatory capacity of MRLNP/mOVA, we generated a B16F10-OVA subcutaneous tumor model to evaluate its antitumor efficacy in combination with anti-PD-1 antibody. As shown in Fig. 5A, treatment was initiated on day 6 post-tumor inoculation. Tumor volume (measured by caliper) and body weight were monitored every other day until reaching the predefined endpoint (day 16), when tumors and major organs were harvested for flow cytometric analysis and histopathological examination.

Notably, all treatment groups maintained stable body weight without obvious loss, preliminarily demonstrating that the treatment regimen has no significant side effects (Fig. 5B). Compared with the PBS group, both LNP and MRLNP groups delayed tumor growth, confirming the antitumor activity of mRNA vaccines (Fig. 5C). Notably, the MRLNP group showed significantly better tumor control than the LNP group, with smaller tumor volumes (Fig. 5H), lower tumor weights (Fig. 5D), and higher inhibition rates (72.7% vs. 48.7%; Fig. 5E). The enhanced antitumor efficacy may be attributed to MRLNP’s superior induction of cellular immunity. Importantly, the combination of MRLNP/mOVA with anti-PD-1 antibody achieved a tumor inhibition rate of 93.5%, representing a 20.8% increase over MRLNP/mOVA alone. These results confirm that the MRLNP delivery system can enhance the therapeutic efficacy of mRNA tumor vaccines and achieves superior antitumor effects when combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors. This is consistent with recent advances in nanosystem-mediated tumor therapy, where such strategies achieve improved efficacy through multiple additive effects [35–38].

Tumor specimens were subjected to histomorphological evaluation using hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining for tissue structure analysis and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay for apoptosis detection. As shown in Fig. 5F and G, the combination therapy group exhibited significantly increased apoptotic cell populations and more extensive areas of necrosis compared to the PBS and LNP control groups, and these pathological features correlated directly with the observed tumor suppression.

The immune status evaluation of the tumor microenvironment (TME) provides critical guidance for therapy [39, 40]. We therefore performed flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells. Both LNP and MRLNP groups increased CD3+ CD8+ T cell infiltration compared to the PBS group (Fig. 6A and D), indicating that vaccine-activated cytotoxic T cells penetrated tumor tissues. However, judging from the results of tumor suppression experiments, their effector functions appeared to be partially restricted by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Notably, the combination group (MRLNP/mOVA + anti-PD-1) exhibited the highest CD3+ CD8+ T cell infiltration, demonstrating that immune checkpoint blockade restored T cell migration, tumor recognition, and cytotoxic function—consistent with this group’s superior tumor control (93.5% inhibition rate). Immunofluorescence imaging further confirmed widespread CD8+ T cell redistribution throughout tumor tissues of the combination group (Fig. 6E). Parallel increases in CD3+ CD4+ T cells and CD3+ NK1.1+ cells collectively indicated a systemic shift toward an immunologically active TME following combination therapy (Fig. 6B and C).

Long-lasting immune memory and suppression of lung metastasis

In tumor immunotherapy, durable immune memory is critical for preventing recurrence and metastasis [41, 42]. To evaluate treatment-induced memory responses, we quantified memory T cell subsets in the spleen across experimental groups. The MRLNP group demonstrated significantly elevated proportions of both CD62L- CD44+ (effector memory) and CD62L+ CD44+ (central memory) T cells compared to conventional LNP (Fig. 7A and B, S15), indicating that MRLNP more effectively induced functional immune memory, which may contribute to long-term antitumor protection. Furthermore, we verified the recall response function of memory T cells. After restimulation with OVA antigen peptides in vitro, the percentage of IFN-γ-producing effector memory T cells was significantly higher in the combination therapy group than in the blank control group (Fig. S16). These results further confirm that the memory T cells induced by combination therapy possess long-term functional activity and can be rapidly reactivated upon re-exposure to tumor antigens.

To validate the anti-metastatic function of vaccine-induced immunological memory, surviving mice from combination therapy cohorts were rechallenged via tail vein injection with tumor cells to establish pulmonary metastasis models (Fig. 7C). The PBS control group consisted of age-matched naive mice without any prior treatment. After 14 days, lungs were harvested for macroscopic examination, photographic documentation, gravimetric analysis, and histopathological evaluation.

As shown in Fig. 7D, the lungs of the PBS group displayed extensive surface nodules, whereas lungs from the combination-treated group retained normal architecture indistinguishable from healthy lungs. Consistently, the PBS group also exhibited significantly increased lung weights due to tumor burden (Fig. 7E). Histological examination revealed widespread intrapulmonary tumor foci in the PBS group, in sharp contrast to only sporadic lesions in the treated group (Fig. 7F and G). These findings collectively demonstrate the critical role of MRLNP/mOVA nanovaccine-primed immunological memory in preventing tumor recurrence and metastatic dissemination.

Therapeutic efficacy in a native tumor antigen model

To evaluate the efficacy of MRLNP in delivering native tumor antigens instead of OVA, we formulated a vaccine encapsulating mRNA encoding the envelope glycoprotein 70 (mGP70). GP70 is widely expressed in various murine tumor cell lines including B16F10, 4T1 and CT26, and has been validated as a native antigen that elicits effective anti-tumor immune killing [43–45]. The therapeutic regimen used in this validation was largely consistent with that employed in the B16F10-OVA tumor model (Fig. S18A). As shown in Fig. S18B, F, and G, MRLNP/mGP70 alone significantly retarded B16F10 tumor growth, while anti-PD-1 monotherapy exhibited only weak tumor suppression. Notably, the combination therapy achieved the strongest anti-tumor effect, which was highly consistent with the results obtained in the B16F10-OVA model. Ex vivo tumor weight analysis and quantification of the tumor inhibition rate further confirmed the superiority of the combination regimen, with a tumor inhibition rate of 68.5% (Fig. S18C-E). In contrast, the inhibition rates were only 13.0% for anti-PD-1 monotherapy and 54.9% for MRLNP/mGP70 monotherapy. Collectively, these results validate the effectiveness of our therapeutic strategy in a native tumor antigen model and demonstrate its promising potential for clinical application.

Biosafety analysis

In addition to continuous monitoring of body weight and health status during treatment, we performed comprehensive H&E staining analysis on major organs from all experimental groups. As shown in Fig. S21, no obvious organ toxicity or pathological necrosis was observed in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys in any group. To further validate the biosafety of the formulation, serum levels of the liver function markers AST and ALT were also measured. The results demonstrated that these markers remained within normal physiological ranges across all treatment groups, with no significant differences compared with the PBS control group (Fig. S22). Collectively, body weight monitoring, histopathological analysis, and liver function assays consistently demonstrate that the combination treatment exhibits favorable biosafety in vivo.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed MRLNP, a novel multifunctional mRNA tumor vaccine delivery system based on conventional LNP formulations, by integrating mannose surface modification and co-delivery of the R848 adjuvant. In vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that mannose modification significantly enhanced lymph node accumulation and dendritic cell (DC) uptake of the vaccine. Meanwhile, co-delivered R848 activated DCs through the TLR7/8 pathway, boosted antigen immunogenicity, and further induced robust T-cell immune responses and durable immune memory. In the B16F10-OVA subcutaneous tumor model, MRLNP/mOVA increased the tumor inhibition rate from 48.7% for LNP/mOVA to 72.7%. In combination with anti-PD-1 antibody, the tumor inhibition rate was further elevated to 93.5%. In the pulmonary metastasis rechallenge model, the combination therapy group showed only sporadic micrometastases, in sharp contrast to the PBS control group, in which lungs were densely filled with metastatic nodules. Notably, in the B16F10 model using the endogenous tumor antigen GP70, the combination therapy still achieved a potent tumor inhibition rate of 68.5%, further validating the efficacy and reliability of this strategy in a genuine tumor antigen setting. Collectively, MRLNP is a high-performance mRNA tumor vaccine delivery system. The enhanced mRNA vaccine constructed based on this system, when combined with immune checkpoint blockade therapy, can not only rapidly mediate immune elimination of tumors but also establish long-term immune protection against tumor recurrence and metastasis, demonstrating promising clinical application potential.
