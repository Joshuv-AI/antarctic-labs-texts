Human papillomavirus genotypes in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia: prevalence and public health implications
Authors: Mai M El-Daly, Arwa A Faizo, Shahad A Madkhali, Nassrin A Badroon, Norah A Othman, Thamir A Alandijany, Anwar Moria, Safaa A Turkistani, Sherif A El-Kafrawy, Esam I Azhar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman papillomaviruses (HPVs) are prevalent sexually transmitted infections and a leading cause of cervical and other anogenital cancers worldwide. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and genotype distribution of HPV in Pap smear samples from women attending gynecology clinics at Dr. Soliman Fakeeh Hospital in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, between September 2022 and July 2023.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed 260 samples using nested PCR and Sanger sequencing to identify HPV genotypes.ResultsThe overall HPV prevalence rate was 6.9%. (18/260, 95% CI: 3.84–10.01%). High-risk HPV (HR-HPV) genotypes 16 (33.3%), 18 (16.7%), and 58 (5.6%) were identified, along with low-risk HPV (LR-HPV) genotype 6 (33.3%). Genotypes 11 and 67 were also detected in 5.6% of cases each. Age-specific analysis revealed the highest prevalence of HPV in the 31–40 age group, at 3.5% (p = 0.04), with HPV16 being significantly more prevalent in this age group. Saudi participants exhibited a higher prevalence rate of 61.1% (11/18) than non-Saudi participants at 38.9% (7/18). Although HPV was more common in non-Saudis, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.39). Cytological findings showed that 88.9% of HPV-positive cases had negative cytology, underscoring the need for HPV screening beyond cytological abnormalities.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the importance of continued surveillance, targeted vaccination efforts, and improved screening programs in Saudi Arabia. Increasing awareness of HPV and enhancing vaccination rates, especially among younger age groups, could significantly reduce the burden of HPV-related diseases. Future research should focus on broader epidemiological factors and evaluate the impact of HPV vaccination programs on genotype distribution and prevalence trends.Keywords: HPV, Prevalence, Genotypes, Cervical cancer, Saudi Arabia

Background

Human papillomaviruses (HPVs) are a group of small, non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses that belong to the Papillomaviridae family. They primarily infect the squamous epithelium of the reproductive system and the anal transformation zone [1]. The HPV genome is approximately 8 kb and consists of circular double-stranded DNA, encoding eight protein-coding genes and a long control region (LCR), which regulates viral replication and transcription [2, 3]. To date, around 200 HPV types have been identified, classified into five genera: α, β, γ, µ, and ν [4]. Among these, the α genus contains high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) types associated with cervical carcinoma, anogenital, and oral cancers [5].

HPVs are among the most common sexually transmitted infections worldwide and are a major cause of cervical cancer. HPVs are linked to nearly 99% of cervical cancers, 90% of anal cancers, 65% of vaginal cancers, 50% of vulvar cancers, and 45–90% of oropharyngeal cancers [6]. Notably, HPV16 alone accounts for about 50% of cervical cancer cases, while HPV16 and 18 combined are responsible for 70% of cases [7–9]. HPV types are categorized as mucosal or cutaneous, with mucosal types infecting mucous membranes and being implicated in cervical neoplasia, anogenital warts, and cancer. HR-HPV types such as HPV16, 18, 31, 33, 52, and 58 are closely linked to various malignancies, including cervical, penile, anal, vaginal, vulvar, and oropharyngeal cancers [10]. In contrast, LR-HPV types like HPV6, 11, and 53 are primarily associated with external anogenital warts and low-grade cervical abnormalities.

The integration of HPV DNA into the host genome is a critical step in malignant transformation and cancer development. This process is driven by the viral oncoproteins E6 and E7, which disrupt cell cycle regulation, promote genomic instability, and suppress tumor suppressor pathways. Additionally, these oncoproteins facilitate immune evasion by modifying cytokine production and interferon responses [11].

Cervical cancers are mainly classified into squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma. SCCs generally arise in the ectocervix, while adenocarcinomas originate in the endocervix [12]. HPV16 is predominantly associated with SCC, accounting for 46–63% of cases, followed by HPV18, contributing 10–14%. Conversely, HPV18 is the primary type linked to cervical adenocarcinomas, responsible for 37–41% of cases [13].

HPV infections affect a substantial proportion of the global population. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), up to 80% of women and 90% of sexually active men are expected to contract at least one type of HPV during their lifetime [14, 15]. While most HPV infections are asymptomatic and resolve without intervention, persistent infection can lead to conditions such as cervical cancer and genital warts. A meta-analysis of 194 studies reported that 11.7% of women with normal cervical cytology were infected with HPV, HPV16 (3.2%), 18 (1.4%), 31 (0.8%), and 58 (0.7%) being the most prevalent oncogenic types. The prevalence of HPV often exhibits a bimodal distribution, with a peak among younger individuals, a plateau in middle-aged groups, and a second peak in older women. This pattern highlights the critical importance of ongoing screening and vaccination programs to reduce the burden of HPV-related diseases [6, 13].

In Saudi Arabia, research on HPV prevalence remains limited, with most studies relying on hospital records of symptomatic patients. To address this gap, the Saudi Public Health Authority introduced the Saudi Clinical Preventive Guidelines 2023 for HPV screening and vaccination. These guidelines recommend two doses of the HPV vaccine for girls aged 11–12 years and cervical cancer screening using Pap smears every three years for sexually active women over the age of 30. However, in the absence of a national HPV screening program, most cervical cancer cases are identified at advanced stages, requiring intensive treatment. Early HPV screening could play a pivotal role in preventing the progression of cervical cancers [16].

Despite the global recognition of HPV as a major public health concern, data on HPV prevalence, genotype distribution, and associated risk factors remain limited in Saudi Arabia, particularly in the Jeddah region. Most previous studies have focused on symptomatic individuals or utilized small sample sizes without comprehensive genotyping approaches. Therefore, this study specifically addresses these gaps by providing updated data on the prevalence and genotype distribution of HPV among women in Jeddah, using robust molecular diagnostic methods. The findings aim to support early detection strategies and inform targeted public health interventions to reduce the burden of HPV-related diseases in this region.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted between September 2022 and July 2023 at Dr. Soliman Fakeeh Hospital (DSFH). Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at DSFH (Approval No. 76/IRB/2020). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Laboratory testing for the study was performed at the Special Infectious Agents Unit-BSL3 (SIAU-BSL3), King Fahd Medical Research Center (KFMRC), King Abdulaziz University (KAU).

Sample collection

260 Pap smear samples were collected in collection vials (BD SurePath, USA) from women attending gynecology clinics at DSFH. Participants were eligible if they were over 18 years old and provided signed informed consent. After giving consent, the participants provided a Pap smear sample and completed a questionnaire that included demographic information, medical history, and risk factor information. All data were reviewed, verified, and securely stored. Pap smear samples were stored at 4 °C and transported to SIAU-BSL3 for laboratory analysis.

HPV detection

DNA was extracted from the Pap smear samples using the ExiPrep™ 96 Viral DNA/RNA Kit (Catalog No. A-5250) on an ExiPrep™ 96 Lite instrument (Bioneer Corp., Daejeon, Korea) following the manufacturer’s instructions. To detect the presence of HPV, nested PCR was performed using the primers MY09/11 and GP5+/6 + in the L1 region and GoTaq Green Master Mix (Promega, Mannheim, Germany) on a 96-well thermal cycler (Eppendorf) [17, 18]. The PCR amplicons were electrophoresed on a 2% agarose gel with ethidium bromide staining and visualized under UV; the PCR band size was 150 bp.

HPV genotyping

Positive PCR products were purified using a DNA Gel Extraction Kit (NORGEN BIOTEK CORP, Catalog No. 13100) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The purified PCR products were then subjected to cycle sequencing, performed on an ABI 3500 Automatic Sequencer with Big Dye Terminator V3.1 (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and internal primers, according to the manufacturer’s protocol, under these conditions: 96 °C for 1 min, followed by 25 cycles of 96 °C for 10 s, 50 °C for 5 s, and 60 °C for 4 min, then cooling at 4 °C. The sequences obtained were aligned with HPV reference sequences from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI).

HPV genotypes were analyzed through phylogenetic analysis using MEGA11. Sequence alignments were performed with ClustalW, applying the Neighbor-Joining method based on the Maximum Composite Likelihood model with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The resulting phylogenetic tree illustrates the evolutionary relationships among the examined taxa, with branches supported by less than 50% of bootstrap replicates collapsed. Evolutionary distances were calculated using the Maximum Composite Likelihood method and expressed as the number of base substitutions per site. Ambiguous positions were excluded for each sequence pair using the pairwise deletion option [19].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed via the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), version 23. All the continuous variables were categorized. Descriptive statistics were conducted to determine the prevalence of HPV. A chi-square test explored the relationships between age, nationality, and marital status with the prevalence of HPV genotypes. Statistical significance was determined using a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

Study population

This study included 260 participants from DSFH who provided Pap smear samples for HPV screening. The distribution of HPV positivity was analysed based on age group, nationality, and marital status. The cohort had a mean age of 42 years (SD = 10.8), ranging from 20 to 71 years. In the 20–30 age group (n = 44, 16.9%), 4 participants tested positive for HPV, yielding a positivity rate of 9.1% within this group (p = 0.53). Among participants aged 31–40 (n = 84, 32.3%), 9 were HPV-positive, resulting in a positivity rate of 10.7% (p = 0.09). In the 41–50 age group (n = 71, 27.3%), three individuals tested positive, corresponding to a positivity rate of 4.2% (p = 0.29). In participants over 50 years (n = 61, 23.5%), two tested positive, with a positivity rate of 3.3% (p = 0.19), as shown in Table 1.

Phylogenetic analysis of HPV genotypes

In this study, the overall prevalence of HPV among 260 participants was found in 18 individuals (6.9%) who tested positive (95% CI: 3.84%–10.01%). The remaining 242 participants, 93.1% (95% CI: 89.99%–96.16%), tested negative for HPV. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that 10 individuals (55.6%) were classified as HR-HPV genotypes, which included HPV16 (n = 6), HPV18 (n = 3), and HPV58 (n = 1). In contrast, 8 individuals (44.4%) were identified as LR-HPV genotypes, comprising HPV6 (n = 6), HPV11 (n = 1), and HPV67 (n = 1), as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Prevalence and distribution of HPV genotypes by age group

It is important to note that the number of participants in the 31–40 age group (9/18, 50%) was higher compared to the > 50 age group (2/18, 11.1%), which may have contributed to the observed differences in the number of HPV-positive cases. Notably, the prevalence of HPV16 was significantly elevated in the 31–40 age group, with a statistically significant p-value of 0.04. In the 41–50 age group, HPV18 showed a marked increase, supported by a statistically significant p-value (p = 0.01). Additionally, a significant association was observed for HPV58 in individuals over 50, with a p-value of 0.003, as shown in Fig. 2. Overall, age appears to be a key factor influencing HPV prevalence, with younger age groups demonstrating a higher likelihood of testing positive for HPV compared to older individuals. This highlights the critical role of age in the distribution of specific HPV genotypes.

Prevalence of HPV and its distribution by nationality

HPV prevalence was higher among Saudi females (11/18, 61.1%) compared to non-Saudi females (7/18, 38.9%), though this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.39). Nonetheless, the observed variation suggests a potential association worth further exploration. Among non-Saudi females, HPV genotype 16 was the most prevalent, accounting for 22.2% of cases, followed by genotypes 18, 58, and 6, each representing 5.6%. Notably, no cases of genotypes 11 or 67 were detected in this group. In contrast, Saudi females displayed a different genotype distribution pattern, with genotype 6 being the most prevalent, 27.8%, followed by genotypes 16 and 18 (11.1% each), and genotypes 11 and 67 (5.6% each). Interestingly, genotype 58 was not identified among Saudi females, as shown in Fig. 3. These differences in genotype distribution between Saudi and non-Saudi females may reflect underlying epidemiological or biological factors influencing HPV prevalence. Although the findings were not statistically significant, the consistent trends across genotypes highlight a meaningful association that merits further investigation using larger sample sizes or more robust statistical methods.

Discussion

In Saudi Arabia, addressing the burden of cervical cancer requires a comprehensive evaluation of several key factors. These include assessing the prevalence of HPV and identifying the primary drivers of genotype transmission, which are crucial for understanding the regional epidemiology of the virus. Insights gained from this analysis will enable the development of targeted interventions to enhance prevention, early detection, and treatment strategies. These efforts aim to substantially reduce cervical cancer morbidity in Saudi Arabia, aligning with the core objectives of this study.

This study investigated preliminary data on the prevalence and genotype distribution of HPV in a limited sample set from Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. Building on that research, this study investigated the prevalence and genotype distribution of HPV among 260 females in Jeddah. The findings revealed an overall HPV prevalence of 6.9% in the study population, emphasizing the notable presence of the virus and the urgent need for ongoing monitoring and preventive measures. HPV16 and HPV6 emerged as the most prevalent genotypes, each accounting for 33.3% of the cases, followed by HPV18 (16.7%) and HPV11, 58, and 67 (5.6% each). This diverse genotype distribution highlights the regional variation in HPV prevalence.

Importantly, HPV16 was identified as the most common high-risk genotype, followed by HPV18, both of which are strongly associated with cervical cancer and carry significant public health implications. Among the low-risk genotypes, HPV6 was the most prevalent and primarily associated with benign lesions. These findings underscore the critical importance of implementing targeted prevention and management strategies to mitigate HPV-related health risks in the region.

The HPV prevalence rate of 6.9% found in our study was lower than the previously reported rate in a study conducted at three hospitals in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, with an HPV rate of 9.8% [20]. Interestingly, a study conducted at a tertiary care Center in Jeddah between October 2017 and April 2018 reported a slightly lower HPV prevalence of 5.9% [21]. Similarly, a longitudinal study conducted in Jeddah from 2013 to 2018 reported an initial HPV prevalence of 4.7%, which slightly increased to 5.0% after a five-year follow-up [22]. Another Study conducted in the western region of Saudi Arabia reported that 5.6% of participants tested positive for HR-HPV [23]. These findings are consistent with the prevalence rates identified in our study and highlight the importance of ongoing surveillance to monitor trends in HPV prevalence and its associated risk factors over time. The observed variation in HPV prevalence between our study and previous reports from different regions of Saudi Arabia may be attributed to several factors. One key reason is the variation in detection methodologies employed across studies, including differences in sample collection techniques, PCR primer sets, and genotyping assays, all of which can influence the sensitivity and specificity of HPV detection. Additionally, the study populations sampled may differ significantly in terms of age distribution, marital status, sexual behaviour, and healthcare-seeking practices. Behavioural and cultural factors, including variations in sexual health education, religious practices, and social norms regarding premarital sexual activity, may also play a crucial role in influencing HPV transmission dynamics. Furthermore, geographic differences, such as urban versus rural settings, could impact both exposure risk and access to preventive healthcare services, including HPV vaccination and routine cervical screening. These factors collectively underscore the importance of standardized methodologies and representative sampling in future HPV prevalence studies to allow for meaningful comparisons across regions.

The overall HPV prevalence in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, aligns with previous reports from the Middle East, which generally show lower rates than Western regions [13, 24]. The rate found in our study is significantly lower than recent findings from the United States (38.4%), Brazil (25.41%), China (21.0%), and Malaysia (14.0%) [25–28]. The lower prevalence of HPV in Saudi Arabia compared to other countries may result from differences in study methods and population demographics. Furthermore, cultural and behavioral factors unique to Saudi Arabia may affect genotype distribution and contribute to the relatively low HPV prevalence [29]. These findings highlight the importance of considering regional and cultural contexts when interpreting HPV prevalence data.

Our study highlights the relationship between age and HPV prevalence, shedding light on the distribution of specific genotypes across different age groups. The highest prevalence of HPV cases was observed in individuals aged 31–40 years, with a notable decline in infection rates among those over 50, however, this pattern should be interpreted with caution, as the number of participants in the 31–40 age group (50%) was greater than that in the >50 age group (11.1%), which may have influenced the observed differences in HPV positivity. Nevertheless, these findings are consistent with a previous study conducted in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, which revealed a similar trend, with most HPV-positive cases occurring in individuals aged 30–39 [30]. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that HPV infection rates are generally higher in younger populations, likely reflecting increased exposure during peak reproductive years and a natural decline in sexual activity with advancing age [13].

In addition, HPV16, the most prevalent high-risk genotype associated with cervical cancer, was notably more common in the 31–40 age group (p = 0.04), which underscores the importance of targeted screening and vaccination strategies in this demographic to prevent the progression of HPV-related lesions. In the 41–50 age group, HPV18 had a significantly higher prevalence (p = 0.01), confirming its status as the second most common high-risk genotype. This finding aligns with a prior study conducted in 2011, which identified HPV16 (30%) and HPV18 (8%) as the most prevalent genotypes [31], potentially reflecting cumulative lifetime exposure and persistent infections in this group. Interestingly, HPV58 was significantly associated with individuals >50 years old (p = 0.003), a less frequently reported observation in global studies. This could point to unique regional or population-specific patterns in genotype distribution, as previous studies have identified geographic variations in HPV58 prevalence, particularly in East Asia and parts of the Middle East [15, 32]. The presence of HPV58 in the older population highlights the potential for persistent infections that may have remained undetected earlier in life.

HPV prevalence was higher among non-Saudi females (9.0%) compared to Saudi females (6.0%), although this difference was not statistically significant. Among non-Saudis, HPV16 was the most prevalent genotype (22.2%), followed by HPV18, 58, and 6. In contrast, among Saudis, HPV6 was the most common (27.8%), followed by HPV16 and 18. The prevalence of HR-HPV genotypes varies among different populations [33], potentially influenced by factors such as logistical barriers and sociocultural practices that affect the distribution of HPV types within ethnic groups [34]. Although the differences in prevalence and genotype distribution were not statistically significant, these trends suggest potential epidemiological patterns that merit further investigation.

The findings of the current study can be effectively contextualized by comparing them with our previous publication [35]. In an earlier study, we reported a higher HPV prevalence of 15.1% among 106 women attending gynecology clinics at King Abdulaziz University Hospital, whereas the current study, with a larger cohort of 260 women, demonstrated a lower prevalence rate of 6.9%. This variation in prevalence may be attributed to differences in sample size and/or population demographics. Both studies consistently identified HPV16 as the most prevalent high-risk genotype, reinforcing its potential dominant role in cervical carcinogenesis within the region. However, the current study revealed a slightly broader genotype distribution, including HPV18, HPV31, and HPV45, possibly reflecting a more diverse participant population. Age-specific prevalence patterns were similar in both studies, with the highest HPV positivity observed among women in their thirties, 35–44 years in the previous study and 30–39 years in the current study, highlighting this demographic as a critical target for preventive interventions. Additionally, the previous study emphasized significant gaps in HPV awareness, with a substantial proportion of participants unaware of the link between HPV infection and cervical cancer or the availability of vaccines. The current study, while primarily focused on molecular findings, further supports the need for integrated public health strategies, including vaccination, routine HPV screening, and educational campaigns. Another potential contributing factor to the differences in the two studies is the nationality of the recruited subjects, as the previous study included mainly Saudi women, and this study included a more diverse population from different nationalities. Together, these studies contribute complementary insights, underscoring the importance of continuous surveillance, standardized methodologies, and culturally tailored public health initiatives to reduce the burden of HPV-related diseases in Saudi Arabia.

The cytology results in our study shed light on the relationship between HPV infection and cytological abnormalities. Among the 18 HPV-positive cases, the majority (88.9%) exhibited negative cytology results, while only 11.1% presented non-malignant findings, and no cases demonstrated squamous lesions. These findings suggest no significant association between HPV infection and abnormal cytological findings in this cohort. The high proportion of HPV-positive individuals with negative cytology aligns with evidence indicating that HPV infections often occur without causing immediate cytological abnormalities. This highlights the essential role of molecular HPV testing as a complementary diagnostic tool for cervical cancer prevention [36, 37]. The finding that most HPV-positive cases in this study exhibited negative cytological results has important clinical implications. It reinforces the well-documented limitation of cytology alone in detecting early HPV infections, particularly transient infections, or those in early stages of viral persistence that have not yet led to detectable cellular abnormalities [37]. This underscores the necessity of integrating molecular HPV DNA testing as a complementary tool alongside traditional Pap smear cytology in cervical cancer screening programs. Several international guidelines, including those from the World Health Organization (WHO) and American Cancer Society, now recommend primary HPV testing or co-testing (HPV DNA testing with cytology) due to its higher sensitivity for the detection of high-risk HPV types and precancerous lesions [38]. Implementing such combined screening strategies in Saudi Arabia could improve early identification of women at risk for cervical neoplasia, even in the absence of cytological abnormalities, and facilitate timely preventive interventions. Additionally, establishing national guidelines that incorporate molecular testing may significantly enhance the effectiveness of cervical cancer prevention efforts and reduce disease burden [37, 38].

Our study identified HPV16 as the most prevalent high-risk genotype, with a prevalence of 33.3%, consistent with findings from other regions of Saudi Arabia, where HPV16 has similarly been reported as the most dominant high-risk genotype, with rates ranging from 30% to 42.1% [31, 39]. This observation aligns with global data, where HPV16 accounts for most cervical cancer cases, emphasizing its critical role in cervical carcinogenesis [32]. Additionally, HPV18 emerged as the second most prevalent high-risk genotype in our study, a finding consistent with other studies conducted in Saudi Arabia and internationally [13, 31, 40]. Together, HPV16 and 18 are responsible for approximately 70% of cervical cancer cases worldwide, underscoring their significant contribution to the burden of HR-HPV genotypes [41, 42]. In addition to the high-risk genotypes, the presence of low-risk types, such as HPV6, which was the most common low-risk genotype identified, also warrants attention. Although low-risk genotypes are typically associated with non-cancerous lesions, their prevalence contributes to the overall burden of HPV infections and highlights the importance of comprehensive preventive measures.

The dominance of HPV16 and 18 in our study highlights the importance of vaccination programs in targeting these high-risk genotypes. Current vaccines, such as Gardasil and Cervarix, provide robust protection against these types and have demonstrated significant success in reducing related cancer incidences in vaccinated populations [43]. Studies indicated a low acceptance rate of HPV vaccines among Saudis [44, 45]. In a previous study, 23.4% of participants declined HPV vaccination for their daughters, believing they were too young or unmarried and therefore did not need it, while 18.8% cited a lack of trust in the vaccine [46]. Despite the inclusion of HPV vaccination in the national guidelines, several studies have reported low vaccine uptake and limited public awareness across Saudi Arabia. Recent investigations have identified key barriers, including misconceptions about HPV transmission, cultural sensitivities surrounding discussions of sexually transmitted infections, and parental concerns regarding vaccine safety and appropriateness for unmarried girls [44, 45, 47].

The awareness of HPV and its link to cervical cancer remains low, as reported by previous studies in Saudi Arabia [47, 48]. This lack of awareness emphasizes the urgent public health initiatives to educate communities about HPV infection, its associated risks, and the benefits of vaccination. Such efforts should prioritize reaching diverse populations across Saudi Arabia to ensure widespread understanding and accessibility. Additionally, targeted campaigns encouraging vaccination among young females are essential to curb the spread of these oncogenic genotypes. Increasing vaccine uptake remains a critical step in reducing the burden of HPV-related diseases and achieving long-term control of cervical cancer in the region.

Conclusions

This study provides valuable insights into the prevalence and genotype distribution of HPV among women in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. The overall prevalence of HPV was 6.9%, with high-risk genotypes 16 and 18 being the most detected, highlighting their significant role in HPV-associated malignancies. Although no statistically significant associations were observed between HPV positivity and sociodemographic factors such as age, nationality, and marital status, the trends identified warrant further investigation in larger and more diverse populations.

The age-specific prevalence and genotype distribution underscore the importance of targeted screening and vaccination strategies, particularly among younger age groups. The findings also highlight the need to enhance awareness about HPV and its potential health consequences. While most HPV-positive cases were associated with negative cytological findings, the study emphasizes the necessity of regular cervical screening to detect subclinical HPV infections and prevent progression to malignancies.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, its small sample size and cross-sectional design prevent the establishment of causal relationships. Additionally, the inclusion of a diverse population may introduce variability that does not accurately reflect the broader Saudi population. There is also a potential selection bias due to the study being conducted in a single hospital. Furthermore, the absence of participants’ HPV vaccination status could influence the distribution of genotypes.

Future research should prioritize several specific areas to address remaining knowledge gaps in HPV epidemiology within Saudi Arabia. First, longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the long-term impact of HPV vaccination programs on genotype prevalence and the potential for genotype replacement. Monitoring these trends will help assess vaccine effectiveness in the local population and inform potential updates to vaccination strategies. Second, studies investigating the persistence and clearance rates of different HPV genotypes across various age groups and risk categories would provide critical insights into the natural history of HPV infections in this region. Third, exploring the role of co-infections with other sexually transmitted infections (STIs), such as Chlamydia trachomatis or herpes simplex virus, may help clarify factors that influence HPV persistence, progression to precancerous lesions, and carcinogenesis. Finally, qualitative research assessing community knowledge, attitudes, and acceptance of HPV vaccination and screening could support the design of more effective public health education campaigns tailored to cultural sensitivities in Saudi Arabia.
