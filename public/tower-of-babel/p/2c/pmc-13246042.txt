Postherpetic neuralgia risk prediction in hospitalised patients with herpes zoster based on MIMIC-IV
Authors: Fei Tang, Fukun Zhao, Yong Jiang, Bangfeng Wang, Yimei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) is a disabling complication of herpes zoster (HZ). Early identification of high-risk patients may enable targeted prevention and follow-up. We developed and compared machine-learning models for PHN prediction using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV database and identified key predictors. This retrospective cohort study used Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV v2.2 (2008–2019). Adult inpatients with HZ were identified via International Classification of Diseases-9/10 codes (053.x or B02.x). The outcome was PHN (binary). Candidate predictors included demographics, pain phenotypes, comorbidities, laboratory markers, and inpatient medications; an antiviral initiation delay ≥ 3 days (AVdelay3d) was derived. Data were split into stratified training/validation sets (70/30). Five-fold cross-validation with Bayesian optimization was applied for categorical boosting, light gradient-boosting machine, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and adaptive boosting; least absolute shrinkage and selection operator logistic regression was tuned via cross-validation. Discrimination was assessed by area under the receiver operating characteristic curve; the optimal probability threshold was determined by maximizing the Youden index. Feature selection used Boruta and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, supplemented by feature importance from the best-performing model. All models were validated internally using the same dataset, and no external validation was performed. Of 383 inpatients with HZ, 81 (21.1%) developed PHN. XGBoost achieved the best discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve = 0.737; 95% confidence interval: 0.652–0.822). Baseline comparisons showed that PHN risk was associated with a higher maximum pain score, AVdelay3d, diabetes, amitriptyline use, a lower hematocrit level, and severe immunosuppression (all P < .05). In the Boruta analysis, the confirmed important features (in descending order) were D-dimer, hematocrit, creatinine, AVdelay3d, age, and platelet count. In the PHN prediction model, tree-based methods (e.g., XGBoost) can effectively estimate PHN risk and outperform linear models. These findings suggest that the pathogenesis of PHN may involve complex, nonlinear interactions. Baseline comparisons and feature-importance analyses further identified a set of core predictors for PHN, providing a reference for early identification of high-risk patients and for implementing risk-stratified management. However, the results should be interpreted cautiously because only internal validation was performed, and external validation was lacking.

1. Introduction

Herpes zoster (HZ) is caused by the reactivation of the varicella-zoster virus, and as populations age and the number of immunosuppressed individuals increases, the burden of the disease and its complications continues to rise.[1] Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) is a common and difficult-to-manage complication of HZ, typically defined as persistent or newly occurring pain lasting for ≥ 3 months after rash onset.[2] Epidemiological studies have shown that approximately 5 to 30% of patients with HZ develop PHN, with incidence varying according to age, immune status, and study setting.[3] PHN can markedly impair quality of life and increase healthcare resource utilization.[4] Standardised antiviral therapy and pain management are considered to reduce acute symptoms and the risk of subsequent complications, and real-world studies have also demonstrated clear preventive effectiveness of the recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) against both HZ and PHN.[5,6] However, owing to insufficient vaccine coverage, variation in treatment pathways across healthcare institutions, and the complexity of patient comorbidities, high-risk individuals remain difficult to accurately identify early and to receive targeted interventions and appropriate resource allocation.[7–9] A recent systematic review suggested that older age, greater acute pain severity, immunosuppression, suboptimal treatment, and multiple chronic conditions are associated with an increased risk of PHN.[10] However, the current evidence is derived mainly from outpatient populations or mixed general inpatient cohorts, whereas evidence specifically concerning hospitalized patients with HZ in critical care settings remains relatively limited. This gap is clinically important because hospitalized patients, particularly those requiring intensive care, often present with impaired immune function, greater systemic stress and inflammatory burden, multiple comorbidities, and more complex treatment exposures, all of which may be associated with an increased risk of PHN.[11] These characteristics also suggest that PHN risk in this setting is unlikely to be explained fully by simple linear relationships between isolated predictors and outcomes. Traditional statistical models are useful for estimating associations, whereas machine-learning (ML) approaches may be better suited for prediction in this context because they can capture complex nonlinear effects and interactions among diverse clinical variables, including demographics, acute pain severity, laboratory markers, comorbidities, and treatment-related factors. Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) is a de-identified electronic health record database centered on intensive care unit (ICU) patients, while also integrating emergency department and general inpatient hospitalization information.[12] This database allows a comprehensive assessment of disease severity, comorbidity burden, laboratory abnormalities, medication exposure, and treatment timing. Using the MIMIC-IV database, this study identified hospitalized patients with HZ, collected their clinical data, compared baseline characteristics between the PHN and non-PHN groups, and developed a PHN risk prediction model to identify key predictors of PHN. These findings may facilitate early risk stratification for PHN in patients with HZ and inform the clinical management of high-risk patients.

2. Methods

2.1. Data source and study population

This study used the MIMIC-IV database (version 2.2, 2008–2019), jointly developed by Massachusetts General Hospital and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in the United States. The database contains detailed inpatient clinical information, including admission data, laboratory results, medications, diagnoses, and clinical course records. Although MIMIC-IV is ICU-centered, it also includes emergency department and general inpatient data. Therefore, this study was not limited to ICU patients but included hospitalized adults with HZ identified from inpatient records, according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. All data were fully de-identified and approved for use under official ethical oversight (Beth Israel Deaconess Institutional Review Board Protocol #2001P001699). Patients with HZ were identified from the diagnostic table (diagnoses_icd) using relevant International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9/10 codes (053.x or B02.x). The inclusion criteria were: first-time diagnosis of HZ with complete inpatient records; documented timing of antiviral treatment initiation; available pain scores as well as inflammatory and metabolic indicators; and age ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria were: presence of severe neurological disorders (e.g., stroke, Parkinson disease, dementia); hospital length of stay < 24 hours or death within 3 days of admission; and missing rates > 40% for key clinical variables. A total of 383 hospitalized patients with HZ were included in the final analysis, of whom 81 (21.1%) developed PHN and 302 (78.9%) did not.

2.2. Variable extraction and preprocessing

2.2.1. Outcome variable

The primary outcome was PHN, modeled as a binary variable (PHN = 1, non-PHN = 0). PHN was operationally defined as HZ–related pain that persisted for ≥ 90 days after rash onset, or new-onset pain occurring ≥ 90 days after rash onset. Outcome labels were derived from a curated analysis dataset constructed from MIMIC-IV. PHN status was ascertained using ICD diagnosis codes: patients were classified as having PHN if a PHN-specific ICD code was recorded ≥ 90 days after the index HZ hospitalization (or index diagnosis date).

2.2.2. Candidate predictors

Candidate predictors covered multiple clinically accessible domains, including: demographics: age, sex, and race/ethnicity; rash- and pain-related phenotypes: skin lesion area > 5%, severe skin lesions, maximum pain score during hospitalization, and prodromal pain; comorbidities/underlying conditions: diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, chronic kidney disease, liver disease, coronary artery disease, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, dyslipidemia, obesity, severe immunosuppression, and autoimmune disease; treatments/medications: acyclovir, valacyclovir, famciclovir, systemic corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline, nortriptyline, duloxetine, tramadol, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, opioids, lidocaine patch, and capsaicin preparations; and laboratory indices: white blood cell count, hematocrit (HCT), platelet count (PLT), procalcitonin, lactate, albumin, creatinine, D-dimer, C-reactive protein, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate. In addition, we derived a key variable (antiviral initiation delay ≥ 3 days [AVdelay3d]) to capture differences in treatment timing.

2.2.3. Missing data handling and coding strategy

To minimize bias from missing data and improve model stability, we applied a tiered approach. Variables with > 40% missingness were excluded from modeling. For variables with ≤ 20% missingness, median imputation was used. For variables with 20–40% missingness, multiple imputation by chained equations was performed. Categorical variables were encoded using one-hot encoding. To avoid data leakage, imputation and standardization parameters were fitted using the training set only and then applied to the validation set. During cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning, imputation and standardization were performed within each fold (i.e., “fit on the training fold: apply to the validation fold”) to ensure an unbiased evaluation. Continuous variables were Z-score standardized (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) before least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression to ensure comparability of penalty effects across variables with different scales. Tree-based models (extreme gradient boosting [XGBoost]/ Light gradient-boosting machine [LightGBM]/categorical boosting [CatBoost/adaptive boosting [AdaBoost]) are generally scale-invariant; nevertheless, the same preprocessing pipeline was applied to maintain workflow consistency and reproducibility.

2.2.4. Follow-up window and outcome ascertainment

Because MIMIC-IV captures longitudinal data within the contributing healthcare system, PHN ascertainment in this study was based on subsequent inpatient readmissions. The index date was defined as the first inpatient admission with a recorded HZ diagnosis. Follow-up started on the index date and extended to inpatient readmissions occurring ≥ 90 days thereafter. PHN was identified within these eligible readmissions using PHN-specific ICD diagnosis codes. Patients with no recorded PHN ICD code during eligible readmissions were classified as non-PHN within the observable data. Accordingly, the present study should be interpreted as being based on hospitalized patients captured within the MIMIC-IV system, rather than an exclusively ICU-defined cohort. This approach reflects real-world, coding-based surveillance and may not capture PHN managed outside the recording system or without rehospitalisation.

2.3. Model development, optimisation, and feature selection

2.3.1. Data split and modelling framework

PHN occurrence was defined as a binary outcome (0 = no PHN; 1 = PHN). The cohort was randomly split into a training set and a validation set at a 7:3 ratio, with the proportion of PHN cases preserved in both sets using stratified sampling for final evaluation of generalisability.

2.3.2. Model types

We developed and compared 5 models: LASSO logistic regression, CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost, and AdaBoost. LASSO was used for sparse linear modeling and variable selection. The other 4 models are ensemble/gradient-boosting approaches capable of capturing nonlinear relationships and feature interactions.

2.3.3. Hyperparameter tuning and cross-validation

During training, 5-fold cross-validation was used for hyperparameter tuning, with log-loss as the primary optimization objective. For LASSO, the penalty parameter (λ) was selected according to the minimum binomial deviance criterion from cross-validation, and the coefficient path and cross-validation curve across λ values were reported. For CatBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM, and AdaBoost, Bayesian optimization/automated search strategies were applied within predefined hyperparameter spaces to tune key parameters (e.g., learning rate, tree depth, and subsampling ratio), aiming to improve validation performance while controlling overfitting.

2.3.4. Feature selection and interpretability analysis

To obtain a more robust core feature set and to elucidate the mechanisms underlying model predictions, this study adopted 2 complementary approaches: Boruta feature selection: On the training set, a random forest was used as the base learner to generate shadow features and iteratively compare the importance of real features against their shadow counterparts. Variables were then categorized as Confirmed, Tentative, or Rejected; LASSO sparse selection: Feature selection was achieved via coefficient shrinkage controlled by the regularization parameter λ. For the best-performing tree-based model (e.g., XGBoost), we further reported the feature-importance ranking to identify combinations of variables that contributed most to predicting PHN.

2.4. Statistical analysis and model evaluation

Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical variables as n (%). Differences between the non-PHN and PHN groups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ2 test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. The cohort was randomly split into training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio using stratified sampling to preserve the proportion of PHN cases. To prevent data leakage, all preprocessing steps (imputation, one-hot encoding, and standardization where applicable) were fit on the training set and then applied to the validation set. Hyperparameters were tuned within the training set using 5-fold cross-validation with log-loss as the optimization objective. Discrimination was evaluated primarily by the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) on the validation set, with ROC curves plotted for the 5 candidate models. On the validation set, the optimal decision threshold was selected by maximizing the Youden index, and sensitivity, specificity, Youden index, and accuracy were reported at the selected threshold.

3.2. Comparison of baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics were summarized in Table 1. The overall median age was 71.0 years (IQR 58.0–82.0) and did not differ significantly between the non-PHN group (73.0 years, IQR 58.0–83.0) and the PHN group (67.0 years, IQR 58.0–78.0) (P = .068). Females accounted for 54.6% of the cohort, with no significant between-group difference (P = .237). Race/ethnicity distribution was also similar across groups (overall P for race/ethnicity = .088). Regarding clinical presentation, the PHN group had a higher maximum pain score during the index hospitalization than the non-PHN group (P = .026). Prodromal pain was more frequent in the PHN group (75.3% vs 63.9%), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .073). AVdelay3d was more common among patients who developed PHN (24.7 vs 12.3%, P = .009). In terms of comorbidities, diabetes was more prevalent in the PHN group than in the non-PHN group (30.9 vs 19.9%, P = .034). Severe immunosuppression was also more common among PHN patients (69.1 vs 54.6%, P = .019). Other comorbidities, including hypertension, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and chronic kidney disease, did not differ significantly between groups. For medications, amitriptyline use was higher in the PHN group (9.9 vs 4.0%, P = .047). No significant between-group differences were observed for other antiviral agents or adjunct analgesics, although opioid use was numerically higher in the PHN group (71.6 vs 60.9%) and did not reach statistical significance (P = .077). With respect to laboratory indices, HCT was lower in the PHN group than in the non-PHN group (P = .034). D-dimer levels were higher in the PHN group, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .062). No significant differences were observed for other inflammatory or biochemical markers, including white blood cell count, albumin, creatinine, and C-reactive protein.

3.3. Model discrimination, calibration, and classification performance

Among the 5 models, XGBoost showed the best overall predictive performance. The AUC was 0.737 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.652–0.822). At the selected threshold of 0.38, the sensitivity, specificity, Youden index, and accuracy were 76.5%, 74.2%, 0.507, and 74.7%, respectively. In addition, XGBoost had the lowest Brier score (0.1327) and a nonsignificant Hosmer–Lemeshow test result (χ2 = 6.182, P = .624), indicating acceptable calibration.

LightGBM and CatBoost showed slightly lower but comparable performance. The AUCs were 0.708 (95% CI: 0.618–0.798) for LightGBM and 0.701 (95% CI: 0.610–0.792) for CatBoost. For LightGBM, the sensitivity was 72.8%, the specificity was 72.2%, the Youden index was 0.450, and the accuracy was 72.3%. For CatBoost, the corresponding values were 71.6%, 71.5%, 0.431, and 71.5%, respectively. The Brier scores were 0.1419 for LightGBM and 0.1452 for CatBoost, and both models showed nonsignificant Hosmer–Lemeshow test results, suggesting acceptable calibration.

In contrast, AdaBoost showed limited discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.534 (95% CI: 0.432–0.636). LASSO logistic regression performed poorly, with an AUC of 0.483 (95% CI: 0.379–0.587), close to random classification. The 2 models also showed less favorable calibration, with higher Brier scores (0.1642 and 0.1671, respectively) and significant Hosmer–Lemeshow test results (AdaBoost: χ2 = 16.327, P = .032; LASSO logistic regression: χ2 = 18.449, P = .018). The ROC curves showed findings consistent with the AUC results, indicating that the tree-based models, especially XGBoost, had better predictive performance in this cohort (Fig. 1; Table 2).

The Boruta feature-importance analysis (Fig. 2) showed that a stable set of core predictors was consistently identified in the “confirmed important” region on the right, mainly including D-dimer, HCT, creatinine, AVdelay3d, age, and PLT. In contrast, variables such as albumin and heart failure were classified as “tentative,” while most other variables were deemed insufficiently important and “rejected” under this framework.

4. Discussion

We built and compared several PHN prediction models in 383 HZ (81 PHN) patients from MIMIC-IV. The tree-based models clearly outperformed the sparse linear model. XGBoost achieved the best AUC (0.737), with 76.5% sensitivity and 74.2% specificity. LightGBM and CatBoost performed similarly, whereas LASSO logistic regression was close to chance (AUC 0.483) (Table 2). This is clinically plausible because PHN reflects several linked processes (nerve injury, inflammation/immune activation, glial responses, and central sensitization) so relationships between predictors and outcomes are unlikely to be purely linear.[13,14] At the feature level, the Boruta importance analysis identified a small set of key clinical and laboratory indicators as “confirmed important” (e.g., D-dimer, HCT, creatinine, age, and an AVdelay3d). This suggests that among the many candidate variables, relatively stable and reproducible predictive signals remain, which may support early identification of high-risk patients and risk-stratified management and clinical decision-making (see Fig. 2 and 4). It should be noted that feature importance indicates a variable’s contribution to the model’s predictions and does not imply causality; these indicators are more likely to reflect composite surrogates of disease severity, systemic inflammation, and organ dysfunction. In contrast, traditional comorbidity indicators and certain medication-use variables did not demonstrate stable, independent predictive contributions in the multivariable ML model. This suggests that once the model includes measures that more directly capture physiological status and disease severity, some diagnosis- or medication-related variables add little incremental information. Their apparent effects may be accounted for by other indicators or may be unstable due to confounding by indication in treatment selection. Therefore, PHN risk assessment should emphasize an integrated characterization of overall physiological responses and disease-severity phenotypes, rather than relying on a single diagnosis or treatment measure.

Acute-phase pain and treatment timing were broadly consistent with prior evidence and may represent clinically modifiable factors. In Table 1, patients who developed PHN reported higher peak pain scores during the acute episode (P = .026), and prodromal pain was more common, although this difference did not reach conventional significance (P = .073). These observations are consistent with prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses showing that severe acute pain (odds ratio [OR], 2.53; 95% CI, 2.10–3.06) and prodromal pain (OR, 1.92; 95% CI, 1.43–2.58) are among the most reproducible predictors of PHN and may reflect a greater burden of neural inflammation and dorsal root ganglion involvement.[10,15] A clinically important signal in our cohort was treatment timing. AVdelay3d occurred more frequently in the PHN group (24.7 vs 12.3%, P = .009) and ranked among the leading variables in the Boruta analysis, suggesting that timing contributes meaningfully to risk stratification in this dataset (see Table 1 and Fig. 2). Current guidelines generally recommend initiating antiviral therapy within 72 hours of rash or symptom onset, which can hasten lesion crusting and healing and reduce acute pain.[5] Whether early antiviral therapy reduces PHN incidence in the longer term remains uncertain, as findings from randomized trials and real-world studies are not fully concordant.[16] In a recent network meta-analysis of 17 randomized controlled trials (n = 5579), famciclovir was associated with a lower incidence of PHN compared with placebo (OR, 0.42; 95% CI, 0.18–0.99), whereas valacyclovir (OR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.18–1.51) and brivudine (OR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.16–1.97) showed nonsignificant reductions.[8] Taken together, our results support clinical pathways that prioritize prompt antiviral initiation and proactive pain control in the acute phase, particularly for patients with higher baseline risk (e.g., immunosuppression and diabetes).

Metabolic and immune status: diabetes and immunosuppression were again confirmed in this cohort. In our cohort, diabetes was more prevalent in the PHN group (30.9 vs 19.9%, P = .034), and severe immunosuppression was also more common (69.1 vs 54.6%, P = .019) (Table 1). Large meta-analyses and recent systematic reviews similarly suggest that older age (OR, 1.52; 95% CI, 1.40–1.64; per 10-year increase in age), severe immunosuppression (OR, 2.08; 95% CI, 1.69–2.57), and diabetes (OR, 1.78; 95% CI, 1.54–2.07) are associated with an increased risk of PHN,[15] potentially by impairing varicella-zoster virus-specific cell-mediated immunity and prolonging the window of viral and inflammatory stimulation, thereby exacerbating neural injury and central sensitization.[10] From a public health perspective, real-world studies indicate that the RZV can substantially reduce the risk of PHN, wit h an estimated vaccine efficacy of 87% (95% CI, 65–96), highlighting that strengthening vaccination uptake and follow-up among patients with diabetes or impaired immune function may reduce the burden of PHN at the source.[6,9]

Laboratory indicators and mechanistic interpretation: D-dimer and HCT warrant attention. Although the univariate comparison showed that the between-group difference in D-dimer did not reach statistical significance, it exhibited a trend toward significance (P = .062), and the PHN group had a significantly lower HCT (P = .034) (see Table 1). However, in the Boruta feature-importance analysis, D-dimer and HCT still ranked among the most important variables. This suggests that they may influence PHN risk through nonlinear effects or via interactions with other factors (e.g., age, immune status, and renal function). An increasing body of research suggests that the development and persistence of PHN are not merely static residual consequences of peripheral nerve injury; rather, they may involve sustained immune-inflammatory activation, cytokine/chemokine-mediated signaling pathways, and neuro-immune interactions (including glial activation).[13,17,18] In addition, microvascular/endothelial dysfunction and activation of the coagulation cascade may contribute to the persistence and amplification of pain by altering the perineural microenvironment and sensitization processes.[19,20] A reduced HCT may indicate anemia, a higher burden of malnutrition and chronic disease, or diminished physiological reserve, potentially impairing neural repair and increasing susceptibility to central sensitization.[21–25] It should be emphasized that these interpretations are primarily mechanistic inferences.

Differences in model performance and feature selection: nonlinear structure is key to PHN prediction. As shown in Figure 2, the gradient-boosted tree models (XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost) showed comparable discrimination, with AUCs around 0.70, whereas LASSO performed markedly worse. The LASSO coefficient path and cross-validation profile illustrate the expected bias-variance trade-off between a more parsimonious specification (fewer nonzero coefficients) and a less constrained model (more predictors). However, even at the optimal λ, discrimination remained poor (Fig. 3), indicating that PHN risk in this cohort is unlikely to be explained by a small set of dominant linear effects. A more plausible interpretation is that PHN arises from nonlinear and interacting influences spanning several domains: e.g., pain burden in the acute phase interacting with immune status, treatment timing, and laboratory/physiological markers. This pattern aligns with recent PHN prediction work, which frequently reports superior performance of ML approaches over traditional linear models when integrating multi-dimensional clinical features.[26] Our Boruta plot (Fig. 2) and the coefficient-based importance bar chart (Fig. 4) provide interpretability cues: delayed antiviral initiation, age, and several laboratory markers repeatedly emerged, suggesting relative stability. By contrast, when medication variables (e.g., amitriptyline, gabapentin, and acyclovir) rank highly in importance plots, potential indication bias or reverse causality should be considered (more severe pain or higher risk may increase the likelihood of receiving these medications). Therefore, such variables are better described as clinical phenotype markers associated with PHN, rather than implying that treatment causes PHN (Table 1).

This study supports a more actionable clinical management pathway. At admission or the initial encounter, rapid risk stratification can be performed using acute pain intensity, delayed antiviral therapy, diabetes/immunosuppression status, and routine laboratory indices such as HCT and D-dimer. For high-risk patients, in addition to initiating antiviral therapy as early as possible, analgesia and follow-up should be intensified early, and at the public health level, priority coverage of the RZV should be expanded among high-risk groups (e.g., immunosuppressed individuals and patients with diabetes).[27]

This study has several limitations. First, PHN was identified using PHN-specific ICD diagnostic codes recorded during readmissions within the same healthcare system, which may have led to underascertainment of cases managed at other institutions or cases without readmission. Second, the single-center retrospective design may have introduced selection bias. Moreover, because the model was developed using data from a specific tertiary-care healthcare system, its generalizability to other hospitals and settings may be limited. Variations in patient characteristics, disease severity, clinical practice, coding patterns, and healthcare resources may affect its performance in external populations. Third, the number of PHN events was relatively small (81 cases), which may have limited the robustness of developing and comparing multiple ML models and increased the risk of overfitting, particularly for more flexible tree-based algorithms. Although an internal evaluation was conducted, the possibility of overfitting cannot be fully ruled out. Model complexity and overfitting risk were addressed to some extent through 5-fold cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning, Bayesian optimization of tree-based models within predefined hyperparameter spaces, and feature selection/regularization using Boruta and LASSO. In addition, preprocessing was performed within the training data and cross-validation folds to avoid data leakage. However, these measures may not fully offset the constraints imposed by the limited number of outcome events. Finally, external validation was not performed. Although internal calibration was assessed using the Brier score and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, a more comprehensive evaluation of calibration, including calibration plots, as well as validation in independent cohorts, is still needed. Future studies should establish large, multicenter prospective cohorts and conduct external validation, calibration assessment, and decision-curve analysis in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis + Artificial Intelligence and Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool frameworks. Building on this foundation, simplified risk scores, nomograms, or web-based calculators should be further developed and validated to promote the model’s implementation in routine clinical practice.[28–30]

5. Conclusion

Using the MIMIC-IV database, we developed and compared several ML models to predict the risk of PHN in hospitalized patients with HZ. Among these models, tree-based methods, particularly XGBoost, outperformed the linear LASSO model, suggesting that PHN development may be driven by complex, nonlinear interactions among clinical features, treatment timing, and physiological status. Descriptive analyses and feature-importance rankings consistently highlighted several key predictors, including greater acute pain burden, AVdelay3d, diabetes, severe immunosuppression, lower HCT, and laboratory markers such as D-dimer, creatinine, and PLT. These findings may facilitate earlier identification of patients at high risk of PHN and support risk-stratified clinical management. Nevertheless, the results should be interpreted with caution, as the models were only internally validated in a single retrospective dataset, and external validation is needed before routine clinical use.
