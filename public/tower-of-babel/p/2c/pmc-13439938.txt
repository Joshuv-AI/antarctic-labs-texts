Extracellular volume fraction by cardiac computed tomography (ECVCT): opportunities and challenges for clinical implementation
Authors: Rhys Gray, Elisa Rauseo, Anna Herrey, Emma Cheasty, Thomas A Treibel, Francesca Pugliese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiac computed tomography (CT)–derived extracellular volume (ECVCT) estimates myocardial interstitial expansion by quantifying the distribution of iodinated contrast within the extracellular space, providing a CT marker of focal and diffuse fibrosis as well as amyloid infiltration. In single-energy CT, ECVCT is calculated from the change in myocardial and blood-pool attenuation between non-contrast and delayed scans, adjusted for haematocrit. Spectral CT, including photon-counting CT (PCCT), can quantify ECVCT from energy-resolved data and may reduce reliance on a separate true non-contrast scan. Across several disease cohorts, ECVCT correlates well with cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR)–derived ECV and, in selected studies, with histological collagen volume fraction, supporting potential for global and regional assessment. ECVCT can add myocardial tissue characterization to examinations already performed for coronary and structural heart disease, including opportunistic screening for concomitant transthyretin amyloidosis during transcatheter aortic valve implantation planning, risk stratification in aortic stenosis, and adjunct phenotyping after CT coronary angiography in troponin-positive acute chest pain or heart failure work-up. Furthermore, CT is more accessible, better tolerated, and potentially cheaper than CMR. However, clinical adoption is potentially limited by added workflow steps due to the delayed acquisition, additional radiation exposure, and heterogeneous approaches to acquisition, reconstruction, and analysis, which reduce reproducibility and complicate the definition of normalcy rates and diagnostic thresholds. Developments in PCCT, automation, and synthetic haematocrit may streamline protocols, but require further calibration and agreement-focused validation. Consensus guidance and prospective multicentre outcome studies are needed to standardize protocols, harmonize reporting, define reference ranges, and support clinically actionable thresholds.Keywords: cardiac computed tomography, extracellular volume, myocardial fibrosis, cardiac amyloidosis, transcatheter aortic valve implantation, photon-counting CT

Introduction

Myocardial fibrosis is a key pathological feature of many cardiac conditions, marked by excessive accumulation of collagen-rich extracellular matrix in the myocardial interstitium,1 leading to adverse remodelling, and functional impairment.2 Cardiac computed tomography (CT) extracellular volume (ECVCT) quantification enables non-invasive assessment of myocardial interstitial expansion by measuring the distribution of iodinated contrast in the extracellular space. ECVCT correlates well with cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR)–derived ECV (ECVCMR) and, in selected studies, collagen volume fraction on histology,3,4 with growing evidence supporting diagnostic and prognostic value across a range of cardiac diseases.1,5

With the widespread availability of cardiac CT and shorter acquisition time than CMR, ECVCT provides an opportunity to assess myocardial interstitial expansion due to fibrosis and amyloid deposition, particularly in patients undergoing coronary or structural cardiac CT evaluation for other reasons. This integrated approach can improve diagnostic yield and impact patient management without additional scans.5 For example, identifying markedly elevated ECV suggestive of cardiac amyloidosis can prompt confirmatory testing, early therapy, and refined prognostication.

However, clinical adoption of ECVCT faces challenges, including radiation exposure, longer scan times that may add an extra workflow step, and considerable variability in acquisition protocols. Differences in imaging approaches, such as single- vs. dual-energy acquisition, post-contrast imaging timing, scan mode, tube voltage, iodine contrast dose, reconstructed slice thickness, and whole heart vs. region of interest (ROI) sampling, may have contributed to inconsistencies in ECVCT estimates across both healthy individuals and patients with cardiac disease. In this review, we summarize the current literature on ECVCT highlighting the key opportunities and challenges associated with its integration into routine clinical practice, with emphasis on standardization, photon-counting CT (PCCT), and pragmatic protocol development.

Comparison of ECVCT with ECVCMR and histology

ECVCT has been evaluated against both CMR and endomyocardial biopsy. ECVCMR derives from pre- and post-contrast T1 mapping and, like ECVCT, reflects myocardial extracellular space expansion, enabling assessment of diffuse interstitial expansion beyond late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). In severe AS, Bandula et al. showed significant correlations between equilibrium single-energy ECVCT and histological fibrosis (r = 0.71, P < 0.001]), as well as between ECVCT and ECVCMR (r = 0.73, P < 0.0002).4 Equilibrium imaging uses a contrast bolus injection followed by slower infusion to achieve true contrast equilibrium during delayed scans. However, this method is impractical clinically as it is time-consuming and requires a high contrast dose. Treibel et al. reported a strong relationship between 5-min single-energy ECVCT and ECVCMR (r2 = 0.85, P < 0.001) in patients with biopsy-proven amyloidosis or severe AS.8 More recently, Ohta et al. compared segmental ECVCT and ECVCMR in 23 patients with either ischaemic or dilated cardiomyopathy who also underwent invasive angiography.9 They reported strong correlations across all myocardial segments (P < 0.001). These findings suggest the potential of utilizing ECVCT for both global and regional assessment of myocardial fibrosis, potentially widening its clinical applications. ECVCT has also been found to correlate with ECVCMR across various other cardiac diseases, including pulmonary hypertension,10 sarcoidosis,11 and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.12 A recent meta-analysis of 13 studies (383 patients with a range of cardiac diseases) reported excellent overall correlation between ECVCT and ECVCMR [mean correlation: 0.90 (95% CI: 0.86–0.95)], although study quality was limited and protocols were heterogeneous.1

Overall, the literature shows strong associations between CT- and CMR-derived ECV across several disease cohorts and, in selected populations, against histology; however, evidence is limited by small sample sizes and heterogeneous acquisition and analysis protocols, reducing generalizability and cross-study comparability. In addition, correlation alone does not establish interchangeability at the individual-patient level, highlighting the need for standardized agreement and repeatability reporting and for larger prospective multicentre studies to define robust normal ranges and clinically actionable thresholds.

Opportunities for clinical use

ECVCT can add myocardial tissue characterization to CT examinations already performed for coronary or structural heart assessment.

In older patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) planning CT, ECVCT may support screening for concomitant transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy, which affects approximately one in seven patients with severe aortic stenosis (AS),13 and its prevalence is higher (up to 30%) in low-flow, low-gradient disease.14 Treibel et al.8 showed substantially higher ECVCT in amyloidosis than AS (0.54 ± 0.11 vs. 0.28 ± 0.04, P < 0.001). Similarly, Scully et al.5 reported good diagnostic performance for detecting AS with concomitant amyloid (accuracy 0.87 overall; 0.95 for Perugini Grade 2), outperforming conventional electrocardiographic and echocardiographic markers. Detecting markedly elevated ECVCT in this setting may prompt targeted confirmatory testing, earlier treatment, and refined risk assessment. ECVCT is also associated with prognosis in severe AS undergoing either surgical or transcatheter aortic valve replacement (AVR). Elevated ECVCT in low-flow low-gradient AS has been linked with increased mortality and heart failure post-TAVI.15 A meta-analysis of 10 studies (n = 1223 patients) showed higher cardiovascular events, mortality, and heart failure hospitalizations in patients with elevated ECVCT (mean 36.8 ± 1.3) over a median follow-up of 17.9 ± 2.3 months after surgical or transcatheter AVR,16 supporting its potential role in incremental risk stratification.

In patients with heart failure or left ventricular (LV) dysfunction undergoing CT coronary angiography (CTCA) to evaluate coronary artery disease as a potential cause, ECV mapping can extend CT from anatomical stenosis assessment to myocardial tissue characterization. Contemporary cardiac CT also assesses plaque morphology beyond stenosis severity, including non-calcified plaque, low-attenuation plaque, positive remodelling, spotty calcification, and other high-risk plaque features. PCCT may further improve plaque assessment through higher spatial resolution, reduced blooming from calcification, and improved contrast-to-noise ratio. Combining plaque assessment with ECV mapping could therefore support integrated evaluation of coronary atherosclerotic substrate and downstream myocardial tissue response within a single examination.17 Regional ECV elevation aligned with a coronary territory may support ischaemic aetiology and increase diagnostic confidence, as illustrated by focal ECV elevation corresponding to prior inferior myocardial infarction in Figure 1. In a prospective study of 56 patients, Liu et al.18 compared global and segmental ECVCT with CMR LGE across myocardial segments with ischaemic LGE, non-ischaemic LGE, and no LGE. They reported good CT-CMR correlation in all groups (r = 0.920, 0.936, and 0.799, respectively; all P < 0.001). ECVCT has also been explored as a prognostic marker in dilated cardiomyopathy.19 However, in advanced remodelling, wall thinning and chamber dilatation may increase partial-volume effects and blood-pool contamination making myocardial segmentation/ROI sampling more sensitive to motion; in this setting, CMR remains preferred for tissue characterization and LGE pattern assessment.

CTCA is routinely used for stable chest pain, and in selected acute settings, it may be incorporated into triple-rule-out protocols.20 In stable chest pain populations, ECVCT quantification can be integrated into the CTCA workflow using low-dose protocols and short post-contrast delays, adding myocardial phenotyping beyond coronary anatomy, with good agreement reported vs. CMR-derived ECV.21

In troponin-positive acute chest pain not meeting myocardial infarction criteria, adding CT myocardial characterization to triple-rule-out imaging can aid diagnosis when CTCA rules out coronary disease, pulmonary embolism, and aortic syndromes. Delayed CT imaging can identify focal myocardial injury through late iodine enhancement (LIE), analogous to CMR LGE, while ECV mapping detects diffuse extracellular expansion. Consistent with this concept, Esposito et al. reported that colour-coded ECVCT maps with LIE supported diagnoses including myocarditis, myocardial infarction with non-obstructed coronary arteries, and amyloidosis.22 Similarly, in a prospective study of 84 patients, Palmisano et al. showed that adding delayed myocardial imaging with LIE and ECV analysis to a CTCA/triple-rule-out pathway increased diagnostic yield from approximately 50–90% by enabling classification of non-coronary causes of myocardial injury.23 Larger studies are needed to define clinical impact, workflow, radiation/contrast burden, and cost-effectiveness.

Finally, additional potential clinical applications of ECVCT include scenarios where myocardial tissue characterization is required, but CMR is contraindicated, such as in patients with ferromagnetic implants or those who are claustrophobic and unable to complete a CMR protocol.

Challenges for implementation

Despite its clinical promise, translation of ECVCT into routine practice faces challenges related to workflow, radiation burden, and methodological variability that may limit reproducibility and threshold definition.

Workflow impact

Cardiac ECVCT imaging requires an additional delayed image acquisition (3–7 min post-contrast), to reach pseudo-equilibrium state, where contrast agent concentration in the vascular bed and interstitial space becomes (near) balanced, while little excretion by renal filtration has yet occurred. This additional delay may impact CT workflows and reduce overall efficiency. The requirement for precise planning, timing, and synchronization with cardiac motion of both baseline and delayed imaging also adds operational complexity.

Radiation exposure

A potential limitation of cardiac ECVCT is the additional radiation dose associated with acquiring a delayed scan, which is particularly relevant in younger patients or those who may require serial imaging. This is more problematic if a baseline scan is also required. It is important that both baseline and delayed scans share acquisition parameters and strategies for dose optimization. Excessive dose reduction in baseline scans may impair image subtraction due to error propagation. Reported effective radiation doses for ECVCT protocols vary substantially across studies because of differences in scanner technology, tube voltages, ECG-gating strategy, and acquisition/reconstruction parameters.4,9,15,26,27 In single-energy protocols, Nacif et al. reported a mean effective radiation dose of 1.98 ± 0.16 mSv, while Treibel et al. reported 1.56 ± 0.58 mSv.26 In dual energy/spectral CT, the delayed phase acquisition has been reported in the range from 1.89 to 4.8 mSv.9,27. Early data from PCCT suggest that total protocol dose can be low but remains protocol-dependent [e.g. 2.07 ± 1.9 mSv in Aquino et al. and 1.2 mSv (0.97–1.75) in Mergen et al.28,29]. For context, the effective radiation dose associated with CTCA alone typically ranges from 1–10 mSv, depending on scanner generation and protocol optimization.30

Single-energy (subtraction method) vs. multi-energy (two-material deconvolution, iodine maps) CT

In terms of acquisition, two primary approaches are currently used to quantify ECV on cardiac CT: the conventional pre- and post-contrast subtraction method and the iodine-based two-material decomposition using dual-energy or spectral/PCCT.

The subtraction method derives ECV from the change in myocardium and blood-pool attenuation between a true non-contrast scan and a delayed post-contrast equilibrium phase. In contrast, multi-energy approaches (e.g. dual-source, rapid kVp switching, dual-layer detectors, or spectral/PCCT) perform two-material decomposition and generate iodine maps from a single delayed acquisition, reducing reliance on a separate true non-contrast scan. While the subtraction-based ECVCT has been widely used, it presents several limitations. Differentiation between myocardial tissue and the blood pool on pre-contrast images can be challenging, particularly due to limited contrast resolution. Co-registration of baseline and delayed imaging with an angiographic dataset has been implemented to overcome this issue. Furthermore, the reliance on separate acquisitions introduces the risk of misregistration artefacts, especially in patients with arrhythmias or significant cardiac motion between scans, which may compromise accuracy. Emoto et al.12 directly compared these two techniques and found that iodine-based ECV mapping provided more accurate and reproducible myocardial ECV measurements than the subtraction method. Specifically, ECVCT quantification using the iodine method showed closer agreement with ECVCMR values, with a bias of −0.9% (95% CI: −2.22–0.42) and a correlation coefficient (r) of 0.95, compared to the subtraction method, which exhibited a bias of −1.5% (95% CI: −4.95–1.95) and r = 0.84. Dual-energy CT enables two-material decomposition but can be limited by spectral overlap; photon-counting detectors mitigate this through intrinsic energy resolution, with implications for ECV imaging discussed in the dedicated PCCT and ECV section.

Scan mode

When single-energy imaging is used (with an energy-integrating detector), scan mode, tube voltage, and contrast dosing influence image quality and ECVCT precision and therefore require standardization. Koike et al. reported that sequential prospectively ECG-triggered acquisition (step and shoot) yielded more reliable and reproducible ECV quantification than high-pitch helical (Flash) scanning, both for septal and global assessments.31 The step-and-shoot approach of sequential mode reduces motion artefacts by capturing stacks of data over multiple heartbeats, during periods of minimal cardiac movement, usually mid-diastole, or end-systole at higher or variable heart rates. High-pitch helical scanning enables rapid acquisition with reduced radiation exposure, but this can come at the expense of image quality and ECVCT precision.

Tube voltage also varies widely in the literature, from low-dose single-energy protocols (e.g. 80 kVp)5 to dual-energy combinations (e.g. 90/150 kVp or 80/140 kVp).32,33

Contrast and image delay

Contrast dosing protocols are heterogeneous, and the optimal delay for ECVCT quantification has not been established by consensus. Shorter delays may risk incomplete contrast equilibrium, whereas longer delays reduce iodine signal due to renal excretion. The net impact on ECV precision depends on protocol and patient factors. In a meta-analysis, Zhang et al. reported delayed imaging time ranging from 5 to 25 min and found that studies using delays ≤ 7 min showed higher correlation with ECVCMR than those using delays > 7 min.3 Similarly, Treibel et al. reported that a 5-min delay post-contrast injection performed better than 15 min, with a higher signal-to-noise ratio and stronger correlation with ECVCMR.8 More recently, Hamdy et al. found no significant differences in ECVCT values across 3-, 5-, and 7-min delays.32

Image reconstruction and analysis

Slice thickness and reconstruction increment vary across studies, with reported slice thicknesses ranging from 0.633 to 5 mm.4 An optimal slice thickness balances minimizing image noise and accurately measuring mean myocardial tissue density. Thinner slices improve spatial resolution and detail but increase image noise, while thicker slices reduce noise at the expense of blurring fine structures and potentially introducing partial-volume effects.

Regarding image analysis, ECVCT quantification has been performed using either a focused septal ROI or a more global whole-left-ventricle approach (with application of 16-segment models). Septal sampling is often used because it can be easier to delineate on non-contrast images and may be less affected by some beam-hardening artefacts, whereas whole-heart methods may better capture regional heterogeneity. In a systematic review of 13 studies including 248 healthy participants, most studies used a whole-heart approach (9/13) while others used septal ROI (3/13), highlighting the absence of a consistent analysis strategy.34 Koike et al. reported that septal ECV provides similar values to global ECV and comparable reproducibility, supporting septal sampling as a pragmatic option when whole-heart analysis is not available.31 Arguably, a septal-only approach may fail to detect areas of focal ECV expansion due to replacement fibrosis, such as myocardial infarction, that are localized elsewhere in the LV wall.

Availability of the patient’s haematocrit value is another potential source of variability in ECV calculation. Most published studies used laboratory haematocrit, but synthetic haematocrit, estimated from non-contrast CT blood pool attenuation (and validated against venous haematocrit), may simplify workflow and improve scalability.35 Early data, including PCCT studies, suggest good agreement between synthetic and laboratory haematocrit-based ECV calculation,29,34,36 in research populations, but performance in routine clinical practice and robustness in challenging clinical scenarios (e.g. severe anaemia, blood attenuation affected by drugs, or recent contrast) needs further evaluation. Therefore, venous haematocrit should currently remain the reference standard when available.

In summary, the principal confounders of ECVCT quantification include haematocrit measurement method (laboratory vs. synthetic), post-contrast delay, scan mode (sequential vs. high-pitch), tube voltage, iodinated contrast dose, slice thickness and reconstruction kernel, and ROI vs. whole-heart sampling strategy (summarized in Table 2). Reproducible thresholds and reference ranges will require standardization across each of these parameters.

Normal values and diagnostic thresholds

Reported ECVCT values vary across studies, even in individuals without known cardiovascular disease. A recent meta-analysis34 reported a pooled mean ECVCT value of 27.6% (95% CI: 25.7–29.4%) in healthy participants, but with substantial between-study heterogeneity and overlap with disease estimates. The pooled mean was 31.2% in patients with severe AS, 50% in cardiac amyloidosis, and 37% in non-ischaemic dilated cardiomyopathy. Meta-regression suggested that CT protocol differences explained ∼25% of this heterogeneity, with key contributors including delayed imaging time, single-vs. dual-energy/spectral technique, contrast/iodine administration protocols, and haematocrit definition method (measured vs. synthetic).34

Building on recent reviews of ECV protocols across CT modalities,35,37 and a recent multi-institutional consensus document specific to PCCT,38 we propose here a pragmatic baseline protocol for ECVCT across single-energy, dual-energy, and photon-counting acquisitions (Table 3). Based on the available evidence and our institutional experience with cardiac CT workflows, we suggest that ECVCT implementation should prioritize reproducibility over technical complexity. In practice, this means standardizing delay time, using whole-heart segmentation when available, retaining septal ROI as a pragmatic fallback, and using venous haematocrit as the reference standard until synthetic haematocrit is more widely validated. Prospective harmonization studies are still required to confirm the optimal protocol strategy.

Photon-counting CT and ECV

Photon-counting CT is reshaping myocardial ECV imaging. Photon-counting detectors directly convert X-ray photons into electrical signals with intrinsic energy resolution, improving spatial resolution, contrast-to-noise ratio, material decomposition, and dose efficiency compared with conventional energy-integrating detector CT.38,39 For ECV imaging, this may enable lower-noise iodine maps in thin myocardial walls and the ability to generate virtual non-contrast images from a single delayed acquisition, potentially obviating a dedicated baseline scan.29,36,38

Clinical evidence is accumulating across the principal indications. Aquino et al. reported strong correlation between first-generation dual-source PCCT-derived ECV and CMR in 29 participants referred for CMR, with good-to-excellent reliability for midventricular and global ECV quantification and a small ECV overestimation of approximately 2%.28 Mergen et al. evaluated dual-source PCCT in 30 patients with severe AS undergoing preprocedural TAVI assessment, showing good agreement between ECV derived from 5-min late-enhancement iodine maps and conventional single-energy subtraction ECV (r = 0.87; mean error 0.9%).29 Disease-specific studies have shown strong PCCT-CMR correlation in acute myocarditis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.24,25 In cardiac amyloidosis, Popp et al. showed excellent correlation between PCCT-derived ECV and CMR-derived ECV using both single-energy and dual-energy approaches. The same PCCT examination also allowed assessment of coronary artery disease burden, supporting the potential dual value of PCCT for myocardial tissue characterization and coronary evaluation in this population.40 Consistent with recent multi-institutional guidance, ECVCT should currently be regarded as a promising adjunct for raising or lowering suspicion of amyloidosis within an integrated structural heart assessment, rather than as a stand-alone diagnostic test.38 Tremamunno et al. showed that photon-counting detector CT iodine maps could detect myocardial LIE with high per-patient diagnostic accuracy using LGE-CMR as the reference standard, with sensitivity of 91.7–100%, specificity of 73.3–80.0%, and substantial inter-reader agreement.41

Synthetic haematocrit derived from PCCT virtual non-contrast images has shown good agreement with laboratory haematocrit-based ECV calculation.36 However, subsequent validation against MRI identified systematic overestimation and residual inter-individual variability even after bias correction.42 Therefore, synthetic haematocrit should currently be viewed as a promising workflow simplification rather than a substitute for laboratory haematocrit in routine clinical ECVCT reporting. In the future, if further validated across vendors, scanners, and patient populations, synthetic haematocrit combined with automated iodine mapping could support lower-burden ECVCT workflows with reduced reliance on laboratory sampling.28,43

From a clinical implementation perspective, the most attractive role for PCCT is to reduce the practical barriers that have limited ECVCT adoption, including the need for separate baseline imaging, the risk of scan misregistration, additional dose, and complex manual post-processing. For this reason, PCCT-based ECV may be best prioritized first in pathways where cardiac CT is already being performed, particularly TAVI planning, coronary CT with suspected myocardial disease, and selected patients unable to undergo CMR.

Future directions

Beyond the PCCT-specific developments discussed above, broader work is needed to consolidate ECVCT across modalities. Priorities include harmonized acquisition timing, motion correction, automated segmentation, cross-vendor calibration, and agreement-focused validation across single-energy, dual-energy, and photon-counting platforms.29,34,37,38

Clinical adoption also requires outcome-driven evidence with prospective trials to establish the clinical validity of ECVCT. For instance, the EXPAND TAVR II Pivotal Trial (NCT05149755) includes ECVCT as an exploratory endpoint to evaluate myocardial tissue characteristics in a multicentre TAVI cohort.43 Similar outcome-driven studies in heart failure, cardiomyopathy, and acute chest pain populations are needed to move ECVCT from diagnostic accuracy towards actionable clinical pathways. Ultimately, inter-societal consensus will be needed to define acquisition protocols, calibration standards, reporting frameworks, reference ranges, and clinically meaningful thresholds.

Conclusion

CT-derived ECV quantification is a promising non-invasive tool for evaluating myocardial fibrosis, with growing evidence supporting its diagnostic and prognostic utility across a wide range of cardiac conditions. Its use may be particularly valuable in patients already undergoing coronary or structural heart assessment, offering complementary diagnostic information with minimal additional procedural burden. PCCT, automation, and synthetic haematocrit may help reduce current workflow barriers, although their wider adoption requires protocol standardization, cross-vendor validation, and prospective multicentre outcome studies.
