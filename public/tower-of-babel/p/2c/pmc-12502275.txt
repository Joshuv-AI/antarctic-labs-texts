Biosecurity practices and their determining factors in commercial layer chickens in selected regions of Tanzania
Authors: Siha Mdemu, Augustine B. Matondo, Jens Peter Christensen, Ahmed E. Amasha, Helena A. Ngowi, Erica Westwood, Sunday O. Ochai, Hezron E. Nonga, Kristina Osbjer, Robinson H. Mdegela
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionBiosecurity measures are crucial for controlling infectious diseases in poultry farms. It involves all measures aiming at preventing disease-causing agents from entering the farm (external biosecurity) and those measures practiced with the aim of preventing the spread of disease-causing organisms within the farm (internal biosecurity). However, their implementation is often limited in low-income countries due to various factors such as socioeconomic challenges, farming practices and limited resources. The aim of this study was to explore the current biosecurity levels and their related factors in commercial layer chicken farms.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey of 203 randomly selected commercial layer farms with ≥ 200 birds across Dar es Salaam (n = 154), Morogoro (n = 28), and Unguja (n = 21) regions from March-June 2023. Biosecurity practices were scored using an adapted Biocheck.UGent™ tool (0-100 scale). One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and t-tests were used for comparing biosecurity scores across groups when data met normality assumptions, while the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis (K-W) H test and Mann-Whitney (M-W) test served as alternatives when the normality assumption was not met. Further, we used multiple linear regression to identify factors associated with biosecurity implementation; with results reported as standardized beta coefficients (β) and 95% confidence intervals.ResultsMean biosecurity scores were significantly (p < .001) below global benchmarks with overall biosecurity 58.2 ± 8.6, external biosecurity (preventing disease entry) at 60.5 ± 8.7 and internal biosecurity (preventing disease spread within the farm) at 52.9 ± 14.5 compared to global averages of 72%, 67% and 77% respectively. The average external biosecurity scores was significantly lower in farms supervised by farmers with primary education (57.8 ± 7.9) than both those under farmers with secondary education (61.3 ± 9.3, p = .042) and tertiary education (62.1 ± 8.2, p = .019). Farm experience of > 2 years significantly improved internal biosecurity practices (55.0 ± 14.6 vs. 47.0 ± 12.9, p = .002). Further, large-scale operations demonstrated superior internal biosecurity compared to small-scale farms (63.1 ± 16.4 vs. 51.5 ± 12.8, p = .002). Multiple regression analysis (F₇,₁₉₅=2.279, p = .030, R²=0.076) revealed that secondary education (β = 0.224, 95% CI: 1.03–6.73, p = .007) and tertiary education (β = 0.209, 95% CI: 0.82–7.20, p = .014) were independently associated with higher biosecurity scores compared to primary education.ConclusionThis paper provides the first assessment of biosecurity implementation in commercial layer farms in Tanzania comparing it with global benchmarks. Biosecurity implementations were largely influenced by three key factors; education level, scale of production, and farming experience. These findings indicate that tailored interventions and evidenced-based policies are essential for strengthening biosecurity practices, and potentially enhancing poultry health and productivity in layer farms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05052-9.

Introduction

Commercial layer chickens are a valuable source of high-quality protein and essential nutrients, offering significant nutritional benefits worldwide [1]. Their high feed conversion efficiency and rapid production cycle make them a sustainable, readily available and affordable food option particularly in developing countries [2]. In Tanzania, commercial layer chicken production is greatly expanding to meet the growing demand for eggs primarily due to a combination of factors like rising population, urbanization, and changing consumer preferences that favor poultry products [3]. The current commercial layer chicken population in Tanzania is estimated at 20 million with an annual production estimated at 7.1 billion eggs and per capita consumption of 119 eggs, which is low compared to 300 recommended by FAO [4]. Although egg consumption is projected to double by 2050, requiring an annual production of 27.35 billion eggs [5], the growth of this sub-sector, faces challenges such as diseases, that impact productivity and sustainability [6]. By addressing challenge of diseases, the commercial layer chicken subsector in Tanzania can achieve its full potential and contribute to sustainable economic growth and food security.

Biosecurity plays a crucial role in the control of diseases and improving the overall health of chickens [7]. According to Huber et al. [8] a biosecurity measure is the implementation of a procedure that specifically aims at reducing the probability of the introduction, establishment, survival, or spread of any potential pathogen to, within, or from a farm, operation or geographical area. Biosecurity measures can be broadly categorized into external and internal biosecurity. External biosecurity encompasses all measures aiming at preventing disease-causing agents from entering the farm, and includes those related to location of the farm, purchase, depopulation and transport of chickens, transport of the eggs, supply of feed and water, removal of manure and dead animals, visitors and staff, infrastructure and biological vectors, and interrelation between farms. Internal biosecurity focuses on the measures practiced with the aim of preventing the spread of disease-causing organisms within the farm and includes measures related to disease management in the farm, cleaning and disinfection, inter-house management, and egg management [9]. Globally, consistent implementation and rigorous practice of biosecurity measures are widely recognized as the most effective and cost-efficient approach to prevent disease introduction and spread on commercial layer farms [10, 11]. These measures minimize the risk of infectious disease agents entering and spreading within the farm, ultimately, improving the health of birds, enhancing the overall productivity and reducing the unnecessary use of antimicrobials, a key driver for the development antimicrobial resistance [12, 13].

Studies indicate a widespread antimicrobial use in Tanzania, particularly in small-scale, less educated and younger poultry farmers who tend to rely more on antibiotic usage for disease prevention and treatment [14, 15]. The overuse of antimicrobials among poultry farms in the country poses a major concern due to the potential for antimicrobial resistance and the risk for antibiotic residues in eggs [16–20]. Moreover the high prevalence of diseases in commercial poultry significantly reduces productivity due to increased mortality and morbidity, resulting in substantial economic losses for farmers [21, 22]. Previous reports emphasize the importance of robust biosecurity measures in Tanzania’s commercial poultry farms to reduce disease burden and reliance on antibiotics [15, 23, 24]. Several factors can influence the implementation of biosecurity measures on farms, including geographic location, farming practices, and socio-demographic characteristics of farmers [12]. A study by Gomez and Mbaga [25] on broiler farms in Pwani region of Tanzania indicated that the prevalence of diseases in chickens is linked to lower levels of biosecurity practices emphasizing the need for improving biosecurity across poultry farms.

Regular assessment of compliance with essential biosecurity measures on poultry farms is crucial for strengthening overall biosecurity [11]. This allows for quantifying the levels of biosecurity and identifying areas where improvements are needed [26]. While research exists on biosecurity in farms in Tanzania, there is a gap in research specifically examining the levels of biosecurity measures and the factors influencing their implementation in commercial layer farms. This makes it challenging to fully understand the extent to which biosecurity practices are adopted and what factors contribute to their implementation in poultry industry. It also hinders the understanding of how biosecurity can be more appropriately and consistently implemented in Tanzania and other Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMIC) settings where financial resources and infrastructure are inadequate [27, 28].

Therefore, this study aimed at investigating the level of adoption of biosecurity practices and the factors influencing their implementation among selected commercial layer farms in Tanzania.

Materials and methods

Study area

This research was conducted in Dar es Salaam, Morogoro, and Unguja (Fig. 1), spanning both urban and peri-urban areas, between March and June 2023. The three study locations were chosen due to their ecological attributes that create suitable conditions for diverse levels of commercial layer production, ranging from small to large-scale operations. Commercial layer chickens in the selected regions are specifically bred for maximum egg production and are kept intensively in controlled environments (primarily using deep litter or battery cage systems) that optimizes their egg-laying potential. Dar es Salaam is the most populous region in Tanzania, with a population of 5,383,728 as of the 2022 census [29]. The recent comprehensive national agricultural data [29] shows that the region holds about 25% of the total commercial layers in Mainland Tanzania, with a population of 4,141,502 layers. Dar es Salaam covers an area of 1,393 km² and has a density of 3,865 people per km². This region is located at 6°49’24.6"S 39°16’10.2"E on the eastern coast of Africa, along the Indian Ocean and is characterized by tropical climatic conditions, with hot and humid weather throughout the year. Morogoro Municipality, located at 6° 49′ 27″ S, 37° 39′ 48″ E, is a densely populated area with a population of 471,409 people, a density of 873 per km², approximately 71,297 commercial chickens and a total area of 540 km². It is situated at an elevation of 1,670 feet above sea level and experiences an annual rainfall of 269 mm and an average temperature of 25 °C. Unguja is an island with an area of about 1,666 km2 located at 6° 8′ 0″ S, 39° 20′ 0″ E in the Indian Ocean. It is hot with an average temperature of 27 °C, and humid island with annual rainfall ranging between 1,064 mm and 1,600 mm. The human population is 1,346,338 and has a density of 768.2 people per km². Unguja has about 1.8 million commercial layer chickens, accounting for 87% of the population in Zanzibar Tanzania [29].Fig. 1Map of study sites

Study design and population

A cross-sectional survey was carried out from March to June 2023. The study targeted farm owners, supervisors or managers responsible for flock management and treatment protocols from farms with more than 200 layer chickens. The selection criteria within these farms required participants to be over 18 years and willing to share farm information.

Sample size

Based on registers from the District Veterinary Offices the number of commercial layer chicken farms (N) was estimated at 398 (302 in Dar es Salaam, 54 in Morogoro and 38 in Unguja). Due to the unavailability of information on the levels of biosecurity in the commercial layer farms in the country, the level of inappropriate biosecurity practice was estimated from the assumed prevalence (p) of 50%. Assuming an acceptable error (e) of 5% at a 95% confidence level (Z2 = 3.8416), the resulting initial minimum sample size was estimated to be 193. Accounting for 5% nonresponse a list of 203 farms was generated, divided as 154, 28 and 21 for Dar es Salaam, Morogoro and Unguja, respectively.

Data collection tool and sampling procedure

A semi-structured questionnaire was used to collect data at the farm level. The questionnaire was divided into sections consisting of socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents, farm characteristics and practices related to biosecurity in the farms. The questionnaire was pre-tested in 23 farms (10% of the planned sample size) in a group similar to the anticipated respondents for validity, question framing, relevance, and sequencing and was corrected accordingly. The questionnaire was administered through face-to-face interviews using Swahilli, a primary national language used by the majority of the population in the country for social interaction, education and government affairs. Farms were selected from a frame of 389 farms using a multistage sampling method. In this case from each study site, farms were selected using systematic random sampling with probability proportional to size (PPS) to ensure accurate representation of the population from each study location. Briefly, a comprehensive list of all participants (N) in the study site was prepared, assigning each a unique identifier (UID). The first participant was then selected from the list at random, followed by the selection of subsequent k-th participants using a calculated sampling interval (K = N/n) until the desired sample size (n) was reached. During the survey, livestock extension officers were utilized as local guides to help researchers navigate to the farms and establish rapport with respondents for questionnaire administration. This approach enhanced collection of more reliable data, culminating in the successful survey of 203 planned farms. The surveyed farms were then classified into small-scale (farms with 200–500 birds), medium-scale (farms with 501–2000 birds) and large-scale (farms with more than 2000 birds) as previously described and recommended [3, 32].

Quantification of biosecurity scores

The levels of biosecurity measures practiced in the study farms were assessed using a questionnaire developed by a team of experts following the format of the Biocheck.UGent™ (https://biocheckgent.com/en) scoring tool for layer farms with modifications of the questions to fit the Tanzanian context (Supplementary materials S1) as recommended by other authors [33, 34]. Score weights were attributed to each of the subcategory as originally suggested by Biocheck.UGent. However, due to limited research on the contribution of each individual measure using the adapted questionnaire for the Tanzanian context, all biosecurity measures within each subcategory were considered equally important [33], hence equally weighted (Supplementary materials S2). Individual biosecurity measures were assigned a score between 0 and 1 (0 for absence of the biosecurity measure and 1 for a full implementation of the biosecurity measure). The proportional score of the subcategory was then multiplied by the weight of the subcategory to obtain the sub category score as shown in formula 1 below. The total farm score (formula 4) was the sum of 70% of external biosecurity score (formula 2) and 30% of the internal biosecurity score (formula 3) based on the perceived relative importance of each aspect in the overall assessment [35].

Data analysis

Data were initially processed using Microsoft Office Excel 2016 and then analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp. Descriptive statistics (percentages, frequencies, medians and means) were used to describe the proportions per category and subcategory of the biosecurity. Bivariate analyses using ANOVA with Tukey HSD method for post hoc comparison, Student’s t-test was carried out when a variable satisfied the normality test to compare farm/socio-demographic characteristics and biosecurity scores. Normality and homogeneity of variance was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test respectively. When the normality was not satisfied, non-parametric K-W H test with significance values adjusted by the Bonferroni correction for multiple tests and M-W test were used to test the differences in the biosecurity scores between groups in variables with more than two categories and those with two categories respectively. Variables associated with biosecurity scores with p values < 0.25 (Supplementary material S3) were considered for multiple linear regression analysis to test the association between biosecurity scores (dependent variable) and farm/socio-demographic characteristics (independent variables). Multiple linear regression was carried out using a stepwise method based on the Akaike Information Criteria (AIC). Linearity of the model was assessed by examining scatter plots, independence was checked by analyzing residual plots for patterns and multicollinearity was evaluated using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). Variables with p values < 0.05 were considered significant in the final model.

Results

Sociodemographic and farming characteristics of the respondents

Most participants were farm owners (83.7%) and the majority had substantial experience (75.9% with > 2 years). The overall gender distribution of participants showed a slight female majority (53.2%), however, the distribution varied significantly across regions. Unguja had a notably lower proportion of female respondents (19.0%), compared to Dar es Salaam and Morogoro (57.1%). The majority of participants had secondary education (42.9%), followed by primary education (29.6%), and tertiary education (27.6). Farm operations were predominantly (50.7%) small-scale with 38.9% medium-scale and only 10.3% large-scale. Farm sizes ranged from 200 to 143,000 layers (median: 500, IQR: 270–1000). The majority of farms (68.5%) housed multiple domestic species on-site, a large portion (27.5%) kept chickens of different ages together, and a substantial number (72.9%) used deep litter housing systems (Table 1).

Biosecurity practices

General biosecurity score

The overall biosecurity score in the study farms ranged from 37.9 to 88.0% (Table 2). The average overall biosecurity score was 58.2 ± 8.6. External biosecurity averaged 60.5 ± 8.7 while internal biosecurity was relatively lower (52.9 ± 14.5).

External biosecurity

Infrastructure and access control revealed high vulnerabilities across study farms. The majority (70.9%, n = 144) of the respondents reported that the nearest poultry farm is within a walking distance. Although subjective, walking distance was assumed equivalent to or less than one kilometer based on local context. Thirty-three (16.3%) respondents reported that they never applied any biosecurity measure when entering into the layer houses (Fig. 2). Twenty-four (38.1%) out of the sixty-three respondents that used footbath reported that they normally appied it when entering the houses but not at the exit. While 64.0% of farms were fenced and 16.7% partially fenced, nearly one-fifth (19.2%) had no perimeter fencing. More so, 24.5% of farms kept backyard or free-ranging birds on-site, and 16% allowed pets access to chicken houses. The majority (85.2%) reported vermin problems.Fig. 2Biosecurity measures applied by persons entering and exiting the layer houses

Biosecurity measures applied by persons entering and exiting the layer houses

Feed and water management showed mixed implementation patterns. Most farms (70.9%) maintained dedicated feed storage rooms, though 18.7% stored feed within chicken houses and 7.4% in residential areas, with 3% having no designated storage space. Feed supplier delivery was available to 45.3% of farms, but 15.3% could not adequately protect feeds from water, birds, and vermin. Most of the farmers (75.9%) took no action to ensure drinking water safety for chickens.

Waste management, procurement, and transport practices demonstrated both strengths and critical gaps. Procurement practices showed generally good biosecurity awareness with only 4.9% purchasing live pullets or laying hens and only 2% occasionally allowing entry of equipment such as feeders from other farms into their chicken houses. Manure removal frequency was poor, with 91.1% of farms removing waste monthly or less frequently, and 51.7% storing manure on-site. Carcass disposal was primarily through burial (59.1%). However, nearly half (49.3%) lacked brooder facilities, and only 59.1% consistently purchased day-old chicks from the same source. Egg transport emerged as a key biosecurity failure, with 90.2% using non-disinfected means of transport (bicycles, motorcycles, tricycles, or trucks). More than three-quarters (78.8%, n = 160) of the respondents reported that eggs were sold at the farm.

Internal biosecurity

Disease management, cleaning and disinfection varied across farms. Less than a quarter of all the study farms (24.6%, n = 50) had a poultry health management programme for regular farm visits by animal health service providers (Fig. 3). More than three-quarters of the farms (76.4%, n = 155) had chickens of different ages kept on the site and 22 farms (10.8%) kept chicken of different ages in the same house.Fig. 3Selected practices related to disease management among commercial layer farms

Although cleaning and disinfection of chicken houses after production cycle was reported to be practiced by the majority, still there was a fraction of respondents who reported that they did not clean (n = 14) or never disinfected (n = 23). Thirty-two respondents (15.8%) reported that they only did a dry cleaning of the houses after each production cycle. More than half of the respondents reported that the equipment used for egg collection were not regularly cleaned and disinfected.

Egg management and materials management between compartments revealed varied practices among farms. Most of the farms (95.6%, n = 194) had no written cleaning guide and 58.6% (n = 119) did not have any personal protective equipment (PPE) available at the site. About a quarter (24.6%, n = 50) of the respondents reported to sell eggs un-cleaned, while half of the respondents (53.2%, n = 108) reported to apply dry methods to clean them. A substantial number of respondents (44.3%, n = 90) reported that farm workers never take any preventive measures when moving between the egg collection area, sorting/packaging room and poultry houses.

Variation on biosecurity scores among sociodemographic and farm characteristics

Internal and external biosecurity scores

A t-test analysis showed that farmers with more than two years of experience in keeping layers had higher internal biosecurity scores than those with experience of two years or less (t = −3.184, p =.002).

An ANOVA test (Table 3) revealed that the mean internal biosecurity scores differed significantly across the three scales of commercial layer production, F(2,202) = 6.10, p =.003 while the mean external biosecurity varied significantly among farmers with different levels of education, F(2,202) = 4.396, p =.014. Post hoc analyses (Table 4) indicated that the average external biosecurity scores was significantly lower in farms supervised by farmers with primary education than both those under farmers with secondary education (p =.042) and tertiary education (p =.019). On the other hand, large-scale farms had significantly higher internal biosecurity scores than both small-scale farms (p =.002) and medium-scale farms (p =.005).

Scores by subcategories

M-W U tests revealed distinct patterns in biosecurity implementation between farmers with different experience levels on commercial layer chicken production (Supplementary material S4). Farmers with more than two years of experience showed significantly higher implementation scores for visitor and worker related measures (U = 3046.5, p =.038), cleaning and disinfection procedures (U = 2425.5, p <.001), egg transport practices (U = 3048.0, p =.039), and egg management systems (U = 3027.5, p =.036) compared to less experienced farmers. On the contrary, farmers with experience of more than two years had significantly low score on measures related to farm location (U = 3071.0, p =.028), and purchasing of laying hens (U = 3258.5, p =.007.) than those who are less experienced.

A K-W test (Supplementary material S4) showed that farmer’s education significantly affected the implementation of biosecurity measures related to visitors and farm workers (H = 7.373, p =.025), feed and water (H = 7.506, p =.023) and disease management (H = 6.454, p =.040). Post hoc indicated that farmers with primary education had significantly lower scores on measures related to visitors and workers (p =.040) and those related to feed and water (p =.026) than those with tertiary education.

Further, K-W indicated that farm size significantly affected the implementation of biosecurity measures related with purchasing of laying hens (H = 9.757, p =.008), cleaning and disinfection (H = 8.795, p =.012), material supply (H = 6.373, p =.041) and egg management (H = 8.563, p =.014. Post hoc test indicated that large-scale farmers had significantly lower scores on purchasing layers (p =.006) than small scale farms but had higher scores on cleaning and disinfection than both small-scale (p =.020) and medium-scale (p =.011) farms. In addition, large-scale farms showed significantly higher scores on egg management biosecurity measures than medium-scale (p =.012) and small-scale (p =.026) farms.

Factors associated with implementation of overall biosecurity measures in layer farms

A multiple linear regression analysis showed that the overall fit of the model was statistically significant, F (7,195) = 2.279, p =.030), suggesting that a significant portion of the variance in the overall biosecurity score was explained by the model. Furthermore, the results showed that the five variables together explained 7.6% of the variance in biosecurity practices (R2 = 0.076). Examining at the individual contribution of the predictor variables, results revealed that farms attended by persons with secondary (β = 0.224, t = 2.722, p =.007) and tertiary (β = 0.209, t = 2.471, p =.014) level of education were more likely to implement the biosecurity measures than those with primary level of education (Table 5).

Discussion

The present study aimed at assessment of biosecurity status on commercial layer chicken farms in Tanzania. The study provides a first overview of biosecurity practices among commercial layer farms in Tanzania, exploring both the measures and the factors that influence their implementation.

The study results revealed lower implementation of biosecurity practices (58.2%) in commercial layer farms than the global average [36] of 72%. The low levels of biosecurity implementation implies a higher risk of disease introduction and spread within the farms, potentially leading to reduced productivity, increased costs, high use of antimicrobials eventually contributing to the development of antimicrobial resistance [37]. The results were in line with the overall biosecurity scores of 58% in Ethiopia [36] but higher than the overall biosecurity scores of 40% in Kenya [36]. In an investigations conducted in commercial broiler chickens in Pwani Tanzania, similar scores were obtained with an overall mean score of 53.83 ± 4.23 [25]. A recent investigation in broiler chickens in Uganda also provided slightly similar scores where the highest score among farms was 52% [38]. Similar findings have also been reported in commercial chicken layer farms in other developing countries including Ethiopia, Kenya, Cameroon, Philippines and Bangladesh [35, 39–43]. This suggests that efforts are needed to improve biosecurity on the poultry farms in developing countries to control poultry diseases, particularly because relying solely on disease treatment is often ineffective and economically unsustainable.

Biosecurity compliance is often lower in most of the low-resource countries due to a complex interplay of factors, including limited resources, weaker regulatory frameworks, and socio-cultural practices [44, 45]. Additionally, several tools for assessing biosecurity are based on practices in high income countries and large scale settings and therefore these tools may not be appropriate to accurately assess biosecurity across low-resource settings and in settings with smaller production scales.

In the present study external biosecurity score was higher than internal biosecurity score, implying that farmers pay more attention on the implementation of measures to prevent infectious diseases from entering their farms than those that prevent the spread of diseases within the farms. This suggests that while farmers may be more focused on controlling the initial entry of diseases from the outside, they might have less robust strategies for managing disease within their own farms. Effective biosecurity requires a comprehensive and balanced approach that addresses both external and internal risks [46]. Focusing on a single aspect of biosecurity can create vulnerabilities particularly in LMIC countries like Tanzania where endemic diseases and a complex human-animal-environment interconnectedness require an integrated biosecurity approach [47]. It is therefore important to institute targeted interventions aimed at improving internal biosecurity practices on these farms such as trainings on practices related to equipment and egg management.

Results show that larger farms were associated with better implementation of biosecurity measures related to egg management, cleaning and disinfection subcategories. The influence of scale of production on biosecurity has been reported elsewhere including Kenya, Ethiopia and United Arab Emirates [42, 48, 49]. Among the suggested reasons for relatively better performance of larger farms include the ability of large-scale farmers to invest in better infrastructure, employ more trained personnel and access to training programmes [50–52]. Therefore, policies aimed at improving biosecurity measures in poultry farming should also encourage and build capacity for intensification of scale of production among small-scale commercial layer farmers as a way to facilitate the implementation of biosecurity practices.

Regarding external biosecurity, farm location had the lowest score, implying that most of the studied farms were near other poultry farms. This proximity raises concerns about potential risk of disease transmission due to shared environmental factors and vectors such as wind, rodents, birds, people and means of transport [53]. In the context of this study, a statistically significant association was found between commercial layer chicken farm location and the experience of the farmer whereby farmers with more years of experience in commercial layer production had significantly lower score on location. The cause of this association is not currently known. It is more likely that, newer farmers are better able to site their farms a bit away from other poultry farms or homes, as against experienced farmers who may just utilize additional household spaces due to a combination of factors, including, preference to use existing infrastructure and proximity to markets [54]. A similar situations have been observed on commercial layer farms in other developing countries [35, 43]. This in part could be addressed by incorporating guidance on the minimum recommended distance between farms [9] in the respective animal disease control regulation.

The influence of experience on biosecurity practices in commercial layer farms has been previously reported both in the developed and developing countries [40, 42, 50] indicating that experience plays a significant role in the prevention of diseases. This suggests that practical knowledge gained over time is crucial for successful biosecurity. On the other hand, this translates to economic constraint as new commercial layer farmers usually face challenges like limited capital, access to credit and high initial costs making it difficult to invest in biosecurity measures [55, 56]. Financial enhancements for these farmers, such as improved access to loans, subsidies for disease prevention tools and training could significantly improve biosecurity levels and ensure long-term sustainability of this subsector. In agreement with the above findings, this study found farmers’ experience plays a positive role in the implementation of biosecurity measures, such as those related to visitors, farm workers, cleaning, disinfection, transport of eggs and egg management whereby more experienced farmers exhibited better practices.

Regression results in the present study has indicated that higher level of formal education among farmers, particularly secondary and tertiary education, is positively associated with better biosecurity practices. Increased education among farmers leads to several improvements, including greater access to information and a higher capacity to adapt to new technologies and practices [57, 58]. The findings are in line with previous studies that indicate a positive correlation between farmers’ education level and the implementation of biosecurity measures in commercial layer farms. For example, a study in Bangladesh demonstrated that commercial layer chicken farmers with more education were more likely to adopt comprehensive biosecurity measures [40]. It is therefore necessary for governments to revise their policy and prioritize formal education, which can have an additional benefit for farming communities to improve biosecurity in the commercial layer chicken farms.

Study limitations

Although the study was conducted in three regions where commercial poultry production was relatively well established compared to other parts of Tanzania, the findings might not capture the full range of biosecurity practices and challenges across the country. Further, the study design did not allow for the determination of the temporal order of events, which limited the ability to establish cause-and-effect relationships between influencing factors and biosecurity practices. The reliance on self-reported data might also introduce social desirability bias, potentially leading to overestimation of biosecurity compliance. Additionally, the weighting system used to calculate biosecurity scores, while based on expert consultation, might not fully reflect the relative importance of different practices in preventing disease transmission under local conditions. It should be noted however that direct comparison is limited given that this study adapted the questionnaire for the local context, and adjusted the scoring based on the revised questionnaire. Moreover, the low R² in the model suggests significant unexplained variability which might be due to unmeasured factors like access to veterinary services, information sources or behavioral norms. Despite these limitations, the findings provide a valuable baseline for informing future interventions and policy development regarding biosecurity practices in Tanzanian poultry systems or similar contexts.

Conclusion

Our study highlights a need for improvement in biosecurity practices on most commercial layer chicken farms. While some farms may have good practices, many have room to enhance their overall biosecurity measures to prevent and control diseases, ultimately improving the overall health and productivity of layer chicken. Overall, results show that although is more in line with other African settings, Tanzania has a lower biosecurity score compared to the global average. The results also indicate that education plays a significant influence on the overall implementation of biosecurity measures. Specific biosecurity measures like farm location, control of visitors, farm workers, cleaning, disinfection, transport of eggs and egg management are also associated with the experience of the farmers. Further, farming scale is an important factor in implementing biosecurity measures. The findings highlight the need for interventions and policy initiatives to prioritize on promoting biosecurity practices that are tailored to the farmer’s demographic and farm characteristics.
