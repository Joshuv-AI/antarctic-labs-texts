Incidence of Carpal Tunnel Syndrome After the Diagnosis of Ulnar Neuropathy
Authors: Harrison S Fellheimer, Eric DiDomenico, Mahaa Ayub, Magnolia Livingston, William L Justice, Gabriel I Onor, Eric Tecce, Pedro K Beredjiklian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of this study is to determine whether patients diagnosed with ulnar nerve lesions (UNLs) have an increased risk of developing carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using the TriNetX database, which contains data of >100 million patients. We identified 360,021 patients with UNLs and matched them 1:1 with controls. The incidence of CTS was assessed at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 years following the diagnosis of UNL. Statistical analyses were used to evaluate the development of CTS in the UNL cohort compared with controls.ResultsPatients with UNL demonstrated a significantly higher risk of developing CTS at all time points. At 1 year post-UNL diagnosis, the risk of CTS was 10.4% in the UNL group versus 3.1% in controls (P < .0001). This difference persisted throughout the 5-year period. Odds ratios similarly indicated a significantly greater likelihood of CTS development in patients with UNL.ConclusionsPatients with a history of UNLs are at a significantly increased risk of developing CTS, especially within the first 2 years. This supports the hypothesis that ulnar nerve pathology may precede and increase susceptibility to median nerve compression.Type of study/level of evidencePrognostic III.Key words: Carpal tunnel syndrome, Incidence, Neuropathy progression, Peripheral nerve, Ulnar neuropathy

The most prevalent compressive neuropathy is carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), which occurs when the median nerve is entrapped within the carpal tunnel.1 Common risk factors for CTS include pregnancy, diabetes, hypertension, and various inflammatory conditions. Both median and ulnar nerve compressive neuropathies are well described. However, the relationship between these conditions within the same patient remains poorly characterized. Population-based studies suggest that individuals with shared risk factors may be predisposed to multiple peripheral nerve compressions, highlighting the importance of understanding these associations to better counsel patients on future nerve health. Ulnar neuropathy represents a continuum of pathology due to compression, trauma, or degeneration of the ulnar nerve, typically at the elbow (cubital tunnel) or wrist (Guyon canal).2 Although cubital tunnel syndrome is a useful UN subgroup, clinical diagnosis of UN includes a broader spectrum of etiologies than entrapment. Previous studies have identified shared risk factors for ulnar neuropathy and carpal tunnel syndrome; however, whether the diagnosis of one condition is associated with the subsequent development of the other has not been well established.3,4 Of special interest, there have been few studies about whether the diagnosis of UN progresses into future CTS.

The primary aim of this study is to assess the incidence of CTS development following a diagnosis of an ulnar nerve lesion (UNL), thereby investigating whether such injuries are associated with an increased risk of CTS.

Understanding whether ulnar nerve pathology is associated with the subsequent development of CTS has important clinical implications. If patients with UNLs are at increased risk for CTS, this information may improve patient counseling, guide longitudinal surveillance, and inform clinical decision-making regarding symptom evaluation and follow-up. Clarifying this relationship may also enhance understanding of the broader patient phenotype prone to peripheral nerve compression, beyond isolated, single-site neuropathy. Therefore, this study seeks to evaluate the incidence of CTS following a diagnosis of UNL using a large population-based database.

Materials and Methods

This study used the United States Collaborative Network database, which contains the data for over 100 million patients within the TriNetX database. TriNetX is a global research database that uses nearly 170 health-care organizations and >400 million patients, with various subsets for different countries and networks. TriNetX is a deidentified database organized by patient diagnosis, procedures, genomics, demographic data, laboratory, and medication data. Health-care organizations send their data to TriNetX and undergo an authorized deidentification process authorized by the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act.

Using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Edition (ICD-10) diagnostic code for lesion of the ulnar nerve (G56.2), we identified a cohort of 360,021 patients diagnosed with ulnar nerve pathologies, including, but not limited to, trauma, compressive, rheumatologic, and degenerative. Following this identification, we queried the same patient cohort for subsequent diagnoses of CTS by applying the primary ICD-10 code for CTS (G56.0). The principal aim of this study was to determine the incidence of CTS (diagnostic code G56.0) occurring after an initial diagnosis of ulnar nerve lesion (UNL). To achieve this objective, we evaluated the development of CTS at several time points following the diagnosis of UNL, specifically at 1-year, 2-year, 3-year, 4-year, and 5-year intervals. This longitudinal assessment allowed us to examine the progression and rate of CTS development among patients with a history of UNL, thereby elucidating the potential relationship between ulnar nerve lesions and subsequent CTS diagnosis.

Patients with a prior history of UNL were matched 1:1 to control patients, identified by the ICD-10 code Z00.00, or “Encounter for general adult medical examination without abnormal findings.” Matching was performed based on age, race, and gender using greedy nearest neighbor matching (the built-in propensity scoring used by TriNetX). Once balanced based on propensity scoring, the patient population was 360,021. The study design is shown in Figure 1. All statistical analyses were performed through the TriNetX platform, and P values were used to determine statistical significance between cohorts, whereas Z scores were used to compare whether there was a risk difference among cohorts. Any P value less than .05 was considered significant, and odds ratios (ORs) were calculated at 95% confidence intervals.

Table 2 displays the risk of progression to CTS following UNL diagnosis, and Table 3 shows the risk difference by year with Z score, P value, risk ratio, and OR with 95% confidence interval. The risk of developing CTS in patients with an UNL between 1 and 365 days of the lesion index was 10.353% compared with the index population of 3.131%. The risk was significantly greater in patients with an UNL (relative risk [RR] of 3.306 (3.225, 3.390) and OR of 3.573 (3.480, 3.668) (P < .001)). Two years following the diagnosis of UNL, patients had a risk of 11.861%. whereas the control was 4.234%. This difference was also statistically significant (7.627% with a P < .001) with an RR of 2.801 (2.741, 2.863) and an OR of 3.044 (2.973, 3.116). At 3 years, the CTS risk in UNL patients was 12.822% compared with control patients who had 4.986%. This difference was statistically significant, but the RR and OR decreased. Similar findings were shown in the fourth and fifth years after diagnosis. Patients were notably excluded if they had a prior history of CTS, decreasing the cohort size from the original 360,021 patients (Table 2).

Discussion

In this large, population-based cohort study, patients diagnosed with an UNL demonstrated a substantially higher risk of subsequently developing CTS compared with matched controls. This increased risk was most pronounced within the first year following diagnosis but persisted throughout the 5-year follow-up period (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that ulnar nerve pathology may serve as a clinical marker for patients who are predisposed to additional peripheral nerve compression.5,6

Risk ratio of development of CTS over the 5-year interval following diagnosis of ulnar nerve pathology compared with control.

The observed association does not imply causation but supports the concept that certain patients may have a broader susceptibility to compressive neuropathies. Shared systemic risk factors, such as metabolic disease, obesity, or inflammatory conditions, as well as occupational or mechanical stressors, may contribute to this vulnerability. Additionally, increased clinical surveillance following diagnosis of 1 neuropathy may lead to earlier recognition of another, potentially contributing to the higher observed incidence of CTS in this population.

From a clinical perspective, these findings are most relevant for patient counseling and longitudinal care. Clinicians managing patients with UNLs should be aware of the increased likelihood of subsequent median nerve compression and maintain a low threshold for evaluating new or evolving symptoms consistent with CTS.7, 8, 9 Early recognition may allow for timely intervention and improved patient education regarding nerve health and symptom monitoring.

This study is limited by the use of administrative diagnostic codes, which preclude assessment of symptom severity, laterality, electrodiagnostic findings, and treatment details. Additionally, the nonspecific ICD-10 code for UNL does not allow differentiation between compressive and noncompressive etiologies. Despite these limitations, the large sample size and consistent findings across multiple time points strengthen the clinical relevance of the observed association.

In summary, patients with UNLs are at increased risk for subsequent diagnosis of CTS. These results contribute to the growing understanding that peripheral nerve compression may represent a patient-level predisposition rather than isolated pathology and highlight the importance of anticipatory guidance and clinical vigilance in this population.
