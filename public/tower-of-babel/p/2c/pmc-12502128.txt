Real-world cardiovascular effectiveness of sustained glucagon-like peptide 1 GLP-1 receptor agonist usage in type 2 diabetes
Authors: Kathrine Kold Sørensen, Puriya Daniel Würtz Yazdanfard, Bochra Zareini, Ulrik Pedersen-Bjergaard, Vanja Kosjerina, Mikkel Porsborg Andersen, Anders Munch, Johan Sebastian Ohlendorff, Stefanie Schmid, Stefanie Lanzinger, Pratik Choudhary, Clare Gillies, Safoora Gharibzadeh, Marcus Lind, Viktor Tasselius, Jens Michelsen, Thomas Alexander Gerds, Christian Torp-Pedersen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular outcome trials have shown that glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP1-RAs) reduce cardiovascular event rates more effectively than placebo and in patients with type 2 diabetes at increased cardiovascular risk. However, the generalizability of these findings to real-world settings remains uncertain.AimThis study aimed to evaluate the real-world cardiovascular effectiveness of sustained GLP1-RA use compared to dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitor (DPP-4i) over 3.5 years.MethodsUsing Danish nationwide registries, we emulated a target trial to assess the real-world effectiveness of GLP1-RAs in a population of individuals with type 2 diabetes mirroring the inclusion and exclusion criteria from the Liraglutide Effect and Action in Diabetes: Evaluation of Cardiovascular Outcome Results (LEADER) trial. The study period was 2012–2022. Outcomes included the composite of myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality (3P-MACE), as well as each component individually, alongside all-cause mortality, heart failure, angina pectoris, and revascularization. Longitudinal Targeted Minimum Loss-based Estimation, a method that adjusts for both baseline and time-varying confounding, was used to estimate absolute risks of cardiovascular outcomes under sustained use of GLP1-RA and DPP 4i (active comparator), adjusting for baseline and time-varying confounding.ResultsWe included 6,681 people initiating GLP1-RA and 19,072 initiating DPP-4i. Accounting for baseline and time-varying confounding, sustained GLP1-RA use showed a 2.5% (95% CI 0.8–4.1%) risk reduction of 3P-MACEover 3.5 years. Risk reductions for cardiovascular mortality, all-cause mortality, heart failure, and unstable angina pectoris were 2.3% (95% CI 1.4–3.1%), 2.5% (95% CI 0.7–4.3%), 0.9% (95% CI 0.01–1.8%), and 0.7% (95% CI 0.01–1.3%), respectively. No significant differences were observed for myocardial infarction, stroke, or revascularization with risk differences of 0.1% (95% CI −1.0 to 0.8%), 0.8% (95% CI −0.2 to 1.7%), and 0.2% (95% CI -0.7–1.1%), respectively.ConclusionsThis real-world study confirms the cardiovascular benefits of GLP1-RAs over DPP-4is, particularly for reducing cardiovascular and all-cause mortality under continuous treatment exposure in patients with type 2 diabetes at increased cardiovascular risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-02915-1.

Introduction

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP1-RAs) have transformed the management of type 2 diabetes, demonstrating cardiovascular benefits in landmark cardiovascular outcome trials (CVOTs) [1]. These trials have highlighted the potential of GLP1-RAs to reduce three-point major adverse cardiovascular event rates (3P-MACE) and, as such, shifted clinical practice. A network meta-analysis of the trial results has confirmed the efficacy of GLP1-RAs, showing a significant reduction in the risk of 3P-MACE compared to placebo (risk ratio: 0.89, 95% CI 0.84–0.94) [2]. Despite these findings, real-world studies are essential to address the generalizability to everyday clinical settings [3].

However, evaluating the cardiovascular effectiveness of GLP1-RAs in real-world settings presents challenges. One challenge is the absence of a placebo arm that an active comparator arm must replace. Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4is), which have non-significant cardiovascular effects and indications similar to GLP1-RAs [4], are a relevant choice for an active comparator. Since their introduction in 2006 (GLP1-RA) and 2007 (DPP-4i) [5], these pharmacotherapies have been widely adopted as add-on treatments for glucose lowering. Systematic reviews of previous observational studies have suggested cardiovascular benefits of GLP1-RAs over DPP-4is [6]. However, the evidence relies on hazard ratios, which may not reflect the parameter of greatest clinical interest because it does not directly quantify the likelihood of a cardiovascular outcome for a given patient [7]. Recent advancements in applied statistical methods enable more clinically relevant estimates of treatment effects formulated on the absolute risk scale. Longitudinal Targeted Minimum Loss-based Estimation (LTMLE), with its double-robustness property and ability to account for time-varying confounding, offers a framework for estimating sustained treatment effects [8].

This work was conducted within the REDDIE consortium, an EU-funded initiative to assess real-world evidence [9]. As part of a broader series of analyses across four countries, this study specifically utilized Danish registry data to evaluate the real-world cardiovascular effectiveness of continuous GLP1-RA use compared to DPP-4i over a 3.5-year follow-up period in patients with type 2 diabetes at increased cardiovascular risk. To enhance comparability, the study emulated a target trial using Denmark’s nationwide registers and applied inclusion and exclusion criteria mirroring those of the Liraglutide Effect and Action in Diabetes: Evaluation of Cardiovascular Outcome Results (LEADER) trial [10].

Methods

Setting and data sources

In Denmark, all citizens have equal access to healthcare services, including both primary and hospital care. Upon birth or immigration, Danish residents are assigned a unique personal identification number, enabling linkage of the national registers [11]. For this study, we linked data from multiple national registers to extract relevant information. Medication use, including dispensing dates, indication, and anatomical therapeutic chemical (ATC) classification codes, was obtained from the Danish National Prescription Registry [12]. Information on outpatient visits and hospital admissions, including diagnoses coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10), was retrieved from the Danish National Patient Registry [13]. Demographic data such as date of birth, sex, immigration/emigration status, and vital status were sourced from the Danish Civil Registration System [14]. Socioeconomic factors were assessed using data from the Income Statistics Registry, which provided information on equivalized annual income [15], and the Population Education Register, which details the highest attained education, categorized according to the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) [16, 17]. Biomarker measurements, including the sampling date, were obtained from the Clinical Laboratory Information System Research Database [11]. Further, HbA1c measurements for individuals with diabetes were extracted from the Danish Adult Diabetes Registry, which compiles data from both primary and secondary healthcare settings [18]. Information on causes of death, coded according to ICD-10, was retrieved from the Cause of Death Register [19].

Study design and population

In developing the study, we applied the target-trial framework, explicitly defining the hypothetical trial in a population inspired by the LEADER trial [20], with the analysis plan published before conducting the analyses [21]. Any deviations from the original analysis plan are listed in the supplementary material. Using data from the Danish Nationwide Registers, we identified all new first-time users of either GLP1-RA or DPP-4i between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2022. Eligibility was assessed based on participant characteristics at treatment initiation (baseline). The drug classes of interest included both single-agent and combination glucose-lowering therapies, excluding combinations of GLP1-RA and DPP-4i, as a hypothetical trial including patients treated with both would not be feasible.

Identification of a LEADER-inspired population

To define the study population, we applied inclusion and exclusion criteria designed to mirror those of the LEADER trial [10], carefully translating the trial criteria to an observational context. Applying these criteria entailed that we excluded individuals with a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes within the 10 years preceding the index date and those who had collected a prescription for insulin at any time before or Sodium-glucose Cotransporter-2 Inhibitor (SGLT2-i) within the six months before initiation. Additionally, participants with type 2 diabetes were required to have an HbA1c level above 53 mmol/mol at the latest measurement before the index, measured within the past year. Further criteria specified inclusion of (1) individuals aged 60 or older with hypertension or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and no prior cardiovascular disease (CVD) or (2) individuals aged 50 or older with a history of cardiovascular or renal disease within the past 10 years. A more detailed breakdown of the criteria used to define the study population of the LEADER trial, along with our translation and specification, is available in the supplementary material (Table S1).

Estimand of interest and outcomes

The primary estimand of interest was the 3.5-year risk of the composite outcome of myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular mortality (3P-MACE) under two treatment regimens: Sustained use of GLP1-RA but no DPP-4i (GLP1-RA regimen) versus sustained use of DPP-4i but no GLP1-RA (DPP-4i regimen). The 3.5-year mark was chosen inspired by the planned trial length of LEADER. In addition, we examined risks at six-month intervals over a follow-up period ranging from 0.5 to 4.5 years, which was the maximum follow-up reported in LEADER. Secondary outcomes included the individual components of the composite endpoint, revascularization, heart failure or angina pectoris requiring hospitalization, and all-cause mortality. Outcomes were identified by the first post-baseline hospital contact with ICD-10 codes specified in Table S2. Cardiovascular mortality was determined through the Cause of Death Register if any cause of death was attributed to diseases of the circulatory system, recorded as the underlying, immediate, subsequent, fundamental, or supplementary cause.

Variable definition

Follow-up time was divided into six-month (183-day) intervals from baseline, defined as the date of first prescription redemption of either GLP1-RA or DPP-4i within the study period. In each interval, we assessed exposure to GLP1-RA and DPP-4i, concurrent medications, and history of comorbidities until censoring (end of follow-up or emigration), outcome, or non-cardiovascular mortality occurred. The ICD-10 and ATC codes used to identify morbidities and medication types are available in Table S2.

Treatment of interest

Patients were classified as exposed to GLP1-RAs and DPP-4s regimens in a given six-month interval if they redeemed at least one prescription during that interval.

Baseline information

Baseline covariates were sex, age, education (basic, general upper secondary, bachelor level, and masters or PhD level), income level (grouped by quartiles of the study population income, with the 4th quartile representing the highest income group), degree of urbanization (rural, suburban, urban) according to the definition by Eurostat [22], duration of diabetes (less than one year, 1–5 years, 5–10 years, or more than 10 years) and the most recent HbA1c level (mmol/mol) measured within one year before baseline. Co-medication use was defined based on prescriptions redeemed within the six months preceding baseline, including additional glucose-lowering treatments beyond GLP1-RA or DPP-4; metformin and sulfonylureas (prior use of SGLT2-i and insulin were exclusion criteria). Further, other co-medicines were mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, beta-blockers, renin-angiotensin-system acting agents, loop diuretics, calcium channel blockers, thiazide diuretics, acetylsalicylic acid, and statins. Comorbidities were identified as any diagnosis of relevant conditions as defined in Table S2 recorded during the 10 years before baseline.

Post-baseline information

Post-baseline variables were co-medicine and comorbidities. Co-medicine included SGLT2-i, insulin, and metformin. Diagnoses of the chronic comorbidities (ischemic heart disease, peripheral vascular disease, renal disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, stroke, angina pectoris, and heart failure) were considered present in an interval if the hospital admission with the corresponding ICD-10 diagnosis occurred before the end of the interval but not longer than 10 years before baseline.

Statistical methods

Baseline characteristics were summarized by frequency at treatment initiation based on the initial prescription drug, defining inclusion. The number of patients initiating treatment each calendar year was tabulated and visualized. Patients were followed from baseline until outcome, death, emigration, 4.5 years, or end of study (December 31, 2022), whichever came first. The Aalen-Johansen estimator was used to estimate the likelihood of first treatment breaks, defined as more than 183 days without a new prescription, accounting for censoring and the competing risk of all-cause mortality. The same estimator was applied to calculate the cumulative incidence of first add-on treatment with insulin or SGLT2i. Investigating the adherence over six-month intervals was based on the redemption of any GLP1-RA or DPP-4i prescription within each interval, stratified by inclusion group, among participants still included in that interval, excluding cases of censoring or all-cause mortality. The analyses of the primary and secondary estimands were conducted using Longitudinal Targeted Minimum Loss-based Estimation (LTMLE) [8]. We evaluated all post-baseline variables, treatment, and outcomes every six months since baseline. At each of the six-month-long time intervals, LTMLE was fitted using regression models for treatment propensity, the likelihood of being uncensored, and the outcome probability (sequential regression). All regression analyses were adjusted for additive effects of all covariates defined by the current history up to the previous interval. All regression models were estimated by penalized regression in which cross-validation was used to tune hyperparameters [23]. The cheap subsampling bootstrap was used to derive 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) based on 25 bootstrap samples drawn without replacement from the full sample [24]. We did not truncate propensity scores and censoring probabilities [25]. We considered p-values below 5% as statistically significant. All data management and analyses were conducted with R [26].

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The primary outcome analysis was repeated across several subgroups inspired by the LEADER trial, defined by baseline characteristics: Sex (male/female), age (under 60/60 years or older), diabetes duration (11 years or less/more than 11 years), cardiovascular risk factors (presence of cardiovascular disease and age under 60/absence of cardiovascular disease and age 60 or older), prevalent heart failure status (yes/no), use of diabetes medication (one glucose-lowering co-medication/no co-medication/more than one glucose-lowering co-medication), HbA1c levels (< 64 mmol/mol/≥ 64 mmol/mol), ischemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, stroke, and COPD. We included a sensitivity analysis where the baseline was moved 30 days from initial prescription redemption so that outcomes in the first 30 days were excluded to assess the potential risk of confounding by indication, specifically, the possibility that patients in poorer health may have been preferentially prescribed the newer drug class, GLP-1 RAs.

Results

A total of 171,933 individuals initiating either GLP1-RA or DPP-4i were identified during the study period. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final study population comprised 25,753 individuals, with 6,681 initiating GLP1-RA and 19,072 initiating DPP-4i. A flowchart is available in Figure S1. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Compared to those started on DPP-4i, those started on GLP1-RA were younger (median age 64 years vs. 68 years), belonged more frequently to the highest income group (33% vs. 22%), and were more commonly from rural areas (44% vs. 37%). Additionally, a greater proportion of GLP1-RA starters had a baseline HbA1c measurement exceeding 69 mmol/mol (35% vs. 30%). GLP1-RA starters had marginally lower proportions of all cardiovascular risk factors at baseline than DPP-4i initiators. Co-medication use was similar for GLP1-RA and DPP-4i starters at baseline (Table 1). Of those starting GLP1-RA, 62% of patients at baseline started treatment with semaglutide, 36% liraglutide, 1% dulaglutide, and the remaining 1% started either lixisenatide or exenatide. The number of DPP-4i starters increased from 2012 to 2016 before declining over the study period, whereas the number of GLP1-RA starters saw a slight decline from 2012 to 2014, followed by a more rapid increase (Figure S2).

Baseline characteristics

Primary outcome

The sustained use of GLP1-RA compared to DPP-4i was associated with a 2.5% (95% CI 0.9–4.1%) absolute risk reduction in 3P-MACE over 3.5 years. The observed risk was 8.8% (95% CI 7.2–10.5%) for the GLP1-RA regimen and 11.3% (95% CI 10.7–11.9%) for the DPP-4i regimen, translating to a risk ratio of 0.78 (95% CI 0.66 to 0.93). Notably, this reduced risk within the GLP1-RA regimen became progressively more evident with each six-month interval from initiation, starting at six months. However, the uncertainty, as reflected in the confidence intervals, increased at later time points (Fig. 1). The 3.5-year risk of 3P-MACE with GLP-1 RA compared to DPP-4i corresponds to a number needed to treat of 41.

Secondary outcomes

At 3.5 years, the GLP1-RA regimen compared to the DPP-4i regimen showed statistically significantly lowered risks for cardiovascular mortality, all-cause mortality, heart failure, and unstable angina pectoris with risk differences of 2.3% (95% CI 1.4–3.1%), 2.5% (95% CI 0.7–4.3%), 0.9% (95% CI 0.01–1.8%), and 0.7% (95% CI 0.01–1.3%), respectively. No significant risk differences were observed for myocardial infarction, stroke, or revascularization with risk differences of 0.1% (95% CI −1.0 to 0.8%), 0.8% (95% CI −0.2 to 1.7%), and 0.2% (95% CI −0.7 to 1.1%), respectively. Absolute risks at time points from 6 to 54 months are presented for all outcomes in Fig. 1, except for angina pectoris and revascularization.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses for the primary composite outcome of 3P MACE at 3.5 years demonstrated that the GLP1-RA regimen had consistent risk reductions across all subgroups. However, the statistical significance of these reductions diminished in certain subgroups, including females, individuals aged < 60 years, those with a diabetes duration above 11 years, an HbA1c equal to above 64 mmol/mol, individuals > 60 years with a high risk of cardiovascular disease, those without heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and stroke, those with COPD, as well as all categories of baseline glucose-lowering therapy (Fig. 2). Notably, the estimated risk reduction for individuals with heart failure was particularly pronounced, at 15.4% (95% CI 9.0–21.7%). Shifting the baseline 30 days forward from the initial prescription only slightly affected the observed 3.5-year 3P-MACE risk, which was 8.8% (95% CI 6.7–10.8%) for the GLP-1 RA regimen and 11.3% (95% CI 10.8–11.8%) for the DPP-4i regimen. This corresponded to a reduced risk of 2.5% (95% CI 0.3–4.7%).

Adherence and post-baseline treatment

An analysis of the cumulative incidence of a gap exceeding 183 days between prescription redemptions of either GLP1-RA or DPP-4i, based on the initial redemption qualifying for inclusion, revealed that at one year, 18% (95% CI 17–19%) of GLP1-RA initiators and 23% (95% CI 23–24%) of DPP-4i initiators experienced such a gap. By three years, these proportions had increased to 34% (95% CI 33–36%) and 52% (95% CI 50–52%), respectively. Further, the proportion of individuals classified as exposed to their initial treatment at each six-month interval, out of those still alive and not censored, was examined. At 6 months, 91% of the GLP1-RA group and 89% of the DPP-4i group remained exposed to their respective initial treatments. This proportion declined over time in both groups, reaching 76% for GLP1-RA and 61% for DPP-4i at the three-year mark (Figure S3). The cumulative risk of collecting an SGLT2-i prescription was similar for GLP1-RA and DPP-4i users (Figure S4, panel A), at 18% for both groups at 2 years. In contrast, the cumulative risk of collecting an insulin prescription was slightly higher for DPP-4i users than for GLP1-RA users (15% [95% CI 15–16%] vs. 13% [95% CI 12–14%]; Figure S4, panel B).

Discussion

This study focused on a clinically relevant estimand of sustained use of GLP1-RA versus DPP-4i, providing estimates of absolute risks. The findings showed cardiovascular benefits with GLP1-RA use compared to DPP-4i under continuous treatment in a real-world setting of patients with type 2 diabetes at increased cardiovascular risk. Subgroup analyses indicated that older patients and those with heart failure may experience greater benefits. However, the absolute risk differences were modest, and the relatively high cost of GLP-1 RAs compared to DPP-4 inhibitors should be considered, particularly in light of a number needed to treat of, for example, 41 for 3P-MACE.

The LEADER trial reported raw proportions that did not account for competing risks or censoring, alongside hazard ratios from an intention-to-treat analysis [10] with limited clinical interpretability [7, 27]. Comparing our standardized absolute risks of sustained use to the trial’s raw proportions using an intention-to-treat approach showed similar differences but smaller absolute magnitudes. For example, 13.0% vs.14.9% of patients in the liraglutide and placebo arms experienced the primary composite outcome over a median follow-up time of 3.6 years [10], compared to our estimates of 9.0% vs.11.4% for GLP1-RA versus DPP-4i. These differences may reflect a slightly healthier population in our study, perhaps owing to the interpretation of inclusion criteria, e.g., defining cardiovascular risk as hypertension, which can be debated. When comparing our findings to the findings of the LEADER trial, another key distinction is that we included all GLP-1 RAs rather than focusing solely on liraglutide. This pooling of GLP-1 RAs warrants consideration, as differences in their effects have been observed. Notably, semaglutide has shown greater benefits in reducing stroke risk, although all GLP-1 RAs have demonstrated cardiovascular benefits compared to placebo [28]. Furthermore, since a placebo comparator was not feasible, we used DPP-4 inhibitors as a reference due to their established cardiovascular neutrality [4]. While other comparators, such as SGLT2 inhibitors, may also be of interest and warrant consideration in future studies, careful attention to the exchangeability of the respective patient populations would be essential. While GLP1-RA trials have primarily utilized placebo-controlled designs, comparative efficacy has also been assessed indirectly. A network meta-analysis of several GLP-1 RA trials, using raw proportions, estimated the risk ratio (RR) for GLP-1 RA versus DPP-4i at 0.89 (95% CI 0.82–0.98) [2], slightly attenuated compared to our RR of 0.80.

Several studies have utilized real-world data to examine the association between GLP1-RAs and cardiovascular outcomes [29–32]. However, as these studies relied on Cox proportional hazard regression models that produce hazard ratios, they do not provide estimates of absolute risk for patients or account for the effects of continuous treatment. Xie and colleagues also employed a target trial framework to evaluate cardiovascular outcomes, aiming to estimate the continuous treatment effect through a per-protocol analysis. Briefly, their method involved the inverse probability of treatment adherence weighting, where participants who remained in the cohort and adhered to the assigned medication until a specific time point were classified as adherent at that time. Using this approach, they reported an absolute risk reduction of 5.33 [2.80–7.90] per 1000 person-years for GLP1-RA compared to DPP-4i. However, while their analysis modeled sustained use, it lacked the double-robust properties offered by LTMLE. Furthermore, the study was conducted in a cohort predominantly comprising US veterans, which may limit the generalizability of their findings to more diverse populations [33].

Strengths of this study include the inclusion of a less selected population compared to a trial setting, where participants must actively opt in. The observational design also enabled complete follow-up and allowed for accounting for time-varying confounding and adherence. This analysis targeted a clinically relevant estimand of sustained use, estimated using an algorithm robust to misspecification [8]. However, the validity of our causal interpretation depends on key assumptions, including exchangeability, positivity, and consistency [34]. In our analysis, we relied on penalized regression models, which offer interpretability and manage multicollinearity, but the absence of a broader super learner ensemble may limit model robustness. A key limitation of using observational data is the potential for residual confounding. Specifically, we lacked data on physician preferences, body mass index, smoking, eGFR, and other unmeasured factors that could affect exchangeability and positivity. Also, the guidelines for treatment have evolved during the study period [35]. GLP1-RA initiators may represent a more affluent group, as this therapy is more expensive than DPP-4i. However, because medications in Denmark are government-subsidized, the maximum annual patient copayment is approximately 595 euros [36], which may mitigate the impact of cost on treatment selection. Additionally, in longitudinal analyses, exchangeability must hold at each post-baseline point. While we included extensive data on comorbidities and concomitant medications, information on reasons for treatment discontinuation, such as gastrointestinal adverse effects, was not available. These gaps necessitate caution in interpreting the estimates causally. The choice of sustained use as an estimand warrants consideration, as it may not always reflect real-world treatment patterns. Our findings highlight substantial real-world non-adherence, e.g., 34% of GLP-1 RA users had discontinued treatment within the first three years after treatment start. While this estimand allows for a clear interpretation under certain assumptions, it may overestimate the effectiveness that could be expected in practice. For example, overestimation would occur if there were medical contraindications for continuing treatment. Although we used prescription redemption as a proxy for adherence, we lacked granular data to model it more explicitly. However, the register data do not contain information about medical contraindication specifically or reasons for non-adherence more generally, and hence they are not suitable to estimate estimands such as the effect of sustained use unless medically contraindicated, even though that might be interesting from a public-health perspective [37]. Another limitation of our analysis is how we have defined the use of a treatment in a given 6-month-long interval. We have defined a person to be a user if the person has redeemed at least one prescription within the interval. A refined definition could include information on the date of redemption, the number of packages, the size of the package, and the strength of the prescribed treatment or leverage newer methods to mitigate such constraints [38]. Lastly, attention to the generalization of these findings should also be considered, since the Danish healthcare system is unique in terms of healthcare access and reimbursement rules. These factors should be kept in mind when generalizing to other countries. Importantly, analyzing the same estimand in other settings would be of interest.

Conclusion

This study highlights the cardiovascular benefits of sustained GLP1-RA use over DPP-4i in individuals with type 2 diabetes at increased cardiovascular risk, emphasizing its potential for reducing absolute cardiovascular risks in real-world clinical practice. Importantly, subgroup analyses indicate that certain populations, including older adults and those with heart failure, may derive even greater benefits, underscoring the need for personalized treatment strategies.
