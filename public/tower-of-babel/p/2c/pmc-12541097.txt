Integrating deep learning and radiomics for preoperative glioma grading using multi-center MRI data
Authors: Shi Yin, Jianguang Ming, Hui Chen, Ying Sun, Chuanlu Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate preoperative glioma grading remains a critical challenge in neuro-oncology. This study presents a novel integrated approach combining deep learning architectures with radiomics features derived from multi-parametric MRI to improve preoperative glioma grading accuracy. In this retrospective multi-center study, we analyzed 847 patients with histopathologically confirmed gliomas from 5 tertiary neurosurgical centers. Multi-parametric MRI sequences (T1, T1-contrast, T2, FLAIR) were processed using a dual-stream framework where: (1) a 3D convolutional neural network extracted deep imaging features, and (2) 1,423 quantitative radiomic features were extracted and selected using a recursive feature elimination algorithm. We developed an ensemble model that integrates both feature streams with clinical variables. Model performance was evaluated through 5-fold cross-validation and external validation on an independent cohort (n = 213). The integrated model achieved superior performance (AUC = 0.946, 95% CI: 0.927–0.965) compared to radiomics-only (AUC = 0.891) or deep learning-only (AUC = 0.903) approaches for distinguishing high-grade (WHO grades III-IV) from low-grade (WHO grades I-II) gliomas. Notably, the model demonstrated robust performance across different MRI acquisition parameters (AUC = 0.921 on external validation). Subgroup analysis revealed particular efficacy in identifying isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) wild-type gliomas (sensitivity 0.954, specificity 0.912). The model accurately identified 89.2% of gliomas with molecular features associated with aggressive behavior but ambiguous conventional imaging characteristics. This integrated radiomics-deep learning approach significantly improves preoperative glioma grading accuracy across diverse patient populations and imaging protocols. The proposed framework offers a non-invasive tool for preoperative risk stratification, potentially informing surgical planning and treatment strategies. The model’s interpretability provides insights into imaging biomarkers associated with glioma aggressiveness. Introduction

According to statistics, gliomas are the most common types of primary malignant brain tumors (tumors coming from CNS) and account for the 80% of all malignant CNS neoplasm (another word for tumor)1. These tumors show strong heterogeneity at many levels: histological, molecular, genetic, and metabolic, which poses major diagnostic and therapeutic issues2. The 2021 WHO CNS Tumor Schema, represents a paradigm shift in the classification of gliomas, wherein molecular parameters are reported along with histopathological parameters. Application of molecular parameters optimally enhances diagnostic accuracy and treatment decision making1. This combined diagnostic tool has changed the way we understand glioma biology and made molecular markers like isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation status and 1p/19q codeletion and TERT promoter mutations essential for accurate tumor classification and prognosis. Even with developments in molecular diagnostics, assessing gliomas before surgery is still complicated. Determining the tumor grade and molecular characteristics preoperatively as accurate as possible helps in surgical planning, treatment decision making and counselling of the patient. According to the conventional method, the biopsy depends on invasive means by stereotactic procedures. These approaches have a sampling error on account of the intratumoral variability. Moreover, it is also accompanied by a complication of the procedure and a delay in starting treatment2. Moreover, despite thorough histopathological and molecular investigations, relevant diagnosis discrepancies could still exist, for example, between WHO grade II and III gliomas, or identification of molecularly-defined high-risk tumors with seemingly harmless imaging appearance3.

MRI (Magnetic resonance imaging) is the main non-invasive diagnostic technique for brain tumors. MRI gives information related to the site of the tumor, the size of the tumor and its macroscopic features4. Nonetheless, conventional radiological assessment has a significant degree of inter-observer variability, and it does not accurately depict the molecular characteristics of gliomas5. The diagnostic gap has led to the development of sophisticated computational methodologies that facilitate the extraction and analysis of quantitative imaging characteristics beyond human visual perception. Over the last few years, two complementary approaches - radiomics and deep learning - have begun to bridge the gap between imaging phenotypes and tumor biology6. Radiomics involves high-throughput extraction of quantitative data from medical images, followed by analysis to identify correlations with clinical outcomes or other biological characteristics. CNNs, a class of deep learning model that excels at image analysis, allow for automatic feature extraction from imaging data, thus capturing complex patterns that might not be picked up by pre-defined radiomic features7. According to multiple studies, glioma characterization by radiomics has shown great potential. Wang et al.8 designed a multiparametric MRI-based radiomics nomogram which achieved an area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.886 for glioma grade prediction. Su et al.9 found the use of radiomics based on multicontrast MRI could achieve high precision for distinguishing glioma subtypes. An example of this is the distinction between IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype gliomas (AUC = 0.921). Kim and colleagues10 found that the accuracy of the diffusion- and perfusion-weighted MRI radiomics model for predicting IDH mutation status was 87.7%. The parameters also correlate for tumor aggressiveness in lower-grade gliomas. The ability of gliomas to infiltrate the surrounding healthy brain tissue and their aggressive nature make gliomas deadly tumors. Cheng et al.11 confirmed that combining intratumoral and peritumoral radiomic features significantly improves the prediction accuracy of glioma grading over the use of intratumoral features. Tan et al.12 carried out a multi-institutional study showing that peritumoral radiomics holds promise for prediction of preoperative glioma grade, with an AUC in external validation cohort of 0.893.

Strategies based on radiomics have been observed to effectively predict specific molecular changes in gliomas. According to Li et al.13, a method for predicting glioma subtype (as defined by tumor grade, IDH mutation, and 1p/19q codeletion) was developed based on radiomics with 90.2% accuracy. Arita et al.14 utilized lesion location information coupled with radiomic features to predict IDH and TERT promoter mutations in grade II/III gliomas, yielding AUCs of 0885 and 0763, respectively. Molecular Classification of Gliomas Using MRI and Radiomics Features. Wang et al.15 employed multi-modal MRI radiomic features to distinguish IDH-mutant TERT promoter-mutant gliomas, achieving an AUC of 0.842. Nevertheless, applications of radiomics have certain limitations such as requirement of explicit feature engineering, redundancy among some features, lack of standardization across imaging protocols and institutions16,17.

The methods of deep learning help in characterizing gliomas, i.e. brain tumours, by directly learning from imaging data. Lao et al.7 were the first to develop a deep learning-based radiomics model for predicting survival in glioblastoma multiforme with a C-index of 0.711. Yang et al.18 showed that neural networks using transfer learning were able to classify high-grade and low-grade glioma with an accuracy of 90%. More recent studies show promising improvements in deep learning for glioma assessment. A multi-task deep learning approach was developed by Van der Voort et al.19 that performs segmentation, grading, and molecular subtyping of gliomas, achieving a weighted accuracy of 0.96 for the integrated diagnosis. Cheng et al.20 proposed a fully automated multimodal MRI based multi task learning system for glioma segmentation and IDH genotyping. The average Dice score achieved by the proposed framework is 0.906 for segmentation and accuracy of 0.887 for IDH status. The deep learning applications in glioma assessment are state-of-the-art transformer-based architectures. The work of Wang et al.21 is CrossFormer++, a universal vision transformer with cross-scale attention for medical image analysis. Niu et al.22 devised an MRI transformer-based method combined with radiomics for identification of IDH-wildtype TERT promoter-mutant gliomas, with AUC = 0.946 in the external validation cohort. Even as advances are being made, deep learning methods are often considered ‘black boxes’ which limit interpretability and clinical uptake4. They generally require large, well-annotated datasets for efficient training, which may prove difficult a challenge for rare tumor subtypes or specific molecular alterations. Some researchers resorted to hybrid approaches, understanding the complementary strengths of radiomics and deep learning. Choi et al.23 proposed a fully automated hybrid approach that combines deep learning and radiomics features to predict the IDH mutation status in gliomas, reaching an AUC of 0.94. Calabrese et al.24 combined radiomics and deep convolutional neural network features from the preoperative MRI to ensure a clinically relevant genetic biomarker in glioblastomas enhancing the predictive performance compared to a single approach. Another method that is becoming used in analyzing the heterogeneity of gliomas is habitat analysis. This method is along with the hybrid radiomics-deep learning frameworks. This approach identifies different subregions within tumors based on images that can capture multiple dissimilarities. This could be vital to capturing those spatial heterogeneities that drive treatment resistance and recurrence25. Bailo et al.26 utilized PET and MRI for space – time habitat analysis to decipher the heterogeneity of malignant gliomas. Their results showed the presence of different habitats, namely, hypoxic, perfused and diffusion-restricted. Moreover, these different habitats exhibited distinct molecular characteristics. According to Yang et al.27, the habitat analysis using diffusion kurtosis imaging can demonstrate the distinctness of high-risk molecular subtypes and heterogeneity patterns in diffuse gliomas which exhibits better prognostic performance than standard imaging.

According to Park and colleagues in 2022, spatiotemporal heterogeneity of multiparametric physiologic MRI is associated with IDH-wildtype glioblastoma patient outcome. More heterogeneous tumors exhibited worse prognoses. Shaheen and colleagues proposed a multiregional radiomics technique for predicting overall survival in glioma patients that potentially provides insight into the relationship between intratumoural heterogeneity and clinical outcomes. Li et al.30 produced a radiomics imaging methods based upon MRI to prognosticate survival and tumor-infiltrating macrophages in gliomas through imaging-tumor immune microenvironment linkages. An increasing body of research supports the assessment and quantification of tumor heterogeneity for characterization and prognosis of gliomas. Combining imaging data with electronic health records and genomic information is another frontier in glioma assessment. Mohsen et al.31 explored the use of artificial intelligence to try to combine electronic health records and imaging data. They found it a possibility to aid diagnosis and treatment. Li et al.32 analyze and correlate radiomics and deep learning approaches for molecular subtyping of diffuse gliomas. They discovered that combined models outperform single-modality approaches. Validation from different centers is becoming important for the generalizability of radiomics and DL models. In this study, Wang et al.33 make a comparison among three-dimensional (3D) vs. two-dimensional (2D) deep-learning-based, radiomics-based and fusion-based models using data from multiple centers. The experts find that fusion-based models achieved one of the most stable performances as compared to the other models across centers and imaging protocols. According to a multicenter study by Lan et al.34, integration of MRI-based deep learning and radiomics significantly improved the performance to predict occult cervical lymph node metastasis compared to clinical assessment. A breast cancer treatment response prediction model based on longitudinal MRI fusion was developed by Huang et al.35. The temporal dimension has been relatively overlooked in glioma assessment, despite its potential importance for measuring disease evolution and treatment response. There have been significant developments in the use of radiomics and deep learning methods for the assessment of glioma. The existing literature, however, has some serious gaps which require further attention. The main limitation centers around the use of computational approaches in compartmentalized manner as most studies use radiomics or deep learning either alone or together but not the both23,24. Even when hybrid models are used, they apply simple integration strategies, for example feature concatenation, without consideration of differences in feature dimensionality, interpretability and information content between radiomics features and deep learning features32. The siloed way of using these models neither uses the biological interpretability and standard features of radiomics nor the hierarchical representation learning and patterns of DNNs.

In this study, we address these gaps by developing and validating an integrated radiomics-deep learning framework for comprehensive preoperative characterization of gliomas. Our approach offers several key innovations:

First, we propose a novel dual-stream architecture that processes multiparametric MRI data through parallel radiomics and deep learning pipelines. The radiomics stream extracts a comprehensive set of 1,423 quantitative features from manually segmented intratumoral and peritumoral regions, capturing shape, first-order statistics, texture, and higher-order characteristics. Concurrently, a custom-designed 3D convolutional neural network with a transformer-based attention mechanism processes the raw imaging data to extract deep features that might elude predefined radiomic calculations. Unlike previous approaches that simply concatenate these feature sets, we employ a feature-level fusion module with adaptive weighting to optimally integrate these complementary information streams.

Second, we implement a multi-regional habitat analysis framework that automatically identifies and characterizes distinct imaging habitats within each tumor based on multiparametric signal intensities. This habitat-based approach captures the spatial heterogeneity of gliomas at an unprecedented level of detail, enabling quantification of habitat-specific features and their spatial relationships. By incorporating these habitat-derived metrics into our prediction models, we account for the complex heterogeneity that drives glioma behavior and treatment response.

Third, we conduct extensive external validation across five independent institutions with diverse patient populations and imaging protocols. This rigorous validation approach ensures the generalizability of our findings and addresses the critical issue of model robustness in heterogeneous real-world settings. Furthermore, we employ domain adaptation techniques to mitigate the impact of scanner-specific variations on model performance, enhancing cross-institutional applicability.

Fourth, we extend beyond the conventional focus on IDH mutation and 1p/19q codeletion to predict a broader spectrum of clinically relevant molecular characteristics, including TERT promoter mutations, CDKN2A homozygous deletion, and MGMT promoter methylation status. This comprehensive molecular characterization aligns with the integrated diagnostic approach emphasized in the 2021 WHO classification and provides a more complete assessment of tumor biology.

Finally, we develop and validate a clinical decision support system that integrates our imaging-based predictions with relevant clinical parameters to generate personalized risk assessments for individual patients. This system not only predicts tumor grade and molecular characteristics but also estimates the probability of specific clinical outcomes, providing actionable information for treatment planning and prognostication. Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This multicenter retrospective investigation was designed and conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines and received explicit approval from the institutional review boards of all participating centers. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the institutional review boards of all participating centers waived the need of obtaining informed consent. We systematically assembled a comprehensive dataset from five tertiary neurosurgical centers with diverse geographical distributions, institutional characteristics, and patient demographics to ensure maximum representativeness: Center A (university hospital, n = 298), Center B (specialized cancer institute, n = 231), Center C (university hospital, n = 189), Center D (neurological institute, n = 129), and Center E (university hospital, n = 213). Centers A through D (n = 847) constituted our primary development cohort for model training and internal validation, while Center E served as a completely independent external validation cohort to rigorously assess generalizability and clinical translatability.

Patient inclusion criteria were meticulously defined as follows: (1) histopathologically confirmed diffuse gliomas according to the 2021 WHO classification criteria (Louis et al., 2021), ensuring contemporary molecular classification standards; (2) availability of high-quality preoperative multiparametric MRI including T1-weighted (T1w), T1-weighted with contrast enhancement (T1CE), T2-weighted (T2w), and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences; (3) comprehensive molecular characterization including isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation status, 1p/19q codeletion, TERT promoter mutation, and CDKN2A deletion status; and (4) no prior surgical intervention, radiation therapy, or chemotherapy before the index MRI examination that might alter the native tumor imaging characteristics. We implemented rigorous exclusion criteria encompassing: (1) significant imaging artifacts precluding accurate analysis as determined by two independent neuroradiologists; (2) incomplete molecular profiling that would prevent accurate tumor classification; (3) interval exceeding 30 days between preoperative imaging and surgical intervention to minimize potential tumor evolution effects; and (4) concomitant neurological disorders that could confound imaging analysis (e.g., prior stroke, traumatic brain injury, or neurodegenerative conditions). Following application of these criteria, our final analytical cohort comprised 847 patients in the development set and 213 patients in the validation set.

Patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and histopathological findings were systematically extracted from electronic medical records using standardized data collection protocols with rigorous quality control measures to ensure data integrity. All histopathological diagnoses strictly followed the integrated histological and molecular parameters defined in the 2021 WHO classification of Central Nervous System tumors. For cases diagnosed prior to 2021, histopathological classifications were retrospectively and comprehensively updated according to the latest classification system through detailed review of available molecular data by experienced neuropathologists. As presented in Table 1, the demographic and clinical characteristics demonstrated robust comparability between development and validation cohorts across all key parameters including age distribution, sex ratio, tumor location, and molecular subtype distribution (all p > 0.05), though some slight variations in high-grade glioma prevalence were observed between centers (p = 0.041), reflecting natural institutional variation. This balanced multicenter design with external validation significantly enhances the generalizability of our findings and rigorously tests the robustness of our models across diverse clinical settings and patient populations.

Image acquisition and preprocessing

All enrolled patients underwent comprehensive preoperative multiparametric MRI examinations according to the standardized institutional protocols at their respective centers. Imaging was performed on both 1.5T (31.7% of cases) and 3 T (68.3% of cases) MRI scanners from various manufacturers (Siemens Healthineers, GE Healthcare, Philips Healthcare), reflecting the real-world heterogeneity encountered in clinical practice. While specific acquisition parameters exhibited some variation across institutions and scanner types (as expected in multicenter studies), all protocols mandatorily included the four required sequences: T1w, T1CE, T2w, and FLAIR. The T1w sequence parameters were typically: repetition time (TR) = 400–700 ms, echo time (TE) = 8–15 ms, flip angle = 70–90°, field of view (FOV) = 220–240 mm, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256 or 512 × 512, and slice thickness = 1–5 mm. T1CE images were acquired with identical parameters at least 5 min after intravenous administration of a gadolinium-based contrast agent (0.1 mmol/kg body weight) to ensure optimal lesion enhancement. T2w imaging utilized parameters typically comprising: TR = 3500–6000 ms, TE = 80–120 ms, with matching FOV and matrix size as T1w. FLAIR sequences employed: TR = 8000–10,000 ms, TE = 90–140 ms, inversion time (TI) = 2200–2800 ms (optimized for effective nulling of cerebrospinal fluid signal), with spatial parameters comparable to other sequences. To maximize standardization, we mandated specific minimum requirements, including that all T1CE sequences be acquired at least 5 min post-contrast administration with slice thickness ≤ 3 mm to ensure adequate spatial resolution for precise feature extraction.

To address the critical challenge of inter-institutional variations in image acquisition protocols and scanner characteristics, we implemented a comprehensive, multi-stage preprocessing pipeline using an in-house developed platform based on ITK and SimpleITK libraries. As illustrated in Fig. 1, this preprocessing workflow consisted of seven sequential stages, each with rigorous quality control mechanisms integrated at critical checkpoints. The process began with standardized DICOM to NIfTI conversion using dcm2niix (version 1.0.20200331) with optimized parameters, followed by robust automated quality assessment to detect and quantify artifacts, signal dropout, and motion degradation through comprehensive analysis of signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), and intensity histogram characteristics. Images identified with severe artifacts were either reprocessed with modified parameters or, if corrections proved insufficient after multiple attempts, excluded from further analysis to maintain data integrity.

Non-local means denoising was then applied to reduce random noise while meticulously preserving structural integrity, particularly at critical tissue boundaries where tumor infiltration often occurs. This denoising algorithm employed optimized parameters (search radius = 3, patch radius = 1, noise level estimation = automatic) derived from extensive preliminary testing on a representative subset of 50 cases from our cohort. Importantly, the denoising strength was adaptively and dynamically adjusted according to the estimated noise level of each sequence for each individual patient, rather than applying uniform parameters across all cases, to prevent oversmoothing while achieving optimal noise reduction.

We addressed the pervasive challenge of intensity inhomogeneities due to B0 and B1 field variations using the N4 bias field correction algorithm with parameters specifically optimized for brain MRI applications (50 iterations at 4 resolution levels, convergence threshold = 0.001, shrink factor = 4). The algorithm employed a sophisticated hierarchical B-spline approximation of the bias field with adaptive fitting capabilities to accurately model the complex spatial variations in signal intensity frequently observed in neuro-oncological imaging, particularly at higher field strengths. All sequences were precisely co-registered to the T1CE image, selected as the reference due to its clear anatomical definition and clinical relevance, using a robust rigid registration algorithm (6 degrees of freedom) with mutual information as the similarity metric. Each registration result underwent meticulous visual quality control by experienced neuroradiologists, with manual adjustments applied when necessary to ensure precise anatomical alignment across all sequences.

Brain extraction, a critical preprocessing step for accurate tumor analysis, was performed using a state-of-the-art deep learning-based approach (HD-BET) specifically trained on diverse brain tumor MRI datasets to accurately delineate brain tissue while excluding skull, meninges, and other non-brain structures that could confound subsequent analyses. This algorithm demonstrated exceptional performance, achieving a mean Dice similarity coefficient of 0.943 when compared with expert manual brain masks on a validation subset of 50 randomly selected cases spanning all participating centers. We maintained the optimized default parameters (probability threshold = 0.5, mode = fast, postprocessing = true) which provided the ideal balance between accuracy and computational efficiency based on extensive validation experiments.

Following brain extraction, all images were resampled to 1 × 1 × 1 mm³ isotropic resolution using B-spline interpolation (order = 3) to ensure consistent voxel dimensions across all cases, facilitating subsequent feature extraction and analysis. For intensity normalization, we implemented sequence-specific approaches tailored to the distinct signal characteristics of each modality: WhiteStripe normalization for T1w and T1CE sequences, which identifies the normal white matter intensity distribution and scales accordingly, and Z-score normalization based on normal-appearing white matter for T2w and FLAIR sequences. To further mitigate scanner-specific intensity variations while preserving the essential relative intensity differences between tissue types that carry diagnostic information, we implemented histogram matching across centers. A reference distribution was meticulously constructed from a carefully balanced subset of high-quality images from all centers, and each image was then transformed to match this reference distribution using a piece-wise linear mapping function with 100 quantiles, providing sufficient granularity for accurate intensity mapping while avoiding overfitting to noise. This approach effectively preserved the critical relative contrast between different tissue types while minimizing scanner-related variations that could introduce unwanted bias into the subsequent analyses.

The entire preprocessing pipeline was implemented in Python 3.8 with parallel processing capabilities utilizing the multiprocessing module to optimize computational efficiency. The complete preprocessing of a single patient’s multiparametric MRI dataset required approximately 3–5 min on a standard workstation (Intel Xeon E5-2690, 64GB RAM). Critical quality control checkpoints were established after each major processing step, with systematic visual assessment performed by two board-certified neuroradiologists (with 10 and 8 years of experience, respectively) who were blinded to clinical and histopathological information. Cases with persistent preprocessing artifacts despite multiple correction attempts were either reprocessed with individually adjusted parameters or, if issues remained, excluded from further analysis to maintain the highest data quality standards.

During initial experiments, we tested various domain adaptation methods such as ComBat harmonization and GAN (generative adversarial network)-based style transfer. Harmonization through ComBat reduced the inter-center variability for the radiomic features by 23%. However, ComBat’s intervention for deep learning features did not accomplish similar feats. The CycleGAN architecture of GAN-based style transfer produced agreeable harmonization of the images used, but at the same time, it created some subtle artefacts in the simulation. This degraded the accuracy of habitat identification, which decreased the mean silhouette coefficient from 0.67 to 0.59. Out of the numerous normalization and harmonization methods available, we found that the combination of histogram matching and sequence-specific normalization was the best-balanced option in terms of achieving harmonization without compromising the effectiveness of the deep learning classifier.

Tumor segmentation

We implemented a sophisticated multi-stage semi-automated segmentation approach to delineate tumor components with high precision while maintaining practical efficiency for application across a large multicenter cohort. Initial automated segmentation was performed using a custom-developed 3D U-Net architecture specifically pre-trained on the Brain Tumor Segmentation (BraTS) 2020 challenge dataset and subsequently fine-tuned on a carefully curated subset of 100 cases from our multicenter cohort, ensuring robustness to the specific imaging characteristics encountered in our study. The network architecture (Fig. 2A) featured an encoder with four resolution steps (each comprising two convolutional layers with 3 × 3 × 3 kernels, batch normalization, and ReLU activation), a bottleneck layer, and a symmetric decoder with long and short skip connections to preserve both fine-grained and contextual information. Feature maps at each resolution level contained 32, 64, 128, and 256 channels, respectively, providing sufficient representational capacity for the complex task of tumor segmentation.

Training employed the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 3 × 10⁻⁴, exponential decay (rate = 0.9), and early stopping (patience = 15 epochs) to prevent overfitting. Data augmentation techniques including random rotations (± 15°), elastic deformations, minor scaling variations (0.9–1.1), and intensity perturbations (± 5%) were applied during training to enhance model generalization capabilities.

This optimized model generated probability maps for three distinct tumor subregions as defined in contemporary neuro-oncological imaging research: enhancing tumor (ET, regions exhibiting contrast enhancement on T1CE images), non-enhancing tumor core (NET, hypointense regions on T1CE within the gross tumor boundaries), and peritumoral edema/infiltration (ED, hyperintense regions on FLAIR extending beyond the tumor core). The probability maps were converted to binary masks using optimal thresholds determined through comprehensive receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis on the validation subset, resulting in thresholds of ET = 0.55, NET = 0.40, and ED = 0.50 that maximized the Dice coefficient while maintaining clinically acceptable sensitivity and specificity.

Each automated segmentation was subsequently reviewed and refined, when necessary, by a team of three board-certified neuroradiologists (with 12, 10, and 8 years of experience, respectively) using 3D Slicer (version 4.11.0). The experts followed rigorously standardized criteria based on BraTS guidelines while remaining blinded to histopathological diagnoses to prevent confirmation bias. Manual refinements employed specialized tools for region growing, thresholding, and morphological operations, requiring an average of 15 ± 8 min per case, a substantial time reduction compared to fully manual segmentation which typically requires 45–60 min per case. To quantitatively assess inter-rater reliability, a randomly selected subset of 50 cases spanning all tumor grades was independently segmented by all three experts. The Dice similarity coefficient demonstrated robust agreement: mean DSC = 0.87 (95% CI: 0.83–0.91) for ET, 0.82 (95% CI: 0.77–0.87) for NET, and 0.89 (95% CI: 0.86–0.92) for ED. Additional metrics including volume similarity coefficient (VSC > 0.85) and 95th percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95 < 3 mm) for all subregions further confirmed high segmentation consistency across multiple evaluation dimensions.

Feature extraction

Our feature extraction strategy was deliberately designed to be comprehensive and multi-faceted, encompassing three complementary approaches: traditional radiomic feature extraction to capture predefined imaging characteristics, habitat-based feature extraction to quantify spatial heterogeneity patterns, and deep learning feature extraction to automatically discover complex patterns that might escape conventional analysis. This integrative approach enabled unprecedented comprehensive characterization of tumor phenotypes at multiple levels of abstraction, from handcrafted features with direct radiological interpretability to complex learned representations capturing subtle patterns.

For radiomic feature extraction, we employed PyRadiomics (version 3.0.1) in strict compliance with the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) guidelines to ensure reproducibility and standardization across the research community. We extracted a comprehensive set of quantitative features from each of the five ROIs (ET, NET, ED, PPZ, DPZ) across all four MRI sequences (T1w, T1CE, T2w, FLAIR), resulting in a total of 1,423 features per patient. These features were systematically organized into the following categories: (1) Shape Features (n = 14): three-dimensional descriptors of tumor morphology independently of intensity information, including volumetric measures (volume, surface area, surface-to-volume ratio), shape descriptors (sphericity, compactness1, compactness2, spherical disproportion, maximum 3D diameter), and advanced morphological metrics (major/minor/least axis lengths, elongation, flatness, maximum 2D diameter); (2) First-Order Statistics (n = 18 per ROI-sequence combination): quantifying the distribution of voxel intensities without considering spatial relationships, including central tendency metrics (mean, median, mode), dispersion metrics (minimum, maximum, range, mean absolute deviation, robust mean absolute deviation, standard deviation, variance, interquartile range), distribution shape metrics (skewness, kurtosis, uniformity, entropy), and percentile-based metrics (10th percentile, 90th percentile, energy); (3) Texture Features: capturing spatial relationships between voxels with similar or dissimilar intensity values at multiple scales, including Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix features (n = 24 per ROI-sequence combination), Gray Level Run Length Matrix features (n = 16), Gray Level Size Zone Matrix features (n = 16), Gray Level Dependence Matrix features (n = 14), and Neighboring Gray Tone Difference Matrix features (n = 5); and (4) Wavelet Features: all first-order and texture features extracted after applying eight different wavelet decompositions (HHH, HHL, HLH, HLL, LHH, LHL, LLH, LLL) to capture multi-resolution characteristics.

For texture feature extraction, images were discretized using a fixed bin width of 25 intensity units rather than a fixed bin count, as this approach provided optimal balance between feature quality and computational efficiency in our preliminary comparative experiments while ensuring consistent intensity resolution across patients regardless of the intensity range. For each texture feature calculation, we considered 26-connected voxels in 3D space (connecting voxels that share at least one corner) to capture the full three-dimensional context, ensuring comprehensive capture of directional textural information in all spatial orientations. Distance weighting was applied using the Manhattan distance to emphasize textural patterns at varying spatial scales.

To characterize the spatial heterogeneity of gliomas at an unprecedented level of detail—a critical aspect given the well-established intratumoral heterogeneity of these neoplasms—we implemented a novel habitat analysis approach that identified and quantified distinct imaging phenotypes within each tumor based on multiparametric signal characteristics. This innovative approach recognizes that tumors are not homogeneous entities but rather consist of multiple subregions with distinct microenvironmental conditions (such as hypoxia, necrosis, angiogenesis, and varying degrees of cellularity) that manifest as differentiable imaging phenotypes. Third, we implemented an Optimal Clustering Strategy where K-means clustering was applied to the principal components to identify distinct habitats within each tumor. During our preliminary analysis, we tested a number of clustering alternatives (NMF, NMU). Although there are some theoretical grounds for using these methods on the idea that they produce fully interpretable, parts-based decompositions, our comparison on a set of 100 parts showed that clustering in PCA space by K-means gave greater stability (silhouette coefficient of 0.67 versus 0.52 in NMF) and gave a higher correlation with known histopathological features. Habitats derived from K-means were more strongly associated with microvascular proliferation (r = 0.736 vs. r = 0.591 for NMF, p < 0.001). Furthermore, K-means clustering is much more computationally efficient and reproducible with initialization seeds (ICC = 0.91 vs. 0.74 for NMF) which helps in clinical practice. Matrix factorization is usually more interpretable compared to K-means clustering. Future work could combine both approaches to provide a more stable and interpretable hybrid approach.

Fourth, we performed comprehensive Habitat Characterization where each identified habitat was systematically characterized based on its centroid values in the original four-dimensional intensity space, resulting in classification as one of six distinct habitat types: High-Enhancement Habitat (HEH, characterized by high T1CE and low T2/FLAIR signal, typically corresponding to areas of disrupted blood-brain barrier with active tumor proliferation), Low-Enhancement/Necrotic Habitat (LEH, exhibiting low signal on T1w/T1CE and variable T2/FLAIR signal, representing necrotic or cystic components), FLAIR-Hyperintense/Edematous Habitat (FHH, displaying markedly high FLAIR signal with variable T1/T2 characteristics, corresponding to vasogenic edema or infiltrative tumor regions), Infiltrative Habitat (INH, showing moderately increased T2/FLAIR signal with minimal T1CE enhancement, potentially representing regions of tumor infiltration beyond the enhancing margin), Transitional Habitat (TRH, exhibiting intermediate signal characteristics across all sequences, representing the interface between distinct tumor components), and Normal-Appearing Habitat (NAH, displaying signal characteristics similar to normal brain parenchyma but located within or adjacent to tumor boundaries).

For each identified habitat, we extracted a comprehensive set of quantitative features including volumetric features (absolute and relative volumes, spatial distribution metrics), intensity features (central tendency and dispersion metrics of each sequence within each habitat), inter-habitat relationship metrics (Mahalanobis distances between habitat centroids, surface area and gradient magnitude at interfaces), and habitat spatial configuration metrics (fragmentation index, dispersion metrics, core-rim distribution patterns). This sophisticated habitat-based approach enabled unprecedented quantification of tumor heterogeneity at a detailed level, capturing not only the presence and extent of distinct microenvironmental conditions but also their spatial interrelationships and distribution patterns.

Feature selection and model development

Given the exceptionally high dimensionality of our extracted feature space (1,423 radiomic features + 512 deep learning features), we implemented a rigorous multi-stage feature selection strategy to identify the most informative features while systematically minimizing redundancy and mitigating overfitting risk (Fig. 4). This carefully designed strategy comprised four sequential stages that progressively refined the feature set while preserving its discriminative power.

In the initial Robustness Assessment phase, we rigorously evaluated feature stability to ensure that only reproducible features would be incorporated into our final models. Features with ICC < 0.85 were excluded from further analysis to ensure high measurement reliability and temporal stability. This threshold was selected based on established guidelines for excellent reproducibility in medical imaging features.

The evaluation of robustness lead to eliminating 247 features or 17.4% of the initial features with ICC < 0.85. Most of the excluded features were high features derived from wavelet transforms that had a high frequency (n = 156, 63.2%); the remaining were shape features sensitive to segmentation variations (n = 43, 17.4%). Notably, first-order statistics and lower-frequency texture features showed solid reproducibility (mean ICC = 0.91 ± 0.04) and were therefore included in the final set of features. The distribution of excluded features did not differ according to tumor grade or molecular subtype (p = 0.167), indicating no systematic feature stability bias.

In the subsequent Redundancy Reduction phase, we addressed the critical issue of multicollinearity by performing comprehensive correlation analysis among the remaining features. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was calculated for each feature pair, and highly correlated features (|ρ| > 0.9) were identified, forming correlation clusters. From each cluster of highly correlated features, only the feature with the strongest univariate association with the target variable (measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) was retained, effectively eliminating redundant information while preserving the essential predictive power. This approach resulted in a substantial dimensionality reduction while maintaining the information content of the feature set.

The third phase involved Statistical Significance Filtering, where features showing significant association with the target variable were identified through rigorous statistical testing. For continuous features, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for binary outcomes and the Kruskal-Wallis test for multi-class outcomes, providing non-parametric assessment that does not assume normal distribution of imaging features. For categorical features, Fisher’s exact test was used to assess statistical associations. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was systematically applied to control the false discovery rate at α = 0.05, addressing the multiple comparison problem inherent in high-dimensional feature analysis. Only features with adjusted p-values < 0.05 were retained for subsequent analysis, ensuring statistical relevance of the selected features.

In the final phase, we implemented Recursive Feature Elimination with Cross-Validation (RFECV) with a support vector machine estimator to identify the optimal feature subset that maximizes predictive performance. We developed strategies to counter possible feature imbalance of Radiomic (32–57 features) and Deep Learning (512 features) streams. Initially, we applied Principal Component Analysis to the features for which deep learning was applied. We took the components explaining 95% of the variance. This was typically standard of 64–89 components. In addition, this standardization resulted in streams of features that were more balanced and in similar ranges. Secondly, since we use an attention-based fusion module (Eq. 16), feature imbalance is inherently handled because it computes adaptive weights to prevent predictions from being dominated by a single modality. We applied separate learning rates during training for each stream (a learning rate equal to 0.0005 for the radiomic stream and at 0.0003 for the deep learning stream) so that both streams are optimized well. The results of the ablation studies confirmed that this approach indeed prevents overfitting. The number of selected features for each task ranged from 32 to 57, providing sufficient information content while avoiding overfitting to the training data.

Notably, the model demonstrated remarkable sensitivity (0.954) and specificity (0.912) for identifying IDH-wildtype gliomas in the external validation cohort (Fig. 9B). For TERT promoter mutation prediction, the model achieved a sensitivity of 0.887 and specificity of 0.835, representing a substantial improvement over previous approach. The prediction of 1p/19q codeletion, a marker defining oligodendroglia lineage, showed sensitivity of 0.903 and specificity of 0.889, with particularly high performance in IDH-mutant gliomas (AUC = 0.931).

Feature importance analysis for molecular prediction (Fig. 9C) revealed that different feature sets contributed to each molecular prediction task. T2/FLAIR intensity characteristics in peritumoral regions were highly predictive for IDH mutation status, while texture features from T1CE in enhancing regions contributed strongly to TERT promoter mutation prediction. Habitat-derived features, particularly the proportion of infiltrative habitat (INH) and the Mahalanobis distance between habitat centroids, significantly enhanced predictive performance for all molecular tasks.

The model’s molecular prediction capabilities were particularly valuable for identifying diagnostically challenging cases. The habitat-enhanced fusion model accurately identified 89.2% of gliomas with molecular features associated with aggressive behavior (IDH-wildtype and/or TERT promoter-mutant) but ambiguous conventional imaging characteristics. This finding has significant clinical implications given the potential for misdiagnosis or underestimation of tumor aggressiveness in standard radiological evaluation.

Habitat analysis and tumor heterogeneity

The habitat analysis revealed distinct patterns associated with different glioma molecular subtypes, providing novel insights into tumor biology and heterogeneity. Figure 10 presents the comprehensive analysis of tumor habitats across molecular subtypes and their association with tumor aggressiveness.

Habitat Analysis Results Demonstrating Tumor Heterogeneity Patterns.

The three-dimensional visualization of habitat distribution (Fig. 10A) showed distinct spatial organization patterns between high-grade and low-grade gliomas. High-grade tumors exhibited a complex, heterogeneous arrangement of multiple habitats with interspersed enhancing (HEH) and necrotic (LEH) regions surrounded by infiltrative zones. In contrast, low-grade gliomas typically displayed a more homogeneous composition with predominant infiltrative (INH) and FLAIR-hyperintense (FHH) habitats organized in a concentric pattern.

Quantitative analysis of habitat composition (Fig. 10B) revealed significant differences between molecular subtypes. IDH-wildtype gliomas demonstrated significantly higher proportions of high-enhancement habitat (28.4% vs. 12.7%, p < 0.001) and low-enhancement/necrotic habitat (23.7% vs. 9.3%, p < 0.001) compared to IDH-mutant gliomas. Conversely, IDH-mutant gliomas showed higher proportions of infiltrative habitat (33.5% vs. 21.2%, p < 0.001) and FLAIR-hyperintense/edematous habitat (27.9% vs. 18.6%, p < 0.001).

Among IDH-mutant gliomas, 1p/19q codeleted tumors (oligodendrogliomas) exhibited distinct habitat signatures compared to 1p/19q intact tumors (astrocytomas). Oligodendrogliomas showed higher proportions of transitional habitat (19.4% vs. 12.7%, p = 0.003) and more homogeneous habitat distribution overall (entropy of habitat distribution: 1.43 vs. 1.68, p = 0.002).

The analysis of inter-habitat relationships through Mahalanobis distances (Fig. 10C) provided additional discriminative information. The distance between high-enhancement and infiltrative habitats was significantly larger in high-grade gliomas compared to low-grade tumors (7.65 vs. 5.28, p < 0.001), suggesting more distinct microenvironmental niches in higher-grade lesions. Similarly, IDH-wildtype gliomas exhibited greater distances between habitat centroids compared to IDH-mutant tumors (mean inter-habitat distance: 6.92 vs. 5.43, p < 0.001), indicating more pronounced habitat differentiation in these aggressive tumors.

Correlation analysis between habitat metrics and histopathological features (Fig. 10D) demonstrated significant associations between habitat characteristics and biological attributes. The proportion of high-enhancement habitat positively correlated with microvascular proliferation (r = 0.736, p < 0.001) and cellular proliferation as measured by Ki-67 index (r = 0.685, p < 0.001). The entropy of habitat distribution showed strong correlation with histological heterogeneity score (r = 0.714, p < 0.001), while the proportion of low-enhancement/necrotic habitat correlated with the extent of necrosis on histopathology (r = 0.762, p < 0.001).

These findings validate our hypothesis that quantitative habitat analysis provides biologically relevant information about tumor microenvironment and heterogeneity that enhances predictive performance beyond conventional radiomic or deep learning approaches.

Feature importance and model interpretability

To enhance interpretability and provide insights into the imaging biomarkers associated with glioma characteristics, we conducted comprehensive feature importance analysis using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values. Figure 11 presents the feature importance results for the habitat-enhanced fusion model.

Feature Importance and Interpretability Analysis.

The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 11A) revealed that features from all three streams (radiomics, deep learning, and habitat analysis) contributed significantly to glioma grade prediction. Among radiomic features, the 90th percentile intensity on T1CE within the enhancing tumor region emerged as the most discriminative feature, followed by GLCM-based texture features (particularly joint entropy and contrast) in the peritumoral regions. From the habitat analysis, the volumetric proportion of high-enhancement habitat (HEH) and the Mahalanobis distance between high-enhancement and infiltrative habitats ranked among the top predictors. Deep learning features, particularly those from the highest convolutional layers (layers 9–12), contributed unique predictive information, suggesting that complex, high-level patterns captured by the CNN significantly enhanced model performance.

SHAP dependency plots (Fig. 11B) illustrated the non-linear relationships between the top features and model predictions. For the 90th percentile intensity on T1CE, a sigmoid-like relationship was observed, with a sharp increase in SHAP value (positive contribution to predicting high-grade) occurring above a threshold of approximately 0.75 (normalized intensity). The proportion of high-enhancement habitat showed a more linear relationship with positive SHAP values, while the relationship for peritumoral texture features was more complex, with multiple inflection points indicating region-specific patterns.

Feature importance patterns varied substantially across molecular subtypes (Fig. 11C). For IDH-mutant gliomas, T2/FLAIR-derived features in the peritumoral regions showed higher importance, reflecting the infiltrative nature and less distinct margins characteristic of these tumors. In contrast, for IDH-wildtype gliomas, contrast enhancement patterns and habitat heterogeneity metrics dominated the prediction, aligning with the typically more aggressive and heterogeneous presentation of these tumors.

The case visualization with feature attribution overlay (Fig. 11D) provided an intuitive representation of model interpretability, highlighting the regions and features contributing most to the prediction. For high-grade gliomas, the model focused on enhancing regions and habitat interfaces, while for low-grade tumors, the attention was more distributed across peritumoral regions and the homogeneity of habitat distributions. These interpretability analyses provide valuable insights into the model’s decision-making process and establish biological plausibility for the identified imaging biomarkers, enhancing the clinical credibility and adoption potential of the model.

Robustness analysis and generalizability

A critical aspect of our study was evaluating model performance across different scanner manufacturers, field strengths, and acquisition parameters to assess generalizability in real-world clinical settings. Figure 12 presents the comprehensive robustness analysis results.

Robustness Analysis and Generalizability Assessment.

Performance across different scanner manufacturers (Fig. 12A) showed remarkable consistency, with mean AUCs of 0.932 (Siemens, n = 438), 0.928 (GE, n = 356), and 0.923 (Philips, n = 266). Similarly, the model maintained consistent performance across field strengths, with AUCs of 0.912 for 1.5T scanners (n = 336) and 0.938 for 3 T scanners (n = 724). The coefficient of variation for AUC across different scanner configurations was less than 5%, indicating robust generalizability. Formal interaction analysis revealed no statistically significant interactions between model performance and scanner manufacturer (p = 0.327) or field strength (p = 0.284), confirming the model’s broad applicability across diverse imaging protocols.

Subgroup analysis (Fig. 12B) demonstrated consistent performance across clinically relevant patient subgroups. The habitat-enhanced fusion model maintained robust performance across different tumor locations, with comparable AUCs for frontal (0.938, n = 406), temporal (0.951, n = 287), parietal (0.928, n = 186), and multilobar tumors (0.913, n = 93). Performance was slightly lower for occipital tumors (0.889, n = 67), potentially due to their relative rarity in the training dataset. Age-stratified analysis revealed consistent performance across age groups: <40 years (AUC = 0.935, n = 187), 40–60 years (AUC = 0.944, n = 479), and > 60 years (AUC = 0.951, n = 394). Similarly, model performance did not differ significantly between male and female patients (AUC = 0.942 vs. 0.949, p = 0.376).

Analysis by enhancement pattern showed higher performance for enhancing tumors (AUC = 0.962, n = 723) compared to non-enhancing tumors (AUC = 0.901, n = 337, p = 0.013). This finding is expected given that contrast enhancement is a recognized imaging biomarker of higher-grade disease. However, the model still demonstrated good performance for non-enhancing tumors, with the habitat-derived features contributing substantially to prediction accuracy in this challenging subgroup.

Sensitivity analysis to variations in preprocessing parameters (Fig. 12C) revealed the stability of the model across different preprocessing configurations. Variations in denoising strength, bias field correction iterations, and intensity normalization methods resulted in only minor changes in model performance (maximum AUC change: 0.021), indicating robustness to preprocessing variations that might be encountered in real-world implementation.

The clinical decision support system developed based on our model was prospectively evaluated with 50 consecutive suspected glioma patients at Center C (Fig. 12D). The system achieved high accuracy for predicting tumor grade (88.0%), IDH mutation status (90.0%), TERT promoter mutation (84.0%), and CDKN2A deletion status (80.0%), all significantly exceeding the accuracy of conventional radiological assessment by experienced neuroradiologists (78.0%, 76.0%, 68.0%, and 64.0%, respectively, all p < 0.05). A multidisciplinary panel determined that had the system’s predictions been available preoperatively, they would have influenced surgical approach in 28.0% of cases, molecular testing prioritization in 42.0% of cases, and treatment planning discussions in 36.0% of cases. Performance analysis in challenging tumor subtypes

We performed targeted analyses of non-enhancing (n = 337) and highly infiltrative tumours (n = 289) defined by > 80% FLAIR hyperintense habitat proportion to examine the specific contribution of deep learning features in diagnostically challenging cases. For non-enhancing tumors, contributions of deep learning features were significantly higher compared to the contributions of radiomic features (mean SHAP value: 0.34 vs. 0.21, p < 0.001). Our fusion model’s attention mechanism gave 67.3% weight to the deep learning features in these cases while in enhancing tumor it gave 45.2% (p < 0.001). In particular, the features from convolutional layers 9–12 of the CNN captured small intensity gradients and spatial patterns, not detected in conventional radiomics analysis. The AUC obtained for the combined deep learning and habitat features in highly infiltrative tumors was 0.889, which was significantly higher than that obtained using only radiomic features (0.751; p < 0.001). The deep learning stream identified complex spatial relationships between the invasive boundary of the habitat and normal-appearing tissue, improving prediction accuracy for IDH mutation 91.2% vs. 76.8%. In terms of MRSI integration, although NMU decompositions and synthetic spectra paved the way for something interesting, our approach presently focuses on widely available standard MRI sequences that are clinically viable. The advanced metabolic imaging will be included in the future extensions in specialized centers.

Discussion

Interpretation of key findings

The integration of radiomics and deep learning approaches for the preoperative characterization of gliomas represents a significant advancement in computational neuro-oncology. Our study demonstrates that a carefully designed fusion of these complementary methodologies can substantially improve prediction accuracy for both tumor grade and molecular characteristics. The habitat-enhanced dual-stream fusion model achieved superior performance (AUC = 0.946 for internal validation and AUC = 0.921 for external validation) compared to single-modality approaches, confirming our hypothesis that the biological interpretability of radiomic features and the hierarchical representation learning capabilities of deep neural networks can be synergistically combined to enhance diagnostic accuracy.

The superior performance of our integrated model can be attributed to several key innovations. First, the dual-stream architecture with attention-based feature fusion enables adaptive weighting of features from different modalities, allowing the model to dynamically prioritize the most informative features for each individual case. This adaptivity is particularly important given the heterogeneous nature of gliomas, where different imaging features may carry varying degrees of importance depending on the specific tumor characteristics. The attention mechanism effectively functions as a learned feature selection process that optimizes information flow based on the complementary strengths of radiomics and deep learning.

Second, the incorporation of habitat analysis substantially enhanced model performance by capturing the spatial heterogeneity of gliomas. The habitat-derived features provided unique information about tumor composition and microenvironmental conditions that was not fully captured by conventional radiomic features or deep learning representations. As shown in Fig. 10B, the habitat distribution patterns differed significantly between molecular subtypes of gliomas, with IDH-wildtype tumors exhibiting a higher proportion of enhancing (HEH) and necrotic (LEH) habitats, while IDH-mutant tumors showed predominantly FLAIR-hyperintense (FHH) and infiltrative (INH) patterns. Furthermore, the inter-habitat relationships, quantified through Mahalanobis distances between habitat centroids, revealed distinct organizational patterns that correlated with tumor aggressiveness.

Our explainability analysis through SHAP values revealed that the most influential features for grade prediction included a combination of radiomic, deep learning, and habitat-derived features (Fig. 11A). Among radiomic features, first-order statistics of T1CE in the enhancing tumor region (particularly the 90th percentile intensity) and GLCM-based texture features in the peritumoral region showed the highest importance. From the deep learning stream, features from the highest convolutional layers capturing higher-order spatial patterns demonstrated significant predictive value. Notably, habitat distribution metrics, particularly the proportion of enhancing habitat (HEH) and the Mahalanobis distance between enhancing and infiltrative habitats, ranked among the top predictors, confirming the value of our habitat analysis approach.

Interestingly, feature importance patterns differed substantially across molecular subtypes (Fig. 11C). For IDH-mutant gliomas, T2/FLAIR-derived features in the peritumoral regions showed higher importance, reflecting the infiltrative nature and less distinct margins characteristic of these tumors. In contrast, for IDH-wildtype gliomas, contrast enhancement patterns and habitat heterogeneity metrics dominated the prediction, aligning with the typically more aggressive and heterogeneous presentation of these tumors. These findings suggest that tailored models for specific molecular subtypes might further enhance prediction accuracy, a direction worth exploring in future research.

Deep learning feature interpretation and Neuro-oncological correlates

In this work, we performed correlation analysis to make the features learnt by deep learning more interpretable and readable. Features from the highest convolutional layers (layers 9–12) were found to exhibit significant correlations with radiological assessment metrics including relative cerebral blood volume correlation (r = 0.672, p < 0.001), T1CE enhancement heterogeneity index (r = 0.708, p < 0.001), and FLAIR signal heterogeneity (r = 0.634, p < 0.001). In particular, feature maps from layer 11 showed spatial concordance with regions of maximum contrast enhancement (overlap coefficient: 0.78 ± 0.12), whereas features from layer 9 correlated with peritumoral edema patterns (κ = 0.71) as judged by expert neuroradiologists. The findings indicate that the CNN automatically learned to extract complex representations of known imaging biomarkers while possibly identifying novel patterns, not identified by usual analysis.

Though we concentrated on imaging-based habitat identification in our research, we must unfortunately concede that the identified imaging habitats lacked direct histological validation. In a cohort of 45 patients with stereotactic biopsy specimens obtained from different tumor regions, there were significant associations between imaging habitats and histology. High-enhancement habitats were related to a higher density of microvessels (r = 0.68, p < 0.001) whereas infiltrative habitats were related to the density of tumor cells in adjacent normal-appearing tissue (r = 0.59, p = 0.002). Nonetheless, multi-regional sampling protocols for histological validation are beyond the current clinical capabilities of these samples. Future studies that include image-guided sampling may definitively validate the habitat—histology relationships.

The robustness of our model across diverse patient populations and imaging protocols represents a significant advancement over previous approaches. The consistent performance in external validation (Center E) and across different scanner manufacturers and field strengths (Fig. 12A) demonstrates the effectiveness of our preprocessing pipeline and domain adaptation strategies in mitigating scanner-specific variations. This cross-institutional generalizability is crucial for the practical clinical implementation of computational models in neuro-oncology, where imaging protocols and scanner parameters often vary substantially between institutions.

Comparison with existing literature

Our results extend beyond previous studies in several important aspects. Earlier work by Wang et al. (2019) reported an AUC of 0.886 for glioma grade prediction using radiomics features alone, while Su et al. (2019) achieved an AUC of 0.921 for distinguishing IDH-mutant from IDH-wildtype gliomas. Our radiomics-only model showed comparable performance (AUC = 0.891 for grade prediction), but the integration with deep learning and habitat analysis substantially improved accuracy (AUC = 0.946).

The concept of peritumoral radiomics has gained particular attention for capturing the infiltrative nature of gliomas. Cheng et al. (2022) demonstrated that combining intratumoral and peritumoral radiomic features significantly improved prediction accuracy for glioma grading compared to intratumoral features alone. Our approach extends this concept by incorporating not only peritumoral radiomic features but also quantitative habitat analysis that captures the spatial heterogeneity and interrelationships of distinct tumor subregions, providing a more comprehensive characterization of tumor phenotype.

Van der Voort et al. (2023) developed a multi-task deep learning approach that simultaneously performed segmentation, grading, and molecular subtyping of gliomas with a weighted accuracy of 0.96 for the integrated diagnosis. While their performance metrics are impressive, their study lacked extensive external validation across multiple institutions and scanner types, which is a key strength of our approach. Furthermore, they did not incorporate the spatial heterogeneity information captured by our habitat analysis, which we have shown significantly enhances predictive performance.

The TERT promoter mutation prediction represents a particularly notable advancement. Previous studies by Arita et al. (2018) and Wang et al. (2023) reported AUCs of 0.763 and 0.842, respectively, for TERT promoter mutation prediction. Our model achieved an AUC of 0.883 (95% CI: 0.857–0.909) for this task, with robust performance across different tumor grades and locations. This improvement is clinically relevant given the significant prognostic implications of TERT promoter mutations, particularly in IDH-wildtype gliomas.

Recent work by Niu et al. (2025) demonstrated the potential of transformer-based architectures for molecular prediction in gliomas, reporting an AUC of 0.946 for IDH-wildtype TERT promoter-mutant gliomas. While our overall performance is comparable, our approach offers broader applicability across multiple molecular characteristics and tumor subtypes, along with enhanced interpretability through the integration of radiomics and habitat analysis. Furthermore, our model’s explicit quantification of tumor heterogeneity through habitat analysis provides unique insights into the relationship between imaging phenotypes and underlying biology that are not captured by pure deep learning approaches.

The habitat analysis methodology presented in our study builds upon previous work in tumor habitat imaging. Waqar et al. (2022) visualized spatial heterogeneity in glioblastoma using imaging habitats, but their approach was primarily qualitative and lacked the quantitative rigor of our methodology. Bailo et al. (2022) decoded the heterogeneity of malignant gliomas using PET and MRI for spatial habitat analysis, but their study included only a small sample size (n = 24) and did not integrate habitat information with deep learning features as in our approach. Yang et al. (2024) employed diffusion kurtosis imaging-based habitat analysis to identify high-risk molecular subtypes in diffuse gliomas, but their method relied on specialized diffusion sequences not routinely available in clinical practice, limiting widespread applicability.

The clinical decision support system developed in this study addresses a critical gap in the existing literature. While numerous computational models have demonstrated impressive performance metrics in research settings, relatively few have been translated into practical clinical tools with prospective validation. Our system’s integration into clinical workflows and evaluation with 50 consecutive glioma cases provides valuable insights into its real-world utility. Notably, the clinical panel assessment indicated that the system’s predictions would have altered surgical planning in 28% of cases and molecular testing prioritization in 42% of cases, demonstrating tangible clinical impact beyond predictive accuracy.

Clinical implications

The ability to accurately predict glioma grade and molecular characteristics preoperatively has several important clinical implications. First, it can inform surgical planning, potentially guiding decisions regarding extent of resection and surgical approach. For tumors predicted to have aggressive molecular features (e.g., IDH-wildtype with TERT promoter mutation), maximal safe resection would be prioritized, while for tumors with more favorable molecular profiles, preservation of function might be weighted more heavily in the risk-benefit assessment.

Second, preoperative prediction of molecular features can guide tailored molecular testing strategies, potentially expediting pathological diagnosis and treatment planning. This is particularly relevant given the increasing complexity of molecular characterization required for glioma classification under the 2021 WHO criteria. Early identification of likely molecular alterations could streamline diagnostic workflows and reduce time to treatment initiation.

Third, our model’s ability to identify gliomas with molecular features associated with aggressive behavior but ambiguous conventional imaging characteristics (89.2% accuracy) addresses a significant clinical challenge. These “radiologically deceptive” tumors often present diagnostic dilemmas in standard clinical practice, potentially leading to suboptimal treatment planning. Early identification of these cases could prompt more thorough preoperative evaluation and multidisciplinary discussion.

The multidisciplinary panel assessment indicating that the model’s predictions would have significantly influenced clinical decision-making across multiple domains demonstrates its potential utility in real-world settings. By providing objective, quantitative assessments based on advanced computational analysis, our approach could complement subjective radiological interpretation and enhance diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning.

The habitat analysis component of our approach not only improves predictive accuracy but also provides potentially valuable information about tumor biology and heterogeneity. The distinct habitat patterns identified in different molecular subtypes suggest that these imaging phenotypes reflect underlying biological processes. Future research could explore the relationship between specific habitat patterns and treatment response, potentially guiding personalized therapeutic strategies.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while our multicenter dataset represents a significant strength, the retrospective nature of the main analysis introduces potential selection biases. The prospective evaluation with 50 consecutive cases partially addresses this limitation, but larger prospective studies are needed to further validate the model’s clinical utility.

Second, our model primarily focuses on conventional MRI sequences (T1w, T1CE, T2w, FLAIR) due to their universal availability across institutions. Advanced imaging techniques such as perfusion-weighted imaging, diffusion tensor imaging, and MR spectroscopy could provide additional valuable information, particularly for characterizing tumor metabolism and infiltration patterns. Future work should explore the integration of these modalities into the framework while maintaining broad applicability across diverse clinical settings.

Third, while our model demonstrates robust performance across different scanner manufacturers and field strengths, the impact of technical variations in acquisition parameters could be further explored. Development of more sophisticated domain adaptation strategies or physics-informed preprocessing techniques might further enhance cross-institutional generalizability.

Longitudinal applicability and treatment response

Our design showed great preoperative predictive performance. However, whether it can be used for longitudinal monitoring is also a concern for its clinical application. Post treatment changes like radiation necrosis, pseudoprogression or treatment related inflammation significantly alters imaging characteristics and habitat distributions. The preliminary analysis of 89 patients with imaging after treatment showed that the habitats changed significantly over the course of 3 months following radiation (mean habitat distribution change 34.7% ± 12.3%.). The model shows lower performance on post-treatment scans (AUC = 0.768 for recurrence versus treatment effects), so needs treatment-specific adaptations. For future development, incorporating temporal modelling approaches and treatment-informed pre-processing can help achieve predictive validity over the pathway. Patterns in longitudinal habitat evolution may also give valuable information about treatment response and progression warranting investigation.

Fourth, the current model treats gliomas as static entities, whereas longitudinal monitoring of imaging patterns could provide valuable insights into tumor evolution and treatment response. Extensions of our framework to incorporate temporal dynamics represent a promising direction for future research. Finally, while we have made significant efforts to enhance model interpretability through SHAP analysis and habitat visualization, the clinical interpretability of deep learning features remains challenging. Further work on developing more intuitive visualization tools and clinical decision support interfaces could facilitate easier adoption by clinicians.

Future research directions should include: (1) Integration with genomic and transcriptomic data to develop comprehensive radiogenomic models that capture the full spectrum of tumor biology; (2) Extension to treatment response prediction and recurrence monitoring, potentially incorporating longitudinal imaging features; (3) Exploration of more sophisticated transformer-based architectures while maintaining interpretability; (4) Development of patient-specific adaptive models that can be fine-tuned based on individual characteristics and treatment history; and (5) Large-scale prospective clinical trials to evaluate the impact on patient outcomes and healthcare costs.

Conclusion

This study presents a novel integrated approach for preoperative glioma characterization that combines radiomics, deep learning, and habitat analysis in a unified computational framework. The habitat-enhanced dual-stream fusion model significantly outperforms single-modality approaches for predicting glioma grade and molecular characteristics, with robust generalization across multiple institutions and scanner types. The model demonstrates particular efficacy in identifying tumors with aggressive molecular features but ambiguous conventional imaging characteristics, addressing a significant clinical challenge in neuro-oncology.

The comprehensive spatial heterogeneity characterization through habitat analysis provides unique insights into the relationship between imaging phenotypes and underlying tumor biology. This approach captures the complex and multifaceted nature of gliomas, revealing distinct patterns associated with different molecular subtypes and grades. The attention-based feature fusion mechanism effectively integrates complementary information from radiomic and deep learning features, optimizing information flow for each individual case.

The translation of our computational approach into a clinical decision support system represents a significant step toward practical implementation in neuro-oncology workflows. The prospective evaluation demonstrates tangible clinical impact, with potential to influence surgical planning, molecular testing strategies, and treatment decisions. While further validation through larger prospective studies is needed, our findings suggest that advanced computational approaches can meaningfully contribute to clinical decision-making and potentially improve patient outcomes.

Beyond the specific methodological contributions, this work illustrates the broader potential of integrating diverse computational approaches in medical imaging analysis. Rather than viewing radiomics and deep learning as competing methodologies, we demonstrate the value of thoughtfully combining their complementary strengths within a unified framework. This integrative philosophy, coupled with rigorous validation and interpretability mechanisms, provides a template for future development of computational tools in oncological imaging.
