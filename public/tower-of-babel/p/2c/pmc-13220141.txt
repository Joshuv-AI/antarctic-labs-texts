Accommodating capacity-restoring interventions in the Mental Capacity Act 2005
Authors: Daisy Cheung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article argues that the lack of an explicit and systematic role for the restoration of P’s decision-making capacity within the Mental Capacity Act 2005 (MCA) framework is problematic, in particular in light of the significant developments being made in neurointerventions that have the potential to restore, or at least repair, the abilities required to demonstrate decision-making capacity under the MCA. The article first demonstrates where the challenge lies, through an examination of the current legal framework. It then explores three points in the MCA framework at which the consideration of whether capacity can and should be restored might be incorporated, including the ‘practicable steps’ requirement, the best interests assessment, and a potential additional stage between the assessment of capacity and the best interests assessment. It is argued that there are significant challenges with all three of these possibilities, and some directions for the way forward are suggested.

I. Introduction

In recent years, there have been a number of significant developments in neurointerventions that can have considerable effects on crucial elements of cognitive and affective processing. An ongoing study at the University of California, San Francisco, for example, has been investigating the effects of closed-loop deep brain stimulation (DBS),1 an intervention that has yielded initial success in the form of rapid and sustained improvement in an individual with depression.2 DBS has also produced promising results in patients with treatment-resistant schizophrenia,3 obsessive-compulsive disorder,4 addiction disorder,5 and anorexia nervosa.6 A less invasive form of deep brain neurointervention, deep brain ultrasound, has also demonstrated positive outcomes in a patient with severe treatment-resistant non-psychotic depression.7

Notably, many of the cognitive and mental states targeted by such therapeutic neurointerventions are central to the abilities that are often understood to be necessary for decision-making capacity. The potential of these interventions to restore, or at least repair, the abilities required to demonstrate decision-making capacity raises important questions for the way this is assessed under the law. In England and Wales, the Mental Capacity Act 2005 (MCA) provides the framework in which the decision-making capacity of persons with mental disability is assessed, and in which decisions can be made on behalf of persons deemed to lack such capacity in their best interests.8 A foundational principle of the MCA is that a person is not to be treated as unable to make a decision unless all practicable steps have been taken to help them do so.9 Section 4(3) of the MCA further states that a person should be permitted and encouraged to participate in any decision about them. While the MCA emphasizes the importance of facilitating a person in making their own decisions, however, the precise scope of this obligation remains unclear. In particular, it is unclear whether, given the law’s emphasis on empowering a person to make their own decision to the greatest extent possible, there is any requirement to restore the person’s capacity if the means to do so exist. In other words, while the MCA includes a requirement to facilitate capacity,10 it is far from clear whether this entails a requirement to restore the abilities necessary for it. As such, the development of these interventions poses important questions that the current legal framework is arguably ill-equipped to answer.

It is worth noting here that this is not a wholly new problem. There is already a range of interventions, such as anti-psychotic and anti-depressant medication, that have the potential to repair the abilities needed for capacity, for which a similar issue arises. What is perhaps most distinctive about therapeutic neurointerventions such as DBS is the power of such technology to change a person’s cognitive and affective states in a particularly profound and rapid manner, which in turn renders the need to address this issue particularly acute. Thus, in exploring this issue, this discussion is expected to capture both the range of existing interventions, as well as any developing and/or potential neurointerventions on the horizon, that are gradually becoming reality. In this way, this article takes a preliminary but significant step in mapping out the problem in relation to this issue, namely the lack of a role that a consideration of whether capacity can and should be restored plays within the MCA framework.

This article is structured as follows. In the next section, I briefly consider what it means to restore the abilities necessary for decision-making capacity. I then examine the current legal framework, demonstrating that a consideration of whether capacity can and should be restored is not currently accommodated within the MCA. This is followed by a discussion of how capacity restoration could be incorporated into the MCA. Here, I explore three potential points in the MCA framework at which one might consider restoring capacity, arguing that there are significant problems with all three of these possibilities. In the final section on the way forward, I consider some future directions one might take to address some of the concerns identified. The article ends with a brief conclusion.

Before I begin this analysis, two preliminary comments are in order. The first comment is terminological in nature. While this article focuses on capacity restoration, which is explored further in the next section, the fact that an individual must be presumed to have capacity according to Section 1(2) of the MCA means that prior to any determination of incapacity, there cannot be said to be any ‘restoration’ of that capacity. Thus, in any discussion involving interventions to be undergone prior to an assessment of capacity, I use the term ‘facilitation’ of capacity instead. In addition, the term ‘intervention’ is used here to refer to measures used to facilitate or restore an individual’s capacity,11 whereas the term ‘treatment’ is used to refer to measures used to address the individual’s mental illness. It should be noted, however, that there may be an overlap between these two types of measures in a specific factual scenario. In other words, there are scenarios in which a treatment for an individual’s mental illness may also act as an intervention to restore their capacity, and vice versa,12 and what would differ would only be the objective, or goal, of the measure.

The second relates to the inherent jurisdiction, as it was ‘rediscovered’ in the 2012 case of DL v A Local Authority.13 This jurisdiction similarly deals with cases in which the individual is thought to be ‘incapacitated’, although importantly, not as a result of mental disorder or impairment. As Munby J explained in the case of Re SA14:

…the inherent jurisdiction can be exercised in relation to a vulnerable adult who, even if not incapacitated by mental disorder or mental illness, is, or is reasonably believed to be, either (i) under constraint or (ii) subject to coercion or undue influence or (iii) for some other reason deprived of the capacity to make the relevant decision, or disabled from making a free choice, or incapacitated or disabled from giving or expressing a real and genuine consent. (paragraph 77)

While there are often overlaps between the two jurisdictions,15 it should be noted that this article specifically focuses on the MCA framework for capacity assessments and best interests determinations, and therefore will not involve an exploration of cases in the inherent jurisdiction. I turn now to the analysis that is the focus of this article.

II. Capacity-restoring interventions

Prior to an examination of how capacity restoration may be accommodated within the MCA, it is first necessary to elucidate not just what capacity-restoring interventions are, but also how they purport to restore capacity. In essence, such interventions are ones that are able to restore, or at least significantly repair, the abilities that are required to demonstrate decision-making capacity under the law.

Section 2 of the MCA defines a person who, due to their mental impairment, lacks capacity in relation to a matter as a person who is unable to make a decision for themselves in relation to the matter at the material time. As to what it means to be unable to make a decision, Section 3 provides the relevant abilities required for decision-making capacity (‘the relevant abilities’) as follows:

a) to understand the information relevant to the decision,

a) to understand the information relevant to the decision,

b) to retain that information,

b) to retain that information,

c) to use or weigh that information as part of the process of making the decision, or

c) to use or weigh that information as part of the process of making the decision, or

d) to communicate the decision.

d) to communicate the decision.

In other words, as long as the person lacks any one of the relevant abilities due to an impairment of the mind or brain, they will be treated as unable to make a decision for themselves, or lacking in decision-making capacity in relation to that decision.16 Mental disorders may have an effect on any of the relevant abilities, to varying degrees. If an intervention were able to repair, or at least improve significantly, the relevant abilities that the individual lacks, it is conceivable that the individual’s decision-making capacity, as defined by the law, might be restored by the treatment.

A straightforward example would be a person with schizophrenia who has delusions that prevent them from understanding key information relevant to the decision they need to make. For example, A is a woman who has early-stage breast cancer and has been asked to consent to a lumpectomy, or a surgery that would remove the cancerous area of the breast. As a result of her schizophrenia, however, A has delusions that she cannot be afflicted by any disease due to the possession of supernatural qualities. Because of these delusions, she is unable to comprehend the key fact that she has cancer, and thereby lacks decision-making capacity in relation to the decision to undergo the lumpectomy. In this scenario, we would need an intervention that could address A’s delusions. This might be an anti-psychotic medication, or, in cases of treatment-resistant schizophrenia, there has been some evidence of success with the use of DBS.17 If either of these interventions were to result in A’s regaining the ability to understand the information that she has cancer,18 this would mean that her decision-making capacity, as defined under the MCA, would be restored.19

Another example would be a person with anterograde amnesia, a form of amnesia that results in the loss of the ability to form new memories after a neurological event. This person would thus be unlikely to be able to retain relevant information about a treatment decision long enough to make a decision. An intervention that would enhance the person’s ability to retain or recall this information may therefore also have the effect of repairing or even restoring their decision-making capacity.20

More controversially, courts have described persons with anorexia nervosa as unable to use or weigh relevant information.21,22 Assuming that these persons genuinely lack this particular ability, interventions to restore their decision-making capacity in this context would be more complex than the examples mentioned above. This is not least because of the significant amount of variation not only in the way that the disease manifests itself, but also in terms of its underlying mechanisms. In particular, even where the outward manifestations of the disease may be similar, there may nonetheless be very different forms of disordered thinking underlying the symptoms.23 Given the differences in these underlying mechanisms, the interventions that could potentially repair or restore decision-making capacity could very well differ according to the specific mechanism at play. Park et al, for example, hypothesize that dysregulated reward processing and compulsivity may be key drivers in the persistence of anorexia nervosa,24 and as a result, ‘[n]euromodulatory techniques such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and DBS applied to targets in reward and compulsivity circuits may have potential as tools for treatment development’ in enduring presentations of the disorder.25

This section has explored what capacity-restoring interventions are and what they might look like in a few specific scenarios. Before considering the implications of such interventions, a brief note is in order. In conceptualizing the capacity-restoring intervention, it is noted that the term ‘intervention’ is used here in its loosest sense. While interventions discussed thus far can be described as medical in nature, other forms of interventions exist. In cases involving the capacity to engage in sexual relations, for example, an education programme may be mandated as an attempt to facilitate capacity prior to a determination on capacity.26,27 In addition, while the term ‘intervention’ generally connotes a positive act, it is used in this article to refer to all the ways in which an individual might be facilitated to restore their decision-making capacity, even if this merely involves waiting for the individual to regain consciousness.

III. Accommodating capacity restoration in the Mental Capacity Act 2005

I have shown in the above section that it is at least plausible that there are currently interventions, as well as developing novel applications of neurotechnologies such as DBS, that are able to target and restore some of the abilities associated with decision-making capacity as defined under the law. The question then arises as to what this means for our mental capacity law framework, since the MCA does not explicitly mention capacity restoration at any point in the process.28

A. Why capacity restoration needs to be incorporated in the MCA framework

Perhaps the first issue that needs to be addressed is why capacity restoration needs to be systematically considered as part of our mental capacity law framework at all. Here, the role of decision-making capacity in the law is relevant. It is trite that the law staunchly protects autonomous decisions, even those that may appear irrational or may result in harm to the individual or death.29 Unlike the richer conceptions of autonomy that abound in the philosophical literature, the concept of autonomy in the law is arguably a very thin conception that requires only decision-making capacity and the lack of undue influence.30 As a result, capacity is often viewed as the gatekeeper for decision-making authority as recognized by the law31: that is, if a person has decision-making capacity, they are generally permitted to decide however they wish.

The law’s protection of capacitous, and thereby autonomous, decisions is also reflected in the legislative principles of the MCA, namely (i) the presumption of capacity and (ii) the requirement that all practicable steps to help a person make a decision be taken before they are to be treated as unable to make the decision.32 Even where an individual has been assessed as lacking capacity and thus requires a best interest determination to be conducted on their behalf, the person making the determination is required to consider whether and when the individual is likely to regain capacity.33 The intention here is clearly that the relevant person should wait for the individual to regain capacity and make the decision for themselves if the regaining of capacity may occur in the near future.

Given the clear importance that the law places on autonomy (and hence capacity, its gatekeeper),34 the possibility of restoring a person’s ability to make their own decisions is something that arguably must take priority. In what way, however, should this consideration be incorporated into the MCA framework?

B. Incorporating capacity restoration into the MCA

While capacity restoration is not explicitly mentioned in the MCA framework, there are two points in the process at which the possibility of restoring capacity might be relevant: (i) prior to the determination that the person lacks the capacity to make a particular decision and (ii) the point at which the best interests assessment is made. In this section, I discuss the possibility of incorporating a capacity-restoration requirement at each of these stages. I also introduce and consider an additional, currently non-existent step in the MCA framework, one that occurs after the person has been deemed to lack capacity but prior to the best interests assessment.

a. Prior to the capacity assessment: the practicable steps requirement

Before it is determined that a person lacks the capacity to make a particular decision, Section 1(3) of the MCA, as briefly mentioned above, requires that all practicable steps to help a person make a decision be taken before they are to be treated as unable to make the decision–call this the ‘practicable steps requirement’.35 Considerations relating to restoring an individual’s capacity are of great significance here; if there is a practicable measure that can restore the individual’s ability to make the decision, it would be difficult to say that all practicable steps had been taken without first allowing the individual to undergo this measure. A key question here, therefore, is whether the measure in question can be said to be a ‘practicable step’.

The phrase ‘all practicable steps’ is not defined in the MCA, although chapter 3 of the MCA Code of Practice (Code) provides detailed guidance as to what should be done to maximize the individual’s decision-making capacity for the purposes of meeting the requirements of Section 1(3). The first half of this chapter explicates in great detail the kind of information that should be provided to the individual and how this information should be communicated, corresponding to the ability to understand information relevant to the decision. In discussing persons with specific communication or cognitive problems, which relates to the ability to communicate the decision, paragraph 3.11 provides a list of possible communication support tools, including picture boards, Makaton, visual aids, written messages, sign language, and mechanical devices such as voice synthesizers, keyboards, or other computer equipment.36

The second half discusses how the person might be put at ease when making the decision, so that their ability to make decisions might be improved.37,38 This section covers such factors as location, timing, and support from others.39 Chapter 3 ends with a discussion of several other factors to consider, in particular (i) whether psychological therapy might be useful in cases of distress or long-standing problems that affect the ability to understand and (ii) that it is important to provide access to technology so that those who can communicate better through computers and other forms of technology can do so.40

While Chapter 3 provides a detailed summary of several important factors that must be taken into account when considering the practicable steps that should be taken to facilitate a person’s decision-making capacity,41 there are two points to note here. The first is that, while psychotherapy is suggested as a way to facilitate capacity in certain circumstances, only the negative effects of medication on capacity are mentioned, with the recommendation that a decision be delayed until any side effects of medication on capacity have subsided. Given that both psychotherapy and pharmaceuticals are forms of medical treatment, it is not entirely clear why only one form is recommended as a way to facilitate capacity. Of course, an underlying, implicit consideration here might be the assumption that pharmaceutical interventions involve a form of bodily invasion in a way that psychotherapy does not, and they might thus be understood to infringe upon a right to bodily integrity42 in a way that interventions in psychotherapy do not.43 Additionally, psychotherapy is seen as an indirect intervention that involves active participation of the individual, which is thought to involve more room for rational deliberation and autonomous choice as compared to direct interventions, in which the individual is more of a passive recipient.44 No matter what the reason may be, if the Code accepts that practitioners should attempt to facilitate treatment decision capacity using psychotherapy, it would be helpful to have an account of why other interventions that could plausibly be used to achieve the same goal should not be used also.

Secondly, while technology is mentioned in Chapter 3, it is only seen as a means of communication, and no mention is given to the potential use of technology to facilitate or even restore other abilities necessary for treatment decision-making capacity. One reason for this is that patients in question may lack ‘facilitation decision-making’ capacity, that is, the capacity to consent to the use of such technologies; this may also be true of any medication used to facilitate treatment decision-making capacity. I discuss this further below. Again, however, if the Code accepts the technological facilitation of one ability necessary for capacity, it would be helpful to understand why this should not be taken to entail that technology should also be used to facilitate other abilities necessary for capacity.

As for case law, there does not appear to be a clear indication in relevant case law as to what the phrase entails. While such a question will undoubtedly be a fact-specific inquiry, which means that the formulation of a highly specific test would be difficult, many judgments do not consider whether the ‘practicable step’ requirement has been met at all. In their review of a decade of Court of Protection (CoP) cases, Ruck Keene et al found that the judge only considered what they call the ‘support principle, or whether practical steps were taken to maximize P’s capacity’, in 23 out of the 40 (or 57.5 per cent) cases in their sample.45 In cases where this issue is discussed, the focus is generally placed upon the provision of information, education, and/or aids such as visual aids or other similar tools.46,47 Interestingly, the possibility of using psychotherapy is very briefly mentioned in the case of Y CCG v KG (by his litigation friend) and others,48 although this is quickly dismissed on the basis that the individual in question had reportedly been resistant to all efforts to deliver it.49 While there was no further discussion as to why this resistance necessarily meant that psychotherapy could not be used as a ‘practicable step’, this may conceivably have been because such resistance would have rendered the psychotherapy itself ineffective. Additionally, though, the resistance may itself have indicated a lack of consent to the intervention. I discuss this further below.

Given that the available guidance does not appear to provide a straightforward answer as to whether the interventions discussed in Section 2 above, in particular, novel neurointerventions, would constitute ‘practicable steps’, how might the practicability of these interventions be assessed? In considering whether any intervention should be adopted as a ‘practicable step’, a number of factors will need to be taken into account. A key issue will be the urgency of the decision that needs to be made. Where time is of the essence, for example, where there is an urgent medical decision to be made, an intervention along the lines of waiting for the natural amelioration of the condition will likely not be considered practicable due to the time it would take to take effect. Also of importance are factors such as the cost and accessibility of the intervention, the reliability of the intervention in terms of its ability to improve capacity,50 and the risks associated with the intervention.

Even if we determine that an intervention is a practicable step to take, there remains a further issue of whether valid consent to the capacity-facilitating intervention can be obtained, given that there is no legal basis to compel an intervention at this stage.51 The issue here is that in instances where ‘practicable steps’ are needed to assist P in attaining the requisite decision-making capacity to make the treatment decision in question, and these practicable steps could include certain other medical interventions to facilitate capacity, P may be unable to consent to these other capacity-facilitating interventions by virtue of the same impairments that preclude their decision-making capacity with respect to the initial treatment decision in the first place. This will be a particularly significant issue where there is a sizeable amount of complexity involved. While it will be easier to demonstrate capacity to consent to interventions such as waiting for natural recovery or well-studied anti-psychotics, it will be much more difficult to demonstrate capacity in relation to interventions such as DBS, with its ‘necessarily experimental character’52 and complex risk profile.53,54

Thus, consideration of whether a person’s decision-making capacity to make the treatment decision might be facilitated should be a natural and arguably even crucial part of this stage (despite the unfortunate lack of explicit consideration of this principle in many CoP cases, as mentioned above). The consideration of such capacity-facilitating interventions at this stage, however, is inevitably limited by a number of factors, such as the practicability of the intervention and the availability of consent from the individual. As such, any requirement to undergo a capacity-facilitating intervention prior to an assessment of decision-making capacity to make the treatment decision would have to depend on the outcome of the weighing of these factors. In practice, such interventions would likely only be considered ‘practicable steps’ by the courts in cases where there is little risk, little urgency, high reliability, and, perhaps most importantly, where the individual is able to (and does provide) consent.55

However, given that in many cases, a patient who lacks capacity to consent to a medical intervention may also lack capacity to consent to an intervention that facilitates their capacity, it is not likely that this stage can be effectively utilized for capacity facilitation. Might there be any mechanism via which there could be a justification for performing a capacity-facilitating intervention? I turn now to the best interests assessment.

b. The point at which the best interests assessment is made

The second point in the process at which the possibility of restoring capacity might be relevant is the best interests assessment that takes place after a person has been deemed to lack capacity. In other words, the potential for an intervention to restore an individual’s decision-making abilities could be relevant to considering whether a particular decision is in their best interests.

The best interests assessment is in Section 4 of the MCA, and contains a number of factors that must be taken into account when this assessment is to be conducted. In relation to decision-making capacity, the individual conducting the best interests assessment must consider ‘(a) whether it is likely that the person will at some time have capacity in relation to the matter in question, and (b) if it appears likely that he will, when that is likely to be.’56 The individual must also, ‘so far as reasonably practicable, permit and encourage the person to participate, or to improve his ability to participate, as fully as possible in any act done for him and any decision affecting him’.57 In addition, the person’s past and present wishes and feelings, the beliefs and values that would be likely to influence their decision if they had capacity, and the other factors that they would likely consider if they were able to do so must be considered as well.58

While there is some consideration of whether the individual will regain capacity in the near future and the need to permit and encourage them to participate or improve their ability to participate in the decision, there is no explicit requirement to otherwise consider the potential for capacity restoration. Indeed, though the courts may consider these two issues,59 they do not generally explicitly consider whether there may be any interventions by which capacity may be restored, although a number of cases can be seen as an attempt to achieve this goal without explicitly stating it as the aim or rationale of the decision.

One example of this is the case of Re E (Medical Treatment—Anorexia),60 in which the court held that a young woman with anorexia nervosa, E, lacked capacity and that it would be in her best interests to be fed, even if this had to be done forcibly.61 The decision was made on the basis that this was a proportionate and necessary measure to protect E’s right to life.62 However, it is notable that the court had heard evidence that compulsory feeding was likely to help E reach a body mass index of 17, which would allow her to recover the capacity to make decisions for herself.63 As the judge explained, ‘[t]he hope is that, with refeeding, E will reach the point where her weight stabilizes at a more normal level (in the order of BMI 17) and leads her to recover the capacity to take decisions for herself.’64 Though the regaining of capacity was ultimately not explicitly included in the final balancing exercise conducted by the court,65 it is thus possible that this was on the court’s mind when the decision was made, and indeed, the return (or attempt to return) to a capacitous state would have been the outcome of this decision.

In the case of Re TS (Pacemaker),66 TS was an 81-year-old man who was held to lack capacity to consent to having a pacemaker fitted due to paranoid beliefs stemming from a delusional disorder. In what the judgment described as a ‘Catch 22 situation’,67 TS’s delusional disorder would not improve, and he would not be able to regain capacity unless he received antipsychotic medication. Yet, he could not receive certain types of medication unless he was fitted with a pacemaker due to a Mobitz type 2 heart block. Peel J decided that, based on the evidence, TS did not have capacity, and that it would be in his best interests to have the pacemaker fitted. In coming to this decision, he noted that the antipsychotic medication that TS would be able to have after the pacemaker was fitted ‘might enable him to regain capacity, or mitigate some of the delusional disorder’,68 although he recognized that there was an uncertainty on this front.69 Apart from a general reference to an improvement of TS’s psychosis and thus his quality of life, however, this reasoning was only discussed as a part of the analysis regarding whether TS lacked capacity, and not as a part of the eight points of Peel J’s decision on TS’s best interests, which formed the basis of Peel J’s view that the pacemaker’s being fitted was in TS’s best interests. The best interest points focused primarily on the likely benefits to TS’s physical and mental health, his quality of life, the risks of the procedure, and the likelihood of his consent if he were capacitous.70 Thus, although TS’s regaining of his capacity was again not explicitly included as one of the factors motivating the court’s final decision on best interests, in this decision it was clearly a consideration that the court took into account, one that appeared multiple times in the judgment.

There are also cases that could be construed as keeping an individual away from an incapacitating influence, presumably with the goal, whether explicit or not, of allowing the individual to restore their capacity. Focusing specifically on the MCA,71 an example of this can be found in a case involving a young woman with primary ovarian failure, epilepsy, and vitamin D deficiency, A, who had hormone treatment administered to her covertly over a period of several years in her best interests.72 The 5-year saga will not be discussed in full: the key decision of relevance is Moir J’s judgment in 2019.73 In this case, A’s capacity to make decisions in respect of residence, care, contact, and medical treatment for her conditions was in dispute. Evidence given by consultant psychologist Dr Ince, which Moir J generally found compelling and ultimately used to ground her findings of incapacity, indicated that even though A’s lack of capacity was directly linked to her underlying mental disorders,74 there was clear evidence that she was subject to undue influence from her mother, B.75 In relation to whether A might eventually gain the requisite capacity, Dr Ince suggested that A could potentially ‘develop skills to critically appraise information given to her’ (in other words, the ability to use and weigh relevant information), in 2 or 3 years’ time, if the undue influence were to be removed.76

As part of her decision in this case, Moir J held that A should continue to reside in residential care and be ‘cared for elsewhere than at home with B’.77 The basis for keeping A away from her mother was, again, not explicitly stated to be for the purposes of restoring capacity. Moir J noted that B appeared to exercise a continuing negative influence over A, both in terms of the fact that B did not have a supportive attitude towards A’s receiving of support, care, and treatment, and the fact that she did not appear motivated to support A in developing a sense of her own identity separate from that of her mother.78 Due to what Moir J described as their ‘enmeshed relationship’,79 this influence would need time to diminish, and A needed to be apart from B in order to have ‘the opportunity to experience life as an independent adult with proper support’.80 Even though, like the cases above, the restoration of A’s capacity was not explicitly included as the basis for the court’s decision, the importance and approval that Moir J gave Dr Ince’s evidence, and the emphasis she placed on B’s undue influence over A, make it possible that this was indeed a consideration the court took into account.

Thus, these cases can all be said to have as a potential outcome the regaining of each individual’s capacity. Since the stated aims of the interventions in these cases are varied and infrequently refer specifically to the regaining or maintaining of capacity as a reason or goal motivating the final decision, however, it cannot be said that capacity restoration has been incorporated consistently into the court’s consideration of such cases.

(i) Is capacity restoration in an individual’s best interests?

In terms of whether a consideration of whether capacity can and should be restored would fit well at this stage of the process, the first question is whether capacity restoration is necessarily in a person’s best interests. In other words, how might capacity restoration be beneficial for the individual?

A key benefit relates to the promotion of the individual’s autonomy—that is, the restoration of the individual’s decision-making capacity is thought to be related to the regaining of their ability for autonomous decision-making. To establish this, however, there are two different conceptual questions that need to be addressed. The first is whether the restoration of capacity (as construed by the abilities required in the MCA capacity test) necessarily entails the restoration of the ability to make autonomous decisions. This will depend on the conception of autonomy being employed. While a full exploration of the various conceptions of autonomy is beyond the scope of this article, it is noted that the standard view of autonomy operative in the law can be said to closely resemble Beauchamp and Childress’ understanding of the concept,81 in other words, what Coggon refers to as ‘current desire autonomy’.82 Both accounts do not require any kind of higher-level reflection or any other authenticity requirement, and, in the absence of any undue external influence, decision-making capacity holds a salient role as gatekeeper in determining whom we allow decision-making authority.83 In other words, unless the facts raise questions of undue external influence,84 the restoration of the individual’s decision-making capacity should entail the regaining of their ability to make decisions autonomously in the eyes of the law.

For most philosophical conceptions of autonomy, however, in particular those that include an authenticity requirement,85 the simple restoration of capacity as it is understood by the MCA may be necessary but not sufficient for regaining the ability to make truly autonomous decisions. In particular, this may be a concern in cases where the capacity-restoring intervention raises concerns about potential changes in personality or values.86 A complete discussion of this scenario is also outside this article’s scope, but in such a case, whether or not capacity restoration is beneficial for the individual is less clear, given that they may no longer be able to make decisions in accordance with their ‘true’ self.

Assuming that restoring capacity can allow the individual to regain the ability to make autonomous decisions, the second question is whether regaining this ability would be beneficial for them. In other words, to what extent is the ability to make autonomous decisions in a person’s interests? While this raises deep philosophical questions about the value of autonomy that I can only briefly address here, there is a significant philosophical tradition that would answer this question affirmatively. More specifically, this broad idea can be sourced from Mill’s understanding of the relationship between individuality and well-being. For Mill, part of the explanation for why states should not interfere with individual liberty is that the individual often has a unique epistemic insight into what will make their life go well.87 More fundamentally, Mill’s view was that there is a deeper prudential value in living in accordance with one’s own mode of being.88

(ii) How should capacity restoration be weighed in the best interests assessment?

Though we have now established that capacity restoration could plausibly be in the individual’s best interests, it is much less clear what weight this interest in our own autonomous agency should have in an overall assessment of what is in a person’s best interests. To parse the issue in concrete terms, while it is clear that individuals have a strong interest in being autonomous, what of a scenario in which we might predictably restore a person’s capacity to make an autonomous decision that is harmful to themselves? This might be where a severely depressed individual has their capacity restored, merely to regain the motivation and energy to take their own life or otherwise engage in severe, self-harming behaviours. Can the decision to restore the individual’s autonomy be in the person’s best interests in such a scenario? If we assume the sort of procedural understanding of autonomy that is implicit in the MCA, the answer to this question will ultimately depend on the extent and nature of autonomy’s value, how broadly we interpret the concept of well-being, or best interests,89 and the role autonomy plays in what constitutes our best interests.

A narrow interpretation of well-being, for example, might limit it to medical/physical benefits and healing, or at least treat this goal as being of particular priority compared to other benefits.90 Under such an interpretation, actions that would be directly contrary to the goal of healing would unlikely ever be in an individual’s interests, contrary to a broader interpretation of well-being that would include other conceivable benefits, and not give any of these benefits priority over others.91 A view that adopts this narrower interpretation of well-being would likely see autonomy and well-being as being conceptually distinct,92 and conflicts between one’s autonomy and well-being would likely arise more frequently. In the scenario above, for example, the restoration of the individual’s autonomy in order to commit self-harming behaviours would demonstrate a clear conflict between satisfying the individual’s autonomous preference and an understanding of well-being that prioritizes medical/physical benefits. Accordingly, the restoration of this individual’s autonomy would unlikely be in their best interests on narrow conceptions of well-being (despite the individual having some interest in autonomous agency).

In contrast, if a broad interpretation of well-being were to be used, where the relative weight assigned to the various benefits would be determined by the individual, the self-harming behaviours could, for example, conceivably be a way for the individual to achieve a sense of control over their own life, something that they valued above preventing harm to their body. In this way, the restoration of autonomy, even if it led to physical harm to the individual, could conceivably still be in the individual’s best interests. Thus, the extent to which restoring autonomy is in someone’s best interests will inevitably depend on how the concept of well-being is interpreted and understood.

In the specific context of the law, it must first be clarified that for individuals who are assessed as having capacity (and not under undue influence), the extent to which the law concerns itself with the individual’s well-being is limited to non-interference with their autonomous choices. This means that the law accepts that individuals with capacity have the right to make autonomous choices, even if these appear contrary to their well-being. Individuals who are assessed as lacking capacity under the MCA, however, will have decisions made in their best interests. While best interests was historically conceived primarily in terms of medical best interests,93 the determination of what is in the individual’s best interests has since expanded into more of a general welfare assessment, taking into account a range of social, personal, and ethical considerations,94 culminating in the 2013 Supreme Court decision in Aintree University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust v James,95 where Lady Hale said the following96:

The most that can be said, therefore, is that in considering the best interests of this particular patient at this particular time, decision-makers must look at his welfare in the widest sense, not just medical but social and psychological; they must consider the nature of the medical treatment in question, what it involves and its prospects of success; they must consider what the outcome of that treatment for the patient is likely to be; they must try and put themselves in the place of the individual patient and ask what his attitude to the treatment is or would be likely to be; and they must consult others who are looking after him or interested in his welfare, in particular for their view of what his attitude would be…

Insofar as it is possible to ascertain the patient’s wishes and feelings, his beliefs and values or the things which were important to him, it is those which should be taken into account because they are a component in making the choice which is right for him as an individual human being.

These quotes demonstrate clearly the court’s view that, in best interests assessments for individuals without capacity, their best interests are to be considered in the widest sense, which includes not only benefits beyond the medical, but, importantly, those from the perspective of the individual. The individual’s own wishes and values are affirmed as a component in deciding what is in their best interests, which supports a broader understanding of well-being that includes a range of benefits with no particular ranking of these benefits except for the individual’s own.97

Though it is thus clear that the law adopts a broad interpretation of best interests, this does not translate into clarity in relation to how the courts are likely to decide in cases where a capacity-restoring intervention is available. In particular, even assuming that capacity restoration and autonomous agency are in a person’s interests, there remains the question as to how this should be factored into the overall assessment of the person’s best interests. In other words, the benefits of capacity restoration would still have to be weighed holistically with all the other risks and benefits relevant to this decision. This would include, for example, considering whether there may be a greater risk in attempting to restore P’s capacity than there would be in carrying out the treatment in question. Of course, where the capacity-restoring intervention is medically of low risk and complexity, and where there is a prior capacitous wish to prioritize one’s ability to make decisions for oneself (or at least clear information about the importance of autonomy and decision-making authority to that individual), the outcome will likely be straightforwardly in favour of proceeding with the capacity-restoring intervention.

In other situations, however, the answer will be less clear. Where, for example, the treatment in question is considered by the healthcare team to be highly beneficial to the individual and less risky than the capacity-restoring intervention, and where there is no evidence of a clear capacitous wish on the part of P to preserve capacity and autonomy, the individual’s interests in their autonomy and decision-making authority will likely take lower priority. In such a scenario, the court will unlikely come to the decision that undergoing the capacity-restoring intervention would be in P’s best interests as a whole. Additionally, there is the issue of the individual’s non-capacitous current wishes. Specifically, what if the incapacitous individual expresses a strong desire not to undergo the capacity-restoring intervention? Even if such wishes are non-autonomous, it may still be in their overall best interests to take them into account.

Thus, while the best interests assessment potentially gives us a way of incorporating a requirement to restore capacity, this approach is problematic for at least two reasons, both of which relate to the lack of primacy that autonomy is given. The first concern stems from the fact that this approach is beset with uncertainty. Though the law is clear that best interests should be interpreted widely, taking into account the individual’s own perspective, there remain questions regarding the role the individual’s autonomy interests should play in the overall assessment of their best interests. As discussed above, this is particularly the case where it is thought that the individual might harm themselves if they regain capacity. Once the ability to restore the person’s capacity is placed among the factors being considered at the best interests assessment stage, the fact that capacity restoration could potentially lead to a harmful decision that would not be in the person’s best interests would inevitably have to be taken into account in some way. In this way, the priority that respect for autonomous choice has over acting in an individual’s interests that is baked into capacity’s gatekeeping role, or what Pugh has termed the ‘anti-hard paternalist claim’,98 would be turned entirely on its head. More concretely, where an individual has capacity, they are recognized in the eyes of the law as having the authority to make decisions even if they are harmful. Yet, because this entire exercise has been incorporated into the wider best interests assessment, its outcome is likely to be precisely the opposite, with their interests in autonomous decision-making—something the law would have rigorously protected—being put aside in the name of their best interests.

The second reason relates more generally to the need to weigh all relevant factors in the best interests assessment exercise holistically. This will likely mean that, even in cases where there is no concern that the individual will make a harmful decision, their autonomy interests will still likely be given lower priority whenever the treatment or decision in question is of high benefit and low risk.

So how might we ensure the individual’s autonomy interests are sufficiently prioritized, in light of the importance that the law affords autonomy? Below, I consider a third stage at which we might consider capacity restoration. This approach involves the creation of a separate stage after the determination of incapacity and prior to the best interests assessment, which is devoted entirely to the consideration of capacity restoration and its autonomy-promoting outcomes. The rationale for this separate stage would be based on the idea that, if there is an intervention that can restore the individual’s ability to make their own decisions, this must be exhausted prior to any consideration of what might be done in their best interests as a whole. This separate stage would thus allow the harms and benefits of the capacity-restoring intervention and the treatment intervention in question to be considered separately.

c. A separate stage prior to the best interests assessment

Some support for the creation of this separate stage can be inferred from the law’s staunch protection of decisions made with capacity, as discussed above. Given that autonomy considerations are of primary importance in English law (where the individual is capacitous), and that they come above objective considerations of welfare, restoring the individual to a position in which they are capable of making the decision for themselves, where this is possible, would seem to be preferable to making the decision on their behalf.

Another reason in favour of the creation of this separate stage is the simple fact that we often will not be able to know what P would have chosen had they been able to make that decision themselves. Would they have prioritized their ability to make the decision autonomously, or would they have prioritized other welfare considerations, such as any beneficial outcomes the treatment in question is expected to produce? Because of this uncertainty, the argument here would be that we should, if possible, leave the choice to the individuals themselves by restoring their capacity to make such a choice.

Adding this new, intermediate stage to consider capacity restoration is not without its challenges, however. The difficulty with conceptualizing capacity restoration as distinct from the best interests assessment is that the imposition of unwanted interventions on individuals without capacity traditionally falls into the sphere of substitute decision-making in their best interests. If the capacity-restoring procedure is to be imposed as a separate step, there is a question as to whether there is a proper legal basis for doing so, and what kind of exercise needs to be undertaken in considering whether to impose this intervention.

The MCA, in its current form, makes it clear that any decisions made by the CoP on behalf of a person who has been declared to lack capacity have to be made in their best interests. Section 1 of the MCA, which contains principles that underlie the application of the MCA, states that ‘an act done, or decision made… for or on behalf of a person who lacks capacity must be done, or made, in his best interests’.99 Section 16(3) confirms that the powers of the CoP to make decisions and appoint deputies are subject to the MCA principles in Section 1, as well as Section 4 on best interests.

This means that any decisions made on the individual’s behalf must be made in their best interests, even if the decision is made outside of the best interests assessment. That would mean that the creation of this separate stage would also need to be guided by the individual’s best interests. What would be different, however, is how this assessment would be carried out. As discussed above, one of the problems with the best interests assessment is that it requires us to make a holistic assessment of best interests. This is problematic, not least because of the way autonomy interests could easily be outweighed by broader beneficence considerations. While the separate stage would also be beholden to the principle of best interests, it would presumably not be beholden to a holistic interpretation of best interests. Instead, we might understand best interests in this intermediate stage to refer solely to the individual’s interests in being autonomous.

This would likely be a highly fact-sensitive inquiry, which would consider such matters as: (i) the individual’s impairment; (ii) the potential of any intervention to aid or restore capacity and thereby allow the individual to regain autonomy; (iii) the urgency of the decision to be made and the time it would take for the intervention to take effect; (iv) to what extent the intervention is associated with risks or harms to autonomy, for example in terms of the irreversibility or hazardousness of the procedure; and (v) whether there is any evidence of prior capacitous wishes or advance decisions to restore capacity should the means to do so become available. While P’s current, non-capacitous wishes may be relevant for the overall best interests assessment, they would not be relevant for this particular exercise, as these wishes would not be considered autonomous.

A potential problem with this approach would be a situation in which the potential risks and harms associated with the capacity-restoring intervention are greater than those associated with the treatment itself. For example, consider a patient who has borderline capacity to consent to a medical intervention that is of low risk and great potential benefit, and potentially even wanted by P (although the likelihood of such a case reaching the CoP in that kind of situation is low). Are there strong reasons to take measures to restore capacity here? Assuming that this is not an entirely academic question, considering this at the best interests assessment would presumably not be particularly problematic because a holistic balancing act of the competing variables can be performed. At this separate stage, however, the separation of the capacity-restoration question from an overall welfare assessment would mean that the courts are not able to balance the risks, harms, and benefits of capacity restoration with those of the treatment decision in question. In other words, while this separate stage avoids the difficult questions associated with the role of autonomy interests in an overall assessment of an individual’s best interests, it does so at the cost of potentially giving autonomy absolute priority over considerations of well-being. This, in turn, may result in highly counterintuitive implications. For example, P may be required to undergo a complex, risky intervention such as DBS in order to restore their capacity to decide whether to undergo a low-risk, highly beneficial intervention such as anti-psychotic medication. Or, pushed to the very extreme, P may be required to undergo this capacity-restoring intervention simply to make their own decision as to what they would like for dessert.

Another potential problem with this approach is that any risks and harms to P would only be considered in relation to P’s interests in being autonomous, rather than P’s well-being more generally. In other words, the exercise would not be able to take into account any risks and harms to P that did not involve P’s autonomy in some way. So, for example, if the capacity-restoring intervention came with a risk of death or serious injury, this could be construed as a risk of harm to P’s autonomy, given that these outcomes would mean either significant or complete loss of autonomy. If, however, the harm to P was in the form of extreme discomfort of some kind that did not entail any substantial loss of autonomy but was nonetheless very distressing to P, this would not necessarily weigh very much in the balancing of autonomy-focused factors at this stage.

Ultimately, the question we must ask is whether the individual should be allowed to, or asked to, make their own choices in all decisions (regardless of the risk and complexity involved), or whether there should be a threshold that must be reached before such capacity-restoring interventions are considered. This question does not have a straightforward answer. Those who consider it problematic for others to be arbiters of the kinds of decisions that one should be facilitated or allowed to make will likely be of the view that the only relevant question is how risky or harmful the capacity-restoring intervention is. Those who are of the view that proportionality is inevitably involved and that a balancing exercise is thus necessary would likely support a more holistic approach instead.

IV. The way forward—a hybrid approach?

The analysis above has proposed three potential stages in the MCA process at which capacity restoration can be considered, and has concluded that there are significant problems at each of these stages. What does this mean for how we should proceed?

In thinking about the way forward, we will need an approach that has both a sufficient emphasis on P’s interests in autonomous decision-making, but also at least some consideration of the effects of any capacity-restoring intervention on P’s well-being, in terms of the potential for significant distress, as well as the possibility of highly counterintuitive outcomes. To achieve this, perhaps some combination of the approaches explored above will be necessary. In this section, I sketch out some preliminary thoughts for such a hybrid approach.100 While further research will be needed to develop and elaborate upon this more fully, it is hoped that these thoughts will provide some direction for further thinking on this matter in the future.

This hybrid approach would see the exercise conducted in two stages. Once it is determined that there is indeed a method to restore P’s capacity, a Stage 1 assessment would be conducted to examine whether the intervention should be undergone at all. This stage would largely resemble the separate stage discussed in Section “A separate stage prior to the best interests assessment” above, and would thus primarily be driven by autonomy interests. In other words, the best course of action would be determined principally by reference to the preservation and/or promotion of the individual’s autonomy.

Unlike the separate stage approach, however, where best interests are only assessed in relation to autonomy interests, Stage 1 would contain one important caveat, or exemption. The capacity-restoring intervention should proceed where a balancing of the autonomy-based factors weighs in favour of P undergoing the intervention, unless doing so would result in significant suffering for P. This consideration of suffering would unavoidably have to go beyond P’s interests in being autonomous. It would be for the courts to work out the parameters of this concept of significant suffering, but it would conceivably be broad enough to take into account both physical and psychological suffering. P’s non-capacitous current wishes, while not relevant to an autonomy-based exercise, may potentially be relevant here: if P is strongly against the intervention and proceeding against their wishes would bring them great suffering, this may be enough to constitute significant suffering. It is also important that the suffering be significant—a de minimis threshold is crucial to ensure that autonomy interests are sufficiently prioritized in this exercise.

Assuming the result of the Stage 1 assessment is that P should undergo the capacity-restoring intervention, this would not be the end of the matter—a Stage 2 assessment would then be necessary under this hybrid approach. This assessment would specifically consider whether undergoing the intervention would be a proportionate measure in light of the decision that is to be made. This stage would be crucial to prevent the highly counterintuitive outcomes discussed above. It would be patently absurd, for example, to suggest that P should undergo any kind of medical intervention, much less a complex or risky one, in order to make a decision for themselves about, say, an after-dinner dessert. Some proportionality to the substantive content of the decision to be made is necessary, relative to the costs of the capacity-restoring intervention that would enable them to make the decision on their own.

Ultimately, therefore, a two-stage approach like this would necessarily introduce some welfare considerations into the assessment exercise. Despite the imperative to promote autonomy as discussed in Section “A separate stage prior to the best interests assessment” above, this would still seem to be the preferable alternative, compared to the counterintuitive outcomes that a solely autonomy-focused approach may entail. This is not to say, however, that any and all welfare considerations are legitimate to consider in this exercise. In designing any such approach for the way forward, therefore, the biggest challenge is arguably the delimiting of the kinds of welfare considerations that can or should be taken into account and weighed against P’s interests in making autonomous decisions. A clear instance of a welfare consideration that should not be relevant, for example, would be the likely subsequent outcomes of P’s autonomous decisions once P regains capacity. And this would be rightly so, given the law’s staunch protection of capacitous decisions, even those that may result in death or other types of harm.101

Significant suffering, on the other hand, seems to be a relevant welfare consideration that should be taken into account. Loose parallels might be drawn here with the previous Section 62 of the Mental Health Act 1983 as amended (MHA). By way of some brief background, Section 57 of the MHA provides that for surgical operations that destroy brain tissue or the function of brain tissue, the individual must provide capacitous consent even though the individual has been compulsorily admitted into hospital for treatment.102 In four specific circumstances, however, Section 62, which regulates urgent treatment, provides that this is not necessary. These circumstances include where these interventions are:

i) immediately necessary to save the patient’s life;

i) immediately necessary to save the patient’s life;

ii) (if not reversible) immediately necessary to prevent a serious deterioration of the patient’s condition; or

ii) (if not reversible) immediately necessary to prevent a serious deterioration of the patient’s condition; or

iii) (if not irreversible or hazardous) immediately necessary to alleviate serious suffering by the patient; or

iii) (if not irreversible or hazardous) immediately necessary to alleviate serious suffering by the patient; or

iv) (if not irreversible or hazardous) immediately necessary and represents the minimum interference necessary to prevent the patient from behaving violently or being a danger to themselves or to others.

iv) (if not irreversible or hazardous) immediately necessary and represents the minimum interference necessary to prevent the patient from behaving violently or being a danger to themselves or to others.

The context of this provision is admittedly entirely different from the discussion at hand, not least because compulsory admission and treatment generally involves individuals with capacity. The parallel that might be drawn here, however, is that the MHA, in its previous form, lent some support to the notion that the alleviation of serious suffering can constitute a sufficiently powerful welfare consideration to act as a limit to the idea that the individual’s own autonomous decision-making is required to legitimize treatment. The difficulty here, of course, is why this should be the case, simply because the person has a mental disorder and poses a risk of harm to themselves or others. Discussions about parity of esteem, while very important, are outside of the scope of this discussion, but it is crucial to mention that the new Mental Health Act 2025 has removed this particular circumstance from Section 62 to support the idea that individuals with capacity should be given the right to decide the amount of suffering they are willing to accept103; that this should be within the sphere of their autonomous decision-making. While this development is to be commended, it does not negatively impact the argument being made here. This is because the context of this article involves individuals who lack capacity and, therefore, do not have the autonomy to decide the degree of suffering they are willing to withstand. What stays the same, however, is the fact that significant suffering remains a compelling welfare consideration that cannot be disregarded when considering whether capacity should be restored.104

Prior to concluding this analysis, I would be remiss if I did not address one key issue. While the focus of this article is on the MCA framework, the significance of the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) looms large over a topic like this. An in-depth exploration is beyond this article’s scope, and is a worthy subject for future research, but I will share brief reflections here. The CRPD, which came into force in May 2008, is an international human rights treaty with a ‘bold new vision’, where non-discrimination is made a core value and disability is conceptualized not as individual limitations or impairments but as the result of inadequate social responses to the needs of certain individuals in society.105 The treaty-monitoring body for the CRPD, the Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, has taken the strong position that any legal regime allowing substitute decision-making on grounds that include the presence of a mental disorder or disability can never be consistent with Article 12 of the CRPD.106 Instead, substitute decision-making regimes are to be replaced by supported decision-making regimes entirely (rather than developed in tandem).107

While the majority of interventions explored in Section ‘Capacity-restoring interventions’ above, in particular novel neuro-interventions, are not currently envisaged and discussed in the literature on supported decision-making,108 providing support to facilitate the individual in making legally recognized decisions for themselves should be of distinct priority. This emphasis on supported decision-making, however, does not necessarily support the use of capacity-restoring interventions. Assuming that the individual is already able to communicate their views and wishes in a reasonably ascertainable manner, advocates of the strong position are likely to be of the view that such views and wishes should be respected, and that any requirement to consider whether capacity can and should be restored is merely demonstrative of an approach that discriminates against those who have not reached a certain level of mental capacity.109 In cases where the individual is unable to communicate their wishes, but where such wishes could potentially be made reasonably ascertainable via the use of capacity-restoring interventions, there may be a stronger case to require capacity restoration prior to substitute decision-making being employed.110

V. Conclusion

The rapid development of neurotechnologies such as DBS in recent years has added new possibilities to the existing range of interventions that can restore, or at least repair, the abilities required for decision-making capacity as defined under the MCA. There is, however, currently no formal requirement in the MCA to consider at any stage whether P’s capacity can be restored, and courts have not explicitly considered this issue, even though there are suggestions that this was the court’s unstated goal in certain cases. I have argued that, given the law’s emphasis on protecting autonomous decision-making, the fact that a proper role has not been given to the consideration of whether capacity can and should be restored in the MCA framework is of significant concern. And, with the inevitable eventual emergence of neurotechnologies that can achieve capacity restoration, this gap is an issue that the law will need to address with haste.

In considering how capacity restoration may be systematically incorporated into the MCA framework, I have examined three potential stages at which a requirement for capacity facilitation or restoration might be built in. Each of these stages, however, is beset with challenges of varying gravity. The discussion of the three potential stages leaves us with the need to devise an approach that places sufficient priority on the restoration of the individual’s ability to make decisions, but also one that gives some consideration to compelling welfare considerations such as the potential to cause significant suffering to the individual, and whether the capacity-restoring intervention is proportional to the treatment decision to be made, so as to prevent highly counterintuitive outcomes. In light of these considerations, I sketched a preliminary proposal for a two-stage hybrid approach. While further research will be required in the future to flesh out the details of how such an approach will be designed and implemented, this represents one potential way forward to address the current gaps in the law.

Such education programmes can also facilitate capacity to make other decisions; see, for example, the case of Re CLF (Capacity: Sexual Relations and Contraception) [2024] EWCOP 11 where the judge only made an interim declaration on CLF’s capacity to make decisions about the use of contraception because there was evidence that a focused educational programme could lead to CLF gaining capacity in this area (para 47).

This kind of intervention may facilitate capacity by a number of different mechanisms. For example, education programmes may lead to an increase in the individual’s ability to retain the relevant information through repeated practice, as well as their ability to use or weigh the relevant material by engaging them in scenario-based learning that allows them to practice this ability in a simulated context.
