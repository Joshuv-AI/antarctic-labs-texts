Two cases (a decade apart) of severe sulfonylurea-positive hypoglycemia associated with inhaled heroin use
Authors: Brittany Weisbrot, Keun Young Kim, Christine Zacharia, Jessica L Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypoglycemic disorders in nondiabetic patients are rare. In the setting of concomitant illicit opioid abuse, hypoglycemia is highly unusual, and an underlying cause should be systematically investigated. We report 2 unique admissions (separated by a decade in time) to the intensive care unit for critical hypoglycemia secondary unintentional sulfonylurea exposure after heroin use. A thorough history, including illicit drug use and critical blood samples at the time of hypoglycemia, was obtained. These subjects had a common exposure: insufflation of heroin mixed with an additive shortly before their admissions with critical hypoglycemia and testing positive for unknown sulfonylurea exposure. A review of the literature was performed for cases of illicit drugs adulterated with sulfonylureas. The diagnosis and management of sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycemia are discussed. One case of sulfonylurea-positive hypoglycemia associated with inhalation of heroin was previously presented as a poster presentation at the Endocrine Society. Within 3 months of this presentation, the second case was seen. These cases highlight the importance of obtaining a detailed clinical history, acquiring timely laboratory samples for toxicologic analysis, interpreting the findings, and sharing cases like these with trainees in many disciplines to curb a potentially deadly epidemic.

Introduction

Hypoglycemic disorders in nondiabetics are rare. We report 2 separate cases of sulfonylurea poisoning in the setting of heroin inhalation admitted to the intensive care unit. These cases highlight the importance of obtaining a thorough clinical history, including potential illicit drug use, acquiring timely laboratory samples for toxicologic analysis, interpreting the findings, and teaching trainees in all disciplines about these cases to curb a potentially deadly epidemic.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 61-year-old nondiabetic female with a history of substance abuse (heroin, cocaine) was found by the paramedics at home with a decreased level of responsiveness. She endorsed insufflating heroin, cocaine, and substantial amounts of alcohol approximately 1 to 2 days prior to admission at a party. She endorsed 2 similar episodes of hypoglycemia in the past requiring hospitalization after heroin use but said she was incompletely evaluated due to leaving the emergency department (ED) before a complete workup and failing to follow up. She had no history of weight loss, appetite change, seizures, nausea, or vomiting. She had never been on antihyperglycemic agents.

Case 2

A 69-year-old nondiabetic male with a history of heroin abuse and hypertension presented to the ED for altered mental status. He was found unresponsive by his girlfriend with “seizure-like” whole body movements. His girlfriend called emergency medical services. Upon detailed history taking, the patient reported inhaling heroin that he had purchased from a new dealer 1 to 2 days prior to developing new-onset hypoglycemia. He denied previous episodes of hypoglycemia, taking any medications that induce hypoglycemia, or living with anyone with diabetes.

Diagnostic assessment

Both patients demonstrated Whipple's triad at the time surrounding their hypoglycemia diagnosis. Both patients endorsed using heroin in the time preceding the admission for critical hypoglycemia. Because of their altered mentation on arrival, there could be some inaccuracy in their perception of exact timing. Case 1 also endorsed using alcohol, which is a known risk factor for hypoglycemia.

In the field, blood sugar for case 1 was <30 mg/dL [SI: <1.7 mmol/L; reference range, 60-109 mg/dL (SI: 3.3-6.0 mmol/L)]. Blood sugar for case 2 in the field was 37 mg/dL (SI: 2.06 mmol/L). Kidney, liver, and adrenal functions were normal in both subjects.

Critical blood samples, including a sulfonylurea screen, were obtained for both patients during episodes of hypoglycemia (Table 1). Both patients had inappropriately normal to elevated insulin and C-peptide levels in the setting of a serum glucose <55 mg/dL (SI: <3.1 mmol/L). The sulfonylurea screen was positive in both individuals.

Treatment

Case 1

The patient received 25 grams of dextrose intravenously for a blood glucose level of <30 mg/dL (SI: <1.7 mmol/L) with some improvement in mental status. She was stabilized and taken to the ED. Due to the fluctuating nature of her glucose, she required dextrose IV fluids and medical intensive care unit (MICU) for monitoring. After 24 hours in the MICU, blood sugars stabilized, and no further treatment was necessary.

Case 2

The patient received 25 grams of dextrose intravenously for a blood glucose level of 20 mg/dL and was transferred to the ED. In the ED, the patient's mental status improved to his baseline. Vital signs were notable for a blood pressure of 229/109 mmHg, but the patient was otherwise stable. Blood glucose level normalized to 99 mg/dL (SI: 5.5 mmol/L). After monitoring in the ED, he was discharged home. Seven hours later, he represented to the ED via emergency medical services for severe hypoglycemia with altered mental status. He received 25 g of dextrose intravenously. Repeat blood glucose levels showed levels persistently in the 30 to 40 mg/dL (SI: 1.7-2.2 mmol/L) range. The patient was admitted to the MICU for further monitoring. The patient was treated with subcutaneous octreotide 50 mg injections every 6 hours for 24 hours while on continuous dextrose IV fluids. After 24 hours in the MICU, blood glucose levels remained normal, and continuous IV fluids and octreotide were discontinued.

Outcome and follow-up

Both patients were informed that they tested positive for sulfonylureas, and the implications of this were explained. Drug samples from the heroin that was insufflated were not available for analysis. The poison control center in Chicago was contacted regarding these cases.

Discussion

Hypoglycemia, defined as a plasma glucose of < 55 mg/dL (SI: <3.1 mmol/L), is a rare finding in subjects with no history of diabetes [1].

Initial workup for hypoglycemia of unknown etiology requires systematically obtaining a sample at the time of hypoglycemia consisting of plasma glucose, insulin, C-peptide, proinsulin, and β-hydroxybutyrate levels [1]. Screening for oral hypoglycemic agents and insulin antibodies should also be obtained. If other clinical clues suggest possible adrenocortical disease (ie, autoimmune disease, glucocorticoid exposure, hemodynamic instability, etc.), obtaining a cortisol level is also essential.

Both of our cases were endogenous/insulin-mediated hypoglycemia based on the Endocrine Society Guidelines for hypoglycemia, as both of our subjects were admitted with symptomatic hypoglycemia with plasma concentrations of glucose less than 55 mg/dL (SI: < 3.1 mmol/L), insulin of at least 3.0 μU/mL (SI: 2 pmol/L), C-peptide of at least 0.6 ng/mL(0.2 nmol/L), and proinsulin of at least 5.0 pmol/L [1]. Upon administration of IV glucagon, β-hydroxybutyrate less than 2.7 mmol/L and an increase of plasma glucose of at least 25 mg/dL (SI: >1.4 mmol/L) also suggest an insulin-mediated etiology of hypoglycemia. The differential diagnosis for endogenous hyperinsulinism with hypoglycemia consists of either sulfonylurea intoxication or rare cases of insulinoma [2]. Without a positive sulfonylurea screen, patients are typically subjected to a 72-hour fast and possibly subsequent imaging to localize an insulin-producing mass. Thus, these cases highlight the importance of obtaining the sulfonylurea screen at the first mention of hypoglycemia of unknown etiology.

Because of their nondiabetic status, compelling history, and results of diagnostic workup, we conclude that these subjects had a common exposure: insufflation of heroin contaminated with the sulfonylurea glipizide shortly before admission with critical hypoglycemia. Several cases have reported unintentional sulfonylurea intoxication associated with the use of recreational drugs [3-6], including sulfonylurea being sold as “street valium” [3-5] and cocaine [6]. To our knowledge, this is the first report of hypoglycemia related to inhalation of heroin adulterated with the sulfonylurea glipizide.

Sulfonylureas belong to the class of hypoglycemic agents that bind to the adenosine triphosphate-sensitive potassium channel, causing channel closure, β-cell membrane depolarization, and downstream insulin secretion. Traditionally, these are oral agents, although successful transdermal delivery of glipizide in mice has been reported [7, 8]. The absorption of sulfonylureas across the lung has not been formally studied; however, numerous studies have demonstrated that many small molecules have high systemic bioavailability and fast absorption rates following inhalation [9]. There has been 1 reported case from 1993 of inadvertent glibenclamide poisoning by inhalation involving an employee working at a pharmaceutical factory, but there was no confirmatory testing performed [10].

Management of hypoglycemia secondary to sulfonylurea poisoning consists of blood glucose measurements every 30 to 60 minutes and infusion of glucose as needed. Depending on the patient's renal function and sulfonylurea dose consumed, hypoglycemia can persist for 24 to 48 hours, so admission to the intensive care unit for frequent blood glucose monitoring is often required [11]. Patients must be kept euglycemic, as IV administration of glucose in the setting of sulfonylurea ingestion can cause exaggerated insulin release [12]. Glucagon has historically been used, but caution should be exercised due to its potential to exacerbate the hyperinsulinemic state. Diazoxide, which opens potassium channels, thereby reducing insulin secretion, has also been in limited use due to an extensive side-effect profile [11]. In cases of severe, refractory hypoglycemia, octreotide (a somatostatin analog that inhibits insulin secretion) can be used as adjunctive therapy, as seen in our second patient [13, 14].

Evidence suggests that illicit drugs are often adulterated with benign substances to enhance the illicit drug effect, facilitate administration, and increase profits by increasing the volume of the drug sold [15]. In a 2004 review, Maxwell et al found that between 2005 and 2007, 22 patients presented to the ED with heroin overdose relating to inhaling “cheese,” containing approximately only 5% black tar heroin, 5% diphenhydramine, and 90% acetaminophen [16].

Commonly prescribed medications have reportedly been swapped for street drugs if they have a similar appearance or intended effect [4]. Hepburn et al studied symptoms associated with acute hypoglycemia in controls and insulin-dependent diabetics and classified these as either autonomic or neuroglycopenic [17]. In the literature, sulfonylureas have been surreptitiously administered for both effects. Lung et al reported a case of glyburide successfully being sold as “street valium” because the hypoglycemic effect of sulfonylureas mimics clinical sedation achieved by benzodiazepines [4]. In 2008, an outbreak of hypoglycemia among nondiabetic South Asian males occurred that was eventually attributed to ingestion of Power-1 Walnut pills, marketed to those desiring enhanced sexual function. This pill contained 2 prescription drugs: sildenafil and toxicological doses of glibenclamide, a second-generation sulfonylurea [18].

Could hypoglycemia in heroin overdose be underreported? Boyd et al retrospectively studied the effect of cardiopulmonary resuscitation in heroin overdose patients and analyzed characteristics of survivors. After drug overdose and successful resuscitation, hypoglycemia was measured in 4 subjects, which was unexpected, and the etiology was unknown [19]. In the setting of heroin abuse, hypoglycemia, concurrent seizures, or persistent somnolence despite naloxone administration should not be attributed to opioid overdose but merits a thorough diagnostic workup.

Do subsets of the opioid-dependent population have undiagnosed insulin resistance where sulfonylurea administration could be unrecognized? Dysregulated glycemic control in heroin-dependent populations has been studied with conflicting results [20]. Reed et al compared the results of 50-g oral glucose tolerance test in 12 heroin-dependent subjects vs 12 age-, sex-, and weight-matched normal controls and found that the heroin users had high basal insulin levels and delayed peak response to an oral glucose load [21]. Ceriello et al subsequently demonstrated that hemoglobin A1c was significantly different between 27 male heroin addicts (7.18%) and 46 age- and sex-matched controls (6.11%), with no difference in fasting plasma glucose or hemoglobin levels [22]. A cross-sectional study compared metabolic profiles of 70 heroin-dependent men with 70 men who had been abstinent from heroin for more than 1 month and 100 age-, sex-, and body mass index-matched controls. Fasting blood sugar was significantly lower in ex-heroin users [69.5 ± 2.9 mg/dL (SI: 3.86 ± 0.16 mmol/L)] than in controls [86.7 ± 1.2 mg/dL (SI: 4.82 ± 0.07 mmol/L)] or heroin-dependent men [81.6 ± 1.75 mg/dL (SI: 4.53 ± 0.10 mmol/L)] [23].

Further studies to determine the incidence of hypoglycemia in opioid overdose and the prevalence of undiagnosed insulin resistance in this population could elucidate this matter.
