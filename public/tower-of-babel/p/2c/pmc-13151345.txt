Circulating miR-378a-3p attenuates pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis via BAT-lung crosstalk
Authors: Rui He, Xuemin Peng, Ruping Pan, Qianqian Kang, Shulin Liu, Min Yang, Zhihan Wang, Hongyan Deng, Danpei Li, Yuyu Xie, Wenshe Wang, Zengzhe Zhu, Jiadai Liu, Jing Ge, Yulian Liu, Ronghui Gao, Yan Yang, Yong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) arises from persistent fibroblast activation and inflammation. Although microRNAs (miRNAs) are promising antifibrotic agents, the contribution of adipose-derived circulating miRNAs to PF remains unclear. Here, we identify brown adipose tissue (BAT) as a major source of exosomal miR-378a-3p that counteracts bleomycin (BLM)-induced PF. In fibrotic mice, BAT activity and circulating exosomal miR-378a-3p were reduced. Activating BAT by cold exposure or β3-adrenergic stimulation increased circulating and pulmonary miR-378a-3p and attenuated collagen deposition and inflammatory infiltration. Adipocyte-specific deletion of miR-378a-3p exacerbated lung inflammation and fibrosis, whereas lung-targeted overexpression of miR-378a-3p or intravenous delivery of BAT-derived exosomes enriched for miR-378a-3p ameliorated disease. Inhibition of exosome release from BAT lowered circulating miR-378a-3p and blunted the anti-fibrotic benefits of BAT activation, supporting a BAT-to-lung transfer mechanism. Mechanistically, miR-378a-3p targets Itga5 to suppress FAK-PI3K-AKT signaling and limiting fibroblast activation, while simultaneously suppressing macrophage inflammatory responses by targeting Fstl1 and suppressing NF-κB activation. Collectively, these findings identify miR-378a-3p as a BAT-derived signaling molecule with dual anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects in pulmonary fibrosis, expanding the pathophysiological scope of BAT-mediated inter-organ communication to lung disease.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04296-3.

Introduction

Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a chronic and progressive interstitial lung disease, including idiopathic PF and forms arising after severe viral pneumonia such as Covid-19-induced PF [1]. Pathologically, PF features persistent activation of myofibroblasts and excessive extracellular-matrix (ECM) deposition, which remodel the lung and ultimately lead to respiratory failure. [1–4] Dysregulated inflammation sustains this process: pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic cytokines, and chemokines, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, and monocyte chemotactic protein (MCP1)/CCL2, that can trigger and maintain myofibroblasts activation. [5] Immune cells are recruited to and infiltrate the fibrotic lung. [5] Depleting alveolar macrophages (AMs) has been shown to alleviate PF progression in experimental models. [6] Although the nintedanib and pirfenidone have been approved for the treatment of PF, [7] they fail to stop the disease progression of PF, [8, 9] underscoring the need for new targets and modalities.

MicroRNAs have emerged as versatile regulators with anti-fibrotic potential in multiple organs, including the lung [10–12]. MicroRNAs are small non-coding RNAs that repress translation or promoting degradation of target mRNA by binding to the 3′-untranslated region (3′-UTR).[13–15] Beyond their intracellular roles, miRNAs can be packaged into exosomes and other extracellular vehicles (EVs), secreted into the extracellular space, [11, 16, 17] and taken up by proximate or distant recipient cells to reprogram cellular functions [18–22]. In PF, for example, miR-21 expression is elevated in fibroblasts and amplifies TGF-β1 signaling [23, 24]. In tissue samples from IPF patients, IL4 and IL13 modulate macrophage profibrogenic program via miR-142-5p and miR-130a-3p. [25] In addition, macrophages-derived exosomal miRNAs act on alveolar epithelial cells and fibroblasts to regulate the progression of PF through a paracrine mechanism [26, 27]. These studies elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which miRNAs regulate the signaling cascade in PF and establish miRNAs as key cytokines in maintaining lung tissue homeostasis. However, to date, the contribution of circulating exosomal to PF pathogenesis remains poorly defined.

Adipose tissue functions not only as an energy reservoir, but also an endocrine organ that can release adipokines [28, 29]. Brown adipose tissue (BAT) is a thermogenic depot with endocrine outputs that communicate with distant organs including brain, pancreas, liver, and bone to regulate systemic physiology [30–35]. For instance, BAT-secreted Maresin 2 (MaR2) reduces inflammation in obesity by targeting macrophages in liver [36]. BAT-derived neuregulin-4 (Nrg4) ameliorates atherosclerosis in mice [37]. Notably, BAT can secrete miRNA-containing exosomes to the extracellular space [32], and BAT activation reshapes the circulating miRNA landscape [32]. BAT transplantation into ADicer KO mouse identified BAT as a significant source of circulating miR-99b that affect hepatic fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) expression and glucose homeostasis [32]. Activated BAT-derived mir-378a-3p has also been shown to regulated systemic glucose metabolism by stimulating hepatic gluconeogenesis [38]. These studies raise the possibility that BAT-secreted exosomal miRNAs mediate inter-organ crosstalk beyond classical metabolic targets.

In this study, we explored whether BAT-derived exosomal miRNAs participate in lung fibrosis and delineated the underlying mechanisms. We found that BAT activation confers anti-fibrotic benefits in bleomycin (BLM)-induced PF, accompanied by increased levels of circulating and pulmonary miR-378a-3p. Furthermore, BAT-derived exosomal miR-378a-3p can be transported to the lung, exerting anti-fibrotic effect by inhibiting inflammation response, recruitment of immune cells, and myofibroblast activation. Mechanistically, miR-378a-3p directly targeted Fstl1 mRNA to restrain NF-κB signaling in macrophages and targeted Itga5 mRNA to attenuate FAK-PI3K-AKT signaling in fibroblasts, thereby reducing cytokine production, immune-cell recruitment, and myofibroblast differentiation. In conclusion, we identified a previously unrecognized BAT-lung axis mediated by BAT-derived exosomal miR-378a-3p protects mice against PF through its dual anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects, and BAT acts as an endocrine organ to regulate the pathophysiological function of lung tissue.

Materials and methods

Animals

All the animal experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Animal Experiments of Tongji Hospital, affiliated with Huazhong University of Science &Technology. Wild type (WT) C57BL/6J mice (812weeks old) were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. The miR-378AdipoKO and miRNA378flox/flox mice were purchased from Cyagen Co., Ltd. The Rab27a KO mice were purchased from Cyagen Co., Ltd. All mice were kept in specific-pathogen-free (SPF) environment, housed at 23 °C with free access to water and chow under a 12-hour light/dark cycle.

For the PF model, mice were subjected to a single nasal instillation of bleomycin (BLM) (4 mg/kg body weight, MedChemExpress Co., Ltd.). The mice were euthanized via CO2 inhalation on day 21 post-BLM injury. For cold exposure, mice were individually housed at either 8–18 °C for 7 days and were sacrificed at either day 7 or day 21. In studies involving CL316,243 (4 mg/kg body weight, Sigma), mice received continuous subcutaneous injection for 7 days beginning on day 1 and were euthanized by CO2 inhalation on either day 7 or day 21. To inhibit exosome production and secretion, BAT was locally injected with GW4869 (3 mg/kg body weight, twice a week, Sigma) for 3 weeks. For in vivo trafficking of BAT-derived exosomes, exosomes were labeled with the PKH26 fluorescent dye (Sigma) and injected into mice via tail vein injection (30 µg/kg body weight, twice a week). The distribution and visualization of the PKH26-labeled exosomes were monitored using an imaging system.

Cell culture

For the culture of brown adipocytes, interscapular BAT was isolated from 2 weeks old male C57BL/6J mice. The BAT was carefully dissected into small pieces and digested in Hank’s solution containing 0.02% type I collagenase (Biosharp) for 40 min at 37 °C. The stromal vascular fractions (SVFs) were filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and then centrifuged at 1400 rpm for 5 min. The resulting cell pellets were resuspended in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)-F12 (Gibco), with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) (Gibco).

The SVFs were then infected with a retrovirus expressing the SV40 large T antigen for immortalization once the cells reached approximately 70% confluence. Immortalized brown adipocytes were then treated with an induction cocktail to promote differentiation. When the cells reached 100% confluence, they were cultured in induction medium containing T3 (1 nM, Sigma), rosiglitazone (0.5 µM, Sigma), insulin (5 µg/mL, no. I5523, Sigma), 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX) (0.5 mM, no. I7018, Sigma), dexamethasone (2 µg/mL, Sigma), and indomethacin (0.125 mM, Sigma). After 2 days, the culture medium was replaced with a maintenance cocktail containing T3 (1 nM), rosiglitazone (0.5 µM), and insulin (5 µg/mL) until the medium was collected on the eighth day for exosome isolation.

Mouse macrophage cell lines (RAW264.7) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% P/S (Gibco). Human fetal lung fibroblast cell lines (MRC-5) were cultured in MEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% P/S (Gibco).

Cell treatment

For cytokines release, LPS/γ-IFN (100 ng/ml, 10 ng/ml) were added to culture medium for 24 h in RAW264.7. After incubation, they were collected for qPCR assay. For myofibroblasts activation, TGF-β1 (2 ng/ml) were added in the medium of MRC-5 for 48 h. For exosomes functional analysis in RAW264.7, BAT-Exos (20 µg/mL) were co-incubated with cells for 12 h following 12 h LPS/γ-IFN treatment. For exosomes functional analysis in MRC-5, BAT-Exos (30 µg/mL) were co-incubated with cells for 24 h after 24 h TGF-β1 treatment. After incubation, the cells were collected for qPCR assay. For the uptake of BAT-Exos in vitro, PKH26-labeled exosomes were incubated with cells for 6 h.

For visualization of exosomes distribution, phalloidin (fluorescein isothiocyanate labeled, 50 ng/mL, Beyotime), used for visualizing the F-actin, was incubated with cells for 15 min according to the the manufacturer’s protocol. Then, nuclei were counter-stained using DAPI (4′,6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride, 10 ng/mL, Beyotime). Images were captured using Microscope System.

Cell transfection

MRC-5/Macrophages were transfected with the cy3-labelled mimic-miR-378a-3p、siRNA、plasmid and miR-378a-3p inhibitor (RiboBio) using the transfection reagent (Neofect Biotech Co., Ltd.), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Scrambled mimic-miRNA was used as a negative control. The cells were transfected for 24 h, subsequently conducting the indicated studies.

Exosomes collection and characterization

The isolation of BAT-derived exosomes was performed using ultracentrifugation. To collect BAT-derived exosomes, interscapular BAT was cut into small pieces and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 2% exosome-free FBS, 0.2 mg/ml RNase A, and 1% (P/S) in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. Exosomes were isolated from the culture supernatants using a differential centrifugation method. Briefly, the BAT supernatants were centrifuged at 300 × g for 15 min, 2000 × g for 15 min, 10,000 × g for 30 min to to remove tissue debris and large vesicles. The resulting supernatant was then ultracentrifuged at 110,000 × g for 90 min. All procedures were carried out at 4 °C. The pelleted exosomes were resuspended in PBS or DMEM and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments. For serum exosome isolation, an exosome isolation reagent (Invitrogen) was employed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The morphology of the isolated exosomes was characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), while the size distribution of the exosomes was analyzed through nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Protein concentrations of the exosomes were quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific). Additionally, western blotting (WB) was conducted to detect specific markers of the exosomes.

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) injection

To overexpress miR-378a-3p in the lungs, AAV serotype 8 miR-378a-3p (AAV-378) was slowly administrated into the lung via intranasal administration (50 µl of 1 × 109 Vg/µl, two times a week) for 2 weeks, as the schema indicated. The miR-378a-3p adenoviruses were provided by Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Western blot analysis

Cell and tissue lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer (MCE) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (MCE). The protein concentration was quantified using BCA protein assay kits (BOSTER). All procedures were performed on ice to maintain protein integrity.

For Western blotting, each sample (30 µg of protein) was subjected to SDS-PAGE and then transferred to a PVDF membrane. After blocking the membranes with 5% BSA (Sigma) for 1 h, primary and secondary antibodies were incubated with the membranes overnight at 4 °C or for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were visualized using a Gelview 6000 Pro (BLT Technology) with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) Western Blotting Substrate (MCE) and quantified using ImageJ software. The antibodies utilized for Western blotting are listed in Table 1.

The preparation of cDNA was performed by reverse transcription with HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme), and each sample was performed in triplicate. The expression levels of target genes were normalized to the housekeeping genes expression. The primers sequence used for qPCR are listed in Table 2.

PET studies

For PET scans, after fasting for 8 h, mice were anaesthetized, and then [18] F-FDG was injected into mice via the tail vein. For PET quantification, SUV values of BAT were measured in accordance with the BAT images. The mice [18] F-FDG PET studies were performed using a Micro-PET/CT system.

Histology

The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight and embedded in paraffin. After slicing, 4 μm sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) or Masson’s trichrome. The images were obtained by the microscope and quantified using ImageJ software. The Ashcroft scoring system, ranging from 0 to 8, was utilized to evaluate the extent of lung fibrosis, with higher scores indicating more severe fibrosis.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm thickness. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), tissues were incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, and then incubation with secondary antibodies for 20 min. After staining with horseradish peroxide (HRP)-conjugated streptavidin for 20 min, the sections were developed with 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution. The images were viewed using a microscope.

For immunofluorescence (IF), after incubation with bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.3% Triton X-100, cells or tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, and then stained with secondary antibodies. Then, DAPI were used to counter-stain nuclei. Images were captured by a Microscope.

Hydroxyproline assay

Hydroxyproline was measured to quantify the contents of collagen in the lung. The assay was performed using the hydroxyproline assay kit (Nanjing Jian Cheng Bioengineering Institute) and following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Elisa

The levels of IL-6 in both serum samples from mice models and cell culture medium were assessed using commercially ELISA assay kits(Universal Biotech Co., Ltd). The ELISA assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The analysis of absorbance at 490 nm was measured by a microplate reader.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

For the validation of miR-378a-3p target genes, the 3’UTR sequences of FSTL1 and ITGA5 and their mutated versions were constructed in the downstream of the Renilla luciferase gene. The dual luciferase reporter assay was performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The reporter plasmids and miR-326 mimic or negative mimic control were co-transfected into 293T cells. After 48 h of transfection, the luciferase activities were detected with a dual luciferase assay kit (Beyotime) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

MicroRNA sequencing

In total, the serum samples isolated from indicated group mice were used for the high-throughput miRNA sequencing analysis. The miRNA sequencing libraries were prepared by MGI Tech Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). The sequencing analysis was performed using the BGISEQ-500 platform provided by the BGI Genomics, Shenzhen, China. Given that the isolation of sufficient quantities of high-quality exosomal RNA necessitates pooling serum samples from multiple mice, a small sample size (n = 2) was used for exosomal miRNA sequencing analysis. Thus, this analysis is not intended to serve as a definitive assessment of differential expression.

Statistics

All data were presented as the mean ± sem. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001, ns, not significant. Statistical analysis was assessed by two-tailed Student’s t test and One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test using Prism software version 8.4.3 (GraphPad Software).

Results

Pulmonary fibrosis reduces BAT activity and circulating exosomal miR-378a-3p

To investigate the role of circulation miRNAs in PF, we established PF mouse model via BLM-treatment (Fig. 1A). Serum exosomal miRNA sequencing revealed a distinct profile between the control group and BLM group (Fig. 1B). Moreover, we also profiled serum exosomal miRNA with differential BAT activity of BAT induced by room temperature (RT, 25 °C) versus cold exposure (8 °C) conditions for 7 days. Among these candidate, miR-378a-3p was the only miRNA that exhibited a consistent and directionally concordant expression pattern across both conditions, suggesting its potential origin from BAT and involvement in this regulatory pathway (Fig. 1C-D). Public dataset GSE 41,306 further indicated miR-378a-3p was increased after cold exposure in BAT (Fig. 1E). These results indicated that BAT contributes to circulating exosomal miR-378a-3p and that BAT activity may be linked to lung injury.

To verify the relationship between BAT and PF development, we performed [18] F-Fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, which is used to measure BAT activity in vivo [39] 21 days after BLM. BAT [18] F-FDG uptake was reduced in PF mice (Fig. 1F), consistent with decreased enlarged lipid droplets on BAT histology (Fig. 1G-H), and uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) protein (Fig. 1I). Accordingly, miR-378a-3p level in BAT and serum were markedly lower in PF mice than in control mice, whereas lung levels showed no significant difference between groups (Fig. 1J). Interesting, it has been reported that BAT can modulate the functions of distant tissues via serum exosomal miRNAs [32]. These data are consistent with suppressed BAT activity and reduced circulating exosomal miR-378a-3p in PF, supporting the hypothesis that BAT is a major source of circulating miR-378a-3p and motivating the idea that BAT may influence PF progression via an endocrine route.

Activating BAT attenuates fibrosis and inflammation in vivo

To test whether enhancing BAT activity mitigates PF, mice received BLM and were then subjected to RT (25 °C) or cold (18 °C) for 21 days, respectively (Fig. 2A). Cold exposure increased BAT activity in BLM-induced PF mice, evidenced by a denser appearance on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 2B, S1A) and higher UCP1 on protein level (Fig. 2C), compared to the PF mice at RT. Of note, lung injury and fibrosis were attenuated in cold exposure group: H&E staining showed less architectural distortion, Masson’s staining revealed reduced collagen, and hydroxyproline content was lower (Fig. 2D-E). Consistently, fibrotic markers (Col1 and α-SMA) were elevated in PF lungs versus healthy controls but were blunted by cold exposure, as shown by western blot (WB) (Fig. 2F-G) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) (Fig. 2H, S1B-C). Inflammatory readouts were also improved: pulmonary transcripts of chemokine CCL2 (MCP-1), pro-inflammatory factors (Il-6, Il-1β, and Tnf-α), pro-fibrotic factors (Tgf-β, Il-4), and macrophages marker (CD68), were decreased in lung of PF mice after cold exposure, compared to the PF mice at RT (Fig. 2I), accompanied by reduced circulating IL-6 levels (Fig. 2J). The levels of inflammatory cytokines reflect the extent of immune cell infiltration and the intensity of inflammatory response [40], which suggested that the attenuation of fibrosis in PF mice with activated BAT was partly due to enhanced anti-inflammatory effects. Taken together, our results suggested that BAT activation ameliorated BLM-induced PF progression in mice, at least in part by dampening lung inflammation and collagen deposition. To further determine whether PF progression is directly influenced by BAT, we performed surgical removal of the interscapular BAT (Fig. 2K). Compared with sham-operated controls, BAT-ablated mice exhibited more severe lung fibrotic injury, increased inflammatory infiltration and tissue remodeling, significantly elevated protein, and gene expression of α-SMA and COL1, and markedly higher levels of released inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 2L-P).

Given these benefits under cold exposure, we next used CL316,243 (a selective β3-adrenergic agonist) to pharmacologically activate BAT. BLM mice received CL316,243 for 7 days, as shown in the schematic diagram (Figure S1D), and then evaluating the severity of PF on the day 7 and day 21, respectively. CL316,243 increased UCP1 expression in BAT at day 7 (Figure S1E). Fibrotic markers (COL1 and α-SMA) did not differ between CL316,243 treated and untreated BLM mice at day 7 (Figure S1F-G). At day 7, inflammatory infiltration was prominent in BLM lungs but was reduced by CL316,243, with no overt tissue remodeling or collagen deposition yet. By day 21, CL316,243 alleviated fibrosis and inflammation: less septal thickening and immune-cell infiltration and reduced collagen on H&E (Figure S1H-I). Immunofluorescence revealed fewer F4/80+ macrophages and neutrophils in the CL316,243 group, consistent with attenuation of BLM-induced immune-cell accumulation (Figure S1J-L). At day 21, both fibrotic markers (COL1 and α-SMA) on protein levels and inflammatory cytokines mRNAs were decreased with CL316,243 administration (Figure S1M-N). In addition to ameliorating the phenotypic features of PF, we also found that CL treatment mitigated BLM-induced body weight loss in PF mice, further implicating BAT activation as a protective mechanism against PF (Figure S1O). To further confirm the anti-PF effect of activated BAT, we also evaluated the protective effect of BAT activation by CL in a mouse PF model induced by a high dose of BLM. As shown in Figure S1P, CL administration significantly improved the survival rate of mice after the potent dose of BLM (Figure S1P).

Taken together, these findings further show that β3-adrenergic activation of BAT recapitulates the anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects of cold exposure in BLM-induced PF.

Activated BAT releases miR-378a-3p–enriched exosomes that appear in circulation and lung

We hypothesized that BAT activation protects against PF by secreting exosomal miR-378a-3p that reaches the lungs. To evaluate this, we isolated exosomes from the BAT of mice housed at room temperature (RT, 25 °C) or in a cold environment (8 °C) (Fig. 3A). Exosomes were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 3B) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) (Fig. 3C). Immunoblotting showed enrichment of the exosomal tetraspanins CD63 and CD81 and the absence of the ER protein GRP94, a negative EV marker. In addition, the apoptotic marker Annexin V (A‑V) was detectable in BAT tissue but absent from the isolated vesicle fraction, arguing against siificant contamination by apoptotic bodies (Fig. 3D). Notably, with equal BAT tissue input to isolation, exosomal markers TSG101 and CD63 were higher in BAT-derived exosomes (BATexos) from cold-exposed mice compared to RT controls (Fig. 3E), indicating increased vesicle releases with BAT activation.

We next quantified miR-378a-3p across tissues. In cold-treated mice, cold-exposure increased miR-378a-3p in BAT, serum, and lung (Fig. 3F-G). Crucially, primary miR-378a-3p (pri-miR-378a-3p) rose in the BAT but not in lung (Fig. 3F), supporting production in BAT with transfer to the lung rather than local transcriptional up-regulation in lung. Using an alternative cold protocol (18 °C), we again observed higher miR-378a-3p in BAT/serum/lung and higher pri-miR-378a-3p only in BAT (Fig. 3H-K). Pharmacological activation of BAT with CL316,243 for 7 days yielded a similar pattern: on day 7, miR-378a-3p increased in BAT, serum, and lung (with pri-miR-378a-3p elevated only in BAT), whereas by day 21 these increases were not sustained (Fig. 3L-O).

Collectively, these data suggest that BAT activation increases exosome release and raises miR-378a-3p levels in the circulation and lung of PF mice, supporting a BAT-to-lung exosomal route that contributes to anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects in vivo. They further suggest that BAT is a major source of circulating exosomal miR-378a-3p, although other tissues may contribute.

Circulating exosome counts in serum were higher in BATexos-treated mice (Fig. 4C), and miR-378a-3p level increased in both the lung and serum (Fig. 4D-E).

Given the anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects observed with BAT activation, we next examined cellular uptake of alveolar macrophages (AMs) and fibroblasts in the lung. Immunofluorescence showed PKH26 signal within α-SMA positive fibroblasts and F4/80 positive in AMs, indicating that about 6% of macrophages and 3% of fibroblasts had taken up exosomes, demonstrating effective uptake of BAT-derived exosomes by these lung cell populations (Fig. 4F). Functionally, BATexos treatment reduced BLM-induced collagen deposition in the lung (Fig. 4G-I, S2D), lowered Col1 and α-SMA at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 4J-K) and decreased inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 4L). These gain-of-function data indicate that exogenous BATexos are sufficient to ameliorate PF phenotypes.

To test necessity, we inhibited exosome biogenesis/release in BAT using GW4869. As indicated in Fig. 4M, mice received local BAT injections of GW4869 or vehicle, followed by cold exposure (18 °C) in BLM model. GW4869 lowered circulating exosomes (Fig. 4N), and reduced miR-378a-3p in serum and lung (Fig. 4O-P), consistent with BAT-derived exosomal delivery of this miRNA. PF outcomes worsened under GW4869: collagen deposition increased on morphology (Fig. 4Q, S2F), Col1 and α-SMA mRNA level were higher (Fig. 4R), inflammatory cytokines rose (Fig. 4S), and lung hydroxyproline content increased (Fig. 4T).

In addition to using GW4869 to inhibit exosome secretion, we employed a Rab27a knockout (KO) mouse model to further validate the necessity of BAT-derived exosomes for the protective effects of BAT, as shown in Figure S2F. Rab27a deficiency impairs the secretion of exosomes from BAT, resulting in reduced exosome levels in the blood and lung tissues (Figure S2G-I). Consequently, the protective effect of cold-stimulated BAT against pulmonary fibrosis was significantly attenuated (Figure S2J-L). These findings collectively indicate that exosomes mediate the anti-fibrotic actions of BAT. Collectively, these gain and loss of function experiments support a model in which BAT-derived exosomes are key mediators of the protective effects of BAT activation in PF, contributing materially to reduced inflammation and fibrosis in vivo.

MiR-378a-3p restrains fibroblast activation by directly targeting Itga5 and dampening FAK-PI3K-AKT

Exosomes collected from brown adipocytes conditional medium were added to MRC-5 cells pre-stimulated with TGF-β1 (2 ng/ml, 24 h), a standard inducer of fibroblast activation and ECM production.41 Exosomes were pre-labeled with PKH26, fluorescence staining revealed that the exosomes (red) were observed in the cytoplasm of phalloidin-labeled cells (green), indicating their uptake by fibroblasts (Fig. 6B). And the exosome uptake rates of the fibroblasts were approximately 4% (Figure S4A). BATexos treatment reducted α-SMA and Col1expression at the mRNA level (Fig. 6C) and attenuated the fibrotic phenotype on immunofluorescence (Fig. 6D, S4B). Transwell and wound healing assays showed that BATexos treatment reduced the migratory ability of fibroblasts (Fig. 6E) and inhibited fibroblast proliferation, as demonstrated by the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 6F). These findings suggest that BATexos inhibits TGF-β1-induced fibroblast activation in vitro.

To further investigate whether miR-378a-3p mediates these effects, we transfected MRC-5 cells with a Cy3-labeled miR-378a-3p mimic (10 nM) (Fig. 6G). MiR-378a-3p mimic transfection decreased the expression of fibrotic markers (Fig. 6H-I, S4C), and inhibited migration and proliferation (Fig. 6J-K), consistent with the BATexos results. These results indicate that miR-378a-3p can suppress fibrosis by inhibiting the activation of fibroblasts.

Bioinformatic analysis identified Integrin alpha-5 (ITGA5), a member of the integrin family, is a crucial regulator in the development of various fibrotic diseases through its downstream FAK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathways.42 It was identified as a potential target of miR-378a-3p (TargetScan, http://www.targetscan.org) (Fig. 6L). Overexpression of miR-378a-3p in fibroblasts lowered ITGA5 at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6M-O). A dual luciferase assay using wild-type (WT) and mutant (Mut) ITGA5 3’ UTRs confirmed direct targeting: miR-378a-3p mimics reduced the luciferase activity of the WT but not Mut reporter activity (Fig. 6P-Q). We further validated these findings in lung fibroblasts. Knockdown of ITGA5 significantly suppressed fibroblast activation, migration, and proliferation, closely phenocopying the anti‑fibrotic effect observed following transfection with the miR‑378a‑3p mimic (Figure S4F-H). Conversely, inhibition of miR‑378a‑3p enhanced activation, migration, and proliferation (Figure S4I-K). These data further demonstrate that miR‑378a‑3p suppresses fibrotic responses in fibroblasts through functional targeting of ITGA5.

ITGA5 is involved in the FAK/PI3K/AKT pathway associated with fibroblast activation42 (Figs. 6R). Consistent with ITGA5 repression, Overexpression of miR-378a-3p decreased the phosphorylation levels of FAK, PI3K, and AKT in fibroblasts compared to the control group (Figs. 6S, S4D). Conversely, inhibiting exosome release with GW4869 in increased of ITGA5 expression and elevated the phosphorylation of FAK, PI3K, and AKT in lungs of PF mice (Fig. 6T, S4E). To further validate the regulatory relationship between ITGA5 and miR‑378a‑3p, we examined ITGA5-associated signaling under both in vivo and in vitro conditions. In fibroblasts treated with BAT-derived exosomes, as well as in mice treated with CL316,243, we both observed a significant reduction in ITGA5 expression, accompanied by decreased activation of downstream FAK/PI3K/AKT signaling (Figure S4L-N). These results recapitulated the phenotypic effects observed following miR-378a-3p mimic transfection, further supporting the physiological relevance of this regulatory axis. Collectively, these findings strongly support ITGA5 as a physiologically relevant downstream target of miR‑378a‑3p.

To further determine whether the anti-fibrotic effect of cold exposure is dependent on, macrophage-mediated inflammation, lung macrophages were selectively depleted by intratracheal administration of clodronate‑encapsulated liposomes (Figure S5A). Cell counting confirmed an approximately 70% reduction in alveolar macrophages (Figure S5B-D). Under condition of macrophage depletion, BLM‑induced PF mice were subjected to cold stimulation. Compared with the room‑temperature control group, cold‑stimulated mice still exhibited reduced collagen deposition, with an overall attenuation in PF phenotype, despite the marked reduction of macrophages (Figure S5E-G). These results indicate that the anti‑fibrotic effect mediated by BAT activation is not entirely dependent on its anti‑inflammatory action, but rather involves macrophage-independent mechanisms that directly suppress the fibrotic response in the lung.

Overall, these data show that BAT-derived exosomal miR-378a-3p limits fibroblast activation by directly targeting ITGA5 and dampening downstream FAK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

To test whether miR-378a-3p mediates this effect, we transfected RAW264.7 with a Cy3-labeled miR-378a-3p mimic (Fig. 7D). The mimic decreased Tnf-α and Il-6 transcripts (Fig. 7E) and lowered IL-6 level in the transfected group by ELISA (Fig. 7F), indicating that miR-378a-3p suppresses macrophage cytokine output.

Bioinformatic analysis identified Follistatin-like protein 1 (FSTL1), a pro-inflammatory factor [43], as a candidate target of miR-378a-3p (TargetScan, http://www.targetscan.org) (Fig. 7G). Overexpression of miR-378a-3p suppressed both mRNA and protein levels of FSTL1 in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 7H-I). Dual luciferase assay with wild-type and mutant FSTL1 3’UTR reporters confirmed direct binding, as miR-378a-3p suppressed the wild-type but not the mutant reporter (Fig. 7J-K). The NF-κB pathway, a major driver of pro-inflammatory gene expression, is involved in FSTL1-mediated macrophage inflammation [43]. Consistent with FSTL1 repression, miR-378a-3p decreased NF-κB activation in RAW264.7 macrophages, whereas conditions that lowered exosome release in vivo (GW4869) increased FSTL1 and phosphorylation of NF-κB in PF lungs (Fig. 7L-N, S6B-C). Similarly, FSTL1 expression was elevated in lung of miR-378a-3p KO mice compared to control mice, as indicated by immunofluorescence (Fig. 7O, S6D). These data suggest a miR-378a-3p/FSTL1/NF-κB axis that limits macrophage inflammation.

Moreover, we performed a series of rescue experiments to further validate the functional relationship between miR-378a-3p and FSTL1. Macrophages overexpressing the FSTL1 plasmid showed increased expression of inflammatory genes, which contrasted with the anti‑inflammatory phenotype observed in macrophages transfected with the miR‑378a‑3p mimic. Notably, in macrophages co‑transfected with the miR‑378a‑3p mimic and the FSTL1‑overexpressing plasmid, the anti‑inflammatory effect mediated by miR‑378a‑3p was significantly reversed (Fig. 7P-Q). These findings confirm that FSTL1 functions as a downstream effector of miR-378a-3p in regulating macrophage inflammatory responses. Consistently, knockdown of FSTL1 led to markedly reduced levels of inflammatory genes in macrophages, whereas subsequent re-expression of FSTL1 in these cells restored inflammatory gene expression (Fig. 7R-S). Together, these gain- and loss-of-function rescue experiments establish a causal link whereby miR-378a-3p modulates macrophage-mediated inflammation through functional targeting of FSTL1.

Multiple delivery strategies of miR-378a-3p attenuate bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis

To evaluate the therapeutic relevance of miR-378a-3p, we first overexpressed miR-378a-3p in the lung via intranasal adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8) (Fig. 8A, S7A). AAV-378 reduced BLM-induced fibrosis and inflammatory infiltration on H&E and Masson staining (Fig. 8B-C), decreased COL1 and α-SMA protein levels (Fig. 8D, S7B), accompanied by decreased expression of inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 8E). Mechanistically, AAV-378 decreased ITGA5 expression together with reduced phosphorylation of FAK, PI3K, and AKT in lung tissue (Fig. 8F, S7C-D), consistent with inhibition of fibroblast activation. In parallel, FSTL1 expression and NF-κB phosphorylation were also markedly reduced in lung (Fig. 8G, S7E), indicating attenuation of macrophage-associated inflammatory signaling. Collectively, these data show that miR-378a-3p in lung mitigates the inflammatory signaling by directly targeting FSTL1 and restraining NF-κB, and that lung-targeted overexpression of miR-378a-3p ameliorates fibrosis and inflammation in vivo.

To further explore an alternative delivery strategy, we constructed PEI-PLGA nanoparticles encapsulating miR-378a-3p and performed in vivo validation experiments (Fig. 8H). The TEM and NTA verified successful nanoparticles formation and appropriate size distribution (Fig. 8I-J). Administration of miR-378a-3p-loaded nanoparticles significantly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and collagen deposition in lung tissues compared with the control group, implying their potential as a therapeutic strategy for PF (Fig. 8K-M). Additionally, no liver tissue damage was found, indicating the nanoparticles have no hepatotoxicity and thus higher feasibility for clinical application (Fig. 8N).

Finally, to specifically validate the role of miR-378a-3p as a BAT-derived signaling molecule, we generated adipose‑specific miR‑378a‑3p knockout mice and performed local delivery of AAV8‑miR‑378a‑3p into BAT to restore the expression of this miRNA specifically in BAT (Figure S7F), thereby further validating the role of miR‑378a‑3p as a BAT‑derived signaling molecule in regulating PF. The rescue results showed that, compared with control AAV injection, BAT-restricted restoration of miR‑378a‑3p significantly increased miR‑378a‑3p levels in BAT (Figure S7G) and concomitantly alleviated lung tissue remodeling, immune infiltration, and fibrosis (Figure S7H-K). These data further support a functional role for BAT‑derived miR‑378a‑3p in modulating PF progression within this protective axis.

Discussion

In this study, we identify a protective role of activated BAT on BLM-induced lung injury and fibrosis. BAT activation elevates circulating and pulmonary miR-378a-3p via exosomal transfer and mitigates both inflammation and fibrogenesis. Mechanistically, miR-378a-3p restrains macrophage inflammatory signaling by targeting the FSTL1/NF-κB axis and reduces fibroblast activation by targeting the ITGA5/FAK/PI3K/AKT pathway. These findings provide BAT-lung crosstalk mediated by exosomal miR-378a-3p suggest a therapeutic opportunity for pulmonary fibrosis.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are emerging regulators in fibrotic diseases [12, 44]. Prior studies have proposed miRNA signatures as candidate biomarkers and modulators of renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis [45], and pro-inflammatory miR-155 has been reported in macrophage-fibroblast communication during lung fibrosis [32]. Our work extends this concept by linking an adipose endocrine output to lung remodeling. Adipose tissue contributes to the circulating exosomal miRNA pool and BAT activation remodels circulating miRNA profiles [32]. Given the recognized endocrine communication from BAT to distant organs [33] our data add the lung to this network and position miR-378a-3p as a candidate mediator of this inter-organ signaling.

We observed that miR-378a-3p was consistently reduced in conditions of BLM -induced PF group and the low-activity BAT group. These observations led us to hypothesize that BAT is a major source of circulating miR-378a-3p, and that BAT activity is linked to lung injury progression. Consistent with this hypothesis, enhancing BAT activity alleviated lung injury in the BLM-induced PF model by inhibiting inflammatory responses and fibrogenesis, and it increased miR-378a-3p in the circulation, although BAT may not be the only source of this circulating miRNA. BAT is known to regulate distant organs, including the muscle, heart, and liver [37, 38]. For instance, BAT-derived miR-378a-3p promotes hepatic gluconeogenesis [38], and the brown-fat metabolite succinate reduces immune cell infiltration in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [36]. Overall, our data provide, evidence that activating BAT exerts therapeutic benefit against PF, offering tractable endocrine route for intervention.

As evidence that exosomes mediate the anti-PF effects of activated BAT, gain-of-function delivery BAT-derived exosomes alleviate inflammatory infiltration and fibrogenesis, Conversely, whereas loss-of-function inhibition of BAT exosomes release markedly attenuated the protection conferred by BAT activation. Because miRNAs are key exosomal effectors, we further validated miR-378a-3p. Specific depletion of miR-378a-3p in adipose tissue abolished the protective effect of BAT against lung damage from PF and exacerbated the PF phenotype, as evidenced by increased infiltration of inflammatory cells in lung tissue and elevated levels of inflammatory genes and fibrogenesis. These results align with reports that miR-378a-3p confers benefits in other fibrotic contexts, including cardiac and hepatic fibrosis [46–48]. In conclusion, these findings suggest that miR-378a-3p as a principal mediator of the anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects of BAT-derived exosomes in PF. Lungs-targeted augmentation of miR-378a-3p therefore warrants further evaluation, including optimization of delivery and dosing and deeper dissection of downstream circuitry. Given the role of BAT and its derived miR-378a-3p in inhibiting inflammatory responses and fibrogenesis in the treatment of PF, we hypothesize that the protective effect of miR-378a-3p on PF may be linked to its influence on macrophages and fibroblasts. Studies have reported that miR-378a-3p can suppress the expression of IL-33 in ulcerative colitis [49] We observed that overexpression of miR-378a-3p reduced pro-inflammatory gene expression and cytokine release in macrophages. Bioinformatic screening and reporter assays identified FSTL1 as a direct target. FSTL1 is a secreted matricellular glycoprotein with immunoregulatory functions. Circulating FSTL1 is elevated in several autoimmune diseases. Secreted FSTL1 has been implicated in metabolic regulation, and in promoting hepatic inflammation, whereas its role in lung has remained unclear [43]. Our study demonstrates that miR-378a-3p represses FSTL1 and thereby limits the FSTL1/NF-κB signaling pathway in macrophages, leading to attenuated pulmonary inflammation.

Fibroblasts play a central role in the pathogenesis of PF, and strategies that target fibroblasts activation are an important therapeutic avenue. We observed that overexpression of miR-378a-3p suppressed fibroblast proliferation and collagen production. Utilizing target gene prediction algorithms, we identified the transcription factor ITGA5 as a downstream target gene of miR-378a-3p. Integrins represent a significant family of extracellular matrix receptors that promote fibroblast proliferation and fibrosis [42]. Our research indicates that miR-378a-3p can inhibit the progression of PF by targeting ITGA5 and inactivating the FAK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. In summary, our findings elucidate the molecular mechanisms through which the miR-378a-3p/FSTL1 axis and the miR-378a-3p/ITGA5 axis contribute to the therapeutic benefits of BAT activation in PF and clarify a mechanistic BAT-lung interaction.

However, this study has certain limitations. This preclinical study reveals a lung‑protective axis mediated by BAT‑derived exosomal miR‑378a‑3p, providing a new perspective for understanding the systemic regulation of pulmonary fibrosis. It should be noted, however, that the work is primarily based on a BLM‑induced acute lung injury fibrosis model in mice, which differs fundamentally from human idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)—a chronic and progressive disease—in terms of etiology, progression pattern, and clinical course. Furthermore, notable species differences exist between humans and mice regarding the anatomical distribution of BAT, its physiological regulation, and the composition of exosomal miRNA profiles. Consequently, the generalizability and pathophysiological relevance of the specific mechanisms identified here in human PF remain to be further validated, and the translation from mechanistic insight to clinical application still faces considerable steps. In summary, this work opens a new direction for research into the mechanisms and potential interventions for pulmonary fibrosis, yet its eventual therapeutic value will depend on subsequent validation in human disease and the overcoming of challenges in drug development.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that miR-378a-3p functions as a BAT-derived endocrine signaling molecule that attenuates pulmonary fibrosis through coordinated regulation of the miR-378a-3p/FSTL1 and miR-378a-3p/ITGA5 axis. These findings identify a novel BAT-lung communication mechanism relevant to fibrotic lung injury and suggest BAT-derived exosomes and miR-378a-3p as potential targets for therapeutic intervention.
