Dissecting the genetic architecture of early root-related traits in grapevine rootstocks across propagation methods and different scion
Authors: Marine Morel, Nabil Girollet, Pierre-François Bert, Marina de Miguel, Elisa Marguerit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoot traits are fundamental to plant establishment and survival, influencing resource acquisition, anchorage, and stress resilience. In grapevine, a perennial grafted fruit crop, early growth-related traits, such as rooting and grafting success, are crucial for both vineyard establishment and nursery production, which require well-developed rootstock mother vines and grafted plants. An understanding of the genetic variability underlying these traits is therefore important. We investigated the genetic architecture of early growth-related traits in a Vitis berlandieri × V. rupestris mapping population of 449 genotypes, using hardwood cuttings and grafted plants with two scion cultivars: V. vinifera cvs. Cabernet Sauvignon and Riesling. Our results indicate that the genetic control of these traits was polygenic and varied with the method of propagation (cuttings vs grafts), with individual QTLs explaining from 3.1% to 14.1% of the phenotypic variance. In particular, we detected largely non-overlapping genomic regions associated with similar traits across propagation methods and observed rootstock × scion interactions, suggesting an influence of scion genotype on root development. Two overlapping roots number QTLs were identified in grafts with different scions accounted for 7 and 14% of the variance in Cabernet Sauvignon and Riesling, respectively. Our results highlight the challenge of applying marker-assisted selection in rootstock breeding programs, due to the different genetic determinants underlying the same traits across the different propagation methods. Here, we found that QTLs colocalized with genes involved in growth and stress responses. Finally, eight genotypes with root-related performance superior to that of commercial rootstocks were identified, providing promising candidates for further evaluation in grapevine rootstock breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00122-026-05197-7.

Introduction

Root traits and architecture play a major role in plant productivity. The roots act as structural anchors and as storage and uptake organs (Lynch 1995). They are also essential for the adaptation of the plant to biotic and abiotic stresses, as reviewed by Warschefsky et al. (2016). Root systems play a particularly important role in conditions of nutrient limitation (Rangarajan et al. 2022) or drought, due to their influences on water uptake (Comas et al. 2013; Fichtl et al. 2023), transpiration regulation (Marguerit et al. 2012), and hydraulic conductance (Alsina et al. 2011). Root system plasticity, the ability of root systems to adjust their growth, architecture, and function in response to environmental variation, is a key component of this adaptability (Hodge et al. 2009). This plasticity, which could enable plants to optimize resource acquisition, is a particularly useful trait in breeding programs aiming to improve resilience. Together, these roles highlight the importance of understanding the genetic determinants underlying root traits to support the development of more resilient and productive crop varieties under challenging environmental conditions.

The concept of ideotypes, a widely used breeding strategy, has been proposed to guide the design of root system architectures suitable for specific environmental challenges and cropping systems (Lynch 2013). In grapevine, a grafted plant, in which productivity is increasingly threatened by climate change and soil (van Leeuwen et al. 2024), this ideotype-driven approach to rootstock (the underground part of the plant) breeding is gaining attention as a strategy for enhancing adaptation (Bernardo et al. 2025).

Vegetative propagation through grafting is widely used in many perennial crops, including fruit and forest trees (Mudge et al. 2009). Grapevine is no exception; since the late nineteenth century, it has been systematically grafted onto Phylloxera-resistant rootstocks to protect the crop against this devastating root aphid-like Daktulosphaira vitifoliae (Ollat et al. 2016). A dual propagation system is commonly used in grapevine: rootstocks are first propagated as hardwood cuttings to establish mother vines. These vines are used to generate wood, which is then used for grafting. The resulting grafted plants are planted and grown for one season in the nursery and are checked before field planting. This approach makes it possible to control root development, graft union quality, and plant shoot growth before establishment in the vineyard (Waite et al. 2014). These traits are often referred to nursery traits.

These traits are not only crucial for long-term resource acquisition but also determine the success of vegetative propagation and early establishment in the field. Differences in rootstock performance during propagation can have significant agronomic and economic consequences (Bavaresco et al. 2015), particularly in large-scale nursery operations and commercial vineyards.

Different grapevine rootstock genotypes confer high degrees of variability to the scion for various traits, including yield (Kidman et al. 2013), aerial biomass (Migicovsky et al. 2021), mineral content (Morel et al. 2024), and drought tolerance (Alsina et al. 2011), as also reviewed by Zhang et al. (2016).

Despite the central role of the rootstock, most grapevine QTL studies to date have focused on scion traits, such as berry quality (Doligez et al. 2006; Duchene et al. 2009), disease resistance (Divilov et al. 2018; Sapkota et al. 2019), or phenology (Costantini et al. 2008; Zyprian et al. 2016). Only a limited number of studies have identified QTLs on rootstock mapping populations, for water use efficiency (Marguerit et al. 2012), tolerance to lime-induced iron deficiency (Bert et al. 2013), or root-related traits (Tandonnet et al. 2018).

Despite the crucial contribution of the root system to grapevine adaptation, the genetic architecture underlying early growth traits and grafting success in this species remains only partially understood. However, there have recently been a few new attempts to decipher this architecture through the phenotyping of root traits (Alahakoon & Fennell 2023; Blois et al. 2023; Tandonnet et al. 2018), but these remain relatively few. Moreover, differences between propagation methods (cuttings vs. grafted plants) have only been explored for leaf morphology (Sharma et al. 2025). In addition, potential rootstock × scion interactions have not yet been integrated into genetic analyses, even though they may strongly influence early growth-related traits.

Efforts have been made to dissect the genetic basis of root traits in other perennial crops. QTLs for root biomass, architecture, or depth have been reported in various species, such as Eucalyptus (Sumathi et al. 2018), Malus (Moriya et al. 2015), Pyrus (Knäbel et al. 2017), Pinus (Shepherd et al. 2006), and Populus (Sun et al. 2019).

Marker-assisted selection, while promising for accelerating breeding, is currently limited in rootstock breeding programs by our incomplete knowledge of the genetic determinants controlling early-stage traits. Integrating approaches combining the phenotyping of early root traits across propagation contexts with high-resolution genotyping in segregating populations is required to address this challenge.

In this study, we aimed to dissect the genetic basis of early growth-related traits in grapevine rootstocks through QTL mapping. Our secondary objective was then to evaluate the variation induced by different propagation methods, comparing cuttings with grafted plants, on the QTLs detected to determine whether the same loci can be reliably targeted for selection. Finally, we aimed to assess the influence of rootstock root traits of different scion genotypes, focusing on Cabernet Sauvignon and Riesling, to highlight potential rootstock × scion interactions.

Material and methods

Plant material

A total of 449 F1 individuals were obtained by crossing the female V. berlandieri 10,585 (BER) with the male V. rupestris cv. Martin (RUP) in 2017. The resulting F1 population, Berl10585 × RupMartin1701, was grown at INRAE Bordeaux, France (44°47′28’’N; 0°34′41’’W).

Before the preparation of the cuttings or grafted plants, dormant canes were hydrated at 25 °C for 24 h. In addition to the progeny, several commercial rootstocks were included in the experiment as control genotypes and were subjected to the same treatments as the F1 individuals. These control genotypes were: 140Ru (V. berlandieri × V. rupestris du Lot), 110R (V. berlandieri × V. rupestris) (Riaz et al. 2019), 1103P (V. berlandieri Rességuier 2 × V. rupestris du Lot), Gravesac (161-49C × 3309C), and RupduLot (V. rupestris du Lot)(Pl@ntGrape 2009–2023).

In 2019 and 2020, 10 hardwood cuttings for each genotype were prepared and grown in a greenhouse for one year. These cuttings were grouped into sets of 60 in rooting boxes measuring 45 × 20 × 10 cm (length × width × depth) and filled with a substrate composed of two-thirds potting soil and one-third sand. After one year, the dormant cuttings were transferred to the field and grown in sandy–gravelly soil for one growing season; the soil composition is presented in Table S1. The plants were harvested in January 2021 and January 2022, respectively. Due to the limited amount of plant material available in 2019, the hardwood cuttings were 5 cm long with one bud. In 2020, one full internode was retained, resulting in 15-cm-long cuttings.

In 2021 and 2022, the progeny was used as a rootstock for grafting with V. vinifera cv. Cabernet Sauvignon clone 169 and V. vinifera cv. Riesling clone 24–209 scions, with 8 to 16 replicates per genotype. Bench grafting was performed using the omega grafting technique. The grafted plants were grown in the field for one year. In 2021, the grafted plants were maintained in the greenhouse for two months before field transplantation. In 2022, the plants were transferred directly to the field after grafting. DNA extraction and genotyping

Young leaves were sampled from all the original genotypes grown in the greenhouse and subjected to DNA extraction with a DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genome complexity was reduced by digestion with the restricted enzyme ApeKI, followed by ligation and pooling. Illumina HiSeq 4000 short-read 2 × 150 bp sequencing was performed by Genewiz. The GBS libraries used were prepared at UMR AGAP, CIRAD (Montpellier, France) as described by Elshire et al. (2011).

We used bwa-0.7.17 to align reads with the PN40024 V2 reference genome, and variants were called with the GATK (McKenna et al. 2010) v4.2.6.1 pipeline, which identified 5,735,473 SNPs. The SNPs were filtered and selected according to the following criteria: minimum depth of 2, mapping quality ≥ 30, Fisher strand ≤ 60, MQRankSum ≥ -12.5, ReadPosRankSum ≥ -8.0, and up to 10% missing data per SNP. In total, 5,030,177 SNPs were retained, and individuals with more than 50% missing data or detected as self-fertilization were filtered out. We used VCFtools (Danecek et al. 2011) with the following criteria: minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.25, linkage disequilibrium (LD) ≤ 0.5, genotype quality (GQ) ≥ 98, and maximum error rate ≤ 0.05. After segregation analysis with HetMappS scripts (Hyma et al. 2015), 77,052 SNPs passed the segregation test (1:1). A second filter was applied to remove genotypes with more than 25% missing data, resulting in the retention of 449 genotypes for further analysis. Significantly distorted markers, identified in a χ2 test (p value < 0.05), were removed, resulting in a final set of 7398 SNPs.

Genetic maps construction

Maps and marker order were calculated using regression mapping with the Kosambi mapping function provided in JoinMap4.1 software (van Ooijen 2006). The population nodes were created for each parent separately. Linkage groups (LG) were constructed with a threshold logarithm of odds (LOD) score of 17.0, a maximum recombination frequency of 0.4, a jump threshold of 5, and a maximum distance threshold of 25 cM. LGs were numbered according to the assignment of the reference genome PN40024 V2 chromosome map-anchored SNP markers. Only markers associated with the LG based on their corresponding physical chromosome numbers were conserved. For pairs of markers considered identical based on the lack of recombination, only one marker was selected for mapping, with the Jittermap function from ‘R/qtl’ package.

Phenotyping

The phenotyping of a given genotype depended on the diameter of the mother wood and grafting success. For this reason, not all genotypes were grafted and phenotyped every year. Two replicates were phenotyped per genotype on hardwood cuttings. Grafted plants were phenotyped only if they passed the thumb test, performed during winter when the plants were in the dormant stage, in which the thumb is used to apply pressure to the graft union to test its mechanical strength (Loupit et al. 2022).

For both grafted plants and hardwood cuttings, the rooting success, the percentage of plants that rooted, was determined for each genotype by examining all the plants produced. For other phenotypes, the trait was assessed on a subsample of two replicates for each combination.

The total biomass (Totalbiom) was measured for hardwood cuttings and grafted plants. For hardwood cuttings in 2020 and grafted plants in 2021 and 2022, shoot biomass (Shootbiom) and root biomass (Rootbiom) were also measured. The number of crown roots (Rootsnum) emerging from the trunk was determined, and the basal diameter of each at the collar was measured with a digital caliper. The sum of root diameters (Diamsum), the number of roots in each class, i.e., with a diameter less than or greater than 2 mm (Rootsnum <2, Rootsnum ≥2), and the proportion of fine roots (Proproot <2) were calculated.

Minimum (Diammin) and maximum (Diammax) diameters, and all root traits, were further characterized based on their position relative to the bud: bud side vs. opposite side (Table S2).

Statistical analysis and QTL detection

All statistical analyses were performed, and visual representations were generated with the R v.4.2.0 Statistics Environment (R Core Team 2014) and the ‘stats,’ ‘agricolae,’ ‘ggcorrplot,’ ‘lme4,’ ‘factoextra,’ ‘FactoMineR’, and ‘R/qtl’ (Broman et al. 2003) packages with RStudio.

Phenotypic data analysis

For each trait, the mean, maximum, minimum, standard deviation, and coefficient of phenotypic variation (CV) were calculated. The commercial rootstocks were excluded from all statistical analyses.

To quantify the relative contribution of rootstock genotype, year, and their interaction to phenotypic variation, a variance analysis was performed using a two-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) model. The percentage of variance explained (PVE) by each factor (rootstock, year, and rootstock × year interaction) was calculated from the corresponding sum squares relative to the total sum squares. This analysis was performed separately for hardwood cuttings and grafted plants.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on phenotypic data using the mean values per genotype and year. Missing values were imputed as the mean of each corresponding variable. Commercial rootstocks were excluded from the computation of principal components and individual coordinates, but were projected as supplementary individuals using the ind.sup argument for visualization and interpretation purposes. The number of principal components retained for representation was determined using the Kaiser criterion, retaining components with eigenvalues greater than 1 (Kaiser 1960).

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate trait correlations on phenotypic data within years at the genotype level. The results were visualized as correlation matrices, with only correlations with a p value < 0.05 displayed.

For each trait and year, a linear random-effects model was fitted with the lmer() function from the lme4 R package, with genotype as a random effect and no fixed effects included. We checked that the model residuals were normally distributed with a quantile–quantile plot, and best linear unbiased predictions (BLUPs) were extracted from this model. As a complementary analysis, BLUPs were also estimated across years using mixed models including year as a fixed effect. Given the strong environmental heterogeneity observed between years, year-specific analyses were retained as the primary framework for QTL detection. BLUP-based year analyses were therefore used to assess the robustness of detected genomic regions rather than to replace year-specific QTL scans.

Broad-sense heritability (H2) was estimated from variance components and calculated as the ratio of genetic to total phenotypic variance extracted from the random-effects model. For hardwood cuttings, individuals that did not root were also included and assigned a null phenotype value.

QTL analysis

QTL analysis was primarily performed using the BLUPs estimated separately for each trait and year, and conducted on the paternal and maternal genetic maps. QTLs detected using BLUPs estimated across years in grafted plants and hardwood cuttings, are reported as a complementary approach to assess the robustness of detected genomic regions.

QTL detection was performed using parental linkage maps constructed from single-parent segregating markers, following a pseudo-testcross strategy that is well suited to highly heterozygous outcrossing species and ensures reliable linkage phase estimation (Grattapaglia and Sederoff 1994). QTLs were identified by performing a single-QTL genome scan by the interval mapping (IM) approach with the scanone() function. A genome-wide LOD threshold (significance level α = 5%) was determined for each phenotype with 1,000 permutations. The peak LOD score was retained to define the position of the QTL.

For the identification of additional loci, particularly multiple QTLs in the same linkage group, composite interval mapping (CIM) was applied with the cim() function and the expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm. This detection was completed with the ScanTwo function to identify QTLs potentially concealed by QTL interactions. The significance of each QTL detected and the proportion of variance explained by the QTL (%var) were obtained from the multiple-QTL models with the fitqtl() function and a step-by-step approach. Moreover, the interaction between QTLs identified within the same linkage group for the same trait was tested with the same approach, but was always found to be nonsignificant. The approximate Bayesian credible interval, set at 0.95, was used to estimate the positional uncertainty of each QTL based on LOD profiles. Bayesian confidence intervals were estimated on the genetic map and subsequently converted to physical coordinates based on SNP genomic positions for the flanking markers. For each marker, the effect of each allele in the homozygous (aa) and heterozygous states (ab) was calculated as the mean of the phenotypic trait for the two groups.

For the binary trait (sex), the QTL was identified by performing a single-QTL genome scan by the interval mapping approach, specifying model = ‘binary’ in the scanone() function.

Gene identification

The genes associated with significant markers were identified based on the information for the corresponding chromosome from the annotated V. vinifera reference genome provided by Xiao et al. (2025), in which correspondences between genome versions are already established. This resource was used to link annotations between the V2 and V4 reference genomes without performing additional genome alignment. Genes were selected if the SNP at the peak was located within a coding sequence; otherwise, the gene closest to the SNP was retained (Price 2006). Functional annotations were retrieved from the UniProt and Gene Ontology databases.

Results

Parental map construction

High-density maternal (V. berlandieri) and paternal (V. rupestris) maps were constructed with 3740 and 2944 SNP markers, respectively (LOD threshold ≥ 17). For each set of markers, 19 LGs were generated with a final size of 1190 cM and 1485 cM for the V. berlandieri and V. rupestris maps, respectively. The average distance between markers was 0.3 cM for the maternal genetic map (BER) (Table S3) and 0.6 cM for the paternal genetic map (RUP) (Table S4).

Identification of the sex locus in linkage group 2, by interval mapping, provided additional confidence in the genetic and linkage maps. Among the 449 genotypes studied, 406 individuals bore flowers: 201 were female, 205 were male, and none had hermaphrodite flowers. A single major QTL was detected with the binary trait model, using the imputation method for interval mapping. The QTL was detected at 26 cM, on chromosome 2 in the male map with a maximum LOD score of 110.4 (genome-wide 5% LOD threshold = 3.09). This QTL explained 94.2% of the phenotypic variance. Its localization on chromosome 2 was consistent with previous reports (Bhattarai et al. 2021; Marguerit et al. 2009; Massonnet et al. 2020).

Phenotypic variability within the progeny and correlations between traits

The mapping population displayed considerable phenotypic variability for both root- and shoot-related traits, as indicated by the coefficients of variation (CV) exceeding 40% for many traits (Table 2). This variability reflected considerable differences between genotypes across multiple traits. A strong and significant year effect was also observed for phenotypic traits, particularly for shoot and root-related traits on grafted plants; the mean biomass was 1.8 g in 2021 vs. 15.3 g in 2022. For hardwood cuttings, the year effect was particularly strong and significant for root number, with a mean of six roots in 2019 and 17 in 2020. Variance component analysis showed that rootstock genotype explained a substantial proportion of phenotypic variance across traits and propagation contexts. Depending on the trait analyzed, the PVE by rootstock genotype ranged from 20 to 51%, while the PVE attributed to year ranged from nonsignificant to 53%. Despite year effect, the rootstock effect was consistent between years. Detailed PVE values and significance levels are reported in Table S5. To evaluate the stability of trait expression across years, we examined correlations among genotypes phenotyped in multiple years (Fig. S1). Biomass-related traits showed low correlations across years, whereas root-related traits, particularly root number, exhibited significant positive correlations, indicating a lower interaction with environmental conditions.

We investigated the genetic contribution to this phenotypic variation further by estimating broad-sense heritability (H2), which was found to be moderate to high, varying across traits and years. In cuttings, H2 values were moderate, ranging from 0.25 to 0.55 depending on the trait, and remained stable across years for the same trait. In grafted plants, however, H2 values were more variable, ranging from 0 to 0.75 depending on the trait. For the Root num≥2 and Proproot <2 traits, intra-genotype variation exceeded inter-genotype variation, accounting for the low heritability. Moreover, grafting combinations also influenced phenotypic performance; shoot biomass and root numbers were smaller when the genotypes were grafted with Riesling (R) compared to Cabernet Sauvignon (CS). Furthermore, the CV and H2 values for all traits were lower when the progeny was grafted with Riesling (Table S6).

Principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 1) provided a synthetic overview of multivariate phenotypic patterns, better illustrating the structure of trait variation within the mapping population. The measured traits captured a substantial proportion of the phenotypic diversity within the mapping population, with the first two principal components explaining 87% of the total variance for grafted plants and 90.5% for hardwood cuttings (Fig. 1). Eigenvalues for PC1 and PC2 were 4.5 and 2.5 in grafted plants, and 3.2 and 2.3 in hardwood cuttings, respectively.

Combined graphics of variables and individuals obtained from the PCA analyses for a hardwood cuttings and b plants grafted with Cabernet Sauvignon. The points labeled and circled in blue correspond to commercial rootstocks

Variable contributions were consistent between cuttings and grafted plants: One dimension was driven principally by biomass-related traits and strongly structured by year, whereas the other dimension was associated with root number, thin root number, and the proportion of fine roots and showed a clearer genotype-driven structure. Variation along the biomass-related component differed markedly across years, as indicated by the limited overlap of year-specific confidence ellipses along this axis, whereas overlap was greater along the second component.

In hardwood cuttings (Fig. 1a), the total root diameter and the number of small roots were greater in 2020, whereas the proportion of small roots and the number of roots were smaller, and biomass was greater in 2019. In grafted plants in 2022 (Fig. 1b), certain genotypes were associated with higher biomass-related traits, including shoot, root, and total biomass. In 2021, an association was found between genotype and the proportion of thin roots (< 2 mm). Overall, the 2022 data revealed a broader range of genotype responses than the data for 2021.

Several genotypes stood out as distinct outliers relative to the commercial rootstock controls, particularly in 2022, when they displayed greater biomass accumulation and a larger number of roots. The eight best performers were identified (Fig. 1b): four along the first dimension of the principal component analysis, i.e., those surpassing the 110R commercial rootstock (Berl10585 × RupMartin1701- × 69, Berl10585 × RupMartin1701- × 199, Berl10585 × RupMartin1701- × 2, and Berl10585 × RupMartin1701- × 114), and four outperforming the RupduLot control along the second dimension (Berl10585 × RupMartin1701- × 49, Berl10585 × RupMartin1701- × 135, Berl10585 × RupMartin1701- × 408, and Berl10585 × RupMartin1701- × 397) (Fig. S2).

The correlation patterns were broadly consistent across the two years for both hardwood cuttings and grafted plants (Figs. 2 and 3). Notably, for hardwood cuttings the relationships between Roots num and Diamsum were strong and positive in both 2019 (R = 0.79) and 2020 (R = 0.80) (Fig. 2). However, in 2020, overall relationships between most traits were similar or stronger, except for the relationship between Root num and Rootnum ≥2. This finding suggests that there was a shift in predominant root size contributing to the overall root number between 2019 and 2020.

For grafted plants, Root num displayed a very strong positive and stable correlation with Rootnum <2, with Pearson coefficients of 0.99 in 2021 (Fig. 3a) and 0.96 in 2022 (Fig. 3b). The principal traits associated with Total biom differed between years. In 2021, Totalbiom was strongly correlated with Rootbiom (R = 0.77), whereas in 2022, it was more strongly correlated with Shoot biom (R = 0.89). In both years, the relationship between Rootnum <2 and Rootnum ≥2 was negative.

QTL mapping of growth-related traits and rooting/grafting success

Rooting traits had a complex genetic architecture, with 21 QTLs. The loci detected explained between 3 and 11% of the phenotypic variance and up to 21% collectively for Rootnum (Table 3), highlighting the complex genetic basis of rooting in grapevine. QTLs associated with biomass-related traits were localized on LGs 9, 10, and 19, whereas root-related traits were localized mostly on LGs 1, 4, 5, and 6.

Among the root-related traits studied on grafted plants, several QTLs detected across years were identified on the same LG1 for Rootnum, with the variance explained ranging from 7.5 to 10.8%. Although peak positions varied slightly between years, the confidence intervals overlapped and mapped to the same genomic region. The variance explained by the detected QTL was higher in the mapping population grafted with Riesling (14%) than in that grafted with Cabernet Sauvignon (7%). These differences could be explained by the different sizes of the population phenotyped (Beavis et al. 1994). Similarly, overlapping QTLs were detected on LG4 in hardwood cuttings across years and the detected QTLs explained a lower proportion of variance (~ 3.5%) for Rootnum and Rootnum<2. No QTLs associated with grafting or rooting success were detected in grafted plants. Two QTLs associated with rooting success were detected for hardwood cuttings in 2019, each explaining a low proportion of the phenotypic variance (3% and 4%, respectively) (Table 3).

As a complementary approach, QTL mapping was also conducted using BLUPs estimated across years to assess the robustness of detected genomic regions. These analyses identified QTLs that largely overlapped year-specific confidence intervals for root-related traits, whereas biomass-related traits did not show genome-wide significant QTL signals but only with a chromosome-level significance (Table 3, Table S7).

When traits were distinguished according to the relative position of roots from the bud on grafted plants, four QTLs were identified for bud-side traits, and 12 QTLs were identified for traits on the contralateral side (Table S8). For bud-side traits, a QTL for thick root, the only QTL detected for this trait, was localized to LG5. For the contralateral-side traits, three QTLs for Rootnum <2 and Diamsum were identified on new LGs (LG8, LG15, and LG14, respectively). The other QTLs did not differ significantly from those previously identified at the whole-plant level. The proportion of the variance explained by QTLs was slightly higher when traits were defined based on their position relative to the bud.

Co‑localization of QTLs with candidate genes involved in early growth-related traits

Of the 27 QTLs identified across the different early growth-related traits, 21 were detected on the BER genetic map, and 6 on the RUP genetic map. These 27 QTLs were associated with 19 unique candidate genes (Table 4). In total, 10 SNPs were located within the coding sequences of genes, while the other nine genes being identified as those closest to the SNPs at peak LOD positions. Only three candidate genes out of 19 lacked functional annotation. The functional categories represented included mostly hormone metabolism, signal transduction, transcription regulation, and metal ion transport.

Discussion

Originality of this QTL study

We present here the first mapping population in a V. berlandieri genetic background. Despite being a key rootstock species known for traits such as lime and drought tolerance (Schmid et al. 2009), V. berlandieri remains underrepresented in genetic studies. Previous grapevine QTL studies have focused principally on V. vinifera or interspecific hybrids (Doligez et al. 2006; Fournier-Level et al. 2009; Guillaumie et al. 2020; Marguerit et al. 2012), with only a few considering American rootstock backgrounds outside of disease resistance studies, and only one GWAS study focusing on the V. berlandieri genetic background (Blois et al. 2023). To our knowledge, this is also the first time that such a large mapping population has been used in a perennial woody species. Most QTL studies in grapevine involve mapping populations of ~ 150 individuals (Battilana et al. 2009; Clark et al. 2018; Lin et al. 2019), although Barba et al. (2018) studied 366 individuals. We characterized 405 different genotypes; populations of such a large size are particularly valuable for detecting small to moderate effects (Beavis 1998; Bradshaw et al. 1998), which are typical for the complex traits of long-lived woody plants. Conducting a study of this scale poses several challenges. Firstly, the assessment of root-related traits, such as root number, root diameter, and root biomass, in woody perennials at this scale imposes logistical constraints. Secondly, a sufficiently large number of plants is required for the evaluation of rooting and grafting success. Finally, the focus on young plants makes it possible to perform early phenotyping and facilitates assessment at a large scale, but it also means that the traits measured may not be fully representative of those expressed at the adult stage. Nevertheless, the evaluation of early growth stages remains highly valuable for identifying the genetic determinants influencing initial rootstock performance.

Previous QTL studies have independently explored traits either on grafted plants (Marguerit et al. 2012) or on hardwood cutting (Cabezas et al. 2006), but no QTL analysis to date has investigated the same traits for two different propagation methods within the same mapping population.

Tandonnet et al. (2018) had an opportunity to investigate scion effects on root-related traits, given that their mapping progeny was grafted with five different scions, but they performed their QTL analysis on all plants regardless of the scion. By contrast, our design incorporated two different scions grafted onto a common set of 100 genotypes. This experimental design made it possible to explore the genetic architecture of early growth-related traits in grafted plants while assessing potential rootstock × scion interactions.

The genetic architecture of early growth-related traits

The propagation method matters: contrasting results for hardwood cuttings and grafts

The genetic architecture of early growth-related traits is complex, particularly when comparing propagation methods, such as hardwood cuttings and grafted plants. In grafted plants, survival may depend on both rooting success and grafting success, making it difficult to determine whether failure is due to defects of rooting or graft union formation, potentially partly accounting for the lack of detectable QTLs for grafting success. Further studies should involve performing experiments with both homo- and heterografts (Tedesco et al. 2020) to help disentangle the two traits. Alternatively, broadening the range of traits measured may better disentangle the complexity of grafting success. For instance, the necrosis or the callus formation at the graft interface can be assessed for QTL mapping, as recently done on apricot by Pina et al. (2021), and a similar approach could be applied to grapevines by evaluating traits such as callus formation at the graft interface. By contrast, we were able to identify rooting success QTLs in hardwood cuttings. However, the loci detected in this study explained only a small proportion of phenotypic variance (~ 3–4%), consistent with findings in other woody perennials. For example, in pear, Knäbel et al. (2017) detected several small-effect loci, suggesting that a polygenic architecture is common for rooting success in hardwood cuttings of long-lived species.

Interestingly, no overlapping QTLs were detected between cuttings and grafted plants, and the detected signals involved different linkage groups. This suggests that the genomic regions associated with these traits were condition-specific in this experiment. However, in both propagation systems, QTLs were identified only for fine root traits, contrasting with usual French nursery practices and regulation, where selection is based on thick root development (C.T.P.S. 2017). To our knowledge, there are no other rules for determining whether a vine plant is being marketed internationally or in another country. Moreover, the heritability of thick roots was lower than that of other classes, consistent with the findings of Tandonnet et al. (2018) and Blois et al. (2023), and suggesting that thick roots are more sensitive to environmental variations and less tractable for genetic mapping.

Overall, these findings highlight the challenges of applying marker-assisted selection to root traits in grapevine. Genetic signals were highly context-dependent and generally explained a limited proportion of phenotypic variance, underscoring the need to carefully consider both propagation method and trait choice when designing breeding strategies.

Scion influence on root development and QTL expression

Most studies have focused on the influence of the rootstock on scion characteristics, but the mechanisms underlying this influence are insufficiently understood (Warschefsky et al. 2016). Conversely, the effect of the scion on rootstock development remains underexplored in perennial crop genetics. In grapevine, Tandonnet et al. (2010) highlighted control exerted by the scion on biomass allocation and root development in grafted plants. In tomato, scion has been shown to affect rootstock architecture only in terms of root biomass and root length, with no significant rootstock × scion interaction (Bayındır & Kandemir 2022). However, these studies did not explore the genetic basis of this effect or quantify it.

In this work, a single genomic region showed repeated QTL detection in grafted plants across the two scion contexts tested, representing the most reproducible signal observed in this study. This suggests the presence of a genomic region contributing to early root development under the experimental conditions tested, although its apparent stability should be interpreted cautiously given the strong environmental and technical variance affecting these traits. However, the absence of other overlapping QTLs may reflect a limited power to detect loci with smaller effects due to the population size (100 genotypes) or a strong rootstock × scion interaction, suggesting that there may be a scion-specific genetic regulation of early root development. This raises a practical question for breeding programs: Which scions should be favored in breeding programs? Should scions that broaden QTL variance be preferred to improve selection efficiency? Or should we opt for more stable combinations that decrease interactions and enhance stability? It is difficult to determine which of the two scions used was the best in our study, because of the difference in genotype numbers (242 for CS vs. 100 for R) and very similar heritability values (0.52 for CS vs. 0.56 for R).

Growing conditions shape genetic effects

Environmental conditions strongly influenced the expressions of traits in our study. While most QTLs were not stable across years, we identified shared QTLs for root number and thin root number, suggesting that genetic control of root emission is more robust to environmental variations than other traits. The consistent detection of these overlapping QTLs across independent environments supports the biological relevance of these loci despite strong year-to-year environmental variation. Multivariate analyses further supported this pattern, indicating that biomass-related variation was largely driven by environmental effects, whereas root-related traits retained a stronger genetic signal across years. No previous study showed stable loci because only one year was phenotyped (Alahakoon & Fennell 2023; Sharma et al. 2025; Tandonnet et al. 2018) or because mixed models were employed without accounting for year-to-year correlations or year effects (Blois et al. 2023). Differences in nursery management practices between years could explain the difference in phenotypic expression across years. We hypothesize that the longer period spent in the nursery in 2022 allowed for more complete plant development, thereby increasing the contrast between genotypes and enhancing QTL detection. Moreover, the shift observed for the rootstock 110R across years is consistent with its well-documented slower early development and stronger performance when plants are given sufficient time to reach a more advanced developmental stage (Galet 1988; Pongracz 1983). This example illustrates how differences in cultivation duration can influence the developmental variation, with consequences for phenotypic contrast and for QTL detection, in particular for biomass-related traits. Such temporal effects may be particularly relevant for complex developmental processes such as root and shoot development, in which growth stage and the duration of cultivation can strongly modulate the expression of underlying genetic variation. These findings highlight the importance of taking into account multi-year and multi-environment trials and, wherever possible, of dissecting the variance explained by the year and experimental design.

Candidate genes and selection perspectives

Three of the 19 candidate genes identified (EXPA5, RD22m, and CYP734A7) had been functionally characterized and previously shown to be associated with growth or stress response pathways in model species, such as Arabidopsis thaliana.

The chr06_229640 marker in linkage group 6 of V. berlandieri, associated with Root num in the CS-grafted combination, is located near EXPA5, a gene from the expansin family. Expansins play a central role in developmental processes requiring cell wall modification, particularly at shoot and root growing points, by facilitating cell wall loosening and cell enlargement (Cosgrove 2000; Samalova et al. 2023). EXPA5 has been less widely studied than other genes from this family, such as EXPA1, but it remains a promising candidate due to its similar functions. In Arabidopsis thaliana, Park et al. (2010) demonstrated that EXPA5 mutants had shorter roots and that the expression of this gene was controlled by a downstream brassinosteroid signaling. However, Yin et al. (2023) reported no detectable expression of EXPA5 in any Populus tremula tissues, suggesting possible species-specific expression.

Other expansins, such as EXPA1 and EXPA4, are better characterized in Arabidopsis thaliana. EXPA1 is involved in cell division during lateral root initiation (Ramakrishna et al. 2019), whereas EXPA4 is involved in root elongation (Liu et al. 2021). Homologous genes with similar functions have been identified in other crops, including soybean, rice, and rose, as reported by Marowa et al. (2016), supporting the relevance of this gene family for root-related traits. Furthermore, the QTL identified in the Riesling-grafted combination may correspond to the same underlying locus because its confidence interval is fully nested within that of the QTL detected in the CS-grafted combination. This consistency across scions highlights the significance of this region, which explains 7% to 14% of the phenotypic variance, one of the highest levels of variance explained in this study.

The chr04_3479775 marker, in linkage group 4 of V. berlandieri, is associated with Roots num <2 in hardwood cuttings and is located within RD22m, a gene responsive to dehydration and regulated by abscisic acid (ABA). RD22-like genes have been studied principally due to their role in responding to abiotic stresses, such as drought (Rao & Virupapuram 2021) and salinity (Seok et al. 2018), particularly in aerial tissues such as the leaves and stems. However, their function in roots remains poorly characterized. ABA plays a central role in both stress signaling and developmental processes, such as root development (De Smet et al. 2003). RD22m may, therefore, contribute indirectly to root traits. However, further studies are required to clarify its potential role.

The chr09_5748638 marker, located on linkage group 9 of V. berlandieri, is associated with Totalbiom and is situated close to the CYP734A7 gene, which encodes a member of the cytochrome P450 enzyme family. CYP734A7 has not been studied in detail, but its sequence is similar to that of the well-characterized Arabidopsis thaliana gene CYP734A1 (BAS1), which encodes an enzyme that inactivates brassinosteroids. These enzymes serve as regulatory checkpoints between multiple photoreceptor systems and brassinosteroid signal transduction, thereby affecting responses to environmental signals (Neff et al. 1999). By controlling the availability of brassinosteroid, CYP734A7 may influence growth-related traits, such as biomass accumulation, by coordinating the regulation of cell division (Jung et al. 2010).

Interestingly, several candidate genes identified in our analysis such as VIT_04s0023g00370, VIT_01s0010g01480, or VIT_04s0023g00370 encode proteins with zinc-binding functions. Zinc-binding proteins, including zinc finger transcription factors and zinc-dependent regulatory proteins, are well-known regulators of plant development and stress responses, and several members of this family have been implicated in root development and growth regulation (Ciftci-Yilmaz and Mittler 2008). For example, ZINC FINGER PROTEIN 1 has recently been shown to be critical for root emergence and elongation under drought conditions in Arabidopsis thaliana (Chen et al. 2024) or Populus ussuriensis (Zhao et al. 2025). This observation suggests that zinc-related molecular pathways may contribute to the genetic control of the traits investigated in this study.

Some other genes identified are expressed in the organs associated with the corresponding traits (Fasoli et al. 2012). This level of expression of these genes in the tissues concerned increases confidence in their potential relevance. Notably, VIT_04s0023g01100, related to Rootnum, is expressed in roots; VIT_00s0187g00350 and VIT_19s0014g00010, which are associated with Totalbiom, were expressed in roots and stems; VIT_01s0010g00740, which is expressed in roots and linked to Diamsum; and VIT_04s0008g05480, is also expressed in roots and potentially involved in rooting success (Fasoli et al. 2012).

We detected QTLs overlapping for some of the analyzed traits with previous studies with grafted rootstocks (Blois et al. 2023; Tandonnet et al. 2018): The QTL for biomass identified by Tandonnet et al. (2018) in LG10 overlaps with the confidence interval of the region containing VIT_00s0187g00350, encoding a transcription initiation factor IIF subunit alpha. Similarly, regarding root-related traits, the marker reported by Blois et al. (2023), chr5_19758975, for the total number of roots, is located within the confidence interval of our candidate region, containing VIT_05s0020g04800, encoding an H15 domain-containing protein. In Tandonnet et al. (2018), root section QTLs were localized to LG1 and LG5, and the small roots QTLs in LG9 appear to lie within the confidence intervals of some of our root-related QTLs. Overall, LG1 and LG5 appear to be particularly relevant linkage groups for root-related traits in our studies. However, the other QTLs identified in this work and previous ones rely on different linkage groups. Promising candidate genes were identified, but cross-referencing with other studies focusing on root-related QTLs revealed only limited overlap. This highlights not only the complexity of root traits but also the influence of both genetic background and environmental factors.

For cuttings, Alahakoon and Fennell (2023) identified QTLs in other linkage groups, with no overlap observed. Root biomass was the only comparable trait, but their study focused on root architecture, making direct comparison difficult and revealing no shared genetic determinism.

These discrepancies reflect the complex and context-dependent nature of root trait genetics. As previously reviewed for Populus by Bannoud and Bellini (2021), the QTLs of root traits are highly context-dependent, varying with environmental (light, soil structure, temperature, growing period, etc.), structural (position and size of the cutting) and endogenous (age, carbon and nitrogen status, hormonal status) factors at mother plant level.

Our study allowed the identification of outlier genotypes that represent promising candidates for the rootstock breeding program. Given the V. berlandieri genetic background, these individuals may also have potentially valuable traits, such as drought or lime tolerance, worthy of further investigation.

In summary, to our knowledge, this study represents one of the largest QTL analyses conducted in a grafted perennial crop across multiple propagation contexts, scions, and years. While this complexity inevitably increases environmental variance, it also reflects the biological reality faced by rootstock breeding programs. Among 21 candidate genes, EXPA5 emerges as a promising target for root-related traits, offering one of the first functional lead for a core QTL region consistently detected across scions. The identified QTLs provide a valuable framework for future studies aimed at dissecting the genetic basis of these traits as functional genomic validation. Our findings highlight the complexity of rootstock breeding with different propagation methods. These results emphasize the need for multi-scion and multi-propagation methods strategies to optimize marker-assisted selection in rootstock breeding programs, even if that will be costly and time-consuming. Alternative mapping strategies, such as four-way cross models integrating shared parental alleles, could increase detection power and a more complete characterization of allelic effects and QTL architecture. While genomic selection is theoretically well suited to the polygenic architecture of early root traits, its application in grapevine rootstocks is currently limited by the lack of large, multi-scion, multi-context training datasets. Our study provides an initial step toward building such resources and highlights the need for coordinated phenotyping efforts to enable genomic prediction in future breeding programs.
