Principles of the Africa health workforce investment charter to stimulate sustainable health workforce investments
Authors: James Avoka Asamani, Kasonde Mwinga, Sunny C. Okoroafor, Ogochukwu Chukwujekwu, Paul Marsden, Christmal Dela Christmals, Maritza Titus, San Boris Kouadjo Bediakon, Adam Ahmat, Juliet Nabyonga-Orem, Regina Titi-Ofei, Karen Zamboni, Maren Hopfe, Nertila Tavanxhi, Yoswa Dambisya, Simphiwe Mabhele, Tana Wuliji, Laurence Codjia, Pascal Zurn, Francis Omaswa, Joseph Cabore, Matshidiso Rebecca Moeti

ABSTRACT
Despite improvements in health workforce density, the World Health Organization (WHO) African Region Member States an additional 6.1 million additional health workers by 2030 to achieve universal health coverage (UHC) and health sustainable development goals (SDGs) targets and ensure health security. Additional investments are needed for their education, employment, and retention. However, most countries cannot meet this and require support to secure increased sustainable long-term domestic and external investments in the health workforce to strengthen health systems as part of broader health and national development efforts to improve health, economic, and social outcomes. The Africa Health Workforce Investment Charter, launched by the WHO Africa Regional Office in May 2024, outlines a set of key principles that countries can adapt to stimulate and secure the multisectoral domestic and external investments needed to reduce Africa’s health workforce shortages by 2030. This would ultimately increase access and availability of health care workers and primary healthcare services, especially in rural and underserved communities. The Investment Charter sets out the following key principles for coordinating and sustaining investments in education, employment, retention, and public health functions: (1) Enabling government leadership and stewardship; (2) Applying evidence-informed prioritisation investment; (3) Aligning multisectoral investments through partnership and collaboration; (4) Stimulating more and better investments; and (5) Securing sustainable health workforce investments.

Background

The African Union Heads of State set a bold vision for the continent’s future with Agenda 2063: The Africa We Want [1], . Aspiration 1 of the Agenda envisions a prosperous Africa driven by inclusive growth and sustainable development by investing in Africa’s human and social capital and ensuring that the African population enjoys a life expectancy of over 75 years. Human capital development is undeniably a sine qua non for inclusive growth and economic advancement. However, the global COVID-19 pandemic significantly disrupted progress, reversing human development by half a decade to 2015 levels [2]. In 2022, Africa’s Human Development Index (HDI) was 18.5% below the global average (0.547 vs. 0.732), highlighting the urgent need for renewed investment in human capital [2], which investing in health workforce yields an impressive return of 1:9 [3]. On the back of multiple crises, global economic activity has been impacted, with high inflation levels [4, 5]. Public debt sustainability is a concern; the cost of living has risen in many African countries, increasing the risk of people falling into poverty with reduced access to health services, especially where social protection mechanisms are limited.

Before the global COVID-19 pandemic, most African countries were off-track in achieving the health-related sustainable development goals (SDG) targets and have since recorded some stagnation. The COVID-19 pandemic illuminated the critical role of the health workforce in delivering essential public health functions, which had been mainly overlooked and led to disparities across countries and fragmented approaches to public health workforce policies, planning, and implementation [6]. Health workforce challenges were also identified in WHO’s Global Pulse Surveys as a prominent barrier to maintaining essential health services and delivering COVID-19 response activities [7]. Therefore, increased and sustained levels of workforce investment are required to ensure access to quality health services and achieve universal health coverage (UHC) and other health-related SDG targets. This magnifies the calls for cross-sectoral collaboration, partnership, solidarity and alignment of efforts to invest in cost-effective health workforce priorities [8].

Health-related SDGs and global health security are linked to investments in the education, employment, and retention of health workers within resilient health systems to prevent and respond to public health emergencies while maintaining the optimal provision of routine essential health services. More than half of the investments required for SDG3 are spent on the recurrent cost of workforce employment (wages and salaries) [9]. It is estimated that almost 75% of investments required for progressive attainment of SDG3 are for health systems, within which the health workforce and infrastructure (including medical equipment) are the principal drivers of the investment costs. Additionally, it is estimated that future pandemics require additional spending of at least US$5 per capita per year, of which two-thirds would be spent on enhanced health workforce capacities for prevention, detection, and response [10]. It is imperative that sustained long-term workforce investments are secured as part of national cross-sectoral efforts to ensure the achievement of health security and improve health, social, and economic outcomes. These investments must go beyond increasing numbers. Expanding employment opportunities in the health sector should be carefully balanced with efforts to provide decent conditions of work to attract and retain skilled workers. The required balance should be informed by robust labour market evidence and regular policy dialogues in order that increases in unit costs of labour do not crowd out the ability to employ more and the quest to employ more do not compromise fair levels of remuneration, job quality and result in a retain the workforce.

This paper outlines the key principles of the Africa Health Workforce Investment Charter, which is aimed at aligning efforts to stimulate investments to tackle the shortage of 6.1 million health workers [11], hal inequalities in access to health workers in Africa, especially in rural areas and in primary health care settings.

The health workforce context in the WHO Africa region

The African Region has recorded a remarkable expansion in its capacity to produce and train health care workers, increasing from 150,000 graduates in 2018 [12] to over 255,000 in 2022 [13]. This significant growth stems from both an expansion in training capacities to more than 4,000 training institutions/programs in countries and improvement in health workforce data availability following the implementation of the National Health Workforce Account (NHWA) [14, 15]. These have resulted in the number of health workers in the Region markedly increasing from 1.6 million in 2013 to almost 5.1 million by 2022, including 850,000 community health workers [13, 16] – see country-by-country details in Table 1. The increased health workforce stock has improved access to health services as the density of doctors, nurses, midwives, dentists, and pharmacists improved from an average of 11 per 10,000 people in 2013 to 27 per 10,000 by 2022 [13], which country comparisons are shown in Fig. 1. However, disparities in equitable access still persist between urban, rural and underserved communities.

Despite these strides, projections indicate a potential shortfall of 6.1 million health workers by 2030, which is necessary to effectively tackle the Region’s disease burden through comprehensive health promotion, disease prevention, treatment, rehabilitation, and palliation [11, 17], . Also, nearly 27% of trained health workers remain unemployed, underscoring a mismatch between training outputs and job creation. This level of unemployment requires an average of 43% increase in current funding levels dedicated to health workforce employment [17, 18]. Moreover, the Region has faced challenges related to suboptimal working conditions, with at least 14 countries experiencing an average of four instances of health worker industrial unrest or strikes annually since 2018, while retention and brain drain continues to be a significant issue. Data reported by recipient countries show that one out of every ten doctors or nurses trained in Africa now working abroad particularly in OECD countries and an about 42% of surveyed health workers in six African countries are considering emigration sometime in the future [13].

Informal work in health and social care remains widespread, often larger than in comparable sectors like education [19]. Unpaid care work hinders gender equality and women’s advancement in health and social care sectors, especially where there is heavy reliance on unpaid, informal and volunteer work, effectively acting as a hidden subsidy to health systems. To address this, it is essential to progressively integrate informal health workers, like community health workers, into the formally employed workforce of national health systems, establish mechanisms for regulating and accrediting their skills, and ensure decent jobs, social protection, labour rights, and equal pay for work of equal value. For example, it is shown that professionalizing CHWs (standardised training with clear selection criteria, competency-based training, regulation, and supervision) and integrating them into national workforce planning, budgeting and making their remuneration predictable and regular has better value from a decent work perspective, enhances their accountability, efficiency and eventually their impact on population health [20–23].

Most countries have health workforce strategies, but their implementation rate is less than 35% due to underinvestment and lack of resources [24, 25], placing an emphasis on the urgent need for progressively increasing domestic contribution to health workforce financing given the flattening and precipitated transition in some cases of once reliable external development assistance [26]. To counteract this, over the past decade, numerous global, continental and regional agendas and declarations have called for the creation of an investment framework to align and synergise efforts in Africa to stimulate investments to address its health workforce challenges [27–31], .

The Africa health workforce investment charter: why is it needed?

Although there are country plans and regional commitments to address health workforce challenges, their implementation has been constrained by inadequate financing, often siloed, ad hoc and inefficient investment approaches. Tackling the persisting and emerging challenges of the health workforce requires a renewed political commitment backed by financial investments in human capital development – investing in education, jobs, and decent working conditions and positioning the health workforce at the centre of socioeconomic development and social justice is critical and requires innovative ways for more and smarter investments in the health workforce. To address this, the Africa Health Workforce Investment Charter was co-created and adopted as an instrument to help Governments align their stakeholders, partners and the private sector around a joint national investment plan to inform policy reforms, expand the level of investments and reduce duplications and inefficiencies in the investment made by the different stakeholders in the health workforce. This will foster joint accountability for government-led, predominantly domestic investments and increase partner confidence in national priorities for co-investment. As an overarching approach, for each country, the Charter encourages government-led joint health labour market evidence generation with local stakeholders, national policy and investment dialogue(s) to build consensus on current and emerging national health workforce priorities informed by the evidence, joint investment planning, negotiating and formalizing commitments through signing of compacts and peer-to-peer accountability between government and their stakeholders to ensure that commitments are honoured. In May 2024, the inaugural Africa Health Workforce Forum was held in Windhoek, Namibia. Nearly 200 decision-makers across health, education, labour and employment, and finance, including ministers, permanent secretaries, directors, development partners, civil society, the private sector, and representatives of health professional associations and unions [32], attended the Forum. During the Forum, the Africa Health Workforce Investment Charter [33] was launched and adopted through the Windhoek Statement on Investing in Africa’s Health Workforce [34]. The Investment Charter is aligned and consistent with the Global Strategy on Human Resources for Health: workforce 2030 [28] and its Africa Regional Implementation Framework adopted by ministers of health in 2017 [27] which has a target to reduce at least by half inequalities in access to health workers by mobilising and sustaining political and financial commitment and foster inclusiveness and collaboration across sectors as part of investment in the development, performance, and retention of health workers.

Several partners played critical roles in the development of the Charter. These include the technical and financial contributions of the joint ILO- OECD- WHO Working for Health programme and its Multi-Partner Trust Fund (MPTF), The Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria (GFATM), the then United States Agency for International Development (USAID), the World Bank, the International Labour Organization (ILO), the Southern Africa Development Community (SADC), the East, Central and Southern Africa Health Community (ECSA-HC), the African Centre for Health and Social Transformation (ACHEST), the African Foundation for Research and Education in Health (AFREHealth), the Harmonization for Health in Africa (HHA) partners, the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC), the Japanese International Cooperation Agency (JICA), the Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa (COMESA), and the International Federation of the Red Cross (IFRC). New partners are encouraged to join hands which several increasingly expressing interest to align with the principles and commitments of the Charter.

Theory of change of the health workforce investment charter

The Charter seeks to guide Governments to work with their partners and stakeholders to align their priorities and resources to stimulate employment and retention of health workers by reducing inefficiencies in current spending and sustainably increasing additional resources for health and the health workforce. It views the investment as a process that applies five key health workforce investment principles: (1) Government leadership, (2) evidence-driven prioritisation, (3) alignment and synergy, (4) stimulation of investments, and (5) ensuring sustainability), as shown in Fig. 3.

Theory of Change of the Africa Health Workforce Investment Charter

Governments, in collaboration with social partners and development partners, need to develop long-term, costed health workforce plans that are driven by health labour evidence (including analysis and projections of workforce supply, demand, needs and fiscal space considerations) in a way that reflect priority population health needs, models of care and are aligned with international labour standards and integrated into national development, employment and health sector strategies.

The investment process begins with evidence-driven identification and prioritisation of workforce policy and investment options. Once identified, alignment and synergy across partners are needed to enable actions. This is achieved through an investment dialogue where consensus and commitments (“investment decisions”) are obtained to inform an investment plan and formalise the commitments of all the stakeholders involved through a national “health workforce investment compact” or memorandum of understanding.

To secure commitments, the Government leading the way with their partners can jointly stimulate investments and direct their financial and technical resources in a complementary way to meet their commitments and accountability obligations. Once investments are made, using existing modalities and investment platforms to protect their value and sustainability is critical to realising their full returns. This is done by ensuring that all investments are linked to measurable health, social, and economic outcomes in the medium to long-term horizons to deliver results and impact.

Therefore, health workforce investments must shift from ad-hoc in-service training, education, and capacity-building to strategic health systems and education sector strengthening, focusing on quality, scale, and skills mix to match health needs while ensuring decent working conditions. The health workforce investments should be (or progressively shift towards a) predominantly domestic sources and government-led, and the contribution partners shifting from ad-hoc health worker trainings and incentives to long-term measures to support the recurrent cost of employment and retention, including the ethical management of international migration and recruitment of health workers.

Coordination and accountability are also critical and should be achieved by applying appropriate tools and methodologies to track, measure, and report on the progress of expanded funding from all partners, including the domestic, external, and private sectors. This information is helpful in informing a workforce investment scorecard. Routine reporting on the Investment Charter will be incorporated by countries into their existing monitoring frameworks and processes, with a consolidated report on the Charter submitted through regional and continental mechanisms. Governments may also facilitate regular national investment dialogues between the Government and partners on the Charter, progress in its implementation, and the results achieved.

WHO and its partners will play a vital role by facilitating regional and continental workforce investment fora. These fora will foster advocacy, alignment, and negotiation between governments, health worker unions and associations, the private sector, and partners on the Charter and its implementation. Box 1 illustrates how Zimbabwe, an early adopter country used the principles of the Charter to inform health workforce policy reforms and investment planning leading to the signing of an investment compact.

Zimbabwe is an early adopter of the Africa Health Workforce Investment Charter principles. The country conducted a comprehensive health labour market analysis in 2022 to inform its health workforce planning. The findings revealed that the country’s registered stock of 74,298 health workers was only 60% of the country’s total and that actively participated in the health labour market. Also, the available stock covered just 47.5% of the needs-based requirement, resulting in a 57,543 health workers shortage. The country’s training output of 3334 health workers per year needed to be improved as the out-migration of health workers was a big concern, with 41% of health workers intending to migrate abroad in the future.

Based on the findings of the health labour market analysis, Zimbabwe held a multisectoral health workforce policy dialogue in February 2023 to build broad-based consensus on the health workforce priorities for policy reforms and investments. Based on the priorities identified, the country developed a national health workforce strategy in 2023, in which it is planned to double training capacity from about 3,400 to 7,000 per year and create 32,000 new public health sector jobs by 2030 and mitigate attrition by 50%.

In line with these commitments, the Government increased study leave slots for specialist training of health workers and expanded the training of primary care nurses from 30 to 200 per year to address gaps left by emigrating nurses and midwives. It also revised its health worker retention scheme in July 2022 and introduced “health-specific” allowances amounting to US$ 67.7 million annual investment. The Global Fund also unlocked a grant of US$30 million to support the payment of incentives linked to the revised retention scheme for two years (2022–2023).

To ensure long-term health workforce development, the Government and its development partners negotiated and developed a national health workforce investment plan approved by the cabinet. The Government, represented by ministries of finance, education, public service and health and the compact partners formalised their commitments by signing the investment plan as a compact in October 2024 to align their priorities and drive an additional US$ 166 million in annual health workforce investments over three years (2024–2026) across education, employment and retention. For the first time, there is an annual allocation of US$2 million is for absorbing and integrating community health workers into the public sector health workforce. Also, the Government’s commitment to absorbing the salaries of more than 400 health workers annually into the government payroll from the compact partners is an important transition and sustainability mechanism. Zimbabwe’s evolving example demonstrates the added value of the principles of the African health workforce investment charter.

Conclusion

The Africa Health Workforce Investment Charter is a crucial instrument to help align efforts to ensuring that governments, key partners and stakeholders are united in working towards a common health workforce investment agenda and approach. By optimising, building, supporting and strengthening the health workforce through sustained and scalable investments in education, employment, decent work and retention, aligned with international labour standards, the Charter aims to create a robust and resilient health system. This alignment of key workforce policies and investments is critical for achieving UHC, ensuring health security, and fostering inclusive growth. Furthermore, integrating workforce investments into national health and development planning ensures a comprehensive and cohesive strategy supporting long-term health and economic outcomes. Moving forward, WHO will constitute the Regional Health Workforce Advisory Committee to spearhead the needed advocacy and implementation. WHO will work with partners to support countries in operationalizing the principles and commitments of the Charter in countries in line with their national policies and aspirations including having evidence-based inclusive health workforce investment plans, using national dialogues to build consensus and formalising commitments through investment compacts to ensure alignment across government ministries, partners, and the private sector to stimulate additional investments while ensuring efficiency in utilizing the resources.
