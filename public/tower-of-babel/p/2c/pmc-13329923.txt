Utilization of large lattice-tip dual energy catheter for left ventricular summit arrhythmia ablation
Authors: Daniel Hanna, Andrew Kossack, Adam Frank, Dinesh Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVentricular arrhythmias arising from the left ventricular (LV) summit are technically challenging to ablate because of limited accessibility and proximity to the coronary arteries. Dual energy lattice-tip catheter (DLTC) could provide both very high-power temperature-controlled radiofrequency (RF) ablation and pulse field (PF) ablation.ObjectiveTo describe the use of combined radiofrequency (RF) and pulsed field (PF) ablation using a dual-energy large lattice-tip catheter for treatment of ventricular tachycardia originating from the LV summit region.MethodsWe report a 66-year-old man with atrial fibrillation, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, and recurrent VT despite prior endocardial and epicardial radiofrequency ablation. Mapping localized the PVC/VT to a LV summit–adjacent region, with an endocardial target along the superior septum/LVOT consistent with an intramural or epicardial summit substrate. Given prior failed RF ablations compassionate-use ablation with the dual-energy lattice-tip catheter and Affera™ mapping system (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) was performed under intracardiac echocardiogram guidance. Interventional cardiology was directly involved, providing coronary angiography and intravascular imaging before and after ablation to ensure the absence of vasospasm or ischemia.ResultsAt 6 week follow up, following dual-energy ablation with DLTC, there was significant suppression of ventricular arrhythmia off antiarrhythmic amiodarone. There was no evidence of coronary injury post ablation confirmed by coronary angiogram and intravascular ultrasound. This case highlights the feasibility of high-power temperature controlled-RFA and focal PFA for VT in anatomically regions where success with conventional energy sources is limited.ConclusionCombined RF and PF ablation using the DLTC achieved safe and effective ablation of LV summit ventricular tachycardia without coronary injury.Keywords: Left ventricular summit, Ventricular tachycardia dual-energy ablation, Pulsed field ablation, Radiofrequency ablation, Lattice-tip catheter

Introduction

Ventricular arrhythmias arising from the left ventricular (LV) summit represent a major therapeutic challenge. The summit is an anatomically complex region bounded by the left anterior descending and left circumflex coronary arteries, the great cardiac vein, and variable epicardial fat. These factors limit accessibility and increase the risk of collateral injury during catheter ablation. Conventional radiofrequency (RF) ablation from endocardial, coronary venous, or aortic cusp approaches often provides incomplete lesion depth and is constrained by venous anatomy, proximity to the coronary arteries, contributing to procedural failure and recurrence [1].

Very high power, low current density radiofrequency (RF) and high-voltage microsecond pulse field (PF) energy delivered through a large lattice-tip catheter represent a novel dual-energy ablation strategy. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that the lattice tip design enables deeper and more homogeneous ventricular lesion with both RF and PF energy [2,3]. Early clinical experience has likewise suggested favorable experience with the dual-energy ablation in ventricular arrhythmia [4,5]. Although most published experience with PFA has focused on atrial fibrillation and pulmonary vein isolation, early reports suggest feasibility of focal ventricular applications [6].

Here we describe a patient with recurrent ventricular tachycardia and premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) from surrounding areas near the LV summit, previously treated with endocardial and epicardial RF ablation, who underwent compassionate-use ablation with the lattice-tip hybrid system (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN). This case highlights the potential role of dual-energy lattice tip ablation catheter for ventricular arrhythmia originating thick tissue such as left ventricle summit.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old man with atrial fibrillation, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, diabetes, hypertension, and ventricular tachycardia (VT) status post implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) presented with recurrent palpitations, chest pain, and near syncope. He had previously undergone two ablation procedures for frequent PVCs. The first, in 2023, targeted the lateral left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT). In 2024, he re-presented with sustained ventricular tachycardia (Fig. 1). Extensive endocardial mapping localized the earliest activation to the LVOT; however, no adequate endocardial target was identified. Epicardial access was obtained via a percutaneous subxiphoid approach using a micropuncture technique, and epicardial mapping/ablation was performed targeting the earliest activation site near the LV summit region. Despite partial success, he continued to experience non-sustained ventricular tachycardias despite being on amiodarone, and an ICD was implanted for secondary prevention.

Twelve-lead ECG demonstrating non-sustained monomorphic ventricular tachycardia with a left bundle branch block morphology, inferior axis, and precordial transition in V3.

In 2025, he developed worsening palpitations, chest pain, and near syncope. Given his history of recurrent ventricular arrhythmias, prior failed endocardial and epicardial ablation, and episodes of hemodynamically unstable VT, compassionate-use ablation with the dual-energy lattice-tip catheter was pursued targeting PVC/VT LV summit substrate.

Procedure

Electrophysiology study was performed under general anesthesia. On isoproterenol, only non-sustained VT was induced, which limited conventional activation mapping. Therefore, ablation targeting was guided primarily by pace mapping of the dominant clinical morphology, demonstrating a 97% match in the LVOT, consistent with the suspected summit-adjacent substrate. Sustained VT was not present during the procedure, precluding systematic activation mapping and earliest electrogram acquisition; therefore, ablation targeting relied primarily on pace mapping of the dominant clinical morphology.

VT preset RF and PF ablation with dual-energy lattice-tip catheter was planned. Given the proximity of the left main coronary artery, diagnostic coronary angiography with intracoronary imaging was performed by interventional cardiology to delineate anatomy and provide backup in case of vasospasm. Vascular access was obtained under ultrasound guidance. An 11F and 8F sheath were placed in the right femoral vein, and an 8F Avanti arterial sheath (11 cm) was placed in the right femoral artery to facilitate retrograde LV access. A backup right ventricular pacing catheter and external defibrillation pads were positioned for safety.

An AcuNav intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) catheter was advanced into the right atrium, and LV access was obtained retrograde across the aortic valve. A three-dimensional electroanatomic map of the LVOT and RVOT was created with CARTO 3 system (Biosense Webster, Inc., Irvine, California, USA) under ICE guidance. Although the dual-energy lattice-tip catheter has integrated mapping capability, in this compassionate-use, CARTO3 was used for chamber reconstruction, ICE integration, and pace mapping, while the Sphere-9 was employed for both local signal acquisition and RF/PF delivery.

Programmed ventricular stimulation was performed from the RV apex at a drive cycle length of 600 ms with up to three extra-stimuli (280/270/260 ms), with and without isoproterenol, which reproducibly induced non-sustained VT matching the clinical PVC morphology (Fig. 1). Pace mapping in the LVOT demonstrated a 97% match (Fig. 2).

Intraprocedural 12-lead ECG (100 mm/s) demonstrating pace mapping at the target site with a 97% morphology match to the clinical PVC/VT. The paced morphology (cyan) closely reproduces the clinical arrhythmia morphology (green), supporting the ablation target.

Following identification of the optimal pace map site (97% morphology match), ablation sites were selected based on spatial clustering of high-fidelity pace maps, relative local electrogram timing compared with adjacent myocardium, and anatomic proximity to the presumed intramural LV summit substrate. Given the suspected intramural origin and prior failed ablation attempts, lesions were delivered in a contiguous fashion to achieve regional substrate homogenization rather than targeting a single focal site.

While late precordial transition can suggest an RVOT septal origin, ECG features may overlap in intramural or summit-adjacent ventricular arrhythmias. In this case, three-dimensional mapping localized the target to the superior septal LVOT near the RCC/NCC junction, consistent with a summit-adjacent substrate. Prior failed endocardial and epicardial RF ablation further supported an intramural or epicardial LV summit origin rather than a purely RVOT septal focus.

Given the proximity of the RCC/NCC junction to the left-sided His region, high-density three-dimensional mapping was particularly helpful in precisely delineating LVOT and RCC/NCC anatomy, tagging the His bundle location, and defining a safe ablation corridor in the absence of sustained inducible VT. The selected ablation sites were spatially distinct, with no His or left bundle potentials recorded and no His capture during pacing. Continuous monitoring during RF and PF delivery demonstrated no transient AV block or conduction delay, and no applications required termination.

The procedural endpoint was defined as (1) non-inducibility of ventricular tachycardia with repeat programmed stimulation (with and without isoproterenol), (2) suppression of spontaneous PVCs, and (3) absence of recurrent non-sustained VT during a waiting period. Coronary safety was confirmed by repeat angiography and intravascular ultrasound prior to sheath removal.

Targeted ablation was performed using a dual-energy lattice-tip catheter, with deliverers very of both RF and PF energy to the LV outflow tract and summit-adjacent region under intracardiac echocardiographic and fluoroscopic guidance (biphasic waveform; four PF trains per application site; Fig. 3). The lattice-tip design permitted high-density mapping and focal lesion delivery at the target region, and a final electroanatomic map was obtained (Fig. 4). Given the proximity of the right coronary cusp/non-coronary cusp junction to the left-sided His bundle region, careful mapping was performed to identify and avoid conduction system tissue. No His or left bundle potentials were identified at the target site, and pacing did not demonstrate His capture. Lesion placement was deliberately offset from the conduction system region under continuous intracardiac echocardiographic and coronary angiographic guidance.

RF lesions were delivered first, followed by adjunctive PF applications. RF ablation was performed in VT preset mode using temperature-controlled settings (maximum power 50 W, temperature limit 65 °C, up to 90 seconds per lesion). PF lesions were then delivered using the ventricular waveform (7.0 A). In total, 30 RF lesions and 19 PF lesions were delivered under continuous coronary angiographic and intracardiac echocardiographic monitoring. Coronary angiography during ablation demonstrated no vasospasm, and post-procedural intravascular ultrasound confirmed preserved coronary lumen without stenosis (Fig. 5, Video 1). Following ablation, VT was not inducible and the patient remained asymptomatic.

At 3-week follow-up patient had no symptoms of recurrent syncope, palpitations, or chest discomfort. Three-week ICD interrogation demonstrated a marked reduction in PVC burden from 45.0/hour to 5.0/hour (Fig. 6). At 6-week follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic and was discontinued from amiodarone. A 1-week Zio patch subsequently demonstrated <1% PVC burden, with the longest episodes of ventricular bigeminy and trigeminy lasting 5.2 seconds and 7.7 seconds, respectively. At 3-month ICD follow-up, PVC burden increased to 20.0/hour. Despite the increase in burden the patient remained asymptomatic without any VT episodes or ICD therapies.

Interrogation of the ICD revealed a significant reduction in premature ventricular complex (PVC) burden, decreasing from 46.5 episodes per hour at baseline to 5.0 episodes per hour on follow-up.

Discussion

The LV summit remains one of the most technically challenging sites for ventricular arrhythmia ablation because of limited accessibility, variable epicardial fat, and close proximity to the left main and LAD coronary arteries. Conventional RF ablation from endocardial, coronary venous, or aortic cusp approaches is often constrained by safety concerns and limited lesion depth, contributing to procedural failure and recurrence despite aggressive attempts [1,7].

PFA delivers high-voltage, microsecond pulses that induce irreversible electroporation, preferentially injuring myocardial tissue while sparing adjacent structures [4,5]. In this patient, dual-energy was employed under compassionate use after prior RF ablations failed to achieve durable arrhythmia suppression.

Coronary safety was prioritized through pre- and post-ablation angiography, supplemented by IVUS to exclude vasospasm or structural injury. The distance between ablation catheter and the left coronary artery was assessed and deemed sufficient, and prophylactic nitroglycerin was intentionally avoided to reduce the risk of procedure-related hypotension. Intracardiac echocardiography and high-density electroanatomic mapping further guided catheter positioning and lesion delivery, underscoring the importance of multimodality imaging of the LV summit and surrounding regions.

In refractory LV summit–adjacent VT, alternative strategies may include coronary venous ablation, surgical/epicardial approaches, or transcoronary venous ethanol ablation when a suitable septal perforator target is present. In this case, given prior failed ablation attempts and the desire to maximize lesion depth while maintaining coronary safety under direct angiographic and IVUS guidance, we proceeded with compassionate-use dual-energy lattice-tip ablation rather than ethanol infusion.

Although acute suppression of PVCs and VT was achieved without complication, important uncertainties remain. The long-term durability of dual-energy lesions in ventricular myocardium is not well established at the LV summit [5]. In addition, multiple PVC morphologies and intramural sources may complicate long-term arrhythmia control even when acute success is observed.

This case illustrates the potential of RFA/PFA with the dual-energy lattice-tip catheter to expand therapeutic options for challenging ventricular substrates while emphasizing the need for careful patient selection, comprehensive coronary imaging, and structured follow-up. As clinical experience grows, systematic studies will be required to define the safety profile, lesion durability, and comparative efficacy of PFA relative to conventional RF approaches in this high-risk anatomic region.
