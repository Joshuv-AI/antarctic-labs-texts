Population pharmacokinetics of ivermectin after mass drug administration in lymphatic filariasis endemic communities of Tanzania
Authors: Adam M Fimbo, Eulambius M Mlugu, Eliford Ngaimisi Kitabi, Gerald S Kulwa, Mohammed A Iwodyah, Rajabu Hussein Mnkugwe, Peter P Kunambi, Alpha Malishee, Appolinary A R Kamuhabwa, Omary M Minzi, Eleni Aklillu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIvermectin (IVM) is a drug of choice used with albendazole for mass drug administration (MDA) to halt transmission of lymphatic filariasis. We investigated IVM pharmacokinetic (PK) variability for its dose optimization during MDA. PK samples were collected at 0, 2, 4, and 6 h from individuals weighing greater than 15 kg (n = 468) receiving IVM (3‐, 6‐, 9‐, or 12 mg) and ALB (400 mg) during an MDA campaign in Tanzania. Individual characteristics, including demographics, laboratory/clinical parameters, and pharmacogenetic variations were assessed. IVM plasma concentrations were quantified by liquid‐chromatography tandem mass spectrometry and analyzed using population‐(PopPK) modeling. A two‐compartment model with transit absorption kinetics, and allometrically scaled oral clearance (CL/F) and central volume (V c/F) was adapted. Fitting of the model to the data identified 48% higher bioavailability for the 3 mg dose compared to higher doses and identified a subpopulation with 97% higher mean transit time (MTT). The final estimates for CL/F, V c/F, intercompartment clearance, peripheral volume, MTT, and absorption rate constant for a 70 kg person (on dose other than 3 mg) were 7.7 L/h, 147 L, 20.4 L/h, 207 L, 1.5 h, and 0.71/h, respectively. Monte‐Carlo simulations indicated that weight‐based dosing provides comparable exposure across weight bands, but height‐based dosing with capping IVM dose at 12 mg for individuals with height greater than 160 cm underdoses those weighing greater than 70 kg. Variability in IVM PKs is partly explained by body weight and dose. The established PopPK model can be used for IVM dose optimization. Height‐based pole dosing results in varying IVM exposure in different weight bands, hence using weighing scales for IVM dosing during MDA is recommended.

Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

The World Health Organization recommends ivermectin (IVM) and albendazole combination for preventive chemotherapy to control lymphatic filariasis. Most Neglected Tropical Disease programs use height‐based pool IVM dosing (roughly corresponding to 150–200 μg/kg). Additionally, pharmacokinetic (PK) profiles of IVM in the mass drug administration (MDA) setting, particularly in sub‐Saharan Africa populations, are lacking.

WHAT QUESTIONS DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

This study describes a population PK model of IVM administered in an MDA campaign and establishes the sources of IVM PK variability across the different dose pools in the Tanzanian population.

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

This study describes an IVM PK profile and compares weight‐ versus height‐based dosing in the Tanzanian population. The current height‐based poles dosing results in underdosing for individuals weighing greater than 70 kg compared to those weighing less than 70 kg.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE DRUG DISCOVERY, DEVELOPMENT, AND/OR THERAPEUTICS?

The study supports the use of weighing scales for precise estimation of the number of tablets and therefore dose of IVM during MDA.

INTRODUCTION

Lymphatic filariasis (LF), commonly known as elephantiasis, is an infection that attacks the lymphatic system. Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi, and Brugia timori are responsible for the etiology of LF. 1 , 2 W. bancrofti accounts for many disease conditions (>90%) seen in patients 3 and it is endemic in 78 countries, including Tanzania, infecting tens of millions of people. 4 The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends periodic mass drug administration (MDA) of anti‐helminthics to all at‐risk populations of LF transmission in endemic countries as preventive chemotherapy to halt the transmission of infection and eliminate LF. 5 In Tanzania, ivermectin (IVM) is given with albendazole (ALB) in MDA campaigns to halt transmission of LF in endemic communities since 2022. IVM acts by killing the larvae stage of the parasite but not adult worms. It has high binding affinity to glutamate‐gated chloride channels, which occurs in the invertebrate nerve and muscle cells, causing an increase in the permeability of the cell membrane to chloride ions with hyperpolarization of the nerve or muscle cell. 6 , 7 ALB has a vermicidal effect and acts by causing degenerative alterations in the intestinal cells of the worm by binding to the colchicine‐sensitive site of β‐tubulin of parasites, thus inhibiting its polymerization or assembly into microtubules. 8

Since 1987, IVM has been widely used in veterinary medicine 9 , 10 and its recent use in humans as preventive chemotherapy against LF has shown a good safety profile. 11 The effectiveness of IVM and ALB combinations (IA) in the MDA programs depends in part on their pharmacokinetic (PK) disposition. IVM is metabolized in the liver through the cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzyme system via CYP3A4/5 and CYP2C9. 12 , 13 Its elimination half‐life ranges between 25 and 80 h and its protein bound is 93%. 14 , 15 , 16 IVM does not cross the blood–brain barrier, but P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) encoded by ABCB1 gene plays a role in its uptake into the central nervous system causing neurotoxicity. 17 Similarly, P‐gp is responsible for efflux of IVM from the gastrointestinal tract membrane and henceforth affects its bioavailability. ALB is a substrate of CYP450, mainly through CYP2J2 and to lesser extent through CYP3A4/5. 18 Thus, ALB co‐administration may compete with IVM for CYP3A4/5‐mediated metabolism leading to drug interactions affecting its plasma exposures. A PK study in 23 healthy volunteers reported that the incidence of overall drug‐related adverse effects were significantly higher following concurrent administration of IVM, praziquantel, and albendazole compared to IVM plus albendazole, which influenced changes in IVM PKs significantly. 19 ALB can interact with many other drugs, such as cimetidine, dexamethasone, and anticonvulsants, leading to alteration of its PK exposure. 20 In addition, the PKs and consequently efficacy of IVM are influenced by food. 21 , 22 Apart from drug and food interactions, IVM disposition can be influenced by pharmacogenetic variations of drug metabolizing enzymes and transporter proteins, including P‐gp. 23 Each tablet of IVM contains 3 mg and the dose is usually 150–200 micrograms per kilogram (kg) body weight taken as a single dose. 5 However, due to logistical challenges of using weighing scales in resource limited settings, the WHO recommends the use of height‐based dosing in MDA programs. 5

The drug disposition characteristics of IVM can also be influenced by gender, age, nutrition, functional status of eliminating organs, comorbidities, and body mass index. 15 , 22 , 24 Therefore, characterization of IVM disposition and covariates in humans is pivotal in clinical practice when assessing its performance in terms of safety and efficacy during MDA programs in endemic communities.

Despite its wide use in humans as part of MDA, there are few drug interactions or dose finding clinical studies that characterized the PK properties of IVM in a few healthy volunteers, including children. 14 , 25 , 26 To the best of our knowledge, studies assessing IVM disposition kinetics administered as preventive chemotherapy in large populations with broad coverage of covariates are missing. We hereby report the PK disposition of IVM after MDA in LF endemic populations of rural Tanzania using a population‐based modeling analysis approach.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design, population, and setting

This PK study was nested in an active surveillance of safety among individuals receiving IA during MDA. 11 The study was conducted in LF endemic communities of the Mkinga district, Tanga region, which is a target area for MDA by the Neglected Tropical Diseases Control Program (NTDCP) in Tanzania. The aim of this study was to investigate the PK disposition of IVM and its association with sociodemographic and clinical parameters of study participants. Study participants were MDA eligible individuals aged between 5‐ and 78 years old living in LF endemic communities of the Mkinga district. Pregnant women and children below 5 years of age were excluded from the study, as IVM and ALB are contraindicated in these age groups. Therefore, a total of 468 eligible individuals were included as study participants. Before receiving MDA, all study participants were prescreened for circulating filarial antigens, microfilaremia (mf), and disease manifestations using AlereTM Filariasis Test Strip (Alere), as described previously. 27 All antigenemia‐positive participants were recalled for night blood sampling for the determination of mf using microscopy.

Treatment and quantification of IVM plasma levels

Study participants received IA in MDA campaigns as per the NTDCP and WHO treatment guidelines. 28 In brief, participants were given a standard dose of IVM (Merck Sharpe and Dohme) based on height (roughly corresponding to 150–200 μg/kg). Individuals weighing greater than 15 kg received 3‐, 6‐, 9‐, or 12‐mg IVM if their heights were greater than 90 cm and less than or equal to 119 cm, greater than 119 cm and less than or equal to 139 cm, greater than 139 cm and less than or equal to 159 cm, and greater than 159 cm, respectively. All individuals received albendazole 400 mg (GlaxoSmithKline). Individuals less than 15 kg did not participate in the MDA program. The drugs were administered by the directly observed treatment approach, as recommended by the WHO. Two mL of venous blood were collected at 0, 2, 4, and 6 h after drug administration from the antecubital arm vein. Plasma levels of IVM were quantified using liquid‐chromatography tandem mass spectrometry method as described previously by Duthaler et al 29 with some modifications.

Population pharmacokinetic analysis

Population PK (PopPK) modeling was done using NONMEM (version 7.5.0, Icon Development Solutions, http://www.iconplc.com) with PsN (version 5.2.6; https://uupharmacometrics.github.io/PsN/) and Pirana (version 2.9.9; https://www.certara.com/software/pirana‐modeling‐workbench/) as modeling and simulation workbenches. Data preparation and NONMEM results postprocessing and visualization were conducted using the R statistical software (version 4.1.2; www.r‐project.org).

The PopPK model was developed by adapting a reference model from literature. This was achieved by following the recommended best practices for using the PRIOR subroutine in NONMEM. 30 Maximum likelihood estimation method (FOCE‐I in NONMEM) was used for parameter estimation. Structural model parameters for individuals (Pi) were derived from equation Pi=PTV×eη, whereby, PTV is typical population parameter value and η (ETA) is randomly sampled from a normal distribution with mean of 0, and variance of ω2, that is, ηi=N0ω2. Proportional (prop), additive (add), or combined (add + prop) residual error models were tested, and the best model was selected to account for residual variability which may arise from experimental errors and model misspecification. Models were evaluated using likelihood ratio test, goodness‐of‐fit (GOF) plots, parameter uncertainty, and visual predictive checks (VPCs). Difference in objective function value (OFV) greater than 3.84 units (equivalent to p value ≤ = 0.05 for χ 2 distribution, degrees of freedom [DF] = 1) between nested models was considered a statistically significant improvement in model fit. Uncertainties of model parameters were measured by relative standard errors (RSEs) or bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs) for the final model. Interindividual variability (IIV) of model parameters, as measured by coefficient of variation (CV%), were calculated from ω2 using the formula: CV%=100×eω2−1. Potential covariates were identified by visual inspection of covariates versus η plots. However, only biologically plausible covariates were added to the model by manual stepwise forward inclusion only. Continuous and categorical covariates were added using the formular, P=TVP×COV/COVmedianβ and P=TVP×βCOV, respectively, where COV is covariate, taking values of 0 or 1 for categorical covariates, P is PK parameter value for individuals with covariate value COV, TVP is the typical population value of P, and β is the estimated covariate effect. Covariates were retained in the model if their addition led to greater than or equal to 3.84 decrease in OFV (i.e., p value ≤0.05, DF = 1). The tested covariates are presented in the table of subject characteristics (Table 1). Mixture modeling was used to find any unknown subpopulations with different typical parameter estimates compared to the main population. The final model was qualified by GOF plots, VPC, and nonparametric CIs obtained by fitting the final model to 200 bootstrapped samples of the current dataset (bootstrap CIs) (model code is presented in Data S1).

Evaluation of ivermectin height‐based dosing

IVM is currently dosed by height in resource limited settings per WHO treatment guidelines. 31 , 32 However, in several clinical trials, IVM doses of 150 μg/kg and 200 μg/kg were found to be equally efficacious in reduction of microfilariae for up to 12 months after treatment. We assessed if height‐based dosing of IVM results in doses greater than 150 μg/kg in different weight‐bands (i.e., 15–30, 31–40, 41–50, 51–60, 61–70, 71–80, and 80–90 kg). Doses that individuals received were divided by weight. Individuals were grouped into weight‐bands and proportion of individuals receiving less than 150 μg/kg in each weight‐band was calculated. We also assessed if the WHO recommended 200 μg/kg dosing followed by rounding to the nearest number of whole tablets achieved doses greater than 150 μg/kg in different weight‐bands. Individuals' weights were multiplied by 200 μg/kg and the resulting dose was rounded to the nearest number of the whole tablets; the final dose was divided by body weight and proportion of individuals receiving less than 150 μg/kg in each weight category was calculated. Finally, model‐based simulation was used to compare IVM exposure (area under the concentration‐time curve from zero to infinity [AUC0–∞]) across weight bands after the current weight‐ or height‐based dosing. In this regard, a virtual adult population was created by bootstrap resampling of individuals greater than 18 years from the current data while a virtual pediatric population was created using the WHO and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention growth charts after adjusting the LMS parameters using the current data, as suggested by Wasmann et al. 33

Ethics statement

The study obtained ethical clearance from the Medical Research Coordinating Committee of the National Institute for Medical Research, Tanzania (Certificate No. NIMR/HQ/R.8a/Vol. IX/2890 dated September 17, 2018). Before study initiation, a meeting was held with district authorities and community leaders to explain the objectives of the study including seeking their permission to conduct the study in their location. As this was a nested study, sensitization meetings were organized to explain the methodology to be used for those who were targeted for the PK study. Informed consent was obtained both orally and in writing after providing clarifications and explanations to the study participants. Assent was sought from parents or guardians in case of children.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 468 individuals (male subjects n = 279, 59.9%) were enrolled in this study. Table 1 presents descriptive statistics of demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics of the study participants stratified by doses of IVM received (genetic characteristics are presented in Table S2). Over half of the enrolled individuals (n = 248, 53%) received a dose of 12 mg. The p values were calculated for each variable to test for any significant differences in baseline characteristics among individuals who received different doses. Enlarged scrotum, painful scrotum, lymphadenopathy, lymphoedema, hydrocele, white blood cells, and monocytes were variables which did not show statistical differences among individuals in different doses (p > 0.05).

Population pharmacokinetic model

The dataset for PopPK modeling consisted of 468 individuals with a total of 1404 PK samples collected at 2‐, 4‐, and 6‐h after a single dose of IVM. Our literature search found five PopPK models of IVM all of which reported two‐compartment disposition kinetics. 25 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 Based on comparisons of OFV values obtained by Bayesian estimation of individual PK parameters using the identified models (with MAXEVAL = 0), we selected Duthaler's March 2019 model 25 as the reference model for our modeling. In addition to its relatively lower OFV compared to other models, Duthaler's March 2019 model was based on rich PK samples, had good precision of parameter estimates, and its predicted mean concentration profiles were more comparable to our observed mean profiles than other models (Figure S1).

The Duthaler's March 2019 model describes two‐compartment disposition kinetics coupled with transit absorption kinetics. The parameters of the model were apparent clearance (CL/F), apparent central volume (Vc/F), apparent inter‐compartment clearance, apparent peripheral volume, absorption rate constant (Ka), mean transit time (MTT), and number of transit compartments. Allometric scaling was applied to clearance (CL/F) and volume (Vc/F) with fixed allometric exponents i.e.CLi=CLpop×WT700.75andVci=Vcpop×WT70. Refitting the model with our data (after supplying priors and weights of the priors) led to a huge decrease in OFV (ΔOFV=−33664). Because we also aimed to test covariates for bioavailability, we estimated the IIV for bioavailability (IIV‐F1), whereas F1 was fixed to 1. This led to a further decrease in OFV (ΔOFV=−13), and therefore the model with IIV‐F1 (shrinkage = 22.4%) became our base model. The next step involved identifying parameters which could be stably estimated without supplying priors. This was achieved by comparing RSEs from the base model with those reported by Duthaler et al., followed by sensitivity analysis. The comparisons of the RSEs indicated that ratios of RSE for Vc/F, MTT, Ka, and IIV‐MTT were less than or equal to 0.5, therefore sensitivity analysis was conducted for these parameters. The sensitivity analysis showed that despite changing prior values by 50%, the estimates of MTT, Ka, and IIV‐MTT only changed by less than or equal to 10% indicating that these parameters were less sensitive to their respective priors. Therefore, priors for these parameters were removed in the subsequent covariate model development. Covariates versus η plots indicated that only the 3 mg dose was a potential covariate on bioavailability (F1). Dose was treated as a categorical covariate because the ETA_F1 (η) versus DOSE (covariate) plot indicated a discontinuous relationship which could not be fitted by neither linear nor exponential model but a step function. The plot indicated that whereas a median of ETA_F1 for 6‐, 9‐, and 12‐mg dose groups could be joined by horizontal line, median ETA_F1 for 3‐mg dose was higher than the rest. The η distribution plots indicated right skewed distribution of IIV‐MTT (shrinkage = 23%). Addition of the 3 mg dose as covariate on F1 led to improved model fit (ΔOFV=−10.0), whereas mixture modeling of the MTT parameter identified two subpopulations and resulted in statistically significant improvement in model fit (ΔOFV=−17, Table S3). The final PopPK parameter estimates, and bootstrap CIs are presented in Table 2. The RSE are consistent with the narrow bootstrap CIs and indicate that the parameters were estimated with good precision. Dose was the only identified covariate of F1; the 3 mg doses had about 48% higher bioavailability compared to the other higher doses. In addition to dose, a subpopulation (16% of the studied population) with 97.4% higher MTT was identified. Based on the GOF plots (Figures S2 and S3) and the VPC (Figure 1), the developed model provides an adequate description of the current data. Similarly, the comparison of observed and model estimated individual secondary PK parameters (Table 3) indicates that model estimates are in good agreement with the observed data. Based on Table 3, the model estimated AUC0–∞ indicate that individuals who received 3 mg doses had comparable exposure to the rest of the study participants who received 6, 9, and 12 mg.

Ivermectin dosing by height and weight

Based on the current height‐based dosing, 6.3% of individuals weighing less than 30 kg were underdosed (i.e., received <150 μg/kg), whereas about 23%, 30%, and 100% of individuals in the weight range of 61–70 kg, 71–80 kg, and greater than 80 kg, respectively, were underdosed. On the contrary, with the WHO recommended 200 μg/kg, rounded to the nearest number of whole IVM tablets (3 mg per tablet), only individuals weighing less than 30 kg would be underdosed; about 17.5% of these would receive less than 150 μg/kg because of rounding to the nearest number of whole tablets (Table S1). These findings were supported by results from model‐based simulations which showed that contrary to weight‐based dosing, height‐based dosing results in relatively lower AUC0–∞ for individuals weighing greater than 70 kg compared to those weighing less than 70 kg (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

IVM is a microfilaricidal drug commonly used in combination with ALB to prevent the spread of LF in endemic settings during MDA. The focus of our study was PKs of IVM administered with ALB to the general population who are at risk of infection. The disposition kinetics of IVM is an important determinant of its efficacy and safety. Data regarding the PKs of IVM in humans are scarce in the literature as most studies were conducted in animals. 24 We report the findings of a study which was designed to investigate the disposition of IVM including identifying predictors of its PK characteristics following MDA. This study was nested in the large safety surveillance study of MDA at Mkinga district, Tanga region, north‐eastern Tanzania. 11 IVM is usually given orally (3 mg tablet) based on body weight (150–200 μg/kg). 5 However, due to convenience, feasibility, and cost consideration in resource constrained countries, a field adapted approach that uses height to predict the body weight was used in this study, as recommended by the WHO 28 and as per routine MDA program procedures.

Blood samples were collected predose and at 2, 4, and 6 h postdose from 468 individuals. However, we could not estimate the AUC0–∞ and elimination half‐life which are critical PK parameters for determining the drug exposure as a measure of bioavailability. By establishing a PopPK model it was possible to use only four sampling timepoints to obtain the intended PK parameters and explore the predictors of IVM disposition. The PopPK analysis was developed according to a two‐compartment model, first order absorption, and linear disposition kinetics, as previous reported. 25 PopPK modeling has added advantages over conventional methods as it uses few sampling timepoints, less costly, and maximizes adherence to study protocol with minimal loss to follow‐up. IVM has an elimination half‐life ranging between 25 and 80 h. 14 , 15 , 16 Therefore, in our study, it could have been challenging to collect adequate samples considering the long half‐life range of IVM.

The developed PopPK model provided a good description of IVM observed data. The GOF criteria showed evidence that the final model was consistent with the observed data and that no bias was seen. The datapoints were scattered closely and randomly around the line of identity (Figure S2). In addition, the weighted residuals were homogenous and randomly distributed indicating that the error model was suitable for describing the variance of the data (Figure S2). Our model evaluation revealed that both fixed‐effects and random‐effects of the final model reflected the true observed data. The predictive criteria indicated that less than 5% of the data were located outside the range of 5th to 95thquantile (Figure 1) suggesting that the model accurately describes the central tendency and the variability of the data. The comparable observed and model estimated secondary PK parameters further support the good agreement between the modeled and observed data (Table 3). The developed model was robust and will help Neglected Tropical Disease (NTD) programs to design future PK studies, including exploring dosing schedules in populations subjected to MDA.

Following single oral dose of IVM (3–12 mg), the observed and model simulated AUC0–6 and maximum plasma concentration (C max) were comparable for each dose strata. When comparing the systemic bioavailability, we observed an overall decreasing trend in AUC0–∞ but an increasing trend in C max from lower doses (3 mg) to higher dose (12 mg). The estimated mean AUC0–∞was relatively higher than that reported in the previous study (1660 h*ng/mL). 25 The observed difference would be explained by the relatively higher sample size in the present study as compared to a previous study.

IVM is highly lipophilic with rapid oral absorption, thus widely distributed in the body. 14 In this study, we found a mean time to C max of 4 h both in observed and model simulated data consistently with previous studies. 25 The model estimated values for CL/F (7.67 L/h) is comparable to that reported by Duthaler et al. 25 (7.6 L/h) in healthy volunteers. However, the estimated apparent volume of distribution was higher in this study (146.1 L) compared to that reported by Duthaler et al. 25 (86.05 L). On the flip side, our findings are relatively less or higher but comparable to those reported by other previous studies (CL/F of 9.0 and 12.3 L/h; Vc/F of 115 and 190 L) in adult healthy volunteers. 16 , 35 This finding suggests a longer elimination half‐life in our study population as compared to the latter. Indeed, the estimated half‐life in this study was relatively higher but falling between the literature reported mean elimination half‐life for IVM dosed at 200 μg/kg (25 to 80 h). 14 , 15 , 16 The estimated terminal half‐life was higher in participants who received lower doses (66 h in the 3 mg group) than those who received higher doses (38 h in the 12 mg group; Table 3) suggesting differences in elimination and intercompartmental distribution constants due to the covariate effect of weight on CL/F and Vc/F.

To explore the predictors of IVM PKs, potential covariates were tested to the model considering all age categories subjected to MDA. This is a strength to our study as many other studies had not examined variations in children or elderly individuals, who normally take part in MDA. 25 , 26

Baseline characteristics did not show significant influence on IVM PKs agreeing to the findings of previous studies. 25 Our study observed a significant influence of body weight on IVM kinetics as reported in other studies. 25 Dose was also a significant predictor of IVM exposure. Our assessment of the current height‐based dosing revealed significant underdosing of some individuals weighing less than 30 kg, 61–70 kg, 71–80 kg, or greater than 80 kg. But for weight‐based dosing (i.e., 200 μg/kg) and rounding to the nearest number of whole IVM tablets (3 mg per tablet) only some individuals weighing less than 30 kg were underdosed. The model‐based simulations also supported this finding by indicating that contrary to weight‐based dosing, height‐based dosing results in relatively lower AUC0–∞ for individuals weighing greater than 70 kg compared to those weighing less than 70 kg. A study conducted by Goss et al. 38 reported that dosing by height‐based dosing of IVM resulted in 27% of individuals receiving treatment doses below those recommended by weight‐dosing.

IVM is metabolized in the liver through the cytochrome P450 enzyme system (CYP3A4, CYP3A5, and CYP2C9). 12 , 13 P‐gp transporter also plays a major role in IVM distribution in different body tissue. 12 , 17 Genetic variations in metabolizing enzymes and a transporter (ABCB1) had no significant influence on IVM PK when added to the model. A previous study reported a nonsignificant effect of ABCB1 genetic variations on the IVM PKs similar to the present study. 25 Furthermore, a study in Ghana compared the effect of single nucleotide polymorphism in ABCB1, CYP3A4, and CYP3A5 on the IVM responses reported nonsignificant differences between wild types and carriers. 39 The PK evaluation was key in this study as the intention was to investigate their influence on PKs and safety of IVM.

As a limitation to this study, food intake was not explored to determine its effect in the model. Considering the lipophilicity nature of IVM, it is pivotal to determine the effect of food on bioavailability of this drug in future studies.

In conclusion, we have demonstrated that a developed PopPK model is useful for describing IVM PK disposition kinetics in a large population exposed to MDA. The data suggests that the model may find applicability in future monitoring of MDA programs. The AUC0‐∞ and terminal half‐life were estimated using the established model. Dose and weight were the only significant covariates observed to influence the PKs of IVM.

As the MDA program uses height poles when dosing individuals, and because this has proven to underdose individuals in different weight bands, we recommend the use of weighing scales for precise estimation of the number of tablets and therefore weight‐based dosing of IVM during MDA. The correct dosing will lead to intended drug exposure and consequently reduce the transmission of LF in endemic communities.
