High Sensitivity ctDNA Analysis Using a Novel Panel and NOIR‐SS Technology for Monitoring Advanced Urothelial Carcinoma
Authors: Tetsuya Danno, Seiji Nakamura, Satoru Taguchi, Yoichi Fujii, Taro Teshima, Yusuke Sato, Taketo Kawai, Yukari Kobayashi, Koji Nagaoka, Yumi Ueda, Ryo Matoba, Kazuhiro Kakimi, Haruki Kume
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdvanced urothelial carcinoma (aUC) has a poor prognosis, and real‐time monitoring of treatment response remains clinically challenging. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has emerged as a promising non‐invasive biomarker that may reflect tumor burden and molecular response. In this study, we analyzed ctDNA dynamics using a urothelial carcinoma‐specific mutation panel and the non‐overlapping integrated read sequencing system (NOIR‐SS), a high‐fidelity deep sequencing platform incorporating molecular barcoding for enhanced analytical sensitivity. Tumor tissue and serial plasma samples were collected from 15 patients with aUC treated with dose‐dense methotrexate, vinblastine, doxorubicin, and cisplatin (ddMVAC). The custom panel targeted hotspot mutations in TP53, FGFR3, KRAS/HRAS, and the TERT promoter. Based on tumor‐derived mutations, a tumor‐informed approach was used to track ctDNA in plasma. ctDNA was detectable in 10 of 15 patients prior to ddMVAC and showed a trend toward association with total tumor volume, especially in cases with liver metastases. In contrast, ctDNA detection was limited in cases with only pulmonary metastases. Longitudinal changes in variant allele frequency largely mirrored treatment response, although discrepancies were observed in two cases, likely reflecting subclonal resistance. While the NOIR‐SS‐based assay proved sensitive and informative, limitations include the cost and time required for sequencing, potential temporal discordance between tissue and plasma sampling, and the absence of correction for clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential. Overall, ctDNA profiling using this targeted panel and NOIR‐SS suggested the feasibility of sensitive, non‐invasive molecular monitoring in aUC, and may have future clinical applicability if validated prospectively in larger cohorts.Keywords: cfDNA, ctDNA, liquid biopsy, NOIR‐SS, urothelial carcinoma

Introduction

Urothelial carcinoma (UC) is among the most common malignancies worldwide, with bladder cancer (BC) ranking approximately 10th in global cancer incidence and upper tract UC (UTUC) representing a rarer but clinically aggressive entity [1]. Histologically, UC is predominantly of the conventional urothelial (transitional cell) subtype; however, variant histologies—including squamous, glandular, micropapillary, and plasmacytoid differentiation—carry distinct prognostic implications and have been increasingly recognized as important determinants of clinical behavior and treatment response [1]. Diagnostically, cystoscopy with biopsy and urine cytology remain the cornerstones of initial evaluation, while cross‐sectional imaging is essential for staging and treatment planning. Notably, urine‐based DNA methylation biomarkers (e.g., Bladder EpiCheck, targeting 15 methylation loci) have demonstrated sensitivities and specificities superior to conventional cytology for detecting high‐grade UC and UTUC during surveillance, and methylation profiling has been linked to programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) expression patterns and immunotherapy response [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. At the molecular level, UC is characterized by a high mutational burden and recurrent genomic alterations involving the TERT promoter, TP53, FGFR3, PIK3CA, and KRAS/HRAS, among others [8]. These molecular features not only reflect distinct biological subgroups but also offer potential therapeutic targets and biomarker candidates. Notably, PD‐L1 expression—a key determinant of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) efficacy—varies substantially across UC molecular subtypes, and elevated PD‐L1 levels have been associated with responses to ICI therapy in advanced UC (aUC) [5, 6, 7]. aUC follows an aggressive clinical course, with median overall survival remaining dismally low at 1–2 years despite platinum‐based chemotherapy [9, 10]. Therapeutic options, including standard and salvage regimens such as gemcitabine–cisplatin, methotrexate–vinblastine–adriamycin–cisplatin, ICIs, and, more recently, the antibody–drug conjugate enfortumab vedotin, have been employed [11]. Moreover, targeted agents addressing FGFR mutations are under development and poised for integration into standard practice [12]. Nonetheless, the capacity for individualized prediction and real‐time monitoring of treatment efficacy remains a substantial clinical challenge.

Dose‐dense methotrexate, vinblastine, doxorubicin, and cisplatin (ddMVAC) has demonstrated considerable efficacy. In the GETUG/AFU V05 VESPER trial, ddMVAC as neoadjuvant chemotherapy for non‐metastatic muscle‐invasive BC yielded favorable three‐year disease‐free survival rates of 64%–66%, whereas postoperative chemotherapy was associated with lower treatment completion rates [13]. Notably, gastrointestinal disturbances and hematological toxicities—such as neutropenia and anemia—were significant adverse events. Recently, avelumab has garnered attention as a switch maintenance therapy following ddMVAC, with our group reporting that administration of avelumab after six cycles of ddMVAC significantly prolonged survival [14]. These findings suggest that avelumab after ddMVAC may emerge as a novel standard for aUC. Future studies are warranted to optimize the timing of avelumab initiation and refine methods for real‐time assessment of therapeutic efficacy.

In recent years, the utility of liquid biopsy in UC has attracted considerable interest, particularly with circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis emerging as a non‐invasive and highly sensitive tool for real‐time monitoring. Conventional imaging modalities are typically performed every one to three months to mitigate radiation exposure, thereby limiting prompt assessment of treatment response. Conversely, ctDNA analysis can capture tumor dynamics between treatment cycles, potentially facilitating early evaluation of therapeutic efficacy and enabling more informed treatment strategy optimization [15]. Conventional ctDNA assays—often predicated on tumor‐informed whole exome sequencing—have been hampered by low sensitivity, particularly given the minuscule quantities of cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) in plasma that necessitate highly accurate mutation detection [16, 17]. In response, advanced analytical techniques employing digital PCR and molecular barcode technology have been developed to enhance sensitivity.

In the present study, we sought to advance clinical utility through plasma cfDNA analysis in patients with aUC. Departing from whole‐exome approaches, we constructed a UC‐specific mutation panel tailored to the pathomorphological characteristics delineated by our group, thereby increasing read depth by limiting the spectrum of predominant driver mutations [8]. Furthermore, we implemented a non‐overlapping integrated read sequencing system (NOIR‐SS) that utilizes molecular barcode technology for next‐generation sequencing (NGS). This system incorporates a proprietary algorithm to eliminate errors introduced during PCR amplification—a limitation of conventional methods—and is capable of detecting rare mutations with frequencies as low as 0.1%. Previous applications of NOIR‐SS in lung and pancreatic cancers have demonstrated its clinical potential [18, 19]. The aim of this research is to develop a new UC panel with high sensitivity and clinical application, and to validate the tool in patients with aUC treated with ddMVAC. The final goal of this study is to enable real‐time monitoring by ctDNA analysis by applying the NOIR‐SS technology.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection and Sample Collection

The study enrolled 15 patients with aUC diagnosed between February 2021 and March 2023 at The University of Tokyo Hospital, all of whom received ddMVAC therapy (Figure S1). Formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue samples were obtained via biopsy or surgical resection prior to ddMVAC administration. Plasma samples were collected before ddMVAC cycles 1 (C1), 2 (C2), 4 (C4), and 6 (C6), as well as during post‐treatment follow‐up. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The University of Tokyo Hospital (approval number #G3511‐23), and all patients provided written informed consent. For tissue DNA extraction, Qiagen's QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit was utilized; for plasma, 2–5 mL per sample was processed using Qiagen's QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit. DNA concentrations were quantified using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer.

Creation of New UC Panels, Library NOIR‐SS

Mutation detection in both tissue and plasma samples was performed using NOIR‐SS, an NGS platform that incorporates molecular barcodes [19]. Prior studies have validated the capacity of molecular barcodes to correct for PCR and sequencing errors, thereby enabling highly accurate and absolute quantification of low‐frequency mutations in plasma. This was achieved by applying an exceedingly low error rate (0.00001%), computing p‐values based on a Poisson distribution, and designating a mutation as positive when p ≤ 0.001 [18, 19]. Specifically, for each sequenced locus, the observed number of mutant molecules was modeled against the expected background under a Poisson distribution using this error rate; a locus was called positive when the resulting p‐value was ≤ 0.001. This threshold was applied consistently across all plasma samples and time points. To minimize false positives, only mutations confirmed in paired tumor tissue were queried in plasma (tumor‐informed approach). Variability between sequencing runs was partially mitigated by the molecular barcode‐based consensus read approach inherent to NOIR‐SS.

In this study, the same error rate (0.00001%) and Poisson‐based positivity threshold (p ≤ 0.001) as described above were employed to derive p‐values and assign positivity. The variant allele frequency (VAF) was determined as the ratio of mutated molecules to the total number of molecules.

For tissue specimens, 40 ng of Covaris‐fragmented DNA was used; for plasma, the entire cfDNA extract was employed. Library preparation followed established protocols [18]. Primer sets were specifically designed for the detection of mutations frequently observed in UCs (TP53, FGFR3, KRAS/HRAS, and TERT), based on a previous work of our group [8] (Table S1). For tissue, all primer sets from Table S1 were utilized, whereas for plasma, only primers corresponding to tissue‐detected mutations—tumor‐informed assay—were applied. Sequencing was conducted on the Ion S5XL or Ion PGM system, yielding an average of 14,155 reads per primer site in tissue and 316,888 reads per site in plasma. Preprocessing of NGS data, including molecular barcode handling in NOIR‐SS, was performed as described in previous literature [18]. The presence of mutations in tissue and plasma was verified by inputting molecular barcode–processed consensus reads into the Integrative Genomics Viewer.

Tumor Volume Assessment in Pre‐Treatment Imaging

Tumor volume (cm3) at both primary and metastatic sites was estimated for each patient using computed tomography (CT) before treatment. Measurements of the maximum orthogonal diameters on horizontal and coronal sections—acquired from both non‐contrast and contrast‐enhanced CT—were used in conjunction with the ellipsoid approximation formula (Width × Length × Height × π/6) to assess the relationship between tumor volume and ctDNA positivity [20]. Detailed tumor volume data for each patient are provided in Table S2.

Evaluation of ctDNA Assays Generated From Tumor Information and Clinical Correlations

Individual ctDNA assays were generated based on tumor‐specific information. ctDNA dynamics were evaluated in the 10 patients who were ctDNA‐positive before C1, using serial plasma samples collected before C2, C4, and C6. In five of these patients, plasma was also analyzed both before and after progressive disease (PD). The results of mutation analysis in tissue and plasma are summarized in Tables S3 and S4, respectively. Table S3 lists the mutations detected in tumor tissue from 15 patients, including affected genes and VAFs. Table S4 presents the corresponding plasma cfDNA results, including the detection of tumor‐informed mutations, their VAFs, and the clinical time points at which plasma was collected (before C1, C2, C4, and C6, as well as extra1 and extra2).

Association Between ctDNA Status and Tumor Volume/Distribution

The relationship between ctDNA status before C1 and tumor volume—ascertained from CT imaging—was examined (Figure 2). Tumors in ctDNA‐positive patients tended to exhibit greater volume. For instance, Case #01 displayed the largest hepatic tumor volume (894.98 cm3) and the highest FGFR3 mutation VAF (33.01%) (Figures 2 and 3). Conversely, all three patients with isolated pulmonary lesions (Cases #05, #13, and #02) were ctDNA‐negative before C1; notably, Case #12 had no residual tumor following video‐assisted thoracic surgery, despite the presence of lung metastases. All three patients with isolated lung metastases (Cases #02, #05, #13) were ctDNA‐negative, whereas 10 of the remaining 12 patients were ctDNA‐positive.

ctDNA Status Before and After ddMVAC Treatment and Re‐Emergence After PD

Of the 15 cases, 10 were ctDNA‐positive before C1. In these patients, plasma samples were further analyzed before ddMVAC C2, C4, and C6 in Figure 3. Figure S2 encapsulates the tissue and plasma sampling intervals, treatment trajectories, clinical outcomes, and ctDNA status for all 15 aUC cases. In Case #01, although one mutated molecule (1/27,251) was detected before C6, it did not meet the predefined Poisson‐based p‐value threshold (p ≤ 0.001) and was therefore classified as ctDNA‐negative. In Case #15, only the sample before C6 was analyzed due to insufficient plasma availability at earlier cycles. In Case #08, although FGFR3 mutation analysis failed before C2, TP53 mutations were detected, thereby confirming ctDNA positivity.

Consequently, three patients (Cases #07, #10, and #14) were ctDNA‐negative before C2, two patients (Cases #01 and #06) before C4, and all patients were ctDNA‐negative before C6. Interestingly, Case #10 showed re‐emergence of ctDNA positivity before C4, followed by a return to negativity before C6.

Among the nine patients who were ctDNA‐negative at C6, one (Case #04) experienced PD as confirmed by imaging, indicating a discordance between ctDNA status and radiological findings.

In five patients who eventually developed PD (Case #01, #06, #10, #14, and #15), post‐progression plasma analysis revealed reappearance of ctDNA in four cases, suggestive of tumor progression (Figures S3 and 4). In Figure 4, the relationship between sequential alterations in CT imaging of liver metastasis and para‐aortic lymph node metastasis and liver metastasis and the corresponding variations in VAF is delineated for Case #01 and Case #14, respectively. Case #15 underwent radical surgery following resolution of metastases but developed pulmonary metastases 39 days postoperatively. In contrast, in Case #01, a second course of ddMVAC was administered as third‐line therapy for newly developed liver metastases. After three cycles, a decrease in VAF was observed, and CT imaging showed shrinkage of the target liver lesion. Nevertheless, new liver and pubic bone metastases were also identified on CT, indicating a discordance between radiographic findings and the VAF reduction.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the dynamics of ctDNA in aUC using NOIR‐SS, a deep sequencing platform that integrates a UC‐specific mutation panel and molecular barcoding technology. The results of this exploratory study suggest a potential association between tumor size, tumor location, and the presence or absence of ctDNA detection; however, these findings must be interpreted with caution, given the small sample size (n = 15). Furthermore, the dynamics of ctDNA observed in this study suggest its potential utility as a non‐invasive biomarker for evaluating treatment efficacy and assessing recurrence risk, pending prospective validation in larger cohorts. The findings of this study represent one of the first investigations to examine the potential clinical applicability of a new UC panel and NOIR‐SS technology for monitoring patients undergoing treatment for aUC.

Our study results showed a tendency toward ctDNA positivity in patients with larger tumor volumes, with particularly notable increases in VAF in cases with liver metastases, most prominently in Case #01 (hepatic tumor volume 894.98 cm3, FGFR3 VAF 33.01%). Notably, ctDNA was detected even in patients with relatively limited tumor burden (Cases #04 and #07), supporting the analytical sensitivity of the platform. Since the present approach does not enable copy number analysis, accurate quantification of the ctDNA tumor fraction was not feasible; accordingly, formal correlation analysis between the ctDNA tumor fraction and tumor volume could not be performed. Instead, we compared total pre‐treatment tumor volume between ctDNA‐positive (n = 10) and ctDNA‐negative (n = 5) patients using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Although no statistically significant difference was observed (median 48.1 cm3 vs. 9.1 cm3, p = 0.129; Figure S4), a trend toward larger tumor volume in ctDNA‐positive patients was noted. On the other hand, all three patients with isolated lung metastases were ctDNA‐negative, whereas 10 of the remaining 12 patients were ctDNA‐positive (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.018). In this context, published data suggest that both tumor volume and metastatic site may influence ctDNA detectability. Reinert et al. demonstrated in a colorectal cancer surveillance study that lung recurrences were 15.1 times more likely to be ctDNA‐negative than liver recurrences, a finding the authors attributed primarily to smaller lesion size at radiographic detection [21]. Caliez et al. reported that in metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, both total tumor volume and liver metastasis volume were significantly correlated with ctDNA quantity, with liver metastasis volume showing a stronger association (Spearman ρ = 0.500, p < 0.001) than total tumor volume (ρ = 0.353, p = 0.01) [22], suggesting that tumor volume and metastatic site may not be fully separable as determinants of ctDNA detectability. The mechanisms underlying the ctDNA‐negative pattern in patients with isolated lung metastases may be multiple and non‐mutually exclusive. Lung metastases in UC tend to occur at an earlier stage of systemic dissemination compared to liver metastases. In colorectal cancer, by contrast, it is hematogenous liver metastases via the portal vein that represent the first site of distant spread; when confined to the liver, such metastases remain potentially resectable and are associated with a comparatively favorable prognosis [23]. By the same logic, lung metastases in UC may represent a similarly early stage of hematogenous dissemination [10], at which tumor‐derived DNA has not yet been shed in sufficient quantities into the systemic circulation to be reliably detected, whereas liver metastases in UC typically indicate more advanced, systemically disseminated disease in which greater ctDNA detectability would be expected. Beyond these mechanisms, organ‐specific genetic and microenvironmental differences may also play a role. Sjödahl et al. demonstrated that molecular subtypes of UC exhibit organotropism, with the genomically unstable subtype significantly depleted of lung metastases [24]. Erlmeier et al. showed that spatial immunophenotypes of distant metastases—but not matched primary tumors—predict ICI response, indicating that the tumor microenvironment undergoes site‐specific remodeling during metastatic evolution [25]. Furthermore, Loriot et al. reported that liver metastases in mUC show distinct transcriptomic profiles, including upregulation of immunosuppressive genes and lower immune infiltration compared to lung and lymph node metastases [26]. Whether the ctDNA‐negative pattern in lung metastases reflects organ‐specific biological differences, smaller lesion size at radiographic detection, or both cannot be determined from the present data; to our knowledge, no published study has directly investigated the relationship between organ‐specific tumor biology and ctDNA detectability in UC. Ideally, ctDNA detectability should be evaluated within the same metastatic organ using methods that enable accurate quantification of ctDNA levels—for instance, by applying established approaches for quantifying ctDNA fraction, including cancer cell fraction estimation, as demonstrated by Sakatani et al. [27]—but such analyses were not feasible given the small and heterogeneous sample size of this cohort, leaving the relationship between tumor burden and ctDNA detectability insufficiently characterized and warranting investigation in larger studies with homogeneous metastatic site distribution.

Additionally, dynamic changes in VAF among ctDNA‐positive cases showed an apparent association with clinical outcomes. The disappearance of ctDNA during treatment was associated with a favorable treatment response, while the reappearance of ctDNA after progression was suggestive of recurrence or progression. For example, in Case #15, postoperative recurrence closely correlated with preoperative ctDNA positivity, suggesting the presence of minimal residual disease. These results are consistent with previous reports. Vandekerkhove et al. reported an association between decreases in ctDNA levels during treatment and favorable outcomes [28]. Lu et al. similarly demonstrated that changes in ctDNA during ICI therapy may precede detection of treatment effects by imaging, suggesting a potential role for early clinical intervention [29]. In summary, these findings suggest that the analytical methods used in this study may have potential future clinical applicability in ctDNA monitoring, though validation in larger cohorts is essential before definitive conclusions can be drawn.

The findings of this study are subject to several important limitations. The most critical is the small sample size (n = 15), which substantially restricts statistical power and limits the generalizability of all findings; this has been acknowledged throughout the manuscript, and all observations should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive. Beyond sample size, the following methodological limitations should also be noted. (1) Cost and technical complexity. (2) Tumor heterogeneity and temporal discordance. (3) Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP). (1) From a practical standpoint, CT imaging remains the standard method for response monitoring in aUC. Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) is faster and less expensive per target than NOIR‐SS‐based NGS but is limited to pre‐specified single‐nucleotide variants; consequently, simultaneous assessment of multiple mutations across several genes requires separate reactions for each target, increasing both reagent costs and the volume of plasma required. NOIR‐SS, by contrast, enables simultaneous interrogation of multiple loci in a single sequencing run through molecular barcoding‐based error correction, achieving 0.1% analytical sensitivity comparable to or exceeding that of ddPCR [30], albeit at higher initial reagent cost and longer turnaround time. Formal health‐economic analyses in larger cohorts will be required to assess the overall cost‐effectiveness of each approach relative to current monitoring strategies. (2) Regarding tumor heterogeneity, tumor‐informed assays may fail to capture real‐time clonal dynamics due to temporal discordance between tissue and plasma. In our study, tissue samples were generally obtained immediately prior to treatment; however, in Case #13, more than one year had passed since tissue collection, which may explain the discordance between the tissue mutation profile and ctDNA negativity before C1. This highlights a key limitation of tumor‐informed approaches when biopsies are not contemporaneous with treatment initiation. By contrast, ctDNA‐based monitoring enables real‐time assessment and may more accurately reflect current tumor heterogeneity [27, 28]. However, this approach is inherently limited by the low quantity of ctDNA in plasma, especially in cases with low tumor burden, increasing the likelihood of false negatives and background noise. Crowley et al. noted that low ctDNA concentrations present significant technical and biological challenges, particularly in early‐stage disease [31]. When performing ctDNA analysis using a tumor‐informed assay, it is recommended to perform a biopsy of the primary tumor and metastatic sites immediately prior to treatment, if possible. In addition, chemotherapeutic pressure can drive clonal evolution or the emergence of resistant clones that are not represented in archival tumor tissue [32]. In Case #04, ctDNA remained negative despite confirmed progression. Similarly, in Case #01, although the target liver metastasis shrank and VAF decreased after three cycles of the second ddMVAC course, new metastatic lesions nonetheless emerged. These likely reflect the emergence of treatment‐resistant clones undetectable by our assay. (3) We cannot formally exclude CHIP as a contributor to low‐level VAF signals, particularly for TP53 mutations detected at VAFs below 1% (e.g., Case #14: 0.10% before C1; Case #15: 0.26% before C1). In the present study, two features partially mitigate this concern: The use of a tumor‐informed approach—querying only mutations confirmed in paired tumor tissue—reduces the likelihood of detecting CHIP‐specific variants absent from the tumor; and the consistent decline in VAF during ddMVAC treatment observed in most ctDNA‐positive cases is inconsistent with the stable or slowly progressive nature of CHIP‐associated variants. Nevertheless, a formal distinction between low‐level ctDNA signals and CHIP requires paired leukocyte DNA analysis with CHIP‐filtering pipelines such as eVIDENCE [33], which was not performed in this study and should be incorporated as a standard quality‐control measure in future investigations, as demonstrated by Sakatani et al. [27].

Further advancements in NOIR‐SS technology and expansion of mutation panels are anticipated to enhance analytical sensitivity and broaden clinical applicability. Prospective validation in adequately powered large‐scale cohort studies will be essential before ctDNA monitoring using this approach can be considered for clinical translation, though the present findings suggest its potential to ultimately contribute to risk stratification and individualized treatment planning in aUC.

In conclusion, this exploratory study demonstrated that ctDNA dynamics, assessed using a UC‐specific panel and NOIR‐SS technology, largely mirror clinical outcomes in patients with aUC, and that ctDNA detectability may be influenced by metastatic site and tumor volume. These preliminary and descriptive findings suggest the feasibility of sensitive, non‐invasive molecular monitoring and highlight the potential of ctDNA as a future biomarker for treatment response assessment and recurrence risk evaluation. However, given the small sample size (n = 15) and the descriptive design of this study, all findings should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating and require validation in adequately powered larger prospective cohorts incorporating paired leukocyte DNA analysis for CHIP correction before the clinical utility of this approach can be rigorously evaluated and implementation considered.
