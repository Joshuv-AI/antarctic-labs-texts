Inset glenoid component stability and bone removal by implantation depth
Authors: Annelise Pageau, Yusra Pervez, Alexander Wolfe, George S. Athwal, Nikolas K. Knowles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAseptic loosening of on-lay all-polyethylene cemented glenoid components in total shoulder arthroplasty is a leading cause of revision surgery and may be accelerated by the rocking-horse phenomenon. In response to this, inset glenoid implants have been developed in hopes that they will lead to lower loosening rates. Presently, little literature exists on the ideal depth of insetting to minimize micromotion while also considering glenoid bone preservation. This study, therefore, evaluated a generic circular inset glenoid component implanted at 4 depths in osteoarthritic glenoids using finite element models. Micromotion under simulated joint loading, bone removal, and underlying bone density were compared. The goal was to determine an optimal inset depth that minimizes micromotion while preserving glenoid bone.MethodsFinite element models of 7 male osteoarthritic scapulae were generated from pre-operative computed tomography scans. Circular inset glenoid components were virtually implanted at 4 depths: 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% (inlay). Glenohumeral joint loading was simulated in 5 directions. Tangential (parallel) and normal (perpendicular) micromotions were measured across 6 backside regions of the component for each loading direction. The volume of bone removed for implantation and bone density within a 5-mm depth region beneath each component were also evaluated.ResultsNo significant relationship was found between inset depth and tangential micromotion across load directions or locations (P > .05). The 25% depth showed the greatest median tangential micromotion in 12 of 42 cases (29%), the 50% and 100% depths in 11 cases each (26%), and the 75% depth in 8 cases (19%). Similarly, no significant relationship was observed between inset depth and normal micromotion. The 25% depth had the greatest median normal micromotion in 15 of 42 cases (36%), followed by 75% in 13 cases (31%), 50% in 8 cases (19%), and 100% in 6 cases (14%).Bone removal increased significantly with increasing inset depth (P < .05). On average, the 100% depth required approximately 4 times more bone removal than the 25% depth. Bone density within the 5-mm depth region beneath the component was highest at the 25% depth. Significant differences were observed between the 25% depth and the 50% (P = .03), 75% (P = .03), and 100% depths (P = .049), while no significant differences were found among the deeper depths.ConclusionInset depth was not significantly associated with glenoid component micromotion, although a trend toward reduced micromotion with greater depth was observed. However, deeper insetting required substantially more bone removal and was associated with lower underlying bone density. Considering implant stability, bone preservation, and supporting bone density, these findings support glenoid component insetting at approximately 25-50% depth.

Aseptic all-polyethelene glenoid component loosening is one of the most common complications after total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA).1,2,18 Factors that contribute to glenoid component loosening include poor bone quality, insufficient glenoid preparation, poor placement, over-reaming, eccentric loading, and glenoid component design.26 Eccentric loading is often referred to as the “rocking-horse” phenomenon, which is characterized by polyethylene edge liftoff arising from the incongruous interaction between glenoid and humeral components.6 Evidence of glenoid component loosening is often visualized radiographically by the presence of radiolucent lines, and results in revision surgery in 2-10% of patients.17 Newer component designs and techniques have gained interest in recent years, especially in younger patients that may undergo multiple revision surgeries during their lifetime.7,8 Recently, inset glenoid components have been utilized in place of traditional onlay components in anticipation they may have lower rates of aseptic loosening.6,10,13,14,23

In contrast to traditional onlay glenoid components that require the entire glenoid surface to be reamed, inset glenoid components require reaming only to accommodate the component. It is thought that by insetting the glenoid component into the glenoid bone, stability will be improved and the effect of edge loading minimized. Finite element analysis (FEA) and cadaveric studies using inset components have shown reduced glenoid loosening11,12 and clinical studies have demonstrated successful short-to mid-term patient outcomes.6,13,14 Previous inset components have been implanted at a fixed depth between 2 and 3 mm, depending on the manufacturer's specifications; however, peg length and configuration of commercially available inset components have been variable. This leads to difficulty in assessing the effect of component depth on component stability, with variability increased across studies due to a lack of consistency behind the applied methodology. The purpose of this study, therefore, was to evaluate the effect of glenoid component inset depth on micromotion using FEA. The goal was to determine the optimal percentage depth of an inset component that would minimize micromotion and volume of glenoid bone removal, while preserving the highest density remaining bone behind the component.

Methods

Computational modeling of the scapula

Computational models were generated from pre-operative computed tomography (CT) scans of patients indicated for THA (REB# 45088). The clinical group used to create the computational models consisted of 7 male patients between the ages of 48 and 70 years diagnosed with Walch type A1 osteoarthritis. The CT scans were completed on a multislice scanner (GE Discovery CT 750HD, Milwaukee, WI, USA) at a single energy (120 kVp, 144 mAs, pixel size 0.473 mm to 0.488 mm, 1.25 mm slice thickness). The scans were loaded into image processing software (Mimics v. 25.0, Materialise, Leuven, BE), where 3D models of the filled scapula were generated using semiautomated methods. The surface model was brought into FEM preprocessing software (3-Matic, v. 13.0, Materialise, Leuven, BE), where the alignment of the components occurred. Component depth and positioning were performed by a fellowship-trained shoulder surgeon (GSA). Once aligned, the surface models were transferred into MeshLab,5 where a Boolean subtraction was applied, virtually reaming the glenoid surface, creating the central peg hole, as well as cropping the medial surface of the scapula to be flat, and reduce model size in regions that were not evaluated. The prepped scapula was transferred back into 3-matic, where a surface mesh was generated with a maximum edge length of 2.0 mm. This surface mesh was imported into Abaqus (v.2022, Simulia, Providence, RI, USA), allowing for the conversion to a volume mesh using tetrahedral elements.22 Assignment of material properties to the bone volume mesh was done using Bonemat, using a dipotassium phosphate density-modulus relationship based on phantom calibrated density.9,20 Finally, the mesh with material properties was brought back into Abaqus, where the element type was reassigned to be a modified 10-node quadratic element as this element type allows for the modeling of complex contact parameters (C3D10 M).

Computational modeling of the component

A computational model was developed of a generic inset glenoid component. Four different sized circular components were designed, which varied based on diameter. The 4 sizes included an 18 mm diameter component (small), a 21 mm (medium), a 24 mm (large), and a 27 mm (extra-large). All components were 4.5 mm thick at the center. In each patient, different component depths were compared where the percentage of the component that was inset into the bone was varied. The depths of inset assessed were 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of component depth (Fig. 1). All patients in this population used an extra-large component at all depths, except for 2 models requiring a large component and 1 requiring a medium component to eliminate peg perforation at deeper depths. A component size-specific virtual reamer was used to prepare the surface of the modeled glenoid to avoid contact between the humeral component and excess glenoid bone during simulation. The glenoid component was assigned vitamin E crosslinked material properties (E = 1,250 MPa; ν = 0.46) and meshed using 8-node linear brick elements (C3D8R).Figure 1Implantation depths of the inset components. Volume of bone removal and bone density below inset components

The volume of the entire cropped scapula and scapular model with each component depth removed was collected to determine the volume of bone removed at each component depth. The average results are compared using a one-way repeated measures analysis of variance (Matlab. v.R2022a, Mathworks, Natick, MA), with significance set to P < .05. The volumetric bone mineral density was calculated within a 5-mm-depth region directly below each inset component at each depth. The average volumetric bone mineral density was compared using a one-way repeated measures analysis of variance, with significance set to P < .05.

Boundary conditions

A cement mantle (polymethyl methacrylate: E = 2,840 MPa; ν = 0.455) and a radiographic marker rod (cobalt chromium [CoCr]: E = 210,000 MPa; ν = 0.28) were included in the assembly of the inset component within the scapula. A simulated humeral component (51.1 mm radius; CoCr: E = 210,000 MPa; ν = 0.28) was used to apply loads through contact with the glenoid component (Fig. 2). The back side of the humeral head was fixed to a single point independent of the scapular assembly. Penalty-based contact conditions were assigned to each of the following pairs of components: humeral head and glenoid inset component (μ = 0.05), inset component and bone (μ = 0.2), as well as inset component and cement (μ = 0.6). The cement mantle was fully fixed to the bone. A compressive load of 956 N was applied to the center of the component through the humeral head, corresponding to 125% of the bodyweight of a 50th percentile male. This load vector was then applied sequentially at 10° in the following directions: inferior, inferior–posterior, posterior, posterior–superior, and superior (Fig. 3). Rotations were fixed in all directions, while no constraints were applied to translations.Figure 2A representative model of the hemisphere humeral component (black), inset component (orange), cement mantle (gray), radiographic marker rod (gray), and scapula.Figure 3Simulated load directions in the posterior (top) and superior–inferior directions (bottom), shown in a representative scapular bone model.

Analysis of simulation

Simulation was performed in Abaqus. The results of the simulation were exported as nondeformed vs. deformed node coordinates. Custom Matlab code was used to evaluate the micromotions of the inset component in 2 directions: normal and tangential. The normal micromotion was perpendicular to the virtually reamed glenoid surface, while tangential micromotion was parallel to the virtually reamed glenoid surface. The micromotion was determined for all 6 loading directions, where the results were classified into 7 locations on the backside of the component. These locations are relative to the positioning of the component, consisting of superior, posterior–superior, anterior–superior, center, posterior–inferior, anterior–inferior and inferior. Comparisons between the maximum micromotion in each location of the glenoid component across patients were reported as medians and interquartile range for both tangential and normal micromotion. Results are reported descriptively.

Relative tangential micromotion

There was no evident relationship between the magnitude of tangential micromotion and inset depth in any load direction or location (Fig. 4). In the compressive and posterior load directions, the 75% component depth exhibited the greatest median value for tangential micromotion across the 7 backside locations characterized by the frequency of having the largest median value. In the inferior, inferior–posterior, posterior–superior, and superior load direction, the 25% component depth exhibited the greatest median value for tangential micromotion across the 7 backside locations. When accounting for all load directions and backside locations together, the 25% component depth had the greatest median value for tangential micromotion in 12 of 42 cases (29%), while the 50% and 100% component depths had the greatest value in 11 of 42 cases (26%), and the 75% component depth had the greatest value in 8 of 42 cases (19%). Relative normal micromotion

There was again no evident relationship between the magnitude of micromotion and implantation depth in any load direction or location when considering normal micromotion (Fig. 5). In the compressive load direction, the 75% depth component had the greatest median value for normal micromotion across the 7 backside locations in terms of frequency. The inferior load direction was uniquely consistent, as the median value for normal micromotion occurred in the 25% depth component across all 7 backside locations. In the inferior–posterior, posterior, and superior load directions, there was no depth with a greater median value for the normal micromotion than others. The 50% depth component had the greatest median value for normal micromotion in the posterior–superior load direction across the 7 locations. In the consideration of all backside locations and load directions together, the 25% depth had the greatest median value for normal micromotion in 15 of 42 cases (36%). The 50% depth had the greatest value in 8 of 42 cases (19%), while the 75% accounted for 13 of the 42 cases (31%). The 100% component depth had the lowest frequency of greatest median value for normal micromotion, accounting for 6 of the 42 cases (14%). Discussion

The results of this study demonstrated minimal variations in relative micromotion based on the depth of component implantation. Furthermore, there was no evident relationship between implantation depth and greatest median value for tangential or normal micromotion in each individual load direction across the 7 backside locations. Therefore, no specific inset depth demonstrated superior behavior in comparison to the others with respect to micromotion under the current loading conditions. These conclusions suggests that the minimal range of component depth tested between 25% and 50% will lead to acceptable values of micromotion, while also significantly decreasing the volume of bone removal required for implantation. A range is clinically recommended as precise depth determination intraoperatively may be challenging, and micromotion values for incomplete seating under 25% depth are unknown and are expected to be substantially higher based on prior onlay research.

In studies using press-fit, noncemented components, it has been reported that micromotion of the implanted component is associated with fibrosis and can negatively impact stability.4 As glenoid loosening is such a common complication related to poor component stability, minimizing glenoid component micromotions may reduce fibrosis and component failure driven by the ‘rocking-horse effect.’ Previous literature has suggested that micromotion exceeding 150 μm may lead to implant loosening and failure, therefore an acceptable range has been defined as between 50 μm and 150 μm.3,19 In the current study, all micromotion values observed were less than 35 μm, meaning they were well under what has been previously classified as problematic. Although all polyethylene glenoid components do not have established values of micromotion to ensure sufficient initial component stability, the low observed micromotions detected with inset components indicate that the insetting design may improve stability and thereby reduce failure associated with glenoid loosening. When comparing the micromotions observed across all inset depths with published values for onlay polyethylene glenoid components, it is evident that the inset components exhibit more favorable performance.16 In a study examining 5 different onlay peg configurations, the mean value for the maximum normal and tangential micromotion was often found to be approximately 50 μm or greater.16 In many of the load directions, this onlay value is double or greater, than what was observed in the current inset investigation. In a study examining micromotion of the component pegs under concentric and eccentric loading, it was found that the micromotion observed was between 0 and 24 μm.24 These results more closely align with the results of the current study, but the micromotion at the single central peg was not examined. A study examining the backside of the glenoid component found that the average normal micromotion was between 45 and 130 μm and the average tangential micromotion was between 56 and 63 μm,21 although this evaluation was performed experimentally in a single onlay glenoid component. The magnitude of the median micromotion in the current study was much lower than these onlay values, which is more impactful considering that maximum values were considered in this context in comparison to averages. Overall, the peg configuration, loading vectors, and boundary conditions are not consistent between the current investigation and published results; therefore, it is important to consider the values in context rather than as a direct comparison.

The 25% depth component removed the least amount of glenoid bone during preparation and resulted in the densest remaining bone supporting the component, while the 50% depth component trended to having lower micromotion values than the 25%. As such, implanting an inset glenoid component between the range of 25% to 50% depth was most favorable. A TSA with an inset glenoid component will most frequently be used to treat osteoarthritis patients, and it is therefore best to minimize glenoid bone removal to allow the implant to rest on the densest remaining glenoid bone. Furthermore, it has been previously demonstrated that excessive glenoid reaming is associated with subsequent loosening.25 Since there was no evident relationship between inset depth and the greatest median value for normal or tangential micromotion, bone preservation with the 25% to 50% depth implantation is believed ideal.

It is intuitive to think of increase in depth resulting in a linear decrease in micromotion; however, micromotion is a combination of inset component deformation and glenoid bone deformation. With an inset component, more peripheral glenoid bone supports the outer edge of the component with increasing depth; however, the glenoid bone density also decreases, which alters the deformation of both the component and glenoid bone. Under the complex contact conditions that this study predicts, it is therefore not expected that this would result in a linear relationship between component depth and micromotion.

As with any study, there are limitations to be noted when contextualizing the results. The study population consisted of only older male patients with osteoarthritis, which are not necessarily representative of the general osteoarthritic population. In addition, the sample size was small (n = 7) and consisted of only male patients with A1 glenoid morphology. This allowed us to keep consistent implantation parameters; however, may be a limiting factor in the observation of trends between implantation depth and micromotion. Although retroverted glenoids also provide an interesting use-case, the retroverted orientation provides difficulty in modeling in these unconstrained models. The posterior-directed force vectors are representative of physiological glenohumeral joint loading; however, without modeling soft-tissue constraints, the models would disarticulate the humeral and glenoid components. Future validated models that include these constraints can include these more complex glenoid morphologies. Due to the use of pre-operative CT scans from OA patients undergoing TSA, experimental validation was not possible. This is an important limitation to consider when comparing to previously reported micromotion as it may impact the observed magnitude. The magnitude of the load vector applied in this study aligns with the loads from activities of daily living (26-164% of body-weight);15 however, this might be an underestimation of the force that would be applied during certain activities such as strenuous sports. Similarly, the cause of glenoid component failure clinically is complex and multifactorial and cannot be fully replicated using simulations studies alone. Future studies so incorporate damage-modeling and longitudinal patient analysis to better replicate the clinical failure scenarios.

Conclusion

The results of this FEA demonstrate that there are no clear advantages to insetting a glenoid component at deeper depths (>50%) when considering component micromotion. In addition, the results demonstrated significantly less glenoid bone removal, and increased bone density with the shallower depths tested for glenoid component insetting. As such, the results of this study favor the 25%-50% depth insetting of the glenoid component when considering minimization of micromotion, glenoid bone preservation, and maximization of the remaining bone density the implant is seated on.
