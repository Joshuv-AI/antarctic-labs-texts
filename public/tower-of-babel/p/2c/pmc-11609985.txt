COVID‐19 pandemic impact on gynecologic cancer treatment pathways in a Finnish tertiary center
Authors: Hanna Pikkujämsä, Tiina Luukkaala, Reita H. Nyberg, Karolina Louvanto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCOVID‐19 and new guidelines during the pandemic affected the gynecologic cancer treatment pathways, resulting in recorded delays and modifications in the treatment protocols. The aim of this study was to determine the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic in one of the major gynecologic cancer care centers in Finland, Tampere University Hospital.Material and MethodsOur retrospective register study included 909 patients that were new gynecologic cancer cases (uterine, cervical, vulvar, vaginal, or ovarian) referred to the Tampere University Hospital Gynecologic Oncology Outpatient Clinic between March 17th, 2018, and March 15th, 2022. The patients were divided into two separate groups depending on their time of referral: time before COVID (March 17th, 2018, to March 15th, 2020), and during COVID (March 16th, 2020, to March 15th, 2022). These groups were compared in terms of patient characteristics, different cancer types and stages, symptoms, and treatment methods.ResultsDuring the COVID‐19 pandemic, patients generally suffered from cancer symptoms longer (p < 0.003) and were more likely to be overweight (p = 0.035). The improved multidisciplinary team meeting gave the patients a faster route to their first intervention during COVID (p < 0.05). An insignificant shift toward nonsurgical first interventions and non‐curative intent was seen during COVID, but the multidisciplinary team treatment plans were mostly implemented accordingly on both eras. No decrease was seen in the number of new gynecologic cancer cases, and the one‐year overall survival remained the same in both groups.ConclusionsOverall, the COVID‐19 pandemic did not significantly alter treatment pathways in gynecologic cancer care at Tampere University Hospital. The number of new patients and given treatments remained relatively stable. During COVID, access from referral to cancer treatment was significantly accelerated, which is likely confounded by changes to the multidisciplinary team protocol made in early 2021.

INTRODUCTION

During the first months of 2020, the SARS‐CoV‐2 virus spread rapidly all around the world. This coronavirus caused a severe acute respiratory infection (COVID‐19), which was linked to many complications, hospitalizations, and deaths. The elderly and people with severe illnesses such as chronic respiratory or cardiovascular disease, immunosuppressive conditions, or cancer were at the most increased risk. 1 COVID‐19 was declared a pandemic by the World Health Organization in March 2020. 2

Around the world, health care professionals, politicians, and officials introduced different kinds of restrictions to ensure sufficient health care resources for the COVID‐19 patients and to limit infection transmissions between people. 3 Also in the medical field, in hospitals and health centers, COVID‐19 caused many kinds of rearrangements of the protocols and working measures. 4 , 5 In Finland, due to the COVID‐19 pandemic, the Finnish Government declared a state of emergency in Finland on March 16th, 2020. 6 After the declaration, Finnish national authorities introduced multiple temporary interventions and instructions to control the development of the COVID‐19 outbreak. Many of these interventions affected the health care in Finland, as the main effort was to ensure sufficient COVID‐19 testing, treatment, and intensive care units for the COVID‐19 patients. Non‐urgent health care was allowed to be postponed, which led to a decline in outpatient visits and diagnoses. 7 Operation theaters were converted into intensive care units, which caused delays for elective surgeries. 6 Although cancer screening programs remained mostly open in Finland, lower attendance rates were observed in 2020. 8

Gynecologic cancer patients (uterine, cervical, vulvar, vaginal, and ovarian cancers) were considered to have a higher risk of severe COVID‐19 infection and thus were vulnerable to its complications. Different national and international gynecologic organizations published updated guidelines to the care of their patients with an aim to limit healthcare‐associated COVID‐19 transmission. 9 , 10 , 11 These guidelines included COVID‐19 screening, limiting the number of visitors in health care facilities, increasing hygiene, using personal protective equipment, and undertaking remote medical consultations, and also prioritizing surgeries for severe acute complications of the cancer over elective primary or interval debulking surgeries and using nonsurgical treatments instead. The guidelines also provided different urgency ratings and protocols for managing cancer care that varied between different types of gynecologic cancers. 9 , 10 , 11

Around the world, studies have reported that these interventions have impacted gynecologic cancer care, and the results have varied locally and over time. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 There have been delays in gynecologic cancer diagnosis, and the overall rates of diagnosis have decreased. 12 , 14 , 20 The aim of this study was to determine the effect of the COVID‐19 pandemic on gynecologic cancer care implementations and adjuvant therapies, patient characteristics, and survival of the patients at Tampere University Hospital in Finland (TAUH).

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study participants and evaluated outcomes

The COVID‐19 pandemic impact on gynecoLogic cAnceR cAre (CLARA) study is a retrospective single‐center hospital‐based study that included a total of 909 women from the Pirkanmaa, Southern Ostrobothia, and Tavastia Proper areas that were referred to the gynecologic oncology unit at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology at TAUH, Finland. TAUH is one of the five university hospitals in Finland and covers roughly 20% of all Finnish gynecologic cancer care. Medical records and charts were reviewed from March 17th, 2018, to March 15th, 2022, to collect the data for this study. All patients diagnosed with new gynecologic cancer of uterine (ICD‐10: C54), peritoneal, ovarian and tubal (C48, C56, or C57), cervical (C53), vulvar (C51), or vaginal (C52) origin and referred to TAUH during the study period were included.

Patients' treatment pathways were followed by collecting information and the dates of referrals, appointments in specialized gynecologic oncology care, and cancer treatments. Delays to treatment were determined as the number of days from the referral date to the first appointment with a gynecologic oncologist, to the multidisciplinary team meeting (MDT), and to the first intervention. Possible deaths among these patients were registered up to March 8th, 2023. The overall survival was calculated from the total number of deaths within a year of follow‐up time. The duration of symptoms was defined by the patients' own assessment of the duration of cancer‐related symptoms at the time of the first gynecologic oncology appointment; these included those typically associated with gynecological cancers, such as, for example, abnormal vaginal bleeding, pelvic pain, abdominal bloating, or vulvar itching and irritation. BMI was calculated if the patient's height and weight were known at the time of the referral or during the first gynecologic oncologist appointment.

During the pandemic, on January 2021, the protocol for MDT was changed so that all new cancer cases were evaluated in MDT before the first treatment intervention (Figure 1). This was done to streamline referral processing and treatment planning. As a result, most of the new cases, regardless of the cancer type, are now addressed in the MDT meeting before the first treatment intervention, instead of being discussed only after a pathology report is available.

Statistical analyses

The patients were divided into two groups depending on the date of the referral to the gynecologic oncology specialist at TAUH, which led to the gynecologic cancer diagnosis and treatment pathway. Two equal time periods were chosen: the first group included referrals received from March 17th, 2018, to March 15th, 2020 (the 2 years before the COVID‐19 pandemic) and the second from March 16th, 2020, to March 15th, 2022 (the first 2 years during the COVID pandemic, i.e., 2 years after the first declaration of the state of emergency due to COVID‐19 in Finland). Each gynecologic cancer diagnostic time, treatment pathways, delays in treatment, and medical interventions were evaluated between each cancer type and the two groups stated above. Pearson chi‐square, Fischer's exact tests, or Mann–Whitney tests were used for statistical analyses. Two‐sided p‐values under 0.05 were considered as statistically significant. Analyses were made with IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

The BMI could be calculated for 405 patients (90%) before COVID and 433 patients (94%) during COVID. During COVID, the frequency of overweight (BMI >25) patients increased significantly (p = 0.035) compared to the time before COVID, and the rate of obesity (BMI >30) was 36% and 29%, respectively.

FIGO staging of cancer was divided into local or locally advanced cancer (FIGO Stages I–II) and metastatic cancer (FIGO Stages III–IV). Concerning stages, no statistically significant differences were found in the whole study population or in any cancer subgroup. In vulvar and vaginal cancer, the difference in stages before and during COVID was most notable, although not statistically significant, as the rate of metastatic cancer increased from 39% to 57% in the COVID era. A slightly similar trend was observed also in the cervical cancer subgroup.

Due to different lengths of follow‐up between the study groups, the mortality rates are not statistically comparable. The 1‐year overall survival remained similar in the whole study population both before and during COVID: 87% vs 87%, respectively. The worst prognosis was during the COVID subgroup of vulvar and vaginal cancers, as more deaths were recorded compared to the before COVID group, 19 vs 16 deaths, despite a notably shorter follow‐up time. However, the survival rates and number of deaths did not differ statistically between the groups.

The referral pathways of these gynecologic cancer patients are shown in Table 2. The sources of referral remained the same in both time eras. However, in the whole study population, the patients had cancer symptoms before getting the diagnosis significantly longer during COVID than before (p = 0.003). Before COVID, 34% of the patients had symptoms for less than 4 weeks before the diagnosis, whereas during COVID the percentage of these patients was reduced to 25%. During COVID, a greater portion of patients (37% vs. 26% before COVID) suffered from symptoms for more than 3 months before the diagnosis. In both before and during COVID groups, 5% of patients had missing information on the duration of symptoms. In a sub‐analysis comparing the duration of symptoms before and during COVID separately in people over and under 65 years of age, the difference was statistically significant only in the older age group (p = 0.028), while in the other group the difference was not significant (p = 0.084). Before COVID in the older age group, 6% were asymptomatic, 39% had symptoms for less than 4 weeks, 26% for 1–3 months, 25% had symptoms for more than 3 months, and 5% of patients had missing information on duration of symptoms. During COVID, the corresponding numbers were 7%, 26%, 29%, 33%, and 6%, respectively. Among cervical cancer patients, the change in duration of symptoms was the most prominent. Before COVID, 75% of patients were either asymptomatic or had symptoms for less than 4 weeks, 12% had symptoms for 1–3 months, and 10% had symptoms for more than 3 months. During COVID, the percentages were 44%, 16%, and 38%, respectively (p = 0.002).

All treatment delay variables examined were significantly shorter during COVID than before when the whole study population was examined, that is, treatment was accessed faster when a patient was finally referred to TAUH (Table 2). The delay from the referral to the first gynecologic oncology appointment among all cancers combined was reduced from a median of 13 to 12 days (p < 0.001), the delay from referral to MDT meeting from a median of 40 to 16 days (p < 0.001), the delay from referral to the first intervention from a median of 28 to 25 days (p < 0.001), and the delay from the first gynecologic oncology appointment to the first intervention from a median of 14 to 11 days (p = 0.009).

Although fewer first‐line treatments were started with curative intent during COVID than before (76% vs. 80%), the difference was not statistically significant (Table 3). The only statistically significant change in treatment strategies was observed among the uterine cancer patients, of whom 198 (92%) and 194 (85%) patients received surgery as the first form of intervention before and during COVID, respectively (p = 0.028). Among the subgroup of vulvar and vaginal cancers, the frequency of primary surgery decreased from 62% to 49% as the rate of radiotherapy increased from 31% to 65% before and during COVID; however, these changes were not statistically significant.

During chemotherapy, the use of granulocyte colony‐stimulating factors (G‐CSF) increased significantly during COVID among the uterine cancer subgroup, where 56% of the patients used these compared to 31% in the before COVID group (p < 0.001), and the same increase in G‐CSF use was seen among the ovarian cancer subgroup (64% vs. 30%, p < 0.001) (Table 3). Despite the more frequent use of G‐CSFs during the COVID era, delays in administering chemotherapy in the ovarian cancer subgroup were more common during COVID than before (22% vs. 14%, p = 0.04). These delays were caused, for example, by infections, abnormal blood values, or other reasons.

Radiation or chemoradiation was administered significantly more often during COVID among the vulvar and vaginal cancer subgroups than before COVID (65% vs. 31%, p = 0.003). In other cancer subgroups or in the whole study population, the frequency of radiotherapy was not significantly altered by COVID (Table 3).

In the whole study population, most of the original MDT treatment plans were implemented without alterations before and during COVID. Among the vulvar and vaginal cancer patients, the alterations of the original treatment plan were more frequent before COVID than during the pandemic, 82% vs. 92%, respectively (p = 0.039).

DISCUSSION

In our study, gynecologic cancer patients suffered from self‐reported cancer‐related symptoms for a longer period before diagnosis during the COVID‐19 pandemic and were more likely to be overweight or obese. During the pandemic, however, likely affected by our improved MDT meeting protocol, the patients had a faster route to their first intervention compared to the time before. Some small but nonsignificant differences were observed in treatment methods and goals of treatment, especially in the vulvar and vaginal cancer subgroups. The use of granulocyte colony‐stimulating factors doubled during COVID. No decrease was seen in the number of new gynecologic cancer cases nor in the one‐year overall survival among the patients during COVID.

The first wave of the COVID‐19 pandemic came to Finland relatively late, which gave national authorities and health care specialists time to reflect on the actions of the other countries and to prepare for the changes in the healthcare capacity and protocols caused by the pandemic. 6 Compared to many other countries, there was only a moderate number of COVID‐19‐related hospitalizations during the pandemic in Finland. 21 Likely, these factors likely preserved healthcare capacity and made it possible to maintain proper cancer treatment pathways. Interestingly, in this study cohort, we only found 14 confirmed COVID‐19 infections in the laboratory of TAUH by September 2022. However, it is likely that more COVID‐19 cases were detected in other laboratories or by home testing kits and are there for missed from our study.

The pandemic did not seem to cause major changes in treatment pathways for gynecologic cancer patients in TAUH. Significant changes in referral rates or delays in treatment during COVID were not observed. However, the longer symptomatic period before cancer diagnosis detected during COVID cannot be ignored, which can explain the trend toward more advanced stages, particularly in the vulvar and vaginal cancer subgroups. The cause of this delay remains unclear. Patients over 65 years in our study might have waited longer before seeking care since they were encouraged to stay at home, in isolation, at the beginning of the pandemic. Some may have also experienced difficulties in accessing treatment in primary care. During 2020, the same laboratory analyzer was used for detecting COVID‐19 and human papilloma viruses (HPV) at TAUH, which caused some delays in reporting the cervical cancer screening results. This might explain the decline in asymptomatic cervical cancer cases in our area. On the other hand, it is possible that patients have paid attention to their own health more closely or have been asked more detailed questions about their symptoms by their clinicians during the pandemic, which may cause potential research bias about the symptomatic periods in the study. The increased BMI that was recorded among our patients has also been seen in other studies. 22 , 23 This is speculated to be due to the decrease in physical activity during COVID‐19 restrictions and lockdowns, which is also most likely the case in this study cohort.

In this study, the treatment pathway for gynecologic cancer patients, especially the access to MDT meetings, was accelerated during the COVID‐19 pandemic. It is difficult to disentangle the effects of the pandemic from the change in the MDT protocol, as it is possible that during the pandemic treatment pathways were accelerated regardless. However, as the number of monthly referrals remained stable during COVID, the faster access cannot be explained by a smaller number of patients. It is likely that the gynecologic oncology clinic's MDT protocol enhancement in early 2021 had a major effect in accelerating the treatment pathways during COVID. Similar trends were also seen in the Netherlands, where the first visit at the outpatient clinic to the beginning of the first treatment was also significantly shortened during 2020, which was explained by the postponement of benign gynecologic surgery, whereas in a single‐center study from Portugal, median time from MDT meeting to treatment initiation increased significantly for endometrial cancer patients. 17 , 18

In our study, primary cytoreductive surgery remained the most common first intervention, and no significant delays or changes in the extent of surgery were observed. In other countries the frequency of neoadjuvant chemotherapy was increased 17 , 18 ; this increase was not however seen in our cohort. The increased use of G‐CSFs in the post‐COVID group was expected, as TAUH had recommended their use early in the pandemic to prevent chemotherapy‐related infections.

Of all the gynecologic cancer types, the biggest impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic was observed in the vulvar and vaginal cancer subgroups. In our cohort, possibly due to a small study group, statistical significance was not reached in all possible variables, but the impact is clinically significant. 24 The patients in this subgroup during COVID were older and their cancers more advanced, which could explain the inferior 1‐year overall survival rate compared to the before COVID era. In addition, the rate of primary surgery diminished, and the rate of radiotherapy increased considerably in this subgroup. Although we have not separated primary (chemo)radiation and adjuvant radiation in our study, it is likely that the increase was caused by more advanced vulvar tumors, not operable at the time of the diagnosis. Major changes in the treatment of vulvar and vaginal cancer during the COVID‐19 pandemic are not reported in other countries. 16 , 17 , 18 It is possible that delays in reaching primary care during the COVID‐19 pandemic affected referral and diagnostic pathways for vulvar and vaginal cancer patients in a way that caused advancing of the cancer.

The strength of our study is that TAUH oversees roughly one‐fifth of all gynecologic cancer patients in Finland each year. Therefore, despite being a single‐center register study, our study provides a good impression of the treatment pathways of gynecologic cancer patients in Finland during the pandemic. The weakness of the study is that during our long observation time of 4 years, some protocol changes may have occurred regardless of the pandemic, which can confound the results. In addition, these results cannot yet draw any conclusions on the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on the prognosis of the gynecologic cancer patients. For that, longer follow‐up will be needed. Also, in some cases, lack of information about the adjuvant treatments from referring central hospitals may have caused some research bias regarding the implementation of the treatment plan.

CONCLUSION

Overall, the COVID‐19 pandemic did not significantly affect the treatment pathways of gynecologic cancer patients at TAUH. The greatest delays during the COVID era in cancer diagnosis and treatment seemed to be caused by the longer symptomatic period before the patients were referred to a specialized cancer clinic. The most crucial change in the COVID era was in the MDT protocol in early 2021, which likely has accelerated the treatment pathways after referral and the cancer patients receive their first intervention faster. This highlights the importance of MDT meetings in the planning and treatment of gynecologic cancer patients regardless of a possible pandemic situation.
