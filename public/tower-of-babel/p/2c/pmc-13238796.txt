Diagnostic reassessment in patients previously diagnosed with childhood‐onset epilepsy during the transition to adult care: A retrospective cohort study in a tertiary epilepsy center
Authors: Tetsuhiro Fukuyama, Masaru Nasuno, Manami Yabe, Makoto Nishioka, Yumi Hoshino, Takenori Natsume, Kohei Kanaya, Kyoko Takano, Tomoki Kaneko, Yushi Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the frequency, predictors, and clinical implications of diagnostic reassessment in patients previously diagnosed with childhood‐onset epilepsy during the transition period to adult care at a tertiary epilepsy center.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 317 patients previously diagnosed with childhood‐onset epilepsy who underwent diagnostic reassessment between April 2018 and December 2023 at age 16 years or older at a tertiary epilepsy center in Japan. Diagnostic revision was defined as a newly established or corrected epilepsy/epilepsy syndrome diagnosis, identification of structural or genetic/metabolic etiologies, or diagnosis of non‐epileptic conditions. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of diagnostic revision.ResultsA new or revised diagnosis was established in 60 of the 317 patients (18.9%). Independent predictors of diagnostic revision included exclusively non‐motor seizures (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 7.610; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.660–21.767; p < 0.001) and weekly or monthly seizure frequency (aOR = 3.370; 95% CI: 1.265–8.976; p = 0.015), whereas prior visits to other epilepsy centers were strongly protective (aOR = 0.047; 95% CI: 0.015–0.146; p < 0.001). Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results. Newly identified etiologies include focal cortical dysplasia, hippocampal sclerosis, and pathogenic genetic variants such as CDKL5, PCDH19, and SYNGAP1. Diagnostic reassessment facilitated antiseizure medication withdrawal in patients with self‐limited epilepsy and non‐epileptic events.SignificanceNearly one in five patients previously diagnosed with childhood‐onset epilepsy required diagnostic revision during the transition to adult care. Non‐motor seizure semiology and moderate seizure frequency were the major predictors of diagnostic revision, highlighting the diagnostic uncertainty associated with subtle clinical presentations. These findings highlight that systematic reassessment, especially for individuals who have not previously undergone specialized epilepsy center evaluation, is essential for achieving an accurate diagnosis and optimizing management in adulthood.Plain Language SummaryMany people diagnosed with epilepsy in childhood continue medical care into adulthood, but their diagnosis may need to be reviewed later in life. INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a neurological disorder that frequently begins in childhood and often requires ongoing medical care in adulthood. Although the initial diagnosis is typically established at disease onset, subsequent changes in the clinical course, advances in diagnostic imaging and genetic testing, and the emergence of non‐epileptic events such as functional/dissociative seizures (FDS) or syncope may necessitate a diagnostic reassessment later in life. Reassessment of the diagnosis is particularly crucial during the transition to adult care, leading to optimized treatment in adulthood. 1

Diagnostic reviews are routinely performed as part of structured transition programs. In a multidisciplinary transition clinic, Geerlings et al. reported that 12.0% of adolescents and young adults experienced diagnostic changes after systematic evaluation, including 2.6% who were ultimately diagnosed with non‐epileptic conditions. 2 Similarly, Nabbout et al. studied a transition program within a tertiary reference center for rare epilepsies and identified previously unrecognized etiologies (genetic and structural) in three of 70 patients. 3 Although these studies highlight the diagnostic value of structured transition programs, both examined highly specialized populations whose clinical trajectories may differ from those of patients with childhood‐onset epilepsy managed within general regional healthcare systems.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have systematically examined the prevalence and clinical predictors of diagnostic reassessment in patients with childhood‐onset epilepsy transitioning to adult care within a broader regional healthcare context. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to evaluate the frequency, categories, and predictors of diagnostic revision in patients previously diagnosed with childhood‐onset epilepsy who underwent diagnostic reassessment during the transition to adult care at a tertiary epilepsy center. By clarifying the status and clinical significance of diagnostic revision, we sought to identify patient characteristics that may indicate the need for systematic reassessment during the transition from pediatric to adult care.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study included patients who had previously been diagnosed with childhood‐onset epilepsy (diagnosed at or before the age of 15 years) and later visited our epilepsy center, which consists of the epilepsy center of Shinshu University Hospital and the affiliated epilepsy outpatient clinic at Nagano Red Cross Hospital. All included patients underwent diagnostic reassessment between April 2018 and December 2023 at the age of 16 years or older. Eligible patients were identified from the medical records of both hospitals. Exclusion criteria were insufficient clinical information, inadequate follow‐up, inconclusive reassessment results, or the emergence of new seizure or seizure‐mimicking symptoms after 20 years of age. This age threshold applied specifically to the timing of new symptom emergence and did not restrict the age at diagnostic reassessment, which could occur after 20 years of age. We selected the 20‐year threshold to allow inclusion of patients who developed new symptoms during late adolescence (ages 16–19), which is clinically relevant to diagnostic reassessment during transition to adult care. Our epilepsy center was established in 2018 and meets the criteria for level 4 epilepsy centers as defined by the National Association of Epilepsy Centers (NAEC). 4 We investigated the frequency and clinical significance of diagnostic reassessment during the transition period.

Variables

The primary outcome was whether a new or revised diagnosis was established on reassessment (yes or no). Positive reassessment was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: a corrected or newly established diagnosis of epilepsy/epilepsy syndrome, identification of previously unknown structural abnormalities, confirmation of genetic and/or metabolic diagnoses, or diagnosis of non‐epileptic events such as FDS or syncope.

The following variables were examined as potential predictors: age at epilepsy onset, age at reassessment, sex, seizure type, epilepsy type, epilepsy syndrome classification, etiology, seizure frequency, specialty of the referring physician, history of prior visit to epilepsy centers including Nagano Children's Hospital that met the criteria of a level 3 center according to the NAEC, 4 intellectual disability (classified into four levels based on intelligence quotient), and drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE) according to the criteria of the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE). 5 Classification of seizure type, epilepsy type, epilepsy syndrome, and etiology type was based on the Commission Task Force reports of the ILAE. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 For seizure semiology classification, motor versus non‐motor categories were defined based on the observed clinical manifestations, regardless of whether the final diagnosis was epileptic or non‐epileptic. Accordingly, FDS presenting with motor phenomena were classified as motor seizures.

Data sources and measurements

Diagnostic reassessments were conducted through detailed clinical interviews, electroencephalography (EEG), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) by epilepsy specialists certified by the Japan Epilepsy Society. Genetic testing, long‐term video EEG monitoring, fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG‐PET) or single‐photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), and intracranial EEG were performed when clinically indicated.

Bias and study size

To reduce selection bias, all eligible patients who visited the epilepsy center or outpatient clinic during the study period were included. As the study was limited to patients referred to a tertiary epilepsy center and its affiliated clinic, referral bias may have been present. To minimize information bias, board‐certified epilepsy specialists performed all diagnostic reassessments. However, the diagnostic procedures were not standardized across patients, and variability in testing may have introduced an assessment bias.

No prior sample size calculations were performed. The final sample size of 317 patients was determined based on the number of eligible patients identified during the study period.

Quantitative variables and statistical methods

Age was treated as a continuous variable, and seizure frequency and intellectual disability were categorized. Univariate analyses were conducted using χ2 or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Variables with clinical relevance or significant associations in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression models using the enter method. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Clinically irrelevant or statistically insignificant variables (e.g., age and sex) were excluded from the final model. Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the primary model. All statistical analyses were conducted using the IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 29.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Participants

Of the 360 patients who met the eligibility criteria, 43 were excluded due to: insufficient clinical information; inadequate follow‐up; inconclusive reassessment results; emergence of new seizures or seizure‐mimicking symptoms after the age of 20 years; or diagnostic reassessment performed at age 16 years or older before April 2018, that is, prior to the start of the study period (Figure 1). Hence, a total of 317 patients were included in the final analyses.

Patient characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the 317 patients. The median age at diagnostic reassessment was 18 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 16–24 years), and the median age at seizure onset was 9 years (IQR: 4–12 years). A total of 144 (45.4%) patients were male. The most common specialty among the referring physicians was pediatric neurologist (n = 153, 48.3%). Focal epilepsy was the most prevalent epilepsy type (n = 136, 42.9%). At reassessment, 126 patients (39.7%) were seizure‐free for more than 1 year, whereas 110 (34.7%) experienced seizures at least monthly. Intellectual disabilities were present in 120 (37.9%) patients. A history of visits to other epilepsy centers was documented in 174 (54.9%) patients. DRE was observed in 139 (43.8%) patients. Structural abnormalities identified by neuroimaging were the most frequent (24 patients, 40.0%). These included newly detected lesions on MRI or functional imaging (FDG‐PET/SPECT), consistent with the seizure semiology and EEG findings. Hippocampal sclerosis was the most common finding (n = 12), followed by cortical dysplasia including focal (n = 3) and multilobar (n = 2).

Correction or reclassification of the epilepsy/epilepsy syndrome was identified in 22 (36.7%) patients. These cases were reclassified from nonspecific focal or unclassified epilepsy to idiopathic generalized epilepsies (IGE, n = 5), Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS, n = 5), self‐limited epilepsy with centro‐temporal spikes (SeLECTS, n = 3), self‐limited epilepsy with autonomic seizures (SeLEAS, n = 3), sleep‐related hypermotor epilepsy (SHE) (n = 2), progressive myoclonus epilepsies, self‐limited infantile epilepsy, epilepsy with myoclonic‐atonic seizures, and epileptic encephalopathy with late‐onset spasms.

Genetic or metabolic etiology was confirmed in nine patients (15.0%) through genetic or metabolic testing. The detected variants included CDKL5, PCDH19, and SYNGAP1, as well as Mowat–Wilson syndrome (n = 2), hyperornithinemia–hyperammonemia–homocitrullinuria syndrome, 13 15q duplication syndrome, and episodic ataxia type 2.

Non‐epileptic disorders were diagnosed in 10 patients (16.7%), including syncope (n = 5), FDS (n = 3), migraine, and episodic ataxia type 2.

Some patients fulfilled more than one category, including two with LGS who also had confirmed genetic diagnoses, two with SeLECTS who were also diagnosed with syncope, and one with episodic ataxia type 2 classified as both genetically confirmed and non‐epileptic.

Overall, these results illustrate that diagnostic revision encompasses a wide spectrum, from structural and syndromic reclassification to the identification of genetic etiologies and correction of non‐epileptic misdiagnoses, reflecting the multifactorial nature of diagnostic uncertainty in childhood‐onset epilepsy. Seizure semiology analysis revealed that patients who underwent diagnostic revision were more likely to experience only non‐motor seizures (p < 0.001), whereas those with bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (including generalized, focal, and unknown onsets) were predominant in the non‐revision group (p < 0.001). This pattern supports the notion that subtle non‐motor manifestations are associated with greater diagnostic uncertainty. Notably, all patients with FDS were classified as having motor semiology. Although three patients with FDS belonged to the diagnostic revision group, the overall association between exclusively non‐motor seizures and diagnostic revision remained robust, indicating that this finding was not driven by the inclusion of non‐epileptic motor events.

With respect to epilepsy classification, focal epilepsy (p = 0.043) and non‐epileptic conditions (p < 0.001) were more frequent in the revision group, whereas generalized epilepsy was more prevalent in those without diagnostic revision (p = 0.013).

Similarly, focal epilepsy syndromes (p < 0.001) were overrepresented among patients with diagnostic revision, whereas generalized epilepsy syndromes were markedly more frequent in those without diagnostic revision (p < 0.001).

Structural etiologies were also significantly more common in the revision group (p = 0.038).

Weekly or monthly seizure frequency at reassessment was significantly higher among patients with diagnostic revision (p = 0.002 and p = 0.013, respectively), whereas seizure freedom for more than 1 year or only yearly seizures was more common in those without diagnostic revision (p = 0.021 and p = 0.028).

DRE was also more prevalent in the revision group (p < 0.001), whereas a prior history of visits to epilepsy centers was strongly associated with the absence of diagnostic revision (p < 0.001).

Patients exhibiting non‐motor seizures were also significantly more likely to undergo diagnostic revision (aOR = 7.610; 95% CI, 2.660–21.767; p < 0.001), as were those with weekly or monthly seizure frequency compared with those who were seizure‐free or had yearly seizures (aOR = 3.370; 95% CI: 1.265–8.976; p = 0.015).

By contrast, the specialty of the referring physicians and the presence of structural brain abnormalities were not significantly associated with diagnostic revision.

These findings indicate that patients who had never been evaluated at a specialized epilepsy center, had non‐motor seizure manifestations, or experienced weekly or monthly seizures were at a substantially higher risk for diagnostic revision.

Sensitivity analyses

In sensitivity analysis A, which included epilepsy type (focal vs. generalized) as an additional covariate, the overall direction and magnitude of the associations remained unchanged, and the epilepsy type itself was not a significant predictor (aOR = 1.072; 95% CI: 0.305–3.771; p = 0.914).

In sensitivity analysis B, which was restricted to patients with purely motor or purely non‐motor seizures (excluding mixed semiology), non‐motor seizure semiology remained a robust independent predictor (aOR = 9.392; 95% CI: 3.122–28.253; p < 0.001), along with weekly or monthly seizure frequency (aOR 4.769, 95% CI 1.430–15.902, p = 0.011).

Conversely, the presence of structural abnormalities was inversely associated with diagnostic revision (aOR = 0.166; 95% CI: 0.039–0.704; p = 0.015), suggesting that patients with well‐defined structural etiologies are less likely to undergo diagnostic change.

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective cohort study of 317 patients who had been diagnosed with childhood‐onset epilepsy and were aged 16 years or older, a new or revised diagnosis was established in 60 patients (18.9%). Independent predictors of diagnostic revision included exclusively non‐motor seizures and weekly or monthly seizure frequency, whereas prior visits to other epilepsy centers were strongly protective. Importantly, patients without prior epilepsy center visits were not characterized by lower rates of DRE, suggesting that under‐referral was not simply attributable to milder disease. Rather, subtle seizure semiology and intermediate seizure frequency may have contributed to delayed referral and prolonged diagnostic uncertainty. Consistent with this interpretation, diagnostic revision occurred in a substantial proportion of patients who had never been evaluated at epilepsy centers, accounting for 54 of 143 cases (37.8%). By contrast, the specialty of the referring physicians, structural abnormalities on neuroimaging, and DRE were not independently associated with diagnostic revision after adjustment. Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent findings, confirming the robustness of these associations across alternative model specifications. Notably, daily seizure frequency and motor seizures were not associated with diagnostic revision, probably because these patients were more readily referred to other epilepsy centers at an earlier stage. These findings highlight that diagnostic reassessment remains clinically valuable, particularly for patients with ongoing non‐motor seizures or moderate seizure frequency who have not previously received care at specialized epilepsy centers.

Previous studies have consistently highlighted the difficulty in achieving diagnostic accuracy in epilepsy, particularly among patients referred to tertiary centers. Misdiagnosis rates of 4.6%–30% have been reported in specialist clinics and epilepsy centers, most commonly reflecting initial diagnostic errors in distinguishing epileptic from non‐epileptic events, such as cardiovascular syncope or FDS. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 While these studies primarily addressed the accuracy of the initial recognition of epilepsy versus non‐epilepsy, the present investigation captures a broader and later phase of the diagnostic process: The need for diagnostic reassessment among individuals already labeled as having childhood‐onset epilepsy. In this context, our observed diagnostic revision rate of 18.9% reflects not only correction of earlier misdiagnoses but also refinement of epilepsy syndrome classification, identification of structural etiologies, and establishment of genetic diagnoses. Thus, although a direct numerical comparison is not appropriate, both lines of evidence converge to indicate that diagnostic uncertainty persists throughout the continuum of epilepsy care, from initial diagnosis to adult follow‐up. Similarly, studies embedded within structured transition programs have demonstrated the value of systematic reassessment. In a multidisciplinary transition clinic, Geerlings et al. reported that 12% of adolescents and young adults experienced diagnostic changes, including 2.6% who were ultimately determined not to have epilepsy, 2 whereas Nabbout et al. identified previously unrecognized genetic or structural etiologies in a subset of patients within a tertiary center for rare epilepsies. 3 However, these programs were conducted in highly specialized pediatric epilepsy settings with long‐standing tertiary access. By contrast, our study showed that a substantial diagnostic yield persisted even in a broader regional population, many of whom had limited prior exposure to specialized epilepsy centers. This distinction suggests that the need for diagnostic reassessment may be even more pronounced in general clinical environments, where initial evaluations are less comprehensive.

Based on these observations, our data further highlight the specific clinical features associated with diagnostic uncertainty. Patients presenting exclusively with non‐motor seizures and those experiencing weekly or monthly seizures were significantly more likely to undergo diagnostic revision. This finding is consistent with that of Ferrer et al. who reported that adolescent‐onset focal epilepsy manifesting solely as non‐motor events is prone to delayed diagnosis, resulting in prolonged morbidity and psychosocial burden. 18 Together, these results emphasize that seizure semiology, particularly the absence of overt motor features, continues to pose a diagnostic challenge even in the modern era of advanced neuroimaging and genetic testing.

Contrary to the expectation that structural etiologies would reduce diagnostic uncertainty, our results revealed that hippocampal sclerosis and focal cortical dysplasia were among the most frequently newly recognized lesions during reassessment. Before re‐evaluation, hippocampal sclerosis was identified in only four cases and focal cortical dysplasia in eight, whereas more extensive lesions, such as lissencephaly, hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, periventricular leukomalacia, post‐encephalitic sequelae, tuberous sclerosis complex, brain tumors, and cerebrovascular disorders, were generally diagnosed earlier in childhood. These findings are similar to those of previous reports demonstrating that subtle or localized structural lesions, particularly mesial‐temporal sclerosis and focal cortical dysplasia, are easily overlooked in community settings. 19 , 20 Konomatsu et al. showed that delays in surgical referral for hippocampal sclerosis are largely attributable to prolonged diagnostic odysseys under non‐specialist care in Japan, 21 a phenomenon mirrored by the present results. Similarly, FDS accounted for a subset of diagnostic revision in our cohort. Konomatsu et al. observed comparable referral delays in FDS, again implicating non‐specialist management as a major factor in diagnostic delay. 22 Taken together, these findings suggest that both epileptic and non‐epileptic conditions can remain misdiagnosed for extended periods, particularly when patients are not evaluated in specialized epilepsy centers.

Beyond structural and non‐epileptic conditions, several patients in our cohort were newly classified with specific childhood‐onset epilepsy syndromes during reassessment. These include IGE, LGS, SeLEAS, SeLECTS, and SHE. Correctly identifying these syndromes requires clinical expertise to recognize the constellations of seizure types and the EEG findings characteristic of each disorder. However, because such syndromes are primarily encountered in pediatric settings, adult neurologists may be less familiar with them. Consequently, when patients transition to adult care without a clearly defined syndrome label, treatment may continue under the broad category of “epilepsy” without subclassification. Among the five patients with IGE, three were ultimately diagnosed with juvenile absence epilepsy. This pattern may parallel the diagnostic challenges observed in focal epilepsy with non‐motor seizures, where subtle clinical features may lead to under‐recognition. In LGS, diagnostic delay is well documented, both epidemiologically and clinically. 23 , 24 Such delays often result from the syndrome's evolving clinical and electroencephalographic manifestations, overlap with other developmental epileptic encephalopathies, and limited awareness among adult neurologists, leading to under‐recognition in adulthood. 25 , 26 Therefore, reassessment during the transition to adult care plays a crucial role in establishing accurate syndromic diagnoses and implementing appropriate management strategies for these patients.

By contrast, reassessment revealed cases of self‐limited epilepsies and epilepsy with myoclonic‐atonic seizures in which antiseizure medications could be safely withdrawn (n = 8, Table S1). Among these patients, two were treated for presumed epilepsy despite having episodes of syncope, and three exhibited transient EEG abnormalities without recurrent seizures. Therefore, diagnostic reconsideration during the transition period helped prevent unnecessary continuation of medication and improved quality of life. These cases highlight that the benefits of diagnostic reassessment extend beyond DRE and encompass patients with well‐controlled or inactive conditions.

Finally, advances in genetic and metabolic testing have emerged as key components of diagnostic refinement. Pathogenic variants or chromosomal abnormalities consistent with an epilepsy etiology were identified in nine patients, four of whom had previously been evaluated at tertiary epilepsy centers (Table 2; Table S1). The transition to adult care provides a critical opportunity to revisit unresolved genetic diagnoses, particularly given the rapid evolution of sequencing technologies and variant interpretation. 1 , 27 Previous studies have shown that approximately 22% of adults with epilepsy and intellectual disability who undergo comprehensive gene panel testing achieve a definitive molecular diagnosis, underscoring the clinical utility of updated genetic evaluation. 28 Unlike other forms of diagnostic revision, even patients who have undergone extensive evaluation may benefit from genetic testing, as demonstrated in our cohort. Genetic re‐evaluation should be viewed as a continuing process that remains clinically meaningful during the transition to adulthood, informing management, prognosis, and therapeutic decision‐making. 29

LIMITATIONS

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because of its retrospective design, the diagnostic reassessment process was not standardized and relied on the completeness and quality of available clinical documentation, which may have introduced information bias. Second, the study was conducted in a single region of Japan, where the structure of epilepsy care is highly centralized. Before the recent establishment of our epilepsy center, Nagano Children's Hospital had served as the main pediatric epilepsy center for the region, which likely contributed to the strong influence of “epilepsy center visit” as a protective factor. Third, our findings may not be generalizable to other populations or healthcare systems with different referral structures. Fourth, potential confounders such as socioeconomic status, access to care (rural versus urban residence), and referral pathways were not systematically assessed, which may have influenced diagnostic opportunities. Finally, although the sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the main findings, the sample size limited further subgroup exploration. Further multicenter, prospective studies incorporating standardized diagnostic protocols and broader sociodemographic data are warranted to validate and extend our observations.

CLINICAL RELEVANCE AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Nearly one in five patients previously diagnosed with childhood‐onset epilepsy required diagnostic revision when reassessed during the transition to adult care. Our findings highlight that diagnostic reassessment during the transition to adult care is particularly important for patients with exclusively non‐motor seizures or moderate seizure frequency who have not previously been evaluated at specialized epilepsy centers. Systematic diagnostic reviews including updated imaging, genetic testing, and detailed clinical evaluations should be integrated into the transition to adult care to ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate long‐term management. Future work should focus on implementing standardized transition‐era reassessment pathways and evaluating their impact on diagnostic yield, treatment optimization, and patient‐centered outcomes, including quality of life, in diverse healthcare settings.
