Salicylic acid–mediated competitive regulation of xylem vessel formation and defense responses in Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Ya Ma, Rune Kurokawa, Chaokun Huang, Ryosuke Sano, Taku Demura, Misato Ohtani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn vascular plants, xylem vessels transport water and contribute to structural integrity. As part of vessel formation, xylem cells deposit secondary cell walls (SCWs), which are composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin polymers. Under environmental challenges such as pathogen attack, a growth–defense trade-off limits xylem vessel development. Understanding the mechanism regulating this trade-off has implications for understanding of plant strategy to utilize their carbon resources because SCWs contain large amounts of densely packed high-carbon compounds. Here, we investigated the effect of pathogen responses induced by the peptide defense elicitor flagellin22 (flg22) on VASCULAR-RELATED NAC-DOMAIN7 (VND7)–dependent xylem vessel formation in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana). Treatment with flg22 decreased xylem transport and delayed ectopic xylem vessel differentiation, including SCW deposition, in plants with a dexamethasone-inducible VND7 system, suggesting that the plant prioritized defense responses over xylem formation. Consistent with this, transcriptome analysis revealed that flg22 treatment suppressed SCW-related genes and activated immune-related genes. Salicylic acid (SA) treatment had a similar inhibitory effect on VND7-dependent xylem vessel formation, and the sid2–2 and npr1–1 mutants, which are defective in SA biosynthesis and signaling, respectively, did not exhibit flg22-induced inhibition of xylem transport activity; these highlight the role of SA, which is biosynthesized from phenylalanine during immune responses, as a key mediator of the competition between xylem vessel formation and immune responses. These findings provide insights into how plants flexibly regulate xylem development under biotic stress to optimize their development.Keywords: flagellin22, growth–defense trade-off, pattern-triggered immunity, salicylic acid, secondary cell wall, xylem vessel differentiation

Introduction

The growth-defense trade-off ensures proper resource allocation within plants in response to multiple developmental and environmental cues. During natural infection by vascular pathogens, plants often exhibit growth retardation due to pathogen colonization in vascular tissues, which blocks water and nutrient transportation and degrades cell wall components (Jha et al. 2005, Sun et al. 2005, Srinivas et al. 2019). In response, plants activate their immune system, including physical barriers such as tyloses, vascular coatings and hypersensitive reactions (Rahman et al. 1999, Fradin and Thomma 2006, Xu et al. 2008), as well as chemical responses such as antimicrobial proteins, reactive oxygen species, phenolics, and sulfur compounds (Williams et al. 2002, Cooper 2004, Van Loon et al. 2006, Gayoso et al. 2010, Chen et al. 2018, Wallis and Galarneau 2020). These immune responses are energetically high-cost, thus plants need to divert resources from growth to defensive responses smoothly and strategically.

Xylem vessels are specialized tissues for water transport that also provide structural support to vascular plants (Lucas et al. 2013). Xylem vessel formation is characterized by two important cytological processes, secondary cell wall (SCW) thickening and programmed cell death (PCD) (Samuels et al. 2006, Lucas et al. 2013, Kamon and Ohtani 2021). SCW deposition involves the biosynthesis of lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose, which help produce rigid, sturdy cell walls that are resistant to negative pressures during water transport (Meents et al. 2018, Kamon and Ohtani 2021). Changes in the composition of cell walls in xylem vessels affect water transport efficiency, leading to changes in plant growth and development (Brown et al. 2005, Taylor 2007). In addition, xylem vessels that are rich in water and accessible to the entire plant body are very attractive to pathogens as reproductive sites. Thus, the understanding of xylem vessel cell differentiation would serve as an ideal research model for studying plant response to developmental and environmental cues.

Following the identification of VASCULAR-RELATED NAC-DOMAIN (VND) proteins, a group of plant-specific NAC (NAM, ATAF1/2, CUC2) transcription factors, as the master regulators of xylem cell differentiation in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) (Kubo et al. 2005), significant advances have been made in understanding the mechanisms regulating xylem vessel differentiation (Nakano et al. 2015, Kamon and Ohtani 2021, Phookaew et al. 2024). VND proteins are evolutionarily conserved across land plants, and the expression of VND genes in developing vascular tissues are also commonly observed in Arabidopsis (Yamaguchi et al. 2008), Populus trichocarpa (Ohtani et al. 2011), and Pinus taeda (Akiyoshi et al. 2020). Downstream of VND proteins, several MYB transcription factors serve as intermediate regulators of SCW formation. For example, MYB46 and MYB83 positively regulate the pathways involved in SCW formation (Zhong and Ye 2012, Ko et al. 2014), and MYB58, MYB63, MYB85, and MYB103 regulate lignin biosynthesis (Zhou et al. 2009, Öhman et al. 2013). The NAC–MYB gene regulatory network model of SCW formation has been reviewed by Nakano et al. (2015) and is widely accepted as a conserved model for xylem vessel formation in vascular plants.

Xylem vessel activity is sensitive to environmental stress. For example, an artificial prolonged soil drought led Pinus edulis Engelm trees to produce tracheid with larger lumens but thinner walls to achieve higher hydraulic efficiency and lower implosion safety (Guérin et al. 2020), suggesting a trade-off regulation between efficiency and safety in xylem in response to drought stress. Moreover, the water limitation triggered to differentiate protoxylem-like cells with spiral secondary walls in place of metaxylem in Arabidopsis (Ramachandran et al. 2021). These observations indicate an active regulation of xylem vessel cell differentiation in response to environmental clues, and possibly some of such regulations would be through a modulation of transcriptional network (Ohtani and Demura 2019). Indeed, the reprograming of VND-based transcriptional regulation can be occurred in response to drought stress; Ramachandran et al. (2021) showed that water limitation and abscisic acid (ABA) treatment influenced the xylem vessel morphology in Arabidopsis roots, and the expression of VND genes was directly changed by ABRE-binding factors (ABFs) and other ABA-related transcription factors (Ramachandran et al. 2021). Notably, ABA-induced xylem plasticity is controlled by VNDs cooperation: VND2 and VND3 accelerate the rate of xylem differentiation, while VND7 promotes the cell fate switch from metaxylem-type to protoxylem-type vessels (Ramachandran et al. 2021). Given the fact that VND2 and VND3 are not conventionally expressed in root xylem vessels (Kubo et al. 2005, Yamaguchi et al. 2008), the alteration of VND-based transcriptional regulatory network by stress-responsive transcription factors (e.g. ABFs) would function to change the program of xylem vessel development, to adapt xylem vessels to the environments. Moreover, immune signaling pathways affect xylem development by negatively regulating the expression of BRI1-EMS-SUPPRESSOR1 (BES1), encoding a transcription factor that regulates the expression of genes responsible for cell wall formation (Kondo et al. 2014a, Kang et al. 2015, Furuya et al. 2024). Tang et al. (2018) identified a link between xylem formation, drought stress tolerance, and pattern-triggered immunity (PTI), as the transcription factor XYLEM NAC DOMAIN1 (XND1) negatively regulates drought stress responses and contributes to defense against vascular pathogens, in both cases by modulating xylem vessel formation (Tang et al. 2018). These findings collectively emphasize that xylem vessel formation should be flexibly modified in the trade-off between plant growth and defense responses, to increase the fitness of plants to biotic stresses.

Based on the above findings, in this study, we explored how immune responses affect xylem vessel cell differentiation and development in Arabidopsis. Our analyses with the peptide elicitor flagellin22 (flg22) and a VND7-inducible Arabidopsis line for ectopic xylem vessel differentiation (Yamaguchi et al. 2010, Hirai et al. 2019, Uy et al. 2023, Phookaew et al. 2024) revealed that the flg22 treatment significantly repressed xylem vessel formation. We also observed a negative effect of salicylic acid (SA) on xylem vessel formation, which might explain how flg22 suppresses xylem vessel differentiation. Our findings provide new insights into the molecular hubs in the plant growth–defense trade-off, specifically the interplay between phytohormone-mediated signaling and metabolic regulation for SCW formation in response to biotic stress.

Results

Xylem vessel functionality is actively regulated in response to flg22

Plants activate PTI to resist a broad spectrum of pathogens, and PTI is triggered by the recognition of pathogenic elicitors (Boller and Felix 2009). One well-studied pathogen-associated molecular pattern is flg22, a 22–amino acid peptide derived from bacterial flagellin (Felix et al. 1999, Zipfel et al. 2004). In addition to inducing PTI, treatment with flg22 negatively affects plant growth, particularly root elongation (Beck et al. 2014, Huang et al. 2023).

To explore the plasticity of xylem vessel functionality in response to biotic stress, we conducted a xylem transport assay using Arabidopsis seedlings under different flg22 treatment conditions. Seedlings were (i) grown on standard medium (mock) for 2 weeks (mock-mock); (ii) grown on medium containing 100 nM flg22 for 1 week, followed by growth on standard medium for 1 week (flg22-mock); (iii) grown on standard medium for 1 week, followed by medium containing 100 nM flg22 for 1 week (mock-flg22); or (iv) grown on medium containing 100 nM flg22 for 2 weeks (flg22-flg22). Treatment with flg22 significantly suppressed root growth, whereas root elongation rates were partially recovered once the stress was removed (Fig. 1a; mock-mock versus flg22-mock).

Significant differences between each group within a single period were analyzed by ANOVA. Values marked with the same letter were not significantly different (Duncan's multiple range test, P < 0.05). (b) The transport pattern of the fluorescent dye rhodamine in the primary roots of 14-day-old Col-0 seedlings grown under different conditions. The fluorescent signal was observed at each time point and recorded. Bars = 1 cm. (c) Quantitative fluorescence intensity (arbitrary units) data for Col-0 grown under different conditions. Data are presented as the mean ± SE. n = 7 (mock-mock), 8 (flg22-mock), 6 (mock-flg22), and 8 (flg22-flg22). Significant differences between each group are described in Supplementary Table S1.

A xylem transport assay revealed a significant reduction in transport efficiency in seedlings treated with flg22 for 2 weeks (Fig. 1b). Two types of abnormalities were observed: (i) reduced transport velocity: flg22-flg22 seedlings exhibited the slowest rate of dye transport during the first 4 min of the assay (Fig. 1b and c; Supplementary Table S1), and it took 9 min to reach a plateau; and (ii) reduced maximum transport volume: flg22-flg22 seedlings displayed the lowest maximum signal intensity after reaching a plateau (Fig. 1b and 1c; Supplementary Table S1). By contrast, mock-flg22 and flg22-mock seedlings exhibited intermediate levels of xylem transport efficiency between those of mock-mock and flg22-flg22 seedlings (Fig. 1b and c; Supplementary Table S1). Notably, mock-flg22 and flg22-mock seedlings showed similar levels of xylem transport efficiency (Fig. 1b and c; Supplementary Table S1). Therefore, based on the restoration of root growth in flg22-mock seedlings, the impacts of flg22 treatment were not continuous, but once the seedlings were transferred back to normal conditions without flg22, xylem vessel formation appeared to recover. These findings demonstrate that the immune response affects the functionality of xylem vessels, but the inhibition of xylem vessel function can be partially restored once the biotic stress is removed.

Treatment with flg22 affects VND7-dependent ectopic xylem vessel cell differentiation

In the Arabidopsis VND7-VP16-GR-inducible system, VND7 is post-translationally activated by glucocorticoid dexamethasone (DEX) treatment, leading to ectopic xylem vessel cell differentiation (Yamaguchi et al. 2010). To investigate the effects of flg22 on VND7-dependent xylem vessel cell differentiation, we treated 7-day-old Arabidopsis VND7-VP16-GR and VP16-GR (vector control) seedlings with 100 nM DEX in the presence and absence of flg22 and observed xylem vessel cell differentiation over time. The treatment with 10 nM, 100 nM, and 1 μM flg22 revealed the inhibitory effects of flg22 on xylem vessel cell differentiation was saturated at 100 nM flg22 (Supplementary Fig. S1), so we decided to use 100 nM flg22 for the subsequent experiments. We also subjected the VND7-VP16-GR seedlings to two types of flg22 treatment: simultaneous flg22 and DEX treatment (flg22 + DEX) and 1 h of flg22 treatment prior to DEX treatment [flg22(−1 h) + DEX] (Fig. 2).

(b) Effect of flg22 on the reduction in chlorophyll levels induced by DEX treatment in VND7-VP16-GR. Seven-day-old VP16-GR and VND7-VP16-GR seedlings were treated with 100 nM DEX and 100 nM flg22. The experiment was independently performed four times, and data are presented as the mean ± SE. For each time point, treatments with statistically significant differences are indicated by different letters (Tukey’s multiple comparison test, P < 0.05, n = 4). (c, d) Effects of flg22 on secondary cell wall deposition in VND7-VP16-GR. Seven-day-old VP16-GR (vector control) and VND7-VP16-GR seedlings were treated with 100 nM DEX and 100 nM flg22. Seedlings treated with DEX only (DEX in Fig. 2) started to turn white after 12 h of DEX treatment, and severe bleaching was observed between 24 and 36 h after DEX treatment (Fig. 2a) owing to the progression of PCD during xylem vessel formation (Yamaguchi et al. 2010, Hirai et al. 2019, Uy et al. 2023, Phookaew et al. 2024). By contrast, the bleaching of seedlings treated with flg22 [flg22 + DEX and flg22(−1 h) + DEX groups in Fig. 2] was not observed until 48 h of DEX treatment (Fig. 2a). The reduction in chlorophyll levels was prominent after 24 h of treatment in the DEX-treated VND7-VP16-GR group. However, the flg22 + DEX and flg22(−1 h) + DEX VND7-VP16-GR groups exhibited significantly higher chlorophyll levels than the DEX group, particularly after 24 to 36 h of DEX treatment (Fig. 2b). After 48 h of DEX treatment, this effect of flg22 treatment was no longer detected strongly (Fig. 2b). These results indicate that flg22 treatment did not block the VND7-dependent ectopic xylem vessel formation completely, but it delayed the process of xylem vessel formation, such as PCD progression.

To address the impacts of flg22 on SCW deposition, we treated 7-day-old VND7-VP16-GR and VP16-GR (vector control) seedlings with 100 nM DEX with or without 100 nM flg22 (Fig. 2c, d). In epidermal cells of cotyledons stained with propidium iodide (PI), we observed dot-like signals throughout the epidermal cells of VP16-GR seedlings after 24 h of flg22 treatment (Fig. 2c). These signals likely reflected callose deposition in response to pathogen attack, as shown in previous studies (Denoux et al. 2008, Luna et al. 2011). By contrast, VND7-VP16-GR seedlings treated with DEX alone exhibited weak but clear spiral-patterned SCW deposition after 24 h of treatment (Fig. 2c), a typical sign of VND7-induced protoxylem vessel-type SCW formation (Yamaguchi et al. 2010). Only weak patterned SCW signals were observed in seedlings of the flg22 + DEX group (Fig. 2c). After 60 h of treatment, the callose-like deposits had nearly disappeared in the VP16-GR and VND7-VP16-GR lines (Fig. 2d). Notably, strong and clear spiral patterns of secondary cell walls (SCWs) were detected in VND7-VP16-GR seedlings treated with DEX only, but co-treatment with DEX and flg22 resulted in reduced SCW signals with loose patterning (Fig. 2d). These results suggest that flg22 perturbs VND7-induced SCW deposition during xylem vessel formation.

Treatment with flg22 disturbs transcriptomic changes, leading to xylem vessel cell differentiation

To obtain a view of the transcriptomic changes in response to flg22, we treated VND7-VP16-GR and VP16-GR (vector control) seedlings with three different conditions: DEX only (DEX), simultaneous treatment with flg22 and DEX (flg22 + DEX), and flg22 treatment 1 h before DEX treatment [flg22(−1 h) + DEX] (Fig. 3a). We collected samples at 0 and 6 h of DEX treatment and subjected them to RNA-seq analysis.

Effect of flg22-induced pathogen responses on transcriptional dynamics during VND7-dependent xylem vessel formation. Seven-day-old seedlings were treated with DEX and flg22 and collected at various time points. Total RNA was extracted from the samples and analyzed by RNA-seq. Three biological replicates were performed per treatment condition. (a) Scheme used for RNA-seq sampling. (b) PCA of the entire transcriptome dataset from all experimental groups. Dim1 and Dim2 are the first and second principal components, respectively. (c) Comparison of transcriptome profiles between VND7-VP16-GR treated with flg22 + DEX for 6 h and DEX only for 6 h. Volcano plots show the fold change and q-values of each differentially expressed gene between the two groups. Genes with log2(fold change [FC])≥1 and q-value < 0.05, and with log2(fold change [FC])≤-1 and q-value < 0.05, were considered significantly upregulated, and downregulated, respectively.(d) Over-represented GO terms in the biological process category for significantly downregulated genes in VND7-VP16-GR upon flg22 + DEX treatment compared to DEX-treated samples.

Principal component analysis (PCA) of the RNA-seq data revealed the clear separation of samples (Fig. 3b), indicating that distinct transcriptomic changes occurred upon DEX and/or flg22 treatment. The first and second principal components explained a significant portion of the total variance, i.e. 24.9% and 13.3%, respectively (Dim1 and Dim2, respectively, in Fig. 3b). Specifically, the VP16-GR lines at 0 or 6 h of DEX treatment (VP16_DEX_0h and VP16_DEX_6h) and untreated VND7-VP16-GR (VND7_DEX_0h) were similarly distributed in Quadrant 2 (Fig. 3b), whereas the three DEX-treated VND7-VP16-GR groups [VND7_DEX_6h, VND7_flg22 + DEX_6h, and VND7_flg22(−1) + DEX_6h] were positioned in Quadrant 1 (Fig. 3b), suggesting that the first component (Dim1) is related to VND7-dependent xylem vessel cell differentiation. By contrast, all flg22-treated VP16-GR samples and VND7-VP16-GR treated only with flg22 clustered in Quadrant 3 (Fig. 3b). Thus, the second component (Dim2) might be attributed to the immune response induced by flg22 treatment. Indeed, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the genes contributing to Dim2 revealed the significant enrichment of immune response–related GO terms (Supplementary Table S2; Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3).

VND7-VP16-GR simultaneously treated with flg22 and DEX (flg22 + DEX) and VND7-VP16-GR treated with flg22 before DEX [flg22(−1 h) + DEX] were closely positioned in the PCA results (Fig. 3b). Therefore, we reasoned that flg22 + DEX and flg22(−1 h) + DEX should have similar effects on the transcriptome. To test this, we selected the flg22 + DEX samples for subsequent analysis. A comparison of gene expression levels between the DEX and flg22 + DEX samples identified 994 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on the criteria |log2(fold change [FC])| ≥ 1 and q-value < 0.05; 696 and 298 genes were significantly up- and downregulated, respectively, in response to flg22 treatment (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). The upregulated genes were enriched in GO terms related to stress responses and pathogen responses, such as ‘response to molecule of bacterial origin’, ‘response to salicylic acid’, and ‘immune response’ (Supplementary Table S5), as well as ‘defense response by callose deposition’ (marked in red). By contrast, the enriched GO terms for the downregulated genes were largely associated with SCW formation, including ‘cell wall polysaccharide metabolic process’, ‘plant-type cell wall biogenesis’, and ‘hemicellulose metabolic process’ (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Table S6). These results indicate that, consistent with the above observations (Fig. 2b and 2c), the VND7-induced SCW formation program was suppressed by flg22 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4). These findings suggest that flg22 treatment activates the immune response and suppresses SCW biosynthesis.

Hierarchical clustering analysis of genes downstream of VND7

VND7 is a master transcription factor that promotes the expression of genes required for xylem cell differentiation in Arabidopsis (Kubo et al. 2005). To clarify the effects of flg22 on the expression of genes downstream of VND7, we performed hierarchical clustering analysis of its 61 known direct target genes (Yamaguchi et al. 2011). The genes showed differential expression under DEX and/or flg22 treatment (Fig. 4). Even in the presence of flg22, the majority of genes (51 genes in Group I) were significantly upregulated in VND7-VP16-GR compared to VP16-GR, but their induction was suppressed by flg22 treatment (Fig. 4). Group I included genes for SCW formation and PCD, such as the cellulose synthase genes CesA4 (AT5G44030) and CesA8 (AT4G18780) (Taylor et al. 2003, Brown et al. 2005) and the xylem cysteine peptidase gene XYLEM CYSTEINE PEPTIDASE2 (XCP2) (AT1G20850) (Avci et al. 2008) (Fig. 4). The suppression of these genes by flg22 reflects the inhibitory effect of flg22 on xylem vessel formation illustrated in Fig. 2.

Group II contained only two genes, encoding putative receptor kinases, RECEPTOR-LIKE PROTEIN20 (AT2G25440) and CYSTEINE-RICH RECEPTOR-LIKE KINASE31 (AT4G11470) (Fig. 4). These genes were significantly induced by flg22 in the VP16-GR and VND7-VP16-GR lines, although the increase was less pronounced in VND7-VP16-GR (Fig. 4). In addition, Group III genes were upregulated by DEX and flg22 treatment (Fig. 4). Group III included cell wall–related genes, such as TRICHOME BIREFRINGENCE-LIKE35 (AT5G01360 in Fig. 4) for xylan acetylation, and PCD-related genes, such as XYLEM CYSTEINE PEPTIDASE1 (XCP1) (AT4G35350 in Fig. 4). Given their expression patterns and molecular functions, the genes in Groups II and III might function in the intersection between xylem vessel formation and pathogen responses.

SA inhibits xylem vessel formation and SCW deposition

To further unravel the molecular mechanisms underlying the competitive regulation between xylem vessel formation and immune responses, we compared genes that were upregulated during VND7-dependent xylem vessel formation (log2FC > 1, q-value < 0.05) with genes reported to be induced by flg22 (Winkelmüller et al. 2021; accession no. GSE115991; upregulated genes after 1, 9, and 24 h of flg22 treatment based on the criteria log2FC > 1, q-value < 0.05). These two gene sets shared 448 genes (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Table S7). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis of these upregulated genes revealed that metabolic pathways, especially for the biosynthesis of nucleotide sugars, phenylpropanoids, and aromatic amino acids, were commonly upregulated (p-value < 0.1; Fig. 5b).

The shikimate pathway starting from chorismate to phenylalanine (Phe) biosynthesis and the subsequent phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway are essential for monolignol biosynthesis, contributing to structural lignification during xylem vessel formation (Rippert et al. 2009; Yao et al. 2021; Fig. 5c). We recently confirmed the metabolic activation of Phe and monolignols during VND7-induced xylem formation experimentally (Uy et al. 2023). Phenylpropanoids also enhance plant defense by producing antimicrobial metabolites (Dixon et al. 2002). Of particular interest was SA, a phenolic compound derived from chorismite or cinnamate, which is particularly important for defense responses against biotrophic and hemibiotrophic pathogens (Peng et al. 2021). This metabolic overlap links SA biosynthesis and monolignol production within the phenylpropanoid pathway (Fig. 5c).

To investigate whether SA affects xylem vessel cell differentiation, we treated VND7-VP16-GR seedlings with DEX in the presence or absence of SA and monitored the progression of xylem vessel cell differentiation. Seedling whitening, a sign of xylem vessel cell differentiation, was visible after 48 h of DEX treatment (Fig. 5d); however, this process was significantly blocked when the seedlings were pre-treated with SA (Fig. 5d). PI staining of VND7-VP16-GR seedlings treated with DEX for 48 h revealed strong SCW signals with distinct spiral patterns in epidermal cells of VND7-VP16-GR leaves treated with DEX only (Fig. 5e). Additional SA treatment decreased the SCW signals (Fig. 5e), indicating that SA has an inhibitory effect on VND7-dependent SCW formation. Winkelmüller et al. (2021) showed that flg22 treatment increased SA levels in Arabidopsis. Thus, the inhibitory effect of flg22 on xylem vessel differentiation might be partly explained by the increase in endogenous SA contents in response to flg22 treatment. To test this possibility, we next examined the xylem transport in sid2–2, a mutant defective in SA production (Wildermuth et al. 2001), and npr1–1, a mutant defective in SA signaling (Cao et al. 1997), under the flg22 treatment. As expected, the flg22 treatment led to a reduction in transport efficiency in Col-0, whereas sid2–2 and npr1–1 did not exhibit the flg22-conferred inhibition on xylem functionality (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. S5; Supplementary Tables S8 and S9). These results clearly indicate that the flg22-induced SA production and subsequent SA signaling affect xylem vessel functionality. Together, given the overlap in metabolic pathways for monolignol and SA biosynthesis, we suggest that the inhibitory effect of flg22 on SCW deposition is directly due to competition for metabolic precursors such as chorismite and/or cinnamate.

Discussion

Molecular intersections of transcriptomic and metabolic regulation between xylem vessel differentiation and defense responses

Tang et al. (2018) reported that the overall xylem area and number of xylem vessels decreased in Arabidopsis in response to flg22 treatment. Consistent with the decrease in xylem vessels, in this study, a xylem transport assay demonstrated that exposure to flg22 significantly impaired xylem transport in Arabidopsis (Fig. 1b and c). Notably, the negative effects of flg22 on root development were partially recoverable (Fig. 1), suggesting that an active and flexible mechanism regulates xylem vessel development in response to environmental stress. We also identified an inhibitory effect of flg22 on VND7-dependent xylem vessel cell differentiation, including SCW deposition and the upregulation of genes downstream of VND7 (Figs. 2–4, Fig. S2). Thus, the reduced xylem vessel activity in response to this treatment was due to inhibited xylem vessel cell differentiation by flg22. Moreover, transcriptome analysis revealed that flg22 treatment also significantly suppressed the VND7-dependent upregulation of xylem vessel–related genes, whereas it induced the expression of genes responsible for immune responses (Fig. 3b; Fig. 4; Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). This suppression is consistent with the delayed PCD and abnormal SCW deposition observed in VND7-VP16-GR seedlings treated with flg22 and DEX (Fig. 2b and c).

Our data also demonstrated that the key immune signal messenger SA also inhibited the VND7-induced xylem differentiation (Fig. 5d, e), and that the induction of key transcription factors downstream of VND7 (Nakano et al. 2015) were weakened in the presence of flg22 (Supplementary Fig. S4). The inhibitory effects of flg22 on xylem transport activity is mediated by SA (Fig. 6), thus, one of simple explanations for flg22-meditaed inhibition of xylem vessel formation is a direct inhibition of VND7 expression by SA. Since the VND7-VP16-GR construct is driven by the 35S promoter, we could not evaluate the effects of flg22 nor SA on endogenous VND7 expression in our system. Instead, we searched public datasets and found that the VND7 expression was not significantly altered in response to flg22 nor SA at the transcriptional level (Blanco et al. 2009, Liu et al. 2015, Ding et al. 2018, Hickman et al. 2019, Winkelmüller et al. 2021, Safaeizadeh et al. 2024), nor at the protein level (Liu et al. 2015, Chen et al. 2025). These facts indicate that the suppression on xylem vessel development by flg22 cannot be attributed to the repression of VND7 expression.

Notably, our results also revealed the impacts of pathogen stress and inducible developmental program on metabolic regulation during xylem vessel formation and defense responses. In accordance with the previous finding that pathogens induced callose deposition, we observed dot-like callose deposits in flg22-treated seedlings (Fig. 2b). The reduced SCW signals of DEX-treated VND7-VP16-GR lines in the presence of flg22 demonstrated that flg22-triggered defense responses altered SCW formation (Fig. 2c). This alteration may reflect a shift in carbon resource allocation from the biosynthesis of SCW-related polysaccharides to callose deposition to enhance plant defense.

Xylem vessel formation and immune responses are closely interconnected, since they share portions of the phenylpropanoid pathway, as revealed by GO analysis of commonly upregulated genes (Fig. 5a, b), which provides precursors for both lignin biosynthesis for SCWs and the biosynthesis of antimicrobial compounds, including SA (Fig. 5c). This overlap points to possible coordinated and/or competing regulatory mechanisms between lignification and SA-mediated defense responses. It is also notable that the antibacterial substance camalexin is biosynthesized from tryptophan (Trp) (Glawischnig et al. 2004), and Trp and Phe share shikimic acid as a biosynthetic starting material. Shikimic acid biosynthesis is activated during xylem vessel formation and defense responses (Fig. 3; Winkelmüller et al. 2021; Ohtani et al. 2016; Uy et al. 2023). Such overlaps in the biosynthetic pathways of SA, lignin, and camalexin suggest that the promotion of xylem vessel formation or defense responses, i.e. the growth–defense trade-off, might be directly linked to a control of carbon resource allocation.

Interestingly, xylem vessel formation and defense responses share PCD as an important cellular event, as well as PCD-related enzymes. Our transcriptome data revealed that XCP1, encoding a key PCD-related peptidase (Avci et al. 2008), was upregulated by the DEX and flg22 treatment but its homolog XCP2 was not (Fig. 4). XCP1 and XCP2 are basically considered to work redundantly in xylem vessel differentiation, with a possible difference in target proteins (Funk et al. 2002, Avci et al. 2008). Of note, XCP1 also regulates PTI by interacting with the CYSTATIN 6 (CYS6) protein, which prevents the degradation of RESPIRATORY BURST OXIDASE HOMOLOG D (RBOHD), a key enzyme of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) production, enabling the ROS accumulation upon pathogen infection (Liu et al. 2024). Remarkably, Chen et al. (2023) discovered that XCP1 cleaved PATHOGENESIS-RELATED PROTEIN1 (PR1) to release the immunomodulatory cytokine CAPE9, an enhancer of systemic acquired resistance (SAR). SA can increase the XCP1 activity for PR1 cleavage (Chen et al. 2023). Together with the expression patterns in Fig. 4, XCP1, probably not XCP2, would function in both xylem vessel differentiation and defense response.

Phytohormonal signaling balances xylem vessel formation and defense responses

Phytohormones are crucial for xylem development (Kondo et al. 2014b, Ramachandran et al. 2020). Gibberellic acid (GA) and auxin play distinct but complementary roles in xylem vessel differentiation in Populus and Arabidopsis (Johnsson et al. 2019): Auxin primarily regulates the timing of xylem development by driving cambial cell division while inhibiting SCW deposition by repressing the expression of SCW-related NAC transcription factor genes (Johnsson et al. 2019). This inhibition might ensure that vessel cells complete their cell expansion prior to significant SCW deposition. Subsequently, GA promotes SCW deposition by upregulating the expression of genes encoding vessel- and fiber-specific NAC transcription factors involved in xylem maturation and lignification (Johnsson et al. 2019).

The interplay of SA, auxin, and GA functions in the plant growth–defense trade-off. SA suppresses auxin signaling by downregulating core auxin signaling components, such as TRANSPORT INHIBITOR RESPONSE1/AUXIN SIGNALING F-BOX genes, leading to the stabilization of AUXIN/INDOLE-3-ACETIC ACID repressor proteins and thereby reducing auxin responses (Wang et al. 2007, Pasternak et al. 2019). Additionally, SA affects the distribution of auxin by inducing the clustering of the auxin efflux carrier PIN-FORMED2 at the plasma membrane, thereby limiting lateral auxin transport and impairing root gravitropic responses (Ke et al. 2021). Yu et al. (2022) elucidated the mechanism by which SA interferes with plant growth by inhibiting GA signaling. The SA receptor NONEXPRESSER OF PR GENES1 interacts with the GA receptor GID1, leading to the degradation of GID1 and thus stabilizing DELLA proteins, which inhibit GA signaling (Yu et al. 2022). These findings help uncover the intricate mechanisms by which SA modulates other development-related plant hormones to prioritize defense over growth. Indeed, we verified the inhibitory effect of SA on xylem vessel cell differentiation and SCW deposition (Fig. 5) and the SA-mediated inhibition of xylem functionality by flg22 (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. S5, Supplementary Tables S8 and S9). Moreover, the complex interplay between GA and SA signaling has been reported to regulate lignin levels in plants (Gallego-Giraldo et al. 2011). Reduced lignin levels, achieved by downregulating the gene encoding hydroxycinnamoyl transferase, led to an increase in SA levels, which was associated with inhibited plant growth and reduced GA responsiveness (Gallego-Giraldo et al. 2011). Elevated SA levels interfere with the GA signaling pathway, as lowering SA levels in these lignin-deficient plants restored growth despite their reduced lignin contents (Gallego-Giraldo et al. 2011). These findings suggest that the SA accumulation induced by altered lignin biosynthesis suppresses GA signaling, forming a regulatory feedback loop in which plant growth is moderated by the SA and GA pathways through the regulation of lignin levels. The feedback regulation among SA, GA, and lignin might help ensure the proper balance between xylem vessel formation and plant immunity in response to developmental cues and environmental stress (Fig. 7). Since lignin directly regulates xylem vessel activity, this regulatory mechanism could allow plants to actively balance water transport and defense, optimizing resilience across changing conditions.

Taken together, our findings uncover an active regulatory mechanism by which plants adjust their developmental programs (e.g. xylem vessel formation) in response to environmental challenges (e.g. pathogen attack) through the interplay of phytohormones and lignin biosynthesis. Under biotic stress, immune responses suspend developmental processes, thus prioritizing survival, by suppressing VND7-mediated xylem vessel formation. Once the biotic stress subsides, plants quickly revert to developmental patterns that restore xylem vessel functionality and transport efficiency, supporting normal physiological events. Notably, we discovered that flg22 suppresses xylem vessel development through SA signaling (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. S6). This finding aligns with previous findings on the complex crosstalk between SA and other development-related phytohormones in the growth–defense trade-off, such as competitive regulation between xylem vessel formation and immune responses. In addition, our transcriptome data suggested that the flg22-induced immune response could be altered upon the VND7 activation (Supplementary Fig. S2). Especially, the upregulation of key transcriptional regulators for plant immunity, including WRKY, NAC, MYB, AP2/ERF, bHLH, and bZIP (Tsuda and Somssich 2015), were suppressed by the VND7 activation (Supplementary Fig. S3). These would imply that the VND7 activation has a suppressive effect on PTI. In other words, xylem development and immune response may be tightly balanced and mutually regulated. The molecular hubs mediating stress signaling and developmental regulation should be explored in the future, particularly focusing on phenylpropanoid pathways.

Materials and Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis (A. thaliana) plants carrying 35S::VND7-VP16-GR and 35S::VP16-GR (vector control) were described by Yamaguchi et al. (2010). SA-related mutants sid2–2 (CS16438; Wildermuth et al. 2001) and npr1–1 (CS3726; Cao et al. 1997) were kindly provided by Dr. Kamiya (The University of Tokyo) (Hashimoto et al. 2023). Seeds were surface-sterilized in 70% (v/v) ethanol containing 0.1% (w/v) Triton-X (w/v), sown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 1% (w/v) sucrose and 0.05% (w/v) 2-morpholinoethanesulfonic acid, and solidified with 0.25% (w/v) gellan gum (pH 5.7). The plates were maintained at 4°C in the dark for 2–3 days and incubated at 22°C in a plant growth chamber under a 16-h-light/8-h-dark photoperiod. For seed harvesting, 2-week-old seedlings grown on plates were transferred to soil and cultured at 22°C under a 16-h-light/8-h-dark photoperiod.

For the root elongation and xylem transport assays, a half-strength MS medium containing flg22 was prepared as follows: flg22 was first dissolved in the sterile water to make a 1 mM stock solution. After autoclave, the flg22 stock solution was added to the cooled medium, to make 10 nM, 100 nM, or 1 μM flg22 as final concentrations. The flg22-contaidning medium was gently mixed, and then poured into plates. Seedings were grown on the first medium for 7 days, and carefully transferred to the second medium using sterilized tweezers. Experiments were repeated as three or more biological replicates to ensure the reproducibility, of results, and a typical result of the root elongation assay was shown in Fig. 1a.

DEX, bacterial elicitor (flg22), and SA treatment of seedlings

For DEX treatment, 7-day-old seedlings grown on MS medium as described above were immersed in 10 ml of sterile water containing 100 nM DEX (Sigma). For flg22(−1 h) + DEX treatment, 7-day-old seedlings grown on MS medium were immersed in 10 ml of sterile water containing 100 nM flg22 (Eurogentec) for 1 h. The flg22 solution was removed, and the seedlings were immersed in 10 μl of 100 μM DEX solution. For flg22 + DEX treatment, 7-day-old seedlings grown on MS medium were immersed in 10 ml of sterile water containing 100 nM flg22 and 100 nM DEX. For SA(−1d) + DEX treatment, 7-day-old seedlings grown on MS medium were immersed in 10 ml of sterile water containing 500 nM SA for 24 h. The SA solution was removed, and the seedlings were immersed in 10 μl of 10 μM DEX solution. The plates were placed in a plant growth chamber, and seedlings were sampled at specific time points as indicated. Experiments were repeated as three or more biological replicates to ensure the reproducibility of results.

Quantification of chlorophyll contents

Fifteen seedlings were collected in a 1.5-ml tube and centrifuged at 4°C and 1000 rpm for 3 min. After measuring the fresh weight (ME403T/00, Mettler Toledo), the seedlings were immersed in 1 ml of N,N-dimethylformamide (Wako) and incubated at 4°C overnight to extract chlorophyll. Chlorophyll contents were determined using a DU640 spectrophotometer (Beckman Coulter) by measuring absorbance at A646.8, A663.8, and A750; the absorbance at A750 was used to correct the absorbance values at A646.8 and A663.8. The chlorophyll content was calculated based on the formula by Porra et al. (1989): chlorophyll a + b = 17.67 * A646.8 + 7.12 * A663.8. Three biological replicates were conducted to ensure the reproducibility of results, of which a typical example was shown in Figures.

Microscopy observation of SCW deposition

After treatment, the seedlings were fixed in 90% (v/v) acetone for at least 2 h at −30°C. The samples were immersed in 90%, 70%, 50%, and 30% (v/v) acetone for 5 min each and hydrated by immersion in sterile water. The samples were then immersed in 0.1% (v/v) PI (Invitrogen) staining solution and incubated in the dark for 15–30 min. Images were taken under a confocal microscope (FV10i, Olympus). Experiments were repeated as three or more biological replicates to ensure the reproducibility of results, of which typical examples were shown in Figs. 2 and 5.

RNA-seq analysis

Seven-day-old wild-type VND7-VP16-GR and VP16-GR (vector control) seedlings were treated as described above (Fig. 3a). The samples were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −70°C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted from the samples using an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using an Illumina Ribo-Zero™ Magnetic Kit and a NEBNext® Ultra™ Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, followed by 150-bp paired-end sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. For RNA-seq data analysis, short reads were mapped against the Arabidopsis genome (TAIR10) using HISAT2 (v2.2.0; https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2). Transcript expression was calculated using the StringTie program (v2.2.0; https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie). Relative expression levels were calculated in transcripts per million. DEGs were detected using the R package edgeR (v3.40.2). Genes with q-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 were identified as DEGs. GO analysis was conducted using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.6.2). Enriched GO terms in the Biological Process category were identified based on the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method, with significance thresholds of P < 0.05 and q-value < 0.1.

KEGG enrichment analysis

KEGG enrichment analysis was performed using DAVID 2021 (The Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery; https://david.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp). DAVID functional annotation was performed on the list of overlapping genes. P value was adjusted by the BH multi-test method, and KEGG terms with false discovery rate < 0.05 were selected as over-represented terms.

Xylem transport assay

The xylem transport assay was conducted as described by Endo et al. (2019) and Phookaew et al. (2024). Fourteen-day-old Col-0 seedlings grown on the half-strength MS medium solidified with 0.8% (w/v) gellan gum were used for analysis. To control relative humidity, the plate lid was kept slightly open for 20 h before the assay. The primary root tips were excised, and 100 μl of 1 mM rhodamine solution was applied to the cut root tip. Fluorescence was observed every minute in the dark using an MSV269 (Leica) fluorescence microscope equipped with the ET DSR filter, with maximum intensity settings. Signal was consistently detected at a specific site located a uniform distance from the cut site. Fluorescent signals were photographed with an iPhone 7 (A1779) using the i-NTER SHOT app with the following settings: temperature = 3000 K, tint = 0, and brightness = 704. Signal intensities were quantified with ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/index.html). Statistical significance among lines was determined using the least significant difference test (Supplementary Table S1). Experiments were repeated as three or more biological replicates to ensure the reproducibility of results. Figure 1c contains results from two independent experiments, and Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. S5 showed the data from independent data.
