HA thermostability mutations S84F, G167N, and D168N potentiate H9N2 virus transmission in a warming environment
Authors: Xinyi Pei, Wei Chen, Hanyan Xiao, Bin Ren, Chaoxuan Guo, Xinyu Gao, Zhoushuang Peng, Lingkai Zhang, Qingshuai Sun, Zhibin Hu, Hongbo Zhou, Shujie Ma, Xuyong Li, Wen Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAvian influenza A (H9N2) viruses are widespread in poultry and occasionally infect mammals, contributing genes to other emerging strains. The factors that allow these viruses to withstand higher temperatures are not well understood. In this study, we used high-temperature selection and deep mutational scanning of a saturated H9N2 hemagglutinin (HA) mutant library. We identified three HA mutations, S84F, G167N, and D168N, that increase viral thermostability. Viruses with these mutations survived better at elevated temperatures. In transmission experiments with chickens, the S84F mutation allowed waterborne spread at a temperature that blocked transmission of the wild-type virus. The combination of all three mutations was associated with evidence of airborne transmission in one of two experimental replicates. These results link HA thermostability to environmental persistence and transmission potential under thermal stress, which may affect the ecology of H9N2 viruses.

Introduction

Global temperatures have increased compared to pre-industrial levels, and this trend is expected to continue. Since environmental conditions like temperature influence the seasonal patterns of influenza virus epidemics, it is relevant to ask how a warmer climate might affect virus ecology and evolution [1–3]. Viral thermostability can be a factor in fitness and spread [4–6]. For example, some avian-origin influenza viruses can remain infectious at elevated temperatures longer than human-adapted strains [7–9]. Also, H7N9 viruses from a 2013 outbreak showed greater heat resistance than contemporary human strains [10,11]. Furthermore, studies indicate that H5N1 virus can survive in raw milk after heat treatment at 72°C for 30 s under experimental laboratory conditions [12–14]. Together, these observations lead to the question of whether sustained environmental warming might select for influenza virus variants with increased thermostability, potentially affecting how they spread.

The stability of the hemagglutinin (HA) surface protein is an important factor in how well the virus particle withstands physical stress [15,16]. HA stability has been connected to transmissibility in several studies. In ferret models, specific HA mutations that increased thermostability and changed receptor binding were necessary for H5N1 viruses to transmit through the air [17–19]. Similarly, stabilizing mutations in the HA of the 2009 H1N1 pandemic virus were important for efficient spread via respiratory droplets [20,21]. These findings suggest a link between HA stability—including both heat and acid tolerance – and a virus's ability to adapt for transmission.

H9N2 avian influenza virus is a subtype of ongoing concern. It is widespread in poultry in many regions, contributes genes to other influenza viruses (such as H5N1 and H7N9), and is one of the avian influenza subtypes most frequently detected in humans. However, the specific genetic features that allow H9N2 viruses to persist in the environment, particularly those determining HA thermostability, are not well understood compared to other subtypes.

In this study, we combined analysis of virus surveillance data with laboratory experiments. We first noted that the proportion of H9N2 viruses isolated in summer has increased in recent years, a period of rising global temperatures. To find the genetic basis for heat resistance, we used a deep mutational scanning (DMS) library of a representative H9N2 HA protein and selected it under high-temperature conditions. This approach identified specific HA mutations that increase thermostability. We then tested the effects of these mutations on virus stability, replication, antigenicity, and transmission in avian models under conditions of environmental heat stress. This work aims to clarify how H9N2 viruses might evolve under warmer environmental conditions.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement and biosafety

All personnel were instructed and extensively trained for work in an Animal Biosafety Level 2 facility (ABSL-2), including handling influenza viruses and managing potential incidents (e.g. spills). To minimize occupational risks, research personnel used appropriate personal protective equipment and were offered seasonal influenza vaccination. For emergency purposes, the laboratory maintains supplies of oseltamivir and has designated quarantine rooms. All experiments involving low pathogenic H9N2 infections were conducted in the ABSL-2/BSL-2 facility at Huazhong Agricultural University. All procedures strictly complied with the biosafety regulations issued by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of the People’s Republic of China, as well as relevant animal welfare guidelines. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University (approval number: HZAUCH-2024-0009).

Cells and viruses

Human embryonic kidney 293 T cells (HEK-293 T) and Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). 293 T cells were cultured in Opti-MEM (Gibco) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. MDCK cells were maintained in MEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 2.5% HEPES, 1% vitamins, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37.0°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

The H9N2 strains A/chicken/Hubei/JX2-1-4/2024, A/chicken/Hubei/JX26-30/2024, A/chicken/Hubei/JX2-1-8/2024, A/chicken/Hubei/JX91-95/2024, A/chicken/Hubei/JX52/2025, A/chicken/Hubei/EZ2-13-16/2025, and A/chicken/Hubei/20G/2025, as well as A/Hubei/11/2025 (H3N2) and A/duck/Hubei/GCX01/2024 (H5N1) inactivated by 0.25% formalin, were stored in our laboratory. The following H9N2 strains were kindly provided by Prof. Xuyong Li (Liaocheng University, China): A/chicken/Shandong/D932/2018, A/chicken/Shandong/D1729/2018, A/chicken/Shandong/D161/2019, A/chicken/Shandong/127/2019, and A/chicken/Shandong/D162/2023. Additionally, Prof. Jianjun Chen (Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China) provided the following H9N2 strains: A/chicken/Guangxi/55/2005, A/chicken/Israel/375/2007, A/chicken/Guangdong/UT12/2011, and A/chicken/Hunan/YYGK5T3/2018. All viruses were propagated in 10-day-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated chicken eggs (Shandong Haotai Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd., China). Viral titers were subsequently determined by plaque assay on MDCK cells and expressed as plaque-forming units per milliliter (PFU/mL).

Global temperature change and sequence analysis of H9N2 viruses

Global temperature changes were visualized using the NASA GISS Surface Temperature Analysis dataset (GISTEMP v4) [22]. Hemagglutinin (HA) gene sequences of H9N2 influenza viruses available up to April 2024 were downloaded from the NCBI and GISAID databases. After removing duplicate entries, viruses were classified into winter (December to February) and summer (June to August) groups based on their isolation dates. Consensus HA sequences were generated separately for the summer and winter groups. Amino acid positions differing between the two seasonal consensus sequences were identified. For these positions, the counts of individual residues at each site were compared between summer and winter viruses using a chi-square (χ²) test to assess statistical significance. In addition, the annual numbers of the thermostability-associated mutations S84F, G167N, and D168N were calculated using custom-written scripts based on the year of virus isolation. All results were visualized using GraphPad Prism (version 8).

Thermostability assay

Viral thermostability was assessed by measuring residual infectivity after heat treatment using a plaque assay. Two complementary experimental paradigms were employed: (i) temperature-dependent inactivation at a fixed incubation time and (ii) time-course stability at constant temperatures. For temperature-dependent inactivation, virus samples (50 µL, approximately 600 PFU) were incubated in 0.2 mL PCR tubes at defined temperatures (4.0°C, 37.0°C, 50.0°C, 50.9°C, 52.2°C, 53.4°C, 54.6°C, or 55.0°C) for 30 min using a thermal cycler, followed by immediate cooling on ice. Residual infectivity was determined by plaque assay and expressed as the percentage of plaques relative to untreated controls maintained on ice (0°C). The temperature causing 90% viral inactivation (TIP90), corresponding to 10% residual infectivity, was determined by linear interpolation between the two adjacent temperatures (T1 and T2) bracketing 10% survival (S1 > 10% > S2), using mean values from triplicate measurements, according to the following equation: TIP90=T1+(10−S1)(S2−S1)×(T1−T2) This approach provides a standardized quantitative metric for comparing the thermal stability of different viral strains.

For time-course stability assays, virus aliquots (50 µL, approximately 600 PFU) were incubated at 41.0°C or 45.0°C for 0.5–4 h (0.5 h intervals), with additional extended time points at 8 and 12 h, or at 50.0°C for 0.5–2.5 h (0.5 h intervals), and then immediately cooled on ice. Treated and control samples were titrated on confluent MDCK cell monolayers in 6-well plates by plaque assay. Plaques were enumerated after 72 h of incubation.

Construction of the virus library and thermal screening

The virus library used in this study was generated in our laboratory based on the method described by Jesse D. Bloom and colleagues [23–25]. Briefly, a full-site saturation mutagenesis library of rgBJ16 HA was rescued and passaged once in MDCK cells to generate the DMS virus library. Thermal screening was performed by heat treating virus aliquots (106 PFU/mL) for 30 min in a water bath at two temperatures: 52.7 and 55.0°C. After heat treatment, 100 µL of the 10-fold diluted virus library was inoculated into MDCK cells or 10-day-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated chicken eggs for viral amplification. After three days of culture, viral supernatants were harvested and subjected to the next round of heat treatment. This cycle was repeated for five passages, yielding fifth-passage thermally screened virus samples that were subsequently subjected to next-generation sequencing.

Next-generation sequencing

To accurately determine mutation frequencies, a barcoded subamplicon sequencing strategy was employed. Viral RNA was extracted from the heat-treated mutant virus library using a viral RNA extraction kit (Vazyme). Full-length HA cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription with the GoScript™ Reverse Transcription System (Promega), followed by PCR amplification using Q5® High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB). Six overlapping subamplicons covering the HA coding region were generated, with primers incorporating unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and partial Illumina sequencing adapters during the first round of amplification. After purification, subamplicons from each sample were pooled at equimolar concentrations and subjected to a second PCR to append sample-specific indices and the remaining sequencing adapters. The final library was purified and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform with paired-end 250-bp reads (PE250) to obtain high-resolution mutation frequency data.

Generation of site-directed HA mutant viruses

Site-directed mutant viruses used in this study were generated using the rgBJ16 HA as a template and rescued via an eight-plasmid reverse genetics system [26]. Briefly, HEK-293 T cells were co-transfected with eight plasmids corresponding to the influenza viral genome segments (500 ng per plasmid). At 48 h post-transfection, the supernatants were inoculated into 10-day-old SPF embryonated chicken eggs for virus propagation. The rescued mutant viruses were verified by RT–PCR and sequencing to confirm the presence of the desired mutations.

Quantitative RT–PCR

Viral RNA from oropharyngeal swabs was quantified using a one-step probe-based quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) assay with the TransScript Probe One-Step qRT-PCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech), targeting the conserved matrix (M) gene of influenza A virus. FluA-F: 5'-GACCRATCCTGTCACCTCTGAC-3’; FluA-R: 5'-AGGGCATTYTGGACAAAKCGTCTA-3’; Probe:5'-FAM-TGCAGTCCTCGCTCACTGGGCACG-BHQ1-3’. The primer and probe sequences were described previously [27].

Hemagglutination inhibition (HI)

HI assays were performed following standard WHO protocols [28] to assess seroconversion in sera from inoculated and contact animals.

Plaque reduction neutralization test

Serum samples were collected from three SPF chickens immunized with the inactivated rgBJ16 vaccine and pooled for the neutralization assay. The neutralizing activity of chicken antisera against rgBJ16 and its mutant viruses was evaluated using a plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT). Heat-inactivated sera were serially diluted two-fold in infection medium, starting at 1:20. A 50 μL aliquot of diluted virus (600 PFU/mL, i.e. 30 PFU per well) was mixed with 50 μL of each serum dilution. The serum–virus mixtures were incubated at 37.0°C for 1.5 h, and residual infectivity was quantified by plaque assay. Fractional infectivity was calculated as the plaque count at each serum dilution divided by the mean plaque count in virus-only control wells (no serum). PRNT50 was defined as the reciprocal serum dilution that reduced plaque counts by 50% relative to the virus-only control wells.

Solid-phase enzyme-linked receptor-binding assays

Receptor-binding specificity was assessed using a modified solid-phase binding assay. Biotinylated glycopolymers representing the avian-type receptor (3’-SLNLN; Neu5Acα2-3Galβ1-4GlcNAcβ-SpNH-LC-LC-Biotin) and the human-type receptor (6’-SLNLN; Neu5Acα2-6Galβ1-4GlcNAcβ-SpNH-LC-LC-Biotin; both from GlycoNZ Ltd) were serially diluted two-fold in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to generate a concentration range of 0.16-10 µg/mL. Aliquots (100 µL) of each dilution were used to coat high-binding 96-well microtiter plates overnight at 4.0°C. The following day, coating solutions were removed and wells were washed three times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST). Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Purified virus was diluted to approximately 106 PFU/mL in PBS containing 1% BSA, added to the coated wells (100 µL/well), and incubated overnight at 4.0°C to allow binding. After incubation, unbound virus was removed by washing wells five times with PBST. Bound virus was detected by sequential incubation with a broadly neutralizing human monoclonal antibody (MEDI8852; 1:2500 dilution) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by an HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG secondary antibody for 1 h. Following a final wash, 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added for colour development, which was stopped after 10 min with a stop solution (1 M H2SO4). Absorbance at 450 nm (OD450) was measured using a microplate reader.

Growth kinetics

MDCK cells were infected at a multiplicity of infection of 0.002 and incubated at 37.0°C. Supernatants were harvested in triplicate at 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 h post-infection (hpi). 10-day-old SPF embryonated chicken eggs were inoculated with 1,000 PFU of virus and incubated at 35.0°C, with allantoic fluid collected at 0, 12, 24, and 48 hpi. Viral titers in both MDCK cells supernatants and allantoic fluid were determined by plaque assay.

Inactivation of viral infectivity at acidic pH

The acid stability of the H9N2 virus was evaluated by measuring the viral survival rate after incubation under different pH conditions (pH 5.1–5.8 and 6.0), with minor modifications to previously described methods [15]. Briefly, 10 μL of virus (2,000 PFU) was added to 190 μL of PBS adjusted to the indicated pH values using citric acid and incubated at 37.0°C for 15 min. PBS-treated samples served as controls. After incubation, the virus was neutralized with infection medium. Each pH condition was tested in at least three independent replicates. The remaining infectious virus titers were then determined by plaque assay.

Heated waterborne transmission in chicken models

To evaluate waterborne transmission under heated conditions, three 4-week-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) chickens (Beijing Boehringer Ingelheim Vital Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were assigned as directly inoculated donors and three as indirect water-contact recipients for each virus tested as described previously [29]. Donor chickens were inoculated intranasally with 106 PFU of virus in 200 µL. The two groups were housed in separate isolator cages, each measuring 32 cm × 22 cm × 7 cm, to prevent direct contact while allowing exposure only through a shared water basin. Drinking water was pre-warmed and maintained at a constant temperature of either 24.0°C (rgBJ16 as the control), 41.0°C, or 52.7°C throughout the 7-day experimental period using a calibrated thermostatic heating device. In addition, 2 L of water was replenished daily in the shared water basin, and all chickens had ad libitum access to food and water. Oropharyngeal swabs were collected from all chickens (donors and contacts) from 1 to 7 days post-inoculation (dpi), and additionally on days 9 and 11. At 14 dpi, blood was collected for serum separation, and HI assays were performed to determine seroconversion in all chickens. Furthermore, whole-genome sequencing was performed on oropharyngeal swab samples collected from contact chickens at the peak of viral shedding titers.

Airborne transmission in chicken models

Airborne transmission was assessed using 4-week-old SPF chickens (Beijing Boehringer Ingelheim Vital Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). For each virus, three chickens were assigned as directly inoculated donors and three as aerosol-exposed contacts. The groups were housed in adjacent, sealed isolation cages positioned 10 cm apart to prevent direct or fomite contact, with a unidirectional airflow of 0.1 m/s maintained from the donor cage toward the contact cage. All animals were kept under a 12-hour light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to feed and water. Donor chickens were inoculated intranasally with 106 PFU of virus in 200 µL. At 1 dpi, naïve airborne chickens were placed into the adjacent exposure cage to initiate the airborne transmission phase, and oropharyngeal swabs were collected from all chickens daily from 1 to 7 dpi and additionally on days 9, and 11 to monitor viral shedding kinetics. At 14 dpi, blood was collected for serum separation, and HI assays were performed to determine seroconversion in all chickens. Furthermore, whole-genome sequencing was performed on oropharyngeal swab samples collected from aerosol-exposed contact chickens at the peak of viral shedding titers.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8. For thermal stability assays and receptor-binding avidity comparisons, the area under the curve (AUC) for each virus was calculated, and differences between mutant and wild-type rgBJ16 viruses were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparison post-hoc test. Temperature-dependent inactivation curves were fitted by nonlinear regression to determine TIP90 values. Two-sided Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlation between viral TIP90 and isolation year. For viral replication kinetics, neutralization titers (PRNT50), and hemagglutination titers, data are presented as mean ± SD from technical replicates, with representative results from two independent experiments. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Climate warming correlates with altered seasonal isolation and enhanced thermostability in H9N2 viruses

To investigate the relationship between temperature and influenza virus survival, we first analyzed global surface temperature data from 1880 to 2025. The data show a sustained warming trend, with recent temperature anomalies reaching approximately +1.1°C (Figure 1(A)). Analysis of H9N2 isolates available in public databases through April 2024 revealed a gradual shift in seasonal isolation patterns: while earlier isolates were predominantly obtained during winter months, the proportion of viruses isolated in summer increased between 2014 and 2023 (Figure 1(B)). To assess whether H9N2 field strains have altered their thermal stability, we measured the temperature required for 90% viral inactivation (TIP90) for sixteen strains isolated from 2005 to 2025. The TIP90 values ranged from 49.4°C to 52.9°C, indicating variability in heat resistance among the tested viruses (Figure 1(C) and (D)). Notably, earlier strains generally exhibited lower thermostability, whereas more recently isolated strains tended to show higher TIP90 values. Correlation analysis confirmed a significant increase in thermostability over time among the tested H9N2 strains (r = 0.7675, p <0.001; Figure 1(E)). These results suggest that H9N2 viruses isolated from chickens may be evolving toward greater heat resistance, potentially as an adaptation to changing external environmental conditions following host spillover. Screening and identification of thermostability-associated HA mutations

To identify genetic factors contributing to thermostability, we used a pre-existing DMS library of the HA protein from the A/chicken/Beijing/16/2013 (H9N2) strain (rgBJ16). This library contains all single amino acid substitutions across HA, which has a TIP90 of 52.9°C (Figure 2A). We subjected the library to 30-minute heat treatments at two temperatures: 52.7°C (slightly below the library’s TIP90) and 55.0°C. After each round of heating, surviving viruses were amplified independently in MDCK cells or in specific pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated chicken eggs. This cycle of heating and amplification was repeated five times to enrich for thermostable variants. The populations from the fifth passage were then sequenced (Figure 2(B)). Heat treatment substantially reduced HA mutation diversity and altered viral population composition. In MDCK cells, 3–4 HA variants reached frequencies above 5% after selection at 52.7°C and 55.0°C, respectively (Figure 2(C) and Table S1). In eggs, only one variant exceeded this frequency threshold (Figure 2(D)). The extent of diversity reduction and the pattern of mutation enrichment depended on both the selection temperature and the amplification system. In MDCK cells, diversity declined markedly at 52.7°C and decreased further at 55.0°C. In eggs, diversity also decreased at 52.7°C (Figure 2(E)), but both the Gini–Simpson index and evenness showed partial recovery at 55.0°C (Figure 2(F) and (G)), indicating that the final mutant spectrum was shaped by a combination of thermal selection and system-specific replication dynamics.

Based on our screening criteria (enrichment across successive heat-selection passages within the same replication system and a minimum UMI count > 30), we selected eight HA substitutions and mapped them onto an AlphaFold3-predicted structure of the rgBJ16 HA monomer. Four of these mutations (R164A, G167N, D168N and G226I) were located in the HA head domain, including residues 167 and 168, which lie near the receptor-binding site (H9 numbering). The remaining mutations were distributed across other surface regions of HA (Figure 2(H)). Together, these findings suggest that heat resistance in H9N2 HA is influenced by multiple surface-accessible residues, consistent with a polygenic mechanism of thermal adaptation.

S84f, G167N, and D168N mutations in HA enhance viral thermostability

Based on the DMS enrichment and sequence analysis (Figure S1), we constructed a panel of rgBJ16 HA mutants using reverse genetics to validate their role in thermostability. Thermal inactivation assays showed that three mutations – S84F, G167N, and D168N – significantly increased viral thermostability. The S84F mutation conferred the strongest effect, raising the TIP90 by 1.3 ℃ to 53.1 ℃, while G167N (TIP90: 52.6°C) and D168N (TIP90: 52.8°C) also produced consistent increases compared to the wild type (Figure 3(A)). Combination mutants (S84F/G167N, S84F/D168N, and S84F/G167N/D168N) exhibited thermostability comparable to or greater than the corresponding single mutants (Figure 3(A)). We next measured viral stability under specific temperature conditions. At 41.0°C (typical avian body temperature), variants carrying the S84F, G167N, D168N, or combined S84F/G167N/D168N mutations maintained higher infectivity than the wild-type virus during 1–4 h of incubation. Notably, the S84F/G167N/D168N triple-mutant virus exhibited significantly enhanced thermostability compared with the wild-type virus (p < 0.0103; Figure 3(B)). In contrast, no stability difference was observed at 45.0°C (Figure 3(C)). At 50 .0°C, the stability advantage of the mutants was only temporary, detectable at early time points (0.5–1 h) before all viruses lost infectivity (Figure 3(D)). Together, these results demonstrate that the S84F, G167N, and D168N mutations enhance rgBJ16 thermostability individually and in combination, with the most pronounced effects occurring at physiologically relevant temperatures.

To evaluate antigenicity, we performed plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT) using chicken serum against the rgBJ16 virus. Compared with the wild-type virus, the S84F mutant showed a similar PRNT50 value, whereas the remaining mutant viruses exhibited reduced PRNT50 values to different extents (Figure 4(B)). In hemagglutination assays, the D168N mutant enabled the rgBJ16 virus to bind to chicken red blood cells, while the G167N and S84F mutants failed to induce agglutination (Figure 4(E)). These results indicate that the thermotolerant mutations altered antigenicity, receptor-binding avidity, and hemagglutination activity but did not change the pH threshold for acid-induced inactivation.

Thermotolerant HA mutants enhance waterborne transmission of H9N2 virus under heat stress

To evaluate whether the thermostability mutations enhanced H9N2 virus persistence, we tested their transmission fitness using a shared drinking water model in chickens (Figure 5(A)). Donor chickens were intranasally inoculated with 106 PFU of wild-type or mutant viruses, and oropharyngeal swabs were collected for virus titration. No clinical symptoms, including sneezing, lethargy, or ruffled feathers, were observed in either donor or contact chickens during the experiment. All inoculated donors shed high but comparable viral titers, indicating that each mutant replicated efficiently and comparably to the wild-type virus (Figure 5(B)–(D) and Figure S3). At a permissive room temperature (24 .0°C), rgBJ16 virus transmitted efficiently to all contact chickens (Figure 5(B)). At 41.0°C, both the rgBJ16 virus and mutant viruses (S84F, S84F/G167N, S84F/G167N/D168N) remained transmissible (Figure 5(C)). Viral RNA and seroconversion data showed that the S84F mutant infected all contact chickens (3/3), S84F/G167N infected 2/3, and S84F/G167N/D168N infected 1/3 (Figure 5(D), Table S2). Analysis of infection timing revealed that, in all transmission-positive groups, the infected contact chickens shed virus synchronously with no evidence of a delayed cascade, indicating that infections originated from the shared water source rather than from bird-to-bird spread (Figure 5). Furthermore, next-generation sequencing analyses were performed on the peak-titre oropharyngeal swab samples collected from water-contact chickens and we did not observe common adaptive mutations in more than one chicken (Figure S4).

These results demonstrate that the S84F mutation substantially enhances waterborne transmission of rgBJ16 virus under high-temperature stress. Interestingly, combining G167N and D168N with S84F did not further improve – and even slightly reduced – transmission efficiency, indicating a non-additive interaction among these stability-altering mutations in shaping transmission fitness under thermal pressure.

To investigate potential adaptive changes, we sequenced viruses recovered from the infected contact chickens. Natural occurrence of thermostability-associated HA mutations in H9N2 viruses

To assess the natural prevalence of the thermostability-associated mutations identified in this study, we analyzed all available H9N2 HA sequences deposited in public databases up to August 2025. The S84F mutation, which conferred the strongest thermostability enhancement in our functional assays, was first detected after 2003 and remained rare throughout the surveillance period, with a frequency of 0.2% (6/2803) observed in 2018 (Figure 7(A)). At position 167, both asparagine (N) and glycine (G) were detected in circulating strains. Although the frequency of G167 has increased since 2013, N167 – corresponding to the experimentally introduced G167N mutation – remained the predominant residue throughout the sampling period (Figure 7(B)). At position 168, multiple amino acid variants (N, D, A, E, S) were observed. Notably, the N168 variant, which corresponds to the D168N mutation tested in this study, has steadily increased in frequency since 2010 and reached approximately 42.4% (1187/2800) of sequenced isolates by 2018 (Figure 7(C)). Together, these data indicate that while certain thermostability-enhancing mutations (e.g. D168N) have become widespread in nature, others (e.g. S84F) remain rare despite conferring strong phenotypes under experimental conditions, suggesting additional selective constraints on HA sequence evolution beyond thermal adaptation alone. Discussion

This study examines the molecular basis of HA thermostability in H9N2 avian influenza virus using deep mutational scanning, reverse genetics, and transmission experiments. Our data link specific HA mutations to increased thermostability and to a greater potential for transmission under conditions of environmental heat, providing insights into how this virus may adapt to warmer climates.

Influenza virus evolution is frequently discussed in terms of receptor binding and antigenic change, which affect replication and immune escape [29–32]. However, transmission between hosts also depends on the virus's ability to persist in the environment [1,33,34]. The stability of the HA protein, which mediates viral entry, is important for maintaining virion integrity outside a host [35–37]. Our work indicates that HA thermostability is an adaptive trait that may be important for viral fitness, acting together with receptor preference and antigenicity. The thermostability mutations we identified (S84F, G167N, and D168N) have diverse effects. The G167N and D168N mutations, located near the receptor-binding site, changed both antigenicity and binding to human-type (α 2,6-linked) sialic acid receptors. This may reflect evolutionary trade-offs, where mutations that aid environmental survival could also affect host adaptation and immune escape, a pattern seen in other influenza viruses. The differing natural frequencies of these mutations (e.g. the rise of D168N compared to the low levels of G167N and S84F) likely result from interactions with other genes and context-dependent fitness costs.

A key observation is that the effect of HA thermostability depends on the transmission route. In a waterborne model, thermostability was the main factor enabling transmission at high temperature. The S84F mutant transmitted at 52.7°C, a temperature that blocked wild-type transmission. In contrast, airborne transmission required the combined effect of the S84F, G167N, and D168N mutations. This suggests that while waterborne spread depends strongly on virion stability in the environment, airborne transmission involves additional barriers where thermostability is necessary but not sufficient on its own. Efficient airborne transmission likely requires not only HA stability, but also enhanced polymerase activity. Previous studies have shown that synergistic interactions between polymerase comprises polymerase basic 1 (PB1) and polymerase comprises polymerase basic 2 (PB2) can enhance viral polymerase activity and are required for efficient airborne transmission [19]. In this study, although the S84F, G167N, and D168N mutations in H9N2 viruses were associated with increased thermostability and enhanced airborne transmissibility, overall transmission efficiency remained limited. This suggests that acquisition of efficient airborne transmission may still require stronger polymerase activity. Notably, additional mutations emerged in the polymerase genes within the monitored transmission groups, indicating ongoing viral adaptation during transmission. These newly acquired mutations may further enhance polymerase activity, viral replication efficiency, or host adaptation, thereby potentially contributing to more efficient airborne transmission.

Our observations have implications for understanding virus spread in a warming climate. The finding that one mutation can allow transmission in warmer water indicates that ambient temperature can act as a selective pressure. This could lead to the local increase of thermostable variants in warm environments, such as seasonal water bodies, potentially affecting local virus ecology and spillover risk. From an applied perspective, these findings support including viral stability in risk assessments. Measurable traits like thermostability, which affect environmental persistence, could be considered alongside genetic markers of zoonotic potential. Models of virus spread might be improved by adding data on viral physical traits and local temperatures.

This study has limitations. First, although the proportion of H9N2 virus isolates recovered during summer has gradually increased over time, this trend may partly reflect changes in influenza surveillance strategies, which have shifted from predominantly winter-based surveillance to year-round monitoring. Second, the transmission data come from one H9N2 strain tested in SPF chickens; further study is needed to see if these findings apply to other viral lineages or wild birds. Within this model, the three recipient birds were co-housed in a single cage sharing the same contaminated water, meaning they do not constitute independent biological replicates for transmission probability. Notably, the synchronous infection timing among contacts showed no evidence of a bird-to-bird transmission cascade, supporting that infections arose independently from the shared water source. This group-exposure setup was intentionally designed to reflect communal poultry drinking-water conditions. Nonetheless, the absence of independent variance estimates should be considered when interpreting quantitative differences among variants. Third, while we show an effect on transmission, the precise biophysical mechanism for how each mutation stabilizes HA requires more structural analysis.

In summary, this work shows that HA thermostability can evolve and affect the ecology of H9N2 viruses by helping them overcome thermal barriers to environmental and airborne spread. By connecting molecular changes to transmission outcomes, it highlights the value of considering environmental stability – and the selective pressure of a warming climate – when assessing risks from avian influenza viruses.
