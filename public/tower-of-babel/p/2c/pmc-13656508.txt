Early pharmacotherapy versus no pharmacotherapy: difference in skin conductance response to physical stimuli in infants with neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome
Authors: Rachel Mayo, Madison Edgar, Jordyn Sutton, Leah Holcomb, Lori Dickes, Windsor Sherrill, Elizabeth Charron, Jessica Boyd, Jennifer Hudson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfants with neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome often exhibit heightened sympathetic arousal that increases with symptom severity, with more severe clinical signs linked to elevated skin conductance responses. It is hypothesized that early pharmacotherapy may be associated with these physiologic stress responses. The purpose of the study is to examine whether early pharmacotherapy (within 24–48 h of birth) is associated with skin conductance responses in infants at risk for neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome.MethodsThis exploratory observational feasibility study recruited 35 infants with neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome at a single large regional medical center. Of those recruited, 14 were excluded (5 due to ineffective stimulus and 9 due to non-severe withdrawal or parental refusal), leaving 21 infants for analysis. Infants were put into non-randomized groups by neonatal abstinence scores into early methadone pharmacotherapy (n = 12) or no pharmacotherapy (n = 9), with the primary outcome being infant stress measured as skin conductance, obtained near discharge, via the Med-Storm device at three time points relative to a standardized stimulus. Mann-Whitney U-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to assess group differences and between-phase differences, with further comparisons between treated and untreated groups performed using independent samples t-tests, Fisher’s exact tests, and descriptive statistics.ResultsAmong 21 infants (76% female; 39-week 5-day postconceptional age) studied, 12 (57.1%) received early pharmacotherapy. Baseline skin conductance was higher in non-treated infants, though not statistically significant. Following alcohol swab stimulation, post-stimulus electrodermal response per second was 0.11 (IQR, 0.10–0.15) in non-treated infants and 0.11 (IQR, 0.07–0.13) in treated infants (p = 0.59). Average peak skin conductance was 0.11 (IQR, 0.05–0.19) vs. 0.08 (IQR, 0.05–0.19), respectively (p = 0.97). Within-patient skin conductance measures did not change significantly following physical stimulation in either treated or non-treated infants.ConclusionsSkin conductance responses did not differ significantly between infants who received early pharmacotherapy and those who did not; however, directional patterns suggest a possible relationship between early treatment and physiological stress responses in high-risk infants. Maternal medication for opioid use disorder differed between groups, potentially influencing withdrawal severity. These findings support the potential utility of objective measures as adjuncts to traditional clinical scoring and warrant further investigation in larger cohorts.

Background

Opioid use disorder among pregnant women

The United States opioid epidemic continues to impact women of reproductive age, with 6.6% of pregnant women reporting opioid use disorder (OUD) in pregnancy, with 21.2% reporting misuse [1]. This increase in OUD among pregnant women has rapidly increased the rate of prenatal opioid exposure, which has been linked to a range of adverse neonatal outcomes, including low birth weight, premature birth (< 37 weeks gestation), respiratory distress, and diagnosis of neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) [2, 3]. Among these complications, NOWS is one of the most clinically significant outcomes, as it can lead to prolonged hospitalizations, increased healthcare costs, and considerable stress for infants and caregivers [4, 5]. Rates of NOWS have increased in the U.S., with 5.7 NOWS infants per 1,000 hospital stays being reported nationally in 2023 [6].

Traditional NOWS scoring methods

Rising rates of NOWS have emphasized the limitations of traditional assessment tools [7]. The Finnegan Neonatal Abstinence Scoring System (FNASS), developed in the 1970s, remains widely used to guide treatment decisions but is criticized for its subjectivity, time consumption, and lack of validation for pharmacologic intervention [8, 9]. Shortened versions of this tool have attempted to simplify scoring, yet challenges related to user consistency and optimal timing of treatment remain [8, 9]. To address these limitations, recent research has explored more objective measures, including skin conductance (SC) monitoring, which can provide a non-invasive approach to capture stress-induced sympathetic nervous system responses in infants [10, 11]. Evidence suggests that skin conductance reliably reflects infant stress and arousal, providing a promising alternative to traditional scoring methods [12, 13].

Skin conductance in neonates

Skin conductance monitoring has successfully been used to assess stress and withdrawal severity in infants with NOWS receiving morphine pharmacotherapy [14]. One study evaluating the utility of skin conductivity measurements sorted opioid-exposed neonates into morphine-treated and non-morphine-treated groups, and SC responses from a 3-second auditory stimulus were measured near discharge [14]. Infants in the morphine-treated group exhibited significantly higher SC responses than non-morphine-treated infants (Mean [SD], 16.06 [5.05] vs. 14.59 [5.10]) [14]. The morphine-treated group exhibited longer hospital stays and higher peak morphine doses, highlighting the severity of their withdrawal [14]. To date, this method has not been used in a population of NOWS infants receiving early methadone treatment to assess withdrawal and objectively guide management.

Skin conductance in methadone-treated neonates

This study extends previous work by introducing a novel application of SC measurement in infants receiving early methadone treatment, an approach that may provide more consistent symptom control [7, 14, 15]. This early intervention strategy is nested within a standardized clinical care model (Managing Abstinence in Newborns, MAiN) designed to improve outcomes for opioid-exposed neonates [16, 17].

The model emphasizes early identification of opioid-exposed newborns and the use of non-pharmacologic (environmental) and pharmacologic treatment, including the novel use of methadone anticipatory weaning to prevent NOWS in high-risk neonates. High-risk fetal opioid exposure was defined as birth mother prescribed methadone 60 mg or more, or buprenorphine 24 mg or more, daily for the final two weeks of gestation. Anticipatory weaning was offered to families within 24 h of birth if their neonate met the exposure threshold. The model aims to improve infant and maternal outcomes by employing a multidisciplinary approach involving clinicians, social workers, and hospital staff [16, 17]. Building on this foundation, this study evaluates whether early methadone treatment is associated with physiological stress, measured by skin conductance responsivity, in infants with NOWS.

The primary research question was:Is early methadone pharmacotherapy associated with differences in skin conductance responsivity in NOWS infants compared to NOWS infants who do not receive methadone pharmacotherapy?

Methods

Ethics/human subjects

The study protocol was reviewed for ethical and regulatory compliance and approved by Prisma Health Institutional Review Board. Informed, written consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian for each participant.

Study population and recruitment

This was an observational feasibility study conducted at a single center recruiting 35 infants with neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome between June 2021 and January 2024. Given the formative nature of this research, formal sample size calculations were not conducted. Instead, thirty-five infants were recruited using a consecutive convenience sampling design, enrolling all infants who met inclusion criteria and had consenting mother/legal guardian. All caregivers were recruited through involvement in the MAiN program and offered a $25 gift card incentive for participation. Of the thirty-five recruited infants, five were excluded due to ineffective stimulus, and an additional nine infants were excluded due to non-severe withdrawal symptoms or parental/legal guardian refusal to participate. Eligible infants included infants who were prenatally exposed to opioids whose mothers, or legal guardians, were able to provide informed consent, as well as infants born and initially admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) for transient respiratory distress but returned to the mother/baby unit within 48 h. Birth mothers of study participants were identified to be opioid dependent by the pediatrician providing care. Infants transferred to the NICU for serious medical illness were excluded. The final sample of twenty-one infants was assigned to either the pharmacotherapy or non-pharmacotherapy group, depending on their clinical treatment pathway and the caregiver providing informed consent. Observers were blinded to group assignment of infants (Fig. 1).

Procedures

A review of the electronic medical records (EMR) was conducted to assess maternal and infant medical and demographic characteristics. Following birth, infants were admitted to the Newborn Nursery (NBN) and monitored for signs of NOWS using the Modified Finnegan Scoring System (MFSS) [9]. Urine and cord drug screens were obtained. Both treated and untreated infants were cared for on the same unit under the same nursing, therapy, and provider team. Treatment decisions were multimodal and not based solely on MFSS scores, but incorporated score trends, degree of weight loss, intake and output, and assessments from staff. Methadone was the sole pharmacologic agent used, with no adjunctive medications employed. Importantly, skin conductance was not used to initiate or guide pharmacological treatment, rather, it was evaluated as an objective physiologic measure of autonomic stress to assess whether early intervention is reflected in sympathetic arousal patterns.

Infants identified as low risk (lower dose of maternal methadone/buprenorphine) were monitored for NOWS for a minimum of five days. If significant signs, including tremors, irritability, high-pitched cries, and/or difficulties sleeping, occurred during the five-day stay (defined as FNASS scores consistently trending ≥ 8, indicating clinically significant withdrawal), symptom-triggered methadone treatment was provided, with escalation to fixed-schedule treatment if necessary [18]. Infants with high risk of NOWS (those exposed to high-dose methadone/buprenorphine) were offered fixed-schedule methadone treatment within the first 24 h (early pharmacotherapy) to prevent severe NOWS, which typically peaks at 72–96 h of age. Once infants provided fixed-schedule methadone were stable, a 10% daily wean from methadone ensued. Medical discharge criteria for all opioid exposed infants included stable vital signs and FNASS scores < 8 without pharmacotherapy for 48 h and a clinically acceptable weight loss level, feeding skills and oral intake.

Skin conductance activity was measured using the Med-Storm Device. This device has been approved for medical use in Europe and deemed acceptable for research purposes only by the Federal Drug Administration (FDA). The electrical conductance measured at low frequency indicates ionic conduction across the stratum corneum of the skin, as determined by sweat duct filling [19]. To measure skin conductivity, three surface electrodes were applied to the infant’s foot. The electrode system consists of a measuring electrode, a counter-current electrode, and a reference voltage electrode to ensure a continuously applied voltage across the stratum corneum beneath the measuring electrode [14, 20–22].

Study measurements were obtained 12–36 h of planned infant discharge, feeding within 4 h, after the infants were deemed stable by the attending pediatrician, and after the final methadone dose was provided. Skin conductance responses to a standardized physical stimulus. A bell was initially used as the auditory stimulus; however, because it did not elicit a response from the infants, the stimulus was changed. The stimulus was then changed to passing an alcohol wipe over the infant’s forehead, with the probe placed on the plantar surface of the foot. Infants exposed to this ineffective stimulus were excluded. The alcohol wipe stimulus was standardized across all infants and data were collected and recorded from all participants.

Pre-stimulation skin conductance responses were obtained from all infants for ten minutes prior to any stimulation or handling. Post-stimulation responses were obtained for an additional ten minutes following a 3-second stimulus with an alcohol swab. The skin conductance variables of interest included the electrodermal responses (EDR)/second (peaks per second) and the mean peak amplitude in MicroSiemens, the average value of all peaks in each time frame. Measurements were conducted in one of two locations: (1) in the patient room where mother and infant received couplet care or (2) in the NBN’s lactation room. Either location provided a calm environment with all infants experiencing minimal handling and soothed with a pacifier for non-nutritive sucking if necessary.

Statistical analysis

To test our hypothesis, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare group differences in SC measures. Within-group differences between pre- and post-stimulus SC measures were assessed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. To evaluate physiologic responses to physical stimulation, within-patient change scores were calculated as the difference between post-stimulus and pre-stimulus SC measures and compared between treated and untreated infants using Mann-Whitney U-tests. While not the focus of this research, information on the need for pharmacologic treatment and the duration of hospitalization was also collected. Comparison of maternal age and gestational age between the treated and untreated groups was conducted using independent samples t-tests. Comparison between treated and untreated groups for length of stay (LOS), total peak weight loss, peak neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS) score, and 5-minute Apgar scores was conducted using Mann-Whitney U-tests. Comparison of infant sex, delivery mode, tobacco exposure in utero, MOUD type, feeding method (breast vs. formula vs. mixed), time since feeding and testing, and receipt of prenatal care (yes/no) was conducted using Fisher’s exact tests. Spearman rank correlation analyses were conducted to examine the association between NAS scores and average peak skin conductance. Additionally, Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess within-patient differences. Descriptive statistics (means/SD, frequencies, medians/IQR) were calculated for mother-baby dyads. SAS version 9.4 was used to conduct all data analysis [23].

Results

Demographic and participant characteristics

Twenty-one opioid exposed infants meeting eligibility criteria with complete data, including all SC measures, were included in this study. Nine [52%] infants studied did not receive medication to manage NOWS, due to parents refusing treatment or symptoms were deemed not severe enough for treatment.

Most infants had a postconceptional age at discharge (PCA) of 39 weeks and 5 days (IQR: 38 weeks 4 days-40 weeks 4 days) and were female (treated, 83% vs. 17% male; non-treated, 67% vs. 33% male). Infants weighed an average of 3027.4 (+/- 583.2) (treated, 2967.6; non-treated, 3107) grams at birth. Infants had a median Apgar score of 9 at 5 min (IQR, 8–9). All infants had a median peak NAS score of 7 (IQR, 6–9), with treated infants having a median NAS score of 7 (IQR, 6-7.5), non-treated infants having a median peak NAS score of 8 (IQR,7–11), where scores consistently greater than 8 were considered clinically significant [14, 15]. LOS varied among infants, with non-treated infants being released 6 days (IQR, 5–5 days) earlier than their counterparts (p = 0.013), with the median number of days for all infants being 10 (IQR, 5–12 days).

Mothers had an average maternal age of 31.7 (+/- 4.3) years. Most mothers delivered their infants vaginally (62%), reported tobacco use (71%), engaged in prenatal care (95%), and utilized formula to feed their infant (57%). Slightly more mothers had taken methadone (52%) than buprenorphine (48%) during pregnancy. Time since last feeding varied, but most participants went less than or equal to 1 h (28.57%) or 1–2 h (28.57%).

There were no statistically significant differences between groups by race, sex, gestational age, 5-minute Apgar score, birthweight, peak NAS score, total peak weight loss, maternal age, delivery mode, tobacco exposure, prenatal care, feeding method and time since last feeding. There was a statistically significant difference in LOS between the treatment groups (p = 0.0134). Median LOS was longer for the treatment group compared to the no treatment group (11 days; (IQR, 8.5–13) vs. 5 days; (IQR, 5–5). Maternal MOUD type also differed significantly between treatment groups (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.0019), with fetal methadone exposure substantially more common in infants who were treated (83.3% vs. 11.1%), and fetal buprenorphine exposure more common in those who were not treated (88.9% vs. 16.7%). Descriptive results

Spearman correlation analyses

Spearman rank correlation analyses were conducted to examine the association between NAS scores and average peak skin conductance (SC) measured both pre- and post-stimulus. Overall, no significant associations were observed between physiologic stress responses and NAS scores. Pre-stimulus average peak SC was not significantly correlated with NAS scores (p=-0.10, p = 0.65; n = 21). Similarly, post-stimulus average peak SC demonstrated a weak, non-significant positive correlation with NAS scores (p = 0.18, p = 0.44; n = 21). These findings indicate that, within this sample, NAS scores were not meaningfully associated with either baseline or stimulus-evoked skin conductance responses.

Pre-test stimulus

As shown in Table 2, median baseline (pre-test stimulus) EDR/sec was 0.08 (IQR, 0.03–0.2) for non-treated infants compared with 0.05 (IQR, 0.04–0.07) for treated infants (Mann-Whitney U: p = 0.46). Median baseline average peak skin conductance was 0.06 (IQR, 0.03–0.09) for non-treated infants and 0.08 (IQR, 0.03–0.11) for treated infants (Mann-Whitney U: p = 0.89).

Post-test stimulus

Median post-test stimulus EDR/sec was 0.11 (IQR, 0.10–0.15) for non-treated infants compared with 0.11 (IQR, 0.07–0.13) for treated infants (Mann-Whitney U: p = 0.59). Median post-test stimulus average peak skin conductance was 0.11 (IQR, 0.05–0.19) for non-treated infants and 0.08 (IQR, 0.05–0.19) for treated infants (Mann-Whitney U: p = 0.97) (Table 2).

Within-patient change scores

To assess the response to stimulation, within-patient change scores (post-stimulus minus pre-stimulus) were compared between groups. No significant differences were observed for EDR/sec (median change 0.03 vs. 0.03, p=0.75) or average SC peak (median change 0.02 vs. 0.05, p = 0.91) (Table 3).

Within-group pre/post comparisons

Within-group pre- to post-stimulus comparisons showed that in the non-treated group, neither EDR/sec (Wilcoxon signed-rank test: p = 0.195) nor average peak SC (Wilcoxon signed-rank test: p = 0.125) change significantly following stimulation. In the treated group, neither EDR/sec (Wilcoxon signed-rank test: p = 0.125) nor average peak SC (Wilcoxon signed-rank test: p = 0.083) changed significantly following stimulation.

Discussion

This study examined whether early methadone treatment to prevent severe NOWS is associated with an objective measure of sympathetic arousal in infants, and results should be considered with caution. The anticipatory weaning approach employed in this study differs from the predominant strategy of minimizing opioid exposure and prioritizing non-pharmacologic interventions. However, all infants received standardized non-pharmacologic care, including rooming-in as the foundation of their NOWS management. Although not universally adopted, it has been associated with favorable clinical outcomes in prior studies and represents the evolving NOWS care management strategies [16, 17]. We found no statistically significant differences in SC measurements both at baseline and after the standardized physical stimulus. Likewise, NAS scores were not significantly associated with pre- or post-stimulus SC responses. These findings suggest that, within this sample, methadone treatment status and withdrawal severity were not associated with measurable differences in physiologic stress responses. Both treated and non-treated infants demonstrated small increases in SC following stimulation; however, the magnitude of these responses was similar between groups and within-group changes did not reach statistical significance.

Several factors may explain the absence of an observed effect. First, the study’s small sample size reduced the ability to detect potentially meaningful differences between groups. Secondly, SC measurements were also obtained near hospital discharge, after treated infants had achieved clinical stabilization and undergone methadone weaning. Consequently, physiologic differences that may have been present during peak withdrawal could have diminished by the time of the assessment. Third, substantial heterogeneity in prenatal opioid exposure, including differences in MOUDs, may have influenced neonatal autonomic responses independent of treatment status. Additionally, environmental factors, feeding status, infant behavioral status and individual variability in autonomic nervous system functioning may have contributed additional measurement variability.

The scope of this study was limited to physiologic stress as measured by skin conductance and was not designed to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of treatment strategies. Longer lengths of stay in the treated group reflect the structured weaning protocol inherent to the MAiN model, which was developed to mitigate clinical burden of withdrawal. As such, SC findings should not be interpreted as evidence for or against the value of early pharmacologic intervention.

Previous research has shown that infants with fetal opioid exposure exhibit heightened sympathetic activation, increased irritability, and altered stress responses compared to non-exposed infants [24, 25]. The present study differs from prior SC research in several methodologic ways. Prior studies assessed SC during early active withdrawal (24–48 h of life), using a physical pain stimulus such as heel lance [19, 25], whereas the present study assessed SC near hospital discharge following clinical stabilization. Additionally, prior work employed auditory stimuli, specifically ringing a bell, to elicit responses near discharge, whereas this study used a standardized physical stimulus of an alcohol wipe [14, 19]. Finally, unlike prior studies that compared reactively treated and untreated infants, the present study evaluated infants receiving prophylactic methadone treatment as part of an anticipatory weaning protocol, representing a treatment model that is not previously examined [14, 19, 26]. These differences limit direct comparisons with prior work and may explain why we did not observe significant associations between measures. Rather, these findings highlight the need for larger studies to determine whether skin conductance can reliably detect physiologic stress responses that may not be captured by traditional clinical assessments. The next critical step is an adequately powered randomized controlled trial to evaluate whether prophylactic pharmacotherapy produced measurable differences in physiologic responses across the withdrawal trajectory.

SC has been validated in multiple pediatric populations as a sensitive measure of pain and arousal [27]. Although no significant differences were detected between treatment groups, variability in autonomic responses may have been influenced by factors such as maternal MOUD type, withdrawal trajectory, and individual differences in physiologic regulation. In this sample, methadone exposure predominates among treated infants, whereas buprenorphine exposure predominates among untreated infants. This pattern is consistent with prior literature demonstrating that buprenorphine-exposed infants typically experience milder withdrawal, require less pharmacotherapy, and experience shorter hospital stays than methadone-exposed infants [24, 28, 29]. These characteristics likely contributed to the variability in physiologic responses and may have limited the ability to detect statistically significant group differences.

Several studies have documented that withdrawal signs in buprenorphine-exposed neonates may peak at or after 96 h [29, 30]. In this context, SC measurements obtained within 48 h of discharge were designed to assess physiologic arousal at a standardized, clinically relevant time point following resolution of all significant signs of withdrawal. As such, these measurements may reflect residual or stabilized autonomic tone rather than maximal withdrawal physiology. Beyond opioid replacement therapy, the sympathetic activation characteristics of NOWS raises consideration of pharmacologic agents that more directly target autonomic dysregulation. Alpha-2 adrenergic agonists such as clonidine reduce noradrenergic activity and attenuate sympathetic outflow. In current practice, however, clonidine has predominantly been used as a second-line agent when withdrawal is inadequately controlled with opioid replacement rather than as routine monotherapy, and available evidence has been limited by small sample sizes and insufficient long-term safety and neurodevelopmental outcome data [31, 32]. Future investigation of alpha-2 adrenergic agonists and their effects on objective physiologic measures such as SC represent a meaningful direction for research in this population.

Variability in NOWS symptom presentation, particularly among buprenorphine-exposed infants, may contribute to underestimation of withdrawal severity when assessments rely solely on periodic clinical scoring like the Modified Finnegan Scoring System (MFSS). Prior studies have demonstrated limited interrater reliability and an inability of MFSS to capture rapid fluctuations in withdrawal severity [7]. Consistent with this work, physiological measures such as SC may have potential to supplement traditional scoring systems because they provide objective measures of autonomic activity [24]. However, additional research is needed to determine their clinical utility and relationship to withdrawal severity.

Skin conductance measurement is not yet a part of routine clinical care for NOWS, and several feasibility considerations warrant discussion. SC monitoring is non-invasive, with an application profile comparable to other standard monitoring modalities used in the newborn’s nursery, such as pulse oximetry and cardiorespiratory monitoring.

A key strength of this study lies in its novel application of a validated physiologic biomarker to characterize autonomic dysregulation in a clinically heterogeneous population. The prospective design, standardized conditions, and controlled measurement environment during data collection further support the internal validity of the findings. Additionally, this study has several limitations. The small sample size limited statistical power and increased the risk of type 2 error. A formal priori sample size calculation was not conducted given the formative nature of the work. This approach may limit the generalizability of findings and reduce statistical power, and results should be interpreted with caution and confirmed in a larger study with a representative sample. Because this was an exploratory study, treatment allocation was decided using structured clinical risk-stratification approach rather than randomization, resulting in non-equivalent groups. Infants at higher risk for significant NOWS received early pharmacologic treatment, while lower-risk infants were managed expectantly. This introduces confounding by indication, as observed differences in skin conductance may reflect underlying differences in disease severity rather than the effect of methadone treatment alone, limiting causal inference. Consequently, the absence of significant differences in SC measures should not be interpreted as evidence that treatment had no physiological effect, as similarities between groups may reflect both treatment effects and baseline differences in withdrawal risks.

A notable limitation of group comparability in this study is the significant difference in maternal MOUD type between groups which reflects the clinical risk-stratification protocol and likely contributed to observed heterogeneity. A comparable distribution across groups would strengthen future research. In addition, SC was measured at a single time point, precluding longitudinal assessment of autonomic regulation. The sample of this study was also limited to only White/Caucasian participants, which may limit the applicability of these findings to other populations. Further, unmeasured maternal substance and prescribed medication exposures were not provided in the present analysis and may have contributed to the variability in withdrawal severity and physiological stress responses among the infants. Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates the feasibility of collecting SC measurements in opioid-exposed infants and supports further investigation of SC as a potential physiologic marker of autonomic activity and withdrawal-related stress in larger, adequately powered cohorts.

Conclusion

In this pilot study of infants with neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS), early methadone treatment was not associated with statistically significant reductions in skin conductance responses compared to no pharmacologic intervention. Although non-treated infants demonstrated slightly higher median post-stimulus skin conductance, these differences were small and not significant. The use of skin conductance as an objective physiologic measure highlights its potential to detect subtle stress responses that may not be captured through traditional clinical scoring systems alone. These findings underscore both the promise and current limitations of physiologic monitoring in assessing NOWS severity and treatment response. Given the small sample size and exploratory design, further research with larger, more diverse and randomized groups is warranted to better understand the role of early pharmacologic intervention and to determine whether skin conductance can meaningfully inform clinical decision-making and improve outcomes for infants affected by NOWS.
