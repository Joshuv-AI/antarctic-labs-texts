Wide QRS Tachycardias With Syncope
Authors: Maria C.B. Şuran, Andreea E. Velcea, Cristina Grigore, Cãlin Silişte, Dragoş Vinereanu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe differential diagnosis of wide complex tachycardias can be challenging but is essential for correct diagnosis and treatment.Case SummaryWe describe the case of a patient presenting with 2 wide complex tachycardias, both with left bundle branch block-like morphology, left superior axis, and slurred onset. The diagnosis of pre-excited atrial flutter with 1:1, respectively, 2:1 conduction was made after careful analysis of the tracings. Electrocardiogram in sinus rhythm showed short PR but inapparent pre-excitation. An invasive electrophysiological study confirmed an anterograde-conducting right postero-septal pathway, and radiofrequency ablation was performed successfully.DiscussionPre-excited atrial flutter may mimic ventricular tachycardia and represents an important diagnostic challenge with significant therapeutic implications.Take-Home MessagesThis case highlights the importance of correct differential diagnosis of wide complex tachycardias, as management strategies differ substantially. Careful electrocardiogram analysis is essential, as pre-excitation may be inapparent in sinus rhythm even for high-risk accessory pathways.

History of Presentation

A 41-year-old man presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset rapid palpitations and syncope, with similar episodes recurrent for 20 years.

Past Medical History

The patient had no chronic medication and no other significant medical history.

Differential Diagnosis

The main differential diagnosis for the acute presentation included tachyarrhythmias (ventricular tachycardias [VT] and supraventricular tachycardias [SVTs] with hemodynamic compromise, particularly in the setting of antegrade accessory pathway conduction), structural cardiopulmonary causes (eg, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, severe valvular obstruction, and pulmonary embolism), and autonomic causes (eg, situational syncope and postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome).

Investigations

Emergency department electrocardiograms (ECGs) (Figure 1A and 1B) showed 2 wide complex tachycardias (WCTs), with similar left bundle branch block-like morphology, left superior axis, and slurred onset, suggesting VT. The QRS was wider in Figure 1A than in Figure 1B, and the ventricular rate in Figure 1A was 270/min, exactly double that in Figure 1B, of 135/min. Atrial flutter waves were visible in Figure 1B, especially in lead II, with a rate of 270/min. Therefore, our diagnosis was atrial flutter with antegrade accessory pathway conduction: in Figure 1A with 1:1 conduction and fully pre-excited QRS, and in Figure 1B with fusion between the accessory pathway and atrioventricular (AV) nodal conduction due to the slower rate. QRS onset in panel Figure 1B was masked by the positive overshoot of flutter waves in the inferior leads. Accessory pathway allowed conduction at >240/min, thus establishing it as high risk. Echocardiography did not show any structural abnormalities.

Management

The tachycardia was electrically cardioverted, as AV nodal blocking agents (beta-blockers or calcium-channel blockers, indicated in atrial fibrillation or flutter with normal AV conduction) and amiodarone (used in stable monomorphic VT) are contraindicated in pre-excited atrial flutter or fibrillation. In this setting, AV nodal blockade may facilitate preferential conduction over the accessory pathway, resulting in very rapid ventricular rates and increasing the risk of ventricular fibrillation.

ECG in sinus rhythm (Figure 1C) showed short PR (100 ms) but inapparent pre-excitation due to fast AV nodal conduction, noting that septal q waves were absent in V6. Negative T waves in the inferior leads were interpreted as T wave memory.

Management of patients with rapidly conducting antegrade accessory pathways is by using ablation as the first-line therapy.2 Electrophysiological study confirmed an antegrade-conducting right postero-septal accessory pathway, and radiofrequency ablation was successful (Figures 1E and 1F). Cavo-tricuspid isthmus ablation was also performed.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postablation ECG (Figure 1D) showed sinus rhythm with a longer PR interval, but still low-normal (120 ms), again proving fast AV nodal conduction. There was no delta wave, normal septal waves were visible, and memory T-wave inversions persisted. The patient was asymptomatic at 1-month follow-up.

Discussion

The differential diagnosis of WCTs remains a challenge. WCTs have several possible mechanisms: VT, SVT with bundle branch block aberrancy, SVT with antegrade conduction over an accessory pathway, tachycardia with a paced ventricular rhythm, severe electrolyte imbalances, and toxicity of antiarrhythmic drugs. Discrimination between VT and SVT is essential for management, and most QRS morphology algorithms differentiate reliably only between VT and SVT with bundle branch block.3 Pre-excited atrial flutter is less common than pre-excited atrial fibrillation, may present as a fast, regular WCT due to 1:1 conduction over a rapidly conducting accessory pathway, and can be morphologically indistinguishable from basal VT.

Conclusions

This case illustrates the importance of systematic differential diagnosis of WCTs and recognition of pre-excited atrial flutter. Careful ECG analysis is necessary for correct diagnosis and treatment, as pre-excitation may be inapparent in sinus rhythm even for high-risk accessory pathways.
