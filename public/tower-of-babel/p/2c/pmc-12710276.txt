Variable Manifestations of Progressive Massive Fibrosis on Chest CT: A Pictorial Essay
Authors: Su Hyeon Im, Ji Young Rho, Se Ri Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The detection of progressive massive fibrosis (PMF) in regions where silicosis is endemic presents diagnostic challenges due to its resemblance to other conditions, such as lung cancer and tuberculosis on radiographs. Therefore, there is an increasing reliance on chest CT due to its higher sensitivity and specificity compared to conventional chest radiography. In this pictorial essay, we aim to illustrate the diverse manifestations of PMF on chest CT, offering valuable insights for an accurate differential diagnosis. Recognizing these manifestations is essential for improving diagnostic precision and minimizing unnecessary procedures.

INTRODUCTION

Pneumoconiosis, particularly silicosis resulting from the inhalation of crystalline silica, contributes significantly to lung fibrosis (1). As silica constitutes over 95% of rocks and stones, high-risk occupations include mining, quarrying, drilling, foundry work, ceramics manufacturing, and sandblasting (12). Silicosis can be acute or chronic, with chronic silicosis being more prevalent (2). Chronic silicosis can be simple, characterized by numerous small nodules less than 10 mm in diameter in the upper region of the lungs, or complicated, also referred to as progressive massive fibrosis (PMF). PMF features large opacities greater than 1 cm in diameter on radiographs (12) and is more effectively detected on CT imaging, which provides greater diagnostic clarity compared to conventional radiography (3467). Diagnosing PMF can be challenging due to its radiographic resemblance to conditions such as lung cancer and tuberculosis (TB) in regions endemic to silicosis. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to highlight both the common and uncommon imaging features of PMF, offering valuable insights for differential diagnosis.

COMMON MANIFESTATIONS OF PMF

PMF is predominantly observed in cases of chronic silicosis, a condition that manifests 10–30 years after extended exposure to low concentrations of crystalline silica. This form of pneumoconiosis leads to severe respiratory impairment due to the extensive damage to lung tissue, presenting as reduced perfusion and compromised lung function (8910).

PMF arises mainly due to the clustering of silicotic nodules, and is radiologically identified by bilateral, symmetric, and calcified masses with irregular borders, typically greater than 1 cm in diameter, and situated in the posterior region of the upper lobes (12). Factors such as increased particle deposition and diminished lymphatic drainage contribute to this pattern (11). As fibrosis progresses, these masses tend to migrate toward the hilum, a movement that is often associated with the development of paracicatricial emphysema. The International Labor Organization (ILO) classification for radiographic appearances categorizes these masses as follows: Category A, masses smaller than 50 mm (Fig. 1); Category B, masses exceeding 50 mm but are confined within the right upper lung zone (Fig. 2); and Category C, masses surpassing this boundary (Fig. 3) (12). Despite a predilection for the upper zones, a notable number of patients exhibited large opacities in the middle or lower zones, with a significant proportion of these being unilateral, predominantly on the right side (13).

A classification system for chest CT scans parallel to the ILO classification for chest radiographs has also been developed (14). The sensitivity of chest CT, particularly high-resolution CT (HRCT), surpasses that of chest radiography, establishing it as a superior method for the early detection of silicosis and characterization of PMF (34567). In recent studies, CT findings of PMF have been thoroughly described. SarI et al. (9) reported that bilateral involvement of PMF was identified in 90% of the cases, with involvement of both upper lobes in 95.6% of these cases. Only 4.4% of cases showed PMF lesions in the middle and lower lobes (9). Among 171 PMF lesions, 78.3% were irregularly shaped, 15.2% were spherical, and 6.4% were discoid. These masses often exhibited calcifications (63%), which were primarily punctate in nature (9). HRCT findings by Ferreira et al. (15) suggested that PMFs are typically located in the upper and posterior thirds of the lungs, featuring air bronchograms and calcifications within the masses in more than half of the examined cases.

Paracicatricial emphysema is strongly correlated with impaired lung function (1617). CT scans frequently reveal pleural involvement and emphysema along with PMF (915). Cicatricial paraseptal emphysema between the PMF lesion and visceral pleura was identified in 39% of the cases, and a thick pleural band extending from the pleura adjacent to the lesion was observed in 86.7% of the cases, indicating advanced disease (Fig. 2) (9). Additionally, pleural pseudoplaques, representing the fusion of subpleural nodules, were observed in 5.6% of the cases (Fig. 1) (9).

Calcifications are frequently observed within the enlarged mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes (12915), with punctate calcifications being more common than eggshell or diffuse types (Fig. 4) (9). Lymphadenopathy may be the first manifestation following exposure to low levels of silica, preceding the parenchymal manifestations of silicosis. Fibrotic nodules in the lymph nodes can potentially diminish the clearance of silica from the lungs, impairing lymphatic drainage, and increasing the burden of dust in the lungs (181920).

UNCOMMON PRESENTATIONS OF PMF ON CT

PMF might manifest in atypical forms, such as unilateral or unusually positioned solitary masses (Fig. 5). Studies have revealed that unilateral PMF occurs in approximately 10% of cases, predominantly affecting the right upper lobe (9). Given that calcification is frequent in PMF lesions (915), rare non-calcified solitary masses might be misinterpreted as lung cancer (Fig. 6) (212223). A characteristic CT finding is the connection of these masses to thickened nodular bronchovascular bundles, aligning with the pathophysiology of pneumoconiosis, which is characterized by nodular or irregular thickening of interstitial tissue due to lymphatic impairment (23).

Researchers have proposed using 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) PET/CT and chest CT to differentiate between PMF and lung cancer. However, the use of 18F-FDG PET/CT in diagnosing PMF is limited, particularly in cases where lung cancer or granulomatous diseases are considered (Fig. 7) (242526). MRI is also beneficial in distinguishing PMF from lung cancer. PMF shows low signal intensity on T2-weighted images due to fibrosis and calcification, whereas tumor cells typically show high or intermediate signal intensities (272829).

The distinction from lung cancer is crucial when PMF presents as a rapidly growing mass, contrary to its usual slow-growing nature, particularly in the absence of other radiological or clinical evidence of pneumoconiosis (Fig. 8) (2330).

Although cavitation is rare in PMF lesions, it may occur due to concomitant TB and ischemic necrosis (Fig. 9) (91529). TB and nontuberculous mycobacterial infections often coexist with silicosis, with a higher incidence in cases of PMF, potentially accelerating pneumoconiosis progression (Fig. 10) (93132333435). Some authors have suggested that new-onset or rapidly enlarging parenchymal shadows, cavitation, pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, and bronchial narrowing or stenosis are more suggestive of TB (36).

CONCLUSION

CT imaging exhibits greater sensitivity compared to chest radiography for detecting PMF. PMF typically presents with upper lung zone predominance and frequently occurs bilaterally, although it might also be found in the mid or lower lung zones and can present unilaterally, with the right upper zone being the most common site of unilateral PMF. Therefore, radiologists should recognize the wide range of PMF presentations to ensure an accurate diagnosis and avoid unnecessary tests and treatments, particularly in areas endemic to silicosis. A comprehensive understanding of both the common and uncommon features of PMF on chest CT scans aids in distinguishing PMF from other pulmonary conditions, facilitating accurate diagnosis and appropriate management strategies.
