USP2-mediated PPARγ stabilization promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression and M2 macrophage polarization via oleic acid
Authors: Jing Cao, Kui Chen, Kuan Hu, Xingyu Mi, Yilin Pan, Desheng Xiao, Shuang Liu, Liang Xiao, Ledu Zhou, Yongguang Tao, Jianing Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is an aggressive liver cancer with poor prognosis. Deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) are critical regulators of tumor progression, yet the functional significance of DUBs in HCC remains poorly understood.MethodsHCC patient-derived organoids (PDOs), HCC cell lines and animal models were used to evaluate the anticancer responses of ubiquitin-specific protease (USP)2 inhibition. We analyzed the correlation of USP2 expression and immune cells infiltration using single-cell RNA sequencing and flow cytometry analysis. Mechanistically, we established an in vitro co-culture system and analyzed metabolic data to find out the bridge between tumor cell USP2 and macrophage in the microenvironment. Immunofluorescence, co-immunoprecipitation, CUT&RUN, ELISA, and mass spectrometry were conducted to explore the molecular pathway.ResultsWe found that the inhibitor (ML364) targeting USP2 shows effective anticancer responses against HCC PDOs. Targeting USP2 significantly inhibits lipid metabolism of HCC and induces cell ferroptosis. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis and multiplex immunohistochemistry analysis indicated that high expression of USP2 in HCC was associated with the infiltration of M2 macrophage. Mechanistically, USP2 deubiquitinates and stabilizes peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) via removing the K48-linked ubiquitin chain at the K142 site. PPARγ promotes the transcription of fatty acid biosynthesis-related genes (ATP-citrate lyase, acetyl-CoA carboxylase and ACSS2) and de novo synthesis of fatty acids including oleic acid. HCC cell-derived oleic acid promotes M2 macrophage polarization by enhancing the fatty acid oxidation of macrophages. Polarized M2 macrophages further secrete interleukin-10, which created an IL-10/STAT3/USP2 positive-feedback loop to activate USP2 expression continuously.ConclusionOur data suggest that USP2, a key molecule mediating the interaction between HCC cells and tumor-associated macrophages, may be a promising therapeutic target for HCC.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver cancer with poor prognosis. Mounting evidence shows that HCC cells often reprogram their lipid metabolism to support rapid growth and survival, creating an immunosuppressive environment that hinders the effectiveness of immune cells. Besides, various immune cells were affected by lipid metabolism, including macrophages. Activated macrophages are generally divided into two categories, M1-type macrophages and M2-type macrophages. In the tumor microenvironment, macrophages often adopt an M2-like phenotype that contributes to tumor growth and progression. Ubiquitin-specific protease (USP)2 plays significant roles in the pathological processes of various cancer types. However, its specific role in hepatocellular carcinoma and macrophage polarization remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver cancer and is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths due to its aggressive behavior.13 Although the current curative therapeutic modalities for HCC, including surgical resection, liver transplantation, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, tumor ablation, transhepatic arterial chemoembolization, targeted therapy and immunotherapy are encouraging, patients with HCC have high mortality and poor prognosis.47 The 5-year survival rate for patients with HCC is less than 15%, even after comprehensive treatment strategies.8 9 Thus, it is of great urgent to develop more effective therapeutic approaches and find more prognostic biomarkers to improve outcomes for patients with HCC.

The deubiquitylation is a process that removes ubiquitin from target proteins, thereby regulating their stability, localization, and activity.1012 Aberrant deubiquitylation has been implicated in cancer development including HCC.13 14 The deubiquitylation is mediated by deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs). Based on sequence and catalytic domain, DUBs are subcategorized into six families: ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs), ubiquitin COOH-terminal hydrolases, Machado-Joseph disease proteases, motif interacting with ubiquitin-containing novel DUB family, ovarian tumor proteases, and Jab1/Mov34/Mpr1 metalloproteases.10 15 Among them, USPs have been recognized as key regulator of drug resistance via regulating DNA damage repair, apoptosis, cancer stemness and immune evasion.16 Understanding the roles of DUBs in HCC can provide insights into potential therapeutic targets for treating this challenging disease. The USP family is reported to play a significant role in the formation and the progression of HCC. Several members of the USP family have been implicated in HCC. USP11 is known to promote metastasis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in HCC by interacting with and stabilizing specific proteins involved in EMT.17 USP14, which is also highly expressed in HCC, was indicated to enhance the glycolytic function and autophagy of tumor cell by regulating the HK2/AKT/P62 axis.18 USP40 enhances HCC cell proliferation, migration, and stemness by deubiquitinating and stabilizing claudin 1,19 a protein crucial for cell–cell adhesion. High USP40 expression is linked to aggressive tumor behavior and poor patient outcomes. High expression of USP32 has been found to contribute to cancer progression and to closely correlate with the tumor microenvironment (TME) in HCC.20

Metabolic reprogramming has been defined as a core hallmark of cancer.21 Elevated de novo lipogenesis is a crucial factor in the development of cancers, including HCC.21 Fatty acid (FA) metabolism plays a crucial role in the development and progression of cancer. Tumor cells often exhibit increased lipid synthesis and uptake, which supports their rapid proliferation and tumorigenesis.22 HCC cells are also observed to prefer de novo FA synthesis to maintain rapid cell growth and cell proliferation.23 Targeting lipid metabolic pathways offers potential therapeutic strategies. Inhibitors of key enzymes involved in lipid metabolism, such as acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), FA synthase (FASN), ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY) and stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), are being explored as potential treatments for HCC.24 The combination of ACLY inhibitor and programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibody largely suppressed chemical-induced hepatocarcinogenesis.25 In addition, the metabolism of immune cells within the TME is also affected by lipid metabolism.26 Dysregulated lipid metabolism in immune cells can influence their function and contribute to the immune evasion of HCC cells.

The DUBs play significant roles in regulating lipid metabolism, which can impact various physiological and pathological processes in cancer development. Several members of USPs have been unraveled with their distinct role in lipid metabolism.27 USP7 controls the protein level of ZNF638 in the hepatocytes through deubiquitination and transcriptional activation via cAMP responsive element binding protein, and then facilitates de novo lipogenesis through activation of ACACA, FASN, and SCD.28 USP10 inhibits hepatic steatosis, insulin resistance, and inflammation through Sirt6.28 High expression of USP22 is accompanied by a significant upregulation of lipid synthesis in HCC, which promotes de novo synthesis of FAs and tumorigenesis.29 USP19 antagonizes RNF1-mediated ME1 degradation by deubiquitination, which in turn promotes lipid metabolism.30 USP30 deubiquitinates ACLY and FASN, which promotes lipogenesis, inflammation, and tumorigenesis of HCC. USP30 knockout largely attenuates DEN-induced mouse hepatocarcinogenesis and inflammation.25

To find out novel therapeutics for HCC, we screened 22 compounds, with the main focus on targeting DUBs. We observed that the compound ML364, a small molecular inhibitor of USP2, showed the most effective anticancer responses against HCC patient-derived organoids (PDOs). We demonstrated that USP2 promotes de novo synthesis of FAs and tumorigenesis by deubiquitinating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) in HCC. Further analysis demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition or knockdown of USP2 in HCC cells inhibited tumor progression and suppressed M2 macrophage polarization both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, targeting USP2 destabilized PPARγ via promoting K48-specific polyubiquitination process on PPARγ protein at K1142 site. And M2 macrophage-secreted interleukin (IL)-10 further increased USP2 expression in cancer cells via the phosphorylation of STAT3. Targeting USP2 may prove to be a potential target for the treatment of HCC.

Results

ML364, a selective inhibitor of USP2, suppresses HCC progression

We successfully established six PDOs from fresh HCC samples using organoid culture kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols (Absin, China) as reported in our previous study.13 To identify the potential DUBs that facilitate HCC progression, 22 selective inhibitors, targeting specific DUB, were applied to the growth environment of PDOs. As a result, GSK2643943A, an inhibitor of USP20, and ML364, an inhibitor of USP2, were revealed to uniformly suppress the viability of all six PDOs (figure 1A). GSK2643943A was reported to promote ferroptosis and reverse chemo-resistance of HCC in our previous study.31 Especially, ML364 showed the most dramatic restraining effects on PDOs. It inhibited the cell viability of HCC PDOs in a dose-dependent manner (figure 1B). The growth and proliferation of HCC PDOs were greatly suppressed on ML364 administration (figure 1C). We then validated the effect of ML364 in HCC cell lines and showed that the cell viability and proliferation were consistently restrained under ML364 treatment, while various sensitivities were also observed between cell lines (figure 1D–F). Primary mouse HCC models were further introduced to determine the anticancer effect of ML364 in vivo. Mice were administered diethylnitrosamine (DEN) to induce HCC formation at 2-week age, and ML364 was injected intraperitoneally to the model at age of 16 weeks. Experimental mice were sacrificed 12 weeks later and the livers were harvested (figure 1G). As a result, ML364 treatment yield to significantly suppressed tumor formation in mouse HCC model, and consistent downregulation of AFP level in plasma (figure 1H–J). Taken together, these results indicate that ML364 suppresses HCC progression.

Targeting USP2 inhibits HCC progression and suppresses lipid metabolism

We further treated HCC cells with two different doses of ML364 (2 µM and 5 µM), and a dose-dependent manner of ML364 was observed. Higher dose resulted in stronger inhibition of cell viability, proliferation and invasion (figure 2A–C). To explore the underlying mechanisms by which ML364 inhibits HCC progression, we performed metabolomics analysis. Our results revealed that ML364 treatment significantly suppressed unsaturated FA biosynthesis in PDOs (figure 2D,E). We then detected the cellular FFA and TG levels in LM3 and Hep3B cells. ML364 treatment significantly decreased the cellular FFA and TG levels in a dose-dependent manner (figure 2F,G). A validation experiment in vivo joined to present that the growth of subcutaneous xenograft tumor was dramatically suppressed on ML364 administration. Detection of cellular FFA level and TG of xenograft tumors also presented downregulation with ML364 treatment (figure 2H,I). Histological analysis of lungs confirmed that ML364 could significantly suppress lung metastasis in mice (figure 2J). Moreover, RNA sequencing analysis revealed that major FA metabolism enzymes declined on ML364 treatment in PDO models. Biological process of ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, FA metabolism, autophagy, lysosome, cellular senescence, cell cycle and ribosome was also significantly altered with ML364 (figure 2K,L). Further RT-PCR results validated that messenger RNA (mRNA) expression of ACSS2, ACLY and ACACA was significantly decreased on ML364 treatment (figure 2M). As mentioned before, ML364 is a small inhibitor of USP2. We then tested the expression of USP2 in HCC cell lines and established stably USP2-knockdown LM3 cell line and USP2-overexpression PLC/PRF/5 cell line to investigate the biological functions of USP2 (online supplemental figure S1 A). In accordance with the results of ML 364 treatment on HCC cells, USP2 knockdown also led to downregulated cell growth, proliferation and invasion of HCC cells (online supplemental figure S1 B-D). Also, knockdown of UPS2 lead to suppressed cellular FFA and TG levels (online supplemental figure S1E). RT-PCR results validated that UPS2 depletion decreased the mRNA expression of ACSS2, ACLY and ACACA (online supplemental figure S1F). Conversely, USP2 overexpression had the opposite effect. In vivo validation was performed using the HCC subcutaneous xenograft model. As expected, USP2 knockdown induced suppressed tumor formation and the FFA and TG levels in tumor (online supplemental figure S1 L-P). Histological analysis of lungs confirmed that USP2 knockdown could significantly suppress lung metastasis in mice (online supplemental figure S1Q). Taken together, these results indicate that ML364 suppresses HCC progression and lipid metabolism.

Targeting USP2 suppressed macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype via oleic acid

Accumulating studies demonstrate that FFAs in the TME can reshape the immune landscape.32 33 Tumor-released long-chain unsaturated FAs are reported to induce immunosuppressive properties of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs).34 Our metabolomics analysis indicated that targeting USP2 significantly suppressed unsaturated FA biosynthesis of HCC cells including 13Z,16Z-docosadienoic acid, cis-8,11,14-eicosatrienoic acid, erucic acid and oleic acid (online supplemental table S1, online supplemental figure S2A-D), suggesting a potential role of USP2 in regulating the polarization of TAMs. We first detected the effects of the four unsaturated FAs on THP-1 macrophage. Interestingly, only oleic acid significantly increased the CD206 mRNA expression of THP-1 macrophage (online supplemental figure S2E), suggesting that targeting USP2 may influence the macrophage polarization through oleic acid. Oleic acid is the most abundant monounsaturated FA in nature and presents widely in vegetable oils and animal fats.35 Previous studies indicated that oleic acid increases cell viability by maintaining ATP levels by increasing FA β-oxidation.36 M2 macrophages are highly dependent on mitochondrial energy production by FA β-oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation.37 38 To dissect whether oleic acid promotes macrophages M2 polarization through FA β-oxidation, we applied exogenous oleic acid to THP-1 macrophages and observed that it could stimulate macrophages towards M2 polarization. Seahorse analysis showed that oleic acid increased THP-1 macrophages oxygen consumption, FA β-oxidation and ATP production in a concentration-dependent manner. And this increase was blocked on pretreating cells with an inhibitor (perhexiline) of the FAO enzyme CPT1 and CPT2 (online supplemental figure S2F-K).

To further understand the role of ML364 in macrophage phenotypic conversion, we performed in vitro experiments by culturing macrophages (THP-1) with LM3 cells. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a decrease in the proportion of CD206-positive THP1 macrophages (M2 marker) when co-cultured with ML364-treated LM3 cells compared with control cells (figure 3A). RT-PCR analysis confirmed that M2 macrophage markers including CD206, TGF-β and IL-10 were significantly decreased in ML364 treated groups (figure 3B). In support, we detected a decreased level of M2 cytokines, IL-10 and TGF-β in the culture medium on ML364 treatment (figure 3C,D). Consistently, oxygen consumption, FA β-oxidation and ATP production levels were also inhibited by ML364 (figure 3E–G). As expected, the addition of extra oleic acid could reverse such effect induced by ML364 (figure 3H–N). To validate the results identified above, we co-cultured Hepa1-6 cells with the bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). And we found that ML364 could significantly inhibit the protumoral polarization of macrophages through FAO metabolic reprogramming (online supplemental figure S3). M2-polarized phenotypes of macrophages are considered as the key cells that facilitate immune escape of malignant cells and instigate CD8+ T-cell exhaustion. We further performed flow cytometry analysis of the proportion of M2-polarized macrophages and CD8+ T cells infiltration in the Hepa1-6 tumor-bearing mice. ML364 significantly decreased percentages of CD206+CD11b+F4/80+ TAMs, while increased CD8+ T cells infiltration. These infiltrating CD8+ T cells were more active, shown by an increased granzyme B and interferon (IFN)-γ production, indicating that targeting USP2 affected the polarization of M2 TAMs and infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (figure 3O,P).

To further evaluate whether USP2 promotes M2 polarization in vitro, we stably expressed shUSP2 in LM3 cells and co-cultured LM3 cells with THP1 human monocytes. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that knockdown of USP2 decreased the polarization of CD206+macrophages (figure 4A). Similar effects for macrophage markers were observed using quantitative real-time PCR (figure 4B). In support, knockdown of USP2 decreased the level of M2 cytokines, IL-10 and TGF-β in the culture medium (figure 4C,D). As expected, USP2 depletion inhibited FA β-oxidation and ATP production in macrophage (figure 4E–G). And the addition of extra oleic acid could reverse such effect induced by USP2 knockdown (figure 4H–N). To evaluate whether USP2 promotes M2 polarization through activating FA β-oxidation, we stably overexpressed USP2 in LM3 cells and co-cultured LM3 cells with THP1 macrophage treated with or without perhexiline. USP2 overexpression stimulates macrophages towards M2 polarization, while perhexiline drastically abolished M2 polarization induced by USP2 overexpression (figure 4O–U). The same results were observed in BMDMs co-cultured with Hepa1-6 cells (online supplemental figure S4). Flow cytometry analysis of xenograft tumors demonstrated that USP2 depletion significantly decreased percentages of CD206+CD11b+F4/80+ TAMs in TME, while increased the infiltration of granzyme B+ and IFN-γ+ CD8+T cells (figure 4V,W). We successfully established the HCC mouse model by inducing with DEN agent according to the protocol mentioned in the method. We then evaluated the functional role of USP2 in HCC progression using hepatocyte-specific USP2-knockout (Alb-cre; USP2fl/fl) mice (online supplemental figure S5A). Hepatic USP2 null mice showed lower liver TC and FFA levels compared with USP2fl/fl mice (online supplemental figure S5B). We also observed that hepatocyte-specific knockout of USP2 led to a dramatically decreased AFP level in HCC mouse model and reduced hepatic tumor formation (online supplemental figure S5C, D). Immunofluorescence analysis indicated that the number of CD206+cells was lower in Alb-cre; USP2fl/fl mice (online supplemental figure S5D). Taken together, our findings suggest that USP2 facilitates tumor progression and promotes the FA β-oxidation of macrophages to stimulate the M2 polarization.

Targeting USP2 confers ferroptosis of HCC

Previous metabolomics analysis revealed that ML364 administration significantly influenced ferroptosis in HCC PDOs. Oleic acid was reported to suppress ferroptosis. We hypothesized that ML364 may induce ferroptosis of HCC through the inhibition of oleic acid. As expected, cells treated with ML364 were more sensitive to RSL3 treatment (online supplemental figure S6A). We then treated LM3 and cells with different concentrations of RSL3. The cell viability analysis demonstrated that ML364 decreased the resistance to RSL3-induced ferroptosis of LM3 cells (online supplemental figure S6B). To further investigate whether targeting USP2 induced cell death through ferroptosis, HCC cells treated with ML364 were incubated with ferrostatin (1 µm) for 24 hours, then cell viability was determined using CCK8 assay. We observed that ferrostatin could largely recover the cell death induced by ML364 treatment, indicating that targeting USP2 at least partly induced cell death through ferroptosis (online supplemental figure S6C). Consistently, ML364 treatment decreased GSH level and increased ferrous iron level, malondialdehyde and lipid ROS in LM3 cells (online supplemental figure S6D-G). The similar results were observed in USP2-depletion cells (online supplemental figure S6H-N). Taken together, pharmacological inhibition or knockdown of USP2 may suppress oleic biosynthesis and confer ferroptosis.

USP2 stabilized PPARγ protein by 48-linked ubiquitination targeting the K142 site of PPARγ

To explore the proteins that were potentially regulated by USP2, we then performed immunoprecipitation-based mass spectrometry (IP-MS) following ectopic expression of Flag-USP2 and immunoprecipitation. IP-MS analysis suggested that PPARγ was immunoprecipitated by UPS2 (figure 5A). In addition, the computational modeling was used to determine whether USP2 binds to PPARγ and which residues are required for their interaction. In the modeled structural complex of UPS2 and PPARγ, the residues, including S576, R270, N271, G273, H557, Y353 and N354 in UPS2 and D251, E351, D337, K336, P246, N335 and L237 in PPARγ, were the most important in maintaining the structural stability of UPS2-PPARγ heterodimer (figure 5B). Further immuno-precipitation analysis and pulldown assay also joined to confirm the interaction between USP2 and PPARγ (figure 5C,D). Immunostaining results indicated that PPARγ and USP2 partly colocalized both in the cytosol of HCC cells (figure 5E). By testing truncated USP2 proteins at different sites, the section between 267 and 403 sites of USP domain was identified to be the active binding site of USP2 in the interaction with PPARγ. Similarly, the LBD domain of PPARγ was identified to be the targeted domain to interact with USP2 (figure 5F–I).

As a DUB, USP2 plays a pivotal role in regulating multiple signaling pathways by removing ubiquitin from target proteins. In this study, we explored whether USP2 regulates PPARγ stability via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. UPS2 depletion led to reduced protein expression of PPARγ in HCC cells, but no altered mRNA expression was observed, while addition of MG132 or overexpression of wild-type (WT) USP2, but not its catalytically inactive mutant (C276A), could reverse the decrease of PPARγ induced by USP2 knockdown. It was therefore indicated that USP2 regulated PPARγ through the Ub-proteasome system (figure 6A–D). To prove that USP2 affects PPARγ stability, LM3 cells were treated with the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide. The half-life of PPARγ was shortened in cells depleted of USP2 (figure 6E). Such stabilization ability was only carried out by the catalytic active USP2 instead of the catalytic-inactive mutant USP2 C276A (figure 6F). We further examined whether PPARγ is a substrate of USP2. As illustrated in figure 6G, the depletion of USP2 significantly increased the level of ubiquitinated PPARγ. Conversely, ectopic expression of USP2-WT, but not USP2C276A, markedly decreased PPARγ ubiquitylation in cells (figure 6H). Inhibition of USP2 by ML364 enhanced PPARγ ubiquitination in a dose-dependent manner (figure 6I). Further, a ubiquitination assay with a series of mutant ubiquitin (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K63) helped to confirm that USP2 deubiquitylates PPARγ through K48-linked ubiquitination (figure 6J). To further determine the region of PPARγ that is modulated by USP2, we performed a ubiquitination assay using the AF1, DBD, LBD or full-length constructs of PPARγ and USP2. It is found that USP2 mainly cleaved the polyubiquitin chains on the DBD domain of PPARγ, but exerted no effect on the AF1 and LBD domains of PPARγ (figure 6K). To determine the specific sites of PPARγ protein that were deubiquitinated by USP2, we mutated the lysine residues of PPARγ in the DBD domain (figure 6L). Consequently, K142 was indicated as the key site on PPARγ targeted by USP2 (figure 6M). Taken together, USP2 proved to be a specific DUB, which de-polyubiquitylated and stabilized PPARγ.

USP2 stabilized PPARγ, which further initiated the transcriptions of ACACA, ACLY and ACSS2

As indicated by the former experiment, the inhibition of USP2 suppressed lipid metabolism and USP2 stabilized PPARγ. Previous studies reported that PPARγ promotes lipogenesis by upregulating lipid synthesis enzymes, including ACC, ACLY and FASN.293941 We hypothesized USP2 may regulate lipid metabolism-related genes by stabilizing PPARγ in HCC cells. After silencing of PPARγ in HCC cells, RT-PCR analysis validated that three key enzymes (ACACA, ACLY and ACSS2) were significantly decreased after silencing PPARγ (online supplemental figure S7A), suggesting that ACACA, ACLY and ACSS2 may be transcriptional targets of PPARγ. To determine the specific regions in the promoter of each selected gene, we used the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net/) to analyze the promoter nucleotide sequence, and found the potential binding motifs of each gene. We then constructed and transfected a series of luciferase reporter plasmids flanked with truncated or mutated promoter sequences into 293T cells for a luciferase reporter assay. The results suggested that the region (–427/–408) in the promoter of ACLY gene (online supplemental figure S7B-D), region (–801/–782) in the promoter of ACACA gene (online supplemental figure S7F-H) and region (–644/–625) in the promoter of ACSS2 (online supplemental figure S7J-L) were essential to PPARγ-induced expression of luciferase reporter. In addition, we performed CUT&RUN-quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis in USP2-depleted LM3 cells, followed by qPCR of the conserved ACLY, ACACA and ACSS2 promoter region. CUT&RUN-qPCR analysis indicated that PPARγ antibody was enriched for the ACLY, ACACA and ACSS2 promoter region in control cells compared with the USP2 depleted cells (online supplemental figure S7E, L, M).

USP2 facilitates tumor progression and M2 macrophages polarization depending on its catalytic activity and transcriptional activity of PPARγ

As mentioned above, USP2 knockdown led to downregulated cell growth, proliferation and invasion of HCC cells. Knockdown of UPS2 suppressed FA and TG biosynthesis. And UPS2 depletion decreased the polarization of M2 macrophages. With the introduction of the catalytic-inactive mutant C276A USP2, it had been validated that only catalytic-active USP2 (not the inactive mutant C276A) could reverse the inhibited HCC cell growth (online supplemental figure S8A), colony formation (online supplemental figure S8B) invasion (online supplemental figure S8C), lipid metabolism (online supplemental figure S8D-E), and M2 macrophages polarization (online supplemental figure S8F-I) caused by USP2 knockdown. Subcutaneous xenograft implantation was established as before. We observed that the tumor growth and FFA/TG levels were restrained by USP2 knockdown. And such restrained tumor progression could be rescued by WT USP2, instead of the catalytic-inactive mutant C276A USP2 (online supplemental figure S6J, K). The xenografts were harvested and proceeded to further analysis. We noticed a significant downregulation of M2 macrophage signature in the xenograft tumor when USP2 was silenced. Yet the catalytic-active USP2 could reverse such signature and re-orient macrophage to M2 polarization. Consistently, USP2 knockdown led to a marked reduction in CD8+ T-cell infiltration within tumors, along with a significant decrease in the proportion of GZMB+ and IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells. These effects could be reversed by USP2-WT overexpression but remained unchanged with USP2-CA overexpression (online supplemental figure S8L). To further determine the mechanism of USP2 in regulating HCC progression through PPARγ, we performed rescue experiments by ectopic expressing PPARγ or PPARγ-ΔDBD (transcriptional inactive mutant) in USP2-knockdown LM3 cells. As expected, only WT PPARγ, but not PPARγ-ΔDBD, could rescue the inhibition effects induced by USP2 depletion (online supplemental figure S9). Taken together, these results indicate that USP2 facilitates tumor progression and M2 macrophages polarization depending on its catalytic activity and transcriptional activity of PPARγ.

M2 macrophage secretes IL-10 to induce STAT3 expression, which further facilitated USP2 transcription

We speculate that the abnormal upregulation of USP2 in HCC is closely related to the liver microenvironment. To determine whether TAMs are involved in USP2 overexpression in HCC, we established an untouched co-culture system of macrophages and HCC cells (figure 7A). Interestingly, we found that only the M2 subtype of macrophage promoted the mRNA and protein expression of USP2 in HCC cells (figure 8B, C), indicating that M2 macrophage-secreted cytokines upregulate USP2 protein expression via translational regulation. After treatment with different cytokines routinely secreted by M2 macrophage, we found that both IL-10 mostly induced USP2 upregulation (figure 7D, E). Taken together, these data indicate that M2 macrophage-secreted IL-10 upregulates USP2 expression via translational regulation in the liver microenvironment. STAT3 is the putative target of IL-10 in tumor context. We thereby further investigated the potential relation between the IL-10/STAT3 pathway and USP2. Intriguingly, both STAT3 knockdown by shRNA and STAT3 inhibition by its selective inhibitor static could suppress USP2 expression in HCC cells, which abolished M2 macrophage and IL-10-mediated USP2 upregulation (figure 7F–I). Additionally, both co-culture with M2 macrophage and external cytokine IL-10 could induce upregulation of USP2 and phosphorylated STAT3 in HCC cells, while having no influence on total STAT3, indicating the potential transcription modulation of USP2 by phosphorylated STAT3.

Luciferase reporter assay was thereby introduced into the system to validate the hypothesis. It was shown that the culture medium of M2 macrophage, as well as external cytokine IL-10 alone, could induce the transcription process initiating by STAT3, which was ablated either by STAT3 knockdown or by the selective inhibitor static (figure 7J–M). With the help of the JASPAR database, we found three putative STAT3 binding motifs in the USP2 promoter (figure 7N). We then constructed and transfected a series of luciferase reporter plasmids flanked with truncated or mutated USP2 promoter sequences into 293T cells for a luciferase reporter assay. The result of serial deletion suggested that the binding site (–494/–484) was essential to STAT3-induced expression of luciferase reporter. In addition, site-directed mutation of USP2 also proved that the binding site (–494/–484) was indispensable for STAT3 to initiate USP2 transcription (figure 7O, P). These findings demonstrated that USP2 is a direct transcriptional target of the IL-10/STAT3 pathway.

Targeting USP2 promotes antitumor immunity

We further detected the expression levels of USP2 in normal liver and HCC samples. We found that USP2 was highly expressed in HCC tissues, and higher USP2 expression indicated poorer prognosis (figure 8A,B). We also analyzed publicly available scRNA-seq datasets (GSE125449, GSE149614, GSE151530, GSE156625, GSE162616, GSE166635, GSE189903, GSE202642, GSE210679, GSE242889, GSE282701) and divided samples into USP2-low expression or USP2-high expression group according to the USP2 expression in tumor cells. No significant difference in infiltrated immune cell types was observed between USP2-low and USP2-high expression groups (figure 8C,D). However, compared with the USP2-low expression group, the infiltrated macrophages in USP2-high expression tumors were more likely to express M2 polarization markers including CD163, ARG1 and IL-10 (figure 8E). Further multiplex immunohistochemistry analysis indicated that tumor with high USP2 expression was infiltrated by more M2-like macrophages (figure 8F). These results suggest that USP2 may facilitate the macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype. We further determined the efficacy of targeting USP2 and programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) with ML364 and anti-PD-1, respectively. As expected, this combination markedly inhibited DEN-induced HCC and xenograft HCC progression (figure 8G,H). To examine whether USP2 functions through regulating macrophages, we eliminated macrophages in vivo with clodronate liposomes (online supplemental figure S10A). Results showed that macrophage elimination partly rescued tumor-promoting effect exerted by USP2 overexpression (online supplemental figure S10B), along with restoration of the infiltration of effector signatures of CD8+T cells (online supplemental figure S10C–E), suggesting that USP2 regulates antitumor immune responses through macrophages. These results suggested that targeting USP2 may be a potential application in cancer immune therapy.

Discussion

HCC has been regarded as a highly heterogeneous malignancy with poor prognosis. Less than 30% of patients are eligible for curative surgical treatments.42 Systemic therapies that primarily aim to relieve the tumor burden and prolong survival stay limited. A recent discovery and better understanding of genetic background and molecular mechanisms of HCC enlighten the promise of potential therapy for this malignancy. Growing evidence has emphasized the crucial roles of DUBs in HCC development, highlighting the therapeutic potential of HCC by targeting DUBs. The DUB inhibitor field has made great strides in recent years, and DUB inhibition is an attractive therapeutic target for cancer and other diseases. USP1 is overexpressed in liver cancer tissue and associated with poor survival in patients with HCC, targeting USP1 inhibits the occurrence and development of HCC.43 44 A previous study found that the protein levels of USP3 are significantly upregulated in HCC tissues compared with adjacent non-tumor tissue or normal liver tissue, USP3 inhibition reduces the growth of HCC cells and induces apoptosis in HCC cells.45 USP5 is overexpressed in highly malignant HCC and promotes the proliferation, metastasis, and invasion of HCC cells by regulating SLUG, LSH and c-Myc.14 46 47 USP7 expression is significantly higher in HCC tissues than in the surrounding non-tumor tissues, and high USP7 expression indicates shorter overall survival and a higher cumulative recurrence rate of HCC.48 USP8 and USP20 regulate ferroptosis of HCC and are significantly upregulated in liver cancer tissues.13 31 49 USP9X is overexpressed in liver cancer tissues and promotes HCC progression through β-catenin.50 USP10, USP19 and USP40 are reported to promote the progression of HCC through the deubiquitination of YAP.5153 USP11 is upregulated in tumor tissue and is associated with the differentiation, vascular invasion, recurrence rate of HCC tumors.54 USP12 is specifically upregulated in liver cancer tissues and regulates the progression of HCC through c-Myc.55 USP13 is reported to enhance the proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT of HCC through TLR4.56 USP14 is highly expressed in HCC tumor tissues and higher USP14 expression is associated with poorer prognosis.57 USP22 and USP30 promote lipogenesis in HCC cells, targeting USP22 and USP30 improves the efficacy of immunotherapy.25 29 58 USP35 is upregulated in HCC tumors, which promotes the development of HCC by deubiquitinating and stabilizing PKM2 and ABHD17C proteins.59 60 USP39 facilitates the proliferation and the resistance to sorafenib of HCC cells.61 62 USP46, USP48 and USP53 serve as tumor suppressors in HCC. USP46 suppresses the progression of HCC by stabilizing MST1.63 USP48 attenuates HCC via regulating SIRT6 stabilization.64 USP53 plays an antitumor role in HCC through deubiquitination of cytochrome c.65 USP2, a member of the USP family of deubiquitinases, is associated with the tumor progression in various cancer types. USP2 stabilizes the expression of VPRBP, which controls the function of p53 and modulates the expression of PD-L1. Knockout of USP2 increases the levels of both p53 and PD-L1 in tumors. Targeting USP2 and PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in combination promotes vigorous tumor regression and long-term survival of all tumor-bearing mice.66 Interestingly, in colorectal and prostate cancer cells USP2 directly interacts with and increases PD-L1 abundance through deconjugating the K48-linked polyubiquitination at lysine 270 of PD-L1. Targeting USP2 enhances antitumor immunity in mice via reducing immunosuppressive infiltration of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells and increasing CD8+T cells infiltration.67 In addition, USP2 promotes EMT and affects the sensitivity of tumor cells to chemotherapeutic drugs through the deubiquitination and stabilization of β-catenin protein.68 USP2 is associated with cancer cell death. USP2 interacts with surviving and increases its stability. Pharmacological inhibition (ML364) or siRNA targeting USP2 enhances tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-related apoptosis-inducing ligand-induced cancer cell death.69 In ovarian cancer, ML364 induces ovarian cancer cell apoptosis and sensitizes cells to ferroptosis through the downregulation of cyclin D1.70 However, both the cancer-promoting and cancer-suppressing functions of USP2 have been reported. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cells, USP2 triggers ferritinophagy and ferroptosis through the stabilization of nuclear receptor coactivator 4 by eliminating the Lys48-linked chain. USP2 depletion enhances the iron ion concentration and suppresses ferroptosis.71 In lung cancer, USP2 inhibited tumor invasion and migration by reducing ARID2 protein degradation.72 Therefore, the cancer-promoting and cancer-inhibiting roles of USP2 depend on the cell state and the specific underlying molecular mechanisms warrant further investigation.

In our research, we successfully established HCC-derived PDOs. By screening DUB selective inhibitors, we managed to find that ML364, inhibitor of USP2, could inhibit the cell viability of HCC PDOs. It is noteworthy that ML364 treatment suppressed the progression of primary liver cancer in mice. Further studies indicated that ML364 prevented tumor malignant behaviors of HCC cells, including cell viability, proliferation, and invasion. As shown in our manuscript, we found that pharmacological inhibition or depletion of USP2 significantly inhibited tumor growth. Further metabolic analysis indicated that targeting USP2 could suppress lipid metabolism in HCC. Lipid metabolism is believed to play a vital role in HCC oncogenesis. A number of studies have found that tumor cells will undergo lipid metabolism reprogramming to meet the needs of rapid proliferation. In addition, lipid metabolism is also an important energy source for tumor cells. On a molecular scale, exacerbated lipogenesis in tumor cells is indicated by the coordinate upregulation of lipogenic enzymes activity. Key enzymes such as ACLY, ACACA, FASN, malic enzyme, SCD1 play a crucial role as rate-limiting enzymes in converting glucose to FAs. Increased lipogenesis was indicated to promote HCC development via various signaling, including mTORC2 signaling,73 AKT-mTORC1-RPS6 signaling.74 Apart from this, lipid metabolism is also intricately linked to tumor immunity, affecting both tumor cells and the immune cells, as well as the TME. Tumor cells often reprogram their lipid metabolism to support rapid growth and survival, creating an immunosuppressive environment that hinders the effectiveness of immune cells. They adapt their metabolism to enhance lipid acquisition and oxidation, to support their unchecked growth and endure harsh conditions with limited oxygen and nutrients. Besides, various immune cells were affected by lipid metabolism, including TAM, T cells, dendritic cells, and MDSCs. These cells rely on lipid metabolism for energy and signaling, which can impact their ability to respond to tumor.75 In TME, the accumulation of lipids can promote immune evasion and inflammation. Stromal cells within the TME also undergo lipid metabolic changes, influencing tumor behavior and immune responses.26 The deubiquitylation has been shown to exert significant roles in regulating lipid metabolism. Several members of USPs have been unraveled with their distinct role in lipid metabolism.27 USP14 has been shown to affect lipid metabolism by modulating the degradation of proteins involved in FA synthesis and oxidation. Its role in maintaining lipid homeostasis is critical for cellular energy balance. USP18 was reported to promote lipolysis and FA oxidation by stabilizing adipose triglyceride lipase and uncoupling protein 1.76 USP22 is implicated in the regulation of metabolic pathways, including FA metabolism. It influences the stability and activity of enzymes that control lipid synthesis and degradation.29 And here in our study, we elucidated that USP2 stabilized PPARγ, which further upregulated the transcription of lipid synthesis-related signature. PPARγ has been unraveled to promote lipid metabolism via supporting FA uptake, triglyceride formation and storage in lipid droplets, thereby might further increase insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism.77 Our present study demonstrated that USP2 promoted the tumor lipid metabolism to contribute to tumorigenesis through PPARγ.

Previous studies have investigated the role of USP2 in tumor immune. USP2 has been identified to promote immune evasion by stabilizing PD-L1 via deubiquitylation.67 Also, USP2 depletion was reported to induce endoplasmic-reticulum-associated degradation of PD-L1 and further promote the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+T cell. Additional study also illustrated that USP2 stabilized VPRBP (viral protein R binding protein), which suppressed IRF1-mediated transcriptional activation of PD-L1.66 While its role in liver cancer remains unclear. In the current study, we explained that USP2 facilitated TAM polarization orienting M2 subtype via boosting lipid synthesis, especially oleic acid. Macrophages act as a major component of the leukocyte infiltrate in tumors. Amounting studies revealed that TAMs were classified into M1 macrophages (promoting antitumor immunity) or M2 macrophages (boosting tumor progression). The versatile macrophages can express different functional programs in response to the microenvironment signals and acquire polarized phenotype. Fully polarized M1 and M2 macrophages exert complex functions in their interaction with neoplastic cells. These functionally polarized cells play a key role in inflammatory circuits that promote tumor growth and progression.78 In the TME, macrophages often adopt an M2-like phenotype, becoming TAMs. These TAMs support tumor growth by promoting angiogenesis, suppressing immune responses, and facilitating metastasis.79 M2 macrophages generate anti-inflammatory cytokines that contribute to exert immunosuppressive and protumor activities, such as IL-10, TGF-β, and Arg-1. In contrast, M1 macrophages produce pro-inflammatory molecules that trigger inflammation and execute antitumor effects, such as IL-23, IL-12, TNF-α, and iNOS. Therefore, elucidation of the crosstalk between cancer cells and TAMs is important for understanding the underlying mechanism and developing novel therapeutic approaches targeting TME. Macrophage polarization is a promising area of research that can lead to more effective cancer treatments. Reprogramming TAMs from an M2-like phenotype to an M1-like phenotype has been explored to raise the immune system’s ability to attack tumors.80 Another strategy is to deplete TAMs using drugs that induce apoptosis in these cells. This can reduce the immunosuppressive environment within the tumor.81 Inhibiting the recruitment of monocytes to the tumor site, which differentiate into TAMs, can also be an effective strategy. And this can be achieved by targeting chemokine pathways involved in monocyte recruitment.79 Tumor cells secrete a variety of cytokines, which can participate in the regulation of the phenotype of TAMs in the TME. IL-4 and IL-10 are powerful inducers of M2 polarization and marker of M2 macrophages. A recent study reported that long-chain unsaturated FAs, such as oleic acid, increase IL-4-induced M2 polarization in allergic airway inflammation. Previous studies also indicated that M1 macrophages make use of an anaerobic glycolytic pathway, while M2 macrophages are known to have an increased uptake and catabolism of FA via catabolic FA oxidation or β-oxidation.8284 In our present study, we presented that the upregulated lipid metabolism induced by USP2 could facilitate M2 macrophage polarization and suppress antitumor immune response by reducing GZMB+ and IFN-γ+ CD8+ T-cell infiltration. Mechanistically, USP2 increases FA biosynthesis and secretion, especially oleic acid, to stimulate the FA oxidation and ATP production in macrophage, which facilitates M2 polarization to decrease antitumor functionality of CD8+T cells. Our results provide new insights into lipid metabolism and tumor immune in HCC.

In addition, we observed that the interaction between tumor cells and M2 macrophage, as well as external cytokine IL-10, could upregulate transcription of USP2 through STAT3. It is therefore indicated that the M2 macrophage could also have reciprocal enhancement on USP2 expression. Together, these data demonstrate a close crosstalk between TAMs and tumor cells. Considering the complex interaction between tumor and immune microenvironment, further research concerning the relationship between USP2 modulation in tumor and other TME components might also help us with better understanding of exploring HCC treatment via targeting USP2.

In summary, we have shown that USP2 interacts with and stabilizes PPARγ in the cytoplasm, leading to an increased total cellular PPARγ expression. PPARγ further enters the nucleus to induce the transcription of its downstream targets (ACSS2, ACLY and ACACA). USP2 facilitates lipid metabolism of HCC cells and further promotes the FA oxidation of TAMs to induce M2 polarization. Polarized M2 macrophages further secrete IL-10, which created an IL-10/STAT3/USP2 positive-feedback loop to activate USP2 expression continuously (figure 8I). Our data suggest that USP2, a key molecule mediating the interaction between HCC cells and TAMs, may be a promising therapeutic target for HCC.

Methods and materials

Single-cell data analysis

We obtained scRNA-seq data of liver cancer samples (GSE125449, GSE149614, GSE151530, GSE156625, GSE162616, GSE166635, GSE189903, GSE202642, GSE210679, GSE242889, GSE282701) from Gene Expression Omnibus. ScRNA-seq data were initially filtered by the R package Seurat and standard Seurat settings were employed for normalization, principal component analysis. Furthermore, the R package harmony was used to remove batch effects between cohorts. Cell visualization was performed using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding analysis, the cell annotations were primarily based on previous studies.

Cell culture and screen of DUB inhibitors

Human HCC cancer cell lines 7404, Hep3B, HepG2, PLC/PRF/5, LM3, Huh7 and HEK293T were obtained from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology. All cell lines were authenticated by the cell banks with short tandem repeat analysis. Human HCC cancer cell lines and HEK293T were cultured with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, 41965, Life Technologies) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10270, Life Technologies). Human monocytic leukemia cells (THP1), and was purchased from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology with short tandem repeat analysis. THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (41965, Life Technologies) medium containing 10% FBS (42401, Life Technologies). THP-1 cells were treated with 100 nM phorbol myristate acetate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 48 hours to induce differentiation into macrophages. For the co-culture system, HCC cells were cultured in Transwell inserts (pore size, 0.4 µm, Corning, USA), and THP-1 macrophages were seeded in the lower chamber. All cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. PDOs from fresh HCC samples using organoid culture kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols (absin, china) as reported in our previous study.13 HCC organoids were dissociated using Organoid Digestion Solution (abs9520, China) and plated at a density of 2,000 cells in 50 µL matrigel droplets in order to form organoids and grown in Human Hepatocarcinoma Organoid Culture Medium (abs9527, China) in 96-well plates. After 3 days, 10 µM of each compound was added to the culture medium, and cell viability was assayed using CellTiter-Glo (Promega) after 48 hours of drug incubation according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Plasmids and generation of USP2 knockdown cell lines

The USP2, PPARγ and mutant plasmids were obtained from Hanbio Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The HA-K6, HA-K11, HA-K27, HA-K29, HA-K33, HA-K48, HA-K63, and HA-Ub plasmids were obtained from Addgene. To create stable USP13-knockdown cells, PLVX-shUSP2-Puro (sh-USP2#1: CCGCGCTTTGTTGGCTATAAT and sh-USP2#2: CCCATTGCTAAGCGAGGTTAT) were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). HEK293 cells were transfected with the PLVX-shUSP2-Puro plasmids, along with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2, using Lipofectamine 2000. Viral supernatants were harvested 24 and 48 hours post-transfection and filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane to remove cell debris. Following a 24-hour infection with the lentivirus, HCC cells were selected and maintained with 1 µg/mL puromycin.

Co-immunoprecipitation analysis

Following a pre-chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) wash, cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Meilun, China) containing protease inhibitors (Meilun, China). After pre-clearing, cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with the specified antibody. The antibody-bound protein complexes were captured using protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads for a further 2 hours. Afterward, the beads were rinsed three times with PBS and boiled at 100°C for 10 min to break the crosslinking, and subsequently subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

Protein stability evaluation

For measuring PPARγ half-life, cells were incubated with 100 µM cycloheximide (Sigma Aldrich), a protein synthesis inhibitor, for the specified time points. PPARγ protein levels were evaluated through western blotting.

Western blotting assay

Proteins were extracted with NP-40 lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (Meilun, China), and protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, Illinois, USA). The extracted proteins were separated on an SDS-polyacrylamide gel and transferred onto a 0.45 µm PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA). The following primary antibodies were employed: PPARγ (Proteintech, 16643–1-AP and 66936–1-Ig), USP2 (Proteintech, 10392–1-AP), β-Actin (Proteintech, 66009–1-Ig), Flag (Proteintech, 66008–4-Ig), Myc (Proteintech, 60003–2-Ig), and HA (Proteintech, 510642-AP). ECL detection (Meilun, China) was used to visualize the signals, followed by imaging on a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad).

Analysis of deubiquitination in vivo

HEK293T cells were transfected with HA-Ub, Myc-PPARγ, Flag-USP2, or Flag-USP2 C276A plasmids for 48 hours. Following transfection, cells were treated with 10 µM MG132 (MCE) for 6 hours. The cells were then washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed using RIPA buffer. Immunoprecipitation of HA-ubiquitinated PPARγ was carried out using an anti-Myc antibody, followed by detection of PPARγ ubiquitination through western blotting with an anti-HA antibody. Moreover, USP2-knockdown LM3 HCC cells were transfected with the HA-Ub plasmid. HA-ubiquitinated PPARγ was pulled down using an anti-PPARγ antibody, and the ubiquitination levels of PPARγ were subsequently detected by western blotting with an anti-HA antibody.

Lipid ROS detection

Cells were first washed with PBS, followed by incubation with 10 µM C11-BODIPY (581/591) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #D3861) in PBS at 37°C for 30 min. The excess dye was removed by washing the cells again with PBS, and the cells were harvested for flow cytometry analysis (Fortessa, BD Biosciences) using the fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) green and Texas red fluorescence channels.

GSH level analysis

The detection of total GSH was performed using the Glutathione Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) following the manufacturer’s protocol. In short, HCC cells were washed with PBS, collected into centrifuge tubes, and immediately treated with three volumes of protein removal reagent S. After vigorous vortexing, the samples were subjected to two freeze-thaw cycles between liquid nitrogen and 37°C water. After standing at 4°C for 5 min, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatant was used for GSH quantification.

Tumorigenesis models

The animal experiments followed the ethical guidelines approved by the Xiangya Hospital Ethics Committee. 4-week-old C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Beijing HFK Bioscience (Beijing, China). When the mice reached 6 weeks of age, Hepa1-6 cells (1×10ˆ6) suspended in 100 µL of DMEM were injected subcutaneously into the flanks. Tumor size was measured using a vernier caliper every other day throughout the experiment. For the DEN-induced hepatocarcinoma model, 14-day-old C57BL/6 male mice were intraperitoneally injected with DEN (dissolved in PBS, 25 mg/kg body weight). Mice were intraperitoneally injected with ML364 (Cat No. HY-100900, MCE, 5 mg/kg per 2 days) for 12 weeks. To determine the effects of USP2 targeted therapy combined with immunotherapy, xenograft tumor mice (Hepa1-6, C57BL/6) were randomly grouped into control, ML364 (5 mg/kg per 2 days), anti-mouse PD-1 treatment (Cat No. BE0146, Bio X Cell, USA, 10 mg/kg intraperitoneally two times a week), and ML364 plus anti-PD-1 antibody treatment (n=6). Mice were euthanized 21 days after drug treatment. For the DEN-induced hepatocarcinoma model, C57BL/6 male mice were intraperitoneally injected with DEN (25 mg/kg body weight) at 14-day-old age and randomly divided into four groups (n=6) as described above. Mice were treated with ML364 (5 mg/kg per 2 days), anti-mouse PD-1 treatment (Cat No. BE0146, Bio X Cell, USA, 10 mg/kg intraperitoneally two times a week), or ML364 plus anti-PD-1 antibody at 20-week and euthanized at 26-week.

Flow cytometry analysis

Tissue samples were dissociated into single cells with RPMI 1640 and red blood cells (RBCs) were removed by RBC lysis buffer. After filtering through a 70 µm cell strainer, cells were resuspended in staining buffer. Then, cells were incubated with Fixable Viability Stain 510 (BD Biosciences, # 564406) for 15 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with Fc Receptor Blocking Solution (BD Biosciences, #553142) for 15 min at 4°C and stained with anti-CD45-APC-Cy7 (BD Biosciences, #557659, 1:200), anti-APC-CD206 (BD Biosciences, #565250, 1:200), anti-FITC-CD11b (eBioscience, 11-0112-82, 1:200), and anti-PE-CF594-F4/80 (BD Biosciences, #565613, 1:200) for 30 min at 4°C in dark. After washing, samples were detected with Fort6essa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA) and data was analyzed with FlowJo V.10.

Cytokine ELISA assays

Cytokines secreted by macrophages were measured by ELISA assays (ABclonal Biotechnology, China). The culture media from co-culture experiments with different conditions and treatments were collected for analyses. The experiments were carried out according to manufacturer’s instructions.

CUT&RUN-qPCR analysis

Hyperactive pG-MNase CUT&RUN Assay Kit (HD101-01, Vazyme) was used for detecting protein-chromatin interactions according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 50,000 cells were washed in wash buffer and bound to activated concanavalin A magnetic beads. Bead-bound cells were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight in antibody binding buffer. The bead-cell complex was washed with wash buffer and incubated in pAG-MNase solution for 1 hour at 4°C. After washing with wash buffer, 2 mM CaCl2 was added to the samples to activate protein A-MNase digestion for 30 min on ice. After incubation, the reaction was stopped with stop buffer. DNA fragments were collected and quantified by qRT-PCR analysis. The primers used here are shown in online supplemental table S2.

Mass spectrometry analysis

The IP-MS technique was performed by OE Biotech, and the detection procedure has been detailed in earlier studies.85 For metabolomics analysis, PDOs were plated and treated with ML364 (5 µM) or DMSO for 24 hours. Cells were collected and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until extraction. The changes in metabolites were examined by LC-MS-based metabolomics. All data analysis was performed by OE Biotech (Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

The quantified data are presented as mean±SD. Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance were used to evaluate the differences between two or more groups. Multiple comparisons with Bonferroni correction were performed. The difference was statistically significant with p<0.05, and all tests were two-sided. All statistical data analysis was performed using Prism V.9.0 (GraphPad, USA). All experiments were repeated in at least three independent biological experiments, and as the mean SD *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001.
