Healthcare Workers Perspectives on Biomedical Waste Management in Indian Hospitals: Insights from Focus Group Discussions
Authors: Chinmay Shah, Lallu Joseph, Malathi Murugesan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Biomedical waste management (BMWM) in health care facilities is important for public health, environmental protection and regulatory compliance. Most of the surveys conducted to know the ground level practices and challenges are not able to fully understand the true reality in hospitals. The primary objective of this study is to conduct a thematic analysis of the provided focused group discussion (FGD) transcripts obtained from the discussions.Materials and Methods:We conducted FGD related to BMWM across four zones in India from 2023 to 2024 as part of a national workshop series. Each FGD included 12–15 volunteers and was led by two trained facilitators using a validated interview guide to ensure consistent data collection. The discussions were audio-recorded after obtaining consent and transcribed for analysis.Results:FGDs revealed major challenges including inadequate segregation, resource shortages, transportation gaps, and limited staff training. Participants highlighted the need for stronger monitoring, adequate manpower, budget allocation, and vendor accountability.Conclusion:In conclusion, effective BMWM requires sustained commitment from the regulatory authorities and collaborative efforts from all stakeholders. Addressing the systemic issues identified in this analysis will not only ensure regulatory compliance but also significantly improve public health and environmental safety.

BACKGROUND

In India, challenges in biomedical waste management (BMWM) include insufficient awareness, inadequate infrastructure, limited financial resources, and weak enforcement of regulations; in particular, post-COVID-19 has led to improper disposal across India.[1] As this is a regulatory statutory requirement, most of the surveys conducted to know the ground level practices and challenges are not able to fully understand the true reality in hospitals.

Focus group discussions (FGDs) serve as a valuable methodology for qualitative research, enabling the collection of rich, nuanced perspectives from multiple stakeholders on complex issues like BMWM.[2] These discussions allow participants to freely express their experiences, challenges, and potential solutions, helping to uncover common problems, perceptions, and remedies. The primary objective of this study is to conduct a thematic analysis of the provided FGD transcripts obtained from the discussions in national workshops. This analysis aims to identify, categorize, and interpret the key themes, challenges, and solutions related to BMWM.

METHODOLOGY

As part of a four-national workshop series conducted by the Consortium of Accredited Healthcare Organizations (CAHO), a nonprofit organization in India, FGDs were meticulously planned and executed. Two trained facilitators in the field of microbiology or quality management guided each session using an interview guide for consistent data collection. The FGD systematically explored current practices, categorized challenges, prioritized issues, and discussed potential solutions for biomedical waste segregation and management [Appendix A]. Each session included 12–15 voluntary participants from the workshops. Discussions were audio-recorded with verbal consent, and strict confidentiality was maintained during transcription. Interaction mapping was plotted in every session [Figure 1]. The transcribed discussions were analyzed using otter.ai, and it was listened two times personally to verify correctness of transcript as a few words were in local language.[3]

Based on the discussions, the following are the actionable, evidence-based recommendations derived from the thematic analysis, categorized for discussion.

POLICY AND REGULATORY REFORMS

There needs to be a mandate rule that a transparent pricing model for CTFs should be displayed in the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) website based on actual waste generated weight rather than bed strength, ensuring fair competition among vendors. In a study conducted among three hospitals in Karnataka, it was shown that the total cost incurred for BMWM was from 7.7 to 12.4 INR per day.[5] Currently, CTFs are functioning with limited capacity and limited numbers in a few states with lack of access or inspection by the HCFs.[6] This needs urgent attention by the regulatory oversight of CTFs, including mandatory random inspections without prior notice, and requires them to provide detailed and accurate reports. Digitalization is the key to improved monitoring of the disposal and to track the wastes.[7] Barcode tracking systems can be brought under the Government portal and should be given with less cost viable options across both public and private HCFs.

OPERATIONAL AND INFRASTRUCTURAL IMPROVEMENTS

Segregation at the point of generation using compact multi-compartment bins or mobile segregation trolleys could help overcome the space constraints commonly faced in general wards.[8] In addition, robust Service Level Agreements (SLAs) with external collection agencies regarding frequency, timing, and handling procedures, along with mechanisms for accountability, can significantly improve system efficiency. Last, uninterrupted access to bins, liners, and sharps containers, supported by efficient, sustainability, and cost-conscious procurement, is vital for sustaining safe and consistent practices.[9]

MANPOWER DEVELOPMENT AND BEHAVIORAL CHANGE

Strengthening BMWM requires investment in capacity building and workforce support as the awareness varies across as evidenced in studies.[1011] Interactive trainings such as skits or third-party training can enhance engagement and retention of practices.[12] Equally important are incentive and recognition mechanisms such as performance appraisals, employee of the month awards, or educational sponsorships that reinforce a culture of compliance.

TECHNOLOGY AND DATA MANAGEMENT

It is recommended that healthcare facilities invest in robust, user-friendly digital platforms for biomedical waste tracking and data management to ensure daily updates, accurate reporting, and prompt resolution of technical issues. In addition, enforcing the use of barcode-based tracking systems from the point of generation to final disposal, as mandated in recent regulations, can significantly strengthen accountability and improve data accuracy.[13]

GOVERNMENT SUPPORT IN FINANCE SUSTAINABILITY

Ensuring financial sustainability is essential for strengthening biomedical waste management systems. Currently, the private hospitals and small nursing homes are facing a huge financial burden in BMWM by paying to the CTFs. This is mainly because of limited availability or options to send the waste for disposal in each state. Advocacy for reforms in the GEM portal[4] to promote competitive pricing through the inclusion of more vendors or the establishment of benchmark prices can help address the challenge of high costs.

CONCLUSION

This analysis reveals that the challenges in BMWM are multifaceted and systemic, encompassing human behavior, resource allocation, technological implementation, and the performance of common treatment facility centres. Technology, particularly through barcoding and data management, holds significant potential to enhance transparency and accountability. Addressing the systemic issues identified in this analysis will not only ensure regulatory compliance but also significantly improve public health and environmental safety.
