Study on the Multifactor Coupling Effect of X80 Steel Inclined Plate Wetting Gradient Surface Promoting the Self-Movement of Underwater–Oil Droplets
Authors: Hongyuan Qi, Zaichao Jiang, Lei Zhang, Yang You, Yulong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To improve the oil–water separation efficiency of inclined plates in oil removal tanks, a wetting gradient was constructed on the surface of X80 steel substrates via chemical modification combined with mechanical polishing. Subsequently, three factors governing the underwater self-propulsion behavior of oil droplets on the wetting gradient surfaces were systematically investigated through single-factor experiments and response surface methodology (RSM). (1) Feasibility experiments demonstrate that underwater self-propulsion of oil droplets can be achieved both when the oleophilic surface faces upward with the oleophobic surface downward and when the oleophobic surface faces upward with the oleophilic surface downward. (2) Single-factor experiments reveal that the distance and velocity of oil droplet self-propulsion increase monotonically with increasing inclination angle while exhibiting a “first increase and then decrease” trend with the elevation of droplet volume and density. (3) Coupling effect analysis indicates that the order of significance of the three factors affecting the self-propulsion distance of oil droplets is inclination angle > droplet density > droplet volume. Additionally, the optimal parameter combination predicted by the model under the experimental conditions is an inclination angle of 58.77°, a droplet volume of 5.16 μL, and a droplet density of 0.73 g/mL.

Introduction

With the advancing development of oil and gas field exploitation and industrial production, the treatment of oily wastewater − has emerged as a critical step to safeguard ecological security and facilitate energy recovery. Currently, the primary technologies for oily wastewater treatment include gravity separation, air flotation, membrane separation, electrochemical processes, and biological treatment. Among these, vertical inclined plate oil separationa subset of gravity separation − exhibits excellent removal efficiency for free oil (particle size >100 μm) and dispersed oil (particle size: 10–100 μm). However, its performance is susceptible to the small particle size of oil droplets and their slow floating velocity, rendering efficient separation challenging within a limited residence time. In recent years, alongside the progressive advancement of surface wettability theory, interfacial materials with special wettability have emerged as a promising research focus in the oil–water separation field. Thus, surface modification of inclined plates in oil removal tanks holds great potential for significantly enhancing oil–water separation efficiency.

Wetting gradient surfaces − represent a class of special wettable surfaces. Owing to the formation of a contact angle gradient along a specific direction, oil droplets can achieve unidirectional motion. To date, significant advances have been made in the fabrication of wetting gradient surfaces. Wang demonstrated that superoleophobic channels constructed on superoleophobic substrates exhibit robust unidirectional transport capability for both water and oil droplets via atmospheric pressure plasma jet and mask technology. Su successfully fabricated a wettable surface with gradient composition and microstructure on a copper substrate through bipolar electrochemical deposition; subsequent hydrophobic modification of the copper oxide layer with n-dodecyl mercaptan enabled the realization of water droplet self-propulsion. Liu sequentially performed pretreatment, laser processing, low-surface-energy modification, and laser patterning of arrow-shaped oleophilic motifs on an aluminum alloy surface. Based on the gradient hydrophobic substrate, oil droplets could achieve unidirectional transport on the arrow-shaped patterned surface. Collectively, these studies confirm that oil droplets are capable of self-propulsion on wetting gradient surfaces. Currently, the mainstream strategies for constructing wetting gradients on material surfaces include solution immersion/spraying, photochemical approaches, plasma treatment, laser etching, and 3D printing. Nevertheless, inherent limitations persist, such as the frequent use of expensive, toxic, and environmentally unfriendly modification reagents in these fabrication processes. Furthermore, the substrates employed in existing studies are predominantly copper, aluminum alloys, and biomimetic materials, whereas steelthe predominant substrate for inclined plates in oilfield oil tankshas rarely been utilized. This discrepancy renders the derived conclusions challenging to directly translate into engineering applications. −

To date, research on wetting gradient surfaces has predominantly focused on the self-propulsion of water or oil droplets in air. For instance, Wang et al. fabricated a wetting gradient surface capable of enabling spontaneous migration of oil droplets in air; this surface exhibited a reduced friction coefficient that could be sustained at 0.05 for an extended period, thereby accelerating oil droplet migration. Yi investigated the spreading behaviors of water droplets in air on uniform versus gradient surfaces, and found that gradient surfaces enable directional spreading of water droplets, with a spreading velocity (7.42 mm/s) significantly higher than that on uniform surfaces (1.16 mm/s). Notably, even when inclined at 30°, the gradient surfaces still facilitated rapid directional spreading of water droplets (3.84 mm/s). However, to further advance the efficient separation of oil–water mixtures in oily wastewater, several researchers have shifted their focus to investigating the self-propulsion of underwater–oil droplets on wetting gradient surfaces. Li et al. investigated the antigravity self-transport behavior of underwater simulated oil droplets (n-octane and 1,2-dichloroethane), achieving complete transport of large-volume oil droplets (22 μL). Additionally, Guo fabricated a curved slit array channel (LS-WSC), which enabled continuous long-distance transport of 1,2-dichloroethane oil droplets in an aqueous environment via simple series-connected channel assembly. Notably, Zhu utilized the principle that oil droplets with low surface tension preferentially transport along the surface of conical spikes, investigating the antigravity transport of oil droplets (using octane, 1,2-dichloroethane, methane, and octane) on biomimetic materials under different inclination angles. The oil droplet collection efficiency reached as high as 97.9%. After further modification, the oil droplet transport speed was doubled under horizontal conditions, with a maximum speed of 67.75 mm/s, thus realizing rapid self-transport of oil droplets. While existing studies have confirmed that wetting gradient surfaces can facilitate the self-propulsion of underwater–oil dropletsenabling their directional transport and efficient collectionnotable limitations and research gaps persist in this research field.

To date, existing research has identified the key factors governing the self-propulsion performance of oil droplets as the contact angle gradient magnitude, surface inclination angle, wetting gradient surface properties, oil droplet density, and oil droplet volume, among others. For instance, Long et al. fabricated a microgrooved surface with heterogeneous wettability, and their findings revealed that an optimal wetting gradient at the leading edge of the superhydrophilic region can facilitate accelerated surface liquid transport. Notably, Ito et al. investigated the variation in oil droplet velocity across surfaces with distinct contact angle gradient magnitudes; their experimental results confirmed that a larger contact angle gradient corresponds to a higher oil droplet movement velocity. Additionally, Zhu et al. systematically explored the impacts of oil droplet volume, fluid properties, and gradient surface inclination angle on oil droplet flow via experimental characterization. Xu et al. employed inclination angles of 3°, 5°, 7°, and 9° to simulate oil droplet transport under various antigravity conditions: even at an interface inclination angle of 3°, the oil droplet transport velocity remained as high as 180 mm/s. Moreover, optimization of U-shaped and S-shaped channel designs enhanced the transport efficiency and stability of oil droplets along complex paths. Gao further demonstrated that, for a fixed contact angle gradient, a narrower groove width yields a greater maximum capillary rise height; conversely, for a consistent groove size, a larger wetting contact angle gradient corresponds to a greater maximum capillary rise height for water. Although significant progress has been made in research into the factors governing oil droplet self-propulsion on wetting gradient surfaces, a systematic exploration of the multifactor coupling effects, significance ranking, and optimal parameter combination pertaining to the inclined plate inclination angle and the physical parameters (volume and density) of actual oil droplets remains lacking.

X80 steel is a widely used structural material for oilfield pipelines and oil separation tanks, featuring core advantages including excellent mechanical strength, superior resistance to the harsh corrosive environments encountered in oil and gas fields, and excellent engineering compatibility. Compared with copper and biomimetic materials commonly employed in existing studies, X80 steel can directly meet the substrate requirements of on-site oilfield equipment, thereby reducing the material adaptation costs associated with technological translation. Building on these considerations, this study focuses on key variables directly relevant to on-site oilfield technologies. Utilizing oilfield-grade X80 steel as the substrate, a wetting gradient surface was fabricated via chemical modification combined with mechanical polishing. Employing actual petroleum products as the research objects, this work selects three variablesinclination angle, oil droplet volume, and oil droplet densityand systematically investigates the individual and coupled effects of these factors on oil droplet self-propulsion through single-factor experiments and response surface methodology (RSM). The ultimate objective is to identify the optimal experimental conditions for enhanced oil droplet self-propulsion performance.

The novelty of this work resides in two key aspects: First, fabricating a wetting gradient surface on X80 steel substrates and employing actual petroleum products to investigate oil droplet self-propulsion behavior, which bridges the gap between existing simulated systems and real-world oilfield operating conditions. Second, focusing on parameters directly relevant to the flow field characteristics of large-scale inclined plate separators, such as inclination angle and oil droplet physical properties. Multifactor coupling analysis of these parameters not only provides theoretical insights for addressing critical engineering challengesincluding the optimization of inclined plate module angles in large-scale applications, the control of surface modification process consistency, and the mitigation of oil droplet polydispersity in practical oily wastewaterbut also bridges the technical divide between laboratory-scale research and industrial large-scale implementation. Consequently, this study offers theoretical support with enhanced engineering value for the surface modification of inclined plates and the optimization of process parameters in oil separation tanks.

Measurement of Contact Angle of Underwater–Oil Drop

Given that oil–water separation inherently involves an oil–water two-phase system, this experiment was conducted using a contact angle measuring instrument (JC2000D2; Shanghai Zhongchen Digital Equipment Co., Ltd.). A schematic diagram of the experimental setup is shown in Figure . The measuring apparatus comprises an end cover, an elastic clamp, a spiral column, an elbow-shaped micro liquid inlet needle, a liquid level scale, an adjustable base, and a sapphire glass cavity. A small hole is machined on the end cover to allow insertion of the liquid inlet needle connected to an external microsample injection pump. Upon tightening, the end cover forms a sealed connection with the cavity. The transparent cavity facilitates in situ observation, and the side-mounted scale enables real-time reading of the liquid injection volume. An elastic clamp installed inside the cavity is used to fix the test specimen; it is adjusted vertically via the spiral column and coordinated with the adjustable base, ensuring that the specimen surface is aligned with the central axis of the contact angle meter lens for accurate image acquisition. To simulate oil–water mixtures representative of oily wastewater, five types of oils92# gasoline, kerosene, 7# white oil, diesel, and 10# white oilalong with distilled water were selected. These oils possess viscosities ranging from 0.28 to 11.0 mm2/s, thus spanning a broad spectrum from low to high viscosity, and surface tensions between 21.0 and 35.0 mN/m. Since the core separation mechanism of the inclined plate oil separator relies on the density difference (Δρ) between oil and water, which directly dictates the buoyancy-driven upward migration of oil droplets, a larger density difference inherently leads to a more pronounced separation tendency; consequently, oil density was systematically investigated as a critical parameter in this study. The volume of each oil droplet was fixed at 6 μL and precisely controlled using a microsyringe. For contact angle measurements, six replicate measurements were conducted at distinct positions on each sample surface, and the average value was calculated to ensure data reliability.

Schematic diagram of contact angle measurement of underwater–oil droplets in Janus membrane.

The specific operational procedures for measuring the underwater–oil droplet contact angle are as follows: First, the test specimen was fixed above the adjustable base using two left and right elastic clamps, and the height of the adjustable base was adjusted to align the specimen plane with the lens center of the contact angle meter at the same horizontal level. Second, distilled water was poured into the cavity until the liquid level was 15 mm above the specimen surface without submerging it; subsequently, the elbow liquid inlet needle was inserted into the small hole of the end cover, and the measuring apparatus was fixed to the clamping groove of the contact angle meter platform. Third, the oil droplets to be tested were delivered into the liquid inlet needle via a microsample injection pump and dripped onto the central area of the specimen. Finally, the camera of the contact angle meter acquired contact angle images, and the underwater–oil droplet contact angle was calculated accordingly.

Results and Discussion

Feasibility Verification of Oil Droplet Self-Motion

Microtopography of the Wettability Gradient Surface

A wettability gradient was fabricated on the X80 steel surface using chemical modification and mechanical polishing, and its microtopography was characterized. Representative SEM images are presented in Figure . The wettability-gradient surface consists of two distinct regions: surface A (the modified layer) and surface B (the original X80 steel), with magnifications of 100× and 500×, respectively.

Microtopography of wetting gradient surfaces based on X80 steel.

The interface morphology of oleophobic coating surfaces and as-received X80 steel shows a clearly distinguishable boundary: the oleophobic coating surface on the left side appears smooth and flat, while the as-received X80 steel on the right side exhibits typical parallel rolling textures, and the coating completely covers the substrate textures, indicating uniform film formation and strong interfacial adhesion. Specifically, the surface morphology of the oleophobic coating on Side A underwater is extremely smooth, with a roughness (arithmetical mean deviation of the profile, R a = 0.972 μm); the synergistic effect of such roughness and the oleophobic chemical components effectively reduces the adhesion of oil droplets underwater. In contrast, the surface morphology of the as-received X80 steel on Side B displays distinct parallel rolling grooves, with a roughness (arithmetical mean deviation of the profile, R a = 0.282 μm); the combined effect of this roughness and the hydrophilicity of the steel leads to a high tendency of oil droplet adhesion underwater.

Self-Motion of Oil Droplets on Wetting Gradient Surfaces

To verify the feasibility of oil droplet self-propulsion on wetting gradient surfaces, two sets of experiments were conducted using gasoline as the model oil: (1) oil droplet self-propulsion on the wetting gradient surface with Region A (oleophilic surface) oriented upward and Region B (oleophobic surface) oriented downward (as illustrated in Figures and ); and (2) oil droplet self-propulsion on the wetting gradient surface with Region A (oleophilic surface) oriented downward and Region B (oleophobic surface) oriented upward (as illustrated in Figures and ). The surface inclination angle (α) of the steel specimens in both experiments was set to 60°, and the volume of the gasoline droplets was fixed at 6.5 μL. In the figures, t denotes the total self-propulsion time, L represents the self-propulsion distance, and F stands for the resultant force of buoyancy and gravity along the oil droplet self-propulsion direction.

As illustrated in Figures and , the red-dyed oil droplet was dispensed from the microsyringe onto the boundary between Regions A and B. Subsequently, the oil droplet moved upward along Region A (oleophilic surface), with an initially high self-propulsion speed that gradually decreased to 0 mm/s. The total time for the oil droplet to move from the starting point to the end point was 0.338 s, covering a self-propulsion distance of 16.2 mm, corresponding to an average self-propulsion speed of 47.93 mm/s. Specifically, the oil droplet traveled a self-propulsion distance of 4.4 mm at 0.01 s. For comparison, Li et al. reported that when underwater 1,2-dichloroethane migrated along a wetting gradient surface at an inclination angle of 20°, the oil droplet only achieved a self-movement distance of 4.22 mm at 0.092 s.

The self-propulsion process of the oil droplet on the wetting gradient surface can be divided into two distinct stages: (1) the accelerated self-propulsion stage, where the oil droplet’s self-propulsion speed increased rapidly from rest with a high acceleration. Within the 0–0.01 s interval, the instantaneous self-propulsion speed increased from 0 to 640 mm/s, with a self-propulsion distance and average instantaneous speed of 4.4 mm and 440 mm/s, respectively; (2) the decelerated self-propulsion stage, during which the instantaneous self-propulsion speed decreased from 640 to 0 mm/s over the 0.01–0.338 s interval. In this stage, the total self-propulsion distance and average instantaneous speed were 11.8 mm and 40.0 mm/s, respectively.

As illustrated in Figures and , oil droplets with Region A oriented downward still migrated from the oleophobic Region B toward the oleophilic Region A; however, their self-propulsion distance and speed were both lower than those observed in the Region A-upward configuration. The total time for the oil droplet to complete its movement from start to finish was 0.421 s, with a self-propulsion distance of 7.2 mm and an average self-propulsion speed of 17.1 mm/s. The variation trend of the oil droplet’s self-propulsion speed was consistent with that of the Region A-upward scenario, and the entire process could be divided into two distinct stages: (1) The accelerated self-propulsion stage, which lasted only 0.04 s (accounting for 9.5% of the total movement time). During this stage, the instantaneous self-propulsion speed of the oil droplet increased from 0 to 100 mm/s, covering a distance of 3.5 mm (corresponding to an average speed of 87.5 mm/s). Despite the short duration, this stage accounted for 48.6% of the total self-propulsion distance. (2) The decelerated self-propulsion stage, with a duration of 0.381 s. Here, the instantaneous self-propulsion speed of the oil droplet gradually decreased from 100 to 0 mm/s, covering a remaining distance of 3.7 mm at an average speed of 9.7 mm/s.

The reason why the oil droplets exhibit a higher initial self-propulsion speed followed by a gradual deceleration may lie in the intrinsic wettability difference between Regions A and B (i.e., Region A is oleophilic while Region B is oleophobic). Initially, when the oil droplets migrate from Region B to Region A, the combined effect of the wetting gradient force and the force component F at the A-B boundary exceeds the sum of the opposing forces, including the surface friction of the specimen, contact angle hysteresis, and the oil–water drag force (i.e., the viscous frictional resistance between the oil droplets and the surrounding aqueous phase). Consequently, the oil droplets accelerate with a certain acceleration. In the subsequent self-propulsion stage, the effective wetting gradient gradually diminishes. For the experiment with Region A oriented upward, the resultant force acting on the oil droplets along the motion direction is dominated by the motion-promoting force component F and the motion-inhibiting resistance forces. At this stage, the resultant force is less than the total resistance exerted by the specimen surface and the aqueous phase on the oil droplets. For the experiment with Region A oriented downward, the oil droplets are subjected to the combined effect of the motion-inhibiting force component E and other resistance forces. Under these respective force conditions, the oil droplets undergo deceleration throughout the latter stage in both experimental configurations. Both experimental results confirm that the fabricated wetting gradient surface can achieve directional self-propulsion of oil droplets. Notably, the configuration with the oleophilic Region A oriented upward and the oleophobic Region B oriented downward exhibits superior performance, which can enhance the oil–water separation effect and accelerate the oil–water separation rate. Therefore, subsequent single-factor experiments were conducted based on this optimal configuration.

Effect of Single Factor on Self-Motion of Oil Droplets

Given that the core objective of this study is to enhance the practical oil–water separation efficiency of inclined plates in oil–water separators, focus should be placed on key variables directly associated with on-site processes. On the one hand, the installation inclination angle of the inclined plate is a core structural parameter of the oil–water separator, which directly affects the gravity-dominated movement trend of oil droplets. On the other hand, oil droplet size and oil droplet density are inherent physical properties of oilfield produced water, determining the natural floating characteristics of oil droplets. Both are closely related to the actual separation efficiency, with the contact angle gradient value fixed as a constant condition. Literature indicates that the influence of viscosity on the self-movement of droplets is weaker than that of density, followed by surface tension. The regulatory effects of these two factors (viscosity and surface tension) on oil–water separation efficiency are relatively insignificant; thus, only density among the three is considered as the key factor in this study. Therefore, this section investigates the influence laws of inclined plate inclination angle, oil droplet size, and oil droplet density on the self-movement distance and velocity of oil droplets through single-factor experiments. , (1)The selection of key experimental parameters must not only align with the actual industrial applications in oilfields but also meet the research requirements for capturing underlying laws. Specifically, the selection of the wetting gradient surface’s inclination angle should conform to the equilibrium separation efficiency of inclined plates in oil separation tanks and the routine installation angle range of equipment. Furthermore, it should cover the transition range from gravity-dominated to gradient force-gravity coordinated dominance to accurately capture the regulatory effect of inclination angle on oil droplet movement. Thus, the inclination angle range was determined to be 30°–60°.(2)The selection of oil droplet volume focuses on the core working range of oil droplet sizes targeted by industrial inclined plate oil separation tanks, aiming to directly reflect the actual treatment efficiency of the equipment. Since the industrial design treatment range for oil droplet volume is 2 μL–10 μL, the oil droplet volume range was determined to be 4 μL–9 μL as the core working interval.(3)The selection of oil droplet density should cover the density range of the most common oils in real-world industrial oily wastewater conditions and match the on-site physical properties of the oil phase in oily wastewater. Therefore, the oil droplet density range was determined to be 0.72–0.87 g/cm3, which encompasses the majority of common oils in industrial oily wastewater.

Experiments were conducted with the inclination angle of the steel sheet surface set to 30°, 37.5°, 45°, 52.5°, and 60°, respectively. For each inclination angle, the self-propulsion distance of the oil droplets was measured, and the corresponding self-propulsion velocity was calculated. The results are presented in Figure .

Effect of inclination angle of steel sheet surface on self-moving distance and velocity.

As clearly illustrated in Figure , the self-propulsion distance and velocity of oil droplets increase monotonically with an increase in the inclination angle of the steel sheet surface, both reaching their peak values when the inclination angle is 60° (distance: 9.2 mm; velocity: 43.2 mm/s). The core mechanism underlying this phenomenon is that an increase in the inclination angle (α) directly leads to a gradual increase in the resultant force F acting on the oil droplets along the steel sheet surface during their upward movement. This resultant force F is defined as the difference between the gravitational component and the buoyancy component (i.e., the effective upward driving force along the steel sheet surface). Notably, this resultant force synergizes with the wetting gradient force of the steel sheet surface, and the combined resultant force of the two continues to increase, ultimately promoting the synchronous increase in the self-propulsion distance and velocity of the oil droplets. Further data analysis reveals that when the inclination angle increases from 30° to 45°, the self-propulsion distance increases from 5.2 mm to 8.0 mm, corresponding to a relative increase of 53.8%. In contrast, when the inclination angle increases from 45° to 60°, the distance only increases from 8.0 mm to 9.2 mm, with the relative increase reducing to 15%a difference of 38.8 percentage points between the two intervals. The fundamental reason for this difference in the growth rate lies in the gradually decreasing increment of sinα within the range of α ∈ [30°, 60°]. Specifically, sinα increases by approximately 41.4% when α increases from 30° to 45°, while the increment of sinα slows down significantly when α increases from 45° to 60°.

With the inclination angle of the steel sheet surface fixed at 60° and the oil droplet density set to 0.87 g/mL, experiments were conducted using oil droplets with volumes of 4 μL, 5 μL, 6 μL, 7 μL, 8 μL, and 9 μL, respectively. The variations of the oil droplets’ self-propulsion distance and velocity with oil droplet volume are presented in Figure .

Effect of droplet size on self-motion distance and velocity.

As illustrated in Figure , the self-propulsion distance and velocity of oil droplets exhibit a trend of “first increasing and then decreasing” with the increase of oil droplet volume. Both parameters reach their peak values when the oil droplet volume is 6 μL (self-propulsion distance: 9.2 mm; velocity: 45.2 mm/s). The mechanism underlying this phenomenon can be analyzed in stages by integrating the initial static friction state, wetting gradient force, contact angle hysteresis, and effective wetting gradient. When the oil droplet volume increases from 4 to 6 μL, the self-propulsion distance increases from 8.3 mm to 9.1 mm, corresponding to a relative increase of 9.6%. The core reason is that the resultant force acting along the surface increases with the oil droplet volume. Specifically, the surface area-to-volume ratio (S/V) of 4 μL oil droplets is approximately 24% higher than that of 6 μL oil droplets. The higher surface energy density leads to greater energy consumption for maintaining interface stability. Meanwhile, small droplets have a relatively higher initial static friction and a more significant contact angle hysteresis effect, which weakens the driving efficiency of the effective wetting gradient force. When the volume increases to 6 μL, the initial static friction decreases, the contact angle hysteresis effect is alleviated, the effective wetting gradient force gradually becomes the dominant driving force, and the resultant force increases, ultimately leading to a gradual increase in the self-propulsion distance. When the oil droplet volume increases from 6 to 9 μL, the self-propulsion distance decreases from 9.1 mm to 6.5 mm, with a relative decrease of 28.6%. This is because small droplets (≤6 μL) are dominated by the effective wetting gradient force and their shape is closer to spherical, enabling the driving force to be efficiently converted into directional kinetic energy along the gradient direction. In contrast, after the initial movement, large droplets (>6 μL) exhibit a more obvious inertia effect and are prone to interface deformation during movement (e.g., front-end spreading and rear-end trailing). This deformation enhances the contact angle hysteresis effect, disperses the effective wetting gradient force such that it cannot be concentrated into directional kinetic energy, and even generates a “drag effect” due to the adhesion between the droplet tail and the steel sheet surface, further shortening the movement distance. Comprehensive analysis of the experimental data indicates that the 6 μL oil droplets achieve the optimal self-propulsion effect (in terms of both distance and velocity). Therefore, the fixed parameters determined for the subsequent single-factor experiments are the inclination angle (α) of the steel sheet surface is 60°, and the oil droplet volume is 6 μL.

Effect of droplet density on self-moving distance and velocity.

As clearly illustrated in Figure , within the oil droplet density range of 0.72–0.87 g/mL, both the self-propulsion distance and velocity exhibit a monotonic decreasing trend with increasing oil droplet density, with significant differences in the attenuation magnitude across different density intervals. Specifically, when the oil droplet density increases from 0.72 g/mL to 0.795 g/mL, the self-propulsion distance decreases from 16.8 mm to 13.2 mm, corresponding to a relative decrease of 21.4%, indicating a relatively mild attenuation. In contrast, when the oil droplet density further increases from 0.795 g/mL to 0.87 g/mL, the self-propulsion distance decreases sharply from 13.2 mm to 8.2 mm, with a relative decrease of 37.9%, and the attenuation rate is significantly accelerated. The core driving force for the upward self-propulsion of oil droplets along the surface is the effective resultant force (defined as the difference between the gravitational component and the buoyancy component). This force needs to synergize with the effective wetting gradient force to overcome the initial static friction and contact angle hysteresis. When the oil droplet density is in the range of 0.72–0.795 g/mL, the density difference between the oil droplets and the continuous aqueous phase (water) remains relatively large, providing sufficient effective resultant force. Under this condition, the initial static friction can be easily overcome, the contact angle hysteresis effect is weak, and the effective wetting gradient force can efficiently synergize with the effective resultant force to drive oil droplet movement. The slight decrease in the density difference within this interval leads to a mild weakening of the driving force.

Conclusion

Focusing on the core demand of improving the oil–water separation efficiency of inclined plates in oil removal tanks, a wetting gradient surface was successfully constructed on X80 steel (a commonly used material in oilfields) via chemical modification and mechanical polishing techniques. The effects of inclination angle, oil droplet size, and oil droplet density on the self-propulsion behavior of underwater–oil droplets, as well as the coupling effects among these three factors, were systematically investigated. Furthermore, the key mechanisms and optimal control parameters governing oil droplet self-propulsion were elucidated. This study provides core theoretical support and technical references for the engineering application of wetting gradient surfaces in the treatment of oily wastewater in oilfields.(1)The feasibility and characteristics of oil droplet self-propulsion on the wetting gradient surface of X80 steel were clarified. Oil droplets can achieve directional self-propulsion under two gradient configurations: “oleophilic surface upward and oleophobic surface downward” and “oleophobic surface upward and oleophilic surface downward”. Notably, the propulsion effect of the “oleophilic surface upward and oleophobic surface downward” configuration is superior. The oil droplet self-propulsion process exhibits a distinct two-stage “acceleration–deceleration” characteristic: the acceleration stage (accounting for 9.5% of the total propulsion time) completes 45%–70% of the total propulsion distance, with a maximum self-propulsion velocity of 350 mm·s–1; the deceleration stage (accounting for more than 90% of the total propulsion time) completes 30%–55% of the total propulsion distance, with a minimum self-propulsion velocity of 9.7 mm·s–1. This characteristic lays a critical foundation for the subsequent precise regulation of oil droplet propulsion efficiency.(2)The mechanism underlying the influence of the three-factor coupling on oil droplet self-propulsion was elucidated: The polynomial regression model established based on the response surface methodology (RSM) experiments exhibits excellent predictive accuracy, with the average relative error confined to ±5.2%. Analysis of the interaction effects reveals that the order of significance of the three factors on the oil droplet self-propulsion distance is inclination angle > oil droplet density > oil droplet volume. The model predicts the optimal parameter configuration as follows: inclination angle of 58.77°, oil droplet volume of 5.16 μL, and oil droplet density of 0.73 g/mL, under which the maximum oil droplet self-propulsion distance reaches 17.50 mm.

The practical implications of this study are as follows: The wetting gradient surface fabricated on X80 steel can directly meet the substrate requirements of inclined plates in oil removal tanks without the need for additional equipment or material replacement, thereby reducing the technical transformation cost. The clarified optimal inclination angle parameters can directly guide the optimization of the inclined plate installation process. In addition, the influence laws of oil droplet volume and density can provide references for the pretreatment of oily wastewater. Significantly improved oil–water separation efficiency can be achieved through the coordinated regulation of multiple parameters.

Future research can focus on the following directions: First, expanding to actual oilfield oily wastewater systems to explore the effects of complex impurities (e.g., suspended solids and chemical agents) on oil droplet self-propulsion. Second, conducting large-scale experiments to address the engineering challenge of achieving uniform wetting gradient preparation on large-area X80 steel surfaces. Third, combining numerical simulation methods to deeply reveal the micromechanical mechanism of oil droplet self-propulsion. Fourth, investigating the influences of oil viscosity and surface tension on self-propulsion to enhance the adaptability of wetting gradient surfaces to different types of oil–water mixtures, which will provide theoretical support for the design of more efficient surface structures.
