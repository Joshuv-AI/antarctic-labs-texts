Impact of revised severity criteria on the acceptability of PGT-M for childhood-onset cancer predisposition syndromes: a survey of genetic professionals in Japan
Authors: Hiroko Terui-Kohbata, Yuri Murai, Yusuke Ebana, Masayuki Yoshida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The scope of preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders (PGT-M) in Japan, initially limited to severe childhood-onset diseases, appears to be expanding following the 2022 revision of the Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology’s definition of “severity.” This study examines the impact of this definitional change on the acceptability of PGT-M by comparing attitudes of Japanese genetic professionals before and after the revision, focusing on three childhood-onset cancer predisposition syndromes: Li-Fraumeni syndrome (LFS), familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), and neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). A two-phase survey was conducted in 2019–2020 and 2024 among clinical genetic specialists supervisors and certified genetic counselors. The survey explored views on PGT-M acceptability, the concept of “selection of life,” awareness of the revised severity definition, and background factors influencing opinions. Among 382 respondents, LFS was most frequently judged acceptable for PGT-M, followed by FAP and NF1. Between the two phases, “unacceptable” responses declined, while “neither” increased. Those viewing PGT-M as “selection of life” were more likely to oppose it (r=-.293, p<.01). Genetic professionals in the pediatric field were more likely to consider PGT-M unacceptable (r=-.22, p<.01). These findings suggest that clinical experience, ethical perceptions, and institutional guidelines shape professional attitudes toward PGT-M. These findings have implications for genetic counseling practice and policy discussions surrounding the evolving scope of PGT-M. Ongoing dialogue and education are essential as eligibility criteria and societal values continue to evolve.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12687-026-00915-6.

Introduction

Preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) is a reproductive genetic technology in which embryos created through in vitro fertilization (IVF) are tested for hereditary genetic disorders or chromosomal abnormalities. A subset of PGT, known as PGT for monogenic disorders (PGT-M), allows couples to select embryos free from specific inherited diseases. Originally it was limited to childhood-onset, life-threatening monogenic diseases with complete penetrance. However, the scope of PGT-M has expanded over time facilitated by the revision of disease severity criteria, which now incorporate the concept of quality of life (QOL), thereby allowing broader indications.

Since its first clinical application in the UK in 1990, PGT-M has emerged as an alternative to prenatal diagnosis (PND), especially in situations where a diagnosis via PND may lead to the ethically and emotionally difficult decision of terminating a pregnancy (Handyside et al. 1990). PGT-M thus offers a way to prevent disease transmission without such consequences. With the advancement of genetic analysis technology, PGT-M has been increasingly used for adult-onset and even multifactorial diseases, including cancer predisposition syndromes (CPS). In 2003, the Ethics Task Force of the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) endorsed the acceptability of PGT-M for adult-onset and multifactorial diseases (Shenfield et al. 2003). Subsequently, regulatory authorities in several countries, such as the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority (HFEA) in the UK and government agencies in the Netherlands and the USA, have approved PGT-M for adult-onset hereditary cancers, including Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancer (HBOC) and Lynch syndrome (Ormondroyd et al. 2012; Chan et al. 2017; Gietel-Habets et al. 2018).

Among CPS, the number of PGT-M applications for highly penetrant disorders, such as neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), Von Hippel-Lindau disease, and retinoblastoma (RB), has gradually increased (Harper et al. 2012). Recent ESHRE data indicate that HBOC and NF1 are among the top conditions for which PGT-M is performed (Spinella et al. 2023). However, the appropriateness of applying PGT-M to CPS remains ethically debated due to incomplete penetrance, variability in severity and age of onset, and the presence of potential treatment or prevention options (Albujja et al. 2024).

In Japan, the use of PGT-M is regulated by the Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology (JSOG). Since its introduction in 1998, JSOG guidelines have limited the use of PGT-M to serious genetic conditions that manifest during childhood (Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology Ethics Committee 2017). At that time, “severity” was defined as a condition that occurs during childhood and significantly impairs daily functioning or threatens life. In a 2022 revision, the definition of severity was expanded by incorporating considerations of treatability, allowing for the inclusion of conditions that lack effective treatment or require advanced, highly invasive interventions (Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology 2022). A notable example that clearly illustrates the implications of this revised definition is the approval of PGT-M for RB in 2023: a representative case demonstrating the expanded scope of eligibility under the new criteria (Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology 2025).

In Japan, PGT-M has been implemented under a professional self-regulatory framework led by JSOG (Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology 2022), whereby eligibility is assessed through case-by-case review of individual clinical applications, rather than by granting approval to specific disease categories in advance. Publicly available ethics deliberation materials issued by JSOG prior to the 2022 guideline revision indicate that the working definition of disease severity emphasized conditions in which, before reaching adulthood, daily functioning is markedly impaired or survival may be threatened. During these deliberations, concerns were raised that removing the “before adulthood” qualifier could substantially broaden the scope of eligible conditions. As a result, the “childhood-onset” qualifier was retained in principle, while allowing limited flexibility through case-by-case review within the existing framework.

From a comparative regulatory perspective, national PGT-M frameworks are often described as ranging between a “medical indication model,” in which professional bodies determine eligibility based on predefined clinical criteria, and an “individual request model,” in which patient autonomy plays a more decisive role in determining access. In several European contexts, including those influenced by guidance from the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE), eligibility for PGT-M may extend to adult-onset cancer predisposition syndromes such as Lynch syndrome or hereditary breast and ovarian cancer, reflecting broader clinical discretion. In the United States, access is largely governed by professional guidelines and institutional policies rather than centralized pre-approval, resulting in comparatively flexible implementation. By contrast, Japan operates a professionally regulated, committee-based indication system under the authority of JSOG. Each case is reviewed individually, and formal approval must be obtained prior to treatment. This structure places Japan closer to a restrictive, professionally gate-kept medical indication model rather than an autonomy-driven request model.

In practice, access to PGT-M in Japan follows a multi-step procedural pathway. Couples first contact a genetic counseling unit, where clinical geneticists or certified genetic counselors assess eligibility and determine whether a case meets JSOG criteria. If considered potentially eligible, an application is prepared and submitted for central review by JSOG. Only after JSOG approval can the treating institution conduct its own clinical ethics review and proceed with treatment. Within this pathway, genetic professionals function as initial gatekeepers, and their interpretation of disease severity may influence whether cases advance to formal review (Fig. 1).

This study investigates how Japanese medical geneticists evaluate the applicability of PGT-M to childhood-onset CPS, particularly in light of the 2022 guideline revision. Based on prior literature and ESHRE data, we focused on Li-Fraumeni syndrome (LFS), FAP, and NF1, excluding RB due to its recent approval in Japan. Each condition presents distinct challenges in clinical management, prognosis, and ethical evaluation regarding reproductive choices. These differences in clinical characteristics may lead to varied professional attitudes toward the application of PGT-M.

For classic LFS, there is an 80% risk of cancer by age 70, with 22% of the cancers occurring between ages 0 and 15 years, 51% between ages 16 and 50 years (Amadou et al. 2018). The efficacy of surveillance has not been fully verified, and reported disadvantages include false positives, overdiagnosis, adverse events associated with sedation during imaging, and psychological burden (Kumamoto et al. 2021). FAP involves hundreds to thousands of colorectal and gastric polyps. By age 35 years, 95% of individuals with FAP have polyps and 93% of untreated individuals with FAP by 50 years develop colorectal cancer. Without colectomy, colorectal cancer is essentially inevitable (Half et al. 2009). The median age of colectomy was 18 years (Anele et al. 2022). NF1 is a multisystem disorder with variable manifestations ranging from mild dermatologic symptoms to malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors, intracranial tumors, and cognitive impairment (Gutmann et al. 2017), all of which can affect physical and psychosocial QOL (Domon-Archambault et al. 2018).

Through this investigation, we aim to explore Japanese geneticists’ perspectives on whether PGT-M should be considered appropriate for LFS, FAP, and NF1. The findings are expected to inform future ethical, clinical, and regulatory discussions on the evolving scope of PGT-M in Japan.

Materials and methods

Subjects

This study targeted clinical genetic specialist supervisors (CGSs) and certified genetic counselors (CGCs), who were certified by the Japan Society of Human Genetics and the Japanese Society for Genetic Counseling. In the first phase (September 2019–April 2020), invitations were sent to 293 CGSs and 251 CGCs. In the second phase (May–August 2024), 330 CGSs and 388 CGCs were invited. Participants were contacted by postal mail (CGSs) or email (CGCs) and were asked to access the online survey upon providing informed consent. Both phases of the study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Tokyo Medical and Dental University (now Institute of Science Tokyo) (1st phase: M2019-308; 2nd phase: C2023-067).

Survey design and questionnaire

Both the first and second surveys investigated the acceptability of preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders (PGT-M) in relation to three childhood-onset CPSs: LFS, NF1 and FAP. These conditions were selected based on differing clinical features, including age of onset, medical actionability, penetrance, impact on body image, and implications for QOL.

The questionnaire included items on demographic and professional characteristics (e.g., age, occupation, experience of genetic counseling for CPSs and PGT-M, and experiences of receiving inquiries about prenatal diagnosis or PGT-M for CPSs), as well as respondents’ views on the acceptability of PGT-M. Additional items asked whether respondents considered PGT-M a way of “selection of life” and providing information on PGT-M in clinical settings related to CPSs. Respondents were also asked to indicate their rationale for judging the acceptability of PGT-M in each CPS, selecting from the options reflecting clinical, ethical, and psychosocial considerations.

The following supplementary questions aimed to examine how respondents’ backgrounds, professional attributes, and prior experiences influenced their views on the acceptability of PGT-M. In the first phase, questions were included regarding respondents’ experiences with pregnancy and childbirth to capture the background characteristics. In the second phase, additional questions were introduced to assess participants’ awareness of the revised definition of “severity” published by JSOG, which expanded the eligibility criteria for PGT-M to include considerations of possibility and invasiveness of treatment. Questions were also added concerning the disease areas in which respondents were involved in genetic counseling, such as perinatal care, pediatrics, hereditary cancer, neuromuscular diseases, other adult-onset hereditary conditions, and cancer genomics.

Statistical analysis

Frequency distributions and response rates were calculated. Chi-square tests and residual analyses were subsequently performed, with Bonferroni correction applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. Correlation analyses were also conducted to assess associations between respondents’ background characteristics and their views on the acceptability of PGT-M. For the correlation analyses, responses to the item “Do you consider PGT-M to be a form of ‘selection of life’?” were assigned a score of 1 (Yes), 0 (Neither), and − 1 (No), and treated as the variable perception of life selection. Similarly, the acceptability of PGT-M for each CPS (LFS, NF1, and FAP) was assigned a score of 1 (Acceptable), 0 (Neither), and − 1 (Unacceptable), and the scores were summed up to generate a composite variable representing the total acceptability score (ranging from − 3 to + 3). Pearson correlation coefficient was used to examine associations between the perception of life selection and the total acceptability score. All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS Statistics, version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), with a significance threshold set at p <.05.

Chi-square tests were conducted to examine changes in response distributions between the first and second surveys for each CPS across the categories “Acceptable,” “Unacceptable,” and “Neither.” No statistically significant differences were found in the proportion of “Acceptable” responses for any of the three conditions: NF1 (p=.94), FAP (p=.60), and LFS (p=.11). In contrast, significant decreases in “Unacceptable” responses were observed for all three conditions (NF1, FAP, and LFS; all p<.001). Similarly, the proportion of “Neither” responses significantly increased for NF1 (p<.001) and FAP (p<.001), whereas the increase for LFS did not reach statistical significance (p=.07).

Overall trends in reasoning behind PGT-M acceptability

The most common reason cited by respondents who answered that PGT-M is “acceptable” was “respect for the client’s autonomy,” which was selected by over 70% of participants (Fig. 2). Among those who answered “unacceptable,” the most frequently cited reason was the wide range of phenotypes and severity associated with the condition (Fig. 3). For respondents who chose “neither,” the most common reason was “it depends on the client’s situation,” followed by “respect for the client’s autonomy” and “there is a wide range of phenotypes and severity” (Fig. 4).

Disease-specific differences in justifications for PGT-M acceptability

We compared the reasons for each disease. For LFS, the reasons given for “acceptable” were “there is no prevention or treatment” (FAP: 13.0%, NF1: 25.4%, LFS: 50.0%; p <.01) and “onset in childhood” (FAP: 19.6%, NF1: 18.3%, LFS: 46.8%; p <.01), which were chosen more frequently than for FAP and NF1. For FAP, the reasons cited for judging PGT-M as “acceptable” included “100% penetrance,” which was selected significantly more frequently compared to NF1 and LFS (FAP: 35.9%, NF1: 21.1%, LFS: 12.1%; p <.01). Among those who answered “unacceptable,” the reason “prevention and treatment are available” was also cited significantly more often for FAP than for the other two conditions (FAP: 65.3%, NF1: 4.0%, LFS: 0.8%; p <.01). Similarly, for those who answered “neither,” the reasons “adult onset” (FAP: 12.0%, NF1: 2.0%, LFS: 2.4%; p <.05), “prevention and treatment are available” (FAP: 21.7%, NF1: 4.0%, LFS: 7.2%; p <.05), and “nearly 100% penetrance” (FAP: 15.2%, NF1: 4.0%, LFS: 1.2%; p <.05) were all selected significantly more frequently for FAP. For NF1, among respondents who answered “neither,” the reason “there is a wide range of phenotypes and severity” was selected significantly more frequently compared to FAP and LFS (FAP: 42.4%, NF1: 59.4%, LFS: 37.3%; p <.05).

Awareness of the revised definition of severity

Awareness of the change in the definition of severity was 97.9%, with 60.1% (n = 140) indicating that they “know in detail,” and 37.8% (n = 88) indicating that they “have heard of it.” This meant that the change was widely recognized by genetic medical professionals. No significant associations were observed between the degree of awareness of the revised definition of severity and respondents’ views on the acceptability of PGT-M for the three CPSs.

Attitudes toward providing information on PGT-M

Regarding the provision of information on prenatal testing and PGT-M to clients with CPS, 56.2% (n = 131) most often answered “if there is a question, we will provide it,” followed by 24.0% (n = 56) who answered, “we have never provided information,” indicating that 80% of respondents have a passive attitude toward providing information on PGT-M. Other answers included “actively provide it for diseases that are deemed to meet the eligibility criteria” 11.6% (n = 27) and “provide it regardless of disease” 4.3% (n = 10).

Regarding the provision of information on prenatal testing and PGT-M for CPSs, respondents who selected “provide it regardless of disease” and “provide it for diseases that are deemed to meet the eligibility criteria” answered “acceptable” for PGT-M for the three CPSs significantly more frequently than those who chose other options (p<.01).

Associations between respondent characteristics and opinions on PGT-M indications

Regarding the acceptability of PGT-M for the three CPSs, no significant differences were found based on occupation, pregnancy/birth history, experience with genetic counseling on CPSs, experience with being asked questions about PGT-M, or experience with genetic counseling regarding PGT-M. Discussion

Disease characteristics and their impact on PGT-M acceptability in childhood-onset CPS

Between the first and second surveys, the number of respondents who considered PGT-M to be “unacceptable” decreased significantly for all conditions. In contrast, responses indicating “Neither” for NF1 and FAP, as well as “acceptable” for LFS, increased. More than half of the responses for LFS indicated “acceptable,” reflecting the highest level of acceptability among the three conditions. At the time of data collection, however, no formal approval for PGT-M involving LFS had been publicly reported in Japan. Subsequently, JSOG reported one approved case involving LFS in March 2026. This subsequent development is consistent with the relatively high professional acceptability observed in our survey and should be interpreted in light of Japan’s JSOG-led professional self-regulatory system.Under this framework, eligibility is not determined by pre-approved disease lists, but is instead assessed through case-by-case review of individual clinical applications. Publicly available ethics deliberation materials issued by JSOG prior to the 2022 guideline revision indicate that the working definition of disease severity emphasized conditions in which, before reaching adulthood, daily functioning is markedly impaired or survival may be threatened. During these deliberations, concerns were raised that removing the “before adulthood” qualifier could substantially broaden the scope of eligible conditions. As a result, the principle of childhood onset was retained, while limited flexibility was maintained through individualized review within the existing framework (Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology 2022).

A 2009 French study on the acceptability of PGT-M for CPSs reported that acceptability was higher for diseases characterized by high penetrance, childhood onset, multifocality, and limited options for prevention or treatment. Conversely, PGT-M was less acceptable for adult-onset, localized diseases with available preventive or treatment options and better quality of life—even when such diseases exhibited high penetrance (Julian-Reynier et al. 2009). Consistent with these findings, in the present study, respondents more frequently selected “childhood onset” and “neither curable nor preventable” as justifications for considering PGT-M in LFS than in the other two conditions. These disease characteristics likely contributed to the high acceptability of PGT-M in LFS.

In contrast, FAP and NF1 were considered less acceptable for PGT-M. The primary reasons were the availability of prevention and treatment (for FAP), adult onset (also for FAP), and variable phenotype and severity (for NF1). As with LFS, disease characteristics can shape the perceived appropriateness of PGT-M. Selumetinib was approved in 2022 as a treatment for NF1 (Suenobu et al. 2023), and therapeutic advancements may potentially lower the perceived necessity for PGT-M in such conditions. Thus, ongoing surveys will be essential to inform evidence-based clinical and policy decisions within the broader community genetics context.

Ethical tensions around “Selection of Life” and their influence on PGT-M acceptance

The questionnaire included an item asking whether respondents considered PGT-M to constitute “selection of life” (inochi no sentaku), a phrase commonly used in debates on prenatal diagnosis, particularly in relation to decisions involving termination of pregnancy. Although the term is more frequently associated with prenatal testing, it has also appeared in discussions of PGT-M in Japan and in international bioethical literature (e.g., Knoppers et al. 2006). The item was included to examine whether this ethical framing extends to PGT-M. Agreement was interpreted as reflecting heightened ethical concern regarding embryo selection.

International research suggests that when embryos are perceived as complete human lives, decisions about discarding embryos become ethically unacceptable, resulting in lower support for PGT-M (Knoppers et al. 2006). In Japan, framing embryo selection as “selection of life” has been associated with concerns about eugenics and discrimination, lending the expression a morally cautionary tone. Consistent with this perspective, respondents who characterized PGT-M as “selection of life” were less likely to consider the procedure acceptable, suggesting that perceptions of the moral status of embryos are closely linked to professional evaluations of PGT-M. A similar framing was observed in our previous study on PGT for hereditary breast and ovarian cancer, suggesting that this ethical lens is not limited to childhood-onset conditions (Terui-Kohbata et al. 2022).

Between the first and second surveys, there was a notable shift in perceptions among genetic medical professionals. The proportion who viewed PGT-M as “selection of life” decreased, while the proportion who felt “neither” increased—approaching the response distribution previously observed among non-medical participants (Terui-Kohbata et al. 2022). This shift may have been driven by the revised definition of severity in the 2022 JSOG guidelines and the increased involvement of genetic healthcare professionals in PGT-M–related practice.

In this survey, half of the respondents had direct experience with genetic counseling for PGT-M. According to the “2023 Case Review Results Report of Preimplantation Genetic Testing for Serious Genetic Diseases” published by JSOG, the number of PGT-M cases reviewed increased significantly (Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology 2025), from 29 in 2015 (Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology Ethics Committee 2017) to 72 in 2023. As the revised guidelines increase the likelihood that the range of indications for PGT-M will expand, the number of PGT-M cases is likely to increase, further expanding professional involvement in its implementation. These findings indicate that attitudes toward the ethical implications of PGT-M—particularly regarding “selection of life”—are dynamic and may continue to evolve in response to societal and institutional developments.

Institutional and professional factors shaping PGT-M implementation in Japan

Regarding the acceptability of PGT-M, factors such as whether genetic healthcare providers had prior counseling experience with CPS or PGT-M did not appear to influence their views. However, respondents involved in pediatric genetic counseling were less likely to regard PGT-M as acceptable. In addition, a significant difference was observed in the approach to providing information to clients based on the acceptability of PGT-M, suggesting that professional practice in genetic counseling may vary depending on the provider’s background.

The predominantly passive approach to providing information about prenatal testing and PGT-M may be shaped by the Japanese regulatory context. Because access requires centralized case-by-case approval by JSOG and eligibility is narrowly interpreted, professionals may hesitate to introduce PGT-M proactively when approval is uncertain. In addition, discussing PGT-M may involve sensitive value judgments in a sociocultural setting where “selection of life” carries strong ethical connotations. Japanese guidelines do not require routine proactive counseling about PGT-M, and thus information provision is left to professional discretion. Although the number of approved PGT-M cases has gradually increased in recent years according to JSOG reports, overall utilization remains limited compared with less centrally regulated systems. This centrally regulated, committee-based framework may partly explain the cautious attitudes observed among respondents, whose professional responsibilities are embedded within a restrictive institutional structure.

With the 2022 revision of the severity definition by JSOG, the review framework for PGT-M applications was also significantly restructured. Publicly available deliberation records from JSOG indicate that, even prior to the 2022 revision, PGT-M in Japan had been implemented through a multi-layered review process in which eligibility was assessed at the society level, while institutional ethics committees determined whether the procedure could be provided at individual medical facilities. Under this framework, clinical geneticists and certified genetic counselors play a key gatekeeping role by initiating consultations, interpreting disease severity, and guiding couples through the application process. Consequently, professionals’ ethical perspectives and interpretations of disease characteristics may directly influence whether and how couples gain access to PGT-M within the Japanese system. The revised PGT-M reviews involve not only the JSOG Ethics Councils but also disease-specific and medical genetics societies, increasing review steps but enabling broader, multidisciplinary input. This revised framework aims to ensure more comprehensive and ethically grounded assessments of eligibility for PGT-M. With this restructuring and new criteria for “serious” conditions, cases once ineligible may now qualify. These changes may be shifting professionals’ views closer to those of the public, especially on sensitive issues like “selection of life”. However, initial contact with clients typically occurs through clinical geneticists or genetic counselors at the applying institutions. Genetic professionals’ interpretation of disease characteristics, their individual values, and their perspectives on the “selection of life” may influence decisions about eligibility, potentially resulting in inconsistencies and client disadvantages.

To address such inconsistencies, some countries, including the United Kingdom, maintain a public list of eligible conditions for PGT-M to clarify the appropriateness of its application (Ginoza and Isasi 2020). While such standardization can mitigate variability in interpretation, Australia’s Ethical Guidelines for Assisted Reproductive Medicine warn that listing conditions may inadvertently stigmatize affected individuals by labeling diseases as inherently severe (National Health and Medical Research Council 2017). Furthermore, the regulatory approach itself may shape public attitudes toward PGT-M (Nakasato et al. 2022).

Within the current Japanese framework, genetic professionals occupy a pivotal position at the interface between institutional regulation and individual reproductive decision-making. The present findings suggest that variability in perceived acceptability of PGT-M may translate into differences in communication practices. Given that proactive information provision is not mandated by national guidelines, counseling practices may depend substantially on individual professional judgment. Ensuring that couples receive balanced, accurate, and non-directive information—regardless of providers’ personal views on the ethical status of PGT-M—remains essential to equitable access within a centrally regulated system. Continued monitoring of professional attitudes and counseling practices will therefore be important as indications and review structures evolve.

Conclusion

This study investigated Japanese genetic professionals’ perspectives on the acceptability of PGT-M for three childhood-onset cancer predisposition syndromes—LFS, FAP, and NF1—following the revision of severity criteria. The findings highlight that clinical factors such as age of onset, penetrance, and availability of treatment strongly influence professional judgments. LFS was more often deemed acceptable due to its early onset and limited therapeutic options, while FAP and NF1 raised concerns related to treatability and phenotypic variability. Ethical perceptions regarding “selection of life” and professional specialization, particularly in pediatrics, also shaped attitudes. As eligibility for PGT-M continues to expand, the development of consistent guidelines and ongoing ethical dialogue remain essential for its responsible implementation. This study underscores the need to balance clinical characteristics with ethical considerations and professional backgrounds.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, acceptability was assessed using a three-category response option (acceptable, unacceptable, neither), which may not fully capture the complexity or gradations of professional ethical judgment. More nuanced scales could provide greater insight into the strength and conditional nature of respondents’ views.

Second, although free-text responses were collected in the first survey, the majority reiterated structured response options (e.g., case-by-case consideration or “premature to decide”) and did not provide substantially novel thematic content. As a result, qualitative findings were not included in the present analysis. A more in-depth qualitative design, such as semi-structured interviews, would be better suited to exploring the nuanced ethical reasoning underlying professional attitudes, which was beyond the scope of this study.

Finally, the item on “selection of life” consisted of a single question and may not comprehensively capture the multidimensional ethical perspectives surrounding embryo selection.
