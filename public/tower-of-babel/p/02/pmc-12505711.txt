Vaccination models of delivery for refugees and migrants: a global scoping review
Authors: Fariba Aghajafari, Dorota Guzek, Huzaifa Kamal, Alyssa Ness, Laurent Wall, Caitlin McClurg, Arshya Pooladi-Darvish, Amanda M Weightman, Annalee Coakley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRefugees and migrants face inequities in healthcare and vaccination access. Diverse vaccination programs have been implemented globally among refugee and migrant populations targeting vaccine hesitancy and other barriers to vaccination. The aim of this scoping review was to provide an overview of current models of vaccination delivery of COVID-19 and other vaccines to inform best practices of vaccine delivery for refugee and migrant populations.MethodsA scoping review was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines. Eleven electronic databases, including SCOPUS, Embase, Medline, and Web of Science, as well as grey literature, were searched with keywords including: ‘COVID-19’, ‘vaccines’,’immunizations’, ‘refugees’, ‘asylum seekers’, and ‘migrants’. The search included all studies published between January 2000 and October 2023 to capture COVID-19 and other vaccine models of delivery. The main outcome was models of delivery of COVID-19 vaccines and other vaccines for refugee or migrant populations. Models of vaccination delivery were reviewed and analyzed with the 2022 World Health Organization’s Strengthening COVID-19 vaccine demand and uptake in refugees and migrants: An operational guide (2022 WHO Guide) as a guiding framework.ResultsA total of n = 11,825 unique studies were identified through database searches. Thirty-three (n = 33) studies were included in this review. Fifteen studies (n = 15) related to the COVID-19 vaccine and eighteen studies (n = 18) focused on other vaccines. Studies were mainly implemented in high-income countries with the majority from the United States (n = 17). Studies targeted various migrant groupings (i.e., migrants, immigrants, refugees, and asylum-seekers), ethnic groups, and age groups globally, including various underserved populations including migrant populations. There was general alignment with most of the 2022 WHO Guide priority action areas across both COVID-19 and other vaccine studies, pointing to ongoing understandings of the importance of administratively accessible and culturally/linguistically appropriate models of vaccine delivery for refugee and migrant populations. Increasingly dominant approaches in the COVID-19 pandemic include multipronged strategies with wide community and multisectoral collaborations to co-design strategies addressing barriers. Additionally, COVID-19 vaccination models increasingly utilized innovative social media and customization strategies, including targeted communication campaigns responsive to misinformation. Although there are increased calls for the use of data to design and evaluate interventions, notable gaps remain in the collection, use and reporting of data used to conduct interventions.ConclusionsFindings summarize vaccination models of delivery for COVID-19 and other vaccines for diverse refugee and migrant populations globally. Healthcare professionals, policy makers, and vaccination campaign planners can draw and build from strategies employed in other settings as aligned with WHO priority actions to increase equitable access to vaccines for refugee and migrant communities. Further collection and use of disaggregated and real-time data to inform and evaluate customized strategies for specific migrant groups is recommended to improve understandings of equitable vaccine delivery models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24315-6.Keywords: Refugees, Migrants, COVID-19, Vaccination, Vaccine delivery, Vaccination barriers

Background

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Canada and other high-income countries reported higher COVID-19 infection rates [1–3] and lower uptake of the COVID-19 vaccine [3–6] among refugee and migrant populations. Although the extent of under-immunization of refugee and migrant populations globally is poorly defined [7, 8], often with complex and context-dependent factors influencing vaccination [7, 8], refugees and migrants within host countries are generally under-immunized for routine vaccinations [2, 6–9] and face higher rates of Vaccine-Preventable Diseases (VPD) than non-migrants [10].

Refugees and migrants experience unique and context-dependent barriers and inequities to healthcare and vaccination access [6, 7, 11–13]. This is attributed to a complex set of intersecting risk factors and barriers [5–8, 11, 14], including difficulties navigating the healthcare system [7, 8, 15], ineligibility or fears of ineligibility [5], distrust of institutions, health systems, and authorities [5, 8, 16], vaccine hesitancy and misinformation [5, 16], discrimination and social exclusion [7, 8, 15], cultural or religious beliefs [16], and communication and language barriers [7, 15–17]. Many social and health vulnerabilities of refugees and migrants were exacerbated by COVID-19 pandemic quarantine measures, restrictions in travel, and interruptions to routine healthcare and vaccination services [6, 11, 18–22]. Vaccine hesitancy was also a barrier to COVID-19 uptake among migrants [23]. Hesitancy is attributed to misinformation, barriers to access to accurate medical information, low vaccine confidence, low perception of COVID-19 risks, and distrust of authority [2, 5].

According to the 2030 World Health Organization (WHO) Immunization Agenda, vaccine delivery should be made universal with no one left behind in receiving essential vaccinations [24]. Many jurisdictions and health authorities have identified migrants among high-risk groups that should be prioritized for receiving COVID-19 vaccines and other routine vaccinations, upholding principles of equity in worldwide vaccine delivery [8, 25–27] with a call for innovative strategies of vaccine delivery to address the diverse needs of hard-to-reach populations and migrants worldwide [7, 14, 25, 26, 28, 29]. In 2022, amidst the COVID-19 pandemic, the WHO conducted an evidence review as part of the Global Evidence Review on Health and Migration (GEHM) series, calling for the need to integrate refugees and migrants into immunization policies and delivery globally [8], with recommendations presented in the 2022 WHO Strengthening COVID-19 vaccine demand and uptake in refugees and migrants: An operational guide (2022 WHO Guide) [25].

To promote equitable vaccine access and uptake, there is an identified need for tailored, community-based strategies of vaccine delivery that address the diverse needs and barriers of migrants worldwide [5, 7, 8, 14, 26–31], with an emphasis on addressing hesitancy and tailoring approaches with the participation of communities [5]. While high level guidelines provide general direction on best practice for vaccine delivery for refugees and migrants [6, 26, 27, 32–36], there is a gap in understanding on-the-ground implementations of COVID-19 and other routine vaccination models and how they are adapted to refugee and migrant populations worldwide. There are calls for ongoing research to assess and measure the success of vaccination models as they are applied in diverse contexts and with different population groups [2, 31]. Furthermore, there is an expressed and immediate need to share resources and practical strategies to develop strategies that improve the health of refugee and migrant communities [19, 37] with innovative, context-based approaches, and lessons learnt from previous campaign efforts [6]. This review examines models of COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 vaccine delivery for refugees and migrants and draws recommendations and promising practices to advance the body of knowledge on how to tailor approaches to promote equitable vaccine uptake. The findings will guide vaccination planning efforts to effectively meet the needs of refugee and migrant populations.

Methods

A scoping review was conducted to review models of delivery of COVID-19 and other vaccines for refugee and migrant populations. The research question guiding this review was: What are the models of delivery of COVID-19 and other vaccines for refugee and migrant populations? Sub-questions included: 1) What are emerging models of vaccine delivery considering the COVID-19 pandemic?, and 2) What is the adherence of vaccination implementations to best practice guidelines? This scoping review followed the JBI approach to scoping reviews [38] along with the methodologically congruent PRISMA-ScR reporting checklist [39, 40].

Search strategy

A search strategy protocol was developed with searches conducted in multiple databases including Medline, OVID Healthstar, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, Academic Search Complete, Google scholar, Social Work Abstracts, and JSTOR within the publication years of January 2000 to May 2022. The year 2000 was chosen to reflect a new phase in global health set forth by the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) to increase partnerships, increase access to pharmaceuticals including vaccines globally, establish standards [41, 42] and to reflect changing trends in migration [43]. With the subsequently developed Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in 2015, vaccination targets continued with goals for universal health coverage and vaccine access for communicable diseases (SDG 3: Good Health and Well-Being) driven by principles of equity to increase capacity of vulnerable refugee and migrant populations [44]. An updated search was repeated in October 2023 to include June 2022 to October 2023 publications using the same search strategy protocol. The search terms included: ‘immunization’, ‘COVID-19’, ‘vaccine’, ‘refugees’, ‘asylum seekers’, ‘newcomers’, and ‘migrants’. The search strategy was designed in consultation with an experienced Librarian (CM) using the PICOS (population, intervention, comparison, outcome, study design) framework (Additional file 1, 2).

A gray literature search was also conducted to capture literature published in non-academic sources between November 2021 and May 2022, using a combination of keywords including: ‘COVID vaccine’, ‘refugees’, ‘newcomers’, and ‘migrants’. Non-governmental organization (NGO) and intergovernmental organization (IGO) search engines were also used, including the WHO COVID-19 database, Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health (CADTH) COVID-19 Evidence Portal, National Collaborating Centre for Methods and Tools COVID-19 Rapid Evidence Reviews, Health Canada, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and United Nations (UN) resources (Additional file 2). The grey literature search results were used to inform best practice guideline selection, ensure rigour in identifying all available studies, and provide contextual background. Authors also screened reference lists of review articles and those of selected primary papers to identify relevant studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they were published in English or French, target population consisted of migrants (i.e., due to variability in terms used by studies, study-defined migrant definitions were used encompassing refugees [45, 46], immigrants, asylum seekers, and/or other cross-border migrants [46]) with interventions consisting of models of delivery or interventions related to the delivery of vaccines to these target populations (Additional file 1, 3). Applying a comprehensive ‘migrant’ definition [46] and in consultation with the research librarian, the population inclusion was broad to comprehensively capture diverse migrant groups without exclusion criteria applied for length of time in host country (Additional file 3). Various migrant groupings were included to equitably capture populations that may normally be excluded from research due to challenges with access to marginalized migrant populations. Studies that specifically focused on internally displaced migrants in humanitarian crises were excluded given such migrants remain in the same linguistic and cultural contexts with less applicability to cross-border migration experiences. Included studies had interventions consisting of models of delivery for either COVID-19 or non-COVID-19 vaccinations, with outcomes aimed at increasing vaccinations among the target population. Interventions included a variety of vaccination implementations to broadly capture multiple approaches to delivering vaccines to refugee and migrant populations for a comprehensive scope including vaccination campaigns of variable sizes, outreach vaccination clinics, as well as small-scale educational sessions to promote vaccination uptake by the target population. All study designs with evaluative components were included. Studies without evaluations of vaccine implementations were excluded with aims to increase evidence-based approaches to implementation practices for improved understandings of intervention claims of success and to ensure increased study quality and rigour. Policy analyses, commentaries, editorials, reviews, as well as studies assessing antibody levels and/or travel vaccine implementations were excluded (Additional file 1). All citations were downloaded and duplicates removed utilizing the systematic review software Covidence™.

Screening and extraction

Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two authors (AP and LW; DG and LW) with conflicts reviewed by a third author (FA or AN). Full-texts were each screened by two authors for eligibility (team of reviewers: DG, HK, AN; LW; DG, LW) and if conflict arose a third reviewer assessed each study for eligibility (FA, AN, LW). The team conducted interrater processes prior to each screening stage (i.e., reviewed sample of studies independently, reviewed all conflicts as a team), and met at regular intervals during the screening process to ensure screening was based on shared understanding and application of inclusion and exclusion criteria. The PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1) was used to track screening and eligibility of studies [47, 48].

PRISMA flow diagram of included studies (n = 33)

Data extraction of included articles was conducted independently by two reviewers (DG, HK). An extraction form was prepared with the following information extracted from included articles: author, year, title, implementation country, characteristics of population(s) studied, study design, sample size, vaccine type, model of delivery (type of intervention, location, registration and documentation processes, agency overseeing intervention/implementation), results, and conclusions/lessons learnt. The data extraction form was trialed by two reviewers independently on a sample of studies, with discussion and minor edits were made to the form prior to independent extraction by two reviewers of all included studies.

Data analysis and synthesis

Extracted data was tabulated, cleaned, and reported in Tables 1 and 2. Descriptive data was synthesized using summary statistics in Table 1: Descriptive and demographic data of included studies, and extracted data relating to the models of vaccine delivery was summarized in Table 2: Summary of models of vaccine delivery of included studies. The extracted data on vaccination models of delivery was analyzed via directed content analysis [49]. This approach allowed for identification of key elements of vaccination models of delivery according to best practice recommendations with additional model components emerging from data for a comprehensive synthesis of employed models of vaccine delivery.

In directed content analysis, the 2022 WHO Guide [25] was identified from grey literature as a key resource among various applicable guidelines and frameworks [6, 26, 27, 32–35] relating to the delivery of vaccinations for refugee and migrant groups to guide analysis (Table 3, Additional file 6). The 2022 WHO Guide [25] was selected given its recent date of publication, global application, and specific focus on the operationalization of COVID-19 immunizations for refugees and migrants. With directed content analysis [49], the vaccine model of delivery data for each study was further extracted under each of the eight priority action areas of the 2022 WHO Guide [25], namely: (i) Be driven by data, (ii) Coordinate, plan and implement, (iii) Address key barriers to health and vaccination systems, (iv) Ensure effective communication and build trust, (v) Monitor and respond to social media, (vi) Ensure effective community engagement, (vii) Reinforce capacity and local solutions and, (viii) Monitor, learn and evaluate [25] (Table 3; Additional file 4). Within each priority area, content was coded independently by two researchers (DG, HK) for key model of delivery features guided by the descriptions of each priority action area [25] (Additional file 4). After initial coding was completed independently by two researchers, researchers met to review and refine operational definitions for each priority action area and to group coded items into categories. As some priority action areas contained overlapping concepts, a general coding framework of what was captured under each priority area and category was established with the research team. For example, although ‘co-design’ concepts could be captured under both priority actions ii: Coordinate, plan and implement and iv: Ensure effective communication and build trust, a research team decision was made for ‘co-design’ elements to be captured under only priority action ii to refer to the underlying approach of collaboration [25]. Upon refinement of the finalized categories within each priority area, models of delivery of all studies were reviewed again to ensure categories in all studies were systematically captured [49] (Additional File 4; Table 3). Lastly, two researchers independently assigned a qualitative level of adherence of each study according to each priority area using the categories of Yes (priority action evident in vaccination model of delivery), Somewhat (some reference to priority action, and/or only sparse information on priority action), No/Not Reported (priority action not incorporated in vaccination model of delivery, or no reference to priority action) (Table 4). In cases of conflict, a third or fourth reviewer was brought in to discuss the selected adherence category. This process allowed for an overview of adherence to best practices and for comparison between COVID-19 and other vaccine studies.

To ensure rigour in the qualitative analysis processes, trustworthiness measures were followed in the preparation, organization, and reporting phases [50, 51]. Dependability in the preparation phase was ensured through meeting minutes and an audit trail of project decisions, conducting a broad literature review with multiple academic databases and grey literature sources, and identifying key frameworks and technical guides to guide analysis [50, 51]. In the organization phase, credibility was ensured by independent extraction of study data, coding by two researchers with third/fourth reviewers brought in to resolve conflicts, summative analysis with cross-checking of all studies for identified categories including full-texts as necessary, and frequent debriefings and revision of categories with the research team [50, 51]. Transferability was ensured by including a large scope of studies along with the globally applicable WHO framework to guide analysis [50, 51]. In the reporting phase, credibility was enhanced by systematically reporting results according to each of the priority action area separately for both COVID-19 and other vaccine implementations. Lastly, confirmability was upheld by having authors specializing in research, evaluation, and public health review the final results and report [50, 51].

Study quality assessment

Study quality was assessed with the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [52] (Additional file 5) for study appraisal in systematic reviews including various study designs [52]. Each study was assessed for quality with the MMAT study type most closely matching the study design (Additional file 5). Two reviewers (DG, HK) independently assessed each study and if conflict arose, reviewers met to discuss with any unresolved conflicts discussed with the research team. Risk of bias was not assessed in this review.

Results

Included studies

A total of 18,177 articles were identified from eleven databases. After duplicates were removed, a total of n = 11,825 unique studies had titles and abstracts screened for eligibility with a total of n = 277 full-texts assessed for inclusion. After full-text screening, a total of thirty-three (n = 33) studies were included and synthesized in this review (Fig. 1). Full-texts were excluded mainly for assessing barriers to vaccination without providing vaccination (n = 62), including wrong population (n = 36), or consisting of reviews (n = 34) or editorials/perspectives (n = 30).

Descriptive and demographic study data

Type of vaccine

Approximately half of the included studies focused on the COVID-19 vaccine (n = 15) [53–67], with the remaining studies (n = 18) [68–85] relating to other vaccines. Studies focusing on non-COVID-19 vaccines included: influenza (n = 6) [70, 72, 74, 75, 81, 84], Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) (n = 2) [69, 71], Hepatitis B (HepB) (n = 2) [77, 83], multiple vaccines (two or more) (n = 5) [73, 78–80, 85] (e.g., Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR), Diptheria-tetanus-pertussis (DTP), poliomyelitis, pneumococcal, Haemophilus influenzae type B, HepB [78]), polio (n = 1) [76], cholera (n = 1) [82], and meningococcal (n = 1) [68] vaccinations. Studies with multiple vaccines included mandatory and non-mandatory vaccination schedules (Table 1).

Years of publication, location, and sample size

Years of study publication of included studies ranged from 2003 to 2023 [53–85], with COVID-19 vaccine studies published between 2021 and 2023 [53–67]. Most studies (n = 28, 84.8%) had interventions implemented in high-income countries (HICs) [53–67, 69–72, 74, 75, 77–79, 81, 83–85], with all (n = 15) COVID-19 vaccine studies [53–67] implemented in HICs according to 2023 World Bank country income groupings [86]. A total of five (n = 5) studies were implemented in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [68, 73, 76, 80, 82]. Most studies were published in the United States (USA) (n = 17) [53–60, 69–72, 74, 77, 81, 83, 84], with the remaining implemented in Australia (n = 2) [75, 79], Italy (n = 2) [61, 85], Kenya (n = 2) [73, 76], Germany (n = 2) [62, 63], United Kingdom [64], Canada [65], Cameroon [68], Thailand [82], Greece [78], Switzerland [66], USA and Canada [67], or set in multiple countries globally [80]. Sample size ranged from 20 individuals [62] in a pilot trial to 888,994 individuals targeted in a communication campaign [63].

Target population demographics

Interventions targeted the vaccination of diverse age groups including children, youth, and adults (Table 1). Most interventions included vaccination of various age ranges (n = 14) [53–56, 65, 68, 70, 71, 75, 80, 81, 83–85], while others specifically targeted adults (n = 6) [61, 62, 66, 67, 72, 74], youth and adults (n = 1)[58], children (n = 5) [69, 73, 76, 78, 82], children and youth (n = 1) [77], youth (n = 1)[79], or unspecified ages (n = 5)[57, 59, 60, 63, 64]. Sixteen interventions targeted specific ethno-cultural groups (n = 16), including Sub-Saharan African (n = 2) [68, 85], East African (n = 3) [71, 73, 76], Latino or Latinx (n = 7) [56, 58–60, 69, 72, 84], Middle Eastern (n = 1) [78], Vietnamese (n = 1) [77], Malayalam (n = 1) [67], and Karen (n = 1) [82] groups, while certain studies did not specify targeted ethnic groups (n = 3) [64, 65, 75] (Table 1). Fourteen (n = 14) did not target a particular ethno-cultural group but rather generally targeted populations from diverse ethnicities [53–55, 57, 61–63, 66, 70, 74, 79–81, 83].

Many studies broadly targeted underserved populations which included migrant groups as well as other minority communities (n = 11) [53, 54, 58, 59, 61, 64, 65, 70, 72, 75, 81]. Underserved and minority communities included populations in various precarious or hard-to-reach conditions, including rural communities [53, 54, 59], individuals in public housing [53], informal settlement dwellers [61], minority ethnic populations [53, 64, 65, 81], homeless [53, 61, 64, 70, 72], individuals with mental illness [64], elderly [72], sex workers [72], individuals in assisted living facilities [53], and substance using populations [64]. Based on study-defined terminologies used to describe migrant populations, COVID-19 vaccine interventions specifically targeted refugees (n = 1) [55], migrants (n = 6) [54, 56, 59, 62, 63, 66] (e.g., undocumented migrants [66]), immigrants (n = 4) [53, 58, 60, 67], refugees and immigrants (n = 1) [57], refugees and asylum seekers (n = 1) [64]), and migrants, refugees, and asylum-seekers (n = 1) [61] (Table 1). Among non-COVID-19 vaccine studies, interventions specifically targeted refugees (n = 4) [68, 76, 80, 82], immigrants (n = 7) [69, 71, 72, 74, 77, 81, 83], immigrants and refugees (n = 1) [70], migrants (n = 2) [73, 85], and migrants and refugees (n = 4) [75, 78, 79, 84] (Table 1).

Models of vaccine delivery

Included studies were largely heterogeneous by intervention (model of delivery) and study design, with implementations often describing multiple components within vaccine programs along with various types of evaluations. Interventions were of different scales and had multiple components including vaccine planning, communication and vaccine promotion, as well as vaccine delivery and implementation components. COVID-19 vaccine interventions included wide-scale information campaigns (n = 3) [54, 60, 63], mobile outreach programs (n = 5) [53, 59, 61, 64, 65], community vaccination campaigns (n = 5) [55–58, 66], small-scale educational sessions (n = 1) [67], and a pilot of a mobile app to assist with vaccine delivery (n = 1) [62] (Table 2). Non-COVID-19 vaccine interventions included campaigns in refugee camps [68, 76, 78, 82], outreach programs [70, 72, 75, 83], educational interventions or communication campaigns [69, 71, 84], school-based vaccination program [79], program in a clinic [74] or multiple clinics [81], a global immunization program [80], as well as other wider multi-component campaigns [73, 77, 85]. A total of five (n = 5) non-COVID-19 vaccine studies were implemented outside of high-income settings, including a two-round mass vaccination campaign in refugee camps in Cameroon [68], a targeted vaccination campaign using key migration routes of mobile populations in Kenya [73], large-scale polio campaign in refugee camps and host communities in Kenya [76], multiple countries worldwide as part of the global immunization program for USA-bound refugees [80], and a two-dose cholera vaccine campaign in a refugee camp with mobile team in the community in Thailand [82].

To capture the heterogeneity of vaccination implementation approaches, both COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 vaccine models of delivery (n = 33) were further analyzed and key categories of approaches within each WHO 2022 Guide [25] summarized (Additional file 4; Table 3). The overall adherence to each priority action area was reviewed and summarized in Table 4.

Be driven by data

COVID-19 vaccine

Studies often employed data to design interventions albeit to different extents. Baseline data on vaccination coverage and target population numbers from a variety of sources was most often used to plan interventions [53–66]. For example, Desens et al., (2023) used vaccination rates from public health data according to zip codes [54]. However, among COVID-19 vaccine implementations, many studies only used sparse readily-available public data to provide some general background to guide interventions, most notably background literature on general equity needs or census data on target population contexts [53, 57, 59, 60, 62, 63, 65, 66]. Some studies actively assessed target population barriers/needs to vaccination prior to planning and conducting interventions [54, 56, 58, 61–64] (e.g., via surveys [56, 58], online and on-the-ground discourse [54], community feedback or interviews with stakeholders and target populations [54, 62–64]). For example, Noack et al. (2022) utilized the STAR (Spiral Technology Action Research) model where the first Listen stage included listening to target groups to discover needs and interviewed medical, organizational, and administrative staff at vaccination centers to create an effective app suitable for the vaccination process [62]. Hence, there were numerous frameworks utilized with COVID-19 studies showing a reliance on frameworks to drive interventions [54, 56, 58, 59, 61, 62] with other examples including the National Academies Framework for Equitable Allocation of COVID-19 Vaccine [61], the PRECEDE (Predisposing, Reinforcing, and Enabling Constructs in Educational Diagnosis and Evaluation) model [58], CDC’s Crisis and Emergency Risk Communication (CERC) framework[56], and the HIPE (Health Information Persuasion Exploration) framework [54].

Other vaccines

Similarly, baseline data on vaccination coverage and target population numbers also underpinned most non-COVID-19 interventions [68, 71, 72, 76–80, 82, 83, 85]. There was also a wide spectrum of the level and type of vaccination coverage and target population data used to inform studies, with data often comprising of the collection of target population numbers (e.g., census conducted in a refugee population three months prior to a campaign [82], enumerating populations with venue-based and door-to-door sampling [72], surveying population to assess vaccination coverage [79, 85]), as well as passive methods utilizing previously collected and readily-available data (e.g., target population identified through refugee camp statistics [68], prior data on vaccination coverage [77]). Some studies also similarly actively assessed barriers/needs to vaccination prior to interventions [71–76, 78, 81, 83] (e.g., surveys [74, 81, 83], door-to-door assessments [72, 78], meetings with stakeholders [75], focus groups [29, 38, 47, 48, 71]. For example, in one implementation focus groups with participatory mapping using geospatial data were used to indicate movement patterns and contexts of migration [73].

Coordinate, plan, and implement

COVID-19 vaccine

Interventions often used extensive multisectoral collaborations to coordinate and implement interventions with collaborators from community, academic, social service, nonprofit, nongovernmental, and health service sectors [53–66]. Notably, collaborations included partnerships with existing local health services and ministries of health [53, 55–59, 61–66] (e.g., coordination between health authorities, regional center, and community partners [66]), or non-profit, humanitarian non-governmental, and community organizations [55–60, 63–66] (e.g., multi-stakeholder collaboration involving non-profit organizations and community leaders to plan and tailor vaccine strategy for underserved community [55]). Other examples of collaborations included governments [59, 65] (e.g., partnerships with Consulates from Latin American countries with collaboration at the governmental, academic, health, and community-based level [59]) or academic institutions [53–55, 58, 65] (e.g., community, academic, public health partnership model [58]).

Multiple studies applied participatory, co-design collaboration strategies [56, 58, 60, 63–65], with studies specifically referencing co-design [58, 64, 65] or generally referring to designing interventions with local stakeholders [63], using participatory design [60], or bidirectional communication between community and academic partners [56]. Additionally, establishing specialized working groups was a common strategy to design and coordinate interventions [54, 56, 58, 60, 61, 64–66]. This included multidisciplinary working groups [64, 66], committees [61], advisory boards [60], and a community-based COVID-19 task force and communication working group [56]. For example, a dedicated group within a regional vaccination program was formed to design and coordinate the COVID-19 Vaccine Maximizing Uptake program [64].

Other vaccines

The use of multisectoral collaborations was also a common strategy among other vaccine studies [68–70, 72, 73, 75–78, 80–83, 85]. For example, a large vaccination campaign for refugee and migrant children in Greece used extensive collaborations to coordinate and deliver vaccinations in refugee camps, reception centers, and in the community with the Ministry of Health & Migration Policy, UNHCR and partner NGOs (e.g., Red Cross, Doctors without Borders (MSF), Doctors of the World, Praksis) [78]. Concepts of co-design were also employed [72, 81] (e.g., community leaders involved as full partners [81]), community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach [72]. Additionally, working groups were similarly used to reach target outcomes [72, 77, 78, 83]. For example, in a city-wide Hepatitis B campaign in New York City targeting immigrants, community leaders, clinicians, researchers, and politicians a coalition was formed to develop the program [83].

Address key barriers to health and vaccination systems

COVID-19 vaccine

Addressing access barriers was a key consideration among the majority of studies [53–66]. Geographic barriers were often targeted [53–62, 64, 65] with a key strategy comprising of the delivery of vaccines in target community and nontraditional locations frequently accessed by target populations [53–56, 58, 60, 64, 65]. This included selecting areas in geographical proximity to the target community (e.g., proximity to free COVID-19 testing site at busy public plaza and transportation hub [58], rural areas [53], supermarkets [64]) as well as on-site venues frequently accessed by the target population (e.g., churches and schools [54], recreational arena [65], community centres or community-based organizations [56, 60]). Mobile outreach strategies were also frequently employed [53–55, 59, 61, 62, 64, 65] (e.g., mobile units [53, 59, 61], pop up clinics [64, 65], or door-to-door outreach [54, 55]), and transportation assistance to decrease geographical constraints to vaccination access [55, 57, 65].

Most studies also used language and cultural supports to increase vaccine uptake [53–60, 62–66]. Translation services and inclusion of ethnic staff/volunteers were approaches often utilized, such as on-site translators, healthcare staff speaking target languages, telephone translation services, trusted bilingual community members, and virtual translation tools [53–60, 62–66]. For example, a mobile smartphone application was used to assist with vaccine delivery in multiple languages [62], and in another study strategies included individual phone calls with language interpreters and the use of a professional interpretation service via iPads [55]. Studies also strived to adopt culturally adapted practices, often engaging staff or volunteers from the target ethno-cultural groups in vaccination efforts (for example, trusted Spanish-speaking Latinx community members [58] and cultural brokers [65] were invited to be present during vaccinations).

Strategies to reduce administrative and other barriers were also used [53, 55–58, 60, 61, 64–66]. Frequently, strategies ensuring no identification (ID)/documentation required to access vaccination services was applied as well as other simplified registration processes [55–60, 64–66] (e.g., walk-in model without need for appointment [56, 58, 65, 66], registration assistance by staff [56, 57]). Other financial and economic barriers were addressed through providing free vaccines and services [53, 55, 59–61, 65, 66] or other social support assistance [65]. For example, in a pop-up community outreach vaccine clinic community agencies provided food hampers and social support to clinic attendees [65]. Extended hours of operation or long opening hours were also utilized to broaden vaccine availability [55, 56, 58, 65, 66].

Other vaccines

Strategies to reduce geographic barriers were also commonly used in other vaccine implementations [68–70, 72–82, 84, 85]. Similarly, targeting community and trusted non-traditional locations were approaches often utilized [68–70, 73, 76, 78, 79, 81, 82, 84, 85]. For example, one model of vaccine delivery planned vaccination sites based on migration routes, temporary settlement locations, and areas potentially lacking healthcare services [73]. Directly delivering vaccinations in refugee camps [68, 76, 78, 82] and asylum seekers’ centres [85] was also a key strategy along with mobile outreach strategies were frequently utilized [70, 72, 75–77, 80, 82].

Language and cultural supports were also frequently considered [68–72, 74, 77–79, 81, 83–85], often by means of provision of translation services [70, 72, 81, 85] and culturally-competent staff or volunteers [58, 68–71, 74, 77–79, 81, 83–85] (e.g., linguistic and cultural mediators acting as intermediaries between model personnel and target groups [85]). Additionally, strategies to reduce administrative and other barriers were used [74, 77, 81, 83, 85] such as free vaccines and services [81, 83], simplified registration [85] (e.g., single ‘on arrival appointment’ [85]), as well as social support assistance [77] (e.g., incentives from charitable agencies to offer prizes for vaccinations [77]).

Ensure effective communication and build trust

COVID-19 vaccine

The dissemination of culturally-appropriate information [53–55, 57–61, 64, 66] was a common strategy with a substantial focus on the provision of multilingual information and educational materials [53, 54, 57, 59, 61, 64, 66]. Customization of messaging was present by adapting information to match target population needs and/or responding to community feedback [54, 56, 58, 60, 63, 64, 66, 67]. The use of trusted messengers was frequently utilized to disseminate vaccine information by enrolling local community members, leaders, and influencers [54–56, 58, 64, 66]. For example, local church pastors and networks of outreach workers [54] or communication leaders based on credibility in the community [56] were recruited and acted as trusted messengers.

Information was often disseminated via print media [53–55, 57, 58, 61, 64, 66] (e.g., multilingual leaflets distributed in informal settlements designed in collaboration with support organizations [61]). A variety of virtual and technological mediums were also employed [53–56, 58–60, 62–64, 66, 67]. Virtual approaches relied heavily on the use of social media [53–56, 58–60, 62–64, 66, 67] (e.g., Facebook live sessions [53, 59], webinars [54, 67], social media advertisements with links to vaccination appointment booking tools and information matched to user language [63], communication leaders sharing messages through social media platforms most widely used by their network members [56], posting of personal photos on social media by community leaders [58], and community member posts of being vaccinated and other promotion of vaccines by local influencers on social media [64, 66]). Other types of information dissemination occurred via educational events [54, 58, 59, 61] (e.g., vaccine townhalls [58], education at vaccination events [54]), as well as through other media (e.g., strategically located billboards [60], ethnic language radio shows [58–60], television channel [59, 66]).

Other vaccines

Similarly, non-COVID-19 vaccine studies relied extensively on culturally-appropriate information dissemination strategies [68, 70–72, 75, 76, 78, 79, 81–84]. Some models also customized messages according to community and partner organization feedback [77, 80, 84] as well as utilized trusted messengers [71, 73, 82, 84]. Using multilingual information and educational materials was common [72, 75, 76, 78, 79, 81, 84]. A high reliance on print and other media [72, 74–84] was utilized (e.g., radio communication [77, 83, 84], TV [83], billboards [77], educational video played in waiting room [74], and print media such as flyers and pamphlets [72, 74, 75, 77]), along with educational events [71, 82] (e.g., interactive educational sessions led by ethnic health professionals with culturally tailored materials [71]). There was also some use of virtual and technological approaches among non-COVID-19 studies [69, 84] (e.g., programs produced for the radio also broadcast live on social media [84], or text-messaging for vaccination appointment reminders [69]).

Monitor and respond to social media

COVID-19 vaccine

Active monitoring and responding to social media strategies were reported in three COVID-19 studies [54, 56, 59]. Desens et al. (2023) analyzed discourse on social media to identify inaccurate and misleading narratives around COVID-19 and the vaccine using crowd-sourced reporting [54]. The intervention used a mobile app where recruited individuals from the community (‘SQUINTers’) detected and reported online misinformation and created customized informational material (‘SQUINTSTAGRAMS’) to address misleading information [54]. Another study by Rosales et al. (2023) documented misleading information shared by users concerning COVID-19 and vaccines to dispel common misconceptions through Facebook Live, open virtual forums, and online videos (although limited reporting on specific process applied to tracking misleading information) [59]. Lohr et al. monitored and responded to narratives using community leaders'social media accounts to distribute COVID-19 messages and respond to questions, with staff systematically tracking concerns, refining messages in response to community feedback and changing public health information, and addressing of concerns at task force discussions [56]. Studies also incorporated other social media interventions [53, 58, 60, 63, 64, 66, 67] without specifically tracking social media to adapt messages (e.g., Facebook live sessions [53], virtual platforms to interact directly with participants and clarify questions [67]). Customization of social media advertisements also occurred by targeting or tailoring messaging to specific ethno-cultural groups [63, 64].

Other vaccines

Although active tracking of online content was not reported in non-COVID-19 vaccine studies, one health education campaign had informational content that was produced for the radio also shared on multiple social media platforms with live broadcasting by community health workers [84].

Ensure effective community engagement

COVID-19 vaccine

Partnering with community based organizations & community members was a common intervention strategy [53–66]. This included collaborations with non-profit organizations such as immigrant/migrant community organizations, community centers, and religious organizations [53, 55, 59, 64–66]. Partnerships were also present with community members and leaders [54–56, 59–62, 64, 65], as well as local peer vaccine ambassadors and alliances [53, 56, 58, 60, 61, 64].

Models of delivery commonly relied on community engagement strategies [53–56, 58–66]. Examples included a community-centered neighborhood ‘Motivate, Vaccinate and Activate’ strategy [58], Rothman’s community intervention approaches with community organization model [56], and community-based participatory research approach [60]. Community was involved in various capacities, including during the planning of interventions [55, 56, 58, 63] (e.g., non-profits and community leaders planned and tailored strategy [55], bidirectional communication between community members and academic partners to inform regional decision makers [56]). Communities were also involved in the promotion of interventions and social mobilization [54, 56, 58, 59, 61, 64, 66] (e.g., at least 50 community partners were contacted and played crucial role in promoting vaccination program [66], community volunteer Promotoras acted as trusted messengers [54, 59]. The use of local influencers was a specific feature that was incorporated in models of delivery [54, 56, 58, 59, 61, 64, 66]. Communities were also involved in intervention rollout [53, 54, 57–59, 64, 66] (e.g., community champions managed booking system for “pop-up” clinics in community locations [64]).

Other vaccines

Partnering with community based organizations & community members was also a common strategy in most non-COVID-19 vaccination interventions [68–70, 72, 73, 75–81, 83–85]. Community engagement strategies were often utilized [68–73, 76–78, 81, 81, 82, 84] (e.g., community mobilization with committees, including advisory, planning and outreach committees [77], community-based participatory research approach [72]). Community engagement strategies included collaborating on intervention planning [71–73, 76, 77] (e.g., pre-campaign focus groups to inform campaign design [76]), promotion and social mobilization [70, 73, 76, 81, 82, 84] (e.g., social mobilization through personal communications by community healthcare workers [82], use of local influencers [73, 81]), as well as intervention rollout [70, 72, 77, 81, 82, 84].

Reinforce capacity and local solutions

COVID-19 vaccine

Interventions often reinforced local solutions and the capacity of current health systems to increase uptake. This included applying the skills, teams, and infrastructure and partnering with local health services partners [53–61, 63–67]. This included partnering with schools of medicine or nursing [53, 58], non-profit healthcare associations [54, 61], vaccine task force teams [53, 56, 58, 64], local public health services [56, 57, 59, 63–65], and trusted local health professionals [54, 55, 60, 64, 65, 67]. For example, in some models of delivery, local health professionals included community health workers [54, 55, 60] or internationally trained medical doctors [65]. Partnerships with existing health services often granted access to existing infrastructure including sharing health informatics systems [55, 58] and health facilities [55–57, 64] (e.g., using a community-based primary care clinic in a diverse community [57]). Collaborative models with local health services also included assistance with service coordination [55, 64]. Examples included having a Registered Nurse coordinator providing follow-up for patients declining the COVID-19 vaccine [55], designing interventions with medical experts working with migrant communities [56, 63, 64, 66], and trusted healthcare professionals delivering regular focus groups and informal conversations to motivate vaccination [64].

Another common approach was improving staffing and capacity [55–59, 62, 66]. This included hiring or acquiring additional healthcare staff [57, 58, 66] (e.g., coordinators and nurses [57], staff/peer vaccine ambassadors who previously worked with COVID-19 testing events [58], hiring those with prior experience vaccinating migrant populations [66], and employing existing teams from partner health facilities [55, 56]). Training and the use of assistive tools also increased team capacity [56, 58, 59, 62] (e.g., all staff were provided regular refresher trainings to answer community member concerns [58], infectious disease experts clarified misunderstandings during weekly meetings [56], smartphone/tablet mobile application facilitated multilingual vaccine administration [62]).

Other vaccines

Similarly, non-COVID-19 interventions often reinforced local solutions by partnering with local health services partners [68–70, 72–85]. For example, in a school-based intervention students were referred to their local general practitioner for the third dose of HepB vaccination [79]. Improving staffing and capacity was also an important strategy [70, 74, 83, 84] which included recruiting students [70], and providing additional staff training [74, 83] (e.g., cultural training among healthcare and frontline workers [74]).

Monitor, learn, and evaluate

COVID-19 vaccine

Studies used a variety of monitoring and evaluation strategies. Most studies evaluated interventions at the end of a project, intervention, or vaccination campaign via summative outcome evaluations [53–66]. Studies often evaluated interventions through vaccination coverage rates using variable approaches and data sources, also frequently capturing sociodemographic data [53–59, 61, 64, 66] (e.g., changes in vaccination rates using public health data according to zip codes [54], percentage of individuals from community organization client lists receiving vaccine [55], vaccine uptake data from national vaccination information systems [55, 64]). Outcome evaluations were also completed at the conclusion of projects and employed measures other than vaccination rates [55, 56, 58–60, 62–65, 67]. These included interviews [64] (i.e., semi-structured interviews with program leaders to gather feedback), observation data [62] (i.e., user experiences of a mobile application), cost analysis [63, 64] or pre- and post-intervention or post-intervention surveys [55, 56, 58, 60, 64, 65, 67] with various measures assessed. For example, pre- and post-intervention surveys were used to evaluate the changes in vaccine confidence following an educational intervention [67], changes in knowledge and attitudes/acceptability of vaccine among community leaders and network members [56], or satisfaction with outreach clinics [64]. Post-intervention surveys were also used to gather feedback from team members or stakeholders [55, 65], assess acceptability of the intervention from participants [58], evaluate the reach of a vaccination campaign [60], or assess reasons for receiving or not receiving vaccination [55, 60]. Innovative evaluation methods for social media interventions were also used by calculating reach via online metrics [59, 60, 63] (e.g., online appointment booking click counts, reach, and estimated conversion rates with automatic tracking by Facebook [63]).

Interim progress monitoring was also conducted to track progress and make intervention adaptations as needed [54–56, 58, 59, 61, 62, 64–66]. This was often completed through meetings and feedback from the community and stakeholders [54, 56, 61, 62, 64–66] (e.g., weekly meetings to monitor project progress [61, 66], review progress by staff and share updates with regional leaders [56], review and adapt campaign messaging with stakeholders [54]). Other progress monitoring included tracking appointment bookings, intervention activities delivered and other process measures [55, 58, 59, 64–66] (e.g., routinely collected data including vaccine call frequency and declining vaccinations [55, 64]) and one study completed a pilot test prior to intervention implementation [62]. Some studies also used frameworks to guide evaluations [54, 55, 58, 65], including the RE-AIM framework for individual client-level and community-level outcomes [58], the HIPE™ framework on formative and impact evaluation [54], and Squire 2.0 guidelines for reporting the system-level work aimed at improving the quality of healthcare [55, 65].

Other vaccines

Among non-COVID-19 vaccine studies, summative outcome evaluations were used in all studies [68–85] with vaccination coverage rates most often used to evaluate interventions [68–83, 85]. Sources of vaccination data included daily compilation from campaign tally sheets [68], a provider validation protocol to verify patient vaccination [77], or accessing health information systems [83]. Studies also utilized other outcome evaluations apart from vaccination coverage [70–73, 75–77, 81, 83–85], including surveys [70–72, 75–77, 81, 83, 84], adverse events following immunizations (AEFI) [82, 85], online metrics (i.e., reach) [84], or cost [83]. Surveys assessed various measures, including pre-post surveys to assess knowledge, attitudes, and intentions for vaccination following an educational intervention [71], awareness/knowledge [77, 84], or interest in vaccination [72]. Surveys were also completed post-intervention to assess reach of the campaign [70, 83], reasons for refusing vaccination [75, 76], or reasons for choosing clinic [81].

Interim progress monitoring was also conducted to monitor and make adaptations [68, 72–74, 76, 78, 81, 83]. This was completed through meetings or feedback from community and stakeholders [68, 72, 78] (e.g., ongoing reporting of challenges and improvements [78]) or appointment bookings, intervention activities delivered, and other process measures [72–74, 76, 78, 81, 83] (e.g., outputs reported such as materials disseminated and presentations conducted [72], tracking vaccinations delivered within each intervention by each team [78], Logic model tracking various process input and output activities [81]).

Adherence to priority action areas & overall findings

COVID-19 vaccine and other vaccine studies generally adhered to guidelines set out by priority action areas of the 2022 WHO Guide [25] (Tables 3 and 4). There was high level of alignment with priority actions ii, iii, iv, vi, vii, viii (Table 4). However, there was a gap in priority action i: Be driven by data especially for COVID-19 vaccine studies where nine studies (n = 9) [53, 57, 59, 60, 62, 63, 65–67] were not clearly driven by data for designing interventions. COVID-19 vaccine studies however had reporting on the use of frameworks to design interventions with many studies calling for the need for increased data for targeted interventions (Table 2 & 3). Although priority action iii had high alignment across all studies with outreach strategies and non-traditional settings frequently used to address barriers, COVID-19 vaccine studies reported increased use of strategies to reduce administrative and other barriers including simplified registration processes, not requiring documentation/ID, free services, and social assistance support (Table 3).

Within priority action iv: Monitor and respond to social media, COVID-19 studies had considerably more reported uses of social media within interventions. Three (n = 3) COVID-19 studies [54, 56, 59] adhered to priority action iv with reference to responding to and adapting misleading vaccine public discourse, and eight (n = 8) studies [58–60, 63, 64, 66, 67] somewhat adhered by using social media in their interventions without specific tracking and responding to discourse. This shows a stark gap with only one (n = 1) non-COVID-19 study [84] referencing active social media use. COVID-19 vaccine studies also frequently highlighted relying on local influencers to disseminate vaccination messages (vi: Ensure effective community engagement) (Tables 3 and 4).

Relating to priority action viii: Monitor, learn and evaluate, all COVID-19 vaccine and other vaccine studies (n = 33) reported a certain degree of success in interventions although definitions of ‘success’ were seldom pre-defined and variably interpreted with different measures used. Most often vaccination rates and vaccination coverage were used to evaluate success among both COVID-19 [53–59, 61, 64, 66] and non-COVID-19 vaccine studies [68–83, 85]. Although vaccination rates and coverage data was most often employed as the main outcome measure to evaluate implementations, rates were generally not compared to target values. For example, in one study vaccination of a total of 7979 high risk individuals was reported without providing the total at-risk population or outcome data related to cases prevented through vaccination [64]. As various other evaluation measures were used (e.g., increase in knowledge/attitudes on vaccination benefits [71, 77, 84], reach of digital and social media campaign [84], acceptability of intervention [56, 58, 65]), benchmarks clearly stating what constituted as success were generally not provided. Also, despite improvements in pre/post survey knowledge and vaccine confidence measures post intervention delivery, it is unclear how these improvements directly impact vaccination rates.

After careful review of model of delivery components of included studies, COVID-19 and other vaccine studies included in this review incorporated many known best practices for vaccination models of delivery as well as emerging practices. Ongoing model of delivery approaches include utilizing extensive collaborations and partnerships, assessing and responding to multiple barriers to vaccination access, using mobile outreach and key target locations, reinforcing capacity of existing local health services, and ensuring culturally and linguistically appropriate services (Fig. 2). Emerging approaches during the COVID-19 pandemic included innovative uses of social media to track and communicate vaccination promotive messaging, increased reliance on frameworks to standardize implementation and evaluations, increased recognition of the need for nuanced, disaggregated and intersectional data for customized and co-designed interventions, and use of multi-pronged interventions to address multiple barriers (Fig. 2).

Summary of COVID-19 models of vaccine delivery for refugees and migrants

Study quality

Study quality was assessed using the MMAT (Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool, 2018) (Additional file 5). Studies included in this review were of low to medium scientific rigor. As studies were of various design types, study quality assessment was completed for the study design best matching the methodology of each study. Twelve studies (n = 12) were categorized as non-randomized quantitative studies [53, 60, 67, 69, 71, 72, 74, 75, 79, 82–84], ten studies (n = 10) as quantitative descriptive [56, 57, 66, 68, 70, 76, 78, 80, 81, 85], seven (n = 7) as mixed methods [55, 58, 59, 61, 62, 64, 73], two (n = 2) as qualitative [54, 65], and two (n = 2) as randomized controlled studies (RCTs) [63, 77]. However, the two RCTs [63, 77] did not meet all quality criteria. Only thirteen (n = 13) studies [53, 57, 58, 61, 63, 65, 67, 69, 71, 72, 77, 79, 84] had clear research questions. The remaining twenty (n = 20) studies [54–56, 59, 60, 62, 64, 66, 68, 70, 73–76, 78, 80–83, 85] had vague research questions with general study aims often being to describe implementations and report on lessons learnt. Among COVID-19 vaccine studies, only one study met all quality criteria within the given study type [58].

Discussion

This scoping review outlined models of delivery of COVID-19 and other vaccines implemented globally among refugee and migrant populations. Many previously known approaches to vaccine delivery for refugee and migrant populations were confirmed, with emerging vaccination models of delivery elements in the COVID-19 pandemic (Fig. 2). The 2022 WHO Guide [25] served as a useful tool to analyze the operational strategies set out to implement vaccinations to refugee and migrant groups. Generally, all studies adhered to guidelines set out by the 2022 WHO Guide [25] with key gaps in being ‘strongly driven by data’ (priority action i) as well as ‘using social media to track and target messaging’ (priority action v) (Tables 3 and 4). Given the target population of refugees and migrants, the gaps in data driven strategies may be due to the known lack of data for migrant populations, who are often not captured in standard health information systems. Although there are increased calls by WHO and organizations such as UNHCR to use social media and other digital health approaches for migrant and refugee health, these approaches especially in refugee and migrant populations in LMICs, are not yet well known [25, 87, 88]. Nonetheless, emerging models of delivery in the COVID-19 pandemic included increased use of social media in models of delivery, use of frameworks, highlighted importance of participatory co-designed interventions, customization, and multi-pronged approaches (Fig. 2). Findings are important to inform practical approaches to promote equitable access to vaccinations for refugees, migrants, and equity-deserving populations in various contexts globally [89].

Vaccination approaches of included studies in this review generally aligned with literature calling for tailored, culturally sensitive approaches that consider the specific barriers and characteristics of target populations [8, 14, 26–28, 37, 90]. Background literature similarly supports evidence-informed campaigns launched in target languages and adapted to local cultures [8, 17, 91] and improved accessibility of vaccines services (e.g., mobile vaccine clinics, combining health-services, mass vaccination campaigns, and ease in vaccination registration [8, 14, 28, 91, 92]). Another key practice similarly highlighted in literature includes leveraging community partnerships for enhanced trust with the target community, including multidisciplinary and multisectoral collaborations between public health authorities and non-governmental associations, agencies, and organizations already providing care to migrants [8, 9, 19, 26, 29, 37, 91, 93]. Multisectoral approaches with collaboration among various stakeholder groups are considered particularly promising to jointly achieve policy outcomes and address complex public health challenges [94].

This review also highlighted emerging trends during the COVID-19 pandemic. Most notably, innovative uses of social media and technologies have been increasingly implemented. Although previous literature and guidelines have called for effective and innovative communication strategies to tackle vaccine hesitancy and overcome barriers to vaccination [8, 9, 26, 37], this review presents implemented examples of social media approaches used in the models of delivery of COVID-19 vaccines. Given the high level of vaccine hesitancy during the novel COVID-19 pandemic, often driven by fears of vaccine harms [16] as well as insufficiency in reliable information to provide awareness of vaccine benefits and disease risks [8, 16, 95], it is unsurprising that innovative communication strategies were often prioritized in COVID-19 vaccine campaigns. Notably, studies employed advanced customization capabilities driven by social media platforms with tracking online discourse and using personalized content to influence target populations. The WHO [8] similarly calls for approaches to normalize vaccines with the use of social media, although specific ways in which social media should be employed is lacking. An improved understanding of how to optimize social media use and the use of built-in analytics is expected to become more important to disseminate information to targeted communities, especially in multilingual contexts of refugees and migrants. For example, literature found that migrants sparsely accessed public multilingual websites but mainly relied on Facebook for COVID-19 information, hence campaigns can dramatically increase effectiveness by using social media experts to carefully design posts along with the collaboration with managers of large social media pages [96]. With the increased reliance on social media and digital technologies to spread information, it must be recognized that there is potential for ‘digital divide’ to be more prominent among certain populations due to low access to digital technologies and various socioeconomic constraints [97]. As evaluations and interventions with social media online tracking may not require ethical approval [63], increased focus on ethical uses of social media and digital tools are also needed with approaches acceptable to communities.

Despite calls for evidence-based, data-driven customized interventions [8, 25], many studies failed to use or report on the specific data used to design interventions. Many studies only relied on limited baseline data to drive interventions, often without disaggregating data by factors such as ethnicity or migration status. The sparse use of data for designing COVID-19 vaccine implementations was unexpected given the recency of publications amidst new advancements in digital technology and artificial intelligence facilitating capturing of real-time data and standardization of health data for increased surveillance and collection of health information [97]. Nonetheless, the insufficiency of data for designing interventions is a finding reported elsewhere, where it was found upon assessment of the COVID-19 vaccine rollout in Canada that limited COVID-19 disaggregated data was available [92]. In a scoping review on immunization uptake studies in Canada, it was similarly found that few studies currently consider intersectional factors in immunization coverage research [98]. However, there are calls for increased intersectional approaches in public health with intersectionality a useful theoretical and analytical framework for understanding intersecting determinants of vaccine uptake and to perceive target population needs in holistic rather than piecemeal ways as embedded in social, political, and economic systems [98, 99]. Increased collection and use of data as facilitated by digital technologies, with bi-directional communication by target populations to express healthcare needs, could be transformational in effectively understanding and responding to target population needs. Furthermore, establishing or improving immunization information systems to capture vaccination data for refugees has been suggested in literature to increase understandings of groups inequitably served and as to pave way for customization of messaging and interventions [9, 27].

Given the large diversity of refugee and migrant populations globally, there should hence be caution in applying pan-migrant vaccination strategies. For example, in Alberta, Canada, a cross-sectional study on COVID-19 vaccine rates found that certain immigrant populations have higher vaccination rates than the local population, with disaggregated data revealing that public health interventions should focus on older immigrants, immigrants living in rural areas, and immigrants from specific continental backgrounds [100]. Other factors, such as being an undocumented migrant [21, 22, 101] and language [102] are important factors in customizing vaccination and other health promoting interventions. Hence, increased recognition of the intersectional realities of individuals by using disaggregated data and by identifying barriers to vaccine and information access with direct engagement with communities is important [92, 103]. However, given the gap in research of COVID-19 vaccine models of delivery in LMICs for COVID-19 vaccine, approaches used to deliver vaccines to refugee and migrant populations in HICs may not be applicable to LMICs. Although it is an expected finding to have lower number of studies from LMICs given the already limited resources available for health programs, additional funding to coordinate studies with evaluations may be challenging to implement especially in emergent pandemic circumstances. Many vaccination programs in LMICs are funded by international development organizations; with recent global funding cuts in international aid, including USAID (US Agency for International Development), reducing vaccination programming globally [104] and expecting to disproportionately affect hard-to-reach migrant and refugee population exacerbating inequities, there may be stark contextual differences in models of delivery most effective in different contexts given variable resource availability.

Increased co-design approaches among COVID-19 vaccine interventions also align with literature recommending the use of participatory approaches including community-based participatory research for vaccination interventions [28][105].With the multi-component nature of many interventions, improved reporting on co-design processes at various stages of the implementation cycle is recommended to strengthen sharing of approaches and lessons learnt. Although co-design is a current trend in public health programming and research, it is important to engage communities responsibly and meaningfully in co-design approaches staying away from tokenistic inclusions of communities and re-creating oppressive power dynamics [106]. Furthermore, increased evaluations and research of emerging approaches should be conducted to ensure ethical and effective equitable approaches are taken. With participatory approaches, full engagement should be sought with community central to driving acceptable evaluation methodologies of outcome evaluations according to the needs of the community.

Various frameworks were also used in the COVID-19 vaccine studies to guide planning, implementing, and evaluating interventions [54, 56, 58, 59, 61, 62]. The inclusion of frameworks to drive interventions is an important step at improving standards and creating tools for practitioners and vaccine implementers globally. However, given studies used multiple frameworks, future research should evaluate the employed frameworks to ensure vaccination interventions for refugees and migrants are guided by sound evidence. As frameworks and guidelines only provide general directions for intervention design, specific on-the-ground implementation strategies may need to be continually re-evaluated (e.g., the Canadian Medical Association Journal [CMAJ] Evidence-Based Clinical Guidelines for Immigrants and Refugees of the Canadian Collaboration for Immigrant and Refugee Health outline general recommendations based on clinical protocols often lacking specific implementation strategies as related to cultural-appropriateness and access barriers [32]). Furthermore, the evaluation frameworks used in the included studies (e.g., RE-AIM [58], HIPE [54]) may be useful tools for research and program evaluation, with further validation of these tools required and the suitability for specific contexts outlined. Developing evaluation and monitoring frameworks was similarly expressed as a priority future direction in the included study by Mitchell et al. (2021) on the global USRAP vaccination program for US-bound refugees [80]. Additionally, given the increased reliance on built-in social media evaluation tools (e.g., social media platform metrics), these tools need to be evaluated for clinical validity, effectiveness, and acceptability.

The specific targeting of refugees and migrants in vaccine models of delivery is also aligned with WHO priorities of inclusive vaccine planning to reduce disease and death burden of COVID-19 [6]. However, even in deemed as successful COVID-19 programs such as in Canada, programs ultimately failed to achieve equity goals with reference to various structural and administrative barriers in many population groups [92]. While public health authorities aimed to ensure that COVID-19 vaccines would be available to all regardless of insurance and migration status, in practice many individuals required health care and identification cards to receive COVID-19 vaccines or proof of vaccination [21]. It is therefore recognized that effective on-the-ground strategies are closely impacted by systemic health access challenges [95]. In unison with effective on-the-ground model of vaccine delivery strategies, broader attempts of strengthening primary care health services with goals of universal access to services, strengthening integration between immunization and other health programs, as well as COVAX-like initiatives for vaccine distribution are key to ensure equitable distribution of vaccines for effective models of delivery [7, 8].

However, it is recognized that the COVID-19 pandemic had extraordinary funding allocated to vaccination campaigns for vulnerable and migrant groups [107]; hence, it is unknown if models of delivery outside of emergency pandemic measures and amidst global shifts to funding allocation would have equal levels of support for this target population. In this review, the five (n = 5) studies from LMICs were large campaigns targeting large populations (e.g., approximately 320,000 targeted in global immunization program for USA-bound refugees [80], or 43,485 refugees reached in Thailand refugee camps [82]). As such, global vaccine initiatives in LMICs tied to government programs such as the US Refugee Admissions Program [80] with low-cost vaccinations may have models of delivery greatly impacted with changes in political climates. One of the 2022 WHO Guide eight action areas specifically relates to effective coordination approaches for vaccination delivery (ii: Coordinate, plan and implements) to achieve equity in vaccinations [25]. In LMICs with reliance on external funding to support public health measures for refugee and migrant non-citizen populations, the effects of cuts in funding may considerably impact the feasibility of models of multi-sectoral collaboration to effectively respond to the needs of populations due to resource constraints.

Strengths & limitations

The key strength of this study was its large scope and comprehensive search strategy. Given the inclusion of various interventions and study types, wide geographic scope, and contexts globally, the findings of this review illustrate a broad range of actionable approaches that may be considered or adapted for future novel or routine vaccine implementations for refugee and migrant populations. The selection of the 2022 WHO Guide [25] for global application to guide analysis allowed for a systematic review of vaccination practices synthesizing emerging trends of vaccine delivery for refugees and migrants and drawing attention to ongoing practices and gaps. These findings are relevant for healthcare professionals, policy makers, and service providers involved in the planning and delivery of immunization services, ensuring that the needs and barriers of migrant and refugee populations are addressed to support vaccine confidence, access, and uptake. This study adds to the field of implementation science to inform the delivery of effective vaccination approaches in real-world settings [108, 109].

A limitation of this study is the limited specificity of findings for given contexts given the large heterogeneity of studies with different interventions, population groups, and contexts, hence making direct comparison of vaccine implementations challenging. For example, as the scope of interventions ranged from small-scale education interventions to large-scale multi-component campaigns, there were challenges in ensuring consistency in data extraction given the diverse scope of interventions, as well as consistently applying the 2022 WHO Guide’s [25] broad concepts for priority action areas to the variety of interventions. Similarly, the priority action area definitions could have taken alternate operational definitions with model of delivery categories grouped variably. As the definitions of ‘migrant’ terminology uses varied between studies (e.g., different lengths of time in host country), this review did not capture the expected differences in approaches between different migrant groups (e.g., those for refugees or undocumented migrants as compared to immigrants and individuals undergoing voluntary migration). Additionally, vaccination campaigns in refugee camps in LMICs are anticipated to vary considerably from campaigns in high-income countries targeting refugee and migrant communities. Thus, the conclusions of this review provide broad rather than specific descriptions of models for vaccine delivery for refugee and migrant populations, with approaches needing to be adapted for suitability for specific contexts. Future research may consider narrowing down the scope to review specific types of interventions among a specific migrant class (e.g., vaccine promotion education interventions among refugees).

Many studies also had vague research questions and most did not meet all quality assessment criteria. The allocation of studies according to study type within the MMAT tool was also only a best approximation given most studies were program implementations without clear research methodologies. Study quality assessments for studies reporting on public health programs would be useful for studies with multiple components and reporting on programs rather than academic research projects. Although this review did not specifically appraise effectiveness of interventions, studies implementing novel models of delivery should consider increased rigour within evaluations to improve understandings of effective practices.

Another limitation of this review is the inclusion of studies from mostly HICs, most notably USA, with LMICs largely not represented in this review as only five (n = 5) non-COVID-19 vaccine studies from LMICs were included. However, there may be important differences between HIC and LMIC contexts, with analysis of differences between these contexts left out of this review. Given the paucity of refugee and migrant research in LMICs, there is a need to invest in further empirical research, co-design studies, and implementation research to better understand effective vaccine approaches in those contexts. Additionally, as examining the differences between models of delivery features between LMIC and HIC contexts was outside the scope of this review, a careful examination of the models as applied to these different contexts is warranted. For example, the reliance on communication campaigns via social media or use of different media may vary, types of vaccines delivered (e.g., polio vaccine or cholera in LMICs), number of vaccines included in vaccination campaign, or types of collaborations (e.g., LMICs may involve increased collaboration with international partners given the reliance on international aid of many vaccination programs). As there was no information provided on the use of social media in vaccination models of delivery in LMICs in this review, further understanding of the utility of digital tools for refugee and migrant populations in these contexts is recommended. Additionally, as most countries offered COVID-19 vaccines without charge and with less administrative barriers during the pandemic, models of delivery may be unique for novel vaccines in global health emergency contexts rather than emerging trends [107]. Along with the gaps in reporting within studies, it was also challenging to discern whether implementations were routine and catch-up vaccination programs in all cases. Future research may examine differences in models of delivery for routine vaccinations, catch-up vaccination programs, as well as emergency use authorization vaccinations for emerging infectious diseases, as well as suitability for specific regional and ethnic contexts globally.

Another study limitation is the exclusion of studies in languages other than French or English given the research team's capacity. Studies in other languages may have provided a wider geographical context of interventions. Additionally, studies were excluded for lacking evaluations to ensure a level of rigour in study claims of success; however, studies without evaluations may have provided valuable insights of the scope of models of vaccine delivery employed worldwide. It is recognized that many refugee and migrant-serving organizations are underfunded and may lack capacity to conduct evaluations of implemented programs that nonetheless provide ample value and impact for target populations. Further study of case studies without evaluations to gather insights from novel approaches in the field may be useful to share with planners and implementation of future campaigns. The analysis in this review of monitoring and evaluation approaches was basic to distinguish between interim monitoring progress measures and summative outcome measures; however, further analysis of the measures used for various model components and measures used would have increased knowledge on the evaluative approaches of studies. Furthermore, as not all studies claimed clearly defined measures of success, further analysis of the effectiveness of model of vaccine delivery components using implementation science principles should be evaluated to understand the effectiveness of each model component [109]. As highlighted in included studies, prioritizing program evaluations to allow for effective evidence-based strategies to improve service delivery and outcomes is key [80, 83].

The overall adherence to the 2022 WHO Guide priority action areas was only a generalized approximation of minimum adherence to each priority action area without using validated methods. If any element of the priority action area was adhered to, the category ‘Yes’ was applied despite high variability in the level to which each priority action area was applied by studies. This is a limitation as the degree to which each priority was taken up in implementations was not fully captured, especially for priority action areas such as i: Be driven by data and viii: Monitor, learn and evaluate with a high variability of extent of data and evaluative measures applied. Further studies may consider developing or using a validated approach of assigning adherence to framework components. Future studies may also consider disaggregating and separately appraising models of vaccine delivery by location of delivery (e.g., models specific to refugee camps, mobile outreach), ethnicity, country of delivery, or intervention scope and type. Although the 2022 WHO Guide was created specifically for COVID-19 vaccination delivery, it remains a valuable resource that can be further examined as a framework for analyzing and planning diverse immunization interventions, with key resources and examples outlined to operationalize uptake of vaccinations among refugee and migrant populations [25]. However, broader incorporation of other available frameworks guiding implementations for routine immunizations and in different regions would be valuable (e.g., 2019 WHO Delivery of immunization services for refugees and migrants: Technical guidance from Europe Region [27]). Lastly, a closer examination of funding models and stakeholder collaboration strategies required for mobilization and coordination of vaccination campaigns may be useful for implementors to better understand effective collaboration approaches and logistics of vaccine campaign planning for refugee and migrant populations.

Conclusions

This review presented current practices on the delivery of COVID-19 and other vaccinations to refugee and migrant populations and highlighted emerging trends of refugee vaccination models of delivery. Actionable strategies to increase vaccine uptake in refugee and migrant populations are identified for academic, policy, clinical, and community audiences. The growing reliance on social media to address vaccine hesitancy, co-design strategies, customization with the use of advanced data capabilities and frameworks, and the use of multi-component interventions, are increasingly recognized strategies in models of vaccine delivery. Building upon best practices for refugee and migrant vaccine delivery, models of vaccine delivery should continue to improve vaccine uptake by facilitating ease of access, eliminating barriers though administratively accessible and culturally tailored services, establishing strong collaborations with community, and reinforcing existing healthcare and vaccine delivery systems. The identified need to target and evaluate specific and changing population needs and implementation in diverse contexts warrants increased collection and use of accurate, up-to-date, and disaggregated data to tailor interventions to the specific needs of equity-seeking groups in shifting global contexts. Importantly, with the growing reliance on technology in healthcare, innovative models of vaccine delivery will require rapid adaptation of vaccination approaches to emerging migrant needs.
