Progression in adult patients with early-stage chronic kidney disease attending tertiary hospitals in Dodoma, Tanzania: prospective longitudinal study
Authors: Denis D Katatwire, Alfred Meremo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo determine progression in adult patients with early-stage chronic kidney disease (CKD) attending tertiary hospitals in Dodoma, Tanzania.DesignProspective longitudinal study.SettingThis study was conducted in two tertiary hospitals in Dodoma, Tanzania.ParticipantsThe population in this study was adult patients aged ≥18 years with early-stage CKD who were attending nephrology and medical outpatient clinics at Benjamin Mkapa Hospital and Dodoma Regional Referral Hospital, which are tertiary hospitals in Dodoma, Tanzania, from November 2020 to March 2022. Inclusion criteria included: patients aged ≥18 years of age, attending the clinic for at least 3 months with baseline clinical data on their files, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥30 mL/min/1.73 m2 and who gave a written informed consent. A total of 352 patients were enrolled, of whom 182 were males and 170 were females.Main outcomeThe dependent variable in this study was CKD progression, which was assessed after 12 months of follow-up.ResultsA total of 352 participants with a median age of 54 (47–59) years were enrolled; the prevalence of progression of early-stage CKD was 28.0% (97/346). For patients with CKD progression, the baseline median eGFR was 43 (41–49) mL/min/1.73 m2, urine protein creatinine ratio was 0.099 (0.025–0.158) mg/g, and haemoglobin was 11.7 (9.7–12.6) g/L. Of the patients with CKD progression, 75.3% (73/97) had diabetes mellitus, 72.2% (70/97) of the patients had hypertension, 58.8% (57/97) of the patients had significant proteinuria, and 58.8% (57/97) of the patients had anaemia. Variables associated with CKD progression after multivariate logistic regression analysis were; diabetes mellitus (OR=7.02, 95% CI 3.01 to 16.39, p=0.001), use of local herbs (OR=27.98, 95% CI 11.08 to 70.70, p=0.001), anaemia (OR=2.49, 95% CI 1.32 to 4.68, p=0.005), proteinuria (OR=7.51, 95% CI 3.49 to 16.19 p=0.001). Half, 52.5% (51/97) of the patients with CKD progression were found to have left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), 26.8% (26/97) of the patients had evidence of coronary artery disease (CAD) on non-invasive testing, and 11.3% (11/97) of the patients died during the study period.ConclusionA substantial portion of adult patients with early-stage CKD were found to have progression after 12 months of follow-up. Diabetes mellitus, proteinuria, anaemia and use of local herbal medicines were significant predictors for CKD progression. Of the patients with CKD progression, more than half of the patients were found to have LVH, almost one third of the patients had evidence of CAD on non-invasive testing, and few patients died.Keywords: Chronic Disease; EPIDEMIOLOGY; Hypertension; Diabetes Mellitus, Type 2

Introduction

The burden of chronic kidney disease (CKD) is increasing worldwide; the prevalence of CKD globally is estimated to range between 9%–13%, and CKD is associated with high morbidity and mortality.1 2 CKD and its associated cardiovascular complications represent an increasing non-communicable disease burden.3 4 CKD is slowly or rapidly progressive depending on the causative agent, resulting in end stage renal disease (ESRD) and/or premature death.5 6 CKD is highly prevalent in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) where patients present for treatment when the disease is in its advanced stages.7 8 SSA has younger populations, mostly not aware of their underlying CKD disease and risks; despite the high demand for renal replacement therapy (RRT), very few patients receive RRT due to resource limitations.9 10 In addition, the burden of CKD in patients with unknown aetiology is high in SSA as compared with other regions, leading to rapid CKD progression.11 12 The prevalence of CKD in Tanzania is estimated to be between 12.4% and 59.1% in community and hospital settings, respectively.13,15

The prevalence of CKD progression was reported to be 15.7% among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and 23% in non-diabetic CKD patients.16 The prevalence of CKD progression in a study that was conducted in the USA was 38% and in a cohort study that was conducted in the UK, it was reported to be 46.7%.17 The prevalence of CKD progression among black patients in South Africa was found to be 49.5% while in a population-based cohort in Canada, among adults with CKD, it was reported to be 51.5%.18 19 The prevalence of early stage CKD progression was reported to be 40% in a study that was conducted in the USA among patients with CKD stage 3,20 while in a study conducted across databases in the USA, UK and Japan, among early stage CKD, it was reported to be 13.7%.21

The predictors of CKD progression include proteinuria, elevated HbA1c, high systolic blood pressure and CKD stage.22 23 Hypoalbuminaemia and elevated low-density lipoprotein (LDL) have also been reported to be predictors of CKD progression.24 25 Studies have also reported younger age, female sex, African ancestry, current smoking, hypocalcaemia, hyponatraemia and heart failure as predictors of CKD progression.26 27 Anaemia induces and worsens hypoxia, leading to inflammation and eventually CKD progression and ESRD.28 29 Studies have reported that use of herbal and traditional medicines can predict acute kidney injury, CKD and CKD progression.30 31 Outcomes of CKD progression are generally poor as they are associated with increased morbidity and mortality ranging from cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), ESRD, frequent hospitalisation and poor quality of life.32 33 It is estimated that 30% to 50% of deaths among patients with CKD are due to CVDs.34 35 CKD is a significant risk for CVDs presenting as arrhythmias, coronary artery disease (CAD), left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), heart failure and sudden cardiac death.36 37

Few studies have discussed progression in adult patients with early-stage CKD; the aim of this study was to determine the prevalence of, predictors of and outcomes of progression in adult patients with early-stage CKD attending tertiary hospitals in Dodoma, Tanzania.

Materials and methods

Study design, population and settings

This was a prospective longitudinal observational study to evaluate the prevalence of, factors associated with and outcomes of CKD progression among adult patients with early-stage CKD attending the nephrology and medical outpatient (MOPD) clinics of Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH) and Dodoma Regional Referral Hospital (DRRH) between November 2020 and March 2022. The BMH and DRRH are tertiary hospitals in Dodoma with a bed capacity of 400 and 420, respectively. Both hospitals are teaching hospitals for the University of Dodoma and provide both specialised and super specialised healthcare to central and nearby zones of Tanzania. It is estimated that 40–60 patients attend MOPD and nephrology clinics per week in both hospitals. Patients were referred to these hospitals by clinicians/general practitioner from other clinics and district hospitals for specialist care. Early CKD was defined as patients in CKD stages 1, 2 and 3 as per their estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) calculations according to the CKD-EPI equation.38 39 Inclusion criteria included: patients ≥18 years of age, attending the clinic for at least 3 months, eGFR ≥30 mL/min/1.73 m2 and who gave a written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were patients with active malignancies, autoimmune diseases and active infections, including HIV/AIDS and Hepatitis B/C.

Data collection and laboratory procedures

Enrolment occurred from November 2020 to March 2021, follow-up was conducted between November 2021 and March 2022. The sample size was obtained using the Krejcie and Morgan formula40 for prospective studies.40 The minimum sample size calculation was based on the DISCOVER study, a global, 3-year, prospective, observational study where the total number of patients (N) was 5819 patients and the prevalence (p) of progression of CKD occurred in 15.7% of the patients.41 The normal standardised variables associated with the confidence level (Z) used were 1.96, and the sample error (ɛ) was 0.04. Therefore, the minimum required sample size of this study was 330 patients who had early-stage CKD. The consecutive sampling technique was used, whereby every patient who met the inclusion criteria during the study period was enrolled into the study until the last participant.42

Patients on recruitment were informed of the nature of the study; demographic and clinical data, including age, gender, history of smoking, alcohol and comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension, were extracted from the ongoing continuous clinic records. Also, face-to-face interviews at the time of enrolment were recorded in a questionnaire and vital signs measurements, including systolic and diastolic blood pressure, were conducted at first enrolment and at follow-up. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure were measured three times, and the average of the second and third measurements was used.43 Weight in kilograms and height in centimetres were measured; Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the NHS-UK BMI calculator.44 BMI categories were according to WHO, namely: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–24,9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.0 kg/m2) and obese (≥30.0 kg/m2).45

Patients accepted to provide blood and urine samples at study entry (0) and at 1-year follow-up; patients agreed to have their clinical data accessed prospectively for 12 months. During the recruitment process, the patients/relatives’ mobile numbers were obtained for communication. This enabled the acquisition of information on their status during the 12-months follow-up. Phone calls were made every 3 months for patients who didn’t turn up to the clinic, and there was no missing data. All laboratory tests were carried out at the BMH main laboratory using the Cobas 6000 analyser (Roche Diagnostics, USA). BMH laboratory is an internationally accredited laboratory by Southern African Development Community Accreditation Services, the laboratory accreditation number is MED 070. The laboratory tests performed were urinary protein creatinine ratio (uPCR), serum creatinine, serum electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1C), fasting blood glucose (FBG), full blood picture and lipid profile. Blood for serum creatinine was collected and analysed using the isotope dilution mass spectrometry traceable assay using the enzymatic creatinine method, and the eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation without using the African American correction factor.46 uPCR was calculated by dividing the amount of protein (mg/dL) in urine by the creatinine level (g/L) on a spot urine test.47 CVDs such as CAD, heart failure, stroke or peripheral artery disease. For patients with CKD progression after 12 months of follow-up, echocardiogram, ECG and brain imaging with CT scan/MRI for those presenting with features suggestive of stroke were done as per standard operating procedures. A patient was considered to be lost to follow-up after being unable to attend the 12-month follow-up despite 3 monthly phone call reminders, and any death that occurred during the 12-month follow-up was reported and recorded.

Outcome variable

The main outcome variable in this study was CKD progression, which was assessed after a 12-month follow-up. CKD progression was defined as a sustained decline in eGFR >4 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year.21 48 49

Independent variables

The independent variables consisted of individual characteristics that is, age, gender, marital status, education level, occupation, residency, history of smoking, history of alcohol use, use of local herbs, comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, CKD stages, BMI), uPCR, eGFR, HbA1c, FBG, Haemoglobin and lipid profile.

CKD was defined as the progressive reduction of kidney function with an eGFR of <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 that has persisted for a duration of 3 months or more, irrespective of the cause(s).49 Early CKD was defined as patients in CKD stages 1, 2 and 3 as per their eGFR calculations according to the CKD-EPI equation as per kidney disease: improving global outcomes guidelines.49 50 Diabetes mellitus was defined as FBG of ≥7 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥6.5.51 52 Hypertension was defined as blood pressure ≥140/90 mm Hg.53 Anaemia was defined as a Hb concentration of <13.0 g/L in males and <12.0 g/L in females.54 Dyslipidaemia (impaired lipid profile) was defined according to the best practices whereby Total cholesterol = ≥5 mmol/L (190 mg/dL), triglycerides = ≥2 mmol/L (175 mg/dL), LDL cholesterol = ≥3 mmol/L (115 mg/dL) and HDL cholesterol = ≤1 mmol/L (40 mg/dL).55 56

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in the design and conduct of the study. The findings from this study will be disseminated to study participants through face-to-face discussions, presentations and seminars.

Data management and analysis

Study data were collected and entered into an Excel spreadsheet and analysed by IBM SPSS Statistics (V.26) predictive analytics software. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic and clinical data. A histogram was used to compare the distribution of data; continuous variables have been reported as medians with IQRs, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for the non-normally distributed variables. Discrete variables have been reported as frequencies and proportions. Pearson’s χ2 test was used to test for association between variables and CKD progression. Logistic regression was performed to assess the relationship between CKD progression and associated variables using the crude and adjusted OR. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were used to estimate the association of variables and CKD progression. Directed Acyclic Graphs were used to prepare the variable list; variables that were identified in the minimal adjustment set were used for logistic regression. Variables with a p value <0.2 on univariate logistic regression models were then fitted into the multivariate logistic regression models with the addition of age and sex as adjusting variables; variables with a p value of <0.05 were considered to have a significant strength of association.

Results

A total of 658 patients attended the medical outpatient clinic during the 4-month period of enrolment; 306 patients were excluded from the study, including 102 patients who had other medical conditions. Thus, 352 participants met the criteria to be included in this study, of which 346 patients completed the 12-month follow-up. The prevalence of progression of early-stage CKD in adult patients by a sustained decline in eGFR >4 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year was 28.0% (97/346) (figure 1).

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of adults with early-stage CKD

Of the 352 patients who were enrolled into the study, 98.3% (346/352) completed the study, of whom 51.7% (179/346) were male and 71.7% (248/346) were married. Progression of early-stage CKD by a sustained decline in eGFR >4 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year occurred in 28.0% (97/346) patients over 1 year of follow-up. The median age was 56 (49–60) years for CKD progressors and 53 (46–59) years for those without CKD progression (p=0.020); the median eGFR at baseline, 43 (41–49) mL/min/1.73 m2 for CKD progressors and 51 (44–56) mL/min/1.73 m2 for those without CKD progression (p=0.001); the median uPCR was 0.099 (0.025–0.158) g/g for CKD progressors and 0.025 (0.021–0.028) g/g for those without CKD progression (p=0.001); median haemoglobin of 11.7 (9.7–12.6) g/L for CKD progressors and 12.7 (11.5–13.2) g/L for those without CKD progression (p=0.001) (table 1).

Clinical profile of the adult patients with early-stage CKD progression

Of the 97 patients who had CKD progression, 75.3% (73/97) of the patients had diabetes mellitus, and 72.2% (70/97) of the patients had hypertension. More than half, 58.8% (57/97) of the patients with CKD progressors presented with significant proteinuria as compared with 21.7% (54/249) of the patients without CKD progression. Anaemia was found in 58.8% (57/97) of the patients with CKD progression and in 34.5% (86/249) of the patients without CKD progression. Use of local herbs was found in 36.1% (35/97) of the patients with CKD progression and in 8.8% (22/249) of the patients without CKD progression. The majority, 84.5% (82/97) of patients with CKD progression were on calcium channel blockers, 71.1% (69/97) were on thiazide diuretics, and 68.0% (66/97) were on ACEs/ARBs. There was no significant difference in CKD progression associated with the use of medications (table 2).

Predictors of early-stage CKD progression

We divided the patients into two subgroups (those with CKD progression) versus (those without CKD progression). A total of 18 potential variables were identified after performing univariate logistic regression analyses. Backward elimination reduced this to 10 parameters; the variables that predicted CKD progression on multivariate logistic regression analysis included: diabetes mellitus (OR=7.02, 95% CI 3.01 to 16.39, p=<0.001), use of local herbs (OR=27.98, 95% CI 11.08 to 70.70, p=<0.001), anaemia (OR=2.49, 95% CI 1.32 to 4.68, p=0.005), proteinuria (OR=7.51, 95% CI 3.49 to 16.19 p=<0.001). Age, male sex, rural residence, current history of smoking, hypertension, CKD stage 3 and dyslipidaemia predicted CKD progression on the univariate logistic regression analysis, but the prediction was lost on multivariate logistic regression analysis (table 3).

Outcomes of adult patients with early-stage CKD progression

Of the 97 adult patients who had early stage CKD progression after 1 year of follow-up, 52.5% (51/97) of the patients were found to have LVH, 43.3% (42/97) of the patients had new onset hypertension/worsening hypertension, 41.2% (40/97) of the patients had new onset/worsening anaemia, 26.8% (26/97) of the patients had evidence of CAD on non-invasive testing, 25.8% (25/97) of the patients had new onset heart failure/worsening heart and 24.7% (24/97) of the patients developed electrolyte imbalance with hyperkalaemia being the most common. A total of 10.3% (10/97) developed end-stage kidney disease, 11.3% (11/97) patients died during the study period (table 4).

Discussion

This study evaluated the prevalence, predictors and outcomes of progression of early-stage CKD in adult patients attending tertiary hospitals in Dodoma, Tanzania. In this study, the prevalence of early-stage CKD progression in adult patients was 28.0% which is lower than the prevalence of 40% that was reported in a study that was conducted in the USA among patients with CKD stage 320 but higher than the prevalence of 13.7% that was reported in a study that was conducted across databases in the USA, UK and Japan.21 Also, lower than the prevalence of CKD progression reported from other previous studies; 51.5% from a population-based cohort in Canada, 49.5% among black patients in South Africa, 46.7% in a UK cohort study and 38% in northern California in the USA.17,19 A higher prevalence of CKD progression than in other previous studies; 15.7% in the DISCOVER Trial and 17% in German CKD clinics.41 57 The possible explanations could be differences in the study populations; in this study, only black patients were enrolled, unlike in other studies, which have reported individuals with advanced CKD stages and African ancestry have a faster rate of CKD progression.58 59 Also, in this study, we enrolled patients with early-stage CKD (CKD stages 1–3) unlike other studies that enrolled patients in CKD stage 4.

Studies have reported T2DM and hypertension as predictors of CKD and are associated with faster CKD progression.60 61 In this study, 46.9% of the patients had T2DM, 75.3% of the patients who had CKD progression had diabetes mellitus and patients with T2DM had 8.2-fold increased risk for CKD progression, similar to findings from other studies where T2DM was associated with rapid CKD progression.62 63 Hypertension was found in 58.5% of the patients, and 72.2% of the patients who had hypertension reported CKD progression. Hypertension did not predict CKD progression on multivariate logistic regression analysis. Unlike previous studies that reported hypertension to be an important predictor for CKD development and CKD progression.64 65 The difference in study findings could be due to similar prevalence rates of hypertension in both groups.

Proteinuria is strongly responsible for progressive loss of kidney function and ESRD.66 67 Almost one third (32.1%) of the study participants had proteinuria; 58.8% of the patients with CKD progression had significant proteinuria, and CKD progression was 8.2 times higher if a patient had significant proteinuria. Similar to findings from other studies that reported proteinuria is traditionally regarded as the single most important predictor for developing CKD and CKD progression.68 69 Anaemia is a significant predictor of CKD and CKD progression; correction of anaemia improves quality of life and may slow down CKD progression.28 70 At baseline, anaemia was present in 58.8% of the patients who had CKD progression, 41.2% of the patients with CKD progression were found to have new onset/worsening anaemia and anaemia had a 2.8-fold increased risk for CKD progression. Similar findings have been reported from other studies; anaemia was a predictor of CKD progression.71 72

It is estimated that in low-income and middle-income countries, the majority of the people and one-third of adults in developed countries use local herbs and medicines; local herbs and medicines can cause crystalluria or hypertension, eventually resulting in CKD, and use of local herbs and medicines predicts CKD progression.73 74 In this study, few (16.5%) of the patients reported history of using local herbs and medicine; 36.1% of the patients with CKD progression reported history of using local herbs and medicines and CKD progression was 27.8 times higher if a patient had reported history of using local herbs and medicines. These findings are similar to previous studies that reported on the use of local herbs and medicines as a predictor of developing CKD and CKD progression due to their nephrotoxicity.75 76 The prevalence of overweight/obesity was 65.3% of the patients who had CKD progression, and 69.1% had overweight/obesity. Overweight/obesity is a predictor of CKD and is associated with a more rapid CKD progression.77 78 Dyslipidaemia increases the risk of developing CKD and is a predictor of rapid CKD progression.79 80 The prevalence of dyslipidaemia was 58.1%; of the patients who had CKD progression, 78.4% had dyslipidaemia. Dyslipidaemia did not predict CKD progression on multivariate logistic regression analysis. The difference in study findings could be due to similar prevalence rates of dyslipidaemia in both groups.

Outcomes of CKD progression included worsening kidney function (ESRD), uncontrolled hypertension and a higher risk of death due to CVDs; CKD specific cardiovascular risk factors were associated with increased cardiovascular events and death.57 81 In this study, 52.5% of the patients with CKD progression developed LVH, 43.3% developed new onset hypertension/worsening hypertension, 26.8% evidence of CAD and 25.8% new onset heart failure/worsening heart failure. Similar findings have been reported from previous studies where LVH, CAD, hypertension and heart failure were found to be highly prevalent in CKD progression.82 83 Electrolyte imbalances and hyperkalaemia are life-threatening conditions that often occur in patients with CKD and CKD progression.84 85 In this study, 24.7% of the patients with CKD progression were found with electrolyte imbalances, with hyperkalaemia being the most prevalent. Similar findings have been reported from previous studies where electrolyte imbalances and hyperkalaemia were associated with CKD progression and ESRD with serious complications.86 87 In this study, 10.3% of the patients with CKD progression developed ESRD and 14 (4.0%) patients died; 11 (78.6%) of the patients who died had CKD progression. Similar findings have been found in previous studies where CKD can progress to ESRD, characterised by an increased risk of morbidity and mortality.88 89

Few studies have discussed progression in adult patients with early-stage CKD; the limitations of this study include: this study was conducted in two tertiary hospitals in Dodoma, thus restricting its generalisability to the entire country of Tanzania. Despite this limitation, this study provides data on the prevalence, predictors and outcomes of progression in adult patients with early-stage CKD.

Conclusion

A substantial portion of adult patients with early-stage CKD was found to have progression after 12 months of follow-up. Diabetes mellitus, proteinuria, anaemia and use of local herbal medicines were significant risk factors for CKD progression. Of the adult patients with early-stage CKD progression, more than half of the patients were found to have LVH, almost one-third of the patients had evidence of CAD on non-invasive testing, and a few patients died. These findings underscore the need for the identification of patients with CKD who are likely to progress and prevent further kidney damage.
