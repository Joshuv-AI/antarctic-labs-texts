Impact of minimal flow anaesthesia on anaesthetic gas consumption and carbon emission - A prospective observational study
Authors: V N Susmitha, Mary Thomas, Bhagyalakshmi Ramesh, Jagathnath Krishna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:There are increasing concerns about heat-trapping properties (global warming potential) of inhaled anaesthetic agents. We decided to assess the reduction in anaesthetic agent consumption in minimal flow anaesthesia and calculate the carbon footprint impact profile, that is, carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) and cost reduction.Methods:This single-arm observational study with modelling using comparative inferential statistics was conducted on 30 patients scheduled for oncosurgery. Data regarding consumption of inhalational agents were collected from the Draeger Primus anaesthesia workstation for 30 patients maintained with minimal flow (0.5 L/min). These data were compared with values of the same patients if they were given low-flow anaesthesia (1 L/min) calculated using a standard formula. Data analysis was done with Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 20 software. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The CO2e from use of inhalational agents for our institution was calculated using an Environmental Protection Agency greenhouse gas equivalency calculator. Annual cost analysis was done. CO2e and cost reduction if minimal flows were used was calculated.Results:Compared to low flow, consumption of sevoflurane and nitrous oxide decreased by 39.5% (P = 0.001) and 51.3% (P < 0.001), respectively, in minimal flow and CO2 e would be only 382.625 tonnes annually (50.85% less). There would be an annual cost reduction of Indian Rupee (INR) 11.9 lakhs.Conclusion:Use of minimal flow is associated with less environmental pollution and a profound difference in overall carbon footprint profile alongside a decrease in total cost of anaesthetic agents.

INTRODUCTION

Growing concerns regarding the global warming potential (GWP) and ozone-depleting properties of inhaled anaesthetic agents have necessitated a shift towards more sustainable practices. This aligns with the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) 2023 Challenge—which targets a 50% reduction in agent consumption through strategies such as low fresh gas flows.[1] This quality improvement study evaluated the feasibility of minimal flow anaesthesia. Our aim was to assess anaesthetic consumption and patient recovery characteristics under these conditions. We attempted to quantify the carbon footprint [expressed as carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e)] and analyse the potential cost reductions. By employing comparative inferential statistics, this study establishes a foundation to motivate further comprehensive research in sustainable anaesthesia.

METHODS

This prospective single-arm observational with modelling study was conducted on 30 patients undergoing elective oncosurgeries under general anaesthesia belonging to ASA class II after obtaining ethics committee clearance (vide approval number HEC no 67/23 dated 5/9/2023) at a tertiary healthcare institute. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and Good Clinical Practice guidelines were followed during the conduct of the study.

Our primary objective was to assess anaesthetic agent consumption and recovery characteristics in minimal flow anaesthesia.[2] Our secondary objective was to calculate the carbon footprint impact profile from use of inhalational agents,[3] that is, CO2e, and assess cost reduction in minimal flow.[4] The sample size was calculated based on the study by Taskin et al.[5] on mean sevoflurane consumption in minimal flow only, though in this study, minimal flows between two agents were compared with an 80% power and a level of significance of 5%. This mean sevoflurane consumption (23.6±10.9) in minimal flow was compared with that calculated value using Biro’s formula for low flow (11.5±3.8) to estimate the sample size for the present study.[6] The minimum sample required for the present study was estimated to be 7. This estimated size of 7 was not adequate to carry out statistical testing and analysis; hence, the minimal flow sample was fixed at 30 in each group.

Patients between 18 and 70 years of age and belonging to ASA class II were included in the study. Drug or alcohol abusers, pregnant or lactating women, and patients with a history of malignant hyperthermia and procedure duration exceeding 4 hours were excluded from the study.

According to routine departmental practice, after induction, the airway was secured with an endotracheal tube and ventilated in volume control mode provided by the Dräger Primus® anaesthesia system. The fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) monitor ensured that the oxygen concentration delivered to the patient remained within the desired range. Initial high flows at 4 L/min and a sevoflurane concentration of 2–2.5% were kept for 15–20 minutes [40% oxygen (O2) and 60% nitrous oxide (N2O)] to achieve a minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) of 1.0. Thereafter, the flow was maintained at 500 ml for the rest of the procedure, a sevoflurane concentration of 1–2% was titrated to maintain a MAC value of 1.0 (60% O2 and 40% N2O), and the respiratory rate was adjusted to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) 35–45 mmHg. Sevoflurane was discontinued 20 minutes before termination of the procedure, but 60% O2 and 40% N2O was continued. After administering drugs for reversal of neuromuscular blockade, patients were ventilated with 100% O2 at a flow rate of 6 L/minute and extubated.

Data regarding intraoperative vital parameters were collected from case records, and sevoflurane and N2O consumption for the study group (minimal flow) were collected from the Draeger Primus anaesthesia workstation. Draeger's algorithms displayed on the screen calculated values for consumption of agents, and these data were recorded (example shown in Figure 1).

These data were compared with values of sevoflurane and N2O utilisation of the same group of patients if they were given low-flow anaesthesia (1000 ml/min), which was calculated using a standard formula.

Recovery characteristics and vital status immediately after surgery were recorded. Time to spontaneous breathing, eye opening, extubation, verbal response, and time to reach a Modified Aldrete score of 9 or 10 were accepted as major determinants of recovery.[7]

Routine anaesthesia practice in our institution includes the use of 1 L/minute maintenance flow. The carbon footprint impact profile from the use of inhalational agents (2024–2025) for our institution was calculated using the following steps:

To determine the CO2e of the anaesthetic agents, mass (kg) was multiplied by the GWP of each gas. The mass of sevoflurane was calculated by multiplying the liquid volume consumed (ml) by its specific density (1.53). One litre of N2O weighs approximately 1.9 g. The mass in kg of N2O and sevoflurane was also entered into an Environmental Protection Agency greenhouse gas equivalency calculator to convert CO2e to equivalent petrol carbon emissions and kilometres travelled by a typical passenger vehicle.[3] The utilisation of inhalational agents using minimal flow derived from a Draeger Primus anaesthesia machine was used to calculate carbon footprint if minimal flows were used.

Hospital cost of procuring both agents was used to calculate annual costs for low flow and cost reduction if minimal flow was used. Even though this is a single-arm observational study, the comparator arm data were derived from a formula-based model (Biro). This model was compared with observed measures analytically.

Data analysis was done with Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20 software (International Business Machines Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 28.0, Armonk, New York). Quantitative variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation, and qualitative (gender, ASA physical status, and surgery) variables as percentages. Mean differences were analysed using repeated measures of analysis of variance (ANOVA) and marginal means using the Wilcoxon Signed Ranks Test. A P of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The mean age of the study population was 58.43 [standard deviation (SD) 8.15] years; the majority were females, 21 (70%), and 9 (30%) were males. Among the total 30 participants, 11 had normal body mass index (BMI), 10 were obese, 8 were overweight, and one individual was undernourished, which accounts for a mean value of 24.28 (SD 3.82). Thirteen participants underwent head and neck surgery, six patients underwent gynaecological oncosurgery, and five had breast oncosurgery done. The mean duration of surgery was 2.44 hours (SD 0.82 hours).

The mean sevoflurane Draeger Primus anaesthesia machine consumption was 11.53 ml (SD 5.32) in minimal flow and 19.07 ml (SD 4.14) in the calculated sevoflurane consumption in low flow (P = 0.001). The mean N2O value observed was 53 L (SD 29.312) in minimal flow and that calculated in low flow was 108.833 (SD 26.8419) (P < 0.001). Total sevoflurane and N2O consumed in minimal flow were 39.5% and 51.3% less in minimal flow than those calculated in low flow [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Low-flow anaesthesia was first described by Foldes et al. in 1952, where the fresh gas flow described was 1 L/min.[8] In minimal-flow anaesthesia, first described by Virtue in 1974, the fresh gas flow was further reduced to 0.5 L/min.[9] Because the system uses a carbon dioxide (CO2) absorbent, anaesthetic agents may degrade over time, potentially affecting the safety of the anaesthetic gas delivered and accumulation of undesirable trace gases. The United States Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) does not recommend fresh gas flow (FGF) rates <1 L/min when sevoflurane is used.[10] However, extensive research has found no evidence of harm in humans due to sevoflurane use with low FGF.[11] Notably, countries of the European Union never introduced minimal FGF restrictions and routinely use low FGF in practice. Furthermore, purposeful selection of CO2 absorbent can eliminate any remaining safety concerns surrounding low-flow sevoflurane.

The results obtained from our calculations were consistent with those generated by the Greenhouse Gas Equivalencies Calculator, which converts emissions or energy data into the equivalent amount of CO₂ emissions. The calculator helps to translate abstract measurements into concrete terms we can understand. Starting in 1990, the IPCC has used the GWP to allow comparisons of the global warming impacts of different gases. Specifically, the GWP is a measure of how much energy the emission of 1 tonne of gas will absorb over a given period of time, relative to the emission of 1 tonne of CO2. CO2, by definition, has a GWP of 1 regardless of the time period used because it is the gas being used as the reference. GWP over a time scale of 100 years is termed as GWP 100. Among inhaled anaesthetics, desflurane has the highest GWP100 (2540), followed by isoflurane (539), N2O (273), and sevoflurane (144). These factors are regularly updated to reflect the evolving composition of the atmosphere as atmospheric chemistry is continuously influenced by anthropogenic emissions from transportation, residential sources, industrial activities, and power generation.

The Greenhouse Gas calculator is useful in communicating the greenhouse gas reduction strategy and reduction targets aimed at reducing greenhouse gas emissions.[3] Heat-trapping properties (GWP) of inhaled anaesthetic agents are estimated to be thousands of times greater than an equivalent mass of CO2.[12] In the current study, N2O was used, though it contributes to the depletion of the ozone layer.[13] As Virtue et al.[9] were able to show, if there is no central waste gas scavenging system, workplace contamination from N2O could be reduced to 29 or 15 parts per million (ppm) for a time-weighted average concentration, purely by the consistent use of rebreathing systems with a reduction in FGF for N2O to 0.5 L/min (low-flow) or even 0.2 L/min (minimal-flow). These values not only fall below the threshold limit of 50 ppm set by the Hamburg Office for Industrial Safety in Germany but also satisfy the more stringent recommendations by the American National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), which suggest a maximum permissible concentration of 25 ppm. N2O reduces the need for volatile anaesthetics by 10 to 15%. As a result of the lower gas concentrations, the saturation of the poorly vascularised tissues is also decreased. This speeds up postoperative awakening.

Though inhaled anaesthetics are mostly vented through gas scavenging systems, they are ultimately released into the atmosphere.[1415] Each anaesthetic gas has a unique infrared absorption spectrum and atmospheric lifetime, and they act as greenhouse gases by absorbing infrared radiation, thereby preventing radiation of this energy back into space.[16]

GWP is mass-based and is compared with an equivalent mass of CO2 (GWP of CO2 = 1), and the international standard is 100 years.[17] CO2 emission of a particular agent is determined by multiplying mass by GWP, which comes to about 49 kg for 250 ml of sevoflurane used. That is, consumption of sevoflurane (one bottle = 250 ml) during anaesthesia is the CO2e of driving 196 km in an average petrol fuel car, whereas desflurane (240 ml) is equivalent to driving 3539 km.[1819] CO2e is expressed in Figure 2.

The total sevoflurane and N2O consumed in minimal flow, as observed from the Draeger Primus anaesthesia machine, were 39.5% and 51.3%, respectively, less than that calculated in low flow [Table 1]. Table 2 shows annual GWP and CO2 emissions of sevoflurane and N2O if minimal flow was used (based on observed values from Draeger Primus in minimal flow and calculated values in low flow). This signifies that if minimal flows are used, a combined total of 382.625 tonnes of CO2 will be emitted annually with the use of sevoflurane and N2O, which is 50.85% lesser than with low flows.

Annual cost analysis in the present study showed that the soda lime was changed twice weekly in the operating theatres when minimal flow was used and once weekly when low flows were utilised. Each canister in the Draeger Primus anaesthesia machine can hold 1.7 kg of soda lime absorbent. The total soda lime used in low flow is 88.4 kg, and that in minimal flow is 176.8 kg, which costs INR 45,879.6 and INR 91,759.2, respectively. The total cost of consumed N2O, sevoflurane, and soda lime in minimal flow anaesthesia and low-flow anaesthesia is INR 15,78,974 and INR 26,42,599, respectively. This shows an annual cost reduction of INR 10,63,625 lakhs. Table 2 shows calculation of cost comparison of sevoflurane and N2O in minimal and low flows.

There are several limitations of the present study, the most important being a small sample size and the absence of a true comparator group and reliance on model-based estimates for comparison. Also, this is a single-centre study with lack of comparison with carbon footprints of other institutions. Also, the effect of using air instead of N2O, which has an impact on global warming over a longer period of time, was not examined in the current study.[17]

The strength of the present study is that it utilised a Draeger Primus anaesthesia machine from which anaesthetic agent consumption data can be accurately obtained at the end of each surgery. Nevertheless, this study is a maiden attempt to create awareness on the impact of inhalational agents on global warming.

CONCLUSION

Minimal flow anaesthesia offers a sustainable, cost-effective solution for modern operating rooms. By reducing total anaesthetic consumption, this approach effectively lowers the carbon footprint of surgical procedures, mitigating both ozone depletion and greenhouse gas emissions. Beyond the environmental and economic advantages, adopting minimal flow protocols as a standard of care minimises occupational exposure, thereby enhancing safety for operating room staff.
