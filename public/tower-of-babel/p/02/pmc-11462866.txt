An ERP investigation of perceptual vs motoric iconicity in sign production
Authors: Meghan E McGarry, Katherine J Midgley, Phillip J Holcomb, Karen Emmorey
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe type of form-meaning mapping for iconic signs can vary. For perceptually-iconic signs there is a correspondence between visual features of a referent (e.g., the beak of a bird) and the form of the sign (e.g., extended thumb and index finger at the mouth for the American Sign Language (ASL) sign BIRD). For motorically-iconic signs there is a correspondence between how an object is held/manipulated and the form of the sign (e.g., the ASL sign FLUTE depicts how a flute is played). Previous studies have found that iconic signs are retrieved faster in picture-naming tasks, but type of iconicity has not been manipulated. We conducted an ERP study in which deaf signers and a control group of English speakers named pictures that targeted perceptually-iconic, motorically-iconic, or non-iconic ASL signs. For signers (unlike the control group), naming latencies varied by iconicity type: perceptually-iconic < motorically-iconic < non-iconic signs. A reduction in the N400 amplitude was only found for the perceptually-iconic signs, compared to both non-iconic and motorically-iconic signs. No modulations of N400 amplitudes were observed for the control group. We suggest that this pattern of results arises because pictures eliciting perceptually-iconic signs can more effectively prime lexical access due to greater alignment between features of the picture and the semantic and phonological features of the sign. We speculate that naming latencies are facilitated for motorically-iconic signs due to later processes (e.g., faster phonological encoding via cascading activation from semantic features). Overall, the results indicate that type of iconicity plays role in sign production when elicited by picture-naming tasks.Keywords: ASL, Sign production, Iconicity, Structured mapping, ERPs, N400

Psycholinguistic and neural evidence indicates that lexical access during language production is very similar for signed and spoken languages. For example, lexical frequency impacts both sign and word retrieval times (Carreiras et al., 2008; Emmorey et al., 2013), and overlapping left hemisphere neural regions are engaged for both sign and word production (Corina et al., 2003; Emmorey et al., 2007). However, sign languages exhibit more lexical iconicity (a resemblance between form and meaning) than spoken languages (Campbell et al., 2024; Thompson et al., 2020), and iconicity appears to have beneficial effects on production. In picture-naming tasks, iconic signs have been found to be produced more rapidly than non-iconic signs in a wide variety of sign languages (Gimeno-Martínez and Baus, 2022; McGarry et al., 2021a; Navarrete et al., 2017; Vinson et al., 2015). These reduced response latencies have been attributed to the existence of a motivated (iconic) mapping between semantic features of the referent and phonological features of the corresponding sign. Specifically, iconic signs are thought to be activated more quickly and robustly than non-iconic signs due to the semantic sensory and motoric features of the referent that are encoded in iconic mappings (Navarrete et al., 2017). This speeded activation cascades into a faster ability to exclude non-targeted signs, and therefore faster production of the correct targeted sign.

However, further investigations into this beneficial effect on sign production suggests that it is task-specific, with some tasks not providing an iconicity advantage. Gimeno-Martínez and Baus (2022) found that when asked to translate written Spanish words into Catalan Sign Language (LSC), signers did not translate words with iconic sign translations more quickly than those with non-iconic translations. Nonetheless, these same iconic LSC signs were produced more quickly than non-iconic signs in a picture-naming task. McGarry et al. (2023) replicated these results with American Sign Language (ASL) using an English-ASL translation task and a picture-naming task. Iconic signs in the picture-naming task were produced more quickly and more accurately than non-iconic signs, but there was no difference in the translation task for these same signs.

It therefore seems that the faciliatory effect of iconicity may be limited to tasks where visual features of the stimuli (pictures) have the ability to map on to the semantic features that are depicted in the phonological form of iconic signs. This mapping, which we refer to as picture-sign ‘alignment’, may facilitate sign production by reducing the amount of lexical activation that is required for the targeted sign to be selected and produced, essentially priming the mental representation of the sign. In the picture-naming study by McGarry et al. (2021a), deaf signers produced the same iconic sign in response to pictures that were aligned with the iconic form of the sign and to pictures that were less aligned with the form of the sign (see Fig. 1). When the alignment was maximized, iconic signs were retrieved more quickly. For non-iconic signs, there is little or no possibility for visual alignment between the sign and the picture.

The picture in a) shows a duck with a prominent beak that visually aligns with the iconic depiction of the duck’s beak in the ASL sign. The picture in b) does not highlight the duck’s beak and is less aligned with the iconic feature depicted by the sign DUCK.

Priming for picture-sign aligned trials has also been found in comprehension studies using a picture-sign matching task (McGarry et al., 2021b; Thompson et al., 2009; Vinson et al., 2015). These studies find that signers respond faster to trials with aligned pictures compared to less-aligned pictures when making picture-sign match decisions. However, McGarry et al. (2021b) provided evidence from Event-Related Potentials (ERPs) that alignment effects on sign comprehension may reflect a strategic use of iconicity, rather than facilitation of lexical access. Specifically, aligned picture-sign trials were associated with a reduced P3 amplitude which indexes decision processes, rather than with a reduced N400 component which indexes lexico-semantic processing. Signers may have used the strong visual alignment between the picture and the iconic sign to facilitate their match decisions. In contrast, for picture-naming tasks, iconicity has been shown to modulate the N400 amplitude (McGarry et al., 2021a; 2023), suggesting that aligned picture stimuli facilitate lexical retrieval and/or phonological encoding for iconic signs when the task requires sign production.

The temporal resolution of ERPs allows researchers to determine the stage of processing where effects of lexical variables like iconicity arise. Specifically, ERPs allow us to explore whether the behavioral facilitation found for iconic signs in picture-naming or picture-sign matching tasks is due to facilitated lexical access or to later processes. Electrophysiological investigations have often focused on the amplitude of the N400 component which is influenced by contextual variables, such as related or unrelated primes (Luck, 2014; for review see Kutas and Federmeier, 2011). Increases in N400 amplitude (greater negativity) also indicate the activation of more robust semantic networks, resulting in an increase of neural activity. For instance, concreteness is associated with behavioral facilitation (e.g., faster lexical decision times), but concrete words also elicit larger frontal N400s compared to abstract words (see Barber et al., 2013; Holcomb et al., 1999; Kounios and Holcomb, 1994; van Elk et al., 2010). Rather than suggesting that concrete words are more difficult to process, which would be inconsistent with the behavioral facilitation, this increase in N400 amplitude is argued to be a product of the stronger and denser network of semantic links caused by the activation of more sensory-motoric and visual information associated with concrete words.

McGarry et al. (2021a) recorded ERPs while deaf signers named pictures with iconic and non-iconic signs. We found reduced response latencies and increased N400 amplitude (greater negativity) for iconic signs compared to non-iconic signs. This effect was strongest over frontal sites. These results suggest that iconicity provides a benefit similar to concreteness in the picture-naming paradigm. In addition, the visually-aligned trials within the iconic condition were associated with reduced N400 amplitudes, which we interpreted as evidence of priming. Specifically, the visual alignment of the picture-prime provides information about the phonological form and semantic features of the targeted sign, and as a result, less processing is required for lexical retrieval, reducing the amplitude of the N400 component.

The LSC picture-naming ERP study by Gimeno-Martínez and Baus (2022) also found increased (more negative) amplitudes for iconic compared to non-iconic signs, though this effect occurred during a pre-N400 epoch. The direction of this effect supports the interpretation that iconic signs elicit more robust neural activation than non-iconic signs. However, a second picture-naming study by McGarry et al. (2023) found that iconic signs were associated with reduced rather than increased N400 amplitudes. This study used picture stimuli that were all visually aligned for the iconic signs, and the reduced negativity was interpreted as evidence that the picture primed lexical retrieval of iconic signs. While it’s not entirely clear why the direction of the iconicity effect differed between McGarry et al. (2023) and the earlier studies, we speculate that it could be explained by differences in the manipulation of picture-sign alignment, as to our knowledge Gimeno-Martínez and Baus did not intentionally manipulate the visual alignment of the signs and pictures.

Iconic signs do not all depict features of a referent in the same manner. Some signs depict visible features of the referent, and there is a motivated mapping between these features and the phonological features (e.g., movement, location, and handshape) of the iconic sign. Consider the ASL sign BUTTERLFLY (Fig. 2a). In the ASL sign, there is a perceptual mapping between the shape, configuration, and movement of a butterfly’s wings and the configuration, movement, and shape of the two hands in the sign. This type of iconicity has been described as ‘perceptual iconicity’, due to the mappings between the form of the sign and signers’ perceptual experience of the referent (Caselli and Pyers, 2020; Hwang et al., 2017; Ortega, 2017; Padden et al., 2013, 2015; Tolar et al., 2008). Perceptual iconicity contrasts with pantomimic or motoric iconicity, in which there is a mapping between the phonology of the sign and signers’ motoric experience with the referent. Consider the ASL sign HAMMER (Fig. 2b). Though hammers have perceptual features (e.g., they have handles that are long and straight), the sign HAMMER depicts how a person grasps and swings a hammer rather than the way the hammer looks. Thus, the iconic sign HAMMER encodes sensory-motoric rather than visual-perceptual features of the referent.

The vast majority of iconic signs (89%) included in studies by McGarry et al. (2021a, 2023) were perceptually iconic, with relatively few motorically-iconic signs. In addition, we estimated the percentage of perceptually-iconic signs (using criteria from Caselli and Pyers (2020); see Methods) that were elicited in other picture-naming studies from their stimuli lists and the citation forms given in Spreadthesign (https://www.spreadthesign.com). As in our studies, the majority of signs were perceptually iconic: British Sign Language = 78% (Vinson et al., 2015), Italian Sign Language (LIS) = 84% (Navarrete et al., 2017), and ASL = 72% (Thompson et al., 2009). Due to the different manner of mapping between perceptually-iconic and motorically-iconic signs, it is possible that different processes occur in the brain during lexical activation and selection. As noted above, perceptually-iconic signs focus more on visual experience and perceptual features, while motorically-iconic signs highlight motoric features related to how an object is held and manipulated. Because the type of encoded feature of the referent differs between these two categories of iconic signs, their ability to be facilitated by structurally-aligned pictures in a picture-naming study may also differ. Overall, perceptually-iconic signs are likely more aligned than motorically-iconic signs, because the same visually-salient features are encoded in the sign and picture. In contrast, motorically-iconic mappings hinge on the way the referent is held and used, and object pictures for these signs typically do not show the items in use. As a result, the pictures targeting motorically-iconic signs may map less successfully onto the semantic features encoded in these iconic signs.

In order to investigate the effect of different types of iconic mappings on sign retrieval the present study explored differences in behavioral facilitation and N400 amplitude modulation between perceptually-iconic and motorically-iconic signs. To do so, we designed a picture-naming study with an equal number of perceptually-iconic, motorically-iconic, and non-iconic target signs (see Fig. 2). For the perceptually-iconic signs, the signs encoded features of the appearance of the referent, and we included picture stimuli with some overlap with these features. This means that, as in McGarry et al. (2023), there was always some degree of visual alignment between the perceptually-iconic signs and the pictures. Pictures targeting motorically-iconic signs did not show the item in use to avoid the production of verbs, and therefore these pictures were generally not aligned with the motoric features of the signs (see Fig. 2b). The goal of the present study was to directly compare the retrieval of perceptually-iconic and motorically-iconic signs for objects, as well as to compare the production of both types of iconic signs to non-iconic signs. To our knowledge, no previous behavioral or electrophysiological investigation has specifically manipulated iconicity type.

We expect that perceptually-iconic signs, which made up the majority of the signs in the studies by McGarry et al. (2021a, 2023), will elicit different N400 amplitudes than non-iconic signs. While we did not manipulate the degree of alignment in the present study as in McGarry et al. (2021a, 2023), object pictures that elicit perceptually iconic signs typically exhibit at least some visual alignment with the iconic form of the sign (see both pictures for DUCK in Fig. 1). In contrast, object pictures that elicit motorically-iconic signs do not usually align with the form of the sign which depicts how the object is used or manipulated, although for some signs visual-spatial features of the object may be also depicted. In general, motorically-iconic signs have less ability to visually align with the picture. Thus, we may find a weaker iconicity effect for motorically-iconic than perceptually-iconic signs.

Finally, we included English speakers as a control group, who named the same pictures in English. This control group allows us to identify possible effects due to differences in the stimuli sets that are not attributable to the perceptual/motoric iconicity manipulation in ASL. Any differences that are found for the signing participants that do not appear for non-signing participants can be attributed to effects of iconicity. We note that the lexical and stimulus properties that impact picture-naming performance are parallel for English speakers and ASL signers, with the exception of iconicity (see Sehyr and Emmorey, 2022).

Methods

Participants

Twenty-two deaf signers were included in the analyses (10 female; mean age = 33 years, SD = 7.88 years). Participants were born into deaf signing families (N = 15) or were exposed to ASL prior to the age of six (N = 7; mean age of exposure = 2.5 years). Control participants were 22 monolingual English speakers (11 female, 10 male, 1 decline to report; mean age = 29.0 years, SD = 8.24 years). Control participants had no knowledge of ASL beyond the fingerspelled alphabet or a few signs. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and reported no history of any language, reading, or neurological disorders. Three participants in each group were left-handed (see Willems et al., 2014, for arguments to include left-handers in cognitive neuroscience research). Three signers undertook the task but were not included due to due to difficulties with the equipment. Two non-signing controls were not included due to excessive artifacts in the electrophysiological data that persisted after artifact rejection.

All participants received monetary compensation in return for participation. Informed consent was obtained in accordance with the Institutional Review Board at San Diego State University.

Materials

Stimuli consisted of digitized black on white line drawings like those in Fig. 2 (the picture stimuli and list of targeted signs can be found on OSF: https://osf.io/6bj34/). A total of 132 different drawings were presented to participants. The picture stimuli were selected from sets of pictures that we have previously used or from on-line resources. One third of the pictures (n = 44) were named with perceptually-iconic ASL signs, while another third were named with motorically-iconic ASL signs (n = 44). The remaining 44 were named with non-iconic signs.

Iconic signs were classified as perceptually-iconic or motorically-iconic using similar criteria to those used by Caselli and Pyers (2020). Signs were classified as motorically-iconic (‘pantomimic’ in Caselli and Pyers, 2020) if the signer uses their own body to demonstrate the way the referent is held or handled, and were classified as perceptually-iconic if the signer uses their body to demonstrate visual-spatial features of the referent (e.g., the shape and/or movement of the referent or of part of the referent, such as an elephant’s trunk). There was a small subset of motorically-iconic signs (n = 9) that also depicted visual-spatial features of the referent; for example, the sign CALCULATOR depicts both the shape of the object (a flat hand representing the face of the calculator) and how the object is used (e.g., tapping the face of the calculator with the other hand). We did not include any signs that expressed body parts (e.g., EYES), which Caselli and Pyers classified as pantomimic. The targeted signs were chosen to be balanced for ASL frequency and English frequency and to be balanced for iconicity (for the two iconic conditions).

Ratings of iconicity and subjective frequency were retrieved from the ASL-LEX database (Caselli et al., 2017; Sehyr et al., 2021), and the citation form of all targeted signs can be found there (http://asl-lex.org). Iconicity ratings were given by hearing non-signers, who judged ASL signs on a 7-point scale (1 = not iconic at all, and 7 = very iconic). Iconicity ratings from deaf signers were not available for all of the targeted signs, but we note that iconicity ratings for hearing non-signers and deaf signers are highly correlated (r = 0.82; Sehyr and Emmorey, 2019). For the present study, iconic signs received a rating of 3.7 or higher, while non-iconic signs received ratings of 2.9 or lower. Subjective ASL frequency ratings were given by deaf signers, also using a 7-point scale (1 = very infrequent in everyday conversation, 7 = very frequent in everyday conversation). English frequency was determined by the HAL model (Burgess, 1998).

ANOVAs were used to determine whether variables were matched across conditions. The perceptually- and motorically-iconic signs were matched for iconicity ratings (F[1,43] = 3.43, p = 0.08). Both the perceptually-iconic signs (F[1,43] = 548.85, p < 0.0001) and motorically-iconic signs (F[1,43] = 339.94, p < 0.0001) were more iconic than the non-iconic signs. The three conditions did not differ in ASL frequency (F[2,86] = 1.03, p = 0.36) or English frequency (F[2,86] = 3.21, p = 0.09). Descriptive statistics for the targeted ASL signs/English words are given in Table 1.

The picture stimuli that elicited perceptually-iconic signs all exhibited some visual alignment with the iconic form of the sign (except for the sign HAT as the head was not shown in this picture). In contrast, there was much less visual alignment between the motorically-iconic signs and the picture stimuli as the pictures did not depict how the object was used (to avoid the production of verbs); however, as noted above, a subset of these signs (n = 9) also depicted visual-spatial features of the iconic sign which aligned with the picture (e.g., the flat hand in CALCULATOR and the picture of a flat, rectangular calculator). For the non-iconic signs there was no visual overlap between the form of the sign and the picture. The picture stimuli in all three conditions had equivalent image complexity (F[2,86] = 1.25, p = 0.29). Values for image complexity were retrieved through Matlab’s entropy function, where higher values indicate increased variability in the placement of pixels and therefore higher complexity in the line drawings. Picture stimuli were run through the entropy function, which returns image complexity values for each picture. The values were 0.08 (SD = 0.06), 0.08 (SD = 0.05), and 0.09 (SD = 0.05) for the pictures targeting perceptually-iconic, motorically-iconic, and non-iconic signs, respectively. Pictures were roughly the same size (~4 square inches on the screen).

Two pseudorandomized presentation lists were created, each including all 132 pictures. Initial list presentation was counterbalanced across participants, and only the first presentation was analyzed for the current study.1 Participants were not familiarized with the picture stimuli prior to the experiment.

Procedure

For signers, each trial began with text advising them to press and hold down the spacebar of a keyboard placed in their lap. Upon doing so, a fixation cross appeared in the center of the screen for 800ms, followed by a 200ms blank screen, and then the picture stimulus. The picture remained on the screen for 300ms, followed by a blank screen. Participants released the spacebar and named the picture in ASL as quickly and accurately as possible. The spacebar release indicated the beginning of sign production, and response times were recorded as the time elapsed from when the stimulus appeared on screen to when the spacebar was released. A deaf native signer recorded the signs produced on each trial in real time, noting any variations. After 500ms, the blank screen was replaced with text advising participants to hold down the spacebar when they were ready to proceed to the next trial. Participants could blink and move prior to pressing the space bar, as the next trial would not begin until they did so. Participants were also provided with three self-timed breaks, which allowed them to take a longer break as needed.

Hearing controls saw the fixation cross for 800ms, followed by a 200ms blank screen, and then the picture stimulus. The picture remained on screen for 300ms, followed by a blank screen for 710ms. Hearing participants were asked to name the pictures aloud as quickly and accurately as possible. Each trial was automatically recorded as a separate audio file using a voice recorder. After the 500ms blank screen, text appeared on the screen advising participants to press a button on the gamepad to move to the next trial when ready, giving them the opportunity to take breaks. Hearing participants were also given three self-timed breaks.

If participants in either group did not know what the picture represented or could not recall the name, they were instructed to respond that they didn’t know, thereby skipping the trial. Before beginning the study, participants were familiarized with the instructions by practicing on a set of 10 pictures not included in the experiment lists. All participants sat in a chair approximately 140 cm from the screen.

EEG recording and analysis

Participants wore an elastic cap (Electro-Cap) with 64 active electrodes. An electrode placed on the participant’s left mastoid was used as a reference during the recording and analyses. Electrodes located on the cheek immediately below the left eye and on the outer canthus of the right eye were used for the identification of trials with blinks and horizontal eye movements. Using Electro-Gel saline gel, all electrode impedances were maintained below 2.5 kΩ. To be consistent with our previous studies (McGarry et al., 2021a, 2023), only 32 active electrode sites were retained. From these, we selected 15 representative sites for analyses, following our previous studies (see Fig. 3 for the electrode montage and the electrode sites selected for analyses). EEG signals were amplified with SynAmpsRT amplifiers (Neuroscan-Compumedics) with a bandpass of DC to 100 Hz and was sampled continuously at 500 Hz.

Behavioral analyses

We compared response latencies and naming accuracy between the perceptually-iconic, motorically-iconic, and non-iconic signs. For ASL signers, trials with reaction times shorter than 300ms, longer than 2.5 standard deviations above the individual participant’s mean RT, and those with incorrect responses were excluded from latency analyses (13 trials per participant or 5% of all data on average). Regional variants of signs were accepted as correct, and the number of regional variants was similar across conditions. In some cases, participants produced signs (or a fingerspelled word) that, while not incorrect, did not maintain the anticipated iconicity (for example, producing the non-iconic sign SALT rather than the targeted motorically-iconic sign SALT-SHAKER). These trials were excluded from analyses, but were not considered errors for accuracy purposes.

For English speakers, any trials with blank wave files (indicating participants took over 1500ms to respond) or incorrect responses were removed. In addition, if participants took less than 510ms to respond, these responses were removed. Due to a programming error, 510ms was the length of time between picture presentation and the beginning of the recording of the verbal response. Therefore, 510ms was the earliest response latency value where speech onset could be captured, and values below this number could not be trusted.2 On average, between participant errors and data trimming, 24 trials per participant (18% of all data) were removed for the English speakers. The distribution of removed trials was roughly equal across the three conditions.

In each participant group, we used an omnibus ANOVA comparing response latencies across all three conditions (perceptual, motoric, and non-iconic) to determine whether response latency varied significantly across conditions. If warranted by the omnibus ANOVA, we conducted follow-up ANOVAs on paired sets of conditions (p-values for pairwise effects are reported after a Holm-Bonferroni correction). We used the same ANOVA structure for response accuracy.

ERP analyses

ICA ocular and movement correction was conducted through Matlab’s EEGlab plugin (Delorme and Makeig, 2004). After correction, trials contaminated by eye and facial movements were rejected from analyses across all participants (less than 1% of all data on average). The primary dependent variable was mean amplitude of the N400 (350–600ms after picture presentation). This time window is similar to that used in our prior studies which revealed electrophysiological effects of iconicity. To identify any interactions between Group and Iconicity condition, we first conducted an omnibus 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 ANOVA with two levels of Group (ASL signers; English speakers), three levels of Iconicity (Perceptual, Motoric, and Non-iconic), three levels of Laterality (Left, Midline, Right), and five levels of Anteriority (FP, F, C, P, O). We then conducted separate 3 × 3 × 5 ANOVAs for each group with the same factors, and if warranted, we conducted follow-up ANOVAs to compare iconicity conditions (all reported p-values for pairwise effects are after a Holm-Bonferroni correction).

Discussion

Perceptually-iconic and motorically-iconic signs are two relatively distinct classes of iconic signs. Perceptually-iconic signs depict how an object and its features are visually perceived, and motorically-iconic signs depict how an object is used or handled (Caselli and Pyers, 2020; Padden et al., 2013). Our prior picture-naming studies with ASL (McGarry et al., 2021a, 2023) and studies with other sign languages have found that iconic signs are retrieved more quickly than non-iconic signs (Baus and Costa, 2015; Gimeno-Martínez and Baus, 2022; Navarrete et al., 2017; Vinson et al., 2015). However, none of these prior studies specifically investigated the nature of the iconic mapping, and they likely included an over-representation of perceptually-iconic signs. The present study aimed to investigate whether the behavioral and N400 effects of iconicity differed between these two iconic sign types, as well as between each type of iconic sign and a set of matched non-iconic signs.

Although our primary focus was on lexical access and the N400 epoch, visual inspection of the ERP waves revealed differences between picture conditions during an earlier epoch (200–300ms) for both English speakers and ASL signers. An exploratory analysis of this early time window revealed that pictures targeting non-iconic signs elicited greater negativities than those targeting perceptually-iconic and motorically-iconic signs for both speakers and signers, and there was no difference between the two iconicity conditions for either group (Fig. 8). We interpret this pattern of results as reflecting both groups’ sensitivity to visual differences between the pictures in the non-iconic condition compared to the other two conditions. Although the pictures were matched on image complexity based on Matlab’s entropy function, it is possible that other factors, such as the configuration or number of elements in the picture, could have resulted in larger negativities within this early window for the pictures targeting non-iconic signs. Greater visual complexity tends to result in larger early negativities (e.g., Shigeto et al., 2011). Importantly, only ASL signers exhibited iconicity effects on naming latencies and on the amplitude of the later N400 component.

We predicted that both types of iconic signs would be retrieved more quickly than the non-iconic signs, consistent with the existing body of research, and our results supported this prediction. ASL signers named pictures targeting both perceptually-iconic and motorically-iconic signs more quickly than those targeting non-iconic signs. For non-signing control participants, there were no differences in response latency or accuracy when the same pictures were named in English. This pattern of results suggests a faciliatory effect of iconicity on sign production in picture-naming tasks, regardless of the type of iconic mapping.

As noted in the introduction, perceptually-iconic signs are always somewhat visually-aligned with the picture stimuli because the iconic mapping encodes features of the referent that are visible in the pictures. Motorically-iconic signs, in contrast, are less likely to visually align with features of the object shown in the picture stimuli. We thus predicted faster naming latencies for perceptually-iconic signs, and this prediction was supported as well. The fact that there were no behavioral effects for the non-signing controls indicates that the results in the signing participants were not driven by semantic category differences or by visual variations in the picture sets. The behavioral findings support the hypothesis that the strength of the mapping between the visual features of a to-be-named picture and the semantic and phonological features of a sign can modulate the speed of lexical retrieval and phonological encoding for ASL signers. While motorically-iconic signs depict semantic features of the referent in the to-be-named pictures (e.g., how a hammer is grasped and used), the phonological form of these signs typically does not map to visual features of the picture (e.g., the shape of the hammer). We argue that this difference leads to weaker facilitation effects for motorically-iconic compared to perceptually-iconic signs.

To our knowledge, the present study is the first electrophysiological investigation to directly compare perceptually-iconic and motorically-iconic signs. As expected, we found reduced (smaller) N400 amplitudes for perceptually-iconic signs compared to non-iconic signs (Fig. 5). No N400 amplitude differences were observed for the English speakers. We interpret the reduced N400 amplitude for ASL signers as evidence that the semantic and phonological features encoded in a perceptually-iconic sign are primed by the picture which facilitates lexical access for these signs. The facilitation effects for perceptually-iconic signs are supported by a recent study by Navarrete et al. (2020) with Italian Sign Language (LIS). This study focused on perceptually-iconic signs that encoded object orientation; for example, the LIS sign PEN depicts a straight object (extended index finger) in a horizontal orientation. Signers (unlike non-signers) were faster to recognize object drawings that depicted the same orientation as the LIS sign (e.g., a horizontal pen vs. a vertical pen). In a second ERP experiment, signers exhibited a larger deviant P300 amplitude compared to non-signers when the oddball picture did not match the orientation of the LIS sign (e.g., standards = horizontal pens; deviant = a vertical pen). No group differences were observed for pictures of control objects. Since no signs were produced or perceived in this study, the results provide evidence for the existence of a structured alignment between the mental representation of the phonological form of a sign and the representation of the form of the object denoted by the sign. Navarrete et al. (2020) hypothesized that this alignment enhances the saliency of the relevant visual features of object representations which can impact object discrimination. For picture-naming, we hypothesize that this structured alignment impacts the lexical retrieval and production of perceptually-iconic signs.

In contrast, for motorically-iconic signs we found that the N400 amplitudes did not differ from non-iconic signs (Fig. 4) and differed from perceptually-iconic signs (Fig. 6), i.e., the N400 amplitudes were reduced for perceptually-iconic compared to motorically-iconic signs. This pattern indicates that lexical access as indexed by the N400 component is only facilitated for perceptually-iconic signs. The fact that we observed faster naming but no N400 effects for motorically-iconic signs provides some clues to the sources of iconicity effects in picture-naming tasks.

Specifically, while lexical access may not be facilitated for motorically-iconic signs – as evidenced by no reduction in N400 amplitude, iconicity could benefit later processes, such as ensuring that the correct sign is produced when the phonology of multiple signs is activated. Navarrete et al. (2017) proposed that semantic features of iconic signs are more strongly activated than those of non-iconic signs, and cascading activation from semantic to phonological representations facilitates the exclusion of non-target signs, thus reducing naming times for iconic signs. Another related possibility is that the mapping between the semantic features activated by retrieval of a motorically-iconic sign (e.g., hammers are held and swung) and the phonological form of the sign (e.g., the grasping handshape and arcing movement) could lead to faster phonological encoding for these signs, which also would lead to faster naming times compared to non-iconic signs. A somewhat similar finding has been reported for semantic and phonological effects in picture-word interference paradigms for spoken language. Specifically, Blackford et al. (2012) found that masked phonologically-related primes did not modulate the N400 component (in contrast to semantically-related or identity primes), but naming latencies were nonetheless faster compared to trials with unrelated primes. The authors argued that this pattern indicated that phonological priming occurred later during encoding of the articulatory response.

In sum, we hypothesize that for perceptually-iconic signs, lexical access is facilitated via picture-priming because visual features depicted in the picture (e.g. a butterfly’s wings) prime both semantic and phonological features encoded by the sign (e.g., the semantic feature that butterflies have wings and the movement and shape of the hands that depict a butterfly’s wings). Naming latencies can be further facilitated for perceptually-iconic signs because (like motorically-iconic signs), the mapping between semantic features and phonological form could speed phonological encoding and/or facilitate the exclusion of non-target signs. This speculative explanation also accounts for the finding that response times for perceptually-iconic signs were faster than for motorically-iconic signs. That is, naming latencies for perceptually-iconic signs are speeded due to both picture-priming during lexical access and facilitation at latter stages (phonological encoding and/or response decision).

One way to test this hypothesis would be to increase the potential for visual alignment between pictures and motorically-iconic signs by targeting verbs using pictures (or videos) of actions. For example, the form of the verb TO-HAMMER resembles the form of the noun HAMMER (see Fig. 2), but the movement is larger (some noun-verb pairs in ASL differ only in movement; Supalla and Newport, 1978). A picture of a person using a hammer would visually align with both semantic and phonological features of the target sign, e.g., the grasping handshape and arm movement. Sehyr and Emmorey (2022) found that pictures of actions were named significantly faster if the targeted verb was iconic. In this study, iconicity was treated as a continuous variable, and the authors did not examine the type of iconicity. However, it is likely that many of the highly iconic verbs were motorically-iconic. The key question is whether the visual alignment between action pictures (e.g., a picture of someone brushing their hair or using a hammer) and motorically-iconic verbs will facilitate lexical access, resulting in reduced N400 amplitudes compared to non-iconic verbs. If so, this result would support our account of the N400 effects for iconic signs in picture-naming. If reduced N400 amplitudes are not found for motorically-iconic verbs, this would suggest that there is some other factor that facilitates lexical access for perceptually-iconic signs (e.g., perhaps the type of semantic features depicted by these signs).

Finally, these results can be interpreted within the structure-mapping framework proposed by Emmorey (2014), based on Taub (2001). One key aspect of this approach is that the type of iconicity, i.e. the nature of the structural correspondences between phonological form and the representative mental image of a concept, can be used to predict when and whether iconicity impacts cognitive processes. Under this account, the structured mapping between the mental image evoked by the object picture and the phonological form of perceptually-iconic signs facilitates lexical retrieval and phonological encoding. In contrast, there are fewer structural correspondences between the phonological form of motorically-iconic signs and the mental image evoked by object pictures, which leads to weaker iconicity effects. This account also predicts that the structural correspondences between the mental image evoked by an action picture would facilitate the retrieval and production of a motorically-iconic verb.

In general, there appears to be a complex relationship between picture processing and lexical access that depends on the participants’ task, as well as on the nature of the structural alignment between the picture and iconic sign. For example, when the production task does not tap into the structural alignment, iconicity effects are not observed, such as when signers respond to pictures with a demonstrative pronoun rather than with the object name (Pretato et al., 2018).

For comprehension tasks (e.g., picture-sign matching), iconicity appears to facilitate the decision process, rather than lexical access (McGarry et al., 2021). In addition, hearing a word (Ostarek and Huettig, 2017) or seeing a sign (Ortega and Ostarek, 2021) facilitates picture processing by activating visual features of the object, but iconicity (picture-sign alignment) does not modulate this effect. Thus, lexical access and picture processing may be less impacted by iconicity during language comprehension tasks than production tasks.

Limitations

One limitation of this study is that we did not quantify the degree of visual alignment between the pictures and the iconic signs. In part, this was because we were unsure of how best to quantify this relationship, but developing such a measure would be a fruitful avenue for future research. In addition, some of the motorically-iconic signs used in this study depicted both how an object was held/manipulated and visual-spatial features of the referent, which made the contrast between the two types of iconic signs less robust with respect picture-sign alignment. Future work would also benefit from the use of a more advanced metric for visual complexity and matching stimuli on other variables known to impact picture-naming times (e.g., phonological complexity; Sehyr and Emmorey, 2022). Finally, we focused on sensory-motoric iconicity due to the constraints of the picture-naming task, but there are other types of iconicity such as metonymic and metaphorical mappings between form and meaning, e.g., the ASL sign for an academic degree iconically depicts a scroll (Taub, 2001). Further work is needed to explore other, more complex types of iconicity.

Conclusions

This is the first study to investigate whether the type of iconic mapping, perceptual vs. motoric, impacts sign production as indexed by picture-naming times and modulation of the N400 component. ASL naming latencies were faster for pictures targeting both types of iconic signs compared to non-iconic signs, and latencies did not differ when the same pictures were named in English by non-signers. Effects of iconicity type were observed in both ASL naming latencies and in modulations of the N400 component. Pictures targeting perceptually-iconic signs were named faster and elicited a reduced N400 amplitude compared to those targeting motorically-iconic signs. We suggest that this pattern of results arises because pictures that target perceptually-iconic signs can more effectively prime lexical access and phonological encoding because features of the picture are more aligned with both the semantic features depicted by the iconic sign and the phonological form of the sign. We speculate that pictures targeting motorically-iconic signs do not prime lexical access due to weak visual alignment, but that naming latencies are facilitated due to speeded decisions for retrieval accuracy (faster exclusion of non-target signs) and/or faster phonological encoding. We speculate that faster phonological encoding could arise from the mapping between semantic features of the referent (activated during lexical retrieval) and the phonological form of the sign. These ideas can be tested in the future with an action-naming paradigm in which elements of the picture are more likely to visually align with the semantic features depicted by the form of motorically-iconic verbs. Overall, our results indicate that the type of iconicity is an important variable to consider when investigating the role of iconicity in sign language processing.
