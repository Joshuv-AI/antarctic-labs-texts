From Parallel Provision to Health System Integration: Exploring the Trajectory and Contextual Drivers of the Healthcare Response for Refugees in Six Low‐ and Middle‐Income Countries
Authors: Maria Paola Bertone, Natasha Palmer, Amina Olabi, Ibrahim Bou‐Orm, Hussam Rekani, Hassan Ould Moctar, Ricardo Chuquimia, Chitalu Miriam Chama‐Chiliba, Ben Ngoye, Zahid Ali Memon, Marcia Vera‐Espinoza, Karin Diaconu, Alastair Ager, Sophie Witter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRising numbers of refugees, prolonged displacement and reduced funding have led to challenges in terms of how to address their healthcare needs, with different approaches taken, ranging from parallel mechanisms to arrangements that are integrated (to different extents) within the national health system. Increasingly, global frameworks call for focus on the inclusion of refugees in national health systems. Based on six case studies (Kenya, Kurdistan Region of Iraq, Mauritania, Pakistan, Peru and Zambia), this paper analyses the trajectory towards health system integration in the healthcare responses for refugees to understand how contextual features play a role, and explores enablers and barriers of greater health system integration. Methods included documentary reviews, key informant interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs). Analysis was carried out separately for each setting and findings were later mapped, compared and contrasted for synthesis. All settings follow a normative pathway from an initial parallel response to hybrid, transitional arrangements to health system integration—though the latter is at different stages across settings. Some elements influence the timeframe of the shift, its completeness and effectiveness. These include: the scale and pace of refugee flow; the salience of political discourses on refugees and public perceptions, in some instances mediated by ethnic and cultural affinity; the country's level of income and social protection systems; existing legal and policy frameworks, refugee rights and societal integration processes; availability of funds; capacity of the national health system, and its universalist approach. While it is difficult to alter some of these elements, each has to be carefully considered for health system integration processes. Priorities will generally include promptly strengthening local health systems to address the difference in healthcare provision for refugees and hosts, and effectively leveraging available funding (including from development and private sector sources) as well as existing, inclusive health system arrangements, such as free healthcare or social health insurance.Keywords: health system integration, Kenya, Kurdistan Region of Iraq, LMICs, Mauritania, Pakistan, Peru, refugee inclusion, refugees, Zambia

Introduction

The arrival of refugees in a host country poses a significant challenge to respond adequately to their healthcare needs in ways that are effective and sustainable [1]. Approaches have in many contexts, especially in emergency situations, relied on the establishment of mechanisms parallel to the national health system [2]. However, due to rising numbers of refugees globally, prolonged displacement and reduced funding, there have been calls to move away from parallel approaches to focus on the inclusion of refugees in national health systems. The Global Compact for Refugees (GCR) [3], endorsed by 181 countries, calls for facilitating access to healthcare services for refugees and host communities, by expanding and enhancing the quality of national health systems, building and equipping health facilities and strengthening services.

The emergence of the health system integration discourse at global level has been analysed widely with several factors identified as driving a shift away from parallel approaches to integration [2]. In line with the GCR, in this paper, we define ‘health system integration for refugees’ as the process of supporting national health systems to facilitate inclusion of refugees and their access to healthcare through national systems. This involves change in policies and programmes across all of the health systems domains that foster sustainable and equitable access to healthcare for all refugees and host communities. Factors contributing to the emergence of the health system integration discourse include: increased collaboration between humanitarian and development agencies; the engagement of traditionally developmental actors, such as the World Bank; framings around human rights and responsibility sharing, and a political context influenced by increased displacement, particularly in relation to the Syrian crisis [2]. However, there is limited empirical analysis of different approaches, and none as yet with a comparative perspective. While a shift towards health system integration and the inclusion of refugees in national health systems is the normative pathway highlighted in the global strategic documents, little is known or documented about how the process is managed and happens in countries.

Based on six case studies carried out in selected low‐ and middle‐income country (LMIC) settings (Kenya, Kurdistan Region of Iraq (KRI), Mauritania, Pakistan, Peru and Zambia), we analyse the trajectory in healthcare response for refugees and the emergence of health system integration policies and practices as well as identify contextual factors that influence these changes.

Methods

The study was carried out under a broader research programme funded by UNHCR. It adopted a mixed‐methods, exploratory, retrospective, comparative longitudinal case study design. Six case studies were developed separately, but following the same overall protocol and methodological approach. In a second stage, synthesis analysis was conducted to identify common patterns, similarities and differences in trajectories of health system integration; within this we reflect on the contextual factors defining these trajectories.

Ethics approval for primary data collection for all case studies was obtained from Queen Margaret University (UK). Additional ethics approval was obtained from Strathmore University, from the National Health Research Ethics Board in Zambia (NHREB003/10/08/2023) and from the Aga Khan University in Pakistan (2023‐9185‐26641). Additionally, clearances were obtained from health and administrative authorities in the KRI, Zambia and Pakistan.

Country and Site Selection

The six case studies focus on Kenya, Kurdistan Region of Iraq (KRI), Mauritania, Pakistan, Peru and Zambia. The selection was focused on LMICs, ensuring representativeness of geographic regions, and avoiding duplication with settings that are well covered in the literature (Uganda, Lebanon, Jordan, Bangladesh, DR Congo, Colombia [1, 4, 5]). Additional policy and logistics consideration, such as interest and availability of UNHCR country offices to support the research, were taken into consideration. In each country, the population of focus was selected to represent the people covered by UNHCR's mandate. Throughout the paper, we use the term ‘refugees’ in reference to the people covered by UNHCR's mandate, which in each case might include also asylum seekers and other statuses (e.g., Venezuelan migrants in Peru).

Two study sites were chosen in each country in order to capture appropriate diversity of circumstances. This was done in consultation with the UNHCR country office with the aim of covering representative contexts (large settlements or camps, significant urban populations). Selected sites across case studies typically consisted of an urban site (often the country capital), and a second site, in a more rural area or smaller town, where refugees are often (though not in all cases) settled in a camp or village (Table 1). In KRI, a third site was selected to ensure all governorates were represented (excluding Halabja/Helebçe where the number of refugees is low), each with a mix of urban and camp settlements.

Data Sources

Fieldwork was carried out in two waves (late 2023‐early 2024 for Zambia, Mauritania and Pakistan; and mid 2024‐early 2025 for Peru, KRI and Kenya).

Table 2 provides a summary of data sources. For each setting, data sources included a context‐scoping documentary review, secondary analysis of existing financial and health datasets, as well as a series of key informant interviews (KIIs) and/or focus group discussions (FGDs). Documents and datasets included peer reviewed articles, grey literature such as government policies, plans and guidelines, reports by external partners, online media reports, and datasets (where available) from routine information system, operational or surveillance data and surveys. They were retrieved through purposive internet searches, as well as shared by the UNHCR country offices and other key informants. KIIs and FGDs included participants involved at different levels in refugee health service provision, such as government representatives, funders and development partners, national and international NGOs, health providers, and refugee and host community representatives. Participants were identified by means of a purposive sampling approach, carried out through existing contacts facilitated by the local UNHCR office and local researchers, and informed by the document review. The choice between individual interviews and FGDs was made depending on the circumstances of the interview and the preference of the participants who in some cases were more comfortable with group interviews (e.g., facility staff). Interviews and FGDs followed a standard topic guide adapted for each settings (Annex 1). Some interviews were conducted virtually, via Microsoft Teams, especially with actors at central level, while interviews at local levels were done in person. Interviews were conducted in English, Swahili, French, Arabic, Kurdish and Spanish depending on the preference of the participants. After reviewing the participant information sheet and addressing any questions, consent was taken in writing or orally. Interviews were recorded where possible and transcribed, and detailed notes were taken. Transcripts and notes were anonymised using alphanumerical codes before analysis.

Data Analysis and Synthesis

Data was analysed thematically using the elements of a conceptual framework developed by the team in advance of the empirical work [5] (Figure 1). In this paper, we focus specifically on presenting the findings in relation to the left‐hand side of the framework, unpacking the ways in which the overall contextual features in each setting have contributed to shaping healthcare responses for refugees. In a separate paper [6], we reflect on the central elements of the framework, looking at refugee inclusion across key health system domains. Within each case study, data analysis of different sources was done iteratively, to allow for triangulation of information. Six stand‐alone reports were developed based on the case studies, which we draw on for this paper.

Findings from the six case studies were mapped, compared and contrasted to enhance analytic generalisation [7] during an analysis workshop held in Edinburgh. Based on the group analysis, a series of hypotheses were developed which were then used to interrogate the data through synthetic tables and summaries presented in the findings section.

Findings

In this section, we first describe the socio‐economic and political contexts of the case study settings, as well as the key health indicators and health system features. Secondly, we review the refugee context, providing an overview of timing, trends of arrivals and the legal frameworks regulating refugee settlement and rights. Finally, we analyse the trajectory of the healthcare response for refugees, highlighting different phases.

Socio‐Economic, Political and Health Contexts, and Health System Features

While all are classified as LMICs, the settings of focus present different features in terms of socio‐economic, demographic and health indicators (Table 3). Peru and Iraq (including KRI) are upper‐middle‐income countries, while all others are in the lower‐middle‐income category. Similarly, life expectancy is highest in Peru and Iraq. According to the 2025 OECD classification [8], all study settings are exposed to high fragility (extreme in the case of Iraq), with the exception of Peru. Despite the non‐fragile status, Peru has experienced a period of instability since 2018 with electoral disputes, an attempted coup and shifts in executive and legislative power.

The level of commitment to UHC, including measures to ensure universal coverage and financial protection, is also relatively heterogeneous. In Peru, universal health rights are enshrined in the 1993 constitution (Article 7) which guarantees free access to health services (Article 11) for all, including irregular migrants. Social health insurance is available for those in the formal sector (EsSalud, covering 26% of the insured), for the Armed Forces (1.7% of the insured) and the Seguro Integral de Salud (SIS)—which was initially covering Peruvians in extreme poverty and vulnerable groups, but since 2020 and the COVID‐19 pandemic was extended to any Peruvian or ‘resident’ (a status granted by the Foreigner's ID Card) not covered by other schemes (62.3% of the insured). The SIS does not entail enrolment premiums, which are subsidised by the government, and covers preventative, primary and secondary care [9].

Other countries are committed to make progress towards UHC, often by attempting to set up social health insurance or other forms of pre‐payment schemes, with only partial success so far. In Kenya, notable progress has been made through the enactment of UHC Act in 2023 that aims to strengthen primary health care (PHC), increase funding at facility level and, importantly, introduce a comprehensive insurance scheme under the Social Health Insurance Fund (SHIF) [10], building on the existing National Health Insurance Fund (NHIF), which covered those in formal employment (17% of households in 2020) [11]. However, the implementation of the Act is just beginning and progress has been slow [12].

Zambia has implemented a range of health reforms to increase access and utilisation of health services in the past 3 decades, including decentralisation of health services planning and delivery, abolition of user fees for PHC facilities, and introduction of performance‐based contracting, and then results‐based financing [13]. In 2020, a health insurance scheme has been introduced to cover secondary and tertiary care, although challenges remain [14].

In Mauritania, the health system relies primarily on OOP expenditures, with very limited exemptions or subsidisation schemes for preventative services, HIV and TB and pregnant women. Insurance mechanisms are in place for those in formal employment (Caisse Nationale d’Assurance Maladie (CNAM)), covering about 15% of the population, for the poorest through the government‐subsidised Taazour Plan (14% of the population, but now phasing out) and a new programme (Caisse Nationale de Solidarité en Santé (CNASS)) established in 2022 with support of development partners to cover the remaining population, still at pilot stage [15]. Healthcare infrastructure is limited, with stark disparities between urban and rural areas where physical access and operational capacity of facilities remain major challenges [16].

In Pakistan, the armed forces, civil service and semi‐governmental organisations provide healthcare to their employees and dependants, though only covering 10% of the population. The Sehat Sahulat Programme (SSP) was launched in 2015 in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP) and expanded nationwide in 2019 to provide vulnerable groups excluded from healthcare access from the schemes above with a health insurance card which allows access to inpatient services for up to one million rupees per year [17]. By 2022, it had reached about 44.6 million households. However, challenges remain in relation to the limited coverage compared to actual costs, and political and economic instability has disrupted the programme's continuity.

In KRI, the conflict of the last decade has weakened the public health system in terms of funding and infrastructure (although arguably less so in relatively stable KRI compared to the more volatile central and southern areas of Iraq) and led to the expansion of the private sector, creating a ‘two‐lane health system’, with private services available for those able to pay, and increasing inequality [18].

Kenya historically had an open‐door policy for refugees from the region, and has hosted large numbers of refugees from neighbouring countries, particularly Somalia and South Sudan. Refugee numbers continued to increase over time and there are currently more than 800,000 registered refugees. Kenya is a signatory to several international treaties, including the 1951 Convention, though the management of refugees has undergone changes over the years. While initially refugees could move freely and work anywhere, since the 1990s, with increasing numbers and perceived insecurity, two camps and one settlement were established ‐ Dadaab in Garissa County, and Kakuma camp and Kalobeyei refugee settlement in Turkana County ‐ and international organisations such as UNHCR took a leading role in managing service provision [19]. While refugees are meant to settle in camps, a minority is living in urban areas. The 2006 Refugee Act enforced encampment policy and limited rights, including movement and work restrictions. To address the shortcomings of that Act, which entailed a complicated refugee status determination process and to align to international standards for refugee protection and management, in 2021, Kenya passed a new Refugee Act that calls for the inclusion of refugees in national and county development plans and is explicit on the right of refugees to engage in economic activities through facilitated documentation and the recognition of qualifications. In 2023, the Shirika Plan was launched which aims to transform the refugee camps into integrated settlements supporting the socio‐economic inclusion of refugees and host communities. The implementation of the 2021 Refugee Act and of the Shirika Plan is only starting, and its start coincides with a rapidly changing aid and geopolitical environment. While progress is uneven, some concrete steps have been made. For example, the Government has designated Kakuma and Dadaab as municipalities, facilitating their administrative integration and enabling government and county budget allocations to the areas, although the full process (which is supported by the World Bank) is likely to take time [20].

In KRI, refugee arrivals corresponded to the start of the conflict in Syria in 2011 and by 2012 camps were set up to manage the influx, with international organisations providing emergency assistance. The majority of refugees are Kurds from North and East Syria, with whom the host communities in KRI share ethnic and cultural ties. The refugee (and IDP) influx peaked around 2014–2015, following the rise of ISIS and the intensification of the Syrian conflict. At that time, the Kurdistan Regional Government (KRG) focused on providing emergency shelter, food, and medical services to refugees. Since 2016, the refugee population in KRI has stabilised, though it remains significant with about 270,000 refugees and the KRG has shifted its focus to longer‐term integration efforts. In terms of legal frameworks, Iraq is not a signatory to the 1951 Refugee Convention, resulting in the absence of a national legal framework addressing refugee status determination. The existing 1971 Political Refugee Law is limited in scope, primarily addressing individuals seeking asylum for political or military reasons [21]. The lack of a formal legal framework has led to the implementation of ad hoc policies to manage refugee affairs, which has impacted various aspects of refugee protection, including camp admission procedures, recognition of refugee status, access to rights and services, and the provision of durable solutions. However, given the ethnic and cultural ties between Syrian Kurds and the host communities in the KRI, the KRG has adopted an open‐door policy as a gesture of solidarity, allowing Syrian to enter freely and settle in the region without significant restrictions. In practice, in KRI, Syrian refugees have access to public services and their rights are fulfiled at the same level as the local community. Movement of the refugees within KRI is fairly easy. However, refugees face movement restrictions between KRI and Federal Iraq, due to differing policies and security considerations. Most recently, Iraq has engaged with international frameworks such as the GCR, for example with the formulation of a National Migration Strategy that aims to address sustainable solutions for displacement and migration, reflecting a commitment to international norms and standards.

Refugees from northern Mali began arriving in large numbers in the Hodh Ech‐Chargui region of eastern Mauritania in 2012 as a result of the armed rebellion and coup that took place in Mali that year. By June 2012, over 74,000 people had been registered in M'bera refugee camp. These numbers have fluctuated in the intervening decade and there were 99,117 people residing in the camp in February 2024 (at the time of our study), which have increased to 118,162 by March 2025 due to the deteriorating security situation in northern Mali. As it is considered that M'bera camp has reached maximum capacity, in line with national and international policies and due to low funding, there is no appetite for establishing new refugee camps. As a consequence, newly arrived refugees are encouraged to settle ‘out‐of‐camp’ within the Hodh Ech‐Chargui region. In total, there are 174,332 refugees in Mauritania. The government has long maintained an open‐door policy regarding Malian refugees, having adhered to international conventions including the 1951 Refugee Convention. Since 2018 under the GCR, the government has made a range of commitments regarding refugee access to employment, identity documentation, social services, and education. A refugee identity card has been created to facilitate refugee internal movement and access to social services [22]. Refugees have the same access to employment rights as nationals, with the exception of the public sector. This broad, societal integration process is supported by the familial, tribal, and economic ties that bind refugee and host communities in eastern Mauritania. These connections create a cultural connection, which has ensured relatively high social cohesion between refugee populations and host communities. In practice, however, there remain obstacles to substantive integration, ones exacerbated by the weaknesses of underlying national systems in place to access basic services in Mauritania.

Afghan refugees have been arriving in Pakistan since the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979. Despite the voluntary return of 4.5 million Afghans since 2002, many continue to trickle back to Pakistan, particularly since 2021 when the Taleban became again the de facto government in Afghanistan. The persistent influx has become a significant concern for Pakistan, straining resources, exacerbating domestic challenges, and complicating Pakistan‐Afghanistan relations. As a consequence, there have been periodic efforts to send all Afghan refugees home, the most recent in April–May 2025. The majority of Afghan refugees are settled in Pakistan's Pashtun belt, particularly in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP), Federally Administered Tribal Areas (FATA), and Balochistan. Refugee camps, overseen by UNHCR and the Pakistani authorities, provided basic services, though over time, many refugees moved out of camps and integrated into urban areas. In terms of legal frameworks, Pakistan is not a signatory to the 1951 Convention and has also not enacted any national legislation for refugee protection. Refugee management through international donors is operated within this vacuum, relying on periodic instructions from the federal government. As a consequence, Afghan immigrants have no regular status in Pakistan and are a vulnerable community susceptible to arrest, detention, and deportation [23]. Despite legal restrictions on employment and property ownership, Afghan refugees found opportunities in sectors like transportation, contributing to the socio‐economic structure of Pakistan.

Peru had historically received a modest number of migrants. However, since November 2017, the influx of Venezuelans in Peru, following the socio‐economic and political crisis in Venezuela has been unprecedented. The number of Venezuelans in Peru has steadily increased, starting in 2016 (8000), with a rapid increase between 2017 (110,000) and 2018 (530,000), and a tapered increase between 2019 and 2022 [24]. By May 2024, over one and a half million Venezuelans resided in Peru, most of them without a resident status and most residing in Lima or other major cities (there are no refugee camps in Peru). Peru's legal frameworks regarding entry and settlement were initially open and flexible as Peru is signatory of several international and regional agreements, including the 1951 Convention and the 1984 Cartagena Declaration [25], but national migration policies have become less receptive over time, largely due to the large influx, the instability in the executive power and a perceived link between immigration and crime that worsened the public perception of Venezuelans [26]. In early 2017, the Temporary Stay Permit (PTP, replaced with a Temporary Stay Permit Card (CPP) in 2020) was established alongside a new Migration Act. The PTP was granted (on a temporary basis) to Venezuelans who entered the country with a regular status and had their criminal records checked, allowing them to work and access social services. The same year, the 2017–2025 National Migration Policy was approved, aimed at promoting inclusion of foreigners into Peruvian society. However, with the change of government, since the second half of 2018, a series of legal measures were enacted to limit access to the territory and to documentation. These measures made Venezuelans' regularisation more difficult and irregular entries surged. Since 2020, various waves of legislations have been issued, in some instances creating opportunities for regularisation, while making them more restrictive in others [24, 25].

Zambia has welcomed refugees since the 1960s, initially due to the civil conflict in Angola (1966). Arrivals continued over the next decades, linked to unrest in the region, including in Rwanda (1994), Somalia (1990–2011), DRC (since 2000) and Burundi (since 2015). Repatriation and resettlement in third countries have occurred, with 339,000 refugees returned to their home countries since 2021 and 114,300 resettled [27]. In addition, many have opted to remain in Zambia under the framework of local integration programmes, assuming the status of former refugees and gaining Zambian citizenship in 2024 under new residency rules [27]. As of 2025, there are a total of 110,518 refugees in Zambia, the majority from the DRC. Refugees primarily reside in designated rural settlements (Mayukwayukwa, Meheba, Mantapala), as the initial government policy was to house refugees in sparsely populated areas of the country. Based on the 2017 Refugee Act, restrictions on movement outside of the settlements exist, and permission to work must be granted, though livelihood support is available through the Refugee Fund [28]. In 2024, a National Refugee Policy was developed which restated the responsibilities of government, including for access to social services and financial support for refugees, with the government pooling resources, but there being clear resource sharing among relevant stakeholders in place [29]. Zambia has made pledges under the GCR in 2019 in relation to the facilitation of documentation and naturalisation for refugees, and the promotion of refugee inclusion in health, education, sports, protection and agriculture.

Evolution of Healthcare Response for Refugees

In this section, we describe how healthcare provision for refugees has evolved over time in each setting. Figure 2 provides a summary overview.

Evolution of healthcare response for refugees across settings.

Kenya

Healthcare provision for refugees in camps and settlements in Kenya has been historically organised and managed by international actors, including UNHCR (which act as fund holder for about 50% of funding for healthcare provision in the two Turkana study sites) and through implementing agencies (national and international NGOs) for service delivery.

This approach has undergone a (slow) process of revision since 2021, in line with the new legislative frameworks, towards increased integration with the national health systems. While integration of health service delivery in Kakuma and Kalobeyei is still in its initial phases, respondents noted that progress has been made in some areas. For example, facilities in the camps and settlement are now under county ownership, registered and fully accredited for the SHIF, and operating as public facilities under the umbrella of an MoU between the county and UNHCR. Facilities are therefore included in some of the public systems: they receive funding from county government through the Health Facility Improvement Act, and receive drugs, vaccines and other supplies from national vertical programmes. Other drugs however are still procured using UNHCR or NGOs' systems, as an attempted shift to procurement through the Kenya Medical Supplies Authority (KEMSA) was put on hold in late 2022 after an audit revealed gaps in KEMSA's quality assurance. Similarly, while the MoH‐UNHCR MoU envisages that the county should take over health staff management, this has not yet happened due to the difficulties that the county government faces in recruiting and retaining health personnel and their regular payment. Some facilities rely on ‘incentive staff’ contracted locally, often from the refugee community. However, issues remain in relation to a full integration of refugee health staff as their prior qualifications are not recognised by Kenyan authorities and they do not have the right to formally work [19]. Development funding is playing an increasingly relevant role in supporting healthcare for refugees and host communities, and the integration process. In particular, the World Bank has launched in early 2025 a 215m USD project, which includes 40m USD for Garissa and Turkana counties, sourced from the IDA20 Window for Host Communities and Refugees to support integration with a focus on the heath sector.

In Nairobi, the inclusion of refugees into the national health system has happened since 2008 (with a transition period until 2013), when parallel facilities run by UNHCR were closed and their staff seconded to public facilities serving refugees and host communities. The support to those health facilities was gradually scaled down and ceased in 2013. Since then, UNHCR has advocated for refugees to access services in public facilities, while supporting the most vulnerable to pay user fees where applicable (i.e., for secondary and tertiary care). However, challenges remain in relation to documentation and identification issues, as well as lack of clarity on systems, procedures, and reporting for refugees [30].

In both rural and urban settings, an important achievement towards refugee inclusion was the registration of 8511 vulnerable urban refugee households into NHIF in 2015, and in 2023 1500 households from the host population and 12,420 refugee households from Kalobeyei/Kakuma. With the rollout of the new SHIF, UNHCR has played an advocacy role in solving the technical issues so that refugee documents are now accepted. Despite this, refugees continue to encounter difficulties in accessing services through the SHIF, largely because refugee data is not linked to the Integrated Population Registration System (IPRS) which is used for proxy means testing to determine monthly premiums. Additionally, SHIF's implementation is highly contested and marred with politically‐driven issues affecting Kenyans and refugees alike [31].

Kurdistan Region of Iraq

At the beginning of the Syrian crisis in 2011, the healthcare response for refugees took an emergency approach with the establishment of service provision within refugee camps. In contrast to other settings, the KRG played a key role, alongside humanitarian agencies, in responding to the influx of refugees and in addressing their healthcare (and other social) needs and has allocated substantial financial resources. During the early years of the refugee crisis, healthcare services for refugees were primarily managed through a multi‐stakeholder approach involving the General Directorate of Health (DoH), UNHCR, WHO, and UNICEF, in collaboration with international NGOs. Primary healthcare centres (PHCCs) were established within refugee camps to provide vaccinations, maternal and child healthcare, and chronic disease management, and a key informant described the health services provided for the Syrian refugees during this phase at ‘5‐star standards’. For referral services, refugees were granted free access to public hospitals. The coordinated approach contributed to social cohesion by addressing the healthcare needs of refugees, IDPs, and host communities, and contributed to preventing tensions between different population groups and conflicts over resources. Trust in the healthcare system was also reinforced.

As the crisis moved to a more protracted situation and international funding declined, starting in 2015, the DoH gradually assumed greater responsibility for refugee healthcare and took over the management of health facilities within refugee camp. At this stage, discussions on how to avoid parallel services and move towards a more integrated approach started, which led to external partners to start building PHCCs near refugee camps (rather than within), signalling a shift towards integration. In 2017, four of such clinics accessible by both hosts and refugees were built in Domiz, Darashakran, Qushtapa, Gawilan areas, and the expansion of public PHCCs continued until 2019. During this transition phase, access to primary and secondary care for refugees and hosts was strengthened. UNHCR continued to supply essential medicines to facilities serving refugees and to support a cost‐sharing scheme for referral care, while free access to PHCCs for refugees continued.

From 2020, the KRG and partners formally adopted an integrated healthcare strategy, phasing out the facilities within camps (which continued until 2024) and strengthening the local healthcare provision by supporting existing facilities or building new ones, with funding from the World Bank's sub‐window for Host Communities and Refugees and the US Department of State's Bureau of Population, Refugees and Migration (BPRM). In mid‐2023, UNHCR ended its direct support of camp‐to‐hospital emergency referrals, and refugees in camps transitioned to using the national emergency system for ambulances. By the end of 2024, the transition to full integration of health system for refugees was complete. PHCCs in camps no longer exist and the local health system has been strengthened, allowing nearby public clinics to take over the caseload. Primary care services are universally available and free for refugees and hosts and targeted support from external partners remain in place only to support quality of care (e.g., addressing drug stock outs), secondary care and access for the most vulnerable, while the majority of refugees navigate the health system in a manner nearly identical to host communities. UNHCR also continues to play an advocacy role in relation to refugee inclusion in areas that require progress (e.g., concerning the inclusion of refugee health workers). Key informants stressed that the inclusion of refugees into the national health system has contributed to greater social cohesion, addressing potential inequalities and avoiding tensions and resentment and reinforcing perceptions of fairness.

Mauritania

With the influx of Malian refugees into eastern Mauritania in 2012, the Belgian section of Médecins Sans Frontiers (MSF) deployed to the region for an emergency response in order to cater for the health needs of refugees and contribute to the construction of the M'bera camp. MSF built and ran the camp facilities, providing a comprehensive range of services. Referrals for secondary care would be made to local public hospitals, where MSF ran surgical units. All services were provided free to refugees and the host population, who were allowed to access MSF‐supported facilities. While this approach worked well from a health outcomes, utilisation and quality of care perspective, the decision for MSF to withdraw was made in 2018, due to lack of funding and the need for prioritisation. Eventually, it was decided to allow the take‐over by an international NGO, rather than a direct hand‐over to the government, as there was a concern that the MoH would have not been able to sustain the same level of care and manage facilities directly. The NGO ALIMA (Alliance for International Medical Action) agreed to handle the transition. Resources and equipment, including a warehouse and remaining drugs and supply, were left in place by MSF. Since 2018, healthcare provision for refugees in M'bera was taken over by ALIMA (with funding from the European Civil Protection and Humanitarian Aid Operations (ECHO)), whose mandate was to facilitate a transition to government healthcare provision, by supporting MoH authorities at district and regional levels and strengthening capacities.

As planned, in September 2020, the integration of healthcare in M'bera camp with the national health system started. This process fits within a broader process of integration of refugees in Mauritania that dates to 2018 [22], but also was seen by many during interviews as addressing the financial and political unsustainability of the parallel approach. Politically, frustrations amongst Mauritanian nationals have been common since the construction of the camp, with perceptions of better quality services being available there compared with the rest of the region and an imbalance between services provided by international organisations in the camp and those available within poverty‐stricken and under‐resourced host communities, which in turn impacted the social cohesion between communities [22]. Financially, supporting the parallel delivery of healthcare in M'bera has been made increasingly difficult by the limited resources of humanitarian actors after the departure of MSF. Integration was made more urgent by the 2023–2024 arrivals and their ‘out‐of‐camp’ settlement that put pressures on the limited resources of the host communities and heightened the imperative for a levelling up of the health services available outside of the camp. The process of inclusion of refugees in the national health care system has been facilitated through a health system strengthening programme of the World Bank, called INAYA, which has been extended to cover the south‐eastern regions of Mauritania with high poverty levels, where M'bera is located. The additional financing was leveraged through the IDA18 Regional Sub‐window for Refugees and Host Communities, and allowed for coverage of both refugees and host communities, thus facilitating the integration process.

Since 2020, the integration process has made slow but steady progress. A first step has been the transfer of the ownership of camp health facilities to the MoH. The process was facilitated by the operational and financial autonomy (and additional performance‐based funding) granted to facilities through INAYA. Because of their better performance, facilities in the camp receive and manage more funding than the average in the region and are able to hire contractual staff, thus retaining health workers previously employed by NGOs as well as from the refugee community, while also having civil servants deployed. Despite the ongoing health system integration, stark differences remain in the services accessed within the refugee camp, including in terms of coverage, extent of the benefit package and quality of care, and those outside of the camp. Another difference is the cost of healthcare, which was free for all refugees at the time of data collection (February 2024) while outside of camp patients are charged user fees with few exemptions.

While the majority of refugees are settled in the Hodh Ech‐Chargui region, the process of inclusion of refugees within the national health system has a bearing upon urban refugees as well. While there has never been a parallel approach to the provision of services for urban refugees, the approach has remained somewhat different from that of the general (Mauritanian) population. In particular, while healthcare services are free at the point of use for refugees in selected facilities, refugees face barriers in accessing good quality services and referrals, and refugees are not yet fully included in the insurance mechanisms that are being implemented in Mauritania, including the recently established Caisse Nationale de Solidarite en Santé (CNASS).

Pakistan

Until 2014, UNHCR worked closely with the Pakistan government to provide healthcare for Afghan refugees, focusing on service delivery within refugee camps. Largely, the healthcare response for refugees relied on UNHCR and other external agencies establishing Basic Health Units (BHUs) in refugee camps or villages.

Since 2014, due to donor fatigue and shifting priorities that led to reduce long‐term funding, there has been a strategic change, moving away from a parallel approach to healthcare provision and a gradual reduction in support to BHUs. This has forced the government to absorb refugee healthcare into the national system, but without the sufficient resources and the legal and coordination frameworks in place for sustainable inclusion. Registered Afghan refugees are allowed to use Pakistan's healthcare services at no cost where available (although they are still not allowed to join health insurance schemes), and external agencies have shifted to supporting the health system more generally with investments in areas with high refugee population. At the time of data collection, registered Afghan refugees had free access to (limited) national health services in Pakistan comparable to Pakistani nationals, mostly including national vaccination programme, tuberculosis control, and HIV prevention and treatment, while other critical services such as maternal and mental health remain underdeveloped. Secondary and tertiary care was available in local hospital, based on user fee payments. As a consequence, refugees shared the same challenges in coverage, financial barriers and quality of care that Pakistani nationals also face, due to Pakistan's growing population and severe economic constraints. In addition, access for refugees was further limited by documentation requirements.

During COVID‐19, in April 2020, Pakistan initiated a relief package for Afghans. The vaccination programme expanded to include registered Afghans and foreigners, with Afghan refugees receiving their first doses by early May 2021. Initially, undocumented Afghans were excluded, but in August 2021, the Sindh Health Department allowed them to register for vaccination on‐site at Mass Vaccination Centres. In November 2021, after the Taliban takeover in Afghanistan, the Prime Minister declared that Pakistan would offer free COVID‐19 vaccines to all Afghans entering the country.

Peru

In the initial stages of the Venezuelan displacement in Peru (2018–2022), service delivery for refugees and migrants was supported by NGOs or other service providers contracted by humanitarian agencies to provide services directly. During this time, UNHCR and IOM acted as fund pooling agents for external funders, by paying public and private health facilities. In addition, healthcare costs of individuals are subsidised through unconditional cash transfers, such as the IOM's Cash Based Intervention programme, focused on vulnerable Venezuelans [32].

Following the initial emergency response, once it became clear that more refugees and migrants would enter the country and would remain for a prolonged period, the healthcare response approach shifted, increasingly directing patients towards accessing the national health system (generally via the SIS although Venezuelans in work can also be covered by EsSalud). This second, transitional phase lasted between 2022 and 2023, with a move away from direct provision of health services by external organisations towards health system strengthening benefiting both host communities and displaced populations. However, relatively quickly, the healthcare response approach moved to a third phase, which started in 2024, when refugee inclusion in the national health system is still the prevalent approach, alongside a focus on legal and policy changes. Some services, such as those for pregnant women, young children and strategic intervention services are free at the point of use and do not require specific documentation for access. However, for the majority of Venezuelan inclusion in healthcare is envisaged to be mostly achieved though their enrolment into the existing social health insurance schemes. SIS, in particular, which does not require premium payment as costs are covered by the Peruvian Government, has been expanded in 2019 to cover anyone not covered by other schemes, although documentation requirements remained in place until 2024. While the inclusion of refugees in the national health system has undoubtedly been facilitated by the existence of quasi‐universal social health insurance mechanisms, challenges remain in particular in relation to the required documentation for enrolment. As a consequence, data from 2022 show that 73% of Venezuelans in Peru lack health insurance [33]. However, this is an improvement from the 2018 situation when only 8% of Venezuelans was enroled in an insurance scheme [34]. Additional challenges relate to the low capacity within existing governmental systems to take into account displaced populations and their needs. Key informants reflected, for example, on the fact that the public finance management system still does not account for migrants and refugees in the resource allocation formula, which is based on information that is not updated. Migrants and refugees are also not well captured in policy, regulations and guidelines, or within the decision‐making structures.

With the shift to an integrated approach to healthcare response for displaced populations, the support of external actors is focused on sustainability and advocacy, by contributing to develop or review relevant regulations, influencing legislation and policies and reinforcing the capacities of stakeholders at all levels. However, some international partners are still supporting or managing direct provision of healthcare services for refugees, although now with more attention to coordination and referrals pathways.

Zambia

Zambia presents a slightly different case from those described above, due to the relatively low number of refugees, the long timeframe and slow pace of arrivals, with large numbers of refugees either returning to their countries of origin or settling permanently in Zambia. Responsibility for the healthcare of refugees settled in rural villages falls under the purview of the MoH. At decentralised levels, this means that responsibility for direct care delivery for refugees falls to district health offices and is supported by UNHCR through the Office of the Commissioner for Refugees (COR) at the Ministry for Home Affairs.

A humanitarian approach to healthcare response for refugees with UNHCR's coordination and NGO service provision has happened in times of peak influx of refugees—and continues to happen during emergencies, due to the lack of capacities of the national health system to respond to such crises. However, respondents noted that the approach regularly transitions back to full government responsibility after the emergencies, when external actors, such as UNHCR, focus on advocacy and advisory functions. While this approach (shifting to external support in times of crisis and transitioning back to government after) allows for rapid resource mobilisation and effective emergency response, as well as ensuring government ownership and stewardship in the longer term, informants noted that it has led to a loss of capacity at local levels, and reduction in staffing, infrastructure and resources, thus negatively affecting service delivery for refugees.

In the post‐emergency phases, health services are delivered via the public health system in facilities staffed by national health staff, and financed via centralised funding mechanisms (the Refugee Fund, which includes funding from the Zambia government, as well as donations, grants and investments). This means that services for refugees are the same as for Zambians. PHC services are accessed for free, and referrals for refugees are made similarly to those for Zambians. UNHCR assures ambulance access for referral for the settlement communities, also extending this service to host communities. Challenges still exist for both host and refugee communities, given limitations in resources for the public health system—particularly as relates to medication and medical supply availability, especially in rural areas. In addition, key informants noted other issues concerning refugees specifically, in relation to legal access to care. Zambia has recently introduced a National Health Insurance scheme [14], and UNHCR has advocated for an MoU with the insurance agency that would see UNHCR paying premium contributions for those refugees who would not be able to pay for themselves. However, implementation was just starting at the time of data collection.

Discussion

This paper set out to analyse the trajectory in healthcare response for refugees and the emergence of health system integration policies and practices at country level in order to understand how contextual features played a role in shaping the evolution of the healthcare response for refugees, with a focus on exploring both enablers and barriers for a shift towards health system integration. Importantly, while we explore contextual drivers that are within and beyond the health sector, our emphasis is on understanding the trajectory of the healthcare response specifically, and not of the broader integration processes, although of course the first happens in the context of the latter, as we reflect on below. Additionally, we acknowledge that health system integration is the normative, and somewhat inescapable, pathway for the response, as defined by global frameworks such as the GCR. The question therefore is about the pace of the shift towards integration, its operationalisation that is its going beyond a change on paper, and effectiveness in addressing the needs of both refugee and host communities.

The contextual descriptions presented above for each setting in turn foreshadow important differences in the health system integration trajectories, and through a comparative lens they highlight contextual drivers that contribute to explain those trajectories. It appears that the overall trajectory has been similar across settings (especially for the early phases) and defined by the normative pathway defined in global frameworks, though timeframes and end‐points differ in each setting. Trajectories often start with the emergency response, characterised by a predominantly humanitarian approach and the setup of parallel systems to address the healthcare needs of refugees, and then move to a transitional, hybrid phase where direct provision by humanitarian agencies is progressively reduced in favour of the involvement of local health authorities, and finally evolve towards the inclusion of refugees in national health systems—at least in some cases, although not in all. Notably, Kenya and Pakistan have not completed this transition, and in Mauritania it remains embryonic. Exceptionally, Zambia presents a cyclical pattern, where the humanitarian response is regularly reintroduced to deal with new arrivals, then followed by a transition to integrated, government provision of care once the peak of displacement has passed.

While the high‐level trajectories in healthcare response approaches are similar, the detailed elements, such as the pace of the change and its end‐point, and their contextual drivers are context‐specific. The comparative analysis of the six cases reveals patterns in the evolution of the healthcare response over time, and allows us to identify facilitators and barriers in the move towards effective health system integration, which we discuss below.

An element that plays an important role in shaping the trajectory of the healthcare response is the scale and pace of migration flow. In cases where the influx is rapid and substantial (e.g., in sparsely‐populated Mauritania, where the M'bera refugee camp is the third largest city), the burden of the healthcare for refugees is impossible to shoulder for the local government in the short term. On the other hand, where refugees arrive at a slower pace, gradual inclusion is more feasible ‐ technically, financially and politically. Zambia provides an example of this, where the emergency approach is restricted to moments of large influx, but these are of short duration, reflecting the fact that refugees have historically arrived in relatively low numbers and in waves, and that there exist feasible voluntary repatriation and naturalisation options.

However, this simple link is mediated by other contextual factors that drive policy trajectories of health system integration. In spite of common financial constraints, higher national income levels appear to indicate better technical, financial and health system capacity to manage the strain that comes with increased populations and appear to play a role in shaping the trajectory of the healthcare response. For example, KRI and Peru (which are upper middle‐income countries) were able to sustain the inclusion of refugees more rapidly, although in a manner not devoid of challenges. Reduced external funding contributed to the triggering of health system integration processes in most of our settings.

The case of Peru also illustrates how the rapid pace and substantial scale of displacement can lead to worsened public perceptions and increasingly hostile narratives around refugees. The political salience of migration in Peru meant that policies evolved to be less receptive and inclusive of migrants and refugees over time, though progress towards inclusion into national health systems has still been made. This is not dissimilar to the case of Jordan, documented in the broader literature, where initially open and inclusive policies have shifted to a more restrictive approach to refugee inclusions, following persistent influxes, prolonged displacement, negative public discourses, and reduced external funding [35]. Equally, security issues, such as in Pakistan and in Kenya, have slowed down the integration process including for the health system. With reference to Kenya, others have similarly argued that the CRRF's implementation has been promoted in Turkana (where the majority of refugees is from South Sudan), while put on hold in Dadaab (predominantly hosting Somali refugees), due to security concerns following terrorist attacks and to the political and military tensions between Kenya and Somalia [36]. In contrast, in other settings such as Mauritania and KRI, negative perceptions have been mitigated by ethnic and cultural affinity between refugee and host communities which have allowed space for solidarity to be upheld and facilitated refugee inclusion.

Unquestionably, the evolution of legal frameworks, particularly on key refugee rights to documentation, movement and work and the societal integration processes, has a bearing on health system integration, and vice versa as the processes are closely interlinked, as confirmed by the theoretical and empirical literature [37, 38, 39]. In Mauritania and KRI, the sectoral integration processes had synergistic effects on resilience and social cohesion, while inclusive policies facilitated service integration [40]. Similarly, in Kenya multisectoral integration processes including societal, legal, political and administrative integration are mutually reinforcing. Administrative integration through the municipalisation of refugee camps emerged as particularly important as it created the structure for the transfer of responsibility for the provision of social services to the local government.

Global level dynamics and the engagement of traditionally development‐focused funders are found to be a key contextual enabler and have played a critical role in promoting the health system integration agenda [2]. Similarly, at country level we also noted that the increased involvement of development actors (which are more used to operating through governmental channels rather than parallel systems) has been critical to support integration processes. In particular, the World Bank's funding through the Window for Host Communities and Refugees has emerged as a facilitator of integration in the health sector in Mauritania, KRI and Kenya. In all settings, leveraging this funding was essential to support the strengthening of the local health system to expand its capacity to respond to increase in the population served and at the same time improve coverage and quality to address the gap in healthcare services between refugee and host population.

Looking specifically at the health system and service delivery, potential barriers to health system integration are the relative weakness of the local health system, and the resulting gap (in coverage, financial accessibility and quality of services) between refugees settled in camps and local (rural) communities, which is often to the advantage of refugees. The wider the gap (i.e., the weaker the local health system), the more difficult it is to manage the inclusion of refugees and the trade‐offs that this entails. This was the case, for example, in Mauritania and Kenya. Additionally, this gap can impact on social cohesion and create tensions, thus making refugee inclusion more difficult. The conundrum calls for a strong focus on supporting the strengthening of local health systems, as well as suggesting that an earlier planning of health system integration could be more desirable as it might limit the widening of the (real or perceived) gap in healthcare services and create a reinforcing positive loop between inclusion and social cohesion. This appears to be the case in KRI. In Mauritania, the 2023–2024 arrivals that mostly settled ‘out‐of‐camp’, putting pressures on local health services, has highlighted that inclusion is an inescapable necessity, but that it cannot be separated from the strengthening of local health systems.

A further enabler of the inclusion of refugees in national health systems is its universalist approach to healthcare. In privatised, fragmented, inequitable health systems that expect individuals to fend for themselves, inclusion of refugees is possible but it is likely to have negative consequences in terms of equity of access and financial protection. This is the case of urban refugees in Kenya and Mauritania, as well as in other settings documented in the literature, such as Lebanon [41]. On the other hand, the existence of functioning, inclusive social health insurance represents an important facilitator for refugee inclusion processes, through their enrolment in the scheme. In practice, even countries where social health insurance schemes exist, such as Peru, enrolment of displaced population is often low. Legal frameworks, and in particular rights to documentation and employment also critically affect the potential of insurance schemes as an entry‐point to inclusion—in addition to the challenges faced by partially operational insurance mechanisms that affect both refugees and nationals, as illustrated by the cases of Kenya, Pakistan, Zambia and Mauritania.

Limitations

Our study has some important limitations, in particular due to data gaps and difficulties in reaching all potential participants. In relation to the document review, our purposive, exploratory approach might have missed some documents compared to a systematic review approach. However, care was taken in the search strategy to combine academic databases with internet searches and manual searches of key websites, as well as by asking participants to share further documentation. In relation to our methods, the selection of study sites was made in consultation with UNHCR country offices, which might have introduced biases. However, we explicitly aimed to ensure representativeness of a variety of situations and cover large numbers of refugees in each setting (sites are often large areas). In addition, the documentary review in each country covered all settings where refugees are settled.

While the sampling for interviews and FGDs reflects that envisaged in the protocol, some categories had fewer participants than planned, such as government officials at central level in Mauritania, Kenya and KRI (the latter due to ongoing parliamentary elections) and refugee and host community representatives (particularly in Kenya, Mauritania and Peru). Their voices were captured through Group Model Building workshops, which are reported on in a forthcoming paper [6] and in the country case studies in this Special Issue. Sampling was also largely mediated by contacts provided by the UNHCR country office and iteratively by previous informants, which might have introduced a bias. Finally, the case studies were carried out before the major shift in funding environment with the Stop Work Order and the subsequent funding cuts from the US government in early 2025, so we are not able to reflect on the impact of that shift.

Conclusions

This article examined how and why countries move towards integration of refugee healthcare into national systems, filling a gap in literature which has focused more on normative and global discourses. It finds that our hypotheses on facilitators and barriers were supported by the six case studies. Factors favouring integration included ones relating to: refugee flows (lower numbers and more gradual arrivals); countries' economic situations (higher country income status); social and cultural affinity between host population and refugees; health system characteristics (whether inclusive policies were already structured into the system for the local population, and where there are smaller differences in the extent and quality of care offered to hosts and refugees); and international actor behaviours (reduced aid funding as an impetus to integration, along with promotion of this approach normatively). However, none of these are mechanistic and political leadership can influence discourse to promote an integrated approach or create divisive narratives. In a parallel paper, we take this discussion forward by examining the process of integration and drawing lessons on health sector strategies and trade‐offs, recognising the pressure that refugee hosting countries are under. This pressure is only likely to increase, meaning that lesson‐sharing is more important than ever, as will be international support to countries as they move toward more sustainable models of refugee support.
