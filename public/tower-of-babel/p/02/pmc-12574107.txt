Neural mechanisms of suicide thoughts and behaviors in major depressive disorder: abnormal regional brain activity and its functional connectivity
Authors: Yifan Jing, Meng Zhang, Yuan Liu, Ying Gao, Bin Zhang, Chengfeng Chen, Zaimina Xuekelaiti, Yan Zhou, Changlin Zhang, Meijuan Li, Jie Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicide thoughts and behaviors (STB), including suicidal ideation (SI) and suicide attempts (SA), are significant concerns in major depressive disorder (MDD), yet their neurobiological mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study aims to identify key regional brain activity and connectivity abnormalities associated with STB in MDD by combining a meta-analysis of regional brain activity comparing MDD patients with STB to non-STB controls (both MDD without STB and healthy controls) and an exploratory functional connectivity (FC) analysis in an independent sample of MDD patients.MethodsA meta-analysis employing Seed-based d Mapping with Permutation of Subject Images (SDM-PSI) software identified regional brain abnormalities. Studies included were those comparing MDD patients with STB to non-STB controls, employing resting-state fMRI with whole-brain analyses, using ALFF, fALFF, and ReHo metrics. The identified abnormal brain regions were used as regions of interest (ROIs) for FC analyses in 57 first-episode, drug-naive MDD patients.ResultsThe meta-analysis included 12 studies (13 datasets) comprising 555 MDD patients with STB and 430 non-STB controls. Compared to non-STB controls, MDD-STB patients showed increased activity in the right middle occipital gyrus (MOG) and right inferior frontal gyrus, triangular part (IFGtriang), while decreased activity in right precuneus. Subgroup analysis of SA revealed increased activity in the left angular gyrus in MDD patients with SA, compared to non-SA controls. SI subgroup analysis and two medication status subgroup analyses showed no significant results. In independent sample, FC analysis yielded two significant FCs after Bonferroni correction. Correlation analysis showed a negative association between right MOG-IFGtriang FC and most severe SI scores measured by the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (P = 0.04), though it was non-significant after correction.ConclusionsThese findings provide novel insights into the neural mechanisms of STB in MDD, identifying specific brain regions and FC patterns associated with STB. These results align with prior studies, highlighting the role of visual processing and cognitive control regions in STB. By combining a meta-analysis of regional abnormalities with an exploratory FC analysis, this study offers a comprehensive understanding of the brain networks implicated in STB and suggests potential targets for future interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07483-y.

Introduction

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a prevalent global mental health condition, impacting over 300 million individuals worldwide [1, 2]. Within MDD, individuals face a heightened risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STB; encompassing suicidal ideation (SI) and suicide attempts (SA)), although the relationship between these manifestations remains complex and may involve distinct risk factors [3, 4]. The lifetime prevalence of SI and SA in MDD patients were reported to be as high as 37.7% and 31%, respectively [5, 6]. Given these alarming statistics, there is an urgent need to identify objective biomarkers for the early recognition of STB in MDD.

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), can identify potential markers of STB by investigating the neurobiological basis [7]. This presents valuable targets for future interventions and is progressively emerging as a crucial approach to probing the STB in MDD [8]. Both region of interest (ROI)-based functional connectivity (FC) analysis and whole-brain analysis are commonly used methods in fMRI studies [9, 10]. Previous fMRI studies based on ROI analysis have reported numerous STB-related findings in MDD, with a recent meta-analysis highlighting the amygdala’s structural and functional connectivity [11–15]. This method examines FC abnormalities within brain circuits or networks, and may not reflect regional brain activity abnormalities in MDD with STB [16]. As a supplement, whole-brain analysis can assess abnormalities in regional brain areas across the entire brain, which is also a common way to select ROIs for subsequent hypothesis-driven FC analyses [17, 18].

Although whole-brain studies have reported numerous abnormal brain regions in MDD with STB, findings remain inconsistent, with variations in both brain region locations and the direction of activity changes persisting [19–29]. These contradictions, which may stem from factors such as small sample sizes, varying patient age ranges, clinical heterogeneity, and different methodological approaches, highlight the need for quantitative whole-brain-based meta-analyses [10, 30]. Although some meta-analyses of whole-brain fMRI studies have investigated STB in MDD, they either combine MDD with bipolar disorder (BD) or focus on task-based fMRI paradigms, introducing additional heterogeneity [31, 32]. Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), as a task-free method, offers more reliable and consistent measurements of brain activity and connectivity [33]. A recent meta-analysis published in April 2025, with a literature search cutoff in December 2023, examined rs-fMRI abnormalities related to STB in MDD [34]. However, it combined static and dynamic metrics with distinct neural implications, introducing substantial heterogeneity, and included correlational studies without group comparisons, violating the assumptions of coordinate-based meta-analysis and limiting spatial convergence [35]. Therefore, a rigorously designed rs-fMRI meta-analysis that includes only static metrics and between-group comparisons is needed to identify convergent abnormalities in regional brain areas associated with STB in MDD.

In rs-fMRI studies, amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF), and regional homogeneity (ReHo) are commonly used static voxel-wise measures of intrinsic brain activity [36, 37]. ALFF quantifies the amplitude of low-frequency blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) fluctuations, reflecting the intensity of spontaneous neural activity at the voxel level, but is sensitive to physiological noise [38]. fALFF improves specificity by normalizing low-frequency power to the full frequency range, thereby reducing non-neuronal influences [39]. ReHo, in contrast, measures the temporal synchronization between a voxel and its neighbors, capturing local coherence rather than signal intensity [40]. While all three characterize regional spontaneous brain activity, ALFF and fALFF focus on signal strength, whereas ReHo emphasizes local synchrony. Their complementary strengths offer a more comprehensive depiction of regional intrinsic brain function [41].

Numerous meta-analyses have successfully combined these metrics to explore regional brain activity abnormalities in various psychiatric and neurological disorders, including MDD, anxiety, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia, highlighting the robustness of this approach [42–45]. For instance, one meta-analysis in MDD identified reduced regional activity in the left cuneus and right middle occipital gyrus [42]. Similarly, in a meta-analysis examining suicidality, increased activity in the bilateral superior temporal gyrus, left middle temporal gyrus, and bilateral middle occipital gyrus has been particularly emphasized [46]. Nonetheless, as individual rs-fMRI studies often involve modest sample sizes, the meta-analytic results may be influenced disproportionately by larger datasets. This potential limitation should be considered when interpreting generalizability.

Given the complementary strengths of whole-brain voxel-wise and ROI-based approaches, the present study adopts a two-stage exploratory framework. First, we conduct a coordinate-based meta-analysis with rigorous inclusion criteria to identify convergent regional abnormalities in MDD patients with STB, based on static rs-fMRI metrics (ALFF, fALFF, and ReHo). Building on these findings, the second stage examines FC, which reflects synchronized activity between brain regions based on BOLD signal correlations, thereby offering insight into network-level organization [47]. A common approach is to use regions with robust local abnormalities as seeds to explore circuit-level dysfunction. However, few studies have specifically investigated FC between meta-analytically defined ROIs. We therefore perform an exploratory FC analysis among these regions in an independent sample of first-episode, drug-naive MDD patients. This combined approach allows for a more integrated understanding of both regional and network-level abnormalities associated with STB in MDD.

Methods

Meta-analysis

Literature search and selection criteria

The meta-analysis followed the PRISMA guidelines [48] and the protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420250652880). We performed an extensive and systematic search across multiple databases, including PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science, using the following terms: “suicide*” AND “depress*” AND (“fMRI” OR “MRI, Functional” OR “Functional MRI” OR “Functional MRIs” OR “MRIs, Functional” OR “Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging” OR “Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Functional”). Independently, two authors (ZY and ZCL) searched for all papers published up to January 2, 2025, on this given topic.

The studies selected for this meta-analysis adhered to the following criteria: (1) published in English-language, peer-reviewed journals; (2) rs-fMRI studies based on whole brain for ALFF, fALFF or ReHo analysis; (3) patients were diagnosed with MDD through the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth or Fifth Edition (DSM-IV or DSM-5) (4) comparison of differences in brain activity between MDD patients with STB (MDD patients with SI or a history of SA) and non-STB control subjects (MDD patients without SI and a history of SA, or healthy controls); (5) explicit reporting of coordinates in standard stereotactic space (Montreal Neurological Institute [MNI] space or Talaraich [Tal] space) at the level of the whole brain. Studies were omitted under the following conditions: (1) subjects had any known co-morbid neurologic disease or head trauma; (2) there was a lack of a non-STB control group; (3) three-dimensional coordinates of the stereotactic space were unavailable (4) type of STB not specified to be SA, SI, or both. All search results were independently assessed by two researchers (ZY and ZCL) for possible inclusion in the meta-analysis. Conflicting opinions were discussed, and unresolved cases were adjudicated by a third researcher (JYF).

Data extraction

Two researchers (XZ and ZY) independently extracted peak coordinates and effect sizes that differed between MDD patients with STB and non-STB controls, along with clinical characteristics (sample size, sex, age, scale scores, comorbidities, illness duration, and medication status etc.) and imaging information (analysis method and multiple comparison correction thresholds), with any disputes resolved by a third researcher (JYF). Each comparison of a STB type subgroup with non-STB controls was treated as a separate dataset. The meta-analysis ultimately incorporated 12 studies, comprising 13 datasets. One study with two comparisons (MDD patients with SA vs. non-STB MDD, MDD patients with SI vs. non-STB MDD) was split into two datasets [49]. In the current meta-analysis, non-STB controls included both MDD patients without STB and healthy controls (HC). Among the included studies, 3 studies compared MDD-STB with HC only, while 9 studies (10 databases) compared MDD-STB with both non-STB MDD patients and HC groups. When both types of controls were used, we prioritized non-STB MDD patients as the control group to reduce potential confounding factors, especially those related to HC. And the detailed selection of the control group for each article is listed in Supplementary Table 1. Regarding rs-fMRI metrics, six studies (seven datasets) exclusively used ALFF, two studies used only fALFF, and one study focused solely on ReHo. Additionally, two studies employed both ALFF and ReHo, and one study combined fALFF with ReHo. Detailed information on the inclusion of each metric is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Voxel-wise meta-analysis

Using the SDM-PSI software (version 6.21, available at https://www.sdmproject.com/), a voxel-wise meta-analysis with anisotropic effect size seeding was conducted to evaluate the regional brain activity differences between MDD patients with STB and non-STB controls. The core principle of SDM-PSI involves utilizing peak coordinates and effect size t-values from each study to reconstruct brain maps, which are then used to compare effect sizes in the aggregated results [50]. We followed the detailed steps outlined in the SDM-PSI guidelines and previous literature [51]. First, the extracted text file containing peak coordinates and peak-t values was loaded into SDM. A SDM table was then generated, incorporating demographic and clinical data. During pre-processing, a 20 mm anisotropic FWHM and 2 mm voxel-sized grayscale mask rolls were applied, enabling the software to reconstruct standard MNI maps for each study [35]. The mean plot was ultimately derived by calculating the random-effects means of the study plots at the voxel level, weighted by sample size and within-study variance [52]. To visualize the SDM-PSI maps, we utilized the BrainNet Viewer package (www.nitrc.org/projects/bnv/). For the above analyses, thresholds of voxel-level uncorrected P < 0.005 and cluster size >100 voxels were applied to achieve an optimal balance of sensitivity and specificity [50]. Additionally, all analyses included sex and age as covariates to control for their potential confounding effects.

Analyses of sensitivity and publication bias

To assess the robustness of the findings, a jackknife sensitivity analysis was conducted [53]. In each iteration, the results of one article were systematically excluded, while all remaining articles were retained for repeated meta-analysis. The outcomes were then compared with the original results to assess reproducibility. Using peak coordinates from the pooled meta-analyses, publication bias was evaluated through Egger’s test [54]. A P-value greater than 0.05 in Egger’s test indicated the absence of significant publication bias [55].

Subgroup analyses and meta-regression analysis

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression analysis were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. First, the datasets were stratified by STB type (SA and SI) to comprehensively characterize regional brain activity abnormalities in MDD patients with STB, with the STB types being determined as per the original groupings in the source studies. Second, to account for medication status, we divided the studies into two groups based on medication status: Medication-naïve group (studies that only included patients who had never used medication) and Medicated group (studies that included patients who had either used medication in the past or were currently medicated). Meta-regression analyses were additionally carried out on the pooled dataset to assess the potential confounding influence of Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAMD) scores. A more stringent statistical threshold (P < 0.0005) was applied in the meta-regression analyses to minimize the risk of identifying false-positive associations [56].

FC analysis in an independent sample of first-episode drug-naïve MDD patients

Participants

A total of 57 first-episode drug-naive MDD patients, aged 18 to 55, were recruited for FC analysis in this study. MDD patients were diagnosed based on DSM-5 criteria, with diagnoses systematically assessed and confirmed by two experienced psychiatrists using the Structured Clinical Interview (SCID). Exclusion criteria comprised a history of antidepressant or antipsychotic use, prior MDD episodes, comorbid psychiatric or neurological conditions, pregnancy, substance use disorders, and MRI contraindications. Prior to participation, all individuals signed written informed consent, and the study protocol was formally approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Anding Hospital.

Clinical assessment

The Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI) was administered to assess the severity of suicidal thoughts, where participants were specifically asked to report the intensity of their suicidal ideation at its most severe point in their lifetime. Higher scores indicate greater severity of suicidal thoughts [57].

MRI data acquisition and preprocessing

On the same day as the BSSI assessment, the study utilized a 3 T Siemens Prisma scanner (Erlangen, Germany) for MRI data acquisition. For functional imaging, participants tried their best to keep their eyes closed, awake, and avoid moving heads. The parameters for acquiring resting-state fMRI and high-resolution T1-weighted images were consistent with those detailed in our earlier publication [58].

For preprocessing, the Brain Imaging Data Processing and Analysis (DPABI V8.1, http://rfmri.org/ dpabi) [59] and the Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12) toolbox (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) were used. A comprehensive description of preprocessing steps is provided in Supplement 1.

ROI based FC analysis

ROIs were constructed as 10-mm-radius spheres centered on the peak coordinates of abnormal regional brain activity regions from the meta-analysis [60]. The 10-mm radius was chosen because previous studies that applied this parameter in FC analysis have demonstrated its effectiveness in capturing relevant brain activity while minimizing overlap with adjacent regions [60–62]. The BOLD time courses of voxels within each ROI were correlated with those of all other ROIs to calculate correlation coefficients. The resulting correlation maps (r-values) were subsequently transformed into z-scores using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation. Significant FC was identified through one-sample t-tests. The Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons, and statistical significance was determined based on the corrected P-value provided by the software. Age, sex, and mean framewise displacement (FD) were included as covariates.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were reported using means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were described in terms of proportions. FC values identified as significant from one-sample t-tests were extracted and further examined for partial correlations with BSSI scores using IBM SPSS 25.0. Covariates, including age, sex, and mean framewise displacement (FD), were controlled for in the analysis. The Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons, with statistical significance defined as a corrected P-value below 0.05/n, where n is the number of comparisons.

Analyses of sensitivity, publication bias and meta-regression analyses

As shown in Supplementary Table 2, in pooled analyses, Jackknife sensitivity assessment revealed that abnormal elevations of regional brain activity in the right MOG and right IFGtriang, could be replicated in 11 of 13 tests. The abnormal decrease in regional brain activity within the right precuneus was reproducible in 12 of the 13 tests. The Egger test did not yield significance in any clusters within the pooled meta-analyses (Table 1). Furthermore, no significant linear relationship was identified between abnormal regional brain activity and scores on the HAMD-17.

FC analysis based on ROI and the correlation analysis

Using age, sex, and FD as covariates, a one-sample t-test was conducted to explore the FC between the ROIs. We identified two significant positive FCs: (1) between the right precuneus and right MOG, and (2) between the right MOG and right IFGtriang (Bonferroni-corrected) (Fig. 4). Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of resting-state regional brain activity and FC in MDD patients with STB, incorporating both a meta-analysis of regional abnormalities and an exploratory FC analysis in first-episode, drug-naïve MDD patients. In the meta-analysis that included 12 studies (13 databases), we found that, compared to non-STB controls, MDD patients with STB showed elevated abnormal regional brain activity in the right MOG and right IFGtriang, while showing reduced regional brain activity in the right precuneus. Subgroup analysis of MDD patients with SA showed abnormally elevated regional brain activity in the left ANG compared to non-SA controls. ROI-based FC analysis in first-episode, drug-naïve MDD patients identified two significant FCs, in which the FC of the right MOG-right IFGtriang was negatively correlated with BSSI scores. Our findings elucidate the neural underpinnings of MDD patients with STB, revealing specific regional brain activity and connectivity abnormalities that may serve as potential biomarkers for STB in MDD patients.

Neural mechanisms

Significant regional brain activity abnormalities in MDD patients with STB

Our pooled meta-analysis identified several key regional brain activity abnormalities in MDD patients with STB. Specifically, we found increased regional brain activity in the right MOG compared to non-STB controls. This finding is consistent with the results of a previous meta-analysis that examined brain region abnormalities associated with cross-diagnostic suicidality, which also reported significantly elevated MOG activity in a resting-state fMRI subgroup [46]. Our focus on MDD, coupled with resting-state fMRI studies, further underscores the relevance of the right MOG in the STB risk of MDD. The MOG, a key region in the visual system, supports spatial visual analysis, depth discrimination, and facial feature discrimination [63]. Visual processing abnormalities have been observed in MDD patients and the abnormal MOG activity was found to be associated with cognitive biases in MDD patients [64, 65]. Suicidal patients have shown heightened sensitivity to negative emotional stimuli, including facial expressions, which may be attributed to visual processing dysfunction [66–68]. Therefore, we propose that the elevated regional brain activity in MOG of MDD patients with STB may reflect heightened sensitivity to emotional stimuli, particularly negative emotional information, which could contribute to the heightened STB risk in MDD.

In our pooled meta-analysis analysis, we also observed elevated regional brain activity in the right inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), triangular part, in MDD patients with STB compared to non-STB controls. Previous research in MDD patients has shown heightened IFG activation in response to negative emotional stimuli, suggesting its role in depression [69]. The right IFG, located in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, plays a key role in response inhibition and cognitive control [70–72]. Dysfunction in these functions, particularly impaired response inhibition, is strongly linked to impulsive behaviors and is considered a significant predictor of suicidal tendencies [73–75]. We propose that elevated activity in this region may reflect disrupted self-perception and may contribute to poor impulse control in the face of negative emotions, increasing vulnerability to STB. A recent meta-analysis reported decreased IFG activity in BD and MDD patients with SA, which contrasts with our findings [31]. This discrepancy may be due to the inclusion of BD studies, varying task conditions, and greater heterogeneity in that meta-analysis. Our more focused study, restricted to resting-state fMRI in MDD, offers a clearer, more reliable result, though further validation is needed.

The pooled meta-analysis conducted in this study also revealed a notable decreased regional brain activity within right precuneus in MDD patients with STB. The precuneus serves as a central hub within the default mode network, closely related to rumination and response inhibition functions and involves situational memory extraction and self-processing [76, 77]. Abnormality in the precuneus was reported to reflect deficits in mood control, thought processes, and negative behaviors in suicidally depressed patients [78]. Thus, dysfunction of the precuneus may reflect excessive self-processing and rumination in emotional responses of individuals, which may in turn increase the risk of STB. A previous meta-analysis also revealed an abnormal decrease in precuneus, in agreement with us [31]. Structural and functional changes in the precuneus, including reduced blood flow, gray and white matter volume, and weakened connectivity, have been linked to STB risk [78–81]. In addition, a longitudinal study found elevated precuneus function in MDD patients with SA after treatment, further supporting our findings [82].

Subgroup analyses and meta-regression findings

Subgroup analyses identified elevated regional brain activity in the left ANG in SA subgroup, highlighting its key role in suicidal behavior in MDD patients. The ANG, located in the parietal lobe, is crucial for visual, language, and higher cognitive functions, including social cognition and memory [83–85]. Studies have reported structural and functional changes in the ANG in MDD patients [86–89] with reduced gray matter volume associated with suicidal behavior [90]. A study further suggests that abnormalities in the ANG may increase the vulnerability of suicide behavior in MDD patients by impairing functions such as self-awareness, emotional decision-making, and social cognition [91].

However, no significant findings were observed in the SI subgroup. The small sample size (n = 4) in the SI subgroup likely contributed to the lack of significant results and reduced the statistical power of the analysis. This limitation weakens the study’s ability to differentiate the neural mechanisms of SI and SA. As such, caution is warranted when interpreting and generalizing these findings to SI. Future studies with larger sample sizes focused on SI are essential to more accurately clarify the neural mechanisms underlying suicidal ideation in MDD patients.

We also conducted subgroup analyses categorizing studies into Medication-naïve and Medicated groups. However, no significant differences in regional brain activity were observed between these subgroups. This lack of significance may be attributed to factors such as small sample sizes and variability in medication status across studies. The heterogeneity in medication types, dosages, and treatment durations could have masked potential effects. Further research with larger sample sizes and more consistent medication conditions is needed to better understand the role of medication status in influencing brain activity in MDD patients with STB.

Additionally, regression analysis revealed no significant relationship between abnormal brain activity and HAMD-17 scores, indicating that depression severity did not significantly influence our results. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of our findings.

ROI-based functional connectivity findings

In the independent MDD sample, ROI-based FC analysis revealed a negative correlation between FC in the right MOG-right IFGtriang and BSSI scores. The right MOG, part of the visual network, is involved in processing visual information, emotion, and attention [92, 93] while the IFG serves as a key hub in the cognitive control network and could curb key executive control processes for misbehavior [94, 95]. It was found that patients showed abnormal activation within different areas of the visual cortex during cognitive control, suggesting that a high degree of functional correlation exists between the two networks [96]. Thus, our findings may reflect a potential association between FC in visual and cognitive control regions and STB in MDD. This was supported by a study showing that MDD patients exhibited reduced activity in executive function and visual processing regions during the Stroop task, which was associated with an increased risk of suicidal behavior [97–99]. In addition, another study reported that self-harm participants had poorer cognitive inhibition abilities when viewing images depicting negative emotional content, which may suggest an association between decreased FC of the cognitive control and visual processing networks and suicide behavior [100]. Given the correlational nature of this analysis, we recommend future studies use task-based fMRI to validate the role of these visual and cognitive control regions in STB of MDD patients.

Clinical implications

Our findings hold substantial clinical implications for STB risk stratification and intervention in MDD patients. Specifically, the abnormal regional brain activity observed in the right MOG and right IFGtriang, along with the negative correlation between the FC of these regions and BSSI scores in first-episode, drug-naïve MDD patients, may serve as reliable biomarkers for assessing STB risk. These FC patterns offer the potential to improve clinical decision-making by identifying high-risk individuals and guiding the development of personalized treatment plans. Targeted interventions, such as neuromodulation of the IFG and MOG regions using techniques like transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), may be effective in mitigating STB. These approaches have demonstrated potential in modulating emotional regulation circuits, offering a promising avenue for the treatment of MDD [101]. However, the clinical utility of these biomarkers requires further validation. Experimental studies with larger, independent cohorts are needed to confirm the robustness and generalizability of these findings. Additionally, future research should focus on exploring the causal relationship between brain activity abnormalities and STB, as well as assessing the therapeutic potential of neuromodulation in reducing STB risk in MDD patients.

Study limitations and future research directions

The current meta-analysis is subject to several limitations. Firstly, our meta-analysis utilized peak coordinates and effect sizes derived from published studies, rather than raw data. These limitations may compromise the accuracy and generalizability of our findings [56]. Future studies should aim to share raw neuroimaging data through open repositories to facilitate more precise image-based meta-analyses. Secondly, our study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria led to the incorporation of only 12 studies, increases the risk of Type II errors and limits statistical power. Larger, multi-center collaborations are needed to strengthen these findings. Thirdly, due to the limited number of studies in this field, we could not exclude studies that only compared STB-MDD patients and HC, which means that the control group in our analysis included both non-STB MDD patients and HC. This may introduce potential confounding factors related to the use of HC controls. Additionally, the lack of comprehensive data on comorbidities and illness duration further limited our ability to assess their impact. Future studies with larger, more homogeneous samples and detailed clinical data are needed to enable more comprehensive meta-analyses and better clarify the influence of these factors. Moreover, the relatively small sample sizes in individual studies may result in greater weighting of a few large-sample datasets in the meta-analysis, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Finally, the FC analysis is based on a cross-sectional study with a small sample, causality between brain abnormalities and STB in MDD remains uncertain. Longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes are necessary to clarify these relationships.

Conclusion

In summary, our meta-analysis revealed significant abnormalities in regional brain activity in MDD patients with STB, including the MOG, IFGtriang, and precuneus. These findings highlight the involvement of visual processing, cognitive control, and self-referential processing in the neurobiological underpinnings of STB in MDD patients. The SA subgroup analysis further emphasized the unique role of the angular gyrus in suicidal behavior. Additionally, our exploratory FC analysis in 57 first-episode, drug-naïve MDD patients demonstrated that the FC in the right MOG-right IFGtriang was negatively correlated with BSSI scores, suggesting a potential pathway for understanding STB risk. These findings provide valuable insights into the neural mechanisms of STB risk in MDD, offering reliable identification of key brain regions and connectivity abnormalities between them. By combining a rigorous meta-analysis of regional abnormalities with exploratory FC analysis, our study enhances the understanding of the brain networks implicated in STB risk. These findings may ultimately serve as targets for future therapeutic interventions.
