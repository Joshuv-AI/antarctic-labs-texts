Mechanistic studies of the human NIPA2 transporter
Authors: Sebastian J Karuppan, Nikolas Connolly, Simran Shrestha, Matthew Medearis, Maryam Gholampour, Lejla Zubcevic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Non-Imprinted in Prader-Willi/Angelman syndrome 2 (NIPA2) is a highly conserved member of the Drug/Metabolite Transporter (DMT) family, originally characterized as a Mg2+ transporter. Dysfunction of NIPA2 has been linked to epilepsy and seizures but despite this the molecular mechanisms of transport and substrate recognition, as well as the functional and structural effects of patient mutations. To answer these questions and gain a fundamental understanding of NIPA2, we performed cryo-EM, and liposomal and cell-based assays. Here we show that NIPA2 passively transports both Mg2+ and nucleotides, revealing a broader substrate profile than previously appreciated with potential physiological implications. Furthermore, we establish that NIPA2 utilizes an alternating-access mechanism that appears distinct from classical elevator and rocker switch models. Finally, we show that mutation A75T, linked to childhood absence epilepsy, results in loss of function. Our data provides a framework for understanding the physiological role of NIPA2 and related proteins.Subject terms: Structural biology, Permeation and transport, Electron microscopy

Introduction

The Non-Imprinted in Prader-Willi/Angelman syndrome (NIPA) genes, NIPA1 and NIPA2 (also known as SLC57A1 and SLC57A2)1, are located in the 15q11-q13 region of chromosome 15, which is paternally deleted in Burnside-Butler (BBS)2, Prader-Willi (PWS)3, and Angelman syndromes (AS)4. NIPA1 and NIPA2 have been proposed to play a role in maintaining the cellular Mg2+ homeostasis5,6, and it has been hypothesized that their dysfunction results in a decrease in cellular Mg2+, which might lead to altered neuronal function7–9. Indeed, loss of NIPA has been linked to the neurodevelopmental and behavioral features of BBS, PWS, and AS10,11. Furthermore, mutations in the NIPA genes have been linked to childhood absence epilepsy and hereditary spastic paraplegia12–19, and repeat expansions in the NIPA1 gene are associated with Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)20—a debilitating neurodegenerative disease.

NIPA are classified as members of the Drug/Metabolite Transporter (DMT) superfamily, which consists of 44 subfamilies, each with a distinct topology and function21. NIPA fall into the EamE/RhaT subfamily of DMTs, which are responsible for transport of nucleosides, nucleotides, sugars, amino acids, and drugs21. Interestingly, within the DMT superfamily, only NIPA are classified as ion transporters21.

NIPA proteins exhibit a remarkably high level of conservation, which might hint at an important physiological function. Indeed, analysis of the evolutionary conservation (Consurf)22,23 of 213 non-redundant eukaryotic NIPA2 orthologs shows that there is >80% sequence identity between any two sequences (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, there are many unanswered fundamental questions regarding NIPA. For example, it is not clear if NIPA transports substrates passively or if it requires a driving force (e.g., a secondary ionic gradient). Second, given its classification as a DMT protein, it is not clear how NIPA might achieve specificity for Mg2+. Third, due to the dearth of structural data on NIPA (and other DMT proteins), we do not know the molecular mechanism by which transport occurs. Indeed, to the best of our knowledge, structures of only three distantly related DMTs are known (the bacterial multidrug resistance efflux transporter EmrE, the yeast GDP-mannose transporter Vrg4, and the bacterial amino acid and drug exporter YddG24–28), and no structures of mammalian DMTs are available. Finally, we do not know how mutations identified in patients might affect protein function or structure.

Here, we show that NIPA2 acts as a passive Mg2+ and nucleotide transporter and uses a distinct mechanism of translocation. In addition, we show that the mutation A75T, associated with childhood absence epilepsy29, results in a reduction in both Mg2+ and nucleotide transport.

Results

NIPA2 transports Mg2+ down the concentration gradient

NIPA2 was originally characterized as a Mg2+ transporter5. Therefore, to determine how NIPA2 transports Mg2+ and what drives this transport, we employed a liposomal flux assay. We purified the protein from sf9 insect cells and reconstituted it into liposomes containing the Mg2+-sensitive fluorescent dye MagFura 2. To determine if NIPA2 requires a secondary ionic gradient (e.g., a Na+ or K+ gradient) to transport Mg2+, we created two sets of liposomes with different ionic gradients: (1) Na+ inside and K+ outside (Na+ in/K+ out) and (2) K+ inside and Na+ outside (K+ in/Na+ out) (Fig. 1a). The concentration of Na+ and K+ was kept at 150 mM on both sides of the liposome. All other conditions (i.e., pH, buffer concentration) were kept constant across the liposomal membrane. If NIPA2-mediated transport was dependent on the direction of the Na+ or K+ gradient (e.g., if Mg2+ was transported either in the same or opposite direction of a counter ion) we expected to see transport in only one of these conditions upon addition of Mg2+ to the extraliposomal buffer (Fig. 1a). However, following addition of Mg2+ we recorded a robust increase in intraliposomal [Mg2+] in both conditions (Fig. 1b, c). Because the Mg2+ gradient was the only existing ionic gradient across the liposomal membrane, we hypothesized that this alone might be sufficient to drive transport by NIPA2. To test this proposition, we prepared liposomes with equal concentrations (150 mM) of Na+ (Na+ in/out) or K+ (K+ in/out) across the liposomal membrane. Both the Na+ in/out and K+ in/out liposomes showed a rise in intraliposomal [Mg2+] following addition of magnesium to the extraliposomal buffer, which was absent in the control liposomes (Supplementary Fig. 1). This result is consistent with the data obtained from Na+ in/K+ out and K+ in/Na+ out liposomes and suggests that NIPA2 can transport Mg2+ down its concentration gradient.

The structure of human NIPA2 reveals a distinct mechanism of transport

We then set out to determine the structure of the human NIPA2 protein by cryo-EM in the presence of Mg2+ ions. The resulting structure of NIPA2Mg was solved to an overall resolution of 3.5 Å (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3, Table 1). The NIPA2 assembles into a dimer (Fig. 1d) of ~80 kDa, and its structure was solved in the absence of fiducial markers. A single protomer of NIPA2 consists of 9 transmembrane helices, which are connected by intra- and extracellular loops, with an extracellular N-terminus and an intracellular C-terminus. The protomer possesses internal pseudosymmetry, with helices 1–4 mirroring helices 6–9. We term the two parts of the protomer lobe 1 and lobe 2, respectively (Fig. 1e). In a single NIPA2 protomer, helices 1–3 from lobe 1 and helices 6–8 from lobe 2 assemble in a cup-like arrangement around a central cavity and helices 4 and 9 participate in dimerization (Fig. 1f). At the extracellular face of the protein, the two lobes are connected by a long, partially helical linker that connects helices 4 and 5, termed the 4–5 loop. Helices 1 and 6 are long, and they traverse the membrane at a slanted angle (Fig. 1f). The linker that connects helices 1 and 2 forms a hairpin structure at the intracellular leaflet of the membrane and makes contacts with the cytosol-proximal region of helix 7 (Fig. 1f).

The dimer interface between helices 4 and 9 is formed by an intricate set of interactions at both the extra- and intracellular sides (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 3). At the extracellular side, residue H129 from helix 4 of one protomer forms hydrophobic interactions with H129 from the opposing protomer. In addition, interactions form between residues D277 and D278 of helix 9 and the backbones of H129 from helix 4 in both protomers, re-enforcing the interface (Supplementary Fig. 3). Similarly, at the intracellular side, residues H111 from helix 4 and F299 from helix 9 form a ring of interactions while residue N109 from helix 9 interacts with the backbone of F299 (Supplementary Fig. 3). The dimer is also supported by a tight hydrophobic interface that forms between helices 4 and 9 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Interestingly, the two protomers in NIPA2 adopt two distinct conformations (Fig. 2a, b): in protomer 1, the central cavity is exposed to the extracellular space (Fig. 2b) but in protomer 2 the cavity is occluded to both sides of the membrane (Fig. 2b). However, the dimer interface remains static. This suggests that the interface acts as a scaffold while the helices surrounding the cavity are responsible for transport. To determine the conformational differences between the outward open and occluded conformations of NIPA2, we overlaid the two protomers using the static scaffold domain as an anchor (Fig. 2c, d). This revealed several important differences between the two conformations. First, there is a dramatic difference in the position of the transport domain relative to the scaffold domain (Fig. 2c, d). When viewed from above the membrane plane, the transport domain in the occluded state rotates clockwise by 25° around the scaffold domain compared to the outward open state (Fig. 2d). Second, the rotation of the transport domain impacts the position of the 4–5 loop with respect to the substrate cavity (Fig. 2d, e). Within the 4–5 loop, an acidic patch of residues (E133, E134, and E135) forms conformation-dependent interactions that stabilize either the occluded or the outward open conformation. Namely, in the occluded state E134 interacts with the carbonyl oxygens of A78 and F79 in the 2–3 loop (lobe 1) while E135 interacts with K145 in the 4–5 loop (adjacent to lobe 2) (Fig. 2e). The residues E134 and E135 therefore appear to staple the two lobes of the protomer together and stabilize the occluded state (Fig. 2e). In the open state, following the rotation, the interaction between E134 and lobe 1 is broken and E133 and E135 now pull the 4–5 loop towards lobe 2 by interacting with K145 and F268. This ensures that the 4–5 loop is not occluding the cavity and that substrate entry or exit is unobstructed (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, an additional set of interactions appear to stabilize the occluded state: K197 from helix 6, which is located just underneath the 4–5 loop in the occluded state (Fig. 2e) forms a bridge between the two lobes of the transporter by forming interactions with backbone of A78 in lobe 1 and F268 and E270 in lobe 2 (Fig. 2f).

We also compared the local conformational changes within the transport domains of the occluded and outward open protomers. An alignment of the transport domains revealed that helices 5–8 remain static during the transition between the outward open and the occluded state while helices 1–3 undergo major local conformational changes (Fig. 3a–c). Namely, the top parts of helices 1–2 bends by ~30° around glycine hinges formed by G25 and G26 in helix 1 (Fig. 3d), and G67 and G69 in helix 2 (Fig. 3e). Helix 3 also shows a small degree of internal bending (Fig. 3f) but, more dramatically, the entire helix 3 tilts by ~30° to accompany the bending of helices 1 and 2 (Fig. 3b, c). The movement of helix 3 might be facilitated by the presence of a single turn of 310 helix (Fig. 3g). Together, the top parts of helices 1 and 2 and the entire helix 3 appear to act as a hinged lid that can either occlude or expose the central cavity.

Next, we considered ion binding in the cavity. In the occluded conformation, the cavity has two distinct non-protein densities that likely correspond to ions. We have termed the sites of these densities site 1 and site 2 (Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Fig. 3). Based on their size, they both likely represent hydrated or semi-hydrated Mg2+ ions. To test this proposition, we utilized the LigandRecognizer30 software. In brief, we used the ligand-free model and the experimental map to generate a difference map in PHENIX31. We then generated NIPA2 models where the densities were assigned to Mg2+, Ca2+, Na+, or K+. We then used the LigandRecognizer ChimeraX plugin30 to calculate the likelihood of the placement being correct and generate a list of the most likely ions and molecules that might populate the density. We performed this experiment for both sites 1 and 2 (Supplementary Fig. 4). These calculations strongly indicate that the placement of the Mg2+ ion in site 2 is correct. The indication is weaker for site 1, but nevertheless it strongly implies that (a) the density does not correspond to a monovalent ion and (b) that a divalent ion (Zn2+, Mg2+, or Ca2+) is the likeliest correct placement. Because Zn2+ or Ca2+ were not included in our prep, Mg2+ seems the most likely occupant. We therefore tentatively assigned both sites to Mg2+ ions. At site 1, the putative Mg2+ ion is surrounded by 4 water molecules arranged in an octahedral arrangement. The ion is coordinated via the water molecules by residues from helix 2 (E70), helix 6 (S192), and helix 7 (Q232) (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 3). At site 2, we also find the putative Mg2+ ion surrounded by 4 waters in an octahedral arrangement. At this site, the hydrated Mg2+ ion is surrounded by aromatic residues Y252, Y253, and F256 from helix 8 and F28 from helix 1. In addition, residues from helix 2 (N73) and helix 3 (the carbonyl oxygen of G89 and S92) are also within interaction distance of the ion (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 3). Interestingly, G89 is part of the 310 helical turn in helix 3 (Fig. 3g). The cavity undergoes an expansion in the outward open state that is caused by the opening of the hinged lid. At the substrate binding sites, the conformational changes in lobe 1 lead to a 2 Å increase in the distance between Cα of G89 in helix 3 and Q232 in helix 7 and a 3 Å increase between Cα of E70 in helix 2 and Q232 in helix 7 (Fig. 4e, f). The widening likely weakens the Mg2+ binding and facilitates its exit from the site.

The residues involved in ion coordination in NIPA2 (E70, N73, G89, S92, Q232, Y252, Y253, F256) are entirely conserved across 213 non-redundant eukaryotic NIPA2 orthologs (Supplementary Fig. 1), which hints at their essential role in the proper function of NIPA2. We expressed and purified the NIPA2 site 1 mutants E70A and Q232A and site 2 mutant F256A, reconstituted them into liposomes, and measured their ability to transport Mg2+. Indeed, the ability of all mutants to transport Mg2+ is severely impaired compared to WT protein, which confirms the critical role of intact sites 1 and 2 in NIPA2 Mg2+ transport (Fig. 4g, h).

Human NIPA2 transports nucleotides

To further probe the substrate binding cavity, we prepared the NIPA2 protein in the presence of EDTA to remove Mg2+ and solved the structure of NIPA2EDTA (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). Overall, the protein assumed a very similar conformation to NIPA2Mg (RMSD = 0.9 Å, Supplementary Fig. 7), but the substrate binding cavity of the occluded NIPA2EDTA protomer was occupied by a strong non-protein density that did not resemble an ion (Supplementary Fig. 7). This suggested that in the absence of added Mg2+, the cavity is occupied by a different, possibly endogenous, molecule that may represent an alternative substrate. To glean the potential identity of the molecule, we employed the LigandRecognizer ChimeraX plugin30. Based on the shape, the software predicted that the density might represent a nucleotide—an ATP or ADP molecule.

This prompted us to consider if NIPA2—like other members of the DMT superfamily—might be capable of transporting more complex substrates, e.g., nucleotides. To test this, we devised two parallel uptake assays, one cell-based and one liposomal, using fluorescent nucleotide analogs (2′/3′-O-(N-Methylanthraniloyl)-ATP, -ADP, and -GDP). In the cell-based assay, we incubated sf9 cells expressing wild-type NIPA2 with a fluorescent nucleotide analog, washed the cells in Hanks’ buffer without divalent cations, and measured the fluorescence. The assay showed that cells expressing NIPA2 exhibit a robust uptake of all tested nucleotides. Importantly, uptake was not observed in non-infected cells or mock-infected cells (Fig. 5a). To further exclude the possibility that transport mechanisms endogenous to sf9 cells might be contributing to nucleotide uptake, we also developed a liposomal assay. To this end, we purified wild-type NIPA2 and reconstituted it into liposomes. The liposomes were prepared with equal concentrations of Na+ across the liposomal membrane (Na+ in/out), incubated with the fluorescent ADP, run over a Sephadex G50 column to remove the extraliposomal ADP, and their fluorescence was measured. Liposomes reconstituted with NIPA2 show a robust uptake of the fluorescent ADP analog, which is not observed in empty control liposomes. These results show that NIPA2 is capable of transport of nucleotides down their concentration gradient (Fig. 5a, b) and are consistent with the known broad substrate specificities of DMTs24,32,33.

We next set out to interrogate how Mg2+ might affect nucleotide transport. To this end, we measured the NIPA2-mediated nucleotide uptake in sf9 cells under increasing concentrations of extracellular Mg2+. (Fig. 5c). At concentrations that correspond to the physiological levels of free Mg2+ (0.5–1.5 mM), the transport of the fluorescent ADP analog was inhibited by ~20% in our assay (Fig. 5c). At the highest Mg2+concentration tested (4 mM), the nucleotide transport was inhibited by ~30%. We also wanted to determine how ATP affects Mg2+ transport. We measured the Mg2+ uptake into liposomes in the presence of 5 mM free extraliposomal Mg2+ and increasing ATP (0–5 mM) (Fig. 5d–f). We used Maxchelator34 to calculate and adjust the concentrations of free Mg2+ and ATP. Our data shows that ATP inhibits Mg2+ uptake in a concentration-dependent manner and that 3 mM ATP is enough to suppress the Mg2+ uptake by ~70% (Fig. 5d, e). In the light of these data and the fact that the cellular concentration of ATP is higher than that of free Mg2+ (3–10 mM vs 0.5–1.5 mM), it appears unlikely that Mg2+ is the primary cargo for NIPA2. However, since both substrates can be transported by NIPA2, it is possible that Mg2+ and nucleotides might, under certain conditions, modulate the transport of each other.

The mechanism of NIPA2-mediated nucleotide transport

To interrogate the mechanism of NIPA2-mediated nucleotide transport, we solved the structure of NIPA2 in the presence of Mg-ATP (NIPA2ATP, Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). The structure resembles that of NIPA2Mg and NIPA2EDTA, but in the occluded cavity of NIPA2ATP, we capture density corresponding to an Mg-ATP molecule (Fig. 6a–c, Supplementary Fig. 9). In the cavity, the triphosphate tail, complexed with a Mg2+ ion, is tucked into the space corresponding to site 2 identified in the NIPA2Mg structure. Here, it is surrounded by the aromatic residues from helix 8 (Y252, Y253, and F256) and the 310 region of helix 3 (G89 and S92). The ribose moiety interacts with hydroxyl group of Y252 and Q232, while the adenosine moiety fills the space corresponding to site 1 in the NIPA2Mg structure and is coordinated by E70 (Fig. 6b, c). Like in NIPAMg, we observed that the cavity expands by ~3 Å around the substrate binding site in the outward open state compared to the occluded state (Supplementary Fig. 10). Widening of the cavity facilitates substrate release and is achieved by opening of the hinged lid- the upper part of helices 1 and 2 and the entire helix 3 (Supplementary Fig. 10).

To probe the contribution of the individual residues to nucleotide transport, we introduced mutations to residues that interact with the adenosine (E70A), ribose (Q232A), and phosphate (F256A) and measured their ability to transport ADP in sf9 cells. In agreement with our structural analysis, all mutants exhibit a severely impaired ability to transport ADP (Fig. 6d). This data also agrees with our findings that E70A, Q232A, and F256A also severely impact Mg2+ transport (Fig. 4g, h) and with the Consurf analysis, which shows that residues that interact with ligands in the NIPA2 cavity are completely conserved (Supplementary Fig. 1) and therefore likely functionally important.

In addition, we also made mutations predicted to disrupt the mechanism of transport by destabilizing the occluded state, E134A and K197A (Fig. 2e, f). Of the two mutations, E134A has a more pronounced effect on nucleotide transport than K197A (Fig. 6d). This finding may signal that residue E134 plays a central role in stabilizing the occluded state by stapling together the extracellular regions of lobe 1 and lobe 2 (Fig. 2e), while K197 provides additional support (Fig. 2f). Expression of all mutants was probed by western blot (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Disease mutation A75T results in loss of function

Several clinically identified NIPA2 mutations have been linked to disease9,12,13. However, without any structural information on this protein or means of testing protein function, the role of mutations in pathogenesis has been unclear. One such mutation is A75T, a variation that has been identified in childhood absence epilepsy patients, but its functional effects have not been assessed29. To determine if and how A75T might affect NIPA2 function, we tested its ability to transport Mg2+ and nucleotides. First, we expressed and purified the NIPA2 A75T mutant and reconstituted it into liposomes. We then measured the ability of the mutant protein to transport Mg2+ under Na+ in/K+ out conditions. We found that Mg2+ transport by the A75T mutant was impaired compared to the wild-type NIPA2 protein. (Supplementary Fig. 11). We similarly observed a decrease in the mutant’s ability to transport ADP in our cell-based nucleotide uptake assay (Supplementary Fig. 11). Therefore, our two assays show that A75T is a loss-of-function mutation.

To determine if the mutation changes the structure or conformational equilibrium of NIPA2, we solved the cryo-EM structure of NIPA2A75T mutant (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). The mutation occurs in the hinged lid region of lobe 1, at the extracellular end of the helix 2, near the 2–3 loop (Supplementary Fig. 11). A75T does not result in any significant local changes, nor does it perturb the overall NIPA2 structure (NIPA2A75T-NIPAMg RMSD = 0.65 Å, Supplementary Fig. 11). Accordingly, the substrate binding site resembles that of the wild type protein, with strong densities that likely represent Mg2+ ions (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 13). While our structural data does not indicate that A75T dramatically changes protein structure, the location of the mutation does coincide with a mechanistically important region—the hinged lid. Therefore, the mutation might be affecting kinetics of substrate binding and release and, in that way, resulting in its impaired ability to transport substrates.

Discussion

Here, we provide a structural and mechanistic study of a mammalian DMT transporter, the human NIPA2. Previously, structures of two DMT proteins were reported: the bacterial amino acid transporter YddG24 and the yeast nucleotide sugar transporter Vrg425. When compared with these two proteins (Supplementary Fig. 14), NIPA2 shows both similarities and differences. While the overall fold is conserved, the order and arrangement of helices is not. Moreover, YddG and Vrg4 do not possess the equivalent of the NIPA2 helix 5, but they do have two additional transmembrane segments in the space that corresponds to the area between helices 2 and 7 in NIPA2 (Supplementary Fig. 14). The cavities of all three proteins are lined with aromatic residues but, consistent with their affinity for different substrates, their positions do not appear to be strictly conserved (Supplementary Fig. 14). The structure of Yddg24 suggests that it exists as a monomer, but Vrg435—like NIPA2—forms dimers. In Vrg4, the dimerization does not appear to be critical for function, as its two protomers likely operate independently of each other35. In the case of NIPA2, more work is needed to determine if the protomers exhibit cooperativity or not.

Perhaps unsurprisingly, NIPA2 does not share significant structural features with other known Mg2+-transporting proteins, such as TRPM7, MgTE, or CorA/Mrs2. In contrast to NIPA2, these proteins (which themselves are derived from different protein super-families) possess ion-conducting pores that are strategically lined with negatively charged residues to capture and coordinate hydrated (or at least partially hydrated) Mg2+ ions36–40. This makes it tempting to speculate if the ability of NIPA2 to transport Mg2+ might be an “evolutionary spandrel” rather than a targeted goal.

Our data shows that NIPA2 can transport substrates down their concentration gradient. The results of our liposomal assays measuring Mg2+ or nucleotide uptake indicate that NIPA2 transport is driven by the substrate concentration and not by the Na+ or K+ gradient. In addition, we do not see any evidence of H+ dependence, as all our experiments were performed under conditions with equal [H+] across the liposomal membrane. This is consistent with a previous study, which suggested that NIPA2 does not require energy to transport Mg2+5. Our liposomal and cell-based assays suggest that NIPA2 also transports nucleotides down their concentration gradient. While these data suggest that NIPA2 functions as a uniporter, we cannot exclude the possibility that, under some circumstances, NIPA2 may exhibit mixed modalities of transport, e.g., antiport or symport. Indeed, the distantly related DMT bacterial drug efflux protein EmrE has been shown to exhibit complex transport behavior and can, depending on conditions, behave as symporter or an antiporter41.

Our data also suggests that NIPA2 preferentially transports the fluorescent nucleotide analogs at physiological concentrations of free Mg2+. Similarly, our competition assays show that, at equal concentrations, ATP significantly suppresses Mg2+ transport. Our findings indicate that NIPA2 has a preference for nucleotides over Mg2+, but it might be possible that Mg2+ and nucleotides exert a modulatory effect on each other to tune transport by NIPA2. Indeed, Mg2+ and ATP levels are intimately linked in the cell, and it is worth pointing out that ATP modulates the function of several Mg2+ transporting proteins42–44.

Furthermore, by capturing NIPA2 in the outward open and occluded states, we were able to determine the molecular mechanism of alternating access employed by NIPA2. This mechanism, distinct from classical elevator and rocker switch models, relies on rotation of protomers around a central scaffold domain, which lies at the dimer interface. The rotation enables an acidic patch of residues at the extracellular side, in the 4–5 loop, to either stabilize the occluded state (by stapling the two lobes of NIPA2 together) or destabilize it (by releasing the staple) (Figs. 2e and 6e). The release of the staple in the outward open state enables the opening of a hinged lid, made up of the top parts of helices 1 and 2 and helix 3, leading to widening of the substrate binding cavity and its exposure to the extracellular space (Figs. 2b–e and 6f). Mutation E134A, which removes one of the acidic patch residues, has a strong effect on the ability of NIPA2 to transport nucleotides, which confirms the importance of the acidic patch in the conformational transitions of the NIPA2 transport cycle (Fig. 6d)

Our work also provides insight into the ability of NIPA2 to bind Mg2+ and nucleotides. We show that the substrate binding cavity can be divided into two sections—site 1 and site 2. Both sites are comprised of residues from both lobes of the transport domain. Residues participating in binding at site 1 are E70 from lobe 1 and Q232 and S192 from lobe 2. These residues coordinate Mg2+ in NIPA2Mg and the adenine moiety in NIPA2ATP (Figs. 3b, c and 6b, c). It is possible that binding of substrates at this position helps stabilize the occluded state and thereby facilitates transport. Mutations E70A and Q232A both have a strong effect on Mg2+ and nucleotide transport which indicates that binding of substrates at this site is necessary for transport to occur (Figs. 4g, h and 6d). At site 2, we observe binding of a second magnesium ion in NIPA2Mg and the phosphate tail of ATP in NIPA2ATP. Here, the substrates are corralled by aromatic residues Y252, Y253, and F256 from lobe 2 and residues F28, G89, and S92 from lobe 1. The aromatic residues can participate in both cation and anion coordination, and we speculate that this is essential for the ability of the site to bind both Mg2+ and phosphate. Indeed, F256 is positioned to form cation-π interactions with the Mg2+ ion in the substrate binding cavity (Fig. 4d), but in the presence of ATP, the edge of the aromatic ring is positioned to interact with the β phosphate of ATP (Fig. 6b, c). Consistent with this, mutation F256A has a profound effect on both Mg2+ and nucleotide transport, likely due to the loss of essential coordination in the substrate cavity.

It is interesting to consider the biological role of NIPA2 in the context of the data presented here. NIPA2 mutations are linked to epilepsy and seizures9,12 and, consistent with this, a study of NIPA2 knock-out mice showed that their neurons exhibit a significantly higher excitability than those from wild type animals7. Earlier studies have hypothesized that the increase in excitability might be related to a reduction in free Mg2+, which in turn could result in impaired BK channel activation or decreased inhibition of NMDAR7–9. Our data prompt us to question if NIPA2 might, in fact, participate in suppression of neuronal excitation by a mechanism involving its ability to transport nucleotides—e.g., by contributing to adenosine signaling and activation of the A1 receptor.

Humans possess six NIPA and NIPA-like (NPAL) genes, all of which remain poorly studied. A comparison of these proteins with NIPA2 reveals that residues involved in substrate binding in NIPA2 are largely conserved (Supplementary Fig. 14) and that NIPA and NPAL proteins might therefore also be involved in nucleotide transport. Our data provide a framework for understanding the mechanism of NIPA2 and lay the groundwork for future studies of the physiological role of NIPA2 and the related proteins in health and disease.

Methods

NIPA2 expression and purification

The human NIPA2 gene (Uniprot ID Q8N8Q9) was synthesized by BioBasic and cloned into a pFastBac vector in frame with a C-terminal FLAG tag for insect cell expression. NIPA2 plasmid DNA was transformed into DH10Bac E. coli cells, and baculovirus was obtained as previously described45. An insect cell culture at a density of 1.2 million cells ml−1 was inoculated with P2 virus and grown at 27 °C for 72 h. The cells were pelleted and resuspended in buffer A (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (1 μg μl−1 leupeptin, 1.5 μg μl−1 Pepstatin, 0.84 μg μl−1 aprotinin, 0.3 mM PMSF), 14.3 mM BME, DNAse I, and 2 mM DTT. For the magnesium-containing preparation, all buffers included 2 mM MgCl2. For the magnesium-free preparation, 2 mM EDTA was included.

Cells were broken by sonication (3 × 30 s) and solubilized in 40 mM dodecyl β-maltoside (DDM, Anatrace) and 4 mM Cholesteryl Hemisuccinate (CHS, Anatrace). Insoluble material was removed by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then incubated with anti-FLAG M2 resin (Millipore-Sigma) at 4 °C. The supernatant-resin suspension was poured onto a Bio-Rad Econo-Column, and the flow-through was discarded. The remaining resin was subjected to 10 column volumes (CV) washes with Buffer B (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 0.1% LMNG, 0.01% CHS, and 2 mM MgCl2 or 1 mM EDTA where noted). Elution was performed with 5 CV of Buffer C (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 0.1% LMNG, 0.01% CHS, 0.1 mg ml−1 FLAG peptide, and 2 mM MgCl2 or 1 mM EDTA where noted). The eluate was loaded onto a Superdex 200 column (Cytiva) equilibrated with Buffer B (including 2 mM MgCl2 or EDTA). Samples NIPA2Mg and NIPA2A75T were prepared in MgCl2. Samples NIPA2EDTA and NIPA2ATP were prepared in EDTA.

Liposomal flux assays

A 10 mg ml−1 3:1:1 mixture of lipids 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) (Avanti Polar Lipids), 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (POPE) (Avanti Polar Lipids), 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-(1’-rac-glycerol) (POPG) (Avanti Polar Lipids) was dried under argon, resuspended in 1 ml 50 mM HEPES pH 8, 150 mM NaCl (or KCl) and clarified by sonication, before being incubated for 1 h with 10 mM DDM. The human NIPA2 was eluted in 50 mM HEPES pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DDM, 0.1 mM CHS, and concentrated to 4–5 mg ml−1. The protein was mixed with the permeabilized liposomes in a 1:50 ratio, and the protein-lipid mixture was combined with 50 μM MagFura 2 fluorescent dye (Sigma Aldrich). The mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 1 h and then transferred to an Eppendorf tube containing 500 mg of freshly prepared Bio-Beads SM-2. The reconstitution mixture was incubated at 4 °C overnight. In the morning, the proteoliposomes were transferred to another fresh Eppendorf tube containing Bio-Beads SM-2 and incubated for another 4 h at 4 °C. Finally, proteoliposomes were aliquoted into Eppendorf tubes, flash frozen in LN2, and stored at −80 °C. Empty liposomes were generated with each batch of proteoliposomes for use as a negative control.

Before conducting flux assays, the liposomes were thawed at room temperature. The excess fluorescent dye was removed and the external buffer exchanged by passage through a 1 ml Sephadex G50 column (Cytiva). For the experiment, 20 μl proteoliposomes were diluted with 180 μl reaction buffer in a 96-well opaque-bottom plate (Grainer). Fluorescence was measured on a SpectraMax iD5 Multi-Mode Microplate (Molecular Devices) reader using two excitation wavelengths (330 and 370 nm) and one emission wavelength (510 nm) at room temperature. A baseline read of 5 min was first measured for all samples. Then transport was initiated by addition of 5 mM MgCl2, and fluorescence was measured for 20 min. At the end of the experiment, 2% Triton-X was added and the maximum fluorescence recorded for 5 min. Finally, minimum fluorescence was obtained by adding 50 mM EDTA and recording for 5 min. The magnesium concentration was calculated using the formula provided by the MagFura 2 manufacturer.

Here, R is the fluorescence intensity ratio between the Mg2+-bound and free MagFura 2 (330 nm / 370 nm), Rmin and Rmax are the minimum and maximum recorded fluorescence intensity ratios, Q is the fluorescence intensity ratio at 370 nm (absorbance of the free MagFura 2), and Kd is the Mg2+ dissociation constant for MagFura 2 (~1.9 mM). Data were plotted using Excel and GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2. Each data point represents data obtained from at least 2 biological replicates (distinct protein purifications and reconstitutions) and at least 4 technical replicates (individual reads). Please see figure legends and source data for more information on individual data points.

For competition assays, concentrations of free Mg2+ and ATP were calculated using Maxchelator34. Free Mg2+ was adjusted to 5 mM at all concentrations of ATP.

Nucleotide uptake liposomal assays

For liposomal nucleotide uptake assays, the reconstitution was performed as described above, with the exception that no MagFura 2 dye was added during the process.

Liposomes suspended in 50 mM HEPES pH 8, 150 mM NaCl were thawed and passed through a freshly prepared 1 ml Sephadex G50 column equilibrated in 50 mM HEPES pH 8, 150 mM NaCl. The liposomes were incubated with 50 μM 2′/3′-O-(N-Methylanthraniloyl)adenosine 5′-diphosphate trisodium salt (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MANT-ADP) for 30 min at room temperature, protected from light. Following incubation, excess nucleotides were removed from the extraliposomal buffer by passage through a fresh 1 ml Sephadex G50 column equilibrated in 50 mM HEPES pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, and the clean liposomes collected for measurement. For the experiment, 20 μl proteoliposomes were diluted with 180 μl 50 mM HEPES, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl in a 96-well opaque-bottom plate. Fluorescence was measured on a SpectraMax iD5 Multi-Mode Microplate using an excitation wavelength of 350 and an emission wavelength of 440 nm. Each data point was obtained from at least 3 biological replicates and at least 24 technical replicates (as defined in the previous section). Also, please see source data for details. Data was plotted using Excel and GraphPad Prism.

Nucleotide uptake sf9 assays

The wild type and mutants of human NIPA2 were expressed in sf9 cells using the Bac-to-Bac system. Uninfected cells and mock-infected cells (cells expressing an unrelated protein) were used as negative controls. An insect cell culture at a density of 1.2 million cells ml−1 was inoculated with P2 virus and grown at 27 °C for 72 h. A resuspension of live cells at a density of 1 × 106 cells ml−1 was prepared in a 15 ml conical tube and washed with calcium-free, magnesium-free, and phenol red-free Hank’s Buffered Saline Solution (HBSS--) [Gibco]. The cells were supplemented with 50 μM 2′/3′-O-(N-Methylanthraniloyl)adenosine 5′-triphosphate trisodium salt (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MANT-ATP), 2′/3′-O-(N-Methylanthraniloyl)adenosine 5′-diphosphate disodium salt (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MANT-ADP), or 2′/3′-O-(N-Methylanthraniloyl)guanosine 5′-diphosphate disodium salt (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MANT-GDP) and incubated for 30 min at 27 °C.

Following incubation, the cells were washed in HBSS--- and again resuspended to a density of 1 × 106 live cells ml−1 in HBSS---. Aliquots of 200 μl were pipetted onto a black 96-well opaque-bottom plate (Nunc). Fluorescence was measured on a SpectraMax iD5 Multi-Mode Microplate using an excitation wavelength of 350 and an emission wavelength of 450 nm. Each data point was obtained from at least three distinct protein expressions and at least 12 individual reads. Data was plotted using Excel and GraphPad Prism.

Cryo-EM sample preparation

For the NIPA2Mg, NIPA2EDTA, and NIPA2A75T peak fractions were concentrated to ~1.5-3.5 mg ml−1 using a 100 kDa MWCO centrifugal concentrator (Millipore-Sigma) for cryo-EM sample preparation. For the NIPA2ATP sample, the concentrated protein (2 mg ml−1) was incubated for 10 min at 4 °C with 0.5 mM Adenosine 5′-triphosphate magnesium salt (ATP, Sigma Aldrich). All samples were centrifuged at 16k × g at 4 °C for 10 min prior to vitrification. For vitrification, 3 μl of purified protein was dispensed on a freshly glow-discharged (Pelco) QuantiFoil R1.2/1.3 Cu 300-mesh grid (Electron Microscopy Services) and vitrified using a Leica EM GP2 (Leica) plunger. The chamber was kept at 20 °C and 100% humidity, and the grid was blotted for 3 s at a blot distance of 43 mm using Whatman No. 1 filter paper (SubAngstrom) immediately before plunge-freezing in liquid ethane cooled to −180 °C by liquid nitrogen.

Data collection and processing

The NIPA2EDTA dataset

14,453 movies of NIPA2EDTA were collected using a Thermo Fisher Titan Krios transmission electron microscope (TEM) operating at 300 keV with a Falcon 4i direct electron detector (DED) at 0.93 Å physical pixel size at a nominal magnification of 130,000×. Movies were collected with a total exposure of ~60 e− Å−2, using a defocus range of −0.8 μm to −2 μm. All data collection was performed with a Selectris energy filter operating at 20 eV.

All data processing was performed in cryoSPARC v446. The 14,453 movies were subjected to patch motion correction and patch CTF estimation47 after which they were curated by removal of full-frame motion, ice thickness, CTF fit, intensity, and astigmatism outliers. The pruning resulted in a stack of 11,418 movies. An initial 2D template was created from 1500 manually picked particles and used to pick from 1000 micrographs. This resulted in a 697k particle stack, which was then subjected to inspection. Normalized cross-correlation and power threshold were used to remove false positives, and the particles were then extracted, binned at 2 × 2 (1.86 Å/pixel, 168 box size), and subjected to two rounds of 2D classification. The resulting 2D classes (107k particles) were used as a template for picking from the entire dataset. This yielded 8.9 million particles, which were inspected to remove false positives, extracted, binned 4 × 4 (3.72 Å/pix, 84 box size), and subjected to three rounds of 2D classification. The remaining 1 million particles were extracted, binned 2 × 2 (1.86 Å/pixel, 168 box size), and subjected to further three rounds of 2D classification. The best remaining particles (514k) were subjected to ab initio reconstruction (8 classes, C1, class similarity 0.1). The classes that exhibited density in the transmembrane region were kept, and the rest discarded. This process was repeated five times. The best ab initio maps from the final two ab initio iterations were compared and found to be identical. The particles were merged and duplicates removed, yielding a stack of 181k particles. These were subjected to another round of ab initio reconstruction (3 classes, C1, class similarity 0). The best two classes (144k particles) were then unbinned (0.93 Å/pix, 256 box size) and a 2D classification was performed to remove any remaining bad particles. The clean set of 135k particles was then subjected to non-uniform refinement (C2 symmetry and symmetry relaxation), resulting in a 4.02 Å reconstruction. This map was used to generate a mask over the transmembrane domains in UCSF Chimera48. The mask was used to perform local refinement of the NIPA2 protein that excludes the detergent micelle. This led to a reconstruction at an overall resolution of 3.7 Å, and the resolution range 2.7–8.9 Å.

These particles and volume were then used in a Local Refinement job along with a mask that excludes the detergent micelle to yield a final map of 3.5 Å.

Model building

The NIPA2EDTA model was built in the cryo-EM electron map in Coot49, using the AlphaFold50 predicted structure as a starting template (AF Q8N8Q9). The structure was built and real-space refined in Coot, with ideal geometry constraints where appropriate. The model was iteratively refined using the Phenix Real Space Refine utility31 by global minimization and rigid body, while maintaining high weight on ideal geometry and secondary structure restraints. Molprobity51 (http://molprobity.biochem.duke.edu/) was used to identify problematic regions, which were subsequently rebuilt in Coot. The refined NIPA2EDTA model was used as a template for building the remaining models. The LigandRecognizer30 ChimeraX plugin was used to examine non-protein densities. eLBOW52 was used to generate ligand restraints for ATP. Ligand restraints were edited in REEL53. Data was visualized using PyMOL54, ChimeraX55, and HOLE56.

Conservation analysis with Consurf

Conservation within the NIPA2 protein was analyzed using the Consurf server22,23. Human NIPA2 was used as an input structure along with a manually curated MAFFT57 sequence alignment of 213 NIPA2 sequences retrieved from a PSI-BLAST58 of a non-redundant sequence database.

Western blotting

All wild-type and mutant proteins were expressed in sf9 cells. The pellets were harvested, sonicated, and extraction was performed as described above. Following extraction, the lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was loaded on a 4–15% SDS-PAGE gel and run at 50 mV for 5 min and then 150 mV for 1 h. The gel was transferred onto a PVDF membrane, and blocked in 50 mM Tris pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween, 5% BSA (TBST-B) for 1 h at room temperature. After blocking, the membrane was washed three times in TBST and then incubated for 1 h in TBST-B supplemented with anti-FLAG antibody conjugated with Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP, A8592, Sigma Aldrich) (1:10,000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was then washed three times in TBST, incubated in horseradish peroxidase substrate for enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Pierce), and imaged. The western blot was performed three times from three separate protein preparations.
