Guideline adherence for the management of cardiovascular disease in primary care: a scoping review
Authors: Elena Zink, Pia Traulsen, Christoph Strumann, Jost Steinhäuser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWorldwide, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) persist as a leading cause of mortality. Clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) aim to support evidence-based decision-making and improve outcomes in primary care. However, application of these guidelines is often inconsistent, raising concerns about potential unconsidered barriers to implementation.ObjectivesThis review aimed to assess the extent to which CVD-related CPGs are followed in general practice and factors influencing the application.MethodsA structured literature review was conducted in August 2025 in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines, using MEDLINE (PubMed), Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. The inclusion criteria comprised original studies in English or German that focused on CPG adherence in primary care related to CVDs.ResultsAfter screening 791 studies, 37 were included. Most of these studies had a cross-sectional design and were conducted in Europe. Reported CPG application varied considerably, ranging from 4% to 100% depending on the disease area and evaluation method. Factors influencing CPG application included time constraints, systemic factors, and limitations within the guidelines themselves. Especially complex cases led to concerns about side effects and to deviations from the guideline.ConclusionDespite high awareness, the adherence to cardiovascular guidelines in general practice has a high variance. As patients in primary care are often multimorbid, evidence-based recommendations often need to be tailored to the patient's needs. Guideline enhancement should therefore emphasize practicality, comprehensibility, and the incorporation of general practitioners’ viewpoints.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately one-third of all annual deaths [1]. Given the high prevalence and preventability of CVDs, clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) have been established to optimize clinical practice in the diagnosis, treatment, and management of CVD. CPGs are systematically developed recommendations that support clinical decision-making and reflect the current state of scientific knowledge and evidence-based practice [2]. There are several national and international guidelines, some of which differ significantly between countries. Furthermore, inconsistencies have been identified within the extant guidelines regarding a single disease pattern. For instance, a comparison of worldwide hypertension guidelines demonstrates considerable discrepancies in the threshold values and treatment strategies [3]. In addition to differences between countries, there are also different recommendations within one country that could be considered. With regard to the example of hypertension in Germany, three major guidelines exist that demonstrate considerable overlap in many areas, whilst also suggesting divergent approaches for specific topics [4]. For example, the target blood pressure to be achieved through therapy is a strict target value independent of age, which differs between guidelines. However, recommendations also exist for target values lying within a risk- and age-dependent range [5–7]. Examining general practices in Germany reveals the existence of a fourth guideline that could be taken into account [8]. Translating these various recommendations into clinical practice remains challenging.

This is particularly apparent in the context of primary care. General practitioners (GPs) are often the first point of contact for patients suffering from CVD and play an important role in the long-term management and secondary prevention of disease. In general practices, clinicians have to balance standardized care for many different conditions in often multimorbid patients with the individual needs of those patients [9]. Therefore, the importance of evidence-based guidelines is high. While GPs are expected to follow the recommendations of these guidelines, they also need to consider a number of other factors including the prevalence of co-morbidities, patient preferences, and systemic barriers such as limited time and resources [10]. Although guidelines for CVDs are widely available and easy to access, research has consistently shown that adherence to guidelines in primary care is variable, suggesting that structural and behavioural barriers may exist [11]. Furthermore, in complex clinical situations, deviating deliberately from guidelines may not necessarily indicate poor quality, but rather an informed, personalized decision [12]. More specifically, adhering to guidelines designed primarily for the treatment of single diseases could have undesirable consequences when treating elderly individuals with multiple comorbidities [13].

Therefore, the aim of this review is to provide an overview of the current level of guideline application for the management of CVD in general practices. Primary objective is to demonstrate the current level of guideline adherence. Structural and individual barriers should be identified to help develop practical recommendations for GPs.

Methods

Study design

This scoping review was conducted based on the PRISMA-ScR Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping reviews (Supplementary File S1). A study protocol for this review has not been registered or published. As this study is a review of previously published literature, ethical approval was not required.

Data sources and search strategies

A structured literature research was conducted in October 2023 using three electronic databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. The initial search strategy was developed by the first and second authors and subsequently refined by the entire team. The search strategy employed terms from the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) along with additional keywords specific to the topic and the respective database. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to refine the search. The main keywords used in the literature search were ‘guideline adherence,’ ‘primary care,’ ‘family practice,’ ‘general practitioner,’ and ‘cardiovascular diseases.’ The full search strategy using PubMed as an example is shown in Fig. 1. The initial search was last updated in August 2025 to ensure comprehensive incorporation of the most recent literature. To identify additional relevant articles, a hand search was conducted by reviewing the reference lists of the included studies. The publications found in the search were entered into Covidence [14].

Search strategy PubMed.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The authors collectively determined the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies were included if they were available in English or German, took place in a primary care setting, and made a relevant statement to the research question. Studies were excluded if they did not take place in a primary care setting, if they did not involve GPs/family doctors/general internists, and if the full text was not available. Additionally, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were excluded as the focus of this study was on the actual level of guideline adherence, rather than the effects of an intervention or implementation strategies. Besides RCTs, other reviews were excluded. But any reviews meeting the inclusion criteria were checked for original research articles, which were then included individually in the selection process. There was no restriction on the date of publication.

Study selection and data extraction

Following the removal of duplicates, two independent reviewers (EZ and PT) screened the research results by title and abstract for relevance, followed by a full-text screening based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria previously mentioned. In case of disagreement on the relevance of the studies, a third, independent reviewer (CS) was involved, and the conflict was resolved by discussion.

A standardized data extraction form was developed by EZ and PT and adapted to collect key information from the included studies, including general information (e.g. country, year), study characteristics (e.g. study's aim, design, participants), main findings in relation to the research question (e.g. application, reasons for adherence or non-adherence), and key conclusions. The extraction of data was conducted by two reviewers (EZ and PT), independently. Any discrepancies that arose were resolved through discussion. If the resolution of a discrepancy was not possible through discussion, a third reviewer (CS) was involved. The extracted data was synthesized narratively, and key themes were identified.

Results

Study selection

After removing duplicates, 791 studies remained for title and abstract screening. By applying the above inclusion and exclusion criteria, 78 studies remained for full-text screening. Of these, a total of 37 were included in the review [15–51]. The flowchart is provided in Fig. 2.

PRISMA flowchart.

Study characteristics

Of the included studies, 26 had a cross-sectional design. The study population consisted of either physicians (mostly GPs) or a specific patient group to examine the adherence of the respective physicians. The most recent study was published in 2025 [32] and the oldest one in 1998 [28]. Twenty-two of the studies were conducted in Europe. Table 1 provides a detailed overview of the included studies.

Amount of guideline adherence

Analysis of the 37 included studies showed that physicians’ awareness and knowledge of CPGs and their use in practice were reported to be high in most studies. However, actual adherence was inconsistent, ranging from 4% to about 100% in some sections (e.g. individual case examples) of the studies. The way in which adherence was measured varied between the individual studies (e.g. questionnaires or analysis of prescriptions expected according to guidelines).

Adherence rates varied between disease areas. Thresholds for starting treatment were often higher than recommended, and use of lifestyle interventions was inconsistent [15–17, 19, 36, 42]. Statins and other lipid-lowering drugs were often not prescribed to high-risk patients [24, 30, 38]. Undertreatment with medication was also observed, particularly in primary prevention, with some studies reporting rates of up to 40% [26, 30, 48]. In contrast, the findings of certain studies demonstrated higher levels of adherence for the purpose of secondary prevention, whilst other studies revealed lower rates [24, 30, 37, 40, 41]. Factors influencing guideline adherence

Physicians’ knowledge of the guidelines and their positive attitudes towards them were associated with higher levels of adherence, although this effect was not universal in all settings or for all guideline areas [27, 29, 36]. Some studies have also considered the physicians’ reasons for deviating from the guidelines and their motivations for following the recommendations. The most common reasons for non-adherence were disagreement with the recommendations [16, 26, 28, 29, 33], patient engagement and compliance [19, 23, 26, 27, 35, 36, 43, 44], concerns about side effects or interactions with other drugs [15, 26, 35, 37, 51], and time constraints [16, 27, 33, 36, 44, 46]. Discussion

The aim of this review was to provide a comprehensive overview of the extent to which guidelines are being followed in the management of CVDs within general practice and which factors are influencing their application.

This scoping review demonstrates that, despite a high level of awareness and a generally positive attitude towards cardiovascular guidelines, adherence to these guidelines in general practice is often limited.

One reason for the lack of adherence to the guidelines in some of the studies was a lack of knowledge about their contents, as well as a lack of agreement with them. These findings align with the conclusions of other studies, which have identified these factors as significant barriers to guideline adherence [11, 52]. Concerns over the evidence and benefits of the guidelines appear to be an even greater barrier to compliance than a lack of knowledge. For instance, disagreement with recommendations was shown to be a more common barrier than a gap in knowledge [11]. Within this context, it is essential to differentiate the level of evidence supporting these recommendations. For instance, when deliberating the frequently discussed topic of LDL target levels, there are recommendations, particularly in primary prevention, that have only a low level of evidence regarding the recommended low values [53]. Consequently, deviations from such recommendations should be evaluated differently than deviations from recommendations with high evidence.

Inconsistency or selectivity in application was observed, particularly in relation to the use of medication in primary prevention. Studies have found varying levels of adherence in both primary and secondary prevention, with no clear trend apparent. However, some studies have shown higher adherence in secondary prevention settings [24, 30]. Different reasons for this could be discussed. On the one hand, various studies have shown that primary prevention measures are only limitedly effective in some cases. A review on using cardiovascular risk scoring in primary prevention as recommended in different guidelines found that they may have limited effectiveness in reducing CVD events and mortality [54]. A separate analysis indicated that preventive measures, such as statin administration, achieve a comparatively low absolute risk reduction in asymptomatic patients from lower risk groups [55]. On the other hand, people may adhere more to secondary prevention measures because clearer indications or stronger external incentives (e.g. disease management programmes) exist.

Another important factor influencing adherence to CVD guidelines is the limited amount of time available to GPs. Many of the included studies identified lack of time as a major barrier to guideline implementation. This is supported by an analysis, which discusses the ‘Time needed to treat (TNT)’ as an important factor when developing clinical guidelines [56]. This study demonstrates that, despite a high level of medical expertise and a positivity towards guidelines, practical implementation frequently fails due to the impracticality of implementing all recommendations within the timeframe available. To illustrate this point, considering a standard GP practice, the implementation of a screening programme in accordance with the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline on physical activity would require an estimated 15% of a GP's total annual patient contact time [56, 57]. This represents a significant challenge, particularly in GP care, which is characterized by high patient numbers and multimorbid cases, in addition to complex dialogue management [56]. It is therefore recommended that realistic time requirements be taken into greater account in the development of future guidelines, and if necessary, that the number and priority of recommendations be adjusted to ensure the practicability of care [56].

In addition to the frequently mentioned problem of time constraints, there are also structural barriers at the level of the guidelines themselves in GP care. Research has indicated that complex formats, overly theoretical language, and a lack of adaptation to healthcare practice play a key role in limited adherence. A potential solution may be the creation of shorter, practice-oriented guideline versions and clearly structured decision algorithms, as well as increased involvement of GPs in the development of these guidelines [58]. A further challenge is the existence of multiple guidelines for the same clinical pictures, for instance from different national specialist societies or international organizations. Different recommendations on the same topic, as well as incomprehensible or missing information, lead to uncertainty among users and present a significant challenge to implementation in everyday practice [59]. In this context, it is also important to consider the quality of the guidelines and the manner in which they are developed. In addition to the methodology, consideration should also be given to potential conflicts of interest on the part of the authors and the underlying studies, as well as how these are addressed. For instance, the website Leitlinienwatch.de provides a platform that evaluates medical recommendations in this regard [60].

Another factor influencing adherence to guidelines can be observed in the context of medical decision-making in complex care situations. GPs frequently report deviations from guidelines, particularly in cases involving patients with multimorbidity or advanced age, where standardized recommendations are not aligned with the overall clinical picture or may conflict with the patient's preferences [15, 19, 35, 39, 44]. This selective application appears to be both unavoidable and medically justified in conditions of real care. A publication addressing the use of CPG in patients with multiple conditions clearly demonstrates that it is not feasible to adhere to each single guideline for such patients. Not only would the patient have to take medications that could interact with each other and cause side effects, but recommendations would partly contradict with each other [13]. Such patients are a common occurrence in general practice, as demonstrated by a recent study from Switzerland, which found that approximately one-third of all patients have three or more chronic conditions, with this proportion rising to more than two-thirds among those over 60 [61]. In those cases, deviations from CPGs may be both appropriate and necessary, which could potentially explain the observed variation in adherence levels from 4% to 100% in this study. Within the German healthcare context, the S3 guideline ‘Multimorbidity’ from the German Society of General Practice and Family Medicine (DEGAM) explicitly emphasizes the necessity of prioritization and contextualization in complex patient cases [62]. Therefore, when creating future guidelines, it should be considered to make clearer distinctions between normative ideal standards and practical feasibility. Moreover, individualized decision-making processes should be recognized as an integral part of medical competence.

Furthermore, the differing socio-economic circumstances of the GPs and the variation in healthcare systems across the study countries may also impact adherence to the guidelines. In the European context, where public funding systems based on the Beveridge and Bismarck models are predominant, the nationwide availability of cardiovascular guidelines is practically guaranteed [63]. However, a study, which was conducted across 12 European countries, revealed that doctors cited limited time as a barrier to the utilization of guidelines [52]. This may also be due to financial reasons. In Germany, for example, remuneration is based on the EBM system (EBM: Einheitlicher Bewertungsmaßstab = Uniform valuation standard). GPs receive a fixed payment per patient, regardless of the length of treatment. As time is not a variable in remuneration, longer consultations or preventive advice are not specifically taken into account in the payment [64]. By contrast, other systems like in the USA are characterized by a high degree of fragmentation and a dominant private sector. Despite reforms such as the Affordable Care Act, around 9.5% of the population under the age of 65 remains uninsured in 2023 [65]. Moreover, of those who are covered by insurance, almost a quarter of working-age adults are underinsured [66]. This directly impacts adherence to guideline-based recommendations, such as preventive measures. It was found that people without insurance and people with temporary insurance were less likely to receive clinical preventive services [67]. When assessing doctors’ adherence to guidelines, these aspects should also be considered.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of the review lies in the comprehensive search string, which has been adapted to align with various database platforms. Additionally, the entire screening process and the data extraction were both carried out using the dual control principle. An additional neutral reviewer was always consulted in the event of discrepancies.

However, there are some limitations that must also be considered. A methodological limitation of the review is that adherence to guidelines in several studies is based exclusively on self-reports by physicians. This “self-reported adherence’ is subject to potential biases, especially social desirability, which can influence the results (e.g. studies 16,17,19,27,32,33,35,36,43,44,51). In the absence of objective validation, the extent to which adherence corresponds to actual care behaviour remains unclear. However, the findings from these studies have provided valuable insights into attitudes, perceived barriers, and physician understanding of guidelines. This is relevant for the development of practical implementation strategies.

Furthermore, RCTs were excluded from the analysis, as the primary objective was to assess the current state of guideline adherence, rather than the efficacy of specific measures to enhance adherence. The review is therefore based mainly on cross-sectional studies, which are suitable for describing the healthcare situation but have a limited level of evidence and preclude the establishment of causal relationships. This methodological decision results in a reduction of the informative value concerning the causes and mechanisms of action whilst also increasing the practical relevance of the results for current GP care.

Conclusion

This scoping review demonstrates that guideline adherence in general practice for CVDs is heterogenous, despite high levels of awareness and positive attitudes. The barriers to effective implementation of guidelines include the time needed for the specific single treatment, system frameworks, and guideline weaknesses, such as practical unsuitability and prioritization concerns, alongside conflicting recommendations. In addition, in complex care situations, GPs often make a conscious decision to deviate from the guidelines in order to meet individual care requirements. Consequently, non-adherence should not be viewed as inherently negative or inappropriate but rather evaluated within the context of the specific situation.
