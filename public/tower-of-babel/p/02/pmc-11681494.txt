Exploring the Impact of Hemoglobin on Cerebral Blood Flow in Arterial Territories and Surgical Outcomes: Potential Implications for Moyamoya Disease Treatment
Authors: Xinfeng Yu, Duo Xu, Junwen Hu, Yannan Yu, Lin Wang, Biao Jiang, Minming Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChanges in levels of hemoglobin would result in alterations of cerebral blood flow (CBF). However, the impact of hemoglobin on CBF in moyamoya disease (MMD) remains largely unknown. This study sought to determine whether CBF would be influenced by hemoglobin before surgical revascularization and to analyze the relationships between hemoglobin and CBF with clinical outcome after surgery in patients with MMD.Methods and ResultsWe prospectively enrolled adult patients with MMD undergoing surgical revascularization between June 2020 and December 2022. Preoperative CBF was measured in the territories of anterior, middle, and posterior cerebral arteries (ACA, MCA, and PCA, respectively) using 3‐dimensional pseudo‐continuous arterial spin labeling magnetic resonance imaging. Clinical outcome at 1 year after surgery was evaluated using the modified Rankin Scale. A total of 60 patients with MMD were included, with 25% (n=15) experiencing unfavorable outcomes. Patients with MMD exhibited lower CBF (ACA: P=0.007; MCA: P<0.001; PCA: P=0.014), compared with healthy controls (n=40). Hemoglobin was negatively and significantly associated with CBF (ACA: β=−0.45, P<0.001; MCA: β=−0.38, P<0.001; PCA: β=−0.54, P<0.001). CBF rather than hemoglobin was significantly related with clinical outcome (ACA: P<0.001; MCA: P<0.001; PCA: P=0.001), and CBF showed high discrimination in predicting clinical outcome (ACA: area under the curve, 0.84; MCA: area under the curve, 0.84; PCA: area under the curve, 0.80).ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that hemoglobin significantly influences CBF, and CBF has a high predictive value for clinical outcome in MMD. The optimal hemoglobin level before surgical revascularization should be further investigated.Keywords: cerebral blood flow, hemoglobin, magnetic resonance imaging, moyamoya diseaseSubject Categories: Moyamoya

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a unique cerebrovascular disease, characterized by the progressive stenosis of the intracranial internal carotid artery (ICA) and its proximal branches, accompanied by the development of collateral vessels at the base of the brain. 1 Although these collateral vessels serve as a compensatory mechanism in MMD, they are insufficient to restore normal cerebral perfusion, leading to a variety of symptoms such as stroke or transient ischemic attack, cognitive decline, and seizures. 2 In a real clinical setting, the factors associated with hemodynamic compromise in MMD are heterogeneous, including not only collateral circulation but a different stage of MMD, age, and vascular risk factors (eg, hypertension). 3 , 4 , 5 , 6

Interestingly, the level of hemoglobin would lead to alterations of arterial oxygen content, which can either increase or decrease cerebral blood flow (CBF). 7 For instance, in patients with anemia, CBF increases in response to the low hemoglobin; the opposite takes place in polycythemia. 8 , 9 Some studies have also found that in patients with arterial disease, even when hemoglobin fluctuates within the normal range, the negative correlation between hemoglobin and CBF persists. 10 , 11 Despite these previous studies, the impact of hemoglobin on CBF in patients with MMD is still unknown. Surgical revascularization remains the preferred procedure for patients with MMD because it can enhance cerebral perfusion, reduce the risk of future strokes, and improve neurological outcome. 12 Therefore, comprehending the relationship between hemoglobin and CBF before surgery can aid in developing personalized treatment plans for individual patients.

In this study, we used the arterial spin labeling (ASL) technique, a noninvasive magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) method, to measure CBF in the territories of the anterior, middle, and posterior cerebral arteries (ACA, MCA, and PCA, respectively). We hypothesized that CBF would be influenced by hemoglobin in patients with MMD before surgical revascularization. First, we assessed CBF in different arterial territories in patients with MMD by comparing with healthy controls. Then, we aimed to determine whether hemoglobin was a significant factor related to CBF and further analyze the relationships between hemoglobin and CBF with clinical outcome after surgical revascularization.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Participants

Patients with MMD were prospectively recruited in the Department of Neurosurgery in our institution between June 2020 and December 2022. All patients were admitted for surgical revascularization. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) MMD diagnosed by digital subtraction angiography, 13 (2) age >18 years, and (3) not in the acute stage of cerebral infarction. All patients underwent multimodal MRI scans and laboratory tests during the preoperative period. To avoid the influence of previous surgery on CBF, we excluded patients with a history of revascularization surgery. Body height and weight were measured in light clothing without shoes, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated. Vascular risk factors including hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking, and drinking were recorded. Disease duration was defined as the time interval between diagnosis of MMD and inclusion in the present study. The degree of functional deficits at admission was evaluated using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), a 7‐point scale ranging from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (death). 14 The initial onset of symptoms was categorized into 3 types: ischemic (including transient ischemic attack or ischemic stroke); hemorrhagic (including subarachnoid hemorrhage, intraparenchymal hemorrhage, or intraventricular hemorrhage); and others (including headache, cognitive decline, and visual disturbance).

To compare CBF, we recruited healthy controls through advertising on WeChat (a messaging and social media app) during the same period as the patients with MMD on the basis of the following criteria: (1) age >18 years; (2) no vascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, or hyperlipidemia; and (3) no history of neurological, cardiovascular, or neoplastic diseases. A total of 40 healthy controls were included in this study. The detailed recruitment process of healthy controls is shown in Figure S1. They were age‐ and sex‐matched with patients with MMD and consisted of family members of doctors, as well as the spouses and friends of patients. All controls had normal magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) findings, with no evidence of cerebral infarction or intracranial hemorrhage.

MRI Scans and Analysis

All patients with MMD and healthy controls underwent multimodal MRI scans using a 3.0T scanner (Discovery MR750; GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL) equipped with an 8‐channel phased array head coil. The following MRI sequences were used: (1) T2 fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (repetition time/echo time=8400/152 milliseconds, inversion time=2100 milliseconds, flip angle=90°, slice thickness=4 mm, matrix size=256×256, field of view=24 cm); (2) 3‐dimensional structural T1‐weighted imaging (repetition time/echo time=7.3/3.0 ms, inversion time=450 milliseconds, flip angle=8°, slice thickness=1 mm, matrix=256×256, field of view=25.6 cm); (3) 3‐dimensional pseudo‐continuous ASL (repetition time/echo time=4823/10.5 milliseconds, labeling duration=1500 milliseconds, post label delay=2025 milliseconds, flip angle=111°, slice thickness=4 mm, matrix=128×128, field of view=24 cm); (4) time‐of‐flight MRA (repetition time/echo time=21/2.5 milliseconds, flip angle=20°, slice thickness=1.4 mm, matrix=512×512, field of view=24 cm).

CBF was quantified using oxford_asl (part of Bayesian Inference for ASL MRI toolbox within FSL; https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/BASIL). The CBF maps were quantified in absolute units (mL/100 g per min) using the standard equation for pseudo‐continuous ASL 15 on the basis of blood T1 (T1b) calculated by Lu's equation. 16 In the MMD group, we measured CBF using individual T1b according to the hematocrit obtained from venous blood samples. Due to the absence of hematocrit data in the control group, we measured CBF using the standard T1b (1650 milliseconds), as recommended in the International Society for Magnetic Resonance in Medicine white paper. 15 The CBF maps were then registered into individual 3‐dimensional structural T1‐weighted images and normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute space using advanced normalization tools. The mean values of CBF in the territories of the bilateral ACA, MCA, and PCA were extracted using a digital arterial territory atlas. 17

The degree of steno‐occlusive changes in the intracranial arteries was assessed by the MRA scoring system (ICA or MCA: 0=normal, 1=stenosis of C1 or M1, 2=discontinuity of C1 or M1 signal, 3=invisible; ACA or PCA: 0=normal A2 or P2 and its distal, 1=A2 or P2 and its distal signal decrease or loss, 2=invisible). 18 PCA stenosis was defined as a PCA score of 1 or 2. The total MRA score ranged from 0 to 10 for each hemisphere, and it was further classified into 4 grades: grade 1 (MRA score, 0–1), grade 2 (MRA score, 2–4), grade 3 (MRA score, 5–7), and grade 4 (MRA score, 8–10). 18 If the 2 hemispheres were classified as different MRA stages, the higher stage was recorded.

The extent of collateral vessels was evaluated by a 4‐point scale on the basis of the arterial transit artifact sign on the subtraction ASL images 19 : 0=no or minimal ASL signal; 1=moderate ASL signal with arterial transit artifact; 2=high ASL signal with arterial transit artifact; and 3=normal perfusion without arterial transit artifact. The collateral score was evaluated on 2 slices corresponding to the Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score location (Figure S2).

According to the longest diameter of lesion, brain infarcts were classified into 4 categories: small (<1 cm), medium (1–3 cm), large (3–5 cm), and huge (>5 cm). 20

The ratings of MRA grade, PCA stenosis, collateral score, and brain infarcts were independently performed by 2 neuroradiologists (X.F.Y., with 11 years of experience; and D.X., with 14 years of experience) who were blinded to the findings of CBF, as well as clinical data. Interrater agreement was good for evaluating MRA grade (weighted κ, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.74–0.98]), PCA stenosis (κ, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.74–1.00]), collateral score (intraclass correlation coefficient, 0.78 [95% CI, 0.63–0.87]), and brain infarcts (weighted κ, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.68–0.90]).

White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) on fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery images were automatically segmented using deepNeuroSeg (https://pypi.org/project/deepNeuroSeg/), a deep fully convolutional network‐based method for automatic detecting WMHs. 21 The segmented WMH masks were further visually checked and manually corrected for false positives and negatives by an experienced radiologist (X.F.Y.) using ITK‐SNAP software (http://www.itksnap.org). The volume of WMHs was log10 transformed to normalize its skewed distribution.

Laboratory Tests

All patients underwent hematological examinations at baseline as part of the diagnostic workup. Venous blood samples were collected in the morning after overnight fasting, and this was completed within 48 hours before the MRI scans. The following hematological tests were used in this study: hemoglobin, hematocrit, red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, international normalized ratio, fibrinogen, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, total cholesterol, fasting plasma glucose, and homocysteine.

Surgical Procedures

All patients underwent combined revascularization surgery that consisted of both direct anastomosis between the superficial temporal artery and the cortical branch of the middle cerebral artery (superficial temporal artery–MCA bypass) and indirect bypass, namely, encephalomyosynangiosis. The surgical indications included (1) ischemic symptoms, such as ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack; (2) hemodynamic compromise on perfusion images, such as decreased CBF; and (3) intracranial hemorrhage. 22 The surgical procedures have been described previously in detail. 23

Clinical Outcome

The clinical outcome after revascularization surgery was assessed using the mRS via a telephone interview. We defined favorable outcome as an mRS score of 0 or 1 and an unfavorable outcome as an mRS score of 2 to 6. 24

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are shown as mean with SD or median with interquartile range, whereas categorical variables are presented as absolute and relative frequencies. We used independent samples t tests, χ2 test, or Fisher's exact test to compare CBF and other variables between patients and healthy controls. We used ANCOVA to compare CBF among patients with PCA stenosis, patients without PCA stenosis, and healthy controls, with age and sex as covariates, followed by a post hoc Fisher least significant difference test. The voxel‐wise comparisons of CBF among patients with PCA stenosis, patients without PCA stenosis, and healthy controls were further performed (Data S1). We used Pearson's correlation to analyze the associations between CBF and laboratory findings. The correlation coefficients were considered significant at P<0.017 (0.05/3) after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Univariate and multivariable linear regression analyses were used to identify the significant factors correlated with CBF (separately for each arterial territory), and variables with P<0.1 were included in the multivariable regression model using stepwise method. Patients with favorable and unfavorable outcomes were compared in univariate analysis, using independent samples t tests or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher exact test for categorical data. Effect sizes for CBF differences in the independent samples t tests were calculated using Hedges' g. We performed receiver operating characteristic curve analysis to assess the performance of CBF in differentiating patients with favorable outcome from those with unfavorable outcome. Area under the curve was calculated and the 95% CI for area under the curve was estimated using 5000 iterations of bootstrap resampling. We further performed decision curve analysis to assess the clinical utility of CBF in predicting the clinical outcome. In addition to voxel‐wise analysis, other statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

A total of 82 patients were recruited. We excluded 22 patients due to a history of revascularization surgery (n=20) and poor quality of ASL images (n=2). Finally, 60 patients were included, with a mean age of 46.05 years, 75% of whom were women. Among them, 14 patients (23.3%) presented with transient ischemic attack, 17 patients (28.3%) with ischemic stroke, 20 patients (33.3%) with intracerebral hemorrhage, 7 patients (11.7%) with headache, 1 patient (1.7%) with cognitive decline, and 1 patient (1.7%) with visual disturbance. Relationships Between Laboratory Findings and CBF

As shown in Figure 2A, among laboratory parameters, hemoglobin showed a negative association with CBF in each arterial territory (ACA: r=−0.49, P<0.001; MCA: r=−0.43, P<0.001; PCA: r=−0.55, P<0.001). The scatter plots of hemoglobin correlated with CBF in each arterial territory are displayed in Figure 2B. In addition, triglyceride was also negatively associated with CBF (ACA: r=−0.31, P=0.017; MCA: r=−0.31, P=0.016; PCA: r=−0.34, P=0.008), and high‐density lipoprotein showed a trend toward positive correlations with CBF (ACA: r=0.24, P=0.066; MCA: r=0.26, P=0.043; PCA: r=0.27, P=0.037).

Relationships Between CBF and Postsurgery Outcomes

All patients completed follow‐up telephone interviews, and 15 patients (25%) experienced unfavorable outcome after surgery. The mean follow‐up duration was 15.8±3.1 months. CBF was significantly higher in patients with favorable outcome in each vascular territory (Figure 4A). Other factors related to clinical outcome were also identified (Table 3), including mRS score at admission, hypertension, PCA stenosis, collateral score, infarct size, and WMH volume. However, no significant differences were found in any of the laboratory parameters.

DISCUSSION

Our study showed that patients with MMD exhibited significantly lower CBF in the territories of the ACA, MCA, and PCA, compared with healthy controls. Consistent with our hypothesis, hemoglobin had a significant impact on CBF in each arterial territory before surgical revascularization. Furthermore, CBF in each arterial territory emerged as a significant predictor of the clinical outcome after surgery, and improvement of preoperative CBF would be beneficial for patients with MMD.

In MMD, CBF has been demonstrated to be decreased as a result of the progressive stenosis of the ICA and its proximal branches, including the ACA and MCA. 25 Our study showed that patients with MMD exhibited decreased CBF in both the ACA and MCA territories, in keeping with the vascular pathological changes in anterior circulation. We also observed decreased CBF in the PCA territory, regardless of PCA stenosis. The PCA is thought to play an important role in blood flow compensation in conditions of ICA steno‐occlusion, such as MMD. 26 The development of leptomeningeal collateral circulation to ICA distribution from the PCA may be a potential cause for the decreased CBF in the PCA territory before PCA stenosis occurs.

We found that hemoglobin was a significant factor negatively correlated with CBF in each arterial territory in patients with MMD. Following cerebral ischemia, CBF would be compensated via collateral recruitment and regulated by a set of powerful mechanisms, such as cerebral autoregulation and neurovascular coupling. 27 , 28 In addition, CBF is affected by the level of hemoglobin due to the alterations of arterial oxygen content. 7 The inverse relationships between hemoglobin and CBF have been well described in hematologic diseases like anemia or polycythemia. 8 , 9 Importantly, even within the normal range of hemoglobin, its negative correlation with CBF still exists. 10 , 11 Although collaterals were detected to be the strongest factor associated with CBF in the MCA territory, hemoglobin showed the highest regression coefficients with CBF in the ACA and PCA territories. Therefore, hemoglobin may play a crucial role in regulating CBF for MMD. Preoperative assessment of hemoglobin can also assist in estimating CBF, particularly lack of CBF data.

We additionally found that WMH burden and BMI also had inverse relationships with CBF in each arterial territory. It is unable to determine whether WMHs directly result in the decreasing of CBF or decreased CBF leads to the development of WMHs. Notably, reversible WMHs have been reported in some patients with MMD after surgical revascularization or medical treatment, 29 , 30 , 31 indicating that WMHs in MMD may be caused by the decreased CBF. In terms of BMI, the finding of decreased CBF related to high BMI was consistent with previous studies. 32 , 33 Similar to WMHs, the causal relationship between BMI and CBF remains difficult to establish.

We further identified that variations in CBF across specific arterial territories hold high predictive value for the clinical outcome after surgical revascularization. Interestingly, we observed that in addition to CBF in the ACA and MCA territories, CBF in the PCA territory was also negatively related to clinical outcome. This finding is consistent with the notion that PCA involvement, such as PCA stenosis, was a strong predictor of poor prognosis in MMD. 34 On the other hand, our decision curve analysis results showed that managing preoperative CBF in each arterial territory could benefit patients with MMD, with a higher benefit observed in improving CBF in the PCA territory. Taken together, these findings emphasize the necessity for assessing CBF in each arterial territory before surgical intervention, particularly focusing on CBF in the PCA territory.

Our findings also have some potential implications for the treatment of MMD. First, preoperative adjustments of hemoglobin should be performed with caution because they may lead to an increase or decrease in CBF in the arterial territories. Future studies are needed to identify the optimal cutoff value of hemoglobin before surgery. Second, surgical revascularization of the anterior circulation is so far the first option in the treatment of MMD. 12 Superficial temporal artery superficial temporal artery–MCA bypass is the most common direct revascularization procedure, which mainly addresses the perfusion in the MCA territory. 35 Although superficial temporal artery–ACA and superficial temporal artery–PCA could be additionally performed if necessary, patients may be unable to tolerate multiple surgical interventions. Other noninvasive therapies to improve CBF in the ACA and PCA territories warrant further investigation. Third, the efficacy of surgical treatment for patients with asymptomatic MMD remains uncertain, and management strategies for asymptomatic MMD have not yet been established. 12 , 36 Compared with surgical treatment, noninvasive medical therapies are more favored by asymptomatic patients. Thus, improvement of CBF by adjusting hemoglobin might be worthwhile to explore in the future.

ASL is a noninvasive perfusion MRI technique that does not require contrast agents. One key parameter linking ASL signal changes to quantitative perfusion is the longitudinal relaxation time of blood (T1b), which strongly depends on hematocrit. In the absence of hematocrit, T1b is usually fixed to a typical value (eg, 1650 milliseconds at 3T). 15 Recently, several studies have recommended the use of hematocrit‐corrected T1b for quantifying CBF in patients with arterial steno‐occlusive disease or anemia, as it could reduce measurement errors. 37 , 38 Thus, in this study, we used individual hematocrit to calculate CBF for each patient with MMD.

Our study has several limitations. First, relying solely on the measurement of CBF is insufficient for capturing comprehensive cerebral hemodynamics. Other hemodynamic parameters like cerebral blood volume, cerebrovascular reactivity, oxygen extraction fraction should be further investigated. Second, due to the absence of hematocrit information in the control group, we quantified CBF using a fixed T1b, potentially leading to overestimated CBF. However, previous studies have shown that the average difference between CBF using a fixed T1b and CBF corrected by hematocrit is small (about 2 mL/100 g per min). 37 , 38 Even if the mean CBF of the control group decreases by 2 mL/100 g per min, it remains higher than the mean CBF obtained from patients with MMD. Meanwhile, we also lack hemoglobin data for the control group, which limits the analysis of the correlation between hemoglobin and CBF in the control group. Third, sex plays a significant role in influencing hemoglobin. The limited size of our study sample precluded us from performing a subgroup analysis following sex stratification. Nevertheless, our multivariable analysis demonstrated hemoglobin significantly correlated with CBF, irrespective of sex. Fourth, we only used the mRS to assess disability and outcome. Despite being the most commonly used scale, the mRS lacks sufficient detail to detect changes within specific domains (eg, cognition, language, emotion, or visual function). Fifth, the use of arterial rather than venous hematocrit would be more appropriate to estimate CBF. However, obtaining arterial blood samples in the clinical setting was challenging. Given the close correlation between venous and arterial hematocrit, 39 venous hematocrit was usually used as an alternative to arterial hematocrit. 40 , 41 Finally, of additional interest are the impacts of mRS at admission, hypertension, vascular status (including PCA stenosis and collateral circulation), and brain parenchymal lesions (including WMHs and infarcts) on clinical outcomes. However, the low number of outcome events limits our ability to perform multivariable logistic regression. This important issue requires further investigation.

In conclusion, hemoglobin had a significant inverse impact on CBF in each arterial territory (including the ACA, MCA, and PCA) before surgical revascularization in patients with MMD. CBF in each arterial territory had a high predictive value for the clinical outcome after surgery, and patients would benefit from improvement of preoperative CBF, especially in the PCA territory. Our findings could inform the development of individualized treatment plans before surgery.
