Analysis of Risk Factors Affecting Epilepsy Seizure Frequency with Statistical Models
Authors: Sirin Cetin, Gokce Yuce Onur, Eylem Topbas, Seda Dagli Sanver
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesEpilepsy is a chronic disease that can cause cognitive, psychological, and social problems due to recurrent seizures. Knowledge of the risk of seizure recurrence is crucial for patient treatment management. Therefore, we aimed to statistically evaluate the effects of stress, sleep deprivation, fasting/prolonged hunger, and excessive physical activity on seizure frequency, despite conflicting results in the literature.MethodsThis study prospectively analyzed data from 40 patients and a total of 60 seizures. The correlation between the proportional hazards assumption and survival rank was investigated using Schoenfeld residuals and correlation tests.ResultsThis study found that stress, sleep deprivation, fasting/prolonged hunger, and excessive physical activity increased the risk of seizures. However, some additional, unidentified environmental or genetic factors were found to increase seizure risk by 2.7 times within the established model.ConclusionWhile our study found that stress, sleep deprivation, fasting/prolonged hunger, and excessive physical activity may increase seizure frequency, it was also determined that some additional, unidentified environmental or genetic factors affect seizure frequency. The limited sample size, heterogeneity of the patient population, and reliance on patients’ subjective assessments constitute the primary limitations of this study. Furthermore, objective scales or validated measurement tools for sleep deprivation, fasting, stress, and excessive physical activity were not used. Further studies with larger, more homogeneous groups and objective assessment methods are needed to validate these findings.

Epilepsy is a chronic disorder characterized by recurrent seizures, which result from sudden and excessive electrical discharges within the cerebral cortex. Depending on the affected brain region, symptoms may manifest as muscle contractions, loss of consciousness, speech impairment, or sensory, visual, and behavioral disturbances. In some studies investigating the factors triggering seizures in patients with epilepsy, discontinuation of antiepileptic drugs, infections and metabolic disorders, sleep deprivation in the last 24 hours, stress, and skipping meals were reported as seizure-triggering factors.[1,2] By determining the factors that trigger epileptic seizures and supporting the use of nonpharmacologic methods, patients’ seizure management skills and quality of life can be improved.[2]

The time between seizures in a patient with epilepsy is related to the time of recurrence. However, this period is difficult to predict. Several methods have been presented in the literature for the statistical analysis of recurrent seizure data. These methods include the frailty model, counting process, Cox-based models, and stratified Cox approaches.[3,4] The epilepsy patient population is heterogeneous. Heterogeneity may be caused by observable risk factors, such as lack of sleep and stress, or unobservable risk factors, such as genetic factors. It is assumed that patients have different vulnerabilities and that a patient with epilepsy with high frailty will experience recurrence earlier than a patient with epilepsy with low frailty.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the risk factors that trigger epileptic seizures, particularly stress, and the effects of these factors on increasing seizure frequency by examining them using the hazard model.

Methods

Data from a total of 60 epileptic seizures were obtained by prospectively including 40 patients diagnosed with epilepsy. Ethics committee approval for the study was received from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Amasya University University on 03.02.2025 with decision number 2023/20. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The interval between the patients’ initial presentation following a seizure and the date of diagnosis was defined as the first interval; the time between the first and second seizures was designated as the first interseizure interval, and the period between the second and third seizures was defined as the second interseizure interval. Age, sex, educational level, socioeconomic status, seizure type (focal/generalized), number of antiepileptic drugs used (single/multiple), time since diagnosis, and the date of the last seizure were recorded.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical evaluation in this study was performed using SPSS version 26.0 and Stata 14 demo version (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). In this study, certain definitions were made to transfer the clinical aspect to the model. Whether the proportional hazards assumption was met was investigated using the correlation test between Schoenfeld residuals and the rank of life expectancy. A frailty model was used to describe unobserved heterogeneity. Seizure-free survival was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method. For the data analysis of patients with epilepsy, parametric regression exponential, lognormal, Weibull, Gompertz, and Weibull regression models were used. The AIC (Akaike information criterion) and BIC (Bayesian information criterion) information criteria were used in model selection. For the frailty term Z, the AIC and BIC information criteria were used to decide which of the gamma, inverse Gaussian, lognormal, and positive stable distributions to use.

Discussion

After the first unprovoked seizure, the recurrence rate was found to be 36%–37% in the first year, and it was reported that seizures recurred with a probability of 50% in the first 6 months.[5] In addition to known risk factors, the relationship between conditions such as sleep deprivation, prolonged hunger, stress, physical activity, and frequent seizures is controversial, and there are a limited number of studies in the literature on these topics. In this context, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between the presence of these conditions and the risk of seizures.

Although the association between psychiatric conditions and epilepsy is known, it has often been difficult to reliably assess the relationship between anxiety, stress, and seizures. The main challenge is that stress is a subjective concept and cannot be reliably measured. Many patients report that the likelihood of a seizure increases when they experience a stressful situation before a seizure. Decreased hippocampal volume, increased amygdala volume, and decreased serotonin receptors in the hippocampus are associated with both epilepsy and depression.[6] Stress leads to the activation of many proinflammatory pathways and is associated with inflammation of the central nervous system.[7] In one study, patients with epilepsy had 58% more frequent seizures on days when they reported being stressed.[8] In our study, consistent with the literature, patients with epilepsy who reported a history of stress in the preseizure period had 2.44 times more seizures than those who did not.

Sleep deprivation has been found to be a triggering factor for epilepsy. It is thought that sleep deprivation may increase neuronal excitability.[9] In this field, evidence on the relationship between sleep disturbances and increased seizure frequency is highly conflicting. In a study conducted with 124 patients with epilepsy, a positive correlation was observed between insomnia and seizure frequency.[10] However, in a retrospective study of 152 patients and a prospective study of 90 patients, insomnia was not associated with increased seizure frequency.[11] In our study, patients with epilepsy who reported sleep deprivation in the preseizure period had 1.95 times more seizures than those who did not.

In recent years, some studies have suggested that physical activity reduces seizure frequency. In both animal and human models of physical activity-induced stress, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis has been shown to influence adrenal steroids and neurosteroids and increase seizure susceptibility.[12] One study found that 10% of patients with epilepsy had exercise-triggered seizures.[13] In another study, physical fatigue was found to be associated with increased seizure frequency.[14] In this study, the frequency and type of activity were not specified, and only the presence of excessive physical activity in the preseizure period was questioned. Excessive physical activity in patients with epilepsy in the preseizure period was found to be associated with a 2.06-fold increase in seizure frequency.

The number of studies on hunger is limited. Alqadi et al.[15] found that fasting had a positive effect on seizure control in patients with epilepsy in a study conducted during Ramadan. In a study of 405 patients, meal skipping was questioned in patients with epilepsy, and 9.1% of the patients had skipped a meal before an epileptic seizure.[16] In this study, patients with epilepsy who reported prolonged hunger before their seizures had 1.88 times more seizures.

In addition to the factors examined that may affect seizure frequency in patients with epilepsy, the model identified a baseline hazard of 2.7, which has significant clinical implications. This finding suggests that even in the absence of the specific triggers examined in this study, namely stress, sleep deprivation, excessive physical activity, and fasting or prolonged hunger, patients still have a substantial intrinsic risk of seizure recurrence. From a clinical perspective, this indicates that although managing lifestyle-related triggers is essential, there is a strong underlying vulnerability, likely driven by unmeasured genetic factors, neurobiological pathways, or subclinical interictal activity. Therefore, clinicians should emphasize that trigger avoidance is a supportive strategy rather than a guaranteed preventive measure, and pharmacological management remains the cornerstone of treatment to address this high baseline risk. Beghi et al.[5] stated that the cause could not be identified in 50% of patients in their study. Not all factors that trigger seizures in patients with epilepsy have been elucidated, and further studies are needed in this field.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the data were based on patients’ self-reports, and no objective scales or standardized measurement tools were used to evaluate factors such as stress, sleep deprivation, excessive physical activity, and fasting or prolonged hunger. Consequently, reliance on subjective assessments without objective evidence may introduce recall bias. Second, the criteria for “fasting” were not specifically defined, leaving it unclear whether skipping a single meal or long-term caloric restriction, such as religious fasting, was the primary trigger. Additionally, the limited sample size and inherent heterogeneity of the epilepsy patient population may affect the generalizability of our findings. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results of the established model.

Conclusion

Assessing the risk of seizure recurrence is crucial for effective clinical management. This study demonstrates that stress, sleep deprivation, fasting or prolonged hunger, and excessive physical activity significantly increase seizure risk. Furthermore, our findings indicate a 2.7 baseline hazard, suggesting that undetected environmental or genetic factors may play a substantial role in this model. To further validate these results, additional research involving larger sample sizes and more homogeneous patient populations is warranted.
