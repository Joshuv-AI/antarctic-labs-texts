Cervical Cancer Screening Utilization and Its Correlates Among Women of Reproductive Age in North Central Nigeria: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Oluwasegun Ajala Akanni, Idris Muhammad Yakubu, Adegoriola Olubisi Ojurongbe, Matthew Olusegun Fijabiye, Olamide Ishola, Odunayo Akanni, Rabiu Muhammad Dansabo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Cervical cancer remains a significant public health challenge in Nigeria despite the availability of screening methods. This study investigated the utilization of cervical cancer screening and associated factors among women of reproductive age attending Federal Medical Center (FMC), Keffi.Methodology:A cross-sectional study was conducted among women aged 15-49 years using a systematic random sampling method. Of 400 eligible women approached, 384 completed the questionnaire (response rate: 96.0%). Data were collected with structured questionnaires and analyzed using SPSS v26. Chi-square tests and logistic regression were used to examine associations at p ≤ 0.05.Results:Only 24% of participants had ever undergone cervical cancer screening, while 76% had never been screened. Among those screened, 65.2% utilized government facilities, and 34.8% used private hospitals. Major barriers to screening included Cost (34.2%), Lack of Awareness (30.2%), not knowing where to access services (15.1%), and the belief that screening is unnecessary without symptoms (15.1%). Multivariable logistic regression confirmed Age (aOR = 0.08; 95% CI: 0.01-0.65; p = 0.018), Marital Status (aOR = 0.09; 95% CI: 0.02-0.43; p = 0.002), Educational Status (aOR = 3.43; 95% CI: 1.34-8.76; p = 0.010), Occupation (aOR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.11 – 0.79; p = 0.016), and Ethnicity (aOR = 0.32; 95% CI: 0.13 – 0.77; p = 0.011) as independent predictors.Conclusions:The uptake of cervical cancer screening services among women at FMC Keffi is low. Cost, poor awareness, limited knowledge of service locations, and misconceptions were key barriers. Marital status, education, occupation, and ethnicity predicted screening uptake. Reducing costs and improving targeted health education may increase utilization.

Introduction

Cervical cancer constitutes a major public health challenge globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).[1,2] According to the World Health Organization (WHO), it is the fourth most common cancer among women worldwide, with over 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths annually.[1] In Nigeria, cervical cancer is the second most common cancer among women, and its high burden has been associated with human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, inadequate screening programs, and limited access to healthcare services.[3,4]

Cervical cancer is largely preventable through early detection and treatment of precancerous lesions.[5] Effective screening methods include the Papanicolaou (Pap) test, visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA), and HPV DNA testing.[6] Nevertheless, the uptake of cervical cancer screening remains low in many LMICs.[7-10] Recognized associated factors include age, marital status, educational level, knowledge of cervical cancer, healthcare provider-patient relationships, service availability, and cost.[11-13] The WHO recommends that women aged 30–49 years undergo cervical cancer screening at least once in their lifetime.[14-16]

In Nigeria, several studies have reported low uptake [17,18], and recent ones from Lagos and Ibadan reported screening rates of 15.6% and 12.8%, respectively.[19-20] These findings underscore the need for evidence to inform interventions to improve screening utilization.[21-23]

The Federal Medical Center (FMC), Keffi, is a tertiary healthcare facility serving women from Keffi and surrounding communities in Nasarawa State, as well as neighboring states. Despite the availability of cervical cancer screening services and the facility's role as a major referral center, there is limited published information on screening uptake and barriers among women attending the hospital. Assessing utilization patterns and identifying barriers in this setting are essential for developing targeted interventions to improve screening uptake and contribute to cervical cancer prevention efforts. Therefore, this study assessed the utilization and associated factors of cervical cancer screening among women of reproductive age attending FMC Keffi, Nigeria.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted at the FMC Keffi, Nasarawa State, Nigeria. The study population consisted of women aged 15–49 years attending outpatient gynecological clinics and general outpatient clinics, which had the highest number of patient attendants.

The study included participants < 21 years (15-19 years old) because they constituted a substantial proportion of the women attending clinics at the FMC Keffi, and most were married. This might not be unrelated to the sociocultural and religious backgrounds of the populations around Keffi.

Sampling Method and Sample Size

Systematic random sampling was used to recruit 400 eligible women. Every fifth patient was recruited after the first participant was randomly selected by balloting. Of these, 384 completed the questionnaire and were included in the final analysis, giving a response rate of 96.0%.

The sample size was calculated using the Cochran formula, based on a prevalence rate of 39.0% from a previous study on knowledge of cervical cancer, risk factors, and barriers to screening among women of reproductive age in Nigeria. The calculated sample size was 365.6, and with a 10% attrition rate, the total sample size was rounded to approximately 400.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria included women aged 15–49 years attending outpatient clinics at FMC Keffi. Exclusion criteria included women who did not provide consent.

Data Collection

Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire adapted from previous studies on cervical cancer screening utilization among women in Nigeria. The questionnaire contained three sections: socio-demographic characteristics, screening service utilization, and barriers to screening. The instrument was pilot-tested among 20 women attending a general hospital outside the study area. Reliability testing yielded a Cronbach's alpha of 0.82. Necessary modifications were made before the main study commenced.

Data collection occurred over six weeks, with an average of nine participants recruited per day.

Data Analysis

Data were checked for completeness and clarity. Quantitative analysis was applied to closed-ended questions with predefined response options. Data were processed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Version 26. Frequency distributions, percentages, means, and standard deviations were computed and tabulated. Chi-square tests were used for bivariate analysis, with statistical significance set at p ≤ 0.05. Multivariate logistic regression was used to determine independent predictors of utilization or uptake. Model covariates were Age Range, Tribe, Religion, Marital Status, Educational Status, Occupation, and Marriage Type (all categorical). Logistic regression model predicting user (yes = event), and all predictors entered as categorical. Values were adjusted odds ratios (AOR) from the multivariable model.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical clearance was obtained from the FMC Keffi Health Research Ethics Committee (FMCKFIHRECO2609/23, dated 22/11/2023). Permission was obtained from the heads of the relevant departments. All participants provided informed consent and were assured that their responses would remain confidential and anonymous. For participants under 18 years of age, consent was obtained from a parent or guardian, and assent was provided by the participant.

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics of Respondents

The majority of respondents were aged 21-30 years (47.4%), followed by those aged 31-40 years (23.7%) and under 20 years (12.0%) (Table 1). The Hausa ethnic group was the most prevalent (55.2%), followed by Yoruba (10.4%), Igbo (16.7%), and others (17.7%). Religiously, 58.9% were Muslim, 31.0% were Christian, and 10.2% practiced African Traditional Religion. Most respondents were married (64.6%), followed by single (27.1%), divorced (4.2%), and widowed (4.2%) individuals. Educational attainment was diverse, with 39.1% having Tertiary Education, 26.6% having Secondary Education, 22.1% having no Formal Education, and 12.2% having Primary Education. Occupationally, Housewives constituted the largest group (38.8%), followed by Others (31.0%), Farmers (16.1%), and Civil Servants (14.1%). Most marriages were monogamous (75.5%) compared to polygamous (24.5%).

Utilization of Cervical Cancer Screening

Table 2 presents an overview of cervical cancer screening utilization among respondents. Only 24.0% of participants had undergone cervical cancer screening, while the majority (76.0%) had never been screened. Among those screened, 65.2% utilized government healthcare centers, and 34.8% opted for private hospitals. Regarding the timing of screening, 72.8% were screened within the past year, while 27.2% were screened more than a year ago. Notably, all respondents who underwent screening received negative results (100%). Among screening modalities, the Pap smear was the most commonly used (46.7%), followed by VIA (44.6%) and visual inspection with Lugol's iodine (VILI) (8.7%).

Religion was significantly associated with screening uptake (χ2 = 14.287, p = 0.001), with African Traditional Religion participants showing the highest uptake (41.0%), followed by Muslims (26.5%) and Christians (13.4%). Marital Status also showed a significant association with screening uptake (χ2 = 28.646, p < 0.001), with Divorced and Widowed participants demonstrating the highest uptake (50.0% each), followed by Married (27.4%) and Single (7.7%).

Educational Status was significantly associated with screening uptake (χ2 = 38.663, p < 0.001), with participants with Primary Education showing the highest uptake (53.2%), followed by those with Tertiary (28.0%) and No Formal Education (20.0%). Occupation was also significantly associated with screening uptake (χ2 = 27.881, p < 0.001), with Farmers demonstrating the highest uptake (38.7%), followed by Civil Servants (29.6%), Housewives (28.9%), and Others (7.6%). However, there was no significant association between marriage type and screening uptake (χ2 = 3.288, p = 0.070), although participants in monogamous marriages showed higher uptake (26.2%) compared to those in polygamous marriages (17.0%).

Variables with p < 0.05 in bivariate analysis were entered into the penalized multivariable logistic regression model due to substantial multicollinearity and complete separation (highest VIF = 7.5; AOR = 0.00; CI = 0.0–Inf), suggesting an unstable ordinary model. There were 384 observations. Reference categories were: Age: 21-30 years; Education: No formal education; Marital Status: Divorced/Widowed; Marriage Type: Monogamous marriage; Occupation: Civil servant; Religion: African Traditional Religion; Tribe: Hausa ethnicity.

In the penalized model (Table 4), Age <20 years was associated with lower odds of screening (aOR = 0.08; 95% CI: 0.01-0.65; p = 0.018) than Age 21-30 years. Primary education was associated with higher odds of screening than No Formal Education (aOR = 3.43; 95% CI: 1.34-8.76; p = 0.010). Single participants had significantly lower odds of screening than the Divorced category (aOR = 0.09; 95% CI: 0.02-0.43; p = 0.002). Participants in the “Others” occupation category had significantly lower odds of screening (aOR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.11 – 0.79; p = 0.016) than Civil Servants, while Igbo had lower odds of screening (aOR = 0.32; 95% CI: 0.13 – 0.77; p = 0.011) than Hausa. Religion was not a statistically significant predictor of screening uptake (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study at the FMC Keffi revealed a low cervical cancer screening uptake of 24.0%, with 76.0% of women aged 15-49 years never having been screened, aligning with broader trends in Nigeria, where uptake ranges from 1.4% to 38.8%.[19] This rate, while higher than some community-based studies, such as the one in Ilorin, which reported only 8.0% uptake [16], reflects persistent challenges in translating awareness into action. For instance, a study in Osogbo reported a comparable 25.9% uptake among mothers at infant welfare clinics, yet acceptance (61.6%) did not translate to utilization due to practical barriers.[15] The hospital-based setting of FMC Keffi likely facilitated slightly higher uptake due to greater exposure to healthcare information, but barriers like cost (34.2%), lack of awareness (30.2%), and uncertainty about service locations (15.1%) remain significant.[21] These findings align with a systematic review that identified financial constraints, lack of knowledge, and health service issues as primary obstacles in Ibadan, Nigeria. [19]

Government healthcare centers were the primary screening locations (65.2%), underscoring the importance of accessible public facilities.[19] Studies in Lagos emphasize the demand for community-based or nearby screening to address logistical barriers like distance.[17] Most screened respondents (17.4%) underwent screening within the past year, indicating timely screening; all received negative results, highlighting the value of early detection.[20] Screening modalities included Pap smear (46.7%), VIA (44.6%), and VILI (8.7%), consistent with recommended low-cost methods for resource-limited settings.[6,8,12] However, low overall utilization mirrors trends in some parts of Nigeria, where VIA predominated but uptake remained limited.[15]

Financial barriers, misconceptions, and cultural attitudes significantly hinder utilization.[22] In Nnewi, fear of results and lack of information about test locations were key predictors of non-uptake [18], whereas in rural Southwest Nigeria, superstitious beliefs and spousal consent were reported as barriers.[17]

Socio-demographic factors influenced screening uptake, with significant associations with Age (highest in 41-50 years), Ethnicity (predominantly Hausa), Religion (Traditional Religion highest), Marital Status (Divorced/Widowed highest), Education (Primary and Tertiary highest), and Occupation (Farmers highest). These findings align with previous studies in Nigeria that associate education and positive attitudes with increased odds of screening.[16] Cultural factors like spousal approval and religious influences also shape uptake.[19] The lack of association with marriage type suggests that other relational dynamics, such as partner involvement, may be more influential. Overall, addressing financial, informational, and cultural barriers through subsidized services, education, and culturally sensitive approaches is critical to improving screening rates.[17]

Limitations

A cross-sectional design limits causal inference. A key limitation was the reluctance of some participants to respond, particularly those who had waited a considerable time for physicians. Efforts were made to shorten interview durations and reassure participants. Additionally, as a hospital-based study, the findings may not fully reflect the situation in community, primary, or secondary healthcare settings, limiting generalizability. Furthermore, recall and social desirability biases could not be completely avoided. Including adolescents (age < 21 years) who are not ordinarily eligible for screening under current guidelines may introduce bias and lower uptake rates. Also, participants were recruited from gynecological and general outpatient clinics, which may have introduced selection bias.

Conclusions

Cervical cancer screening uptake among women attending FMC Keffi was low despite the availability of screening services. Screening uptake was significantly associated with socio-demographic characteristics, particularly age, marital status, education, ethnicity, religion, and occupation. Commonly reported barriers included cost, lack of awareness, and poor knowledge of service locations. Interventions to reduce financial barriers and improve awareness may enhance screening utilization.

Policy Implications

Policy efforts should prioritize reducing barriers to cervical cancer screening. Subsidized or free screening at government facilities may increase uptake. Integrating screening into antenatal care and routine health visits can expand access. Intensive health education is needed to address misconceptions and raise awareness of cervical cancer screening.

Recommendations

Improving cervical cancer screening uptake requires coordinated action among multiple stakeholders. Policymakers should strengthen awareness campaigns, integrate screening into maternal health services, and subsidize screening and treatment. Healthcare providers should enhance counseling, address misconceptions, recommend screening during routine visits, and improve access to affordable, same-day screening.

By implementing these policy implications and recommendations, cervical cancer screening uptake can be improved, ultimately reducing the incidence of cervical cancer and improving maternal health and well-being.
