Six‐month real‐world amyloid PET outcomes after lecanemab: Greater amyloid reduction at the MCI stage than in dementia
Authors: Geon Ha Kim, Hai‐Jeon Yoon, Eun Hee Kim, Ji‐Young Kim, Jiyoung Yeom, Seo Young Kang, Boeun Lee, Jee Hyang Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONEvidence from routine clinical practice regarding stage‐dependent responses to lecanemab remains limited. We examined early amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) and clinical trajectories according to disease stage in a real‐world cohort.METHODSAmong 89 patients who received lecanemab, 50 individuals with baseline amyloid positivity and follow‐up amyloid PET were included in the primary amyloid PET efficacy analysis. Amyloid burden was quantified using the Centiloid scale. Longitudinal Centiloid trajectories were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models including disease stage, time from baseline amyloid PET, and the disease stage × time interaction, with adjustment for cumulative dose exposure, age, sex, and apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 carrier status. Cognitive and functional outcomes were assessed using mixed‐effects models.RESULTSAmyloid burden decreased substantially during follow‐up. After adjustment for cumulative dose exposure, age, sex, and APOE ε4 carrier status, the disease stage × time interaction was significant (β = +1.62 Centiloid/month; 95% confidence interval, 0.80 to 2.43; p < 0.001), indicating a faster rate of Centiloid reduction in patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) than in those with dementia (estimated −2.34 vs. −0.72 Centiloid/month). Conversion to amyloid‐negative status (full treatment‐related amyloid clearance) occurred in 9 of 50 patients (18.0%) and was more frequent at the MCI stage (p = 0.022). Patients treated at the MCI stage also showed more favorable short‐term cognitive and functional trajectories than those treated at the dementia stage.DISCUSSIONIn real‐world clinical practice, lecanemab treatment was associated with substantial amyloid reduction, with faster amyloid clearance observed at the MCI stage. These findings support the potential value of earlier intervention with anti‐amyloid therapy, although longer follow‐up and controlled comparisons are needed to determine whether early biomarker changes translate into sustained clinical benefit.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by a prolonged preclinical and prodromal phase during which amyloid beta (Aβ) pathology accumulates years before the onset of overt dementia. 1 The development of anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies has marked a paradigm shift toward disease‐modifying therapies that directly target the upstream pathological cascade of AD. 2 , 3 Among these agents, lecanemab, a humanized immunoglobulin G1 monoclonal antibody targeting soluble Aβ protofibrils, has demonstrated consistent amyloid‐lowering effects in randomized controlled trials involving patients with early symptomatic AD. 4 , 5

In the phase 3 Study to Confirm Safety and Efficacy of Lecanemab in Participants with Early Alzheimer's Disease (CLARITY AD) trial, lecanemab produced substantial reductions in amyloid burden and slowed cognitive and functional decline over 18 months in patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to AD and mild AD dementia. 5 However, translating these trial results into routine clinical practice raises several unresolved questions. Patients encountered in real‐world settings are typically more heterogeneous with respect to disease stage, comorbidities, treatment adherence, and monitoring practices, and therapeutic delivery often occurs under conditions that differ from those of controlled trials.

Emerging evidence suggests that baseline amyloid burden and disease severity may influence biological responsiveness to anti‐amyloid therapy, potentially determining whether patients reach amyloid‐negative thresholds during treatment. 5 , 6 , 7 At the same time, interpretation of early clinical trajectories after amyloid reduction remains challenging. Amyloid removal is hypothesized to precede and possibly enable subsequent stabilization of tau pathology and neurodegeneration, processes that may require longer observation periods to translate into measurable cognitive benefit. 8 , 9

Against this background, we conducted a retrospective observational study of patients treated with lecanemab at two academic medical centers in Korea. The objectives of this study were to characterize the magnitude and variability of amyloid reduction over 6 months in real‐world clinical practice, to examine whether amyloid clearance and full treatment‐related amyloid clearance (full TRAC) 10 differ by disease stage, and to explore the relationship between changes in amyloid burden and short‐term cognitive and functional outcomes.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This retrospective observational study included all patients who received at least one infusion of lecanemab at Ewha Medical Center, comprising Ewha Womans University Mokdong Hospital and Ewha Womans University Seoul Hospital, between January 1 and December 31, 2025.

In routine clinical practice, lecanemab was primarily offered to individuals with early AD, including MCI due to AD and mild AD dementia. In selected circumstances, patients with subjective cognitive decline or more advanced dementia (Clinical Dementia Rating [CDR] = 2) were also treated after individualized discussion of potential risks and benefits and with agreement from patients and caregivers. For the purposes of the present analysis, disease stage was classified at treatment initiation as MCI or dementia due to AD, based on multidisciplinary clinical evaluation and established diagnostic criteria. Evidence of cerebral amyloid pathology was required prior to treatment initiation in all cases. Amyloid positivity was determined by expert visual interpretation of amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) scans by board‐certified nuclear medicine physicians.

For longitudinal amyloid PET analyses, patients were included if they had (1) a baseline amyloid PET scan with quantitative Centiloid measurement and (2) a follow‐up PET scan obtained ≈ 6 months after treatment initiation. Patients who had not yet reached the scheduled follow‐up time point at the time of data cutoff were not considered dropouts. Among 51 patients with both baseline and follow‐up amyloid PET data, one participant with dementia had a baseline Centiloid value below the amyloid‐negativity threshold despite visual amyloid positivity and was excluded from the primary amyloid PET efficacy analysis because of limited potential for further Centiloid decline. The primary amyloid PET efficacy cohort therefore comprised 50 patients.

The study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the Ewha Medical Center (EUMC 2025‐02‐008 & EUMC 2025‐02‐012). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for treatment was obtained from patients or their legally authorized representatives.

Lecanemab administration and safety monitoring

During the study period, lecanemab was delivered in a non‐reimbursed, real‐world clinical context in Korea. Initial dosing and subsequent escalation were individualized according to clinician judgment, taking into account patient age, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 carrier status, concomitant antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, and baseline neuroimaging findings. In general, treatment was initiated at lower doses, with gradual escalation toward the target dose of 10 mg/kg only when no safety concerns were identified. Decisions regarding dose escalation were made at the discretion of the treating neurologist, taking into account interim clinical assessments, the absence of infusion‐related reactions, and follow‐up brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings to monitor for amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIAs). All patients were monitored according to institutional safety protocols throughout the treatment period. Concomitant use of acetylcholinesterase inhibitors or memantine was permitted and recorded. Treatment continuation or discontinuation was determined by treating physicians based on tolerability, adverse events, or caregiver preference.

Neuroimaging assessment

Amyloid PET

Baseline amyloid PET data were obtained either from previously performed clinically indicated scans confirming amyloid positivity or from newly acquired scans conducted for treatment eligibility assessment. Follow‐up amyloid PET imaging was performed at ≈ 6 months, operationally defined as the time point after completion of at least 13 lecanemab infusions and prior to the 14th infusion.

All amyloid PET scans were acquired using 1 8F‐florbetaben (Neuraceq), manufactured in accordance with the approval process of the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS), at both participating hospitals. PET/computed tomography (CT) acquisition was performed according to each institution's standardized clinical protocol using dedicated PET/CT scanners (Discovery MI, GE HealthCare; Biograph mCT, Siemens Healthineers). Images were acquired 90 to 100 minutes after a bolus injection of 300 MBq 1 8F‐florbetaben.

Quantitative amyloid burden was assessed using the Centiloid method, which was used to enable standardized quantification of cerebral amyloid burden across PET tracers and imaging platforms. 11 Centiloid values were calculated using a commercially available software platform, BTXBrain (version 1.1.2, Brightonix Imaging). 12 , 13 , 14 All Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) images were imported into the software and processed to calculate Centiloid values without any additional preprocessing, including partial volume correction or harmonization of image quality across scanners or vendors. The software applies the original Centiloid Project regions of interest (ROIs) to calculate global standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs), using the whole cerebellum as the reference region, and subsequently converts SUVRs to Centiloid values using the original Centiloid equations. The same software and processing pipeline were used at both hospitals to ensure methodological consistency.

MRI and ARIA assessment

Brain MRI was performed at baseline and during follow‐up using standard protocols, including three‐dimensional T1‐weighted imaging, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI), and susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI). SWI was used to assess cerebral microbleeds and ARIA–hemorrhage (ARIA‐H), while FLAIR sequences were used to evaluate ARIA–edema/effusion (ARIA‐E). The number of cerebral microbleeds and the presence of ARIAs were systematically recorded and included in safety analyses.

Cognitive and functional assessments

Cognitive function was assessed using standardized neuropsychological instruments. Global cognition was evaluated using the Korean version of the Mini‐Mental State Examination, 2nd Edition (K‐MMSE‐2), and the extended version of the MMSE‐EV (K‐MMSE‐2: EV). 15 The MMSE–EV is an expanded form of the standard MMSE that incorporates additional subtests assessing episodic memory, language, visuospatial ability, and executive function, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 90, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance. Dementia severity was assessed using the Korean version of the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale and the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SOB). 16 , 17 Functional status was evaluated using the Korean version of the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (K‐IADL) 18 and the Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study–Activities of Daily Living (ADCS‐ADL) scale. 19 Global disease stage was assessed using the Korean version of the Global Deterioration Scale (GDS). 20 Depressive symptoms were measured using the Korean version of the Geriatric Depression Scale, 21 and caregiver burden was assessed using the Korean version of the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI). 22

Outcomes

The primary amyloid PET outcome was longitudinal change in Centiloid values over time. For descriptive analyses, the absolute change in Centiloid (ΔCentiloid) was defined as the Centiloid value at the follow‐up minus the baseline Centiloid value, with more negative values indicating greater amyloid reduction. Relative amyloid reduction (%) was calculated as ΔCentiloid divided by the baseline Centiloid value and multiplied by 100.

Full TRAC was defined as conversion from amyloid‐positive to amyloid‐negative status at follow‐up. 10 Amyloid status was determined by expert visual assessment by board‐certified nuclear medicine physicians. For research standardization, full TRAC was operationalized as follow‐up Centiloid < 25 among participants with baseline Centiloid ≥ 25. 23 Changes in cognitive and functional scores over the follow‐up period were included as clinical outcomes.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as number (percentage). Group comparisons were conducted using t tests or chi‐squared tests, as appropriate. To account for variability in follow‐up intervals, longitudinal amyloid PET, cognitive, and functional outcomes were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models with exact time from baseline assessment modeled as a continuous variable in months. For amyloid PET outcomes, time was defined from baseline amyloid PET; for cognitive and functional outcomes, time was defined from baseline cognitive assessment. Fixed effects included disease stage, time, the disease stage × time interaction, cumulative dose exposure, age, sex, and APOE ε4 carrier status, with participant‐specific random intercepts. Cumulative dose exposure was calculated as the total cumulative lecanemab dose divided by body weight and expressed in mg/kg; for model interpretability, it was entered per 100 mg/kg. Models were estimated using maximum likelihood with robust standard errors clustered by participant. Descriptive change‐score analyses and Spearman correlations between amyloid reduction and clinical changes were considered exploratory. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata (version 19; StataCorp). A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics and follow‐up availability

Baseline characteristics of the total cohort (n = 89) and comparisons between participants with (n = 51) and without (n = 38) 6‐month amyloid PET follow‐up are summarized in Table S1 in supporting information. Among the 38 participants who did not undergo 6‐month follow‐up amyloid PET imaging, 31 had not yet reached the scheduled imaging time point, 2 declined PET imaging, and 5 discontinued treatment before the follow‐up scan. The mean age was 71.4 ± 8.2 years, and 70.8% were female. Most participants were diagnosed with dementia (67.4%), followed by MCI (31.5%), with mean K‐MMSE‐2 22.0 ± 5.5 and CDR‐SOB 4.7 ± 3.1. Approximately 60.7% of participants were APOE ε4 carriers. No significant differences were observed between participants with and without follow‐up PET in age, sex, education, baseline cognitive and functional measures, APOE ε4 status, vascular comorbidities, or concomitant medications. Baseline imaging characteristics were also comparable, including Centiloid values (76.2 ± 22.4 vs. 80.7 ± 30.1, p = 0.422) and cerebrovascular imaging markers, including Fazekas scale scores for white matter hyperintensities and number of cerebral microbleeds.

Treatment exposure, discontinuation, and safety outcomes

Treatment exposure, discontinuation, and safety outcomes are summarized in Table S2 in supporting information. Among the 89 patients who received at least one infusion of lecanemab, the mean number of infusions administered during the observation period was 11.7 ± 7.5. At the time of analysis, 75 patients (84.3%) remained on ongoing lecanemab treatment, while 14 patients (15.7%) discontinued treatment during follow‐up. The most common reason for treatment discontinuation was adverse events (n = 9), followed by caregiver request (n = 4) and other reasons (n = 1). Infusion‐related reactions occurred in 10 patients (11.2%). One patient (1.1%) experienced symptomatic ARIA‐E. Asymptomatic ARIA‐H occurred in 13 patients (14.6%), while no symptomatic ARIA‐H events were identified. Most patients (91.0%) received dose titration, starting at a lower dose followed by gradual escalation to the target dose of 10 mg/kg, while eight patients (9.0%) initiated treatment at the standard dose of 10 mg/kg from the first infusion.

Among the 50 patients included in the primary amyloid PET efficacy cohort, treatment exposure did not differ significantly between the MCI and dementia groups with respect to the number of infusions received, the proportion receiving ≥ 13 infusions, attainment of the target dose of 10 mg/kg, or cumulative dose exposure (Table 1).

Changes in amyloid burden and cognitive function

Amyloid PET trajectories

Amyloid PET outcomes and treatment exposure stratified by disease stage are summarized in Table 1. Patients with dementia showed higher amyloid burden than those with MCI at both baseline and follow‐up. In contrast, the interval between baseline and follow‐up amyloid PET and cumulative dose exposure did not differ significantly between the two groups.

To account for variability in PET scan intervals, longitudinal Centiloid trajectories were analyzed using a linear mixed‐effects model with exact time from baseline amyloid PET as a continuous variable (Table 2 and Figure 1). After adjustment for cumulative dose exposure, age, sex, and APOE ε4 carrier status, the disease stage × time interaction was significant (β = +1.62 Centiloid/month, 95% confidence interval 0.80 to 2.43, p < 0.001), indicating a slower rate of amyloid reduction in the dementia group than in the MCI group. The estimated monthly Centiloid change was −2.34 in the MCI group and −0.72 in the dementia group. Full TRAC occurred in 9 patients (18.0%) overall and was more frequent in the MCI group than in the dementia group (p = 0.022). After achieving full TRAC, all but one patient did not continue lecanemab treatment, primarily because of the financial burden of continued non‐reimbursed therapy. Treatment decisions after amyloid clearance were made individually through discussion among treating physicians, patients, and caregivers.

Correlation between amyloid reduction and cognitive changes

Exploratory pairwise Spearman correlation analyses between amyloid PET changes and clinical changes are summarized in Table S4 in supporting information. Absolute Centiloid reduction showed limited correlations with clinical changes, whereas relative amyloid reduction was associated with changes in K‐MMSE‐2, MMSE‐EV, CDR‐SOB, instrumental ADL, and ADCS‐ADL. Absolute Centiloid reduction was associated only with change in ZBI.

DISCUSSION

In this real‐world cohort, lecanemab treatment was associated with substantial amyloid reduction over 6 months with an acceptable safety profile. Patients treated at the MCI stage showed a faster rate of Centiloid reduction than those treated at the dementia stage. This stage‐dependent difference remained significant after adjustment for cumulative dose exposure, age, sex, and APOE ε4 carrier status. Longitudinal cognitive and functional trajectories also differed by disease stage, with significant disease stage × time interactions for K‐MMSE‐2, CDR‐SOB, I‐ADL, and MoCA.

The faster amyloid clearance observed in the MCI group may reflect stage‐dependent biological differences in amyloid pathology and treatment responsiveness. Several mechanisms may underlie this stage‐dependent difference in amyloid clearance. Lecanemab preferentially binds soluble Aβ protofibrils, which are more abundant in early AD stages. 5 , 24 , 25 With advancing disease, a larger fraction of amyloid may exist in more compact, fibrillar, or vascular forms, potentially reducing the availability of antibody‐accessible targets. 26 , 27 Differences in vascular and neuroimmune environments may also contribute, including blood–brain barrier (BBB) alterations and perivascular clearance changes. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 However, these mechanisms remain speculative and were not directly assessed in this study.

In addition, full TRAC was more frequently observed in patients with MCI than in those with dementia. This finding should be interpreted cautiously because full TRAC is a threshold‐based outcome influenced by both baseline amyloid burden and follow‐up amyloid status. Patients with lower baseline amyloid burden may be more likely to cross the amyloid‐negativity threshold during short‐term follow‐up, even when the absolute magnitude of amyloid reduction is modest. Therefore, full TRAC should be considered a clinically intuitive but threshold‐dependent marker of amyloid clearance rather than a direct measure of treatment effect magnitude.

Given that lecanemab targets soluble aggregated Aβ species, including protofibrils, and that biomarker reaccumulation has been observed after treatment discontinuation, 4 , 5 , 24 continued or maintenance dosing after the initial treatment period has a biologic and pharmacodynamic rationale. 34 In our cohort, all but one patient who achieved full TRAC did not continue lecanemab treatment after amyloid clearance, primarily because of financial burden. This real‐world pattern highlights the need for evidence‐based guidance on treatment continuation, discontinuation, or maintenance strategies after amyloid clearance.

Cognitive and functional outcomes showed modest overall worsening over short‐term follow‐up, but the trajectories differed by disease stage. Patients treated at the MCI stage showed relatively stable cognitive and functional trajectories, whereas those treated at the dementia stage showed greater decline in K‐MMSE‐2 and MoCA and greater worsening in CDR‐SOB and I‐ADL. This pattern is consistent with the concept that clinical stability during anti‐amyloid therapy may be more likely when treatment is initiated before the dementia stage, when downstream tau pathology, neurodegeneration, comorbidities, and loss of cognitive reserve may be less advanced. 3 , 6 In the absence of an untreated comparison group, however, these differences cannot be attributed solely to therapeutic effect and are also compatible with known variations in natural history across stages.

The exploratory correlation analyses between amyloid PET changes and clinical changes should be interpreted cautiously. Although relative amyloid reduction was associated with several cognitive and functional changes, this measure is mathematically dependent on baseline Centiloid values and may therefore be confounded by pre‐treatment amyloid burden. In addition, these analyses were not adjusted for multiple comparisons and should be regarded as exploratory.

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, the retrospective design and modest sample size limit causal inference and statistical power. Second, the follow‐up duration was limited to 6 months, which may be insufficient to detect delayed clinical effects of amyloid reduction. Third, tau and neurodegeneration biomarkers were not systematically assessed, precluding direct evaluation of downstream biological effects. Fourth, cognitive outcomes were assessed using multiple instruments with varying sensitivity to short‐term change, and practice effects cannot be fully excluded. Fifth, follow‐up PET completion may introduce selection bias, although comparisons between patients with and without follow‐up PET showed no significant clinical differences, as summarized in Table S1. Sixth, the number of patients who initiated lecanemab at the standard dose was small, limiting comparison with dose titration. Finally, baseline amyloid PET was not always obtained immediately before treatment initiation. Therefore, baseline Centiloid values may not have fully reflected amyloid burden at the actual start of lecanemab treatment. This issue may be particularly relevant because the interval between baseline amyloid PET and first lecanemab infusion tended to be longer in patients with dementia than in those with MCI, although the difference was not statistically significant. To address variability in PET timing, longitudinal Centiloid trajectories were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models with exact time from baseline amyloid PET as a continuous variable, with adjustment for cumulative dose exposure and relevant clinical covariates. Nevertheless, this approach cannot fully eliminate potential bias related to variability in the timing of PET acquisition.

In summary, lecanemab therapy in routine clinical practice was associated with meaningful early amyloid reductions. Stage‐related differences were observed, with patients treated at the MCI stage demonstrating greater biomarker change and more stable short‐term trajectories. These findings are consistent with the concept that earlier intervention may facilitate more efficient amyloid clearance, although longer follow‐up and controlled comparisons will be required to determine how these early biomarker changes translate into sustained clinical benefit.
