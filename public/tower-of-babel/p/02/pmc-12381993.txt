Hydration-induced hydronephrosis in healthy adults: a diagnostic pitfall in renal ultrasound imaging
Authors: Liqa A. Rousan, Khaled J. Zaitoun, Sofyan Freihat, Mohammad A. Albatayneh, Abdel Rahman M. Al Serhan, Aghyad Alsalkhadi, Saad Tayyem, Ali M. Abdel Kareem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHydronephrosis is a common finding on renal ultrasound, but its physiological causes-such as hydration-induced distention-remain under-recognized in healthy individuals. Misinterpreting such cases as pathological may lead to unnecessary investigations. This study aimed to determine whether rapid oral hydration induces transient hydronephrosis in healthy adults, and to characterize its timing, laterality, and reversibility.MethodsFifty-six healthy volunteers (30 males, 26 females; aged 19–45 years) without chronic illnesses underwent baseline renal and bladder ultrasound, followed by rapid oral hydration (20 ml/Kg within 15–30 min). Ultrasound scans were repeated at 60 and 90 min post-hydration, before and after voiding. Hydronephrosis was graded using the Radiology classification system. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.005.ResultsFifty-six healthy adults (30 males, 26 females; aged 19–45 years) were enrolled in the study. At 60 min, 46.6% of participants developed hydronephrosis, increasing slightly to 48.2% at 90 min. A significant increase in hydronephrosis grade over time was observed (p = 0.0005), predominantly affecting the right kidney (p < 0.001). Post-void imaging showed partial resolution (p = 0.0003), but moderate hydronephrosis persisted in 12.5% at 90 min. Bladder volume did not significantly correlate with hydronephrosis severity. Although males had larger kidneys and bladder volumes, there was no significant gender difference in hydronephrosis incidence.ConclusionsRapid hydration can induce transient, non-obstructive hydronephrosis in healthy adults, especially on the right side. Radiologists and clinicians must recognize this physiological variant to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary work-up in asymptomatic patients undergoing renal ultrasound.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

The kidneys maintain fluid balance by filtering blood and adjusting urine output according to hydration levels under the influence of antidiuretic hormone. When fluid intake is high water reabsorption decreases and urine production increases. In contrast, dehydration enhances water reabsorption and reduces urine volume to conserve body fluids [1].

Hydronephrosis, or dilatation of the renal collecting system, is a frequent ultrasound finding and often prompts evaluation for obstructive uropathy, as seen in congenital anomalies like pelviureteric junction obstruction, vesicoureteral reflux, and ureteroceles (commonly seen in children), or from acquired conditions in adults, including kidney stones, benign prostatic hyperplasia, and tumors [2–4]. While obstruction is a key cause, hydronephrosis can also arise from physiological processes such as pregnancy, high urinary output states, or rapid hydration [5–7]. However, transient non-obstructive hydronephrosis (NOH) due to fluid loading remains underappreciated in healthy adults, and is not routinely considered during ultrasound interpretation. This oversight may result in unnecessary imaging, referral, or invasive evaluations.

In NOH, the large volume of urine leads to bladder distension and subsequent hypertrophy of the bladder wall, which may cause intramural obstruction of the distal ureters. In adults, the urinary system, including the kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra, can accommodate 600-1000mL, with the bladder alone capable of holding 400-800 ml. A healthy adult usually experiences the urge to void when the bladder is approximately 50% full [5].

Ultrasound is a simple, safe and widely accessible imaging technique for detecting renal changes, including hydronephrosis, since it involves no radiation and can be performed at the bedside. Various grading systems exist for assessing hydronephrosis-such as: the anteroposterior (AP) diameter, the Society for Fetal Urology (SFU) system, the radiology system, the Urinary Tract Dilatation (UTD) classification, and the Onen grading system. While these systems mainly assess the degree of calyceal dilatation-with the UTD and Onen systems also considering parenchymal thickness and other parts of the urinary tract-no single method has yet achieved universal consensus [3, 8].

Several previous studies addressed the effect of rapid oral hydration on the degree of renal system dilatation. One study, which included only healthy male volunteers, found 80% developed hydronephrosis after hydration [7]. Similarly, another study conducted on pregnant women reported that 80% of the subjects developed hydronephrosis [6]. However, these studies focused on specific populations.

Although hydration-induced hydronephrosis has been described in healthy males and pregnant women, there remains a lack of systematic investigation in a broader adult population. In particular, studies evaluating both sexes with standardized scanning protocols and time-based assessments are lacking. We therefore aimed to determine whether rapid oral hydration induces transient NOH in healthy adults, and to characterize its time course, laterality, severity, and resolution, as well as to assess which gender is more frequently affected. Understanding this phenomenon is essential to improving diagnostic accuracy and reducing unnecessary interventions in clinical practice.

Methods

Patient selection: Healthy adults of both genders between 19 and 45 years were recruited in our study. Inclusion criteria included individuals free of chronic diseases (renal, cardiac, diabetes, hypertension), evidence of obstructive hydronephrosis, pregnancy, prostate enlargement, medications, and any active medical conditions. The examination was performed in the early day, participants were instructed to maintain their regular fluid and food intake on the day of exam, however, no consumption of caffeine-containing beverages were allowed after midnight before the study and during the study. The height and weight of each patient were recorded.

Ultrasound imaging protocol: three radiologists with different levels of experience (3,5, and 20 years) performed the ultrasound examinations on two ultrasound machines (Toshiba, TUS-A500, 2016, Japan. And, GE LogicE9, 2015, USA) using the curved array probe C1-6 in renal settings. Each patient was scanned once by a single radiologist. The distribution of the patients to the designated radiologist and ultrasound machine was random. The senior radiologist reviewed all the scans to ensure appropriate image quality and interpretation. Four phases of scanning were performed:

Phase 1: Baseline: the length of both kidneys was obtained, and assessment of any renal pathologies. In addition, the presence of hydronephrosis and the bladder volume were recorded pre and post voiding.

Phase 2: Hydration phase: each participant was asked to consume 20 ml/Kg of bottled water within 15–30 min, and was asked to refrain from voiding before scanning at any given time.

Phase 3: 60 min after water consumption: Scans were performed for each kidney in addition to the urinary bladder volume at pre and post voiding, participants voided in a nearby restroom to ensure proper timing. The grade of hydronephrosis-if present-was recorded by measuring the anterior-posterior renal pelvic diameter (AP) obtained in the transverse plane according to the Radiology grading system: Rad-I: only renal pelvis dilatation, AP: 5–7 mm. Rad-II: some calices visible, AP: 7–10 mm. Rad-III: marked dilatation of the calices, AP > 10 mm. Rad-IV: narrowing parenchyma. Rad-V: extreme hydronephrosis with thinning of the renal parenchyma [8].

Phase 4: 90 min after water consumption: the grade of hydronephrosis was recorded for each kidney in addition to the urinary bladder volume at pre and post voiding.

Data were analyzed using Jamovi 2.4.6.0. Patient demographics and baseline characteristics were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous measures. Temporal changes in hydronephrosis grades were assessed with Friedman tests. And pairwise comparisons were performed using Durbin-Conover tests. Associations between hydronephrosis presence and kidney dilatation diameter were evaluated with Mann-Whitney U tests. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Healthy individuals with no chronic or acute illnesses were enrolled in our study, there were 56 participants, 30/56 (53.6%) were males. Both genders were matched for age and BMI, with no significant differences in either parameter (p = 0.22 and p = 0.681, respectively). The overall median age was 27.0 years [22.0–35.2] and the overall median BMI was 25.5 kg/m² [22.3–28.8]. Male participants had a significantly higher bladder volume capacity than females (1560 mL vs. 1320 mL, p = 0.007), mostly attributed to the higher fluid intake per body weight and individual variability in voiding habits and timing. Additionally, the right kidney length was significantly greater in males (11.0 cm vs. 10.1 cm, p = 0.009), and the left kidney length showed a similar trend (10.6 cm vs. 9.9 cm, p = 0.015). Grade 3 hydronephrosis emerged in the right kidney at 90 min pre-void with no higher grades occurring on the left side at any given time. There was a significant statistical difference between the pre- (baseline) and post-hydration state for the left kidney (p = 0.022), and for the right kidney (p = < 0.001). In addition, there was a significant difference between the left and right kidneys pre- and post-hydration (p < 0.0001).

The average bladder volume was 263.2 ml and 301.5 ml at 60 min and 90 min respectively. 46.4% of cases developed hydronephrosis at 60 min with an average of bladder volume 261.5 ml in the affected cases, and 48.2% at 90 min pre-void with an average bladder volume 174. There was no significance regarding the bladder volume and the degree of hydronephrosis.

Mean voided volumes at baseline, 60, and 90 min for participants with and without hydronephrosis were compared. No statistically significant differences were observed in the left kidney group at any time point. However, participants with persistent right-sided hydronephrosis had a higher voided volume at 60 min (182.2 vs. 113.4 mL, p = 0.014). At baseline and 90-minute comparisons were not significant. The residual urine volume at 60 min was 17.3mL, and at 90 min was 19.2mL which is considered not significant residual volume.

Discussion

Hydration significantly influences the degree of renal hydronephrosis over time, which can cause misdiagnosis. The awareness of the patient’s hydration status enhances the diagnostic approach and prevents mistreatment. In our study, an increasing proportion of mild to moderate hydronephrosis observed at 60 and 90 min post hydration, both pre- and post-void was observed. The effect is more pronounced pre-voiding, where a single case of severe hydronephrosis appeared. Post-voiding results suggest partial recovery, as seen in the slightly higher proportion of kidneys reverting to Grade 0 (no hydronephrosis).

Non-obstructive hydronephrosis in diabetes insipidus and psychogenic polydipsia without mechanical obstruction is not known to cause significant renal damage, with only one case reporting temporary renal damage that resolved after suprapubic catheterization [9].

In our cases, post hydration hydronephrosis was noted to occur more commonly on the right side with no gender predilection. Right-sided preference was also noted in pregnant ladies in a study by Nyholm et al. [6]. Hydronephrosis occurs in up to 80% of pregnant women usually in the second trimester peaking at 60–90 min post hydration, usually occurring bilaterally with a predilection to the right side as a result of compression by the gravid uterus where it compresses the right ureter against the iliopsoas muscles at the level of the sacral promontory [10, 11]. Few studies hypothesized that progesterone-induced smooth muscle relaxation contributed to early ureteral dilatation [12]. In our study, there is no convincing physiological explanation as to why hydronephrosis occurred more commonly on the right, although there were no pregnant participants and was also seen in males.

Few studies are available in the literature discussing the occurrence of NOH, one case report in 2012 reported a 53-year-old woman who developed bilateral moderate hydroureteronephrosis after consuming approximately 5.5 L of fluid daily as a social habit, no intervention was done, she was advised to reduce the fluid intake. A follow up ultrasound after six weeks revealed complete resolution of the hydronephrosis with no subsequent renal impairment [5]. In our study, resolution of the hydronephrosis was observed in both kidneys more evident in the left kidney, whereas the right kidney exhibited persistent hydronephrosis. The right kidney exhibited more pronounced changes, suggesting its high susceptibility to urine retention or delayed drainage.

Nyholm et al. studied 35 pregnant patients in their 3rd trimester, doing baseline ultrasonography of the kidneys before hydration with 1 L of water. Prior to hydration, 19/35 (54%) patients had renal pelvic dilation, and following hydration, 33/35 (94%) patients had renal pelvic dilation. The study concluded that maternal hydration status had a significantly marked impact on renal pelvic-calyceal diameters, suggesting that clinical encounters and subsequent studies regarding renal pelvic dilatation should adequately control for hydration status [6].

Another study by Morse et al. in 1999 done on healthy male volunteers showed 80% of the participants developed mild or moderate hydronephrosis at 60 and 90 min post hydration. Pre hydration status showed only 8.6% had mild hydronephrosis [7]. In our study, 46.4% showed mild or moderate hydronephrosis at 60 min, and 48.2% at 90 min. Pre-hydration, only 6.2% had mild or moderate hydronephrosis.

A study in 1979 by Morin et al. showed mild to moderate hydronephrosis in 74% of healthy volunteers, 61% bilaterally, and 13% unilaterally. In their study, there were 23 healthy volunteers 15 males, and 8 females, scans were done only on a full bladder after intake of 1.6 L of water [13].

In 1990 a study conducted by Mann on patients with full bladder after consuming 800 ml of water, the data showed normal kidneys in 83.3%, mild hydronpehrosis in 13.9%, and moderate in 2.8% in a sample size of 72 healthy adults-majority were women-which all disappeared when the bladder was emptied [2].

In our study, there was no gender predilection for the occurrence of hydronephrosis post-hydration, although, the higher grades occurred in females.

Several hypothesis regarding the development of post-hydration hydronephrosis were suggested, altered ureteral peristaltic patterns resulting from elevated intravesical pressure and anatomic changes at the urterovesical junction may provide a physiologic explanation for the transient hydronephrosis. Anatomic changes accompanying bladder distension include stretch of the trigonal musculature continuous with the lower segment of the ureter. These muscle fibers are responsible for the competence of the ureterovesical junction [7]. Stretching leads to compression of the intramural ureter, an increase in the oblique angle of insertion of the ureter into the bladder and a progressive obstruction to the flow of urine. Elevated intravesicular pressure and anatomic changes at the ureterovesical junction have been associated with changes in ureteral peristaltic patterns. Rapid diuresis influences ureteral peristaltic patterns leading to dilatation of the collecting system [2]and the amount of urine produced is larger than the capacity of the urinary collecting system [7].

The fact that moderate hydronephrosis cases increased by 12.5% at 90 min post void in our study and that there was no association between the bladder volume and the occurrence of hydronephrosis, suggests that bladder distention is not the sole factor contributing to post-hydration hydronephrosis, which is in alignment with Morse et al. observation [7].

In our study, compared to the previous studies, we included both genders and scanned both kidneys in pre- and post-void status which underscores the importance of our study in determining the presence of hydronephrosis in post hydration states.

This study was conducted at a single center on a relatively small cohort of healthy adults, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although we used a standardized hydration protocol and systematic imaging approach, external validation in a larger and more diverse population across different settings is warranted.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that rapid oral hydration can induce transient non-obstructive hydronephrosis in healthy adults, with a significant proportion of cases persisting even after voiding. These changes were more prominent in the right kidney and occurred irrespective of gender, although higher grades were more commonly seen in females. These results emphasize the importance of accounting for recent fluid intake when interpreting renal ultrasound findings. Recognizing hydration-induced hydronephrosis as a physiological variant can help avoid misdiagnosis, reduce patient anxiety, and prevent unnecessary diagnostic procedures. We recommend that clinicians consider recent hydration history and, when needed, re-scan patient after voiding or after a delay. Future studies should explore these phenomena in clinical populations to guide diagnostic protocols.
