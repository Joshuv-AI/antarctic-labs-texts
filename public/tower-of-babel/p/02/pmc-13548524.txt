Elucidating the mechanism of taurine in alleviating osteoarthritis progression based on bioinformatics, machine learning algorithm, and experimental validation
Authors: Xiaofei Luo, Xuchang Zhou, Yuehui Wang, Zhe Li, Xier Chen, Xuan Wei, Guoxin Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFerroptosis, a form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, plays a pivotal role in chondrocyte death and the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis (OA). Taurine, a sulfur-containing amino acid with established anti-inflammatory and anti-ferroptotic properties, has been implicated in joint protection; however, its potential to mitigate OA progression by targeting ferroptosis mechanisms remains largely unexplored.MethodsRNA sequencing was performed on normal and OA knee joint cartilage tissues to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was used to screen for OA-related modules, and ferroptosis-related targets were retrieved from public databases. Intersecting genes from the DEGs, WGCNA modules, and ferroptosis targets underwent pathway enrichment analysis.Taurine target genes were predicted via multiple databases, and machine learning algorithms were applied to identify hub genes. A nomogram model was constructed to evaluate diagnostic accuracy. Immune infiltration was assessed using the CIBERSORT algorithm. Furthermore, in vitro experiments involving interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β)-stimulated chondrocytes and in vivo studies using a destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) mouse model were conducted to validate the effects of taurine.ResultsMachine learning analysis identified four key hub genes: CSRNP1, CTH, OSBPL3, and CITED2. CTH was selected as the primary focus due to the effect of taurine on its expression in IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes. In vitro assays demonstrated that taurine treatment specifically reversed the IL-1β-induced downregulation of CTH. Mechanistic studies showed that taurine directly bound to the CTH protein and attenuated IL-1β-induced ferroptosis by restoring CTH expression, thereby increasing glutathione (GSH) levels, reducing iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation, and upregulating solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). Consistently, taurine alleviated cartilage degradation and synovial inflammation in DMM-induced OA mice, whereas these benefits were reversed by the CTH inhibitor propargylglycine (PAG).ConclusionTaurine exerted significant chondroprotective effects, at least in part, by directly targeting CTH to suppress chondrocyte ferroptosis, thereby ameliorating OA progression.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13075-026-03844-4.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) represents a degenerative condition affecting joints that is frequently identified among the geriatric demographic. Defined by persistent discomfort in the joints and functional limitations, this condition drastically diminishes the standard of living for patients while creating a massive fiscal strain on the healthcare system [1].

At present, OA is acknowledged as one of the most widespread health issues internationally, impacting an estimated half a billion individuals globally [2]. Being a chronic and progressive ailment, it involves pathology across the entire synovial articulation; its principal disease hallmarks consist of the ongoing breakdown of articular cartilage, inflammation within the synovium, structural alterations to subchondral bone, the growth of bone spurs, a reduction in joint space width, and the destabilization of surrounding tendons and ligaments [3]. In severe cases, OA leads to functional disability [4, 5]. Current clinical management strategies for OA primarily focus on symptom relief [6–8]. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need to identify effective therapeutic agents that can provide both symptomatic and structural improvement.

Taurine is an abundant free amino acid widely distributed in various mammalian tissues, particularly in the retina, skeletal muscle, and liver. The biosynthesis of taurine primarily occurs in the liver and kidneys, with its levels dependent on the metabolic processes of cysteine and methionine [9]. As an ideal regulator, taurine participates in numerous fundamental physiological processes, including the regulation of protein phosphorylation, antioxidant responses, and lipid metabolism [9]. Studies indicate that taurine exerts an essential antioxidant effect in various diseases and is extensively applied in research regarding the prevention and treatment of tumors, myocardial injury, and osteoporosis [10]. Existing research has confirmed that taurine has a protective effect against the progression of osteoarthritis [11], demonstrating its potential as a therapeutic agent for the condition. However, the specific underlying mechanisms responsible for its protective effects remain to be further investigated. Recently, the integration of machine learning in OA research has emerged as a transformative approach to address these complexities, facilitating the identification of diagnostic biomarkers and the stratification of OA phenotypes through the analysis of high-dimensional biomedical data [12, 13]. Also, machine learning contributes to drug discovery [14].

These advanced computational strategies have also proven instrumental in elucidating potential therapeutic targets and uncovering underlying pathological mechanisms by extracting hidden patterns from complex multi-omics datasets [15, 16].

Articular cartilage is particularly susceptible to the effects of OA and undergoes significant degenerative changes during the progression of the disease [17]. As the sole cell type within cartilage, the physiological state of chondrocytes is crucial for maintaining extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis and regulating cartilage metabolism. Chondrocyte death has been identified as a pivotal element in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis [18, 19]. Ferroptosis signifies a distinct form of regulated cell death that is defined by iron reliance and metabolic irregularities related to lipid peroxidation. This mechanism triggers the generation of surplus reactive oxygen species (ROS) and facilitates the buildup of lipid peroxides, eventually resulting in the termination of the cell. In the course of the last few years, investigations have revealed that ferroptosis is tightly linked to numerous health issues and pathological states, such as brain injury, Parkinson’s disease, myocardial injury, tumor suppression, and the modulation of immune system functionality [20]. Recently, the phenomenon of chondrocyte ferroptosis has attracted widespread attention, with evidence confirming that it exacerbates cartilage degeneration and drives the progression of OA. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is a key inhibitor of ferroptosis, and its expression level is significantly lower in osteoarthritic cartilage tissue compared to normal cartilage; furthermore, the knockdown of Gpx4 has been confirmed to aggravate the severity of OA in post-traumatic OA mouse models [21]. Forkhead box O3 (FOXO3) attenuated ferroptosis by inhibiting the inactivation of the NF-κB/MAPK signaling pathway, thereby effectively alleviating the pathological damage associated with OA [22].

This study integrated weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), machine learning algorithms, and network pharmacology to identify key targets of taurine in regulating ferroptosis during OA progression. By combining in vitro cellular and in vivo animal models, the research elucidated the mechanisms by which taurine ameliorates OA progression via the ferroptosis pathway, thereby laying an experimental foundation for the clinical application of taurine in OA treatment.

Materials and methods

Identification of key genes for ferroptosis

To investigate the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis, this study utilized the GSE114007 dataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE114007) to conduct RNA sequencing analysis on 18 normal human knee cartilage tissues and 20 osteoarthritic human knee cartilage tissues. Data analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package for differential gene expression analysis. During data preprocessing, a strict filtering strategy was employed to retain genes with raw counts greater than 10 in at least N samples, where N was defined as the size of the smallest group. Finally, the differentially expressed genes were visualized using a volcano plot to display the significance and direction of gene expression changes.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

To systematically investigate gene co-expression patterns and identify key functional modules significantly associated with human knee osteoarthritic cartilage tissue samples, a WGCNA was employed. The analysis was conducted based on the expression profiles of the top 10,000 genes across 38 samples, following rigorous preprocessing including log2 transformation, normalization, missing value quality control, and variance-based selection. Initially, by evaluating the scale-free topology fit index and mean connectivity under various soft-thresholding powers, a minimum soft-thresholding power (β) of 20 was selected to construct an adjacency matrix (network type: unsigned), satisfying the scale-free topology criteria (R2 = 0.818, mean connectivity = 20.1). Subsequently, the adjacency matrix was transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) to robustly characterize the interconnectedness between genes. Hierarchical clustering was performed using TOM dissimilarity, and the dynamic tree cut algorithm was applied to identify co-expression modules. After calculating the module eigengenes (MEs), their Pearson correlation with osteoarthritis was evaluated; modules with a P < 0.05 and |correlation coefficient|> 0.3 were defined as significantly correlated modules.

KEGG and reactome pathway enrichment analysis

To elucidate the potential associations among WGCNA co-expression modules, differentially expressed genes, and ferroptosis-related genes, a total of 166 intersecting genes were identified. Systematic KEGG and Reactome pathway enrichment analyses were subsequently performed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/). To illustrate the key biological processes, the top 10 significantly enriched pathways, ranked by False Discovery Rate (FDR), were displayed using a bubble plot.

Target prediction of taurine

To systematically screen for potential targets of taurine, this study integrated predictions from the CTD (https://ctdbase.org/), SwissTarget (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), SEA, and Super-PRED databases, yielding a total of 633 target genes after deduplication. Subsequently, the intersection between these targets and key ferroptosis genes in knee cartilage tissue was identified to pinpoint core regulatory genes. Finally, Cytoscape software was employed to construct and optimize the “taurine-intersection gene-disease” network.

Machine learning algorithm analysis

To precisely screen for key feature genes, the “glmnet 4.1.8” package in R was employed to perform Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression analysis, identifying target genes based on the optimal value of lambda.min.

Concurrently, the “e1071 1.7.16” package was utilized to implement the Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) algorithm, selecting the gene combination with the highest accuracy or the smallest error. Furthermore, the Random Forest (RF) algorithm was executed using the “randomForest 4.7.1.2” package. Model performance was evaluated via 5-fold cross-validation to determine the number of trees corresponding to the lowest average error rate and the significant genes identified by the model.

Construction of the nomogram model

To integrate the refined diagnostic biomarkers into a quantitative prediction tool with clinical value, a nomogram model was constructed based on the key screened genes using the rms package in R. Subsequently, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to systematically evaluate the predictive efficacy of the nomogram model and the candidate genes for osteoarthritis, with an area under the curve (AUC) greater than 0.7 considered indicative of good diagnostic performance. Additionally, calibration curves were generated to verify the predictive accuracy of the nomogram. To ensure the robustness of the model and effectively mitigate the risk of overfitting, both leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) and Bootstrap validation with 1000 resamples were employed as internal validation strategies.

Immune infiltration analysis

In this study, the CIBERSORT tool based on a deconvolution algorithm was employed, utilizing the LM22 signature gene matrix to estimate immune cell abundances from the RNA-seq expression matrix with high precision. The significance of the results was evaluated via 1000 permutation tests, and the Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to correct for multiple testing (FDR < 0.05). Subsequently, significantly different immune cell subpopulations were screened based on the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < 0.05. Their distribution characteristics between the disease and control groups were analyzed in depth, and their correlations using the expression of the Spearman rank correlation coefficient with BH correction.

Molecular docking

To conduct molecular docking studies, the SDF format file of taurine was first downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.gov/) and converted to the mol2 format. The PDB structure of the target protein was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). PyMOL software was employed to remove solvent molecules and ligands, preparing the small molecule and protein components for docking. AutoDock Tools 1.5.6 was used to add hydrogen atoms, calculate charges, and assign atom types, after which the processed structures were saved in PDBQT format for analysis. Subsequently, the protein-ligand interactions were analyzed using the PLIP online platform (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip/index). Finally, molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDock 1.5.17 to evaluate the binding characteristics between the ligand and the target protein, and the docking conformations were visualized with PyMOL.

Cell culture and treatment

Primary human chondrocytes (Cellverse, Shanghai, China) were cultured in primary human chondrocyte medium (#iCell-s018-002 h, Cellverse) at 37˚C with 5% CO2.

Upon reaching approximately 90% confluency, they were subcultured utilizing trypsin. Subsequently, cells harvested from the second passage were plated into 6-well culture plates. To establish an in vitro osteoarthritis model, the chondrocytes were exposed to 10 ng/mL IL-1β (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) for a duration of 24 h. In order to evaluate the potential impact of taurine against this cellular damage, the chondrocytes were incubated with different concentrations of taurine (MedChemExpress, Princeton, NJ, USA) for 24 h, followed by treatment with IL-1β.

Cell transfection

The small hairpin RNA against CTH (shCTH) and the matched control (shNC) were provided by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The shRNA constructs were introduced into the cultured cells via Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

Chondrocytes were initially cultured in 10-cm culture dishes for 12 h. Subsequently, the cells were collected, rinsed with PBS, and resuspended in PBS containing a comprehensive protease inhibitor cocktail. The resulting cell suspensions underwent three rapid freeze-thaw cycles utilizing liquid nitrogen. Following centrifugation to remove cell debris, the clarified lysates were divided into two equal portions. One portion was treated with PBS, whereas the other was incubated with taurine at a concentration of 100 mM. Following a 30-minute incubation period, each lysate sample was subdivided and heated at specific target temperatures for 3 min, after which they were allowed to cool down at room temperature for 3 min.

Ultimately, the samples were subjected to analysis using Western blotting.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was utilized to assess the viability of the chondrocytes. The cells were initially seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Subsequently, the cultures were subjected to the designated treatments for an additional 48 h. Upon completion of the treatment phase, the culture medium was discarded, and a solution containing 10% CCK-8 reagent was added into each well.

The plates were then incubated at 37 °C in a dark environment for 1 h. Finally, the absorbance of the samples was measured using a microplate reader.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity assay

The evaluation of LDH activity was carried out in strict accordance with the instructions provided in the commercial LDH assay kit (#C0016, Beyotime). To determine the enzymatic activity levels, the absorbance of each sample was measured at 490 nm utilizing a microplate reader.

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) analysis

The quantification of H2S levels was performed following the instructions outlined in the H2S content detection kit (G0133W, Grace Biotechnology, Suzhou, China). For the cellular component, the harvested cells were transferred into centrifuge tubes and subjected to centrifugation, after which the supernatant was carefully removed. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in extraction buffer and lysed via ultrasonic treatment while kept in an ice bath. The lysate mixture was then centrifuged, and the clarified supernatant was collected. Regarding the tissue samples, the tissues were weighed and combined with extraction buffer, followed by homogenization in an ice bath. This homogenate was subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was collected and kept on ice for further analysis.

Finally, the absorbance of the samples was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader.

Quantification of malondialdehyde (MDA) and glutathione (GSH)

To measure the levels of MDA and GSH, the study utilized the Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit (S0131S, Beyotime) and the GSH Assay Kit (ab239727, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), respectively. For the cellular analysis, chondrocytes were seeded into 6-well plates and subsequently treated as previously described. Regarding the tissue samples, they were homogenized and lysed using the provided lysis buffer. In accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols, the concentrations of MDA and GSH were determined by measuring the absorbance.

Measurement of intracellular Fe2+ levels

Chondrocytes were initially seeded into 12-well culture plates and subjected to the specific treatment protocols mentioned earlier. Subsequently, the cellular monolayers were washed three times with serum-free medium. Following this step, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 1 µM FerroOrange (F374, Dojindo, Shanghai, China). The cells were allowed to incubate with this staining reagent for 30 min, maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere. Immediately following the incubation period, fluorescence detection was measured using a microplate reader.

Assessment of intracellular lipid ROS

Chondrocytes were plated into 12-well plates. Once the cells had adhered to the surface, they were subjected to the experimental interventions described above. Afterward, the cultures were rinsed three times with serum-free culture medium. The wash buffer was then removed and replaced with medium supplemented with 5 µM C11 BODIPY dye (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Upon completion of the staining, the cells were washed again with serum-free medium to remove excess dye, and the resulting lipid ROS levels were quantified by flow cytometry.

Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Following cell seeding, the cultures were subjected to the designated treatment protocols for 48 h. Subsequently, total RNA was extracted utilizing TRIzol reagent (Beyotime), after which the concentration and purity of the isolated RNA were determined using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). For the subsequent synthesis of complementary DNA, the extracted total RNA was reverse transcribed using the Evo M-MLV RT Kit (Accurate Biology, Changsha, China). The amplification process for the quantification reaction was then performed using SYBR Green (Accurate Biology).

To determine the relative levels of mRNA expression, data analysis was performed using the 2–ΔΔCt calculation method, with GAPDH utilized as the endogenous internal control for normalization. Primer sequences are shown in Table 1.

Western blotting

Chondrocytes were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails and maintained on ice for 20 min. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged for 10 min to pellet cellular debris. Protein concentration within the collected supernatant was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). For separation, 30 µg of each protein sample was loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels, after which the resolved proteins were electrophoretically transferred onto PVDF membranes.

Following this transfer step, the membranes were blocked to prevent non-specific binding. The membranes were then probed with the specific primary antibodies against CTH (1:8000, 12217-1-AP, Proteintech, Rosemount, MN, USA), SLC7A11 (1:2000, 26864-1-AP, Proteintech), GPX4 (1:2000, 67763-1-Ig, Proteintech), MMP13 (1:2000, 18165-1-AP, Proteintech) and GAPDH (1:8000, 10494-1-AP, Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the appropriate secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher). Signal development was achieved using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Beyotime), and the results were captured utilizing a gel imaging system.

Destabilization of medial meniscus (DMM) mouse model

Twenty-eight 10-week-old male mice used in this investigation were procured from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and subsequently housed in a strictly controlled environment where the temperature was maintained between 23 °C and 25 °C, and relative humidity was maintained within a range of 40% to 60%. The facility utilized an artificial light-dark cycle that alternated every twelve hours, and the animals were provided with standard laboratory chow and water ad libitum. Upon arrival, the animals were randomized into four distinct experimental groups: the Sham group (N = 7), the DMM group (N = 7), the DMM+Taurine group (N = 7), and the DMM+Taurine + PAG group (N = 7). Mice assigned to the Sham cohort were subjected to a sham surgical intervention that involved exposing the joint structure without causing any meniscal damage, and these animals were administered saline as a control. In contrast, mice in the DMM group were first anesthetized through an intraperitoneal injection of an anesthetic agent; following this, the joint capsule located medial to the patellar tendon was surgically opened using scissors, and the cranial meniscotibial ligament of the medial meniscus was transected by a qualified surgeon. Animals belonging to the DMM+Taurine cohort underwent the identical DMM surgical procedure and were subsequently treated with taurine, which was administered at a dosage of 30 mg/kg per injection, with a total volume of 5 µL, on a twice-weekly schedule. The mice in the DMM+Taurine + PAG group were subjected to the same DMM procedure and received the same regimen of taurine (30 mg/kg, 5 µL per injection) twice a week, but in addition, they were treated with PAG (Abmole, Shanghai, China) at a dosage of 50 mg/kg, which was delivered via intraperitoneal injection 30 min prior. Eight weeks after the surgical procedures were performed, all the mice were humanely euthanized, and their cartilage tissues were carefully harvested for use in subsequent experimental analyses.

The study was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Laboratory Animal Center, Xiamen University (Approval No. XMULAC20250226).

Safranin O/Fast green staining

Cartilage tissue sections were initially deparaffinized using xylene solution and subsequently dehydrated using a graded series of alcohol concentrations. Following this preparation, the sections were immersed in Fast Green dye for 10 min and then rinsed thoroughly with water; this washing step was continued until the cartilage matrix became visibly colorless, after which the sections were differentiated using an acidic ethanol solution to achieve optimal contrast. Subsequently, the tissue sections were stained with a Safranin O solution (ScyTek, Logan, UT, USA) for 30 s, followed by a clearing step involving xylene for 5 min and finally mounting using neutral resin. To assess the tissue integrity, any morphological alterations present in the sections were evaluated in a blinded fashion by three independent certified pathologists utilizing an optical microscope. Cartilage thickness was measured on Safranin O/Fast Green stained sections using ImageJ software (three different regions were measured per specimen, and the average value was calculated). Proteoglycan content was quantified by calculating the percentage of the Safranin O-positive area relative to the total cartilage area.

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining

Cartilage tissue samples were harvested from each experimental group. These sections were subsequently subjected to staining utilizing the H&E method.

Histological evaluation was performed by three independent researchers under blinded conditions. The degree of cartilage degeneration was semi-quantitatively assessed using the OARSI grading system (grades 0–6).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Regarding the quantification of inflammatory markers, the concentration of IL-1β within cartilage tissues was determined using a commercially available Mouse IL-1β ELISA Kit (PI301, Beyotime). In brief, tissue homogenates were generated by mechanically homogenizing 20 mg of tissue in 1 mL of sodium phosphate buffer. Following centrifugation at 15,000× g for 15 min at a temperature of 4 °C, the resulting supernatant was collected to assay IL-1β levels in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0. The data obtained from these experiments are expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD).

For comparative analysis, Student’s t test was applied when comparing two distinct groups, whereas comparisons involving multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P < 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Identification of and pathway analysis of key ferroptosis-related genes in knee joint cartilage tissues

RNA sequencing was performed on 18 normal human knee joint cartilage tissue samples and 20 osteoarthritis human knee joint cartilage tissue samples. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 in R package, and a total of 1,419 DEGs were identified (Fig. 1A). To systematically investigate gene co-expression patterns and identify functional modules significantly associated with knee osteoarthritis cartilage tissue samples, the WGCNA method was employed. First, the study selected the optimal soft-thresholding power by evaluating the scale-free topology fit index and mean connectivity. The minimum β value of 20, which satisfied the scale-free topology criterion (R2 = 0.818, with mean connectivity = 20.1), was chosen to construct the adjacency matrix. The dynamic tree cut algorithm was then applied to identify co-expression modules, ultimately yielding 7 modules (excluding the grey module), as shown in Fig. 1B. MEs were calculated, and their correlation with osteoarthritis was assessed using the Pearson correlation method. Modules with a P-value < 0.05 and an absolute correlation coefficient |r| > 0.3 were defined as significantly correlated modules. A total of 4 significant modules were identified, containing 1,098 key genes (Fig. 1C). Ferroptosis target genes were predicted using the GeneCards and FerrDb databases, and after removing duplicates, 4,662 ferroptosis-related genes were obtained. Finally, a Venn diagram was used to identify the intersection of WGCNA modules, DEGs, and ferroptosis target genes, resulting in 166 intersecting genes (Fig. 1D). Subsequently, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that these genes were associated with PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, FoxO signaling pathway, and HIF-1 signaling pathway (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, Reactome pathway enrichment revealed that these genes were associated with cellular responses to stress, cytokine signaling in immune system, cellular senescence, interleukin-4 and interleukin-13 signaling, signaling by interleukins, immune system, RNA polymerase II transcription, and generic transcription pathway (Fig. 1F).

Construction of a nomogram model based on two genes

To integrate the refined diagnostic biomarkers into a quantitative predictive tool, a nomogram model was constructed. This nomogram utilized the scores of CTH and CITED2 to calculate a total score and predict the risk of osteoarthritis (Fig. 4A). The ROC curve compared the predictive performance of the nomogram with that of individual genes (CTH and CITED2), demonstrating that the nomogram possesses higher predictive accuracy (Fig. 4B). To evaluate the generalization ability of the model and avoid overfitting, two internal validation strategies, LOOCV and Bootstrap validation, were employed. Bootstrap validation further confirmed the robustness of the model, with a median AUC of 0.922 and a 95% CI of [0.812-1.000], as shown in Fig. 4C. The LOOCV results showed an AUC of 0.947 (95% CI: 0.849-1.000), as shown in Fig. 4D. Further, through the analysis of the GSE114007 dataset, the study discovered that the expression levels of CTH and CITED2 were lower in OA samples than in normal samples (Fig. 4E). These results consistently indicated that the diagnostic model constructed based on CTH and CITED2 exhibited highly powerful and stable discriminative performance in distinguishing osteoarthritis samples from normal samples.

CTH was identified as a target protein of taurine in OA

The above two-gene signature (CTH/CITED2) demonstrates cross-cohort robustness as a diagnostic biomarker for OA; however, its functional association with taurine requires individual experimental verification. The study then analyzed the effects of taurine treatment on the expression levels of CTH and CITED2 in an OA cell model (IL-1β-induced chondrocytes). As shown in Figure S1A, taurine treatment (400 mM) inhibited cell viability. Since 400 mM showed significant cytotoxicity, a concentration gradient below this level (10, 100, and 200 mM) was selected for subsequent experiments. Cell viability was then evaluated following co-treatment with taurine and 10 ng/mL IL-1β.

The results indicated that cell viability was highest following combined treatment with 100 mM taurine and 10 ng/mL IL-1β (Figure S1B). Thus, 100 mM taurine was used for further studies. The researchers discovered that IL-1β treatment dysregulated the mRNA levels of CTH and CITED2 in chondrocytes; however, only CTH expression was restored after taurine treatment (Figure S1C). Thus, CTH was selected as a study subject. Comparatively, IL-1β treatment inhibited CTH protein expression, whereas this effect was attenuated after taurine treatment (Figure S1D). The molecular docking results of taurine with CTH are presented in Fig. 6A. The binding energy of the complex was − 4.9 kcal/mol. The estimated dissociation constant (Ki) was ~ 255 µM (Ki = exp(ΔG/RT), where R = 1.987 × 10− 3 kcal·mol− 1·K− 1, T = 298.15 K). The RMSD for taurine was 1.438. Key interacting residues and precise geometric parameters were analyzed using the PLIP software (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip/index), and the results are shown in Fig. 6B. Two-dimensional interaction diagrams were generated with Discovery Studio 4.5 Client. The results showed that the taurine-CTH complex was stabilized by seven van der Waals interactions with residues ASN 241, TYR 114, GLY 116, GLY 115, SER 358, HIS 356, and TYR 60, and four hydrogen bonds with ARG 119, SER63, ARG 62, and THR 355 (Fig. 6C). Using the natural substrate of CTH, L-cystathionine, as a positive control, docking was performed with exactly the same grid box parameters as for taurine (center_x = -23.834, center_y = -12.915, center_z = 104.203; size_x = 92, size_y = 94, size_z = 100). L-cystathionine exhibited a binding energy of -5.3 kcal/mol (Ki ≈ 130 µM). Meanwhile, the binding energy of taurine (-4.9 kcal/mol) was very close to that of the natural substrate, suggesting that taurine possesses affinity for CTH comparable to that of a physiological ligand (Figure S2A-C). The CETSA results revealed a distinct rightward shift toward higher temperatures in the melting curve of the taurine-treated group compared to the control group (Fig. 6D). This indicated that the CTH protein exhibited increased thermostability in the presence of taurine, demonstrating a thermal shift and confirming direct binding between the two.

Taurine treatment protected against IL-1β-induced ferroptosis by regulating CTH in chondrocytes

The study transfected CTH shRNA and the control shNC into chondrocytes and then treated them with 100 mM taurine, followed by 10 ng/mL IL-1β treatment. The efficiency of CTH knockdown is shown in Figure S1E. The results showed that IL-1β treatment inhibited CTH protein expression, whereas the effect was alleviated after taurine treatment (Figure S1F). In addition, IL-1β treatment inhibited cell viability and promoted LDH activity, but these effects were attenuated after taurine treatment (Fig. 7A and B). The results also showed that IL-1β treatment inhibited H2S production, decreased GSH levels, and increased MDA levels, Fe2+ levels and lipid ROS levels, whereas taurine treatment relieved these effects (Fig. 7C-G). As shown in Fig. 7H, taurine treatment also attenuated IL-1β-induced inhibition of SLC7A11 and GPX4 protein expression. However, the effects induced by taurine treatment or IL-1β stimulation on CTH protein expression, cell viability, LDH activity, H2S production, GSH levels, MDA levels, Fe2+ levels, lipid ROS levels, and SLC7A11 and GPX4 protein expression were all reversed after CTH knockdown (Fig. 7 and Figure S1F).

Taurine therapy ameliorated OA progression by regulating CTH in vivo

Destabilization of the DMM surgery was performed to induce OA in mice to analyze the effect of taurine on OA progression in vivo. Articular cartilage was evaluated using HE staining and Safranin O/fast green staining. The DMM group exhibited more severe synovial inflammation and a lower amount of proteoglycans compared to the Sham group; however, following taurine treatment, cartilage degradation and synovial inflammation were alleviated (Fig. 8A and B). As shown in Fig. 8C-E, DMM groups displayed increased OARSI score and decreased Safranin O-positive area and cartilage thickness, but these effects were reversed after taurine treatment. In addition, the results showed that the articular cartilage tissues from the DMM mice showed decreased H2S and GSH levels and increased MDA and IL-1β levels; however, taurine treatment attenuated these effects (Fig. 8F-I). Moreover, articular cartilage tissues from the DMM mice showed decreased protein levels of CTH, SLC7A11, and GPX4 and increased MMP13 protein expression; however, these effects were relieved after taurine treatment (Fig. 8J). But these taurine-induced effects were all counteracted after the addition of the PAG, a CTH inhibitor (Fig. 8A-J).

Discussion

With the significant extension of global life expectancy, OA has emerged as one of the most prevalent chronic diseases, severely impairing joint function in the elderly population. Regrettably, the lack of effective drugs or therapeutic interventions to delay OA progression constitutes a significant gap in current clinical practice. Previous studies have established a close association between OA pathogenesis and the disruption of matrix homeostasis. Hallmark pathological mechanisms include the upregulated expression of matrix-degrading enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTS), alongside the degradation of critical components like type II collagen alpha 1 and aggrecan [23, 24]. Furthermore, recent research has highlighted the critical role of ferroptosis in the pathological progression of OA [25]. Although taurine demonstrates therapeutic potential for OA, the specific mechanisms involved, particularly regarding the regulation of ferroptosis, remain to be fully elucidated. This study integrated WGCNA, machine learning algorithms, and network pharmacology to identify CTH as a key target gene of taurine. The results confirmed that taurine effectively inhibited chondrocyte ferroptosis by directly targeting CTH, thereby significantly ameliorating OA progression and exerting profound chondroprotective effects.

In the current study, taurine treatment effectively countered IL-1β-induced cytotoxicity, evidenced by restored cell viability and reduced LDH activity. Previous investigations have attributed the protective effects of taurine against OA to the suppression of MMP-3 and CHOP expression [11] or the attenuation of endoplasmic reticulum stress-mediated apoptosis [26]. While these findings are significant, they largely focus on inflammatory or apoptotic pathways, whereas the present results specifically highlight the critical role of ferroptosis in chondrocyte injury. A recent study suggested that taurine inhibited chondrocyte ferroptosis by regulating OGT/Gpx4 signaling in anterior cruciate ligament transection-induced osteoarthritis [27]; however, distinct from this prior work which identified OGT as the upstream regulator, the current study reveals a novel and direct molecular target of taurine by identifying CTH as its a binding protein. This distinction represents a significant advance in understanding the primary receptor-targeted mechanism of taurine. Moreover, while the previous study focused on the OGT/GPX4 axis, our data provide a more comprehensive metabolic profile and reveal a distinct downstream cascade, demonstrating that taurine restores the redox balance by reversing the depletion of GSH and preventing the accumulation of MDA, Fe2+, and lipid ROS via the upregulation of the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis. Therefore, rather than duplicating the OGT/GPX4 findings, our study establishes a novel CTH-dependent pathway, demonstrating that taurine directly binds to CTH to activate the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis, which serves as the primary mechanism through which taurine inhibits ferroptosis in our model. In vivo validation further confirmed that taurine exerted chondroprotective effects by inhibiting oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, restoring antioxidant system function, and reducing matrix degradation in OA mice.

The gene CTH encodes cystathionine-γ-lyase (CSE), a crucial natural vitamin B6-dependent enzyme primarily responsible for catalyzing the biosynthesis of H2S [28]. Studies have indicated that the CSE-H2S signaling pathway enhances osteogenic activity by increasing the S-sulfhydration of RUNX2, thereby significantly promoting bone tissue repair and regeneration from a mechanistic perspective [29]. Furthermore, in research related to intervertebral disc degeneration, CSE has been confirmed to play a vital role by significantly reducing vascular invasion and ameliorating the inflammatory environment, thereby effectively delaying the pathological progression of the disease [30]. Research also demonstrates that CSE attenuates chondrocyte catabolism, inflammation, apoptosis, and senescence by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway [24]. Building upon this established knowledge, the present study identified CTH as a direct binding target of taurine, revealing that taurine treatment promoted CTH protein expression in IL-1β-induced chondrocytes. This discovery highlights the novelty of CTH as a previously unrecognized direct target of taurine in OA, extending beyond its known downstream metabolic functions. Functionally, the protective effects of taurine against ferroptosis were significantly abrogated following CTH knockdown, establishing CTH as an indispensable mediator rather than a coincidental by-stander in taurine’s anti-ferroptotic action. In vivo experiments also confirmed that the chondroprotective effects of taurine on joint cartilage in osteoarthritis mice are mediated through CTH. Moreover, a diagnostic model constructed based on CTH exhibited extremely powerful and stable discriminative performance in distinguishing osteoarthritis samples from normal samples. Immune infiltration analysis further revealed significant associations between CTH and various immune cell populations, including activated and resting memory CD4 T cells, follicular helper T cells, gamma delta T cells, activated NK cells, M0 and M1 macrophages, and eosinophils. It is crucial to note that these findings are derived from bioinformatic correlation analysis rather than functional immune assays; therefore, they do not establish a causal relationship. These results imply that CTH may not only function intrinsically within chondrocytes but also potentially be associated with joint inflammation through correlations with immune infiltration (for instance, showing associations with macrophages or T cell subsets).

Despite the promising findings of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the in vivo administration of the CTH inhibitor PAG attenuated the chondroprotective effects of taurine, PAG is not exclusively specific to chondrocytes and may systemically inhibit CTH activity in other tissues. Consequently, while these results confirm the necessity of the CTH pathway for taurine’s efficacy, they do not definitively exclude the potential contribution of paracrine effects from other joint cells to the overall observed therapeutic outcome. Second, while CSRNP1, OSBPL3, and CITED2 were identified as key genes with diagnostic potential, the study did not further explore the specific mechanisms by which these genes contribute to OA pathogenesis or their lack of response to taurine treatment. Third, although bioinformatic analysis revealed significant correlations between CTH (as well as the other hub genes) and immune cell infiltration, our current functional experiments are limited to chondrocytes. The study lacks direct experimental evidence, such as immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, or immune cell depletion, to verify a functional role of CTH in immune regulation. Therefore, the proposed link between CTH and immune infiltration remains correlative and hypothesis-generating, necessitating dedicated immune-functional validation in future studies. Another important limitation in the design of this study’s animal experiments is the absence of a DMM + PAG alone group. Consequently, the current data cannot completely rule out the following alternative explanations: (i) PAG itself may directly exacerbate cartilage damage or induce off-target toxicity, rather than simply reversing the effects of taurine; (ii) the high-dose antioxidant effects of taurine might non-specifically counteract PAG-induced injury, rather than acting through specific binding to CTH. Including a DMM + PAG alone group would clarify the net effect of PAG itself on OA progression, thereby providing a more rigorous demonstration of the dependence of taurine on CTH. The study will prioritize completing this experiment in future studies. Finally, although taurine demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy in the current preclinical models, its pharmacokinetics, optimal dosage, and long-term safety profile in human OA patients remain to be investigated in future clinical trials.

Conclusions

This study elucidated the protective mechanism of taurine against OA progression by identifying CTH as a critical target and highlighting the central role of the CTH-ferroptosis pathway. The clinical value of this research is significant, as it proposes CTH as a novel therapeutic target and underscores the potential of repurposing taurine for OA treatment. By establishing a link between taurine, CTH, and ferroptosis suppression, this study provides a theoretical foundation for developing targeted nutraceutical strategies to improve joint health in patients suffering from OA.
