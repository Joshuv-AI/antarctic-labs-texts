Prediction of late seizures after ischemic stroke using cognitive scores
Authors: Hiroya Ohara, Hironori Shimizu, Masami Yamanaka, Nanami Yamada, Naoya Kikutsuji, Ryuhei Miyoshi, Hiromi Kanesaki, Takahiro Kanda, Keisuke Honda, Masako Kinoshita, Kazuma Sugie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLate seizures are well-known sequelae after stroke. Previous history of stroke and dementia is common etiology of epilepsy, however, the effect of cognitive impairment on late seizures has not been fully investigated. We investigated the clinical significance of cognitive scores in predicting the occurrence of post-stroke late seizures.MethodsAdult patients with acute cerebral infarction were analyzed. Their cognitive function was evaluated using the Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination (ACE)-III and the Japanese version of Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-J) within two weeks after stroke. Factors associated with late seizures and accuracy of cognitive scores to predict late seizures were analyzed.ResultsOf 45 patients enrolled (28 males, age 77.2 ± 8.5 years, mean ± SD), eight patients had late seizures within 123.8 ± 126.5 days after cerebral infarction. Cognitive evaluation was performed at 8.0 ± 3.9 days. ACE-III and MoCA-J scores were significantly lower in patients with late seizures than in those without late seizures (ACE-III: 27.5 ± 17.3 vs. 59.1 ± 27.2, MoCA-J: 7.6 ± 5.9 vs. 15.4 ± 8.6, p < 0.05, unpaired t-test). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis revealed that area under curve of ACE-III was larger than that of MoCA-J and size of cerebral infarction. The optimum cut-off scores of ACE-III were ≤ 58.5 (Sensitivity: 1.00, specificity: 0.62) and ≤ 45.0 (0.88, 0.73). Kaplan-Meier estimates showed that each cut-off score significantly associated with late seizures. Sizes of infarcts and of cortical lesion were not significantly different between patients with and without late seizures. ROC curve and Kaplan-Meier survival analyses showed a significant association between size of infarct and late seizures, however, ACE-III scores more strongly associated with late seizures than the size of infarct did.ConclusionCognitive scores, especially ACE-III, within two weeks after cerebral infarction can be useful for predicting post-stroke late seizures.Keywords: Post-stroke epilepsy, The Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination-III, The Montreal Cognitive Assessment, Cerebral infarction, Late seizure, Cognition

Introduction

Poststroke late seizure is a well-known sequela after stroke that impairs functional outcome [1]. Cortical involvement is one of the most important risk factors for post-stroke seizures, followed by stroke severity at presentation, hemorrhagic lesions, young age less than 65 years, and anterior circulation infarcts [2–4]. On the other hand, previous history of stroke and dementia is common etiology of epilepsy [5, 6].

Cognitive tests represent higher cortical functions and are utilized to evaluate cognitive impairments in post-stroke patients [7]. However, the relationship between cognitive impairment and late seizures has not been fully investigated. This study aimed to investigate the clinical significance of cognitive scores in predicting post-stroke late seizures in patients with cerebral infarction.

Part of this manuscript was presented in the American Epilepsy Society 75th Annual Meeting in 2021, Chicago, in an abstract form. This study is to evaluate diagnostic test accuracy, based on the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy (STARD) guidelines.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study was conducted as a pilot study for the prospective study of Stroke Assessment for Cognition and Late Seizures (SACLa study). We retrospectively enrolled consecutive adult patients with acute cerebral infarction who were admitted to the Department of Neurology, Minaminara General Medical Center between January 1, 2019 and December 31, 2019, and followed up for 14 months or more (Fig. 1). Our institute is located in rural area of the southern part of Nara prefecture and provides both primary and secondary healthcare. Severe stroke patients who potentially need endovascular therapy are directly transported to tertiary center, and during the period 226 less severe patients were admitted to our hospital. Among them, 161 patients were excluded; 58 patients with intracranial hemorrhage were treated in the Department of Neurosurgery, 91 patients with significant medical complications were treated in other corresponding departments, 24 patients were followed up less than 14 months, and 8 patients with severe consciousness disturbance were not eligible to cognitive assessment. Finally, 45 patients were enrolled in this study. The Ethics Committee of Minaminara General Medical Center approved the research protocol (Approval Number #22). Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective, non-invasive, and anonymous design.

Factors analyzed

Information on age at the onset of cerebral infarction, sex, lesion side, middle cerebral artery territory involvement, cortical involvement, secondary hemorrhage, diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, early seizures, and late seizures. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was evaluated using diffusion-weighted image (DWI), apparent diffusion coefficient map (ADC map), fluid attenuated inversion recovery image, and MR angiography (MAGNETOM Avanto fit Tim4G, 1.5 Tesla, Siemens Healthcare K.K./Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics K.K., Tokyo, Japan). All patients were routinely evaluated by board-certified speech and occupational therapists using the Japanese version of the Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination-III (ACE-III) [8] and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-J)9 within two weeks after stroke onset and underwent regular neurological examination monthly or more often by a board-certified neurologist. Cognitive scores were taken when the patients were not showing epileptic seizures. Early seizures were defined as epileptic seizures occurring within two weeks from stroke onset, and late seizures were defined as unprovoked epileptic seizures occurring after two weeks or later from stroke onset (Table 1) [10]. According to a practical clinical definition of epilepsy by the International League Against Epilepsy, patients with a single unprovoked epileptic seizure occurring at least a month after a stroke is diagnosed as epilepsy [11]. Thus, in the current study we refrained from using the term of ‘post-stroke epilepsy (PSE)’.

Statistics

Continuous variables were analyzed using the unpaired t-test or Mann-Whitney U test after confirming the assumption of normality. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the optimum cut-off scores. The Kaplan-Meier estimate was used to evaluate the cumulative risk of late seizures. All statistical tests were performed using SPSS statistical software (version 27; IBM Japan, Tokyo, Japan). The significance level was set at p = 0.05.

Results

Late seizures and cognitive scores

Of the 45 patients included in this study (28 men, age 77.2 ± 8.5 years (mean ± SD)), eight patients (17.8%, 4 men, age 82.1 ± 4.2 years) developed late seizures (Table 1; Fig. 1). All patients underwent MRI evaluation on the day of arrival at our hospital; on the day of stroke onset: 33 patients, 2nd day: 8 patients, 3rd day: 2 patients, and 6th day: 2 patients. No patient developed provoked seizures or acute symptomatic seizures during the follow-up period.

The ratio of early seizures was significantly higher in patients with late seizures than in those without late seizures (54.5% vs. 5.9%, p < 0.05), although all patients with early seizures were treated with therapeutic dose of levetiracetam (LEV).

Cognitive scores were examined at 8.0 ± 3.9 days after stroke. It took approximately 20 min to perform ACE-III and 15 min to perform MoCA-J. The ACE-III and MoCA-J scores were significantly lower in patients with late seizures than in those without late seizures (ACE-III: 27.5 ± 17.3 vs. 59.1 ± 27.2, MoCA-J: 7.6 ± 5.9 vs. 15.4 ± 8.6, p < 0.05).

Utility of cognitive scores for predicting late seizures

ROC curve measures of cognitive tests to predict late seizures are shown in Fig. 3. Area under the curve (AUC) of both ACE-III and MoCA-J was significantly larger than that of the diagonal reference line (asymptotic significance p < 0.005). AUC of ACE-III was larger than that of MoCA-J (0.82 vs. 0.74) but the difference between the tests was not statistically significant (Fig. 3; Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the clinical significance of cognitive scores in predicting the occurrence of post-stroke late seizures in adult patients with acute cerebral infarction. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the usefulness of cognitive scores, especially ACE-III, for risk measurement of late seizures. Moreover, the utility of ACE-III was superior to the MRI findings for predicting late seizures.

Prediction for late seizures after cerebral infarction

A previous study developed a multivariate prediction model for late seizures after ischemic stroke using the SeLECT score, consisting of five well-defined parameters (Severity of stroke, Large artery atherosclerotic etiology, Early seizures, Cortical involvement, and Territory of middle cerebral artery involvement) [12]. According to this model, a SeLECT score of eight points predicted a 44% risk within one year and a 65% risk within five years of late seizure after stroke onset, which is higher than the practical definition of epilepsy by the International League Against Epilepsy, that is, at least 60% over the next 10 years [11]. In our study, the positive predictive value of ACE-III ≤ 45.0 was 0.412 for late seizures within 14 months (Table 2). Thus, these patients are at an almost equivalent risk of developing late seizures. Even though they experienced no unprovoked seizures, it would be reasonable to consider the commencement of antiseizure medication. Recently, a retrospective case-control study in Japan incorporated information on cortical superficial siderosis (SeLECT-S) and showed its superiority over SeLECT (AUC: 0.84 vs. 0.78) [13]. In our study, ACE-III score, which can be utilized in areas of limited medical resources, achieved comparable prediction to SeLECT-S with AUC of 0.82.

Overall late seizure occurrence in this study was as high as 17.8% in 14 months, whereas in the SeLECT study 4% within 1 year and 8% within 5 years after stroke [12]. The difference is possibly because patients who were followed up for 14 months or more were included in the present study, while 433 out of 2369 patients (18.3%) were censored in 1 year in the SeLECT study [12]. In addition, our study population can be biased because our hospital serves both primary and secondary healthcare as a sole neurological institute in rural area. The board-certified neurologist regularly examined face-to-face interview monthly or more often in our study, whereas a structured telephone interview was performed in the SeLECT study [12]. Thus, precise history taking and thorough neurological evaluation enabled us to detect subtle seizure manifestations compared to previous studies conducted in tertiary referral centers. We consider our study can serve as a reliable basis for further large studies.

As for localization of ischemic lesion, stroke lesions tend to widely distribute according to artery territories irrelevant to functional organization. The SeLECT study employed infarction of the middle cerebral artery territory and cortical involvement as potential parameters but did not include lesion size or localization [12]. Thus, we did not analyze the site or size of the ischemic lesion in this study. If we focus on the effect of lesion localization on functional networks, a more suitable approach would be analyses of small lesions. For example, in Japanese patients with branch atheroma disease, small subcortical infarctions involving the basal ganglia and/or thalamus in the territory of the lenticulostriate artery associate with late-onset seizures [14].

Impact of early seizures on late seizures

Presence of early seizure is one of the most important risk factors for late seizures (hazard ratio 6.2)12 and for PSE (odds ratio 4.4–4.5).15,16 More than half of patients with early seizures developed late seizures in this study despite treatment with LEV in therapeutic dose. Similarly, in the SeLECT study 27–92% of patients with early seizures were treated with antiseizure drugs [12]. Thus, conventional antiseizure medications after developing early seizure would be insufficient to suppress late seizures. These findings are in concordance with current stroke guidelines which do not recommend primary and secondary antiseizure drug prophylaxis [17, 18].

However, secondary prophylaxis is commonly performed in many stroke centers to reduce the risk of neuronal toxicity and inflammation during the acute phase [17]. It is strongly recommended that recurrent seizures after stroke should be treated and anti-seizure medications should be selected according to patient characteristics,18 but during acute stroke phase most seizures remain unrecognized because of severe neurological deficit and only detected by continuous electroencephalography (EEG) monitoring [19]. Several studies indicate a need for trials for primary antiseizure drug prophylaxis in high-risk patients who would have unfavorable outcome including mortality by early seizures [20]. A rat global ischemia model study showed that administration of LEV in high dose (100 mg/kg) for seven days from bilateral ligation of carotid arteries eliminated EEG paroxysmal activities and showed beneficial effect on neurological status [21]. In addition, animal model studies have revealed several novel targets for anti-epileptogenic and disease-modifying interventions [22]. These data suggest importance of suppressing early seizures including subclinical seizures not only to prevent late seizures but also to improve neurological outcome.

Thus far it seems difficult to perform a randomized placebo-controlled double-blind prophylactic trial in stroke patients for the prevention of post-stroke seizures [23]. A recent phase 3 trial showed safety and efficacy of prophylactic LEV to prevent seizures in acute phase of intracerebral hemorrhage, but larger studies with longer observation period are needed [24]. Cognitive score evaluation would enable us to detect ischemic stroke patients at high risk for late seizures and to monitor neurological functions. LEV is one of the most common anti-seizure drugs that meets the characteristics required for treatment of post-stroke patients, i.e., quick titration, intravenous administration, and lack of drug-drug interactions [25].

Utility of cognitive scores

In the field of dementia, the utility of cognitive scores has already been established. The optimum ACE-III cut-off scores in Japan were 88/89 for mild cognitive impairment and 75/76 for dementia [8]. As for the post-stroke population, the cut-off ACE-R score for detecting any cognitive impairment was 93/94 (Sensitivity 0.83, specificity 0.73) [7, 26]. In contrast, the cut-off ACE-III scores to predict late seizures in the present study were substantially low. Our study proposes a novel usage of cognitive scores for the prediction of late seizures and possibly other sequelae. Cognitive scores are convenient, require minimal training, and take only tens of minutes to administer [7].

Cognitive scores are closely related with presence of cortical involvement and early seizures. However, the superiority of using cognitive scores is that detection of early seizures, especially non-convulsive seizures, is difficult without critical care continuous EEG monitoring, which is not commonly performed in stroke centers, and that continuous variables of cognitive scores is more useful to adjust diagnostic power than binary variables. Due to small number of patients, we could not perform multivariate analysis in this study.

Why do patients with low cognitive function after stroke develop late seizures?

Based on close relationships among dementia, cerebrovascular disease, and epilepsy, it is already known that these conditions share common vascular risk factors [27, 28]. Among stepwise process of epileptogenesis in PSE, two genetic risk factors are known [29]. rs671 of mitochondrial aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 gene (ALDH2) (Glu504Lys, also known as ALDH2*2 or allele A), common in the East Asian population, decreases catalytic activity and increases risk of myocardial infarction, lacunar infarction, Alzheimer’s disease, and PSE [30–32]. Polymorphism CD40 −1 C/T can cause exaggerated response to oxidative stress in penambra, causing PSE, dementia, and poor neuropsychological test scores [33–35].

Similar to previous studies, our data showed association of early seizures with late seizures [12]. Persistent and frequent epileptic seizures, including unrecognized subclinical seizures, can cause impairment of awareness and focal dysfunction of cognitive process due to seizures themselves. Interictal epileptiform discharges can also interrupt neuronal network activities such as default mode network [27, 36]. A rat model study using pilocarpine-induced status epilepticus showed that memory impairment was the most severe at 2 months after status epilepticus and that, in addition to neuronal loss and gliosis in the hippocampus, structural changes of the somatosensory, entorhinal, and ectorhinal cortices contributed to declarative memory dysfunction [37]. In order to detect clinically unrecognized EEG seizures, and to evaluate EEG findings related with cortical injury represented by the pattern of relative attenuation without delta, there is a strong need for continuous EEG monitoring in stroke care unit [19, 24]. Sleep is another important contributor because sleep fragmentation increases amyloid β deposition in Alzheimer’s disease model mice,38 and sleep micro-architecture alteration is associated with cognitive impairments in patients with temporal lobe epilepsy [39]. Currently it is not practical to apply continuous EEG with sleep record to all patients with stroke, thus cognitive evaluation can be useful to select patients who should undergo intensive EEG evaluation.

Cortical involvement is another important predictive factor for late seizures [12]. In our study, all patients who developed late seizures had cortical involvement of the ischemic lesion. While high intensity in DWI with low ADC depicts cytotoxic edema in ischemic core,40 currently it is difficult to visualize whole ischemic area including penumbra in acute cerebral infarction. Perfusion-diffusion mismatch has been used to evaluate penumbra and to select patients who need endovascular treatment among acute cerebral infarction [41]. Arterial spin labeling is a non-invasive sequence recently put into clinical practice to evaluate brain perfusion using magnetically-labeled water as a tracer, which can be beneficial to obtain perfusion-diffusion subtraction image easily in safe [42]. However, there is a caveat that arterial spin labeling images also depict perfusion abnormality due to status epilepticus and hyperexcitability of functional network including deep brain structures and cerebellum [43, 44]. Close observation correlating brain image findings and precise examination of higher cortical functions would reveal measures to prevent post-stroke epileptogenesis causing poor neurological outcome and PSE while rescuing penumbra.

Limitations

This study has three main limitations. First, a small number of participants were included, and therefore multivariate analysis could not be performed. Second, the baseline propensity for seizures has not been fully evaluated. Third, patients with severe consciousness disturbance were excluded. These concerns will be addressed in subsequent larger prospective studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, cognitive scores, especially ACE-III, are useful for predicting late seizures in adult patients with cerebral infarction.
