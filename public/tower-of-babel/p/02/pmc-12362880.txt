Prevalence and determinants of multidrug-resistant uropathogenic Klebsiella species and associated antimicrobial resistance genes in Port Harcourt, Nigeria
Authors: Moore Ikechi Mike-Ogburia, Tombari Pius Monsi, Easter Godwin Nwokah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Klebsiella species in urinary tract infections (UTIs) poses substantial challenges for effective treatment and management. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence and determinants of MDR uropathogenic Klebsiella species and associated antimicrobial resistance genes among presumptive UTI patients in Port Harcourt.Methods A cross-sectional study was conducted from March to August 2022 at the Rivers State University Medical Centre and the Rivers State University Teaching Hospital in Port Harcourt. A total of 300 presumptive UTI patients were selected using a systematic random sampling technique. Midstream urine samples were processed using standard bacteriological techniques, followed by antimicrobial susceptibility testing using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays were performed to detect resistance genes, including blaCTX-M, blaTEM-1, blaSHV, KPC, and qnrA. Data was analysed using GraphPad Prism 9, employing descriptive and inferential statistics, with significance set at p ≤ 0.05.ResultsKlebsiella was isolated in 48 (16%) of the 300 samples, with 41 (85.4%) isolates classified as MDR, representing 13.7% of the total samples. High resistance rates were observed against cephalosporins (60.4 − 100%), fluoroquinolones (64.6%), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (100%), and tetracycline (81.3%) with multiple antimicrobial resistance (MAR) indices ranging from 0.17 to 0.83. Significant determinants of MDR Klebsiella infections included female sex (aOR = 3.60; 95% CI: 1.34–10.90; p = 0.0151), pregnancy (aOR = 5.13; 95% CI: 1.97–14.04; p = 0.0010), frequent antibiotic use (aOR = 2.49; 95% CI: 1.09–5.77; p = 0.0309), and recent hospitalisation (aOR = 2.84; 95% CI: 1.14–7.01; p = 0.0230). A history of previous UTI approached statistical significance (aOR = 2.10; 95% CI: 0.91–4.79; p= 0.0786). The blaCTX-M gene was detected in all representative MDR Klebsiella isolates (100%), blaTEM-1 and blaSHV genes in 66.7% each, and the KPC gene in 44.4%.ConclusionsThere is a high prevalence of MDR uropathogenic Klebsiella species in Port Harcourt, associated with female sex, pregnancy, frequent antibiotic use, and recent hospitalization. The blaCTX-M gene was found to be ubiquitous. These findings emphasise the need for robust antimicrobial stewardship programs, enhanced infection control measures, and continuous surveillance to mitigate uropathogenic MDR Klebsiella species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11351-1.Keywords: Klebsiella, Multidrug resistance, Urinary tract infections, Antimicrobial resistance genes, Port Harcourt

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections globally [1], affecting millions of individuals each year and imposing a significant burden on healthcare systems. These infections can range from mild urethritis to severe pyelonephritis [2, 3], potentially leading to serious complications if not properly managed. While Escherichia coli is the most frequently identified uropathogen [4], uropathogenic Klebsiella species have emerged as significant causative agents, particularly in nosocomial settings and among patients with recurrent infections [5–7].

Klebsiella species, notably Klebsiella pneumoniae and Klebsiella oxytoca, are Gram-negative bacteria known for their ability to acquire and disseminate antimicrobial resistance genes [8, 9]. The rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Klebsiella strains poses a substantial threat to public health due to limited therapeutic options and increased risk of treatment failure [8]. Multidrug resistance in Klebsiella and other uropathogens is frequently driven by genes located on mobile genetic elements such as plasmids, transposons, and integrons [10–12]. These elements facilitate the horizontal transfer of resistance determinants between different bacterial species [13], contributing to the rapid dissemination of AMR in both hospital and community settings. Common resistance genes in Klebsiella include blaCTX-M, blaSHV, and blaTEM, which encode extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) capable of hydrolysing a wide range of β-lactam antibiotics [14, 15]. Additionally, genes encoding carbapenemases, such as KPC (Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase) and NDM-1 (New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase), further undermine last-resort treatments by conferring resistance to carbapenem [16, 17]. The co-existence of multiple resistance genes within a single isolate can substantially limit therapeutic options [18], emphasising the importance of monitoring and characterising these genes for informed treatment strategies.

The prevalence of MDR uropathogenic Klebsiella species has been reported to be increasing worldwide, complicating the management of UTIs and leading to prolonged hospital stays, higher healthcare costs, and increased mortality rates [19–21]. Low- and middle-income countries, including those in sub-Saharan Africa, often face a disproportionate share of this burden [22, 23]; possibly due to factors such as limited healthcare infrastructure, inadequate surveillance systems, and over-the-counter access to antibiotics [24–26]. In Nigeria, the interplay of these factors is exacerbated by the high prevalence of counterfeit or substandard drugs and the lack of strict antibiotic stewardship programs [27, 28]. Consequently, resistant strains of Klebsiella have become increasingly prevalent in hospital settings in Nigeria [29–35] and other sub-Saharan African nations [32–35], contributing to outbreaks of healthcare-associated infections and complicating patient management. The rapid emergence of resistance in this region reiterates the need for ongoing surveillance and the implementation of effective infection control policies.

In Nigeria, and specifically in Port Harcourt, a major urban centre and hub of economic activity in the Niger Delta region, there is a paucity of data regarding the prevalence and determinants of MDR Klebsiella infections. This gap in knowledge limits the development of effective antimicrobial stewardship programs and infection control policies tailored to the local context. To address these critical gaps, this study aims to determine the prevalence and determinants of multidrug-resistant uropathogenic Klebsiella species among patients with presumptive UTIs in Port Harcourt, as well as assess the prevalence of specific antimicrobial resistance genes in the uropathogenic Klebsiella species isolated. By elucidating the prevalence and determinants of MDR Klebsiella infections and identifying the resistance genes involved, this study provides valuable insights for healthcare providers and policymakers, enhancing the understanding of the local epidemiology of MDR uropathogens, supporting the implementation of targeted interventions, and ultimately improving patient outcomes in Port Harcourt and similar settings.

Methodology

Study design and area

A cross-sectional study was conducted from March to August 2022 in Port Harcourt, the capital of Rivers State, Nigeria (Fig. 1). The research was carried out at two healthcare facilities: the Rivers State University Medical Centre and the Rivers State University Teaching Hospital. The Rivers State University Medical Centre is a public facility located on the university campus, serving the medical needs of staff and students. The Rivers State University Teaching Hospital is a government-owned tertiary hospital situated in Old GRA, Port Harcourt. It has over 375 licensed beds and 731 medical staff, offering a wide range of departments including Medicine, Paediatrics, Laboratory Services, Radiology, Family Medicine, Pathology, Obstetrics and Gynaecology, Surgery, Pharmacy, Accident and Emergency, and General Administration.

Study population

The study included patients with presumptive UTIs who attended the Rivers State University Medical Centre and the Rivers State University Teaching Hospital. Patients of all ages and genders exhibiting clinical signs and symptoms of UTIs such as dysuria, increased urinary frequency, urinary urgency or lower abdominal pain, were considered. To avoid antimicrobial influence on culture results, patients who had used antibiotics within the two weeks prior to presentation were excluded, as were menstruating women.

Sample size and sampling technique

A sample size of 300 was calculated using a prevalence study sample size calculator [37], based on an expected prevalence of 25.5% for uropathogenic Klebsiella species in Port Harcourt [38]. A systematic sampling approach was applied independently at each study site. Eligible patients were assigned sequential numbers upon presentation. A sampling interval of five was determined by dividing the estimated number of eligible patients in each study site by the desired sample size. A random starting point between one and five was selected using a random number generator, and every fifth patient was subsequently included until the required sample size was reached.

Data collection

A structured questionnaire adapted from previous studies [39–42] was used to collect data on participants’ demographic characteristics, clinical history, and antibiotic usage patterns.

Sample collection and microbiological analysis

Midstream urine samples were collected from each participant using sterile containers following standard aseptic procedures. Samples were transported to the microbiology laboratory within two hours of collection, where delay was envisaged; samples were stored in the refrigerator at 4˚C. Urine specimens were cultured on MacConkey agar and cystine lactose electrolyte-deficient (CLED) agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Klebsiella species were identified based on colonial morphology, Gram staining, and a series of biochemical tests, including lactose fermentation, indole production, urease activity, and citrate utilisation.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility was assessed using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. The antibiotics tested were selected based on clinical relevance, local prescribing patterns, availability within the study area, and recommendations from the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (NCDC) for Klebsiella spp [43]. The tested antibiotics included Cefpodoxime (30 µg), Tetracycline (30 µg), Ceftazidime (30 µg), Amoxicillin-Clavulanic Acid (30 µg), Imipenem (10 µg), Cefepime (30 µg), Amikacin, Gentamicin (120 µg), Cefotaxime (30 µg), Cefuroxime (30 µg), Ofloxacin (5 µg), and Ciprofloxacin (5 µg). Inhibition zone diameters were measured, and isolates were classified as sensitive or resistant according to CLSI interpretative criteria. Isolates resistant to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories were defined as multidrug-resistant. The Multiple Antibiotic Resistance (MAR) index was calculated using the formula: MAR = a/b, where a represents the number of antibiotics to which the isolate showed resistance, and b is the total number of antibiotics tested.

Detection of antimicrobial resistance genes

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays were conducted to detect specific antimicrobial resistance genes associated with the observed resistance patterns. Genomic DNA was extracted from nine representative Klebsiella isolates using the ZR Fungal/Bacterial DNA MiniPrep Kit (Inqaba Biotec, South Africa) following the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR amplification targeted genes, including blaCTX-M, blaTEM-1, blaSHV, KPC, and qnrA. The genes were amplified using the respective primers in Table 1. Amplified products were resolved on a 1% agarose gel at 130 V for 30 min and visualised using a blue light transilluminator.

Statistical analysis

Data was analysed using GraphPad Prism version 9. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Associations between the prevalence of multidrug-resistant Klebsiella species and categorical variables were assessed using the Chi-square test. Bivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to estimate crude odds ratios (cOR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). All variables, regardless of their significance level in the bivariate analysis, were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression model to determine adjusted odds ratios (aOR), thereby accounting for potential confounders. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Out of the 300 subjects enrolled in the study, 48 (16%) yielded Klebsiella isolates, indicating a prevalence of 16% for Klebsiella infections among the study population. Other uropathogenic bacteria identified included Escherichia coli (18.7%), Staphylococcus aureus (7%), Enterococcus faecalis (2.3%) and Proteus species (1.3%); these findings have been reported elsewhere [45]. Further speciation of the Klebsiella isolates highlighted a predominance of Klebsiella pneumoniae over Klebsiella oxytoca (Table 2).

The multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) indices of the isolated uropathogenic Klebsiella species varied substantially, ranging from 0.17 to 0.83 (Table 7). A smaller but notable proportion (10.4%) showed even broader resistance, with a MAR index of 0.83, encompassing resistance to ten antibiotics spanning five classes. The remaining isolates displayed varying intermediate levels of resistance.

The prevalence of specific antimicrobial resistance genes among the nine representative MDR uropathogenic Klebsiella isolates was assessed (Fig. 2). The blaCTX-M gene was detected in all nine isolates, corresponding to a prevalence of 100% (Fig. 3). Both the blaTEM-1 and blaSHV genes were identified in six isolates each, yielding a prevalence of 66.7% for each gene (Figs. 4 and 5). The KPC gene, encoding Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase, was present in four isolates, resulting in a prevalence of 44.4% (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The study investigated the prevalence and determinants of MDR uropathogenic Klebsiella species among patients with presumptive UTIs in Port Harcourt, Nigeria, revealing a high prevalence of MDR isolates. Specifically, 41 out of 48 Klebsiella isolates were classified as MDR, accounting for 85.4% of the Klebsiella strains and representing 13.7% of the total samples. This high prevalence indicates that the majority of Klebsiella strains in this population are resistant to multiple antimicrobial agents, posing significant challenges for public health and clinical management. The implications of these findings are profound, suggesting limited therapeutic options for treating UTIs caused by Klebsiella species in the study area, which can lead to higher rates of treatment failure, prolonged illness, increased healthcare costs, and a greater risk of complications or mortality. The proportion of Klebsiella isolates identified as multidrug-resistant (85.4%) in this study was lower than the 94.6% reported in Cameroon [34] and 98.5% in a separate Ethiopian study [33], but comparable to the 83.5% reported in another Ethiopian study [33]. However, it was notably higher than the 68% MDR prevalence among Klebsiella isolates reported in northwest Ethiopia [32]. Several factors may contribute to the elevated prevalence of MDR Klebsiella isolates observed in this study. Misuse and overuse of antibiotics are prevalent in many parts of Nigeria, driven by self-medication, over-the-counter availability of antibiotics without prescription, and inadequate adherence to antibiotic stewardship principles. Additionally, inadequate infection control practices within healthcare facilities promote the spread of MDR organisms. The genetic adaptability of Klebsiella species further exacerbates the problem, as these bacteria can acquire and disseminate resistance genes through horizontal gene transfer.

The antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed a concerning pattern of high resistance among the Klebsiella isolates to multiple commonly used antibiotics. Notably, 64.6% of the isolates were resistant to both ofloxacin and ciprofloxacin, indicating significant resistance to fluoroquinolones; this finding aligns with previous studies by Raei et al. [46], Mbamyah et al. [34], and Awoke et al. [35], which also reported high levels of fluoroquinolone resistance among Klebsiella species. Furthermore, all isolates (100%) were resistant to cefpodoxime, and high resistance rates were observed for other cephalosporins: cefepime and cefuroxime sodium each had resistance rates of 60.4%, ceftazidime had 64.6%, and cefotaxime had 62.5%; these results are consistent with the findings of Rizwan et al. [47], Muhammad et al. [48], and Mbamyah et al. [34], who documented substantial cephalosporin resistance in Klebsiella isolates. Additionally, all isolates were resistant to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, corroborating the observations of Mbamyah et al. [34], and a substantial proportion of the isolates (81.3%) were resistant to tetracycline, supporting the findings of Awoke et al. [35] and Kebbeh et al. [49]. Conversely, resistance to amikacin was low, with only 2.1% of isolates exhibiting resistance, and all isolates remained susceptible to imipenem. While this pattern aligns with prior studies [34, 35, 50, 51] and suggests that imipenem may remain an effective treatment option, it should be interpreted in the context of local antimicrobial practices. In Nigeria, imipenem is ideally reserved as a last-line agent for severe or complicated infections due to its critical role in treating multidrug-resistant organisms. However, despite its clinical efficacy, the high cost of imipenem [29] and limited availability make it inaccessible to many patients who may need it. These constraints highlight the need to ensure equitable access and promote antimicrobial stewardship to preserve the effectiveness of last-resort therapies. The high prevalence of resistance to multiple antibiotic classes in the current study significantly limits therapeutic choices and poses a serious public health concern; this extensive resistance could be attributed to factors such as the misuse and overuse of antibiotics, lack of stringent antibiotic stewardship programs, and the horizontal gene transfer of resistance determinants among bacterial populations.

The study found a significantly higher prevalence of MDR Klebsiella infections among female patients compared to males. This finding aligns with previous literature [49, 52, 53], reflecting the widely reported higher frequency of urinary tract infections in females compared to males. The increased susceptibility of females to UTIs is primarily attributed to anatomical and physiological differences, such as a shorter urethra and closer proximity of the urethral opening to the anus, facilitating easier bacterial ascent into the urinary tract [54, 55]. Additionally, hormonal fluctuations, sexual activity, and certain contraceptive methods may increase susceptibility to UTIs in females [56–58]. It is plausible that the elevated occurrence of UTIs in females also predisposes them to a greater likelihood of harbouring resistant bacterial strains such as MDR Klebsiella.

Among female participants, MDR Klebsiella infections were significantly more common in pregnant women than in their non-pregnant counterparts. This observation is corroborated by previous studies [19, 59, 60] and suggests that pregnancy is a significant risk factor for acquiring MDR Klebsiella UTIs. The increased susceptibility in pregnant women could be attributed to physiological changes during pregnancy, such as urinary stasis due to ureteral dilation and decreased bladder tone, which facilitate bacterial ascent and colonisation [61]. This observation has critical clinical implications, including increased risk of complications like pyelonephritis, and potential adverse outcomes for both the mother and foetus [62].

The study found that frequent antibiotic users had a significantly higher likelihood of MDR Klebsiella infections compared to those who used antibiotics rarely or occasionally, suggesting a strong association between frequent antimicrobial exposure and the emergence of resistance. This finding is comparable with the findings of previous studies [32, 49, 63] and implies that excessive or inappropriate use of antibiotics may contribute to the development and spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria by exerting selective pressure that favours resistant strains. Factors such as inappropriate prescribing practices, self-medication, and over-the-counter availability of antibiotics without prescription could be attributed to this association.

Individuals with prior UTIs showed a higher prevalence of MDR Klebsiella infections compared to those without; while this trend only approached statistical significance in the multivariable analysis, it suggests that past UTI episodes may predispose individuals to subsequent infections with resistant Klebsiella strains. This finding is comparable with previous research outcomes [49, 64], suggesting that previous UTIs may contribute to the selection and persistence of resistant bacterial populations, possibly due to repeated exposure to antibiotics during prior treatments, which can promote the development of resistance through selective pressure.

Individuals recently hospitalised had a significantly higher likelihood of MDR Klebsiella infections compared to those without recent hospitalisation in this study. This finding is corroborated by previous studies [32, 53] and suggests that hospitalisation is a significant risk factor for acquiring MDR Klebsiella infections, likely due to increased exposure to resistant organisms within healthcare settings where antibiotic use is prevalent. The hospital environment can facilitate the selection and transmission of resistant bacteria through factors such as inadequate infection control practices, lack of good antimicrobial stewardship programs and the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics [65].

The study found that the isolated uropathogenic Klebsiella species exhibited high multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) indices, ranging from 0.17 to 0.83, with over half of the isolates showing resistance to nine or more antibiotics. This suggests significant exposure to environments with intense antibiotic pressure and highlights the alarming breadth of resistance among these strains. These findings are comparable with previous Nigerian studies [51, 66, 67] and imply that a significant proportion of the Klebsiella isolates harbour extensive multidrug resistance, posing serious challenges for the treatment and control of infections caused by these organisms. The high MAR indices could be attributed to factors such as the misuse and overuse of antibiotics in both community and healthcare settings, which create selective pressure that promote the emergence and persistence of highly resistant bacterial strains.

The study investigated the presence of specific resistance genes in nine representative MDR uropathogenic Klebsiella species with high MAR indices. Notably, all isolates harboured the blaCTX-M gene, which encodes CTX-M-type ESBLs, while 66.7% each carried the blaTEM-1 and blaSHV genes, and 44.4% possessed the KPC gene, indicating significant multidrug resistance. Compared to previous studies, the prevalence of the blaCTX-M gene in this study (100%) was markedly higher than prevalence reported by Okogeri et al. [68] in Port Harcourt (25%) and Mohammed et al. [69] in Northern Nigeria (27.3%) but aligned closely with Ahmed et al. [70], who observed a 68.4% prevalence and Ogbolu et al. [71] and Yamasaki et al. [72], reporting prevalences of 83.3% and 89.4%, respectively. Similarly, the presence of the blaTEM-1 gene in 66.7% of isolates corroborates findings by Okogeri et al. [68], who reported a 75% prevalence, whereas Mohammed et al. [69] noted a lower prevalence of 27.3%. The detection of the KPC gene in 44.4% of isolates contrasts with lower prevalences reported in other regions, such as Mohammed et al. [69] with 8.7%, Egwuatu et al. [73] with 7.69%, and Bina et al. [74], who found no KPC genes. These discrepancies may be attributed to regional variations in antibiotic usage, differences in infection control practices, and the genetic adaptability of Klebsiella species. The presence of multiple β-lactamase genes and carbapenemase genes within the same isolates poses significant challenges for treatment, as it limits therapeutic options and increases the risk of treatment failure.

This study has a few limitations that may affect the generalizability and depth of its findings. The study was conducted in only two healthcare facilities in Port Harcourt, which may not fully capture the variability of uropathogenic Klebsiella infections in other regions or clinical settings. The cross-sectional design precludes drawing definitive causal relationships between associated factors and the emergence of MDR Klebsiella infections. Potential recall bias and underreporting of antibiotic usage or clinical history could have influenced the accuracy of the collected data. Finally, the molecular analysis focused on a limited set of resistance genes and representative isolates, which might underestimate the overall diversity of resistance mechanisms present in uropathogenic Klebsiella isolates.

Conclusions

This study identified a high prevalence of multidrug-resistant Klebsiella species among presumptive UTI patients in Port Harcourt, with significant resistance to commonly used antibiotics, including cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid. Key determinants of MDR infections included female sex, pregnancy, frequent antibiotic use, and recent hospitalisation. The detection of resistance genes such as blaCTX-M, blaTEM-1, blaSHV, and KPC highlights the molecular basis for resistance in this setting. The implementation of context-specific antimicrobial stewardship programs, prioritising rational antibiotic prescribing, especially in pregnant women and patients with recent hospital exposure, is recommended. Continuous molecular surveillance of resistance genes and stricter regulation of over-the-counter antibiotic access should also be enforced. These interventions should be driven by public health policymakers in collaboration with clinicians and hospital administrators, while researchers should continue to monitor resistance patterns and inform policy with local data. Strengthening health education to promote responsible antibiotic use in the general public is also essential. Together, these actions can help mitigate the spread of MDR Klebsiella and improve infection management in similar resource-limited settings.
