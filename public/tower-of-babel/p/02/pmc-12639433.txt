Three-Year Outcomes of Second Bioprosthesis After Early Surgical Aortic Bioprosthetic Failure
Authors: Le Nguyen, Evan Walser-Kuntz, Larissa Stanberry, Jennifer Nguyen, Tremayne Ansani, Kayla Wong, Maurice Sarano, Vinayak Bapat, Benjamin Sun, Mario Gössl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBioprosthetic valves are widely used in patients aged ≥65 years, but their durability is limited by structural valve deterioration (SVD). Data on repeat bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement (AVR) for early SVD (≤5 years) are scarce.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to evaluate 3-year hemodynamic outcomes and the incidence of recurrent SVD following repeat AVR.MethodsThe authors retrospectively studied patients with surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) and early SVD requiring reintervention at Abbott Northwestern Hospital from January 2012 to December 2022. All patients had echocardiographic follow-up ≥30 days after repeat AVR. The primary endpoint was early SVD of the second bioprosthesis as per Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 criteria within 5 years after repeat SAVR or valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). Secondary outcomes were mortality, congestive heart failure admission, and acute stroke.ResultsAmong 92 patients requiring reintervention for early SVD after SAVR, 76 had TAVR upon reintervention, and 16 had repeat SAVR. Recurrent early SVD occurred in 20 patients (21.7%); 13 (65.0%) occurred in the first year. Recurrent early SVD rates were higher, but not statistically different among patients receiving TAVR-after-SAVR (23.7%) vs SAVR-after-SAVR (12.5%) (adjusted HR: 3.31; 95% CI: 0.43-25.5; P = 0.23). Mean gradient ≥20 mm Hg, aortic valve area <1.0 cm2, and moderate/severe aortic regurgitation at discharge or 30 days were independent predictors of recurrent early SVD. At 3 years, mortality was 18.5%, stroke was 5.4%, and congestive heart failure was 26.1%.ConclusionsEarly failure of the second bioprosthesis after reintervention, especially following valve-in-valve TAVR, mostly occurred within the first year. Residual hemodynamic abnormalities at discharge strongly predict early SVD.Key words: clinical outcomes, failed bioprostheses, preoperative surgical aortic valve replacement, structural valve deterioration, valve hemodynamics, valve-in-valve TAVR

Bioprosthetic valves are now the predominant prosthesis of choice for aortic valve replacement (AVR) in cases of severe aortic valve disease.1 Multiple comparative studies have demonstrated both short- and long-term outcomes comparable to those of mechanical valves, even among younger patients.2, 3, 4 However, the primary limitation of bioprosthetic valves is structural deterioration over time, which can lead to complications such as regurgitation and stenosis.5 As advancements in valve technology and implantation techniques continue to evolve, so too does the management of prosthesis-related complications, including bioprosthetic failure.

The expected durability of bioprosthetic valves in most adult patients ranges from 10 to 15 years. However, early data from single-center studies suggest that structural and hemodynamic deterioration may occur in <10 years, with signs of “early” structural valve deterioration (SVD) appearing as soon as 5 years.6,7 Two current options to treat early degenerated surgical aortic bioprostheses are repeat surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) (re-SAVR) and valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) (ViV-TAVR). However, limited data on outcomes following reintervention for early (≤5 years) aortic bioprosthetic failure pose challenges in selecting the optimal treatment strategy. Moreover, direct comparative studies assessing the durability of the second bioprosthetic valve after ViV-TAVR or re-SAVR remain scarce.

This study evaluated the overall incidence of aortic valve reinterventions due to early surgical bioprosthetic failure. It also examined the clinical outcomes of reintervention with a bioprosthesis over a 3-year follow-up period, comparing the progression of SVD after ViV-TAVR vs re-SAVR. The analysis focused on hemodynamic performance, mode of failure, postprocedural adverse events, and quality-of-life measures through 3 years.

Methods

Patient selection

This observational, single-center clinical study retrospectively analyzed data prospectively collected from adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent either ViV-TAVR or re-SAVR to replace early (≤5 years) failed bioprosthetic valve due to structural valve degeneration between 2012 and 2022 at Abbott Northwestern Hospital. Bioprosthetic failure was defined based on severe stenosis, severe regurgitation, or a combination of at least moderate stenosis and at least moderate regurgitation according to standard echocardiographic criteria.8 We excluded hospitalizations with concomitant mitral, pulmonary, or tricuspid valvular diseases to identify patients with only aortic valvular diseases. We also excluded patients with a history of reintervention for valve thrombosis, endocarditis, or paravalvular regurgitation. Additionally, we excluded patients with mechanical valve replacement, those without ≥30-day echocardiographic follow-up after repeat AVR, and those undergoing concurrent procedures such as coronary artery bypass grafting, ascending aorta reintervention, atrial or ventricular septal defect closure, or mitral/tricuspid valve repair or replacement, resulting in a final cohort of patients with aortic bioprosthetic valves who underwent either ViV-TAVR or re-SAVR alone.

Clinical data from the preintervention, reintervention, and postreintervention phases were prospectively recorded in an institutional database. However, because this study was not a predefined analysis at the time of database creation, it should be considered retrospective. Transthoracic echocardiograms performed before and after reintervention were retrospectively evaluated according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 (VARC-3) criteria.9 The study protocol was approved by the Allina Institutional Review Board and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Only records of patients who did not object to having their data used in research were investigated.

Valve durability definition

Types of bioprosthetic valve deterioration before reintervention were categorized per the VARC-3 criteria: 1) stenosis, if the mean gradient was ≥30 mm Hg without significant aortic regurgitation (AR) (≥moderate); 2) regurgitation, if AR was ≥moderate without significant stenosis (gradient <30 mm Hg); or 3) mixed if mean gradient was ≥30 mm Hg with ≥moderate AR. SVD was determined based on 2 consecutive mean gradient measurements meeting these criteria.

Endpoints

The primary study endpoint was early degeneration of the second bioprosthesis as per VARC-3 criteria within 5 years after repeat re-SAVR or ViV-TAVR. Second bioprosthesis degeneration events were included if they occurred anytime from day one postprocedure through 5 years or the last follow-up. Degeneration on the same day as the index procedure, and patients who died on day zero were excluded. Secondary outcomes were 1) longitudinal mortality, 2) congestive heart failure (CHF) admissions, and 3) acute stroke.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized by either mean ± SD or median (Q1–Q3) and were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-squared or Fisher exact test, as deemed appropriate. The change in mean gradient, aortic valve area (AVA), and their association with the risk of all-cause mortality was estimated from the joint longitudinal and time-to-event model. Kaplan-Meier estimates were used to assess the survival free from all-cause death and cumulative incidence of first CHF admission after repeat AVR with groups compared using the log-rank or Gray’s test, respectively. HRs with 95% CIs were estimated using Cox regression models to assess the association between reintervention type (re-SAVR or ViV-TAVR) and survival or risk of first CHF admission. Unadjusted regression models were used for survival, and cause-specific regression models for CHF admissions, with proportional hazards assumptions confirmed. All statistical computations were conducted in R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Utilizing the hospital-wide administrative database from the period 2012 to 2022, we identified 4,707 implanted patients: 1,606 SAVR and 3,101 TAVR. In 5 years, there were 311 (6.6%) reintervention events due to bioprosthetic failure, of which 113 patients (7.0%) underwent index SAVR compared with 198 patients (6.4%) who had index TAVR. Only 7 patients initially receiving TAVR and meeting inclusion criteria had early SVD; all received repeat/ViV-TAVR. These 7 patients were excluded due to the small population. The primary study cohort comprised 92 patients receiving SAVR as the initial intervention (Figure 1), of which 76 had TAVR for reintervention and 16 had re-SAVR.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort before reintervention are presented in Table 1. Briefly, patients were elderly (76 years [Q1-Q3: 71-81 years]), had a median STS score (Society of Thoracic Surgeons) of 5.0 (Q1-Q3: 3.1-7.2), were predominantly men (58.7%), and were severely symptomatic (77.2% NYHA functional class III/IV). The median time from the first AVR to reintervention was 5 years (Q1-Q3: 4-6 years). In patients who underwent ViV-TAVR, transfemoral access was used in 76 (100.0%) patients; commonly implanted surgical valve sizes were 23 mm (47.8%) and 26 mm (32.6%) (Supplemental Table 1).

Outcomes at 3 years

Among the 92 SAVR patients with early SVD requiring reintervention, 3-year follow-up was available for 65 patients (70.6%). The remaining 27 patients (29.3%) were either lost to follow-up or later declined research authorization to allow prospective data collection beyond the index hospitalization. Mortality was 18.5% (95% CI: 11.9-27.6). Stroke affected 5.4% (95% CI: 2.3-12.1) of patients (3.5% [95% CI: 1.1-9.2] major stroke), and the cumulative incidence of new-onset CHF was 26.1% (95% CI: 18.2-35.9) (Figure 2).

Hemodynamic outcomes

Detailed echocardiographic characteristics at follow-up are displayed in Table 2. Echocardiographic assessments were performed at multiple time points for all 92 patients, enabling longitudinal evaluation of hemodynamic parameters. At 3 years after repeat AVR, the mean gradient decreased by 18.6 mm Hg (95% CI: −21.3 to −17.1; P < 0.0001), and the AVA increased by 0.57 cm2 (95% CI: 0.42-0.59; P < 0.0001) compared to baseline. The peak aortic velocity decreased by 2.80 m/s (95% CI: −2.92 to −2.75; P < 0.0001). Median LVEF increased by 15.0% (95% CI: 12.2-17.6; P = 0.15). At the 3-year follow-up, 77.5% (31/40) of patients had no or trace total AR, 8.5% (7/40) had mild regurgitation, 2.5% (1/40) had moderate regurgitation, and 2.5% (1/40) had severe regurgitation. Total aortic stenosis at 3 years was less than mild in 95.0% (38/40) of patients, with 2.5% (1/40) having mild, and 2.5% (1/40) of patients had moderate-to-severe stenosis.

Hemodynamic comparison between ViV-TAVR and RE-SAVR

Differences in hemodynamic performance were observed between re-SAVR and ViV-TAVR groups, particularly in mean gradient reduction. Over 3 years, the re-SAVR group had a greater mean gradient decrease (1.5 mm Hg; 95% CI: −6.3 to 2.1; P = 0.80), with an additional decrease of 0.61 mm Hg per year (95% CI: −1.7 to 0.2; P = 0.20) (Supplemental Figure 1). AVA increased over 3 years for both groups, with the re-SAVR group showing a greater increase (0.1 cm2; 95% CI: −0.43 to 0.62; P = 0.80) (Supplemental Table 2, Supplemental Figure 2). Peak aortic velocity and LVEF showed no significant differences between groups. Over 3 years, both groups had a comparable decrease in peak velocity (−0.18 m/s; 95% CI: −0.06 to 0.42; P = 0.10). Three-year LVEF improved by 2.8% in re-SAVR (95% CI: −0.36 to 6.0; P = 0.082), and at 3 years, an additional improvement of 6.0% was observed in re-SAVR (95% CI: −2.3 to 14; P = 0.20) (Supplemental Table 2, Supplemental Figure 3).

Structural valve deterioration

The onset of SVD occurred in 20 (21.7%) patients over 3 years, with most cases (65.0%) observed during the first year (Figure 3). Among these patients, 9 (45.0%) exhibited signs of moderate/severe stenosis, 8 (40.0%) showed worsening regurgitation, and 3 (15.0%) met both criteria concurrently (Figure 1). SVD as defined in VARC-3 was initially detected at a median time of 11 months (range: 1-37 months) after surgery. The average duration of the second bioprosthetic valve was shorter than that of the initial bioprosthetic valve (11 months vs 88 months; 95% CI: 43.8-75.0; P < 0.001), respectively. Additionally, the second bioprosthetic valve displayed varying degeneration rates based on the initial procedure: 23.7% (18/76) for TAVR after SAVR (ViV) and 12.5% (2/16) for SAVR after SAVR.

Factors associated with early structural valve deterioration

In the overall study population (ViV-TAVR + re-SAVR), the factors associated with early SVD following reintervention in univariable and multivariable analyses are presented in Table 3. In multivariable analysis, echocardiographic parameters at discharge or 30 days were significantly associated with the risk of early SVD. A mean transvalvular gradient ≥20 mm Hg (HR: 1.97 [95% CI: 1.89-2.05]; P = 0.020), an AVA <1.0 cm2 (HR: 1.83 [95% CI: 0.75-4.54]; P = 0.020), moderate/severe AR (HR: 1.75 [95% CI: 1.00-3.12]; P = 0.049), and moderate/severe aortic stenosis (HR: 3.12 [95% CI: 1.40-6.97]; P = 0.007) were independently associated with higher risk of early degeneration. These echocardiographic thresholds were based on the VARC-3 criteria for SVD.

Quality of life

The 3-year trend in quality of life after repeat AVR showed significant improvement. The mean overall summary Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire score was 49.5 ± 21.5 at baseline, increasing to 77.6 ± 23.4 at 30 days (P < 0.0001), with sustained improvement at 3 years (P = 0.551) compared with baseline (Supplemental Figure 4). There was no difference in the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire score between ViV-TAVR and re-SAVR.

Discussion

This observational single-center study describes the outcomes of reintervention in 92 patients who underwent repeat AVR (re-SAVR or ViV-TAVR) over a 3-year follow-up period with a second bioprosthetic valve of the implanter’s choice (Central Illustration). The main findings of this study were 1) approximately 2 out of every 10 patients (21.7%) developed moderate or severe SVD in 3 years, with the second bioprosthesis showing significantly lower durability by eightfold; 2) the majority of valve failures after intervention occurred within the first year, with failures after ViV-TAVR typically appearing earlier, whereas failures after re-SAVR were more delayed; 3) periprocedural all-cause mortality and heart failure hospitalization rates were significantly high with both reinterventions, but re-SAVR was associated with lower risks of postprocedural complications.

Over the past decade, bioprosthetic valve utilization for managing severe aortic stenosis has steadily increased, surpassing mechanical valves as the preferred prosthesis, as TAVR expanded to lower-risk populations.3,4,10 However, bioprosthetic valves have shorter durability as they are prone to degeneration over time, increasing the risk of rehospitalization.5 Early intermediate-term data from retrospective cohort studies suggest relatively low rates of bioprosthetic valve deterioration within the first 5 years.11, 12, 13 More contemporary reports from randomized controlled trials and single-arm registries, out to 10 years, have similarly demonstrated low reintervention rates associated with bioprosthetic aortic valves, regardless of the initial intervention type.14, 15, 16 Our study observed similarly low early failure rates, aligning with previous findings. These low reintervention rates through 5 years after TAVR or SAVR provide valuable insights for physicians in making long-term management decisions for patients with aortic valve disease.

In cases of failed surgical bioprosthetic aortic valves, ViV-TAVR is a more appealing approach than repeat open-heart surgery because of its lower procedural risk and shorter recovery time.17 Unlike TAVR for native valves, which was approved based on randomized trials across all surgical risk levels,18,19 the adoption of ViV-TAVR has been largely driven by single-arm registry studies and the relative technical simplicity of the procedure.20, 21, 22 While current international guidelines recommend ViV-TAVR for symptomatic patients or those at high or prohibitive surgical risk,23,24 its use has expanded widely and rapidly, raising concerns about potential indication creep beyond well-supported populations. Our analysis underscores the need for caution, especially in younger patients with early surgical bioprosthetic valve failure.

The unexpectedly high SVD rate within the first year after reintervention, primarily ViV-TAVR, in our analysis can be attributed to several factors. One key contributor is the higher incidence of significant prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM) and residual obstruction to left ventricular outflow following ViV-TAVR compared to re-SAVR.17 Severe PPM has been reported in 25% to 50% of ViV-TAVR cases and 12% to 15% of repeat SAVR cases, and is a well-established risk factor for heart failure and increased mortality after index TAVR and SAVR.22,25, 26, 27 PPM is known to result in elevated transvalvular mean gradients and smaller effective orifice area28 and may therefore underlie the hemodynamic abnormalities observed in our cohort. In support of this, our analysis identified several echocardiographic parameters as independent predictors of early SVD. These included mean gradient ≥20 mm Hg and AVA <1.0 cm2 at discharge or 30 days after reintervention. The additional presence of moderate or severe AR or stenosis further compounded the risk.

Additionally, previously identified risk factors for bioprosthetic deterioration include mitral valve position, end-stage renal disease, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, lipid-mediated inflammation, and diabetes, while the influence of age remains uncertain.12,29,30 In our analysis, however, age, hypertension, and diabetes were not linked to accelerated SVD (Table 3). Another potential factor is implantation techniques, including suboptimal valve sizing algorithms and limited operator experience with implantation. In our patient cohort, most SVD cases were associated with aortic stenosis in early-generation stented valves. Stented valves tend to develop stenosis over time,31 but most advanced valves, combined with refined sizing algorithms, may mitigate this issue and yield better outcomes in current clinical practice.

The factors contributing to the increased risks of mortality and heart failure hospitalizations following repeat AVR, as observed in this study and previous reports, remain unclear.26,32,33 Significant baseline differences between treatment groups, referral and selection biases, and individual patient preferences may not be fully accounted for by propensity matching. Since the treatments were not randomized, a direct head-to-head comparison is not possible. To date, no randomized clinical trials comparing ViV-TAVR and re-SAVR for failed aortic bioprostheses have been conducted, largely due to the lack of equipoise in treatment decisions for older adults at high or prohibitive surgical risk.17 The best way to address this uncertainty would be through a well-designed randomized clinical trial focusing on younger patients with extended follow-up.

With advancements in surgery and increased life expectancy, many patients who undergo AVR using bioprostheses are expected to require valve reinterventions in the future. This trend is especially notable in the current era of transcatheter replacement, where bioprosthetic valves are increasingly preferred over mechanical ones. The findings of this study, alongside previous research, emphasize the crucial role of a multidisciplinary heart team and an individualized approach. It is essential for clinicians to thoroughly understand the risks and benefits of various options for valve reinterventions, such as ViV-TAVR, re-SAVR, TAVR in SAVR, and explant TAVR, particularly regarding the initial choice between bioprosthetic and mechanical valves. These details should be discussed with patients at each intervention to facilitate informed decision-making. The heart team should consider factors like surgical risk, mode of bioprosthetic valve failure, type of bioprosthetic valve, comorbidities (eg, complex coronary artery disease, significant mitral or tricuspid regurgitation), and feasibility of transfemoral access to select the most appropriate reintervention type.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, detailed procedural and anatomical data were not available for inclusion in this analysis. The study did not capture granular information regarding valve sizing and potential underexpansion in ViV-TAVR cases. These factors are known to significantly influence procedural success, long-term hemodynamic performance, and the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch. Furthermore, the absence of data on procedural nuances, such as the use of techniques like bioprosthetic valve fracture or commissural alignment, limits the ability to contextualize the observed outcomes in relation to modern procedural practices. Second, the relatively small cohort size limits statistical power and the robustness of comparative analyses. This limitation is further compounded by variability in baseline characteristics and procedural details, which may have influenced differences in SVD rates and all-cause mortality. Additionally, the low follow-up rate (71%) at 3 years limits the generalizability of long-term outcomes. Third, the retrospective design and nonrandomized treatment allocation introduce the potential for unmeasured confounders and residual bias, despite efforts to adjust for baseline characteristics. Observational studies inherently lack the rigor of randomized controlled trials, and treatment selection bias may have influenced procedural outcomes and durability estimates. For instance, the selection of ViV-TAVR vs re-SAVR may have been influenced by unmeasured factors such as frailty, anatomic considerations, or institutional expertise, which were not captured in this analysis. Lastly, the study does not include detailed data on rehospitalizations, intermediate-term quality-of-life metrics, or economic analyses, which are critical to comprehensively understanding the impact of repeat bioprosthetic valve interventions. Additionally, the absence of rehospitalization data limits the ability to assess the burden of recurrent hospital admissions and associated health care costs, which are increasingly important considerations in the era of value-based care.

Conclusions

Periprocedural all-cause mortality and heart failure hospitalization rates were significantly high with both reinterventions. Reintervention for early surgical bioprosthetic failure is associated with accelerated SVD within 5 years. The majority of valve failures after intervention occurred within the first year, with SVD occurring most often at ≤1 year after ViV-TAVR and >1 year after re-SAVR. Echocardiographic findings at discharge, specifically mean gradient, AVA, and moderate/severe aortic stenosis or regurgitation, were independent predictors of early SVD.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Bioprosthetic valve failure remains a challenge, with a high risk of stenosis or regurgitation causing early deterioration of the second bioprosthesis, especially within the first year. ViV-TAVR failed earlier than re-SAVR, emphasizing the need for careful procedural selection.

COMPETENCY IN PATIENT CARE: Repeat AVR requires consideration of hemodynamic performance, PPM, and durability. Close echocardiographic monitoring post-reintervention is essential for early detection of SVD.

COMPETENCY IN PRACTICE-BASED LEARNING AND IMPROVEMENT: Early failure patterns emphasize the importance of refining prosthesis selection and procedural techniques to improve long-term outcomes.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK 1: Further research is needed to identify biomarkers and imaging modalities that can predict early SVD after repeat AVR. Advanced hemodynamic assessment tools and computational modeling may improve patient-specific risk stratification.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK 2: Randomized controlled trials comparing ViV-TAVR and re-SAVR in younger, lower-risk populations are essential to determine the optimal strategy for bioprosthetic failure. Such studies should evaluate long-term survival, hemodynamic performance, and quality of life.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK 3: Future innovations in bioprosthetic valve design, including modified tissue processing and anticalcification treatments, may enhance durability and reduce the likelihood of early deterioration.
