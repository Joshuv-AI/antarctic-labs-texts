Some misinterpretations of Inferential statistics in dental public health literature
Authors: Talal S. Alshihayb, Lubna A. Alnasser, Walid A. Al-Soneidar, Mohammad A. Mansournia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesInferential statistics such as p-values and confidence intervals (CIs) are ubiquitously used in research studies. Still, researchers can incorrectly interpret them, impacting the validity and utility of the respective results. The aim was to quantify how often studies commit one incorrect interpretation, dichotomization, in the dental public health literature.MethodsThe authors carried out an electronic search using PubMed to extract original papers published in 2018/2019/2023 (either online or in print) in five dental public health journals. Four trained and calibrated reviewers extracted information from the abstract and main text on the following: reporting any p-value (Yes/No), reporting p-value as inequality (Yes/No/NA), reporting non-significant p-value (Yes/No/Not applicable (NA)), reporting any confidence interval (Yes/No), reporting non-significant confidence interval (Yes/No/NA), and concluding there is no association because the p-value or confidence intervals were not-significant (Yes/No). In addition, investigators extracted if studies explicitly reported that p < 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsThe results indicate that p-values were reported more frequently than CIs in both the abstract and main text. The majority of studies interpreted non-significant p-values or CIs to mean no association or no effect in their main text. Also, non-significant p-values and CIs were less frequently reported in abstracts compared to the main text. These findings varied across the five dental public health journals, but were less notably changed by the print publication year.ConclusionThe results of this paper showed that some common misconceptions about p-values and CIs still linger in the dental public health literature after seven years had passed since warnings against such practices. More advanced training may be needed to overcome the issues with p-values, confidence interval reporting, and interpretation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06962-8.

Introduction

P-values and confidence intervals (CIs) are inferential statistics used ubiquitously in research and lack intuitive interpretations. Previous studies have discussed the most common misconceptions regarding p-values and CIs based on the frequentist statistical paradigm in the literature (a paradigm that defines probability as the relative frequency of an event over the long run) [1, 2]. The other statistical paradigm, Bayesian, which defines probability as the incorporation of prior information along with the likelihood of data; has its own measures and misconceptions. Ideally, in the frequentist paradigm, p-values and CIs should be interpreted descriptively as advocated by Amrhein et al. [3] and Greenland and Rafi [4]. When calculating p-values and CIs, various model assumptions are considered beyond the test hypothesis being true, including no uncontrolled systematic errors (see Table 1 for correct interpretations) [2, 4]. Unfortunately, these other assumptions are often taken for granted when interpreting p-values and CIs [4]. These misconceptions, as general beliefs, around p-values and CIs open the door to the practice of misinterpreting the statistical outputs. The misinterpretations of p-values and CIs are so common in the literature (see Table 1 for incorrect interpretations) [5–8]. One of the most problematic misinterpretations is dichotomization; that is, enforcing a cut-off point of the p-value, conventionally as significant ≤ 0.05 < not significant. Collectively, misconceptions around p-values led the American Statistical Association to issue a statement on p-values in 2016, calling for abandoning statistical significance [9]. Furthermore, there have been several calls against dichotomizing p-values and CIs [10–16]. P-values and CIs misinterpretations, including their dichotomization, lead to practices where authors are semi-consciously looking for results that confirm their hypotheses. Consequently, P-hacking, fishing, and selective reporting practices have appeared in the literature [3]. Ultimately, the pervasive misinterpretations, including dichotomization, of p-values and CIs mean that many conclusions from scientific studies could be inaccurate. Inaccurate conclusions are detrimental as they shape treatment decisions, guidelines, policies, and priorities [12]. Further, judging the importance and validity of results based on statistical significance has led to today’s publication bias, in which research with non-significant results gets viewed as less valuable and is therefore ignored and denied publication [17].

P-values and CIs misconceptions, and consequently, misinterpretations, shape the health care literature, including oral health [18]. As of now, it is unclear how prevalent the dichotomization of p-values and CIs, as one form of misinterpretations, is in the oral health literature. Therefore, examining how p-values and CIs are interpreted in the oral health literature may highlight this issue to oral health researchers and hopefully improve the quality of the oral health literature by exploring how frequently they are in the dental public health (DPH) literature to showcase the issue as a pragmatic approach.

This study aimed to determine how prevalent the dichotomization of p-values and CIs is in the dental public health literature. The objectives were to estimate the prevalence of dichotomization of p-values and CIs in five leading DPH-focused journals and then compare the prevalence between journals and years of publication. By targeting DPH-focused journals, the authors aimed to investigate the prevalence of the problem in a pragmatic manner, as these DPH-focused journals accept submissions from all dental specialties. Therefore, they could serve as an example to showcase the issues of p-values and CIs to their wide oral health audience. To meet the study objectives, the investigators included studies from the selected journals, extracted their p-values and CIs, and analyzed them.

Methods

This study adopts a meta-research approach that systematically examines patterns in research reporting, methodology, or interpretation to assess the quality and/or reproducibility of published studies. Specifically, the authors searched PubMed for original observational studies and Randomized Clinical Trials (RCTs) published in 2018, 2019, and a random sample of 2023 (either online or in print) to examine pre-selected misinterpretation of p-values and CI. Study investigators selected those years to allow for sufficient time between the published calls to avoid misinterpretations and lingering misinterpretations to be reflected in dental journals [2, 9]. This study was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/sp2r3/). Changes from the pre-registered protocol are listed and justified in the appendix. The study conforms to STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies.

Journals selected

The following dental journals were selected because they publish many observational studies by and/or targeted at DPH: [1] BMC Oral Health; [2] Community Dental Health (CDH); [3] Community Dentistry and Oral Epidemiology (CDOE); [4] JDR Clinical and Translational Research (JDR CTR); and [5] Journal of Public Health Dentistry (JPHD). These journals were categorized into more DPH-focused journals (CDH, CDOE, and JPHD) and relevant but less DPH-focused journals (BMC Oral Health and JDR CTR). CDOE and CDH are long-established journals that specialize in public health dentistry and oral epidemiology. BMC Oral Health regularly publishes research on social determinants, access to care, and prevention. JPHD focuses on oral health policy and population-level interventions. JDR CTR, while broader in scope, emphasizes research with real-world impact, including studies relevant to communities and health systems. Beyond thematic relevance, we also considered factors like geographic representation, journal visibility, and impact metrics, as in to methods utilized in conducting similar research in other specialties [19]. These journals varied in terms of SciMago journal rank and publishing models (open access and subscription-based) and are based in different regions, including Europe and North America. This diversity helps ensure that our review captures a broad and balanced picture of current research in dental public health from different settings, levels of visibility, and areas of influence within the field.

Search strategy

The authors consulted a medical librarian on their proposed search strategy, who suggested conducting and comparing multiple search strategies to ensure that PubMed had good coverage of the five selected journals (Appendix Table 1). The study team varied the years included in the search, added/removed keywords on study type “cohort, case-control, observational, etc.” and included/excluded the Boolean operator “NOT” in the search (Appendix Table 1). Including “NOT” in the search strategy did not add an appreciable number of results (compare strategy #2 and #5). Therefore, search strategy number 5 (including the years 2018–2019, with disjunction of additional text words (e.g., observational) and no exclusions was selected for this study (n = 345 studies).

To evaluate whether PubMed had good coverage of the included journals, the number of publications was manually counted in all issues of the journals in 2018–2019, then compared to the numbers found by the PubMed search strategy (Appendix Table 2). The investigators found that the PubMed search strategy provided comprehensive coverage of the selected journals, so they restricted their search to PubMed only. The PubMed search results were extracted as a comma-separated value (CSV) file.

To validate the selected search strategy, a random sample of 200 studies was taken from the search strategy that represents the gold standard search strategy #8 (n = 1,022 studies) using journals’ names and years 2018–2019 without adding further words such as (“case-control”, “observational”, etc.) and no exclusion by using “NOT”. Then, the authors checked whether the 200 randomly selected studies would be included in the study (by screening them for the inclusion/exclusion criteria). After that, the authors carried out selected search strategy #5 and checked how many of those 200 randomly selected studies from strategy #8 with known inclusion status (127 yes and 73 no) were captured by search strategy #5. Strategy #5 captured all 200 randomly selected studies. However, some of these captured studies were false-positives (37%), which in this context will not affect the final number of included studies but would have inflated the initial number of studies that the reviewers would have needed to screen the false-positive studies out (Appendix Table 3). The same search strategy was applied to 2023 (n = 385), after which a random sample was taken so that 100 papers would be included (Fig. 1). Therefore, the inclusion rate of papers published in 2018 and 2019 was also applied to papers published in 2023, and 115 randomly selected papers were identified in 2023.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria and training of reviewers

The authors targeted original research studies that implemented frequentist statistical methods and, therefore, manually excluded reviews, letters to editors, editorials, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, Bayesian studies, studies without p-values or confidence intervals, or studies that were done for prediction modeling. The following research team members screened studies and extracted data (TSA, LAA, WAA) in addition to recruiting a biostatistician colleague to assist as a fourth reviewer (SA); all these reviewers had post-graduate degrees in epidemiology, and the first three were originally trained as dentists. The members underwent three rounds of training on screening and data extraction (round one = 17 studies, round two = 10 studies, and round three = 5 studies). The iterative training process was conducted until all members achieved high agreement on the inclusion criteria of studies, and the data collection form was modified after each round of training based on team discussions. The training was conducted on published studies from the same journals in the years before 2018 and 2019. The final calibration of the reviewers was conducted through a 1-hour workshop on the data extraction tool using two studies for each round of training. Inter-examiner reliability was calculated using percent agreement for rounds two (Appendix Table 7) and three (Appendix Table 8). The average percent agreement between reviewers per item ranged from 0.73 to 1 in round three. The authors were not able to calculate Kappa or Kripindorff’s alpha (i.e., Kappa for more than two reviewers) due to issues of invariability in answers of some reviewers.

Data extraction from included studies

The following data were extracted separately from each of the abstract and main text (methods and/or results sections): reporting any p-value (Yes/No), reporting p-value as inequality (Yes/No/NA), reporting any non-significant p-value (Yes/No/Not applicable (NA)), reporting any confidence interval (Yes/No), reporting any non-significant confidence interval (Yes/No/NA), and concluding there is no association because the p-value or CIs were not-significant (Yes/No). In addition, the authors extracted the following data from the main text: if the authors reported any statement in the methods section, that p < 0.05 would be considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

To estimate the prevalence of p-values and CIs dichotomization across all five journals, Study investigators reported their proportions and then compared these proportions among the five journals and across publication years. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS software Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Data and analysis code are available at https://osf.io/sp2r3/.

In the abstract section, almost half of the studies reported any p-value, and at least two in every five studies reported p-values as inequalities (40.6%). P-values and 95% CIs in the main text

In the main text, most studies had some form of a statement indicating the significance of the results based on a cut-off point (65.0%) (Table 3). Confidence intervals were reported less frequently compared to p-values (64.3%). About half of the studies had non-significant confidence intervals in the main text (54.0%). Regarding the conclusions, studies mostly concluded no association due to insignificant p-values or confidence intervals in the main text (63.8%).

When comparing more vs. less DPH focused journals (Appendix Table 6), notable differences were found in reporting p-values and CIs. However, no differences in their interpretations were observed.

Discussion

The results indicate that p-values were reported more frequently than CIs in both abstract and main text across the five DPH journals included. Most studies had interpreted non-significant p-values or CIs to mean no association or effect in their main text. Also, the investigators found that non-significant p-values and CIs were less frequently reported in abstracts compared to the main text. These findings varied across the journals; however, the research team found less variation across print publication years.

Despite the American Statistical Association’s call for reporting CIs in research in 2016 [9], the authors found that seven years later, the authors of studies published in the five DPH journals still reported p-values considerably more than confidence intervals. Moreover, comparing 2018, 2019, and 2023, there was no evidence that trends in reporting CIs have improved since the publication of the American Statistical Association’s paper (except for CI reporting in the abstract). The comparisons between publication years were driven in part by differing compositions of journals and whether they were more DPH-focused in those years. The relative lack of improvement in statistical reporting quality may have contributed to the recent development of checklists aimed at enhancing statistical reporting standards, particularly in the oral health literature [20, 21].

The investigators noticed differences in the reporting of p-values and CIs as well as their dichotomization across the five DPH journals. One potential explanation for this finding could be that some journals have policies on reporting p-values, reporting of CIs, and/or their interpretations (see Appendix Table 5). Moreover, editors and reviewers of some journals could have played a role in increasing the reporting of CIs as well as increasing/decreasing the use of p-values and CIs as significance tests. One further explanation is that the authors published in these journals are clinicians who are not formally trained in public dental health and preferred to publish in DPH journals for their relevance to the general dental community rather than specific clinical journals (see comparisons between more and less DPH-focused journals in Appendix Table 6). While reporting p-values is not inherently problematic, they are frequently misinterpreted. However, reporting CI is recommended since they convey more information on the magnitude and precision of the measure of association (i.e., not just the uncertainty as in p-values) [22]. It is also of great importance to distinguish clinical from statistical significance, especially when it comes to biomedical research findings. Not every “statistically significant” effect would translate to something meaningful. For example, the difference between treatment A and treatment B might appear significant statistically, because of a large sample size, but the translation to clinical superiority might be incorrect.

Study investigators found that a considerable number of studies published in DPH journals had interpreted non-significant p-values to mean no association, thereby committing the fallacy of assuming that absence of evidence equals evidence of absence [1, 2, 22, 23]. A related interesting finding was that non-significant p-values and CIs were less frequently reported in abstracts compared to the main text, suggesting that authors tend to highlight associations with significant p-values and CIs, potentially due to limits on word count. Additionally, this could be explained by the authors’ focus on the main study associations or more important associations, which happen to be generally significant. Another explanation is that this could stem from the authors’ desire to overcome publication bias against non-significant results, a phenomenon that highlights statistically significant findings, which has been called out and discussed in publication practices [17]. Alternatively, the associations under study could have been clinically significant; thus, they were highlighted in the abstracts. On the other hand, the possibility exists that authors indeed believe that non-significant p-values and CIs mean no associations and consequently will only report statistically significant results. Ultimately, this selective reporting leads to potentially wrong conclusions in evidence synthesis due to incomplete accounting for all information on a given topic [18].

Although the results showed that CIs were reported in the majority of studies, the authors tended to often interpret them as surrogate significance tests. As previously indicated by a publication [13], using confidence intervals as surrogate significance tests underutilizes the information provided by confidence intervals on top of committing the dichotomization fallacy.

Recent developments in reporting p-values and CIs have emerged. These developments include using checklists to assess statistical output in medical papers [24], recommendations to accurately report statistical output in medical research [20, 21, 25], interpreting p-values with more categories instead of just two categories (a middle ground solution that aligns with interpreting p-values as continuous measures) [19], pushing back against reviewers and editors about significance testing [26], and using compatibility and surprise instead of confidence and significance [27–29]. Hopefully, these new reporting practices will improve the reporting of statistical output in the oral and broader health literature. Proper interpretation of p-values and CIs for a hypothetical research scenario can be found in the appendix as a guide (Appendix Table 9).

This study had a few limitations that could affect its results. First, despite multiple rounds of training, some differences were still observed between reviewers. To overcome this challenge, we provided conservative estimates for reporting p-value, CI, and their interpretations and all analyses adopted the interpretation of the most conservative reviewer. It should be noted that the differences between reviewers were unlikely to explain the differences observed between the years (2018, 2019, 2023; Table 3), journals (BMC Oral Health vs. CDOE; Table 4), or journal/year combination (more DPH vs. less DPH focused journals in all years; Appendix Table 6). Because journals and years were distributed equally among reviewers, any potential issues related to reviewer heterogeneity would apply equally across journals and years. Second, the authors considered any p-value inequality problematic regardless of the cut-off point, which could be justified if the p-value was very low (e.g., < 0.0001) or in genome-wide association studies. This could lead to an overestimation of the frequency of equating absence of evidence with evidence of absence in this study. However, the dichotomization of very low p-values occurred in very few instances in the studies the authors reviewed and may not change the conclusion substantially. Still, this study represents novel efforts in screening and quantifying misinterpretations in DPH literature- an endeavor that, hopefully, will stimulate self-reflection and incite changes in the relevant literature and/or reporting practices.

The results of this paper showed that some common misinterpretations about p-values and confidence intervals still linger in the dental public health literature, even seven years after warnings about them were voiced. More advanced training in research methods and/or reporting practices, similar to the new practices mentioned above, may be needed to overcome the issues with p-values, confidence interval reporting, and interpretation. Study investigators hope that these issues change soon.
