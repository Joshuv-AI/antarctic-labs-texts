Cone Reconstruction for Ebstein Anomaly: Effect on Tricuspid Valve Growth and Biventricular Remodelling in Children
Authors: Jae Hong Lee, Woong-Han Kim, Seung Min Baek, Hye Won Kwon, Sungkyu Cho, Jae Gun Kwak, Yoon Seong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the surgical outcomes of cone reconstruction (CR) in paediatric patients with Ebstein anomaly (EA).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed seventeen paediatric patients (<18 years) with EA (Carpentier types A, B, and C in 3, 12, and 2, respectively) who underwent CR without prosthetic annuloplasty materials between January 2014 and December 2024. Clinical outcomes, including tricuspid valve (TV) and biventricular function and size, were evaluated using echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR).ResultsThe median age and body weight at surgery were 7.8 years and 21.2 kg, respectively. No operative mortality occurred; however, 3 patients required permanent pacemaker implantation before discharge. No late deaths, reoperations, or significant tricuspid regurgitation (TR, ≥moderate) or stenosis (mean pressure gradient ≥5 mmHg) occurred during the 8.2-year median follow-up. TR was significantly reduced postoperatively (odds ratio 1.34, 95% CI, 1.10-1.64, P = .008) and remained stable without recurrence of significant TR during follow-up. The mean TV annulus z-score decreased significantly after CR (1.81 [1.19] preoperatively versus −1.74 [0.92] at discharge, mean difference: −3.55, 95% CI, −4.30 to −2.80, P < .001) and remained within the normal range during follow-up (−1.74 [0.92] at discharge versus −1.61 [1.11] at the last follow-up, mean difference: 0.04, 95% CI, −0.02-0.10, P = .207). Postoperative CMR demonstrated significantly reduced TR fraction and right heart volumetric parameters, except functional right ventricular end-systolic volume and pulmonary forward flow volume indices. Based on CMR, left ventricular end-diastolic (change 2.40, 95% CI, 0.52-4.27, P = .014) and systolic (change 1.75, 95% CI, 0.74-2.76, P = .002) volume indices significantly increased over time.ConclusionsLong-term outcomes after CR without prosthetic annuloplasty materials were favourable in paediatric patients with EA. CR can achieve durable reduction of TR with sustained TV growth, potentially facilitating biventricular reverse remodelling.Keywords: Ebstein anomaly, tricuspid valve, tricuspid valve regurgitation, tricuspid valve stenosis, tricuspid valve repair

INTRODUCTION

Ebstein anomaly (EA) is a rare congenital heart disorder with a broad spectrum of anatomical and clinical manifestations, characterized by the malformation of the tricuspid valve (TV) and right ventricle (RV).1 These include failed delamination of the TV leaflets, apical displacement of the septal and posterior TV leaflets, and atrialized RV. Although the principal pathological processes in EA primarily involve the RV, left ventricular (LV) abnormalities, including paradoxical septal motion, dyssynchrony, impaired filling, and dysfunction, have also been reported.2,3 Since its introduction by da Silva, cone reconstruction (CR) has become the preferred surgical approach for EA, demonstrating durable TV competence with significant reduction in tricuspid regurgitation (TR) and low rates of recurrent TR and reoperation.4–13

Therefore, the application of CR has progressively expanded to paediatric populations, including neonates, along with the broadening of surgical indications.6–13 Several studies have reported encouraging outcomes in children, demonstrating the feasibility and safety of the procedure.6–13 Nevertheless, major concerns remain regarding the long-term durability of the reconstructed TV, particularly relative to somatic growth in the paediatric population. Specifically, issues regarding adequate TV annular growth, the risk of tricuspid stenosis (TS), and the impact on biventricular function and remodelling warrant further investigation.

Several recent studies have expanded the focus on CR effects beyond TV- and RV-specific outcomes to include biventricular remodelling.5,7,10,12–15 These investigations suggested that the procedure promotes favourable ventricular reverse remodelling, potentially supporting sustained biventricular function. To more accurately and objectively evaluate these effects, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) has been used alongside echocardiography, as it provides a more comprehensive assessment with multiple parameters. Nevertheless, studies confined to paediatric patients remain limited.

Accordingly, we aimed to comprehensively evaluate the outcomes of CR in paediatric patients with EA. The study focused on longitudinal trends in TV function and annular growth assessed by echocardiography, as well as biventricular size and function, primarily evaluated with CMR.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Ethics statement

The study was reviewed and approved by the Seoul National University Hospital Institutional Review Board (approval number: H-2509-120-1677; approval date: September 23, 2025). The study involved the analysis of stored clinical and imaging data obtained during routine clinical care. In accordance with the approved ethics protocol, anonymized clinical data were handled in compliance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Taipei. As the board considered this to be a minimal-risk retrospective study, the requirement for individual consent was waived.

Patients

Data from consecutive patients who underwent definitive surgical treatment for EA at our institution between January 2014 and December 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. Inclusion criteria consisted of paediatric patients (aged <18 years) undergoing CR. Exclusion criteria included: (1) surgical procedures other than CR, including Starnes procedure, pulsatile bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (BCPS), or TV repair excluding CR; or (2) adult patients (aged ≥18 years). No neonatal patients underwent CR during the study period.

The morphological severity of EA was categorized according to the Carpentier classification, which was determined based on preoperative transthoracic echocardiography.

All patients underwent preoperative and postoperative transthoracic echocardiography. For volumetric analysis, CMR was performed preoperatively and postoperatively when feasible. In patients who did not undergo postoperative CMR owing to an implanted permanent pacemaker (PPM), cardiac computed tomography (CT) scanning was performed.

The surgical indications for EA included significant cyanosis or symptoms and signs suggesting congestive heart failure, such as dyspnoea, exercise intolerance, or progressive cardiomegaly. The presence of progressive RV enlargement, RV dysfunction, and clinically significant tachyarrhythmia further supported the surgical decision and its timing, even in mildly symptomatic patients. In addition to TR severity, CMR-derived RV volumetric and functional parameters were also considered. All cases were discussed in a multidisciplinary team meeting for surgical considerations and the optimal timing of surgery. In our institution, we prioritized timely surgical intervention during the early phase of disease progression to optimize haemodynamics and preserve RV function, rather than delaying surgery until advanced RV dysfunction had developed.

Surgical procedure

CR was performed in all 17 patients by a single surgeon with a consistent and standardized surgical technique and an identical operative strategy.4,16,17 Extensive delamination of the anterior, inferior, and septal leaflets was performed to enhance leaflet mobility and enable circumferential reattachment. The mobilized leaflets were rotated clockwise and reapproximated to form a cone-shaped valve, which was secured to the true tricuspid annulus. Atrialized RV plication was routinely performed to exclude non-contractile segments and reduce annular dimensions, with additional annular plication performed when necessary to optimize leaflet coaptation and restore valve geometry and competence.

Following atrialized RV plication and leaflet reattachment, valve competence was assessed by direct cold saline injection into the RV. When suboptimal leaflet coaptation, significant (≥moderate) residual TR, or newly developed TS was identified on intraoperative saline injection test or transoesophageal echocardiography, adjunctive procedures—including leaflet augmentation, coaptation sutures, artificial chordae implantation, and papillary muscle reimplantation or splitting—were selectively employed to optimize TV competence while preventing or alleviating significant TS. TV annuloplasty using prosthetic rings or bands, as well as native pericardial strips, was avoided in all patients owing to concerns regarding somatic growth and the potential long-term risk of TS in the paediatric population. Instead, a Kay annuloplasty using pledgeted sutures was employed to maximize the potential for proportional TV annular growth relative to the patient’s somatic growth.

For patients with subnormal RV or TV size, or with RV dysfunction, either BCPS or preservation of an atrial-level shunt could be considered. In our institution, we generally prefer atrial-level shunt preservation over BCPS. Atrial-level management can be determined according to the characteristics of the existing atrial septal defects, including partial closure, closure with a fenestrated patch, or preservation of a patent foramen ovale. These decisions were established through a comprehensive preoperative evaluation incorporating echocardiography as well as CMR, with particular emphasis on RV volumetric and functional parameters derived from CMR. The final decision regarding atrial-level management was re-evaluated intraoperatively based on trans-oesophageal echocardiographic findings in conjunction with haemodynamic stability during and after weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass. Additionally, reduction right atrioplasty was performed in selected cases at the surgeon’s discretion.

Echocardiographic evaluation

All preoperative and postoperative echocardiographic data were obtained and subsequently reviewed by a single cardiologist, who validated the final findings. The annulus size and function of the TV were assessed using transthoracic echocardiography, with z-scores calculated based on the body surface area.18 Perioperative and follow-up degrees of valvular regurgitation were classified as follows: 0, none-to-trivial; 1, mild; 2, mild-to-moderate; 3, moderate; and 4, severe.19 In addition, significant TS was defined as haemodynamically significant TS with a mean pressure gradient of ≥5 mmHg.20

CMR and CT assessment

While CMR is the preferred gold standard for quantification of the ventricular volumes, especially the RV, cardiac CT was postoperatively utilized as an alternative in patients where CMR was not feasible.

All CMR scans were performed using a 1.5-T unit (Avanto; Siemens). Our CMR protocol included thoracic survey images captured from axial, coronal, and sagittal angles; cine sequences utilizing steady-state free precession in 2- and 4-chamber views, right ventricular outflow tract view, and short-axis stacks spanning from the base to the apex. The cine sequence parameters were as follows: 6-mm slice thickness; 300 × 300 mm field of view, 240 × 216 matrix; and 45.63 ms temporal resolution. The analysis was conducted using the Argus post-processing tool (Siemens Medical Solutions). RV and LV endocardial contours were obtained from the short-axis view in a semi-automated manner, excluding papillary muscles and trabeculae from the cavity. The RV and LV systolic and diastolic volumes were determined using Simpson’s rule.

Cardiac ventricular volume was assessed using CT imaging in 2 patients who were unable to undergo CMR. Radiation modulation was disabled during the scan to ensure consistent imaging quality. The imaging data were reconstructed at 10% intervals of the cardiac cycle, ranging from 00% to 90%. The images representing the smallest ventricular volume during systole and the largest volume during diastole were selected for further analysis. For volume quantification, the endocardial contour of the ventricles was manually traced using the Rapidia program (version 2.8, Infinitt Healthcare). The ventricular volume data were subsequently obtained based on these manual tracings.

Follow-up and evaluation of clinical outcomes

Postoperative clinical and echocardiographic follow-ups were conducted at 3- to 6-month intervals for the first 2 years and annually thereafter in all patients. Data on survival status and the presence of cardiovascular events were collected from electronic medical records. Information on reoperation and the occurrence of major adverse events was collected. The clinical follow-up examinations were closed on April 30, 2025. Operative mortality was defined as death within 30 days postoperatively or during the same hospitalization. Late mortality was defined as death following discharge. Native TV failure was defined as significant (≥moderate) TR and/or TS (mean pressure gradient ≥5 mmHg).

Statistical analysis

The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation [SD]) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) according to their distribution, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies (%). Follow-up duration was calculated from the date of surgery to that of the last follow-up examination before April 30, 2025. Annual changes (changes per year) from discharge to the last follow-up were evaluated by including follow-up duration as a fixed effect and patient as a random effect, using linear mixed-effects models for continuous variables and generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with a cumulative logit link (cumulative logit GLMM) for TR grades. The New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class was evaluated using a cumulative logit GLMM, with time (preoperative versus final postoperative follow-up) included as a fixed effect, to assess the net clinical impact of the surgical intervention by comparing preoperative and final postoperative NYHA functional class. In 2 patients who were unable to undergo CMR, CT-derived volumetric data were used. CMR/CT-based right heart-related parameters (including TR and RV volume indices) were evaluated by preoperative and postoperative differences to assess surgical impact, whereas CMR/CT-based left heart-related parameters were assessed via annual change to capture temporal trends following surgery. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 25.0; IBM Corp.) and SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute). All P-values of <.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

During the study period, 41 patients underwent definitive surgical treatment for EA at our institution. After excluding the patients who underwent other surgical procedures (n = 7; TV repair excluding CR [n = 5], Starnes procedure [n = 1], or pulsatile BCPS [n = 1]) and were adult patients (aged ≥18 years, n = 17), 17 patients were finally enrolled in the present study (Figure 1). Preoperative CMR was performed in 15 of the 17 patients. Postoperative CMR or CT was completed in 16 patients. CMR was performed in 14 patients; among the 3 patients without postoperative CMR due to PPM implantation, 2 underwent volumetric assessment using CT scanning. The median clinical and echocardiographic durations were 8.2 (5.6-10.2) and 6.7 (4.1-9.7) years, respectively.

Baseline characteristics and perioperative data

The median age and body weight at surgery were 7.8 (3.7-11.7) years and 21.2 (14.0-35.3) kg, respectively. According to Carpentier’s classification, type B (n = 12) was the most common, followed by types A (n = 3) and C (n = 2). The most commonly associated anomaly was atrial septal defect (n = 12), followed by ventricular septal defect (n = 2). Three patients had a history of cardiac surgery: right modified Blalock-Taussig shunt (n = 1, 1.5 years prior), a BCPS following right modified Blalock-Taussig shunt (n = 1, 7 years prior), and TV repair using the Carpentier technique (n = 1, 7 years prior). Except for the patient who underwent TV repair using the Carpentier technique, the other procedures were performed at outside institutions, considering single-ventricle palliation. Preoperative arrhythmias were identified in 4 patients, including sinus node dysfunction (n = 1), second-degree atrioventricular block (n = 1), paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (n = 1), and atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome (n = 1). Patients with sinus node dysfunction and second-degree atrioventricular block underwent planned epicardial PPM implantation during surgery, whereas those with paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia and atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia underwent radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) prior to CR. Thirteen (76.5%) patients had significant (≥moderate) preoperative TR (severe in 9 and moderate in 4).

Additional TV repair techniques and other concomitant procedures were performed in 13 (76.5%) and 15 (88.2%) patients, respectively. The baseline characteristics and perioperative data are summarized in Table 1.

Early and late results

There was no operative mortality. Postoperative complications occurred in 7 patients. Postoperative arrhythmia developed in 4 patients during hospitalization; 1 required PPM implantation for complete atrioventricular block, 1 underwent RFCA for sustained ventricular tachycardia, whereas the remaining 2 were treated with antiarrhythmic medication, restored to sinus rhythm, and maintained in sinus rhythm until discharge. One patient required delayed sternal closure for low cardiac output syndrome. Intracranial haemorrhage and vocal fold palsy occurred in 1 patient each.

Three patients underwent epicardial PPM implantation before discharge. Of these, 2 received planned PPM implantation during surgery for preoperative sinus node dysfunction and second-degree atrioventricular block, whereas 1 required PPM implantation for complete atrioventricular block that developed postoperatively.

During follow-up, there was no late mortality, reoperation, or native TV failure. Late arrhythmia occurred in 1 patient 6.8 years postoperatively. This patient had experienced a transient postoperative arrhythmia that was controlled medically and restored to sinus rhythm before discharge. Atrial tachycardia at follow-up was treated with a beta-blocker and restored to sinus rhythm. The postoperative NYHA functional class improved compared with the preoperative NYHA functional class (odds ratio 25.16, 95% CI, 1.30-487.68, P = .035) (Table S1). All patients achieved biventricular repair, except for 1 who had previously undergone BCPS at an outside institution. In this patient, a one-and-a-half ventricular repair strategy was adopted by maintaining the BCPS, given the restrictive functional RV volumetric parameters (RV end-diastolic volume index [RVEDVI] 22.2 mL/m2 and RV end-systolic volume index [RVESVI] 15.2 mL/m2) and relatively decreased RV function (RV ejection fraction 42.7%) on preoperative CMR. The decision was also made in consideration of the patient’s age (7.75 years) and the prolonged duration of the BCPS (7 years). The procedure included CR combined with primary closure of the atrial septal defect, with the potential for future biventricular conversion depending on subsequent RV growth and functional recovery.

Echocardiographic assessment of TV and LV functions and sizes

After CR, TR was significantly reduced and remained stable without recurrence of significant TR during follow-up (odds ratio 1.34, 95% CI, 1.10-1.64, P = .008) (Figure 2A and Table S2). At discharge, significant TR (moderate) and TS (mean gradient 5.3 mmHg) were identified in 1 patient each; however, given the intra- and post-operative echocardiographic findings, as well as stable postoperative clinical status, both were managed with close surveillance and improved at the last follow-up. At the last follow-up, no patient had significant TR or TS.

Quantitative assessment of bi-ventricular size and function using CMR

CMR was performed in 15 patients preoperatively and in 14 postoperatively. The 2 patients who did not undergo preoperative CMR underwent postoperative CMR. Among the 3 patients who did not undergo postoperative CMR owing to an implanted PPM, 2 underwent cardiac CT scanning for volumetric analysis. The median age at postoperative CMR or CT and follow-up period between CR and postoperative CMR or CT were 9.11 (5.20-18.11) years and 15.1 (0.42-71.0) months, respectively.

The comparative analysis between preoperative and postoperative CMR data demonstrated significant reductions in TR fraction (mean difference: −31.16, 95% CI, −46.91 to −15.41, P = .005), total RVEDVI (mean difference: −90.66, 95% CI, −122.49 to −58.83, P < .001) and RVESVI (mean difference: −55.21, 95% CI, −81.09 to −29.34, P = .001), functional RVEDVI (mean difference: −33.45, 95% CI, −65.43 to −1.47, P = .042), RV stroke volume index (RVSVI) (mean difference: −33.25, 95% CI, −49.89 to −16.60, P = .001), and RV cardiac index (mean difference: −3.39, 95% CI, −5.05 to −1.73, P = .001) after CR. In addition, there were significant changes in LV end-diastolic volume index (LVEDVI) (mean annual change: 2.40, 95% confidence interval, 0.52 to 4.27, P = .014), LV end-systolic volume index (LVESVI) (mean annual change: 1.75, 95% CI, 0.74 to 2.76, P = .002), and LV ejection fraction (mean annual change: −1.29, 95% CI, −2.44 to −0.14, P = .029) over time. In contrast, other CMR-derived volumetric and functional parameters of the right and left heart, including aortic and pulmonary forward flow volume indices, showed no significant changes. DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrated three key findings. First, CR showed favourable clinical outcomes in paediatric patients. Second, during long-term follow-up, TV competence was preserved, and the annular size remained within normal ranges relative to somatic growth. Third, the comparison of preoperative and postoperative data revealed significant reductions in TR fraction and RV volumetric indices (RVEDVI, RVESVI, RVSVI, and RV cardiac index) postoperatively, while RV ejection fraction was preserved.

CR has demonstrated favourable outcomes across all age groups except neonates, with low rates of mortality, reoperation, and TR recurrence.4–7,10–13 Building on these encouraging results, research on CR has expanded beyond clinical outcomes, focusing on late biventricular remodelling and exercise capacity.5,7,10,12–14 Moreover, CR has been increasingly performed in paediatric patients, reflecting the trend towards earlier surgical intervention for EA.5,6,8–13 Nevertheless, studies focusing exclusively on paediatric patients remain limited. Given the impact of somatic growth, major concerns regarding the long-term durability of CR in the paediatric population have yet to be fully elucidated. In this study, in addition to clinical outcomes such as mortality and reoperation, TV function and annular growth were assessed by echocardiography, whereas ventricular size and function were quantitatively evaluated primarily using CMR. When CMR was not feasible, ventricular size was assessed by cardiac CT scanning.

Surgical outcomes of CR in paediatric patients

In previous CR series, overall mortality remained generally low at below 5.0%, whereas reoperation rates and residual or recurrent significant TR rates ranged from approximately 5.0% to 13.5% and 5.0% to 11.0%, respectively.4–7,10-13 Previous series from other centres have generally demonstrated low mortality and acceptable TV durability after CR, but early reoperation, recurrent TR, PPM implantation, and arrhythmia-related interventions have still been reported.

Compared with previous reports, our surgical outcomes after CR were favourable, without operative mortality, late mortality, or native TV failure.4–7,10–13 After excluding 2 patients who had undergone preoperative RFCA, 2 patients required surgical (n = 1) or catheter-based (n = 1) interventions for arrhythmia during hospitalization or follow-up—PPM implantation for postoperative complete atrioventricular block and RFCA for sustained ventricular tachycardia.

Notably, the postoperative NYHA functional class improved significantly compared with the preoperative state. This functional improvement may be attributed to qualitative restoration of cardiac haemodynamics and systemic oxygen delivery. Considering that most of our paediatric patients presented with significant TR and symptoms of heart failure preoperatively, the durable reduction of TR likely relieved chronic right-sided volume overload and systemic venous congestion, leading to symptomatic improvement. Furthermore, closure of interatrial shunts may have contributed to functional improvement by relieving right-sided volume overload. In selected patients with preoperative bidirectional shunting, elimination of right-to-left interatrial shunting may also have improved effective systemic oxygen delivery.

TV function and growth

Following CR, TR was markedly reduced, and TV competence was ultimately maintained without native TV failure in all patients at the last follow-up. The TV annular size, assessed by z-scores, predominantly remained within normal ranges during follow-up. Given ongoing concerns regarding annular growth and potential TS, our strategy deliberately avoided the use of prosthetic annuloplasty materials and relied exclusively on native tissue, with various adjunctive TV repair techniques employed as required. This approach was intended to preserve TV annular growth potential and mitigate the risk of TS. Consistent with this rationale, the annulus size increased in proportion to somatic growth, and no patient developed significant TS. These findings support the reliable durability of CR without the use of prosthetic annuloplasty materials (rings or bands) in paediatric patients. This approach can achieve sustained TV competence with annular growth in proportion to somatic growth, provided that valve integrity is preserved.

Trends in bi-ventricular size and function suggesting potential bi-ventricular remodelling

Previous studies have suggested that CR restores TV competence and reduces the atrialized RV, thereby unloading the RV and decreasing RV volumetric indices.5,10,12–15 As regurgitant burden decreases and ventricular geometry improves, LV filling may be enhanced, as reflected by increases in parameters including LVEDVI and LV stroke volume index (LVSVI).7,12–15 These changes support the concept that CR may promote biventricular reverse remodelling.

Accordingly, we hypothesized that sustained TV competence after CR would facilitate long-term normalization of biventricular size and function. In our study, TV competence was well preserved during follow-up. Consistent with this, CMR/CT-based right heart-related volumetric and functional parameters that were elevated preoperatively—including TR fraction, RVEDVI, RVESVI, RVSVI, and RV cardiac index—largely reflecting intracardiac shunt-related volume overload, RV dysfunction, and significant TR, showed significant postoperative reductions and a trend towards normal ranges.21 Furthermore, CMR/CT-based LVEDVI and LVESVI increased significantly over time. Although LVSVI and LV cardiac index did not change significantly, these results, together with echocardiographic findings observed during longer follow-up, may still be consistent with favourable LV reverse remodelling after CR.

Nevertheless, interpretation of these findings requires caution, particularly in paediatric patients. Although postoperative changes in ventricular volumetric indices, including the significant increases in LVEDVI and LVESVI over time, may intuitively suggest biventricular reverse remodelling after CR, such an interpretation may be limited by several important considerations. Some patients exhibited elevated preoperative LVEDVI, LVESVI, LVSVI, and LV cardiac index, reflecting preload overload and a hyperdynamic, high-output physiology driven by significant TR and intracardiac shunting. Therefore, despite the trend towards earlier surgical intervention in patients with EA, it should be considered that surgery in some paediatric patients may still have been undertaken at a relatively advanced stage of disease, accompanied by clinically significant RV dysfunction associated with progressive TR and intracardiac shunting. In addition, given that younger children, particularly neonates and infants, have inherently smaller normal ventricular volumes that can increase physiologically with somatic growth, and that our analysis did not include serial postoperative comparisons, our findings should be interpreted cautiously and approached differently from adult-based data, and should not be regarded as definitive evidence of LV reverse remodelling.21 In this context, LV reverse remodelling in paediatric patients might be more appropriately considered not solely on the basis of further increases in LV volumetric indices, but also as an adaptive remodelling process characterized by the overall normalization of left heart-related volumetric and functional parameters towards age-adjusted normal ranges (Figure S2).

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, this was a non-randomized, retrospective single-centre study, which may be subject to selection bias related to our institutional surgical strategy and indications favouring proactive intervention. Second, the small sample size, inherent to the rarity of the disease, may have increased variability and reduced the precision and robustness of the results. Third, neonates were not included in the study cohort, limiting the applicability of the findings to this high-risk subgroup in whom surgical outcomes may differ substantially. Fourth, although the procedure followed a standardized CR technique, all surgeries were performed by a single surgeon. Consequently, the findings may inherently reflect surgeon-specific expertise and practices, which could limit the reproducibility and generalizability. Fifth, CMR was not performed in all patients; in some cases, CT was substituted to measure ventricular volumes, which may have limited the comprehensiveness and consistency of ventricular assessment. Although we employed manual segmentation for CT-based volumetric analysis to minimize this discrepancy, the use of cardiac CT in a subset of patients may introduce bias, as CT testing has been reported to slightly overestimate ventricular volumes compared with CMR. Furthermore, the timing of postoperative CMR was not standardized. Some postoperative CMRs were performed relatively early, possibly before ventricular reverse remodelling had stabilized. In addition, interpretation may have been influenced by age-related changes in normal reference ranges, considering both the wide age distribution of the cohort at baseline and the somatic growth of individual patients during the follow-up period. Therefore, the generalization of our findings should be made with caution, and long-term studies with larger sample sizes and standardized longitudinal CMR follow-up protocols are warranted to validate the results and further delineate the trajectory of biventricular reverse remodelling after CR.

CONCLUSION

Long-term outcomes after CR without prosthetic annuloplasty materials were favourable in paediatric patients with EA. Our results suggest that CR can achieve durable reduction of TR while ensuring sustained TV annular growth and might facilitate biventricular reverse remodelling in the paediatric population.
