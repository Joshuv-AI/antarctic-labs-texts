Plasmalogens as biomarkers and therapeutic targets
Authors: Colleen S Curran, Alan T Remaley, Parizad Torabi-Parizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlasmalogens are structurally similar to phosphatidylcholine or phosphatidylethanolamine but differ at the Sn-1 position, containing a vinyl-ether instead of an ester bond. Reduced levels of plasmalogens in circulation or in cell membranes are associated with rare peroxisomal disorders, systemic disease, neurological impairment, cancer, and diseases of the heart, kidney, and liver. Roles for plasmalogens have been identified in lipid rafts, myelin, chlorolipids, bromolipids, hemostasis, cholesterol metabolism, and redox responses. The possible interconversion of plasmalogens into platelet-activating factor and the dysregulated activity of enzymes involved in the synthesis and catabolism of plasmalogens results in the reduced levels of these lipids during disease development. These enzymes also play dual roles in cell signaling, cellular respiratory homeostasis, innate immunity, inflammation, thrombosis, ferroptosis, autophagy, and neuron action potential. To further our understanding of plasmalogens as a biomarker and potential therapeutic, we have summarized clinical data on the role of these lipids in various diseases and the use of plasmalogens and their precursors in clinical trials. We also describe the complexities of plasmalogen synthesis and catabolism and detail aspects of these pathways in specific diseases that identify plasmalogens as a biomarker. Lastly, we summarize current and future research to better harness the effects of plasmalogens in the pathogenesis and treatment of systemic and/or organ-specific disease.Supplementary key words: biomarker, cholesterol, innate immunity, phospholipids, plasmalogens

A reduction of circulating plasmalogens is increasingly used clinically as a biomarker of disease. Altered plasmalogen metabolism is described in chronic disease (heart (1, 2), liver (3), kidney (4, 5, 6)), systemic disease (COVID-19 (7, 8, 9, 10), systemic lupus erythematosus (11, 12), sepsis (1, 2, 13), diabetes (14)), neurological disease (Alzheimer's (15, 16), Parkinson’s (17) schizophrenia (18, 19)), and cancer (breast (20), ovarian (21), pancreatic (22), and liver (23)). Plasmalogens account for approximately 5–20% of the phospholipids in mammalian cell membranes (24) and are purified from blood plasma, serum, plasma high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), erythrocytes, platelets, or peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (Table 1). Published mass spectrometry isolation protocols for plasmalogens vary (Table 1) and are challenged by mass overlaps between lipid species (25). In circulation or bound to cell membranes, the types of plasmalogens differ based on the cell type (26), tissue (27), and disease (Table 1). The ubiquitous biological presence of plasmalogens is further complicated by their diverse structures and the lack of commercially available standards to measure the specific classes of plasmalogens (28), which tend to differ by species (29).

Despite these complications, dietary supplementation with plasmalogens (30, 31) or plasmalogen precursors (32, 33, 34), is being investigated in the treatment of several conditions and diseases (Tables 1 and 2). In small human studies, plasmalogen dietary supplementation improved sleep disorders (35), memory in patients with mild Alzheimer’s disease (30), and clinical symptoms in patients with Parkinson’s disease (17). Administration of plasmalogen precursors (e.g., alkylglycerols) to patients increased circulating plasmalogen levels, resulting in reduced serum lipid peroxidation and improved cognition in one study (32) and reduced cholesterol, triglycerides, and C-reactive protein in another study (33).

Preclinical studies of plasmalogen dietary supplementation have also revealed reduced plasma cholesterol levels (31) and improved memory responses (36). Increased plasmalogens in blood or tissue in preclinical models occurs in response to oral administration of vinyl-ether synthetic plasmalogens (37) or intraperitoneal administration of the plasmalogen precursor myo-inositol alone (38) or combined with ethanolamine (39). Studies targeting enzymes in the production (40, 41), or catabolism (42, 43, 44, 45, 46) of plasmalogens are also being explored in regulating the pathogenesis of disease. These different studies, involving various types of lipids and distinct endpoints across many disease types, emphasize the need to more fully understand the functions of plasmalogens. In this review, we describe the role of plasmalogens in immune responses and the pathogenesis of disease.

Lipid biosynthesis

Lipids contribute to the structure of cell membranes, provide a primary form of energy storage, and act as precursors for intracellular cell signals (e.g., phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate) and extracellular signaling molecules (e.g., eicosanoids) (47). Glycerophospholipids are dominant in most cell membranes and are categorized based on their head groups, such as phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate, phosphatidylglycerol, and cardiolipin (48). Phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine are the most abundant phospholipids in mammalian cellular membranes and are formed through a multistep process (also known as the Kennedy pathway) in the endoplasmic reticulum (49, 50) (Fig. 1A).

Synthesis of lipids (A) Synthesis of phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylcholine. Ethanolamine or choline kinases and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) catalyze the phosphorylation of the molecules, resulting in the formation of adenosine diphosphate (ADP), phospho (P)-ethanolamine, or P-choline. CTP reacts with a CTP cytidyltransferase that catalyzes the formation of an inorganic pyrophosphate and CDP-ethanolamine or CDP-choline. A transferase promotes diacylglycerol replacement of CMP to form phosphatidylethanolamine or phosphatidylcholine, which generally contain a saturated fatty acid (e.g., palmitic acid) at the R1 position and an unsaturated fatty acid (e.g., oleic acid) at the R2 position. In the liver, phosphatidylcholine can also be produced by S-adenosyl-L-methionine (adoMet)-dependent methylation which is associated with adoMet conversion to S-adenosylhomocysteine (adoHcy). Reversible head groups in phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylserine allow interconversion of these lipids. Ethanolamine and choline also contribute to the formation of plasmalogens, which differ from glycerophospholipids as identified by an ether bond (red highlight). (B) Synthesis of plasmalogens. In the cytoplasm, triose phosphate isomerase catalyzes the reversible interconversion of dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP). In the peroxisome, glyceronephosphate O-acyltransferase (GNPAT) generates 1-acyldihydroxyacetone 3-phosphate by adding a fatty acyl group to DHAP. Alkyldihydroxyacetone phosphate synthase (AGPS) substitutes the acyl chain with a saturated fatty alcohol to form 1-alkyldihydroxyacetone 3-phosphate. In the endoplasmic reticulum, alkyldihydroxyacetone phosphate reductase forms 1-alkylglycerol 3-phosphate and alkylglycerol phosphate acyl transferase adds an unsaturated fatty acid to the R2 position. Like the formation of phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylcholine (A), head groups (ethanolamine or choline) are attached in a series of steps involving cytidine and transferases, resulting in the formation of plasmanyl lipids (orange highlight). A mixed function oxidase and desaturases (e.g., TMEM189) generate the vinyl-ether bond, which is a characteristic feature of plasmalogens (red highlight, also termed plasmenyl lipids). Circulating choline and ethanolamine are also incorporated into lipid membranes through the synthesis of plasmalogens. These lipids are formed through a separate multistep process involving precursors in the cytoplasm, peroxisome, and endoplasmic reticulum (51) and commencing with the formation of the glycolytic signaling molecule, dihydroxyacetone phosphate (52) (Fig. 1B). More recently, transmembrane protein of unknown function 189 (TMEM189) has been identified as the plasmanylethanolamine desaturase responsible for the formation of the vinyl-ether double bond in the ethanolamine class of plasmalogens (53, 54, 55). Because of the absence of plasmanylcholine desaturase activity, choline plasmalogens are indicated to be synthesized by utilizing the preformed vinyl-ether group in plasmenylethanolamine synthesis (56).

Defects in genes involved in plasmalogen formation are associated with the progression of the rare inherited peroxisomal disorder, rhizomelic chondrodysplasia punctata (RCDP). Glyceronephosphate O-acyltransferase (GNPAT) and alkyldihydroxyacetone phosphate synthase (AGPS) are enzymes involved in plasmalogen production. The peroxisomal targeting signal 2 receptor imports AGPS into the peroxisome and is transcribed by the peroxisomal biogenesis factor 7 (PEX7) gene (57). AGPS substitutes an acyl chain with a saturated fatty alcohol formed by fatty alcohol reductase 1 (FAR1) (58) in the formation of 1-alkyl-dihydroxyacetone phosphate (Figs. 1B and 2). Separately, the peroxisomal targeting signal 1 receptor, which is transcribed by PEX5, controls the antioxidant status of the cytosol by regulating the import of the antioxidant catalase into peroxisomes (59). Mutations in GNPAT (type 1, RCDP1), AGPS (type 2, RCDP2), PEX7 (type 3, RCDP3), FAR1 (type 4, RCDP4), and PEX5 (type 5, RCDP5) represent the forms of RCDP (60) (Fig. 2). Lower blood levels of plasmalogens in RCDP may be associated with reduced survival (61). This disease also presents with skeletal malformations, respiratory disorders, and severe developmental and neurological impairment (62, 63). Peroxisomal mutations in additional rare diseases that demonstrate reduced levels of plasmalogens in various cells and tissues include cerebral adrenoleukodystrophy (ABCD1 (64)), Zellweger syndrome (PEX1, PEX6, PEX10, PEX12, or PEX26 (65)), and infantile Refsum disease (PEX6 (66)). In contrast, in neonatal adrenoleukodystrophy, peroxisomal mutations (PEX1, PEX5, PEX13, and PEX26) result in increased plasmalogens in infant brain tissue (67), highlighting plasmalogen biosynthesis in the context of these rare diseases.

Plasmalogen catabolism

Oxidative stress (68, 69) induces calcium-independent phospholipase A2 (iPLA2) activity involved in the hydrolysis of ester linked fatty acids from the Sn-2 position of phospholipids and plasmalogens (69, 70) (Fig. 2). The downstream responses involve the release of fatty acids (e.g., arachidonic acid (71), eicosapentaenoic acid (72), docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) (73)) that can be further processed by cyclooxygenases or lipoxygenases to form lipid mediators involved in cell signaling, inflammation, hemostasis, vasoconstriction, and neuroprotection (74, 75). The loss of the fatty acid at the R2 position in plasmalogens additionally forms lysoplasmalogens (also known as lysoplasmenyls) (25, 69, 70). These lipids are thought to have similar functions to plasmalogens, but their ether bonds are targeted for degradation by distinct enzymes (42, 43, 44, 45) (Fig. 3A).

Plasmalogen catabolism. (A) Oxidative stress induces activation of calcium-independent phospholipase A2 (iPLA2) at the stereospecific numbered (Sn)-2 position, creating a hydrolysis reaction that forms lysoplasmalogens. This reaction also releases unsaturated fatty acids (R2) that may include arachidonic acid, eicosapentaenoic acid, or docosahexaenoic acid. Lipoxygenases (LOX) or cyclooxygenases (LOX) generate cleavage products involved in cell signaling, inflammation, hemostasis, vasoconstriction, and neuroprotection. Cytochrome c (Cyt-c) under oxidative stress and TMEMs cleave the vinyl-ether linkage in plasmalogens and lysoplasmalogens, respectively. Oxygenated cholesterol (oxysterols) activates liver X receptors to induce transcription of TMEM86A. (B) Plasmalogens are located throughout the mitochondria. Cardiolipin is localized to the mitochondrial inner membrane and requires the transacylase tafazzin to form its mature tetralinoleoyl form. Inherited tafazzin mutations occur in Barth syndrome, reducing the levels of tafazzin, tetralinoleoyl cardiolipin, and phosphatidylcholine plasmalogens and increasing levels of mono-lyso-cardolipin (MLCL) in heart tissue. The accumulating MLCL forms complexes with cyt-c, which acquires peroxidase activity to promote defective oxidative phosphorylation, oxidative stress, and dysregulated mitophagy and manifests into primarily dilated cardiomyopathy.

Cyotchrome c (cyt-c) typically serves as an electron carrier in the mitochondrial membrane, but under oxidative stress, cyt-c undergoes a conformational alteration conferring peroxidase activity that cleaves the vinyl-ether linkage in plasmalogens (42). Mutations in the transacylase tafazzin prevent maturation of the lipid cardiolipin, resulting in the accumulation of mono-lyso-cardiolipin that forms complexes with cyt-c and acquires peroxidase activity (76). Inherited tafazzin mutations occur in Barth syndrome, inhibit cardiolipin maturation, and reduce the levels of protective phosphatidylcholine plasmalogens in the heart, resulting in defective oxidative phosphorylation, oxidative stress, and dysregulated mitophagy and manifesting into primarily dilated cardiomyopathy (77, 78) (Fig. 3B). Currently, mitochondria-targeted antiperoxidase agents are being investigated to prevent cyt-c peroxidase activity in Barth syndrome (76), which suggests that these agents may have an effect in additional diseases demonstrating reduced plasmalogen levels (Table 1).

In lysoplasmalogens, the vinyl-ether linkage is cleaved by transmembrane protein of unknown function (TMEM)-86A (43, 44) or TMEM86B (44, 45, 46). In mouse adipocytes, TMEM86A is higher than TMEM86B and its expression is induced in mice fed a high-fat diet, resulting in lower levels of plasmalogens and lysoplasmalogens, particularly in white adipose tissue (79). Adipocyte tissue is a major reservoir for free cholesterol storage (80) and the surface localization of cholesterol in adipocytes of mice fed a high-fat diet increases during weight gain and promotes lipolysis that is abrogated in mice deficient in the major cholesterol efflux receptor (ATP binding cassette subfamily A member 1 [Abca1]) (81). These signaling networks may have a role in murine bone marrow–derived macrophages that demonstrate decreased cellular cholesterol-ester content and reduced levels of lysoplasmalogens in response to the activation of liver X receptors (LXRs), which are known to induce ABCA1 and TMEM86A expression (43). Thus, oxidated cholesterol (oxysterol), an LXR ligand, promotes the activity ABCA1 and TMEM86A to reduce cellular levels of cholesterol and lysoplasmalogens, respectively (Fig. 3A).With respect to TMEM86B, knockout mice had increased plasmalogens and lysoplasmalogens levels in the liver, plasma, and natural killer cells cells compared to wild-type controls (46), supporting TMEM86B tissue specificity and localized catabolism of plasmalogens in innate immune cells.

Plasmalogen catabolism in myeloid cells and platelets

Myeloid cells contain more than twice the level of plasmalogens compared to lymphoid cells (26) and are involved in the catabolism of plasmalogens through various signaling networks. As stated above, TMEM86A has been identified in macrophages (43), suggesting that additional immune subsets may also be modulated by this enzyme or TMEM86B (46). Plasmalogens accumulate in lipid rafts that are required for the activation of toll-like receptors (TLRs) by pathogen- or damage-associated molecular patterns (82, 83). Although ethanolamine plasmalogens are enriched in lipid rafts and tend to contain higher concentrations of arachidonic acid (82), TLR activation induces cytosolic group IVA phospholipase A2 and secreted group V phospholipase A(2) to release arachidonic acid from phospholipids in macrophages (84). Because reactive oxygen species (ROS) induce iPLA2-dependent release of arachidonic acid in macrophages (68) and TLR signals generate ROS (85), the release of fatty acids from plasmalogens at the Sn-2 position (Figs. 3 and 4A) may be delayed subsequent to TLR activation or require more potent oxidative damage responses to induce iPLA2-dependent arachidonic acid release. For example, thrombin activated platelets selectively hydrolyze choline and ethanolamine plasmalogens in an iPLA2γ-dependent manner, resulting in the formation of lysoplasmalogens and the release of arachidonic acid (86) (Fig. 4B).

Immune cell plasmalogen catabolism. (A) Plasmalogens accumulate in lipid rafts required for the activation of toll-like receptors (TLRs). Arachidonic acid (AA) can be released from phospholipids by TLR activation and from plasmalogens by oxidative stress via distinct phospholipases. (B) Activated neutrophils release myeloperoxidase (MPO) and MPO-derived hydrochloric acid catabolizes plasmalogens, resulting in 2-chlorofatty aldehyde (2-ClFALD), which can be oxidized to form 2-chlorofatty acids (2-ClFA). These chlorolipids damage the endothelium and promote the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) that bind thrombin associated with platelet activation. Thrombin hydrolyzes plasmalogens to form lysoplasmalogens and AA. (C) Neutrophils stimulated with ionophore produced platelet-activating factor (PAF) that stimulates various cells during disease pathogenesis through its receptor (PAFR). Plasmalogens may act as precursors to PAF and PAF can be enzymatically converted to plasmalogens that may also block PAF:PAFR activity. Lipoprotein-phospholipase A2 (Lp-PLA2) is the PAF acetylhydrolase that inactivates PAF and generates lyso-PAF. Darapladib is a Lp-PLA2 inhibitor.

Activated platelets also produce cytokines and chemokines to stimulate and recruit neutrophils (87). Activated neutrophils produce chlorolipids which are formed from plasmalogens (88). Specifically, neutrophil myeloperoxidase-derived hydrochloric acid targets the vinyl-ether bond of plasmalogens, resulting in 2-chlorofatty aldehyde, which can be readily oxidized to 2-chlorofatty acids (89, 90). These antimicrobial chlorolipids stimulate the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (88) that damage the endothelium and bind thrombin associated with platelet activation (91) in infectious, systemic, and chronic disease (92) (Fig. 4B). Eosinophil peroxidase shares roughly 70% sequence identity with the neutrophil myeloperoxidase and is able to convert plasmalogens into bromolipids that may exhibit similar functions as chlorolipids (93). Further study of chlorolipids and bromolipids are warranted to understand the functions of plasmalogens in host defense.

Moreover, neutrophils stimulated with an ionophore generate an ethanolamine plasmalogen analog of platelet-activating factor (PAF) (94). A subsequent study has identified the metabolic interconversion of these lipids (e.g., plasmalogens may act as precursors to PAF and PAF can be enzymatically converted to plasmalogens) (95) (Fig. 4C). PAF is a potent proinflammatory mediator in cancer, cardiovascular, neurological, chronic, and infectious disease (95), suggesting that increased PAF levels may inversely correspond to lower ethanolamine plasmalogen levels identified in human diseases (Table 1).

Plasmalogens in infectious disease

A reduction of plasmalogens in a wide variety of diseases appears to be a culmination of injury and inflammatory responses in targeted tissues. However, the isolation of plasmalogens from blood plasma, serum, plasma HDL-c, erythrocytes, platelets, or PBMCs in various diseases identifies a more systemic response (Table 1). Therefore, reduced levels of plasmalogens in disease may not only be due to biological synthesis and catabolism of these lipids but may also involve their altered trafficking in tissue, cells, and blood. In severe clinical infections of COVID-19 or sepsis, circulating levels of total cholesterol are reduced, particularly for HDL-c in COVID-19 (96) and LDL-c in sepsis (97). Plasmalogens are transported in these lipoprotein particles with higher content reported in HDL than LDL (98). Like plasmalogens, cholesterol localizes to lipid rafts for effective TLR activation (82). In macrophages, TLR activation promotes cholesterol storage (99) and inhibits cholesterol export by blocking LXR activity and downstream cholesterol efflux via ABCA1 (100, 101). LDL is also an identified scavenger of bacterial lipopolysaccharide and lipopolysaccharide-binding protein, which are then internalized via the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) (102). In SARS-CoV-2, LDLR is also implicated in spike protein internalization (103). A reduction of cholesterol and plasmalogens during infectious disease may therefore be in response to the rate of increased uptake of LDL particles and increased TLR activation that inhibits efflux receptors involved in the formation of HDL particles (Fig. 5).

Plasmalogens in infectious disease. Plasmalogens and cholesterol accumulate in lipid rafts required for the activation of TLRs. Plasmalogens also localize in low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-c) and high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-c) particles. Pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) molecules can be incorporated into LDL-c and internalized via the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), further encouraging the activation of TLRs. LDLR internalization of LDL-c and TLR activation promotes cholesterol storage. TLR signals also inhibit liver X receptor (LXR) activity known to promote cholesterol efflux via ATP binding cassette subfamily A member 1 (ABCA1) and lysoplasmalogen catabolism via TMEM86A. Cholesterol and phospholipids including plasmalogens are exported out of the cell via ABCA1 to initially interact with apolipoprotein A1 (Apo-A1) for the formation of a nascent HDL-c particle.

Additional mechanisms in infectious disease plasmalogen catabolism may include chlorolipids that have been identified in human sepsis (90) but have not been fully explored in COVID-19. Thrombin activation of platelets, possibly via the contribution of neutrophil neutrophil extracellular traps during COVID-19 (104) (Fig. 4B), may reduce levels of plasmalogens in platelets in clinical studies examining these cells (9, 10) (Table 1). cytochrome c (Cyt-c) is a factor in COVID-19 plasma (105) that is experimentally investigated in sepsis (106). Whether cyt-c in these infectious diseases exhibits peroxidase activity to cleave ether bonds requires further study. Lastly, PAF is upregulated in both sepsis (107) and COVID-19 (108), encouraging research into the clinical use of a PAF acetylhydrolase to degrade PAF into the inactive metabolite lyso-PAF in sepsis (109) or the possible use of plasmalogen analogs to compete with PAF for binding to PAF receptors and antagonizing PAF downstream prothrombotic, inflammatory signals (110).

Plasmalogens in systemic lupus erythematosus

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disorder driven by delayed macrophage clearance of apoptotic cells and the extracellular release of apoptotic intracellular components (e.g., dsDNA, nucleoproteins), which are targeted by the immune system and are bound by antibodies to form immune complexes (111). Previous study revealed that SLE patient serum contains dsDNA and dsDNA immune complexes that bind LDL (112, 113), suggesting that LDLR may internalize SLE autoantigens that activate endosomal TLR7 or TLR9 (114) and possibly inhibit ABCA1, cholesterol/plasmalogen efflux, and HDL formation like the infectious disease model (Fig. 5). Levels of HDL-c are reduced but levels of LDL-c and total cholesterol are elevated in patients with SLE (115). Whether LXR agonists employed in experimental models to increase ABCA1 and inhibit type I interferon production also affect plasmalogens requires further study (116).

Oxidative stress is a characteristic feature of SLE and an identifying factor in the degradation of plasmalogens in the disease (11, 12) (Table 1). The role of cyt-c in potentially degrading plasmalogens is complex given that the enzyme can also be an autoantigen in SLE (117). PAF appears not to be a significant factor in SLE unless there is kidney involvement (118).

Plasmalogens in diabetes and kidney disease

Kidney disease can be caused by primary kidney disease or indirectly by systemic diseases, such as SLE or type 2 diabetes mellitus and progress to renal failure and end-stage renal disease (119). Like SLE, plasmalogens are reduced in type 2 diabetes and kidney disease (Table 1) coupled with dyslipidemia involving lower HDL-c and higher levels of LDL-c (4, 6). However, plasmalogens were isolated from erythrocytes in these diseases instead of serum or PBMCs as in SLE (Table 1). Because erythrocytes are enucleated and organelle devoid (e.g., mitochondria, lysosome, endoplasmic reticulum, peroxisome) (120), reduced levels of plasmalogens may be related to decreased dietary consumption of the lipids (31), reduced HDL-c efflux (121), and/or increased oxidative damage (5). The increased production of PAF in kidney disease (122) may indicate a possible increase in the interconversion between PAF and plasmalogens. Elevated levels of PAF in type 1 but not type 2 diabetes (123) suggest that lower plasmalogens in type 2 diabetes (Table 1) may not be a function of plasmalogen interconversion to PAF.

Plasmalogens in cardiovascular disease

Decreased expression of ABCA1 (124), higher levels of LDL-c, and lower levels of HDL-c are biomarkers of coronary artery disease (CAD) (125) that may play role in lower levels of circulating plasmalogens identified (98) (Table 1). Although mitochondrial dysfunction is associated with CAD (126, 127), cyt-c and its ability to acquire peroxidase activity (Fig. 3B) currently do not have a clear role in altered plasmalogen metabolism in CAD. High neutrophil counts is an identified factor in cardiovascular disease (128) and in a model of myocardial infarction, chlorolipids were identified in myocardium (89), suggesting that heart tissue damage and reduced levels of plasmalogens in heart disease may be due to chlorolipids. Human studies have identified elevated levels of PAF in patients with CAD compared to control subjects (129), suggesting a role for plasmalogens in PAF interconversion or in PAFR antagonism. Interestingly, the PAF inhibitor, PAF acetylhydrolase (also known as lipoprotein-associated phospholipase [Lp-PLA2]), is upregulated in atherosclerotic plaques. Furthermore, in murine models of atherosclerosis, the administration of darapladib, a Lp-PLA2 phospholipase inhibitor, decreased plaque burden but did not affect the serum levels of PAF in mice (130, 131). To expand on this research, two large randomized, placebo-controlled trials employed darapladib in CAD (132, 133), revealing that darapladib did not reduce the risk of major coronary events. The lack of effect in these trials could be due to comorbidities in humans, differences between humans and mice, and/or the functions of plasmalogens in the formation and inhibition of PAF (Fig. 4C).

Plasmalogens in neurological disease

Plasmalogens are abundant in myelin and therefore crucial to the function of central nervous system oligodendrocytes and peripheral nervous system Schwann cells in supporting neuronal action potential (134) (Fig. 6). In murine neurons, plasmalogens maintain the homeostatic positioning of the axon initial segment in the generation of action potentials by reducing the activity of Unc-51–like autophagy activating kinase 1 (ULK1), a common marker of autophagy (135). In Gnpat knockout mice, oligodendrocytes (136) and Schwann cells (137) generate shorter myelin segments and exhibit a reduced capacity to myelinate. Similar findings were identified in Schwann cells from PEX7 knockout mice (137) (Fig. 2). Myelin also contains cholesterol (134), further associating the functions of plasmalogens with cholesterol metabolism. In models of schizophrenia (138), Alzheimer’s disease (139), and Parkinson’s disease (140), increased PAFR signaling is identified, potentially via microglia-induced inflammation and injury. Because plasmalogen levels are reduced in these diseases (Table 1), plasmalogens may be involved in PAF interconversion and/or the treatment of neurological disease.

Plasmalogens in the nervous system. Plasmalogens are required for oligodendrocyte and Schwann cell myelination, which supports neuronal action potential. Plasmalogens in circulation are reduced in schizophrenia, Alzheimer’s disease, and Parkinson’s disease. Platelet-activating factor (PAF) signals via the PAF receptor (PAFR) on microglial cells may promote inflammation and damage responses in neurological disease. Interconversion between plasmalogens and PAF may occur in neurological disease.

Plasmalogens in cancer

Lower circulating levels of plasmalogens have been identified in various types of cancer (Table 1). TMEM189, which is involved in the formation of the vinyl-ether bond (53, 54, 55) (Fig. 1B), is upregulated and identified as a poor prognostic factor in bladder, breast, lung, liver, and skin cancer (141). TMEMs are multifaceted integrated membrane proteins ubiquitously distributed in the lipid bilayer of cellular and organelle membranes, where they act as receptors, enzymes, or ion channels to activate and inhibit signals during disease progression (142). Depending on the cells and experimental techniques employed, TMEM189 may (143) or may not (144) play a role in ferroptosis, which is a form of cell death dependent upon ROS, iron accumulation, and lipid peroxidation. Ferroptosis, like autophagy, is associated with tumor progression and may be linked to autophagy via ferroptosis-induced activation of ULK1 (145). In human cell lines, TMEM189 is a negative regulator of autophagy (146), supporting studies that identify plasmalogens in the negative regulation of ULK1 in neurons (135). The role of circulating enzyme GNPAT (Figs. 1B and 2) in cancer patients requires further study in the context of plasmalogens (147). Lastly, PAF is produced by tumors and promotes the differentiation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (148), suggesting that plasmalogens may support the production of PAF via interconversion or provide a therapy to block PAFR signals (Fig. 4C).

Plasmalogens in nonalcoholic steatohepatitis

In mice with a hepatic-specific PEX5 deletion, plasmalogen levels were not altered in plasma or tissue (e.g., heart, liver, lung, brain) (149). However, TMEM86B knockout mice increased plasmalogen and lysoplasmalogen levels in the liver and plasma (46), highlighting enzymatic differences in regulating plasmalogen homeostasis in the liver. Moreover, research revealed that mice fed a high-fat high-cholesterol diet induced nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), decreased liver Gnpat mRNA expression, and reduced the levels of hepatic docosahexaenoic acid-containing plasmalogens and these conditions were abrogated with plasmalogen precursor (alkyl glycerol) supplementation (150). Another study identified that mice fed a high fat diet supplemented with plasmalogens decreased liver triglyceride and cholesterol levels and reduced ULK1-induced lipophagy compared to controls (151). Overall, these studies support clinical findings of lower plasmalogens in NASH patients (Table 1) and potential plasmalogen supplementation as a form of treatment for this disease. Lastly, because PAF is a contributor to the development of NASH (152), further study of plasmalogens in the interconversion to PAF or therapeutic antagonism of PAFR is warranted.

Conclusion

Mutations in plasmalogen synthesis enzymes (e.g., TMEM189, GNPAT, PEX7, FAR1, AGPS) can lead to the development of several diseases (Fig. 1, Fig. 2 and 2). Catabolism of plasmalogens occurs in response to oxidative stress (e.g., iPLA2, TMEM86A, TMEM86B, cyt-c) and the activation of TLRs, which promote pro-inflammatory responses during disease progression. This may include the production of chlorolipids, bromolipids, PAF, and thrombin, resulting in injury and prothrombotic responses. The release of fatty acids (e.g., arachidonic acid, eicosapentaenoic acid, docosahexaenoic acid) during plasmalogen catabolism can either exacerbate or resolve proinflammatory and thrombotic responses depending on the type of fatty acid released and mediator produced (Fig. 3). Each of these facets of plasmalogens are distinctly altered in cancer and chronic, systemic, infectious, and neurological disease to generally produce lower levels of plasmalogens in plasma, serum, HDL-c, erythrocytes, platelets, or PBMCs (Table 1). Levels of plasmalogens in cells and circulation are influenced by cholesterol metabolism. Cholesterol signals control plasmalogen catabolism via TMEM86A and the efflux of plasmalogens, which are concentrated in HDL. The colocalization of plasmalogens in lipid rafts and in myelin further highlights the integrated roles of these lipids in cell signaling and neuron action potential. These ubiquitous and targeted functions of plasmalogens in disease and immunity warrant further study.

Future direction

Plasmalogens differ based on the cell type (26) tissue (27), species (29), and disease (Table 1), creating a need for commercially available standards. Methods to isolate and quantify plasmalogens require standardization to not only evaluate the presence the vinyl-ether bond but the type of molecule bound at each position (Sn-1, Sn-2, Sn-3) (25). Continued evaluation of TMEM189 in the synthesis of plasmalogens, ferroptosis and autophagy may yield new insights into liver lipophagy, tumor progression, and neuronal function. Further exploration of oxidative stress–induced cyt-c peroxidase activity, thrombin activity, cholesterol metabolism, TMEM86A/B, and the formation of chlorolipids, bromolipids, and PAF with respect to plasmalogens is required to better understand plasmalogens as a biomarker of the immune response and the pathogenesis of disease. Finally, continued research of the types of plasmalogens and plasmalogen precursors and their natural or synthetic sources, the frequency and amount of plasmalogens administered, the route of administration, and the timing of treatment is needed before further investigating these lipids in the clinical setting as a therapeutic.
