Development of an in vitro method to assess the immunogenicity of biologics in the prevention of infectious diseases
Authors: Joanna Baran, Łukasz Kuryk, Mariangela Garofalo, Marta Prygiel, Aleksandra Zasada, Teresa Szczepińska, Anna Staniszewska, Deepak Kala, Piotr Majewski, Radosław Charkiewicz, Monika Staniszewska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present a series of preclinical studies focused on developing in vitro 2D and 3D models for assessing immunogenic factors in preventing infectious diseases. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) and Calu-3 cell lines (bronchial epithelial cells) were used to develop 2D and 3D models. Peptides: Spike-S1-His (S-His), nucleocapsid-His and adjuvants: human adenovirus five serotype-based viral vector (AdV-D24-ICOSL-CD40L), armed with inducible co-stimulator (ICOSL) and CD40 ligand (CD40L), and a vector lacking these transgenes (AdV5/3) were used due to their effective initial interaction with antigen-presenting cells (APC). Studying the potency of biologics in vitro revealed a significant increase in the percentage of CD4+ TCM, CD4+ TEMRA, and CD4+ TEM lymphocyte subpopulations involved in memory cell generation after 24 h of treatment. Prolonging the exposure for 7 days, a significant increase in CD4+ cells was observed when PBMCs were treated with AdV1 (56.00 ± 0.26% vs. 48.17 ± 1.10%). In contrast, a decrease in CD8+ cells was observed in those treated with AdV1 (37.93 ± 0.35%) compared to AdV1 + S-His + N-His (38.47 ± 0.38%) versus the untreated group (44.63 ± 1.07%). A decrease in EMRA was noted when PBMCs were treated with AdV1 + S-His + N-His (2.97 ± 0.23% vs. 4.50 ± 0.35%). Moreover, it was pointed out that PBMCs treated with AdV1 alone or in combination with S-His and N-His showed an elevated number of naïve CD4+/CD8+ and SCM CD4+/CD8+ cells. No changes in the number of EMRA CD4+ subpopulations were detected when PBMCs were treated with AdV2 compared with untreated ones (4.27 ± 0.06% vs. 4.50 ± 0.35%). Analysis of the humoral response induced by AdV1, AdV2, S-His, N-His, AdV1 + S-His + N-His, and AdV2 + S-His + N-His showed that AdV1 alone (4.17 ± 0.25% vs. 3.17 ± 0.06%) and in combination with S-His and N-His (3.87 ± 0.25 vs. 3.17 ± 0.06%) slightly increased the number of CD19+ cells. RNA-Seq analysis of PBMC cells in the 3D model revealed gene overexpression, including FGFR4, associated with the Rap1 pathway in samples exposed to AdV1 + S-His + N-His. Thus, the proposed platform's impact on lymphocyte differentiation was confirmed, and cytokine profile analysis in this sample revealed elevated levels of IL-10, IL-12p70, and IL-8. All samples exposed to AdV showed increased levels of IFN-γ. The safety and biodistribution studies of the vaccine platform demonstrated that a 30-day exposure did not impact mice's survival or organ morphology. Exploring the CD40 pathway notably reveals its significant impact on immune cell populations, suggesting potential therapeutic avenues.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12026-025-09681-y.

Introduction

Human-derived PBMC models in vitro and ex vivo offer a more relevant and customizable platform for evaluating vaccine candidates, providing ethical advantages over animal models [1, 2]. That is why it is crucial to establish reliable and optimized models for developing immunogenic factors. Practical therapeutic approaches are critical for mitigating the spread and consequences of viral infections [1, 2]. Generally, two routes of adenoviral vaccine administration are practiced—intramuscular and intranasal [3]. Six intramuscular vaccines currently have emergency use authorization (EUA), and five intranasal vaccines are under development. Between the EUA vaccines, two types of viral vectors dominated in the EU. Among them was the AstraZeneca vaccine, Covishield/Vaxzevria (no longer manufactured or supplied due to infrequent side effects, such as blood clots), which uses ChAdOx1 (chimpanzee adenovirus Oxford 1) as a vector. The Oxford-AstraZeneca COVID-19 vaccine (ChAdOx1) was engineered to carry the gene that codes the spike protein of the SARS-CoV-2 virus. Other adenoviral vaccines carry either rAdV5 (recombinant adenovirus serotype 5) or rAdV26 (recombinant adenovirus serotype 26) [3]. Our study focused on conditionally replicating adenoviruses (AdV-D24-ICOSL-CD40L, armed with inducible co-stimulator ICOSL and CD40 ligand CD40L, and a vector lacking these transgenes, AdV5/3), which served as an adjuvant due to its effective initial interaction with antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and surface antigens of COVID-19.

T lymphocytes are involved in cell-mediated immunity and play a pivotal role in the immune response to viral infections [2, 4, 5]. ICOSL is a member of the CD28/B7 family of co-stimulatory molecules, playing a crucial role in T cell activation. When naïve T cells encounter antigens presented by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), the interaction between the T cell receptor (TCR) and the antigen-major histocompatibility complex (MHC) complex is often insufficient for T cell activation. Co-stimulatory signals from ICOS/ICOSL are required for optimal T-cell responses [1, 2, 6, 7]. The ICOS/ICOSL pathway is also particularly involved in CD4+ T cell differentiation. ICOS stimulation promotes the generation of Th2 cells and Tfh cells.

The humoral response provided by B cells is crucial in the adaptive immune system. CD19+ B cell activation is regulated by a complex network of signaling molecules, cytokines, and co-stimulatory receptors [8]. A cytokine storm is a severe immune response when the body's immune system overreacts to an infection or other stimulus, releasing large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines into the bloodstream. Cytokines play a crucial role in the immune response, but when produced in excessive amounts, they can cause damage to tissues and organs throughout the body [9].

The primary objective of the study was to develop adequate in vitro non-clinical screening and confirmatory assays to evaluate the immunogenicity of the adjuvant-adenovirus (AdV1 with ICOSL and CD40L or AdV2 without ligands) and the antigens (recombinant spike-S1 and/or nucleocapsid N). We accomplished the main goal by (1) isolating peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy donors and using them in combination with lung epithelial cells Calu-3 to establish the 2D and 3D models; (2) assessing whether the immunogenic factors affected human cell viability and induced apoptosis; (3) testing several mechanisms by which adenoviruses (AdV1 and AdV2 without ICOSL and CD40L) act as adjuvants. We focused on innate and adaptive immune cells, which could help reveal cell-mediated responses. In detail, we used flow cytometry-based assays to assess the percentages of CD4+, CD8+, and CD19+ subpopulations, and (4) RNA sequencing of Calu-3 and PBMCs treated with the vaccine platform. The up- and down-regulations were observed in each condition, with the highest number of differentially expressed genes. We identified the signaling pathways that control immunological mechanisms by which the platform induces protective immunity; (5) CD4+ and CD8+ cell phenotyping and cytokine quantification were performed in response to infection with SARS-CoV-2 Delta (AY.4), SARS-CoV-2 Omicron (BA.1.1), SARS-CoV-2 alpha VOC (lineage B.1.1.7), Influenza Puerto Rico (H1N1 PR8), and Influenza Udorn (H3N2 UD72); (6) a 30-day biosafety analysis using BALB/c mice to determine the potential in vivo risk of the platform.

Materials and methods

Adherent cell cultures

VERO E6 ATCC cell line (Cercopithecus aethiops monkey kidney epithelium VERO C1008 [VERO 76, clone E6, VERO E6) was grown in Minimum Essential Medium MEM (1 ×) supplemented with 2 mM GlutaMAX TM (Gibco), 10% FBS (Gibco), 5 mM pyruvate (Gibco), and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). VERO cells were used in the Spearman-Kärber method and CD40 gene relative expression analysis. Human lung tumor H226 ATCC (mesothelioma epithelial cells NCI-H226 H226) and human lung tumor MSTO-211H (biphasic mesothelioma fibroblasts MSTO-211H) cell lines were used in the Spearman-Kärber method. H226 was grown in RPMI 1640 medium (1 ×) (Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco) and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). The Calu-3 ATCC cell line was used to create a 3D model of alveoli, which was cultured in Eagle’s MEM (1 ×) (ATCC), supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 5 mM pyruvate (Gibco), and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). These cell lines were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 upon reaching a semi-confluent state (80–90%). Cells were seeded at a defined concentration of 104 cells/100 µL into 96-well plates.

PBMC isolation, cryopreservation, thawing, and culturing

Blood samples from healthy donors underwent rigorous testing for HBsAg, anti-HCV, HCV RNA, anti-HIV-1/2, and HIV RNA, ensuring the highest safety and reliability standards in the Regional Centre for Blood Donation in Warsaw health protocols. The donors comprised a dynamic group of males aged 20 to 30, reflecting a vibrant and active population segment. The blood samples from groups O, A, B, and AB were included in our study. PBMCs were isolated from buffy coats of forty random donors using Ficoll-Paque Premium. After the last washing of the pooled PBMCs, the cell pellet was suspended in the freezing medium (20% FBS, 10% DMSO) and stored at − 150 °C. Before the tests, PBMCs were thawed immediately, and the cell growth medium (warmed to room temperature) was added dropwise until the duplicated volume was achieved. The cells were centrifuged at 150 × g for 5 min (Centrifuge 5910 R, Eppendorf). Cells (1.0 × 105/100 µL of medium) were seeded in 96-well and 24-well plates. Cells (2.5–3 × 106/mL of medium) were seeded in Corning Transwell inserts. PBMCs were cultured in Opti-MEM (Gibco) medium with 10% FBS (Gibco) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The overnight rest was assessed, and the culture time depended on the experiment’s length. Passages were not applied when PBMCs were mature and did not proliferate.

3D alveoli-like co-culture consisting of Calu-3 cells and PBMCs onto Corning® Transwell® Inserts

The Calu-3 cell line (1 × 105 cells/mL) was seeded onto Corning Transwell Inserts (700 µL of cell growth medium under the insert, 300 µL of cell suspension on the insert) and incubated for 72 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Then, the culture medium was discarded to create an air–liquid interface, allowing the Calu-3 cells to form microvilli, as monitored by SEM. The cells were cultured for 14 days, with the medium exchange every 72 h to stimulate cell growth. After 14 days, PBMCs in a total volume of 700 µL (2.5–3 × 106 cells/mL in Opti-MEM) were added to the liquid site of the culture. The model prepared in this way was immediately used for further experiments.

Ad5/3-D24-ICOS-CD40L (AdV1; stock concentration of 3.2 × 103 TCID50/mL) was obtained as described in [10]. AdV1 has two potent co-stimulatory molecules: the inducible co-stimulator ligand (ICOSL) and CD40 ligand (CD40L, also known as CD154), which can activate the immune system. The reference for AdV1 is AdV5/3-D24-E3 (AdV2; stock concentration of 7.7 × 1012 VP/mL), a 5/3 serotype but without CD40L and ICOSL [10]. Both AdVs were used as immune response inducers (adjuvants), alone or in combination with the commercial spike and/or nucleocapsid proteins. Spike-S1-His is a recombinant protein created by InvivoGen (cat. code his-sars2-s1). It contains the S1 subunit of the spike protein from SARS-CoV-2, which is responsible for binding to the ACE2 receptor of host cells. The protein is modified with a histidine (His) tag at its C-terminus (InvivoGen). Nucleocapsid-His is a recombinant protein generated by InvivoGen (cat. code his-sars2-n). It contains the nucleocapsid protein from SARS-CoV-2, an internal protein that plays an essential role in viral replication and packaging. The protein is modified with a histidine (His) tag at its C-terminus (InvivoGen). Both proteins were used as immune response inducers (antigens), alone and in combination with adenoviral vectors. In our studies, spike and nucleocapsid proteins of SARS-CoV-2 were used. ELISA validated the protein upon incubation with coated anti-SARS-CoV-Spike-RBD antibodies. The absence of bacterial LPS (endotoxins) was confirmed using HEK-Blue TLR2 and HEK-Blue TLR4 cellular assays (his-sars2-n, his-sars2-s1; InvivoGen, 2023).

SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus: Lentifect SARS-CoV-2 Spike is a type of pseudotyped lentivirus that utilizes the VS.V vector as its backbone (GeneCopoeia, USA). VS.V is a virus that carries a negatively charged RNA strand and enters cells with the help of the envelope glycoprotein G [11]. The VS.V-G envelope glycoprotein of VS.V was replaced with the S protein of the SARS-CoV-2 virus to create the pseudovirus. In the study, we used the following viruses: SARS-CoV-2 Delta (AY.4), SARS-CoV-2 Omicron (BA.1.1), SARS-CoV-2 Omicron (BA.1.1), SARS-CoV-2 alpha VOC (lineage B.1.1.7), Influenza Puerto Rico (H1N1 PR8), and Influenza Udorn (H3N2 UD72).

Cell metabolic activity assay

The cells, at a concentration of 104/100 μL (adherent cells) or 105/100 μL (PBMCs) of culture medium, were seeded in triplicate in 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Then, the cells were treated with the immunogenic factors for 18 h at 34.5 °C with 5% CO2. Untreated cells were used as controls. The metabolic activity of the cells was evaluated using the MTS kit (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)−2-(4-sulfophenyl)−2H-tetrazolium), according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega G3582, CellTiter 96 Aqueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay). Briefly, after incubating the cells with the immunogenic factors, 20 μl of MTS solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 490 nm using a microplate reader. A complete set of the treatments (immunogenic factor concentrations) used to study PBMC metabolic activity (three technical replicates in three biological replicates) is as follows: LPS (0.25 µg/mL), AdV1 (100 VP/mL), AdV2 (100 VP/mL), hCoV-OC43 (100 VP/mL), Spike-S1-His (0.25 µg/mL), Nucleocapsid-His (0.25 µg/mL) [12, 13].

7-day exposition of PBMCs to the immunogenic factors with increased AdV1 concentrations (7-day experiment using a 2D model)

PBMCs were seeded into 24-well plates in triplicate to study all immunogenic factors and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Then, the cell growth medium was replaced by 1 mL/well of the OptiMEM medium with reduced FBS concentration (5% v/v), and the immunogenic factors were added in the concentrations as follows: AdV1 (100 VP/cell, [10], Spike-S1-His (0.25 µg/mL, InvivoGen USA), Nucleocapsid-His (0.25 µg/mL, InvivoGen USA), AdV1 + Spike-S1-His + Nucleocapsid-His (100 VP/cell + 0.25 µg/mL + 0.25 µg/mL) [12, 13]. Plates were incubated for 7 days at 34.5 °C with 5% CO2, and then the cells were collected for the immunophenotyping and apoptosis assay [12, 14, 15]. The cell growth medium was collected and stored at − 80 °C for cryopreservation before cytokine profiling.

Immune response of PBMCs using the 3D co-culture model of human alveoli

The immunogenic factors were added to the cell growth medium, including PBMCs under the insert with the human lung epithelial cell line (Calu-3). The plates were then incubated to assess the effect of the plates on the immune response. Immunogenic factors representing the vaccination process were added to the cell growth medium (OptiMEM with 5% of FBS), including PBMCs under the insert. The incubation was performed for 72 h at 34.5 °C with 5% CO2 (Table 1). After 72 h of incubation, the PBMCs were rinsed and returned to the wells in fresh culture medium without immunogenic factors for the next 4 days at 34.5 °C with 5% CO2. The PBMCs were then collected to perform immunophenotyping and apoptosis assays. The cell growth medium was used to assess cytokine profiling, and untreated Calu-3 inserts were used in the SEM analysis as the control. Every individual treatment was repeated at least three times. The appropriate controls included those without immunological factors (vaccination) and untreated (control). The different models and concentrations used in the experiments are presented in Table 1. Immunophenotyping

This study evaluated the development and application of three antibody panels for the immunophenotyping of PBMCs. Cell staining was performed per the manufacturer’s protocol (BD Biosciences, USA). Briefly, 50 µL of the antibodies mixture was added to 50 µL of the PBMC suspension containing 106 cells. Three antibody panels were developed and described in Tables S2, S3, and S4. The suspension was then mixed, incubated for 30 min, and protected from light at room temperature. After the incubation, the cells were washed twice. Two milliliters of BD CellWash were added to each tube, and the cells were centrifuged at 150 × g for 5 min using a Centrifuge 5910 R (Eppendorf). Each cell pellet was suspended in 250 µL of BD CellWash, and flow cytometry analysis was performed. Analyses were performed using a BD FACSLyric ™ Flow Cytometry System. Before each series of studies, the Performance QC protocol was conducted as recommended by the supplier. Gating strategies and hierarchy are illustrated in Figures S1–S3.

Apoptosis assay

In the apoptosis assay, the following solutions were used (BD Biosciences, USA): 10 × Binding Buffer (cat. no. 556454): 0.1 M HEPES, pH 7.4; 1.4 M NaCl; 25 mM CaCl2; BD Cell WASH (cat. no. 349524); Propidium Iodide (PI, cat. no. 556463); Annexin V-FITC (cat. no. 556420, 556,419). The cell staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. PBMCs were collected, washed with cold BD Cell Wash, and suspended in 1 × Binding Buffer (BD Biosciences, USA) at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Then, 100 µL of the cell suspension (containing 105 cells/100 µL) was transferred to a 5-mL tube. PI (0.5 µL) and Annexin V-FITC (2.5 µL) were added to a suspension and incubated for 15 min (protected from light). Then, 400 µL of 1 × Binding Buffer was added. Analyses were performed using a BD FACSLyric™ Flow Cytometry System (BD Biosciences, USA). Before each series of analyses, the Quality Control protocol was performed as recommended by the supplier (BD Biosciences, USA). The gating strategy is shown in Figure S4.

RNA sequencing

In this study, RNA-seq was performed by CeGaT GmbH (Tübingen, Germany). To prepare the sequencing libraries, rRNA depletion was conducted. The sequencing process was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 machine, which generated 2 × 100 bp reads. In paired-end sequencing, read two corresponds to the sense strand. The sequencing reads were demultiplexed using Illumina’s bcl2fastq (version 2.20). The first three nucleotides of the second sequencing read were derived from the Pico v2 SMART adapter for samples prepared with the Takara kit. Skewer (version 0.2.2) [16] was used to trim adapters. The quality of the FASTQ files was analyzed using FastQC (version 0.11.5-legal).

Only reads longer than 35 nucleotides and properly paired were retained [17] (cutadapt 3.5). The reads were then aligned to the human GRCh. 38 genome with GENCODE version 43 primary annotation, using the RSEM-1.3.3 [18] pipeline coupled with STAR-2.7.10b [19] (65,462,834 ± 13,914,672 mapped read pairs, Table S6). Estimated read counts per gene were used for differential expression analysis using the DESeq2-1.34.0 R package [20]. However, sample number 8 from the PBMC AdV1 + S + N condition was identified as an outlier due to its clustering outside of all PBMC cell samples and having almost double the read count compared to other samples (FigureS6). The samples were clustered using the complete linkage hierarchical clustering method. Euclidean distance between samples was measured by R dist function on read counts transformed by DESeq vst function. All conditions were included in the DE-seq2 model, and three comparisons were made. Differentially expressed genes were analyzed separately for Gene Ontology enrichment using GProfiler [21] or the DAVID tool [22]. The Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted FDR threshold of 0.05 was used to identify differentially expressed genes in each comparison. Plots were created in R (version 4.0.4) [R Core Team, 2015] using the ggplot2 package [23]. Raw RNA-seq fastq files and gene count files are available from the GEO repository under accession number GSE294796.

In vivo analysis of adenovirus-based platform

The study employed a mouse model, BALB/c, to evaluate the therapeutic intervention’s potential adverse effects and safety profile. Three mice were included in each experimental group, and the health and well-being of the animals were monitored daily. The therapeutics were administered via subcutaneous injections with a standardized dose of 500 µl. The administration of therapeutics begins on Day 0 of the experiment. These injections were provided in a series of up to three doses, with intervals of 3–4 days between each injection. Precise administration dates were documented to monitor the timing of interventions. The experiment encompasses three cycles of therapeutic administration to assess the safety profile over an extended period. The weight of the mice was evaluated before each injection to track any changes that may indicate safety concerns. In Table S7, the details of the experiment are shown. The in vivo experiments, including breeding, platform administration, and euthanasia, were at the National Institute of Public Health – National Institute of Hygiene – National Research Institute. The procedure was performed using the Ethical Statement no. WAW2/150/2022, dated 7 December 2022, of the Local Ethical Commission for Animal Experiments in Warsaw.

Statistical analyses

This study’s quantitative and qualitative data were subjected to statistical analysis. The mean ± SD was used to express continuous data unless stated otherwise. Various tests were employed to evaluate multiple variables, including numerous unpaired t-tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, paired t-test, RM one-way ANOVA, and correlation (Pearson’s r). These tests were used to identify any significant variable changes between the different groups. A P-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests performed. GraphPad Prism 9.5.1.733 (San Diego, USA) was used to analyze the data.

Discussion

Our research’s core is the ex vivo model, which integrates peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) with the Calu-3 cell line. We employed cell immunophenotyping [24] to evaluate the distribution and activation of immune cell subsets before and after stimulation with biologics, illuminating the essential interactions that influence immune behavior. We presented the first in vitro investigation utilizing the adenoviral platform for delivering T cell immunogens, ICOS and CD40 ligands, in combination with antigens: S-His or N-His.

The effects of biologics in various combinations on the viability and metabolic activity of VERO E6 and PBMC cells were examined (Figures S9–S10). VERO E6 is a valuable model for studying cytotoxicity and viral internalization [25, 26]. While VERO E6 cells may not fully represent the complexity and diversity of human respiratory cells, the findings were studied cautiously and complemented with relevant cell lines and models, such as PBMCs, Calu-3 cells, and 3D alveoli models [27–29]. The Calu-3 cells exhibit a respiratory bronchial epithelial cell-like phenotype, enabling the study of respiratory infection pathology [30–32]. The Calu-3 cell line, cultured on Transwell inserts in air–liquid conditions, developed a mucociliary phenotype, expressing a cilia structure on its surface in contact with air (Figure S11). Next, the viability of the VERO E6 cells exposed to different concentrations of immunogenic factors was investigated (Figure S9). The analysis revealed no significant changes in PBMC viability compared to the control group (Figure S10), suggesting that the selected concentrations accurately study the immune response [12, 13]. The VERO E6 cells treated with AdV1 at 5.2 × 10^4 VP/mL showed a slight increase in metabolic activity (127.0 ± 3.0%) compared to the untreated control (100.0 ± 16.0%). AdV1 is armed with two costimulatory ligands, CD40L and ICOSL, which can contribute to this increase [12], demonstrating that AdV1 induces the relative expression of the CD40 gene in VERO E6 cells.

The PBMCs were exposed to immunogenic factors for 24 h (Fig. 1) to optimize the immunophenotyping process. Treatment with AdV1 + S-His, AdV1 + S + N (-His), and AdV1 + N-His resulted in a significant increase in the number of CD4+ TCM and CD4+ TEMRA. Nucleocapsid, combined with spike, induces CD4+ TEM (Fig. 1). Thieme et al. [7] proved that it is crucial to individually evaluate the spike and nucleocapsid proteins for their ability to induce T-cell responses. Both proteins should be considered prophylactic targets [7]. We provided the nucleocapsid for further studies to follow the latest trends in vaccine design. Our results align with Thieme et al. [7], who reported a more vigorous response of CD4+ T cells than CD8+ T cells. Subpopulations of CD4+ T cells play a synergistic role in orchestrating the immune response [6]. CD4+ T cells can differentiate into various subsets of helper T cells with effector functions that protect against different pathogens [6].

We tested whether the AdV1-treated samples showed a significant increase in the number of cells in early apoptosis compared to the control and AdV2-treated samples (Fig. 2). The ability of the CD40 ligand to induce apoptosis is widely used in therapies using oncolytic viruses [33]. While the precise mechanisms are not fully understood, CD40L-mediated apoptosis involves the modulation of apoptotic regulators and the upregulation of pro-apoptotic molecules such as Fas ligand (FasL) and TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) [34]. CD40L drives the activation of a Th1 response by engaging with CD40 on APCs [33]. This response involves the secretion of IFN-γ and other cytokines, which enhance the immune response. The 7-day exposure of PBMCs to AdV1 resulted in a strong induction of CD4+ T cells. The substantial implications in CD4+ TNAÏVE and TSCM were observed (Fig. 3). TSCM cells have antiviral potential and downregulate the expression of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD1), according to the findings of [35]. We noted a significant decrease in TSCM cells (Fig. 3). This suggests that the experimental treatments used in the study had cytotoxic effects, leading to the death of these specific cell populations, which correlates with the results of the apoptosis studies (Fig. 3). The use of AdV1 in the tested samples also contributed to an increase in CD19+ cells (Fig. 3). The study by Schulz et al. [36] demonstrated a strong correlation between the number of CD19+IgD+CD27-naïve B cells and the antibody level, suggesting that this cell type may predict the humoral response. The 7-day experiment proved that by extending the time, we can obtain stronger immune responses in the proposed model (Fig. 3), which are coherent with the in vivo studies on tendencies [37].

The next step was evaluating a more complex model, involving the co-culture of highly differentiated lung epithelial cells and PBMCs, as a model for pathogenesis studies. The AdV1 + S + N vaccine platform was checked to determine how it affects the genome level of the cells used in co-culture in the 3D model. PBMCs exhibited increased expression of eight genes associated with the Rap1 signalling, which are crucial for the immune response and influence the differentiation of T and B lymphocytes (Tables 2 and 3 and Figs. 4 and 5). The Rap1 signalling plays a key role in the immune response to antigens [38]. RAP1 is (1) involved in the development and differentiation of the T-cells, (2) a component of the TCR signalling pathway, (3) required for optimal T-cell activation and proliferation, (4) involved in the regulation of Treg cells, (5) playing a role in B-cell development [38]. Moreover, Rap1 acts as a key regulator of T cell and APC interactions by modulating the adhesive interactions between T cells and antigen-loaded APCs [38]. The activation state of Rap1 plays a crucial role in determining the outcome of T-cell responses to antigen stimulation [38], ranging from productive activation to activation-induced cell death or unresponsiveness. It is worth emphasizing that, in our studies, one of the Rap1 pathway genes, FGFR4, is underexpressed (padj = 7.83 × 10(−5)) in the PBMC genome. In an in vivo study by Easter et al. [39] involving mice, it was demonstrated that FGFR4 plays a crucial role in airway inflammation. The loss of FGFR4 inhibits the translational repressor 4E-BP1, which is required for mTORC1-dependent regulation of proliferation in mammalian cells [40]. FGFR4 knockdown significantly increased total protein synthesis and coherent gene expression [41]. The loss of FGFR4 is associated with increased mTORC1-driven cell proliferation regardless of the genetic and transcriptomic background of the cells [41]. mTOR signalling is a key regulator of immune cell metabolism and function [42]. The absence of FGFR4 in the lungs is associated with increased levels of inflammatory mediators, such as IL-6 [39]. On the contrary, the downregulation of the FGFR4 gene in our in vitro study does not correlate with the increased expression of IL-6. Li et al. [43] showed that kinases negatively regulate the production of IL-12p70 by the mTOR pathway, which inducts IL-10 to inhibit IL-12p10. After stimulation with TLR and ligation CD40, increased production of IL-10 influenced the production of IL-12p10 [43]. Moreover, an increase in IL-10, IL-12p70, and IL-8 was observed in PBMCs treated with AdV1 + S-His + N-His (Fig. 7). The increased level of anti-inflammatory IL-10 suggests that immune cells attempt to regulate vaccine-induced inflammation to manage tissue damage [33]. IL-12p70 indicated a robust immune activation and association with immune responses involving T-cells and NK cells, effectively defending against infections [44]. Elevated concentrations of IL-8 and IL-12p70 were found to induce antibody levels [45]. Notably, IL-12p70, recognized as a pivotal cytokine in promoting a Th1-dominated cellular immune response, exhibits the capacity to activate B lymphocytes, thereby promoting the synthesis of antibodies [44]. Lack of IL-10 leads to higher levels of INF-γ production after specific antigen exposure, while an adjuvant induces increased concentrations of IL-10 in human PBMCs [43]. Najafi-Fard et al. [46] showed that treatment with IL-10 reduces TNF-α, IL-2, and INF-γ produced by CD4+ and CD8+ from blood. The same trend was noted after spike stimulation; differences were remarkable for TNF-α [43]. IL-10 has pleiotropic activity, meaning it plays a role in inflammation and cell homeostasis by acting via STAT3 and JAK1/TYK2. It has been shown that bioactive compounds that induce the expression of IL-10 are a valuable strategy for reducing inflammation [47].

We showed that AdV1 elevated the INF-γ level (Fig. 7). INF-γ is crucial in activating immune cells, such as macrophages and T cells, to target infected cells [11, 48, 49]. In the context of assessing cellular immunity following COVID-19 vaccination, the dynamics of interferon (IFN-γ) changes emerged as a significant indicator of immune responses. IFN was implicated in the body’s defence against SARS-CoV-2, the virus causing COVID-19 [48, 50, 51]. A well-modulated IFN response can help control viral replication and promote the development of immune memory [52]. The platform without the spike protein increased IL-6 and IL-17F (Fig. 7). Elevated IL-6 levels are typically observed in patients who have received inactivated SARS-CoV-2 vaccines [44]. Since the elevated level of IL-17A is associated with COVID-19 severity and progression, our study’s decrease in IL-17A and IL-2 levels could indicate a controlled immune response in vitro [53]. PBMC provides a valuable model for investigating the impact of viruses on the immune system [54]. By exposing PBMCs to viral antigens, we established a model for immune studies in the preclinical step. We observed an increase in TNF-α in response to Omicron. In line with our data, the increased expression of genes such as TNF, IFN-γ, antiviral, HLA-DQA1, and HLA-F was observed in patients with severe COVID-19 [55]. Other authors [2, 56, 57] have used PBMCs to investigate immune responses to various viruses, including SARS-CoV-2, HIV, Dengue, and Influenza. Transcriptomic analysis of PBMC data from COVID-19 patients revealed abnormal mRNA and lncRNA (long non-coding RNA) expression patterns, which may serve as potential biomarkers for disease severity [58]. Karwaciak et al. (2021) [59] investigated the impact of SARS-CoV-2 proteins, specifically the spike (S) and nucleocapsid (N) proteins, on the differentiation of CD4+ lymphocytes. Karwaciak et al. [59] revealed the induction of IFN-γ in CD4+ cells, suggesting the involvement of these lymphocytes in the response to SARS-CoV-2.

The research was completed with an in vivo experiment in which it was proven that the proposed vaccine platform system based on adenoviruses AdV1 and AdV2 does not negatively affect the survival of mice (100% survival in a 28-day experiment; Fig. 8) and does not cause changes in the development of organs such as the lungs, heart, kidneys, spleen, and perinate sac (Table S6). Notably, the lack of tolerability of vaccine formulations was established, as evidenced by high TNF-α levels (Fig. 7) and detectable IL-6 levels (Fig. 7). In line with Hervé et al. [60], these cytokines are associated with vaccine reactogenicity. Formulations are not beneficial in amplifying immunogenicity in vitro and tolerance, with no weight loss in mice (Figs. 9 and 10).

Limitations of the study and prospects

The proposed model of the vaccine platform contains commercially available proteins with His-tags. Some studies suggest that His-tag increases the overall immunogenicity and alters the fine specificity of immune responses [61]. It has been proven that adding a His-tag can significantly impair protein immunogenicity against SARS-CoV-2 [62]. However, other studies indicate that its presence has no apparent effect on the immune response [63]. Ambiguous conclusions from literature studies suggest that testing the vaccine platform using proteins that do not contain a His-tag would also be beneficial. However, this poses numerous challenges regarding antigen purification during the production stage. Another limitation of the study is the length of the immune response experiments. It is a well-known fact that in human organisms, antibody production after vaccination generally occurs within 2–4 weeks, with some detectable as early as 2 days; however, peak titers are reached between 1 and 8 weeks [64]. Most corresponding 3D models cannot be cultured for long periods, especially while maintaining a reliable amount of each immune cell subpopulation, which is already isolated in its mature forms [2]. The choice of cell lines also significantly impacts the model's utility. VERO E6 and Calu-3 have proven reliable for studying viral infections [65]. However, the findings may not directly translate to human organisms in vivo. Therefore, caution should be exercised in extrapolating the results to human immunological responses.

Therefore, an important reinforcement for the conducted research would be using different antigens, with or without tags, to determine their effect on the immune response. It is also crucial for future research to extend the duration of experiments, which would enable the vaccines to come closer to the time of action in the human body. Exploring more cell lines and creating more complex 3D models would further reflect in vivo interactions and be beneficial. Expanding genome analysis to include different genes from immune response pathways is crucial for advancing research and drawing new conclusions. Furthermore, native viruses should be tested on the model in the next step.

Another limitation of existing studies is the selection of a few animals, which may result in missing significant differences in the population [66]. In this article, based on a literature [66–68] review conducted by us, we suggest using three animals per group in the pilot study. To ensure the reproducibility of the present data, we carefully controlled factors that could affect errors, such as the concentration of the immunogenic factor injected. The experiment was performed following the experts’ instructions, resulting in minor errors in the estimates of the distribution parameters and dose [67].

The following limitation is that pooling multiple PBMC donors significantly reduces the median inter- and intra-assay variations [69]. Using pools of PBMC from multiple donors may extend beyond the benefit of having an abundant and more sustainable source of cells for assessing large numbers of samples from clinical protocols [69]. From a practical perspective [70], using randomly selected PBMC donors to compose a reference PBMC pool may provide a viable approach based on pilot experiments, enabling the production of a high number of test aliquots while maintaining the discrimination of immunophenotype at days 2 and 7, respectively.

Conclusions

The 3D in vitro model, utilizing PBMCs and the Calu-3 cell line, enabled a comprehensive exploration, yielding insightful findings into the immune response to viruses. The research revealed new insights into immune regulation and the impact of the CD40 pathway on immune cell populations. For further research, a deeper evaluation of the CD40 pathway’s mechanistic role would be beneficial, particularly when controlled antigen release using biomaterials as carriers can enhance the efficient development of immunity and combine multiple-dose immunogenic factors into a single injection. Exploring its impact on the different viral infections could reveal broader therapeutic applications. Human in vitro 3D modeling is an emerging approach for the preclinical evaluation of candidate vaccines, enabling benchmarking in the same study participants while accounting for demographic variables such as age, sex, and comorbidities. 3D modelling of tissue architecture can simulate innate and adaptive immune responses, accelerating and de-risking vaccine discovery and development. The 3D model provides a reliable research model for studying reduced FGFR4 expression, which aligns with the hypermethylation of the gene.
