Development and evaluation of a virtual reality driving test for patients with cirrhosis
Authors: Simon J. Gairing, Eva M. Schleicher, Leonard Kaps, Sophia Schulte-Beerbuehl, Kristina Steiner, Joachim Labenz, Jörn M. Schattenberg, Peter R. Galle, Marcus-Alexander Wörns, Christian Labenz
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Patients with cirrhosis and especially those with HE may have impaired driving skills and may be prone to car accidents. This proof-of-concept study aimed to develop and evaluate the applicability of a virtual reality (VR)–based driving test in patients with cirrhosis. Additionally, the association between the results in the VR test and car accidents was investigated.Methods:A short driving test using a VR head–mounted display (HTC Vive Pro Eye) was developed to simulate five hazardous situations. The patient has to pull the brake when the hazardous situations occur. The time from triggering the event to pressing the brake is recorded as reaction time. Total reaction time (TRT) is defined as the combined reaction time to all 5 events. Car accidents were assessed retrospectively (previous 12 months) and patients were followed prospectively for 6 months. Minimal HE (MHE) was diagnosed using Psychometric Hepatic Encephalopathy Score.Results:In total, 112 outpatients with cirrhosis and 52 controls without cirrhosis were recruited. MHE was detected in 14% (n = 15). Patients with cirrhosis and MHE (5.67 s) had higher TRTs compared to patients without MHE (5.02 s) and controls without cirrhosis above the age of 50 (4.98 s) (MHE vs. no MHE or controls p<0.01, no MHE vs. controls: not significant). Seven patients reported car accidents during the twelve months prior to study inclusion. TRTs were numerically higher in patients with reported car accidents (p = 0.099). When patients who stopped driving for HE-related/cirrhosis-related reasons (n = 14) were added to the group of patients with accidents, then a longer TRT was significantly associated with the modeled outcome in univariable (p<0.01) and multivariable analyses (OR 2.83, p<0.01). Two car accidents occurred during follow-up. Both patients had TRTs above the 90th percentile.Conclusions:The VR driving test is easy and rapid to perform in patients with cirrhosis and could be helpful as a point-of-care tool for predicting car accidents.

INTRODUCTION

Liver cirrhosis and its complications have detrimental effects on patients’ well-being, daily life, and prognosis.1 One of the most important consequences of cirrhosis for patients themselves and society is the potential impairment of driving ability.2 Driving a car is a complex task and requires an adequate level of vigilance, attention, and responsiveness. Importantly, the ability to drive a car is mandatory for various professions. HE is a severe complication of cirrhosis and affected patients show psychomotor slowing, impairment of visuomotor coordination, and inhibition of executive function.3 Several studies demonstrated that even patients with minimal HE (MHE)—the lowest stage of HE according to the West Haven criteria—may be unfit to drive a car according to on-road driving tests.2,4 Moreover, these issues also seem to translate into severe consequences, as MHE is associated with a higher frequency of car accidents.5 Although the impact of HE on driving ability has been studied in many studies, recommendations by physicians regarding the driving abilities of patients with HE are heterogenous. A recent survey of German gastroenterologists revealed that 41% recommend their patients with HE to stop driving, while 1 in 4 decides on a case-by-case basis.6 This uncertainty regarding a clear recommendation to patients is most likely due to the fact that the predictive value of MHE tests is insufficient to reliably predict traffic accidents and that no other adequate test is yet available for routine use.

The key components that are needed for driving a car are vigilance, attention, and responsiveness. These characteristics can be determined by a test in a driving simulator and Bajaj et al. showed that driving simulator performance of patients with cirrhosis reflects real driving performance.7 However, most driving simulators are expensive, and testing is time-consuming. In recent years, virtual reality (VR) head–mounted displays (HMD) have been introduced in various fields of medicine, such as the planning of liver surgery.8 VR-HMDs have the advantage of allowing the participant to be completely immersed in the simulated situation and are ideal as point-of-care diagnostics due to their small size. In this study, we therefore aimed to develop an easily applicable point-of-care driving test with a focus on the reaction time to hazardous events based on VR, test its feasibility in patients with cirrhosis, and compare results between patients with cirrhosis and noncirrhotic controls. In a secondary step, we aimed to investigate the predictive value of the developed VR test regarding real-life car accidents to use it as a screening test to identify patients with cirrhosis in need of further elaborate driving tests.

METHODS

Study cohort

In this prospective study, consecutive patients with cirrhosis referred to the outpatient clinic of the Cirrhosis Center Mainz (University Medical Center Mainz) were recruited between April 2021 and September 2022. Diagnosis of liver cirrhosis was based on histology, conclusive appearance in ultrasound or radiological imaging, endoscopic features of portal hypertension, and medical history. Patients with cirrhosis with preterminal comorbidities, severe neurological comorbidities, overt signs of HE, insufficient German language skills, or ongoing chronic harmful alcohol consumption (defined as self-reported alcohol abstinence for at least 6 months with random testing for urinary ethyl glucuronide at the discretion of the treating physician) were not enrolled.

A sample of 52 noncirrhotic volunteers of both sexes and from all relevant age decades was recruited as a control group. These volunteers were either employees of the department without relevant previous illnesses or patients from the outpatient clinic without severe illnesses and with controlled liver diseases and no signs of advanced fibrosis (FibroScan < 6 kPa, negative HBV-DNA, normalized liver enzymes in patients with primary biliary cholangitis or autoimmune hepatitis).

Diagnosis of covert HE

First, all patients with cirrhosis were screened for clinical signs of overt HE (OHE) according to the West Haven criteria.9 If OHE was excluded, the Psychometric Hepatic Encephalopathy Score (PHES) was used to screen for MHE. MHE was diagnosed in patients with a PHES below −4 using the German norms as also described elsewhere.10,11 To minimize distraction and other confounding factors, patients performed the test between 9 AM and 2 pm in a quiet, separate room.

VR driving test

The VR driving test was developed for application on the HTC Vive Pro eye. We chose to use this device because it is readily commercially available for an acceptable price (~1000 €). During the planning phase, we decided that the participant would be only able to accelerate the car once by pushing a button of the controller and to break by pulling a trigger of the controller. We decided against active steering by the participant to minimize complexity and costs. At first, the VR driving test was planned as a round circuit with varying occurrences of hazardous events. However, this idea had to be discarded during feasibility analysis in healthy controls not participating in the current analysis due to serious issues with motion sickness in nearly all participants. Therefore, we decided to develop the VR driving test as a straight road. In the final version, the participant sits in a car (without a steering wheel due to motion sickness issues) and drives on a straight road. The drive is intentionally designed to be very monotonous to challenge the participant’s ability to pay more attention. During the drive, the car enters invisible hitboxes triggering one of 5 hazardous events (a cyclist driving on the street, a bird landing in front of the car, a dog walking on the street, a child running on the street, and a drone falling on the street; Supplemental Figure S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A603). The time between the occurrence of 2 events is random and ranges between 10 and 30 seconds. Prior to the VR driving test, all participants were instructed that hazardous events, for example, someone running on the road or something falling in front of the car, would occur and that one should pull the break as soon as the event was detected. During the test, the application measures the time from triggering the event by driving into the invisible hitbox and pulling the break (reaction time). The total reaction time (TRT) is defined as the sum of the reaction times to all 5 events.

Self-assessment of driving skills

Prior to the VR driving test, patients were asked to rate their subjective driving skills based on a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 10 (1 reflecting very poor driving skills and 10 reflecting perfect driving skills). Afterward, patients were asked about car accidents during the previous 12 months.

Test-retest reliability

Ten young and healthy controls were tested twice within 7–14 days with the driving test under the exact same conditions. Cronbach alpha and intraclass coefficient analyses were performed to evaluate the test-retest reliability.

Follow-up evaluation

Patients were followed for 6 months regarding the occurrence of car accidents. Patients were interviewed in a structured manner either during their appointment for HCC surveillance in the outpatient clinic or by phone call.

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and Istanbul (seventh revision, 2013) and was approved by the ethics committee of the Landesärztekammer Rheinland-Pfalz (2019-14698). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data are expressed as frequencies and percentages, and continuous data as medians with IQR. Differences between two independent groups with continuous data were evaluated using an unpaired t test or a Mann-Whitney U test depending on data distribution. Differences between three or more groups with continuous data were evaluated using an ordinary 1-way ANOVA with Tukey multiple-comparisons test. For the comparison of 2 patient groups with categorical variables, a chi-square test was applied. For correlation analyses, the 2-tailed Spearman rank correlation coefficient (Spearman ρ) was used. For purposes of testing test-retest reliability, Cronbach alpha and the intraclass coefficient were determined.

To identify variables associated with longer TRT, we fitted multivariable linear regression models. Included variables focused on disease-related variables (MHE, MELD, history of OHE, albumin, alcohol-associated cirrhosis) and biologic plausibility (age, sex). To identify variables associated with car accidents, multivariable logistic regression models were fitted. Given the lack of events in this analysis, we restricted variables to age, TRT, and the key cirrhosis-related variables (MELD, PHES, history of OHE). To analyze the discriminative ability of the results in the VR driving test to identify patients with future car accidents, the AUC of the ROC curves and their respective 95% CI are given. p-values below 0.05 were considered significant. Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.4, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

RESULTS

A total of 122 patients were screened for eligibility. Ten patients were not included due to refusal of participation (n = 5), ongoing harmful alcohol use (n = 3), or insufficient German language skills (n = 2). Finally, 112 patients with cirrhosis and 52 noncirrhotic controls were available for analysis. Baseline characteristics of the study cohort are displayed in Table 1. At study inclusion, 15 out of 111 patients with available PHES testing (14%) were diagnosed with MHE. Of these, 3 patients had a history of OHE. The median age of the noncirrhotic controls was 50 years (IQR 34; 60) and 52% (n = 27) were male.

Test-retest reliability

Ten young and healthy controls were tested twice with the VR driving test during 7–14 days. Test-retest reliability was good with Cronbach alpha of 0.82 and an intraclass coefficient of 0.81.

Results of the VR driving test in patients with cirrhosis and noncirrhotic controls

The VR driving test was well tolerated and no participant had to cancel the test. Mild forms of motion sickness were present in 16% of the patients (n = 27). The median test time was 3 minutes and 24 seconds. Median TRT in patients with cirrhosis was 5.15 (IQR 4.59; 6.20) seconds. Median reaction times to the 5 events are displayed in Table 1. The results of the TRT in the VR driving test were influenced by age, and the results of noncirrhotic controls stratified by age decades are displayed in Table 2. Correlation analyses between TRT and age (Spearman rho = 0.41, p < 0.01) and MELD (Spearman rho = 0.06, p = 0.56) are displayed in Supplemental Figure S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A603. In a multivariable linear regression model including all patients with liver cirrhosis (n = 112), higher age, male sex, MHE, and lower serum albumin levels were significantly associated with a longer TRT, while MELD or alcohol-associated etiology of cirrhosis was not (Table 3). In another multivariable linear regression model including all participants of the study (n = 164), liver cirrhosis (β = 0.15, p = 0.04), age (β = 0.44, p < 0.01), and male sex (β = 0.14, p = 0.04) were associated with a longer TRT in the VR driving test.

VR driving test and car accidents

All patients with cirrhosis provided self-reported information on their regular driving behavior and car accidents during the 12 months prior to study inclusion. In total, 93 (83%) patients with cirrhosis were actively driving. In those who were actively driving, the median distance covered per year was 10,000 km (IQR 5000; 15,000). Seven patients (8%) reported car accidents during the 12 months prior to study inclusion. In the cohort of actively driving patients (n = 93), TRTs tended to be higher in patients with car accidents compared to patients without accidents, however, without reaching significance (Figure 2A, p = 0.099). The TRT of the VR driving test separated the patients with and without car accidents with an AUROC of 0.70 (95% CI 0.56; 0.82), which was comparable to age (AUROC 0.70, 95% CI 0.54; 0.86). In contrast, the AUROCs for MELD (0.54, 95% CI 0.34; 0.74) and PHES (0.53, 95% CI 0.31; 0.75) were poor. Due to the low frequency of car accidents, we did not fit a multivariable regression model.

In total, 19 patients reported that they were not driving. All patients provided reasons for why they were not driving. In 14 of these patients, the reasons appeared to be liver-related or HE-related: patients reported that their physician or family advised them not to drive or patients stopped driving because they felt unsafe. These 14 patients had significantly longer TRTs compared to the patients who were driving and had no car accident (median TRT 6.51 vs. 4.97 s, p < 0.01). For additional analyses, we combined the aforementioned 14 patients who felt unsafe to drive with those who had a car accident during the 12 months prior to study inclusion resulting in a total of 21 patients with events (total cohort n = 107). This group was called “car accidents/unsafe to drive.” In this analysis, the TRT of the VR test separated the patients with and without an event with an AUROC of 0.79 (95% CI 0.69; 0.89). Additionally, patients with this modeled composite event had significantly longer TRTs compared to the patients without this event (Figure 2B, p < 0.01). In addition, patients were followed for 6 months after study inclusion for the occurrence of car accidents. In total, follow-up data were available for 92 patients. Two (2%) patients had car accidents (both hitting another car). These 2 patients had TRTs of 6.82 and 6.87 s, which are located above the 90th percentile when compared to the total cohort (top 8.9% and 6.25%, respectively). Notably, both patients had no history of OHE.

DISCUSSION

The easy and routine assessment of the driving ability of patients with cirrhosis is a highly relevant and still an unmet clinical need in routine practice. This proof-of-concept study is the first to develop and assess the value of a short, point-of-care driving test using a VR-HMD in patients with cirrhosis. Our findings demonstrate that using a VR driving test is feasible in routine clinical practice in these patients and may serve as a proof-of-concept for planning further studies on this important topic. In addition, we found that the group of patients with car accidents and those who do not drive due to HE-related or cirrhosis-related reasons performed worse in the driving test than patients without an accident or noncirrhotic controls. This association was independent of MHE defined by PHES and the driving test outperformed PHES in this context.

Point-of-care tests for the assessment of the driving performance of patients with cirrhosis are currently not available besides elaborate driving simulators. However, these are comparatively expensive and will therefore not be widely used in routine clinical practice. In this proof-of-concept study, we were able to demonstrate that a short and easy-to-perform VR driving test using a portable HMD may improve the assessment of driving performance in patients with cirrhosis. Reaction time to an unexpected hazardous situation is critical to driving a vehicle safely. Thus, we decided to measure the reaction time to several events in our newly developed VR driving test. The evaluation of reaction time has been used extensively in the past for the assessment of the fitness to drive and is therefore well justified as a surrogate marker.12–14 In general, reaction time to an unexpected event is mainly determined by the cognitive function as well as the visual and neuromuscular capacity of the driver, all of whom may be impaired in patients with cirrhosis.

Patients with cirrhosis are prone to impaired driving. This is mainly explained by factors affecting brain function, such as HE or alcohol misuse. A study by Kircheis et al2 investigated the fitness to drive in 51 patients with cirrhosis by utilizing a driving instructor’s assessment. Here, only 75% of the cognitively unimpaired patients and 48% of the patients with MHE were deemed fit to drive. Another study by Wein et al4 found that the number of times that a driving instructor had to intervene during a real driving test to avoid car accidents was 10 times higher in patients with MHE compared to patients without MHE. In a secondary step of our study, we did not assess the fitness to drive in general, but the history of or the occurrence of car accidents—a potentially even harder outcome. To our own surprise, we found that car accidents were rare in our German cohort, although a relevant proportion of all patients with cirrhosis seems to suffer from driving impairment according to the Kircheis study.2 This finding is in line with a study from Italy, where the frequency of car accidents was also in the low single-digit range.15 A US study by Bajaj et al5 found a frequency of car accidents of 11%; however, this difference to our study may be explained by country-specific differences. In summary, although car accidents are a problem in patients with cirrhosis, they seem to be less frequent than one would expect.

Given that patients with cirrhosis are at higher risk for car accidents compared to the general population, it is therefore of pivotal importance to identify those patients who may be unfit to drive. However, withdrawal of the driving license has serious consequences for those affected and is a negative predictive factor of health-related quality of life in patients with epilepsy.16 Several studies tried to identify cost-effective tools for predicting car accidents or the fitness to drive. The most obvious ones are cognitive tests used for the detection of MHE. In this context, Bajaj et al5 demonstrated that MHE defined by the inhibitory control test was associated with car accidents over the preceding year and during follow-up compared to patients without MHE. Of note, PHES results were not. This lack of predictive ability of the PHES is also validated in our current study in a German population and highlights that MHE defined by PHES may not be used alone to restrict driving. However, it has to be acknowledged that our cohort of patients with MHE was quite small in this study and these results should therefore be interpreted with caution. A study by Tandon et al17 recently found that in-office testing using the DriveABLE Cognitive Assessment Tool predicts more errors during on-road driving tests, but was insufficient to detect patients who are unfit to drive. Formentin et al15 evaluated the usefulness of the Psychomotor Vigilance Task for predicting car accidents and were able to demonstrate a potential predictive value. Our study expands the existing literature by demonstrating the predictive value of a point-of-care VR–based driving test for the first time. Although our test was designed to take as little time as possible—median run time was 3 minutes and 24 seconds—the predictive ability was comparably good for the combined endpoint of patients with car accidents and patients not driving due to HE-related/cirrhosis-related reasons. The test has the advantage that it can be conducted in the outpatient clinic and is completely examiner-independent. Moreover, after purchasing the VR device, testing patients would be free of any additional costs. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the test is far from perfect and there may certainly be room for improvement to further increase the predictive ability for car accidents. However, our study should serve as a blueprint to open the field of VR research in the context of liver cirrhosis and the test may be used as a screening test detecting “at-risk” patients who need further evaluation with an on-road driving examination.

Our study has limitations. First, predicting car accidents is difficult and, in this context, it has to be acknowledged that the number of events in our study was very low. That’s why we combined patients who did not drive due to HE-related reasons or insecurity with patients with car accidents in a separate analysis. However, these results have to be interpreted in the context of the design and our study may stimulate future multicenter studies on the usefulness of VR for predicting car accidents including larger-scale cohorts. Second, our cohort mainly included patients with Child-Pugh A and B cirrhosis, and patients with very advanced cirrhosis are lacking. This is also reflected by a comparatively low frequency of MHE. Therefore, our findings may not be generalizable to patients with Child-Pugh C cirrhosis. However, patients with very advanced stages of cirrhosis are frequently not driving routinely and a recent study demonstrated that the frequency of patients who are unfit to drive is quite low even in Child-Pugh C cirrhosis.17 Third, it is a limitation that traffic accidents were assessed by self-assessment, which may be prone to recall bias. Fourth, the control group does not represent the general population of drivers and we are therefore reluctant to produce reliable norms for the VR test based on this group. Fifth, it has to be highlighted that our VR test does not reflect a real driving experience due to several simplifications, such as driving on a straight road or the lack of a steering wheel. As a result, this test does not fully reflect the complexity of driving in real life. However, specialized driving simulators are very expensive and are therefore not usable for in-office testing in routine care. Last, we did not take driving experience into account in our analyses, which may be a potential bias.

In conclusion, we developed and assessed the value of a short, point-of-care driving test using a VR-HMD in patients with cirrhosis. Our findings demonstrate that using a VR driving test is feasible in these patients and may serve as a proof-of-concept for planning further studies on this important topic. In addition, we found that the group of patients with car accidents and those who do not drive due to HE-related or cirrhosis-related reasons performed worse in the driving test than patients without an accident or noncirrhotic controls. Future studies in multicenter cohorts are warranted to further elucidate the usefulness of VR tests in the context of this important topic and evaluation of possible stepwise algorithms using VR as a screening test, followed by on-road driving tests.
