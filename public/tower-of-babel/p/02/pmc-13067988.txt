Canadian Transplant Candidates’ and Recipients’ Perspectives on Mandatory Vaccination and Access to Transplantation: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Savannah‐Lou Cochran‐Mavrikakis, Carina Sancho, Aliya Affdal, Fabián‐Andrés Ballesteros Gallego, Marie‐Françoise Malo, Laurence Ducharme, Sébastien Foccroulle‐Bussières, Lucy An, Charles Poirier, Geneviève Huard, Noémi Tousignant, Marie‐Chantal Fortin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGiven the stronger immunological effects of vaccines pre‐transplant, the question of whether pre‐transplant COVID‐19 vaccination should be mandatory has been raised. The objective of this study was to gather transplant patients’ perspectives on ethical issues related to mandatory vaccination and access to transplantation in Canada.MethodsWe conducted semi‐structured interviews with 45 transplant recipients and five transplant candidates from a Québec transplant program. The interviews were digitally recorded and transcribed. Thematic and content analysis was conducted.ResultsMost participants were in favor of pre‐transplant vaccine mandates given that vaccination is part of pre‐transplant assessment, transplant patients have moral responsibilities and/or vaccine mandates are legitimate especially in a public health emergency. Restrictions on patient autonomy, the ethical duty to ensure access to life‐saving transplantation and unequal access to vaccines were concerns raised by participants about vaccination mandates and access to transplantation. Many participants did not support excluding unvaccinated patients from transplant lists. Most, however, agreed with prioritizing vaccinated candidates in organ allocation.ConclusionThis study indicates broad support for the promotion of pre‐transplant vaccination. However, preventing access to life‐saving organ because an individual is missing specific vaccinations raised concerns among participants. The results of this study could inform vaccination policies in transplantation in future public health emergencies.

Introduction

Vaccine mandates have been controversial since their introduction in the 19th century [1]. Vaccination is recognized as one of the safest and most effective public health interventions, offering both individual and collective protection against infectious diseases. Although vaccination is often framed as a civic duty, its imposition by law raises important ethical and legal questions about the legitimacy of state authority over individual freedom and bodily autonomy. In the context of transplant care there are additional stakes: vaccination is most effective prior to starting transplant‐related immunosuppressive therapy, while unvaccinated transplant recipients may face elevated risks from post‐transplant infections [2, 3]. The COVID‐19 pandemic gave rise to a heated debate about the justifiability and risks of adopting vaccination requirements for transplant listing [4, 5, 6, 7].

In transplant candidates and recipients, routine and additional immunizations are strongly recommended. However, there is no unified national policy on vaccine requirements for transplantation across Canada. Vaccination policies are instead determined at the provincial or institutional level. For instance, in British Columbia, a vaccine mandate applies to several vaccines for transplant candidates, with requirements varying according to organ [8]. In Québec, there is no provincial vaccine mandate for transplant candidates and recipients [9, 10]. The decision to list a patient for transplantation ultimately falls under the responsibility of individual transplant programs and/or treating physicians [11].

The context of COVID‐19 brought new urgency to the question of vaccine mandates in transplantation. Reported mortality rates among adult transplant recipients with COVID‐19 ranged from 3% to 50%, with high rates of morbidity [12, 13]. Effective vaccines against SARS‐CoV‐2 became available in 2021, significantly reducing COVID‐19‐related mortality and morbidity among transplant recipients [14, 15]. As with many vaccines, immunosuppressive therapy reduces the immunogenicity of mRNA vaccines [16, 17], prompting the Canadian Society of Transplantation (CST) to recommend in 2021 that vaccination be completed prior to transplantation whenever possible [18]. A joint statement by three major American transplant professionals’ societies similarly “strongly encouraged” pre‐transplant COVID‐19 vaccination but stopped short of recommending mandates [19]. By contrast, the CST's lung section issued a national consensus statement in late 2022 supporting the requirement that patients be fully vaccinated against SARS‐CoV‐2 as a condition for being listed for a lung transplant, which considered vaccine refusal as non‐adherence to medical care, and thereby as a contraindication for transplantation [20]. Reports of unvaccinated patients being denied transplant listing received high profile media coverage [21, 22, 23, 24]. In Québec, reports that three unvaccinated patients received lung transplants to replace organs damaged by COVID‐19 raised questions about pre‐transplant vaccine requirements [25].

Transplantation stakeholders’ perspectives on mandatory vaccination remain little studied. A handful of studies have explored attitudes toward vaccination, but not vaccine mandates, among transplant patients in the United States., Australia, Germany and Italy, fueling concerns about gaps in vaccine uptake and confidence [26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. The objective of this study was therefore to gather the views of transplant recipients and candidates on potential COVID‐19 vaccine mandates, as well as mandatory vaccination more broadly, in relation to access to solid organ transplantation. The findings of this study aim to contribute to the development of future vaccination policies, particularly in the context of public health emergencies affecting transplantation.

Methods

This study was exploratory in nature. We used the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research checklist [32]. We conducted individual semi‐structured interviews to gather the perspectives of lung, liver, kidney and heart transplant recipients and candidates. The Centre hospitalier de l'Université de Montréal (CHUM) research ethics board approved the study and all participants provided informed consent (CHUM CE 22.178).

Recruitment was carried out between June and October 2023. Convenience sampling was used [33]. Participants were recruited among lung, liver, kidney, pancreas and heart transplant participants and candidates followed at the CHUM transplant program. To be included in the study, participants had to be at least 18 years old and speak English or French. Invitation emails were sent to 1949 potential participants from the CHUM database, with no follow‐up phone calls. One hundred and twenty‐seven individuals expressed interest in participating in the study and were contacted by phone by a member of the research team (FB). Of these, 62 declined or were unavailable to participate, or were removed from the recruitment list following two unsuccessful contact attempts, and 65 participants consented to participate. Ultimately, 50 participants took part in an individual interview, as the participation threshold was set at 50. These included: 45 transplant recipients (13 kidney [including a kidney‐pancreas], 16 lung, 15 liver [including a liver‐kidney] and one heart), and five transplant candidates (two kidneys, two lungs, and one liver).

The issues covered during the interviews were outlined in an interview guide comprising open‐ended questions that was developed by the research team and pre‐tested by three participants for question length and clarity in both English and French. The questions addressed the following topics: (i) participants’ perspectives on vaccine mandates and transplantation; (ii) arguments for or against vaccine mandates; (iii) ethical issues related to vaccine mandates; (iv) conditions for the implementation of a vaccine mandate; and (v) access to lung transplantation for unvaccinated participants whose lungs have been damaged by COVID‐19. Consistent with qualitative methodology, the interview guide was modified during the study as new topics emerged from the interviews [33]. Participants were also asked to fill out two anonymous online questionnaires on REDcap, one for basic sociodemographic information, and the other covering vaccination status and attitudes toward immunization, which was adapted from a CDC questionnaire (COVID‐19 Vaccine Confidence Survey Questions Bank) [34].

Thirty‐three interviews were conducted by phone and 17 by videoconference, 48 in French and two in English, by two female members of the research team (SLCM and AA) who had no relationships with participants. French interview excerpts were translated by a professional agency. Interviews lasted an average of 36 (23–69) minutes and were digitally recorded and transcribed. Each participant received CAN$30 as financial compensation for their time. Interview transcripts were sent to all participants for review and approval. No participants provided feedback or modifications after receiving the interview transcripts.

We used a qualitative description approach to describe the perspectives of transplant recipients and candidates on vaccine mandates and access to transplantation [35, 36]. The goal of this pragmatic approach is to stay close to the data and provide a comprehensive summary of the topic studied [36], using thematic analysis [33, 37]. The latest version at the time of NVivo (Lumivero) qualitative data analysis software, NVivo14, was used to support analysis. Prior to coding verbatim transcripts, the research team created an initial coding frame based on the interview guide and a review of the literature, to which new codes were added based on interview content. The research team met frequently to discuss the coding frame and data analysis. SLCM coded all interviews and no new codes were created after the 30th interview, indicating that the number of participants allowed for data saturation [38]. That being said, we extended recruitment beyond 30 individuals in order to include participants with a broader range of perspectives on vaccination. An independent researcher (CS) with experience in qualitative methods coded 13 interviews to ensure interrater reliability. All coding differences were discussed and agreement reached among team members. Coded quotes were organized by theme and subtheme.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Forty‐five transplant recipients (13 kidney [of which one kidney‐pancreas], 16 lung, 15 liver [of which one liver‐kidney] and 1 heart) and five transplant candidates (two kidneys, two lungs, and one liver) participated in this study. Thirty‐two identified as male and 18 as female and their mean age was 59 years (range: 20–74). The majority of participants identified as White and reported a high level of education. Table 1 summarizes participant characteristics. Responses to the COVID‐19 survey, summarized in Table 2, indicated that almost all participants (96%) had been vaccinated against COVID‐19, receiving from 4 to 8 doses. Moreover, 58% thought COVID‐19 vaccines were very safe for them as transplant candidates or recipients, and a further 28% thought they were moderately safe, with only 8% opting for “not” or “a little” safe. Respondents also reported a high level of trust in public health agency vaccine recommendations, with only 6% indicating low or no trust. The main reason participants gave for getting vaccinated was to protect their health.

Qualitative Interviews

Vaccination Policies and Transplant Waitlist Requirements (Table 3)

The first topic area discussed in interviews concerned the requirements that patients must meet to be added to the transplant waitlist, and how vaccination related to these. Most participants expressed a limited awareness of waitlist criteria, mentioning at least one of these, such as having started dialysis (for kidney transplant), not using alcohol or tobacco, completing pre‐transplant medical exams and dental assessment, and being in good general health. Some participants saw mandatory vaccination as comparable to other waitlist criteria and therefore as warranted. The majority of participants described mandatory vaccination as a measure for maximizing the chances of good graft and recipient health. Some participants noted the importance of strengthening their immune system prior to taking immunosuppressants and described vaccine adherence as a way of showing that one could be “trusted” with a transplanted organ. However, a few participants questioned the fairness of denying a patient transplant listing based on past “mistakes” such as alcohol use, smoking or not being vaccinated. They argued that individuals should be given a “second chance” to be healthy and responsible, with one participant stating, on this basis, that pre‐transplant mandatory vaccination is unfair and exclusionary.

Many participants expressed uncertainty about whether vaccination was mandatory within their own pre‐transplant procedures. Most reported having accepted vaccination prior to being waitlisted regardless of whether it was mandatory. In a similar vein, many patients attributed their own adherence to medical advice – on both vaccination and other pre‐transplant measures – to trust in the transplant center's staff and procedures.

Supporting Arguments for Mandatory Vaccination (Table 4)

The reasons participants gave in support of pre‐transplant mandatory vaccination fell into two categories. The first referred to moral responsibilities arising from the specific relations and obligations that surround transplantation. The second pertained to more general principles and conditions that justify vaccine mandates.

Vaccination as a Moral Responsibility in Transplant Settings

In discussing vaccination's relation to other waitlist criteria, many participants expressed trust in the medical reasoning that underpins pre‐transplant requirements. Most also offered additional moral arguments for pre‐transplant vaccine mandates. These were associated with the perception of donor and transplanted organs as a “gift of life,” as well as the morally responsible use of limited donor organs and healthcare resources.

Many participants argued that mandatory vaccination is a means of preventing the “waste” of scarce organs and resources, of fairly allocating these resources, and more generally of protecting societal investments in transplantation. They described organ donation and transplantation as a “gift” and, by extension, mandatory vaccination as a means of honoring and expressing gratitude for generosity and altruism, with some participants referring to non‐vaccination as a “sin” and “selfish.” In the same vein, some participants spoke of mandatory vaccination as a form of respect toward others, including fellow waitlisted patients as well as donors and their relatives. For example, a few participants spoke of unvaccinated recipients who “ruined” or lost their graft as depriving other candidates of a transplant, or as putting other transplant recipients at risk of infection.

Finally, many participants stated that although individuals were and should be free to refuse vaccination, they should accept the consequence of this choice on their eligibility for transplant listing. In other words, they viewed vaccination as a legitimate condition for obtaining access to transplantation, even if they considered it to be a personal choice outside the transplant setting.

Vaccine Mandates Are Legitimate, Especially in a Public Health Emergency

Participants also articulated more general justifications for mandatory vaccination. Some participants believed that vaccination should not be individual choice because its stakes are collective, especially in a pandemic. A few emphasized that vaccination is highly accessible, illustrating this with examples from the COVID‐19 vaccination campaign. Many participants thought that medical exemptions should be allowed to adjust vaccine requirements to individual health risks. Among these, a few further noted that medical professionals could be trusted to weigh both individual and collective interests in making judgments about exemptions.

Most participants thought there should be no exemptions to mandatory vaccination on religious or ideological grounds. Many among these pointed to the contradiction between objections to vaccination and willingness to receive a transplant, given that both medical acts have similar implications for bodily integrity. A few insisted that religions in general prohibit neither vaccination nor transplants. Others referred to the primacy of collective health and rights over individual or religious beliefs and/or stated that medical and scientific logic should prevail in vaccine‐related decision‐making.

Concerns About Mandatory Vaccination (Table 5)

When asked about their position early in interviews, most participants stated being in favor of vaccine mandates in transplant settings, with some participants initially reporting opposition or ambivalence. In response to further questions, however, many went on to express concerns about the application and impact of such mandates.

A few participants cited gaps in evidence or efficacy, pointing out that the long‐term consequences of new vaccines are unknown, that vaccination does not prevent 100% of infections, or that there is no direct and definite relationship between vaccination status and post‐transplant outcomes. A few participants were concerned about social and geographical inequalities in access to vaccination or information about vaccines, entailing that vaccine mandates could have further discriminatory impacts on access to healthcare. One participant referred to unethical medical research on African Americans as a legitimate basis for mistrust, linking this to disparities in vaccination services and coverage among neighborhoods in the city of Montreal.

Most participants were opposed to the rigid enforcement of vaccine mandates across all situations. When prompted with a scenario in which an unvaccinated patient urgently required a life‐saving transplant, many participants expressed discomfort with denying them access to care based on their vaccination status. Some participants emphasized the need to consider exceptional circumstances such as medical emergencies. In response to another scenario, many participants opposed denying a lung transplant to an unvaccinated patient with COVID‐19‐related lung failure. Many participants mentioned the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms or “the law” as an obstacle to the application of vaccine mandates. Some pointed out that, from a legal standpoint, individuals have the right to refuse vaccination, as they do other medical treatments, and to govern their own bodies. Not all agreed that this should be the case, however, with some suggesting these legal impediments were unfortunate. Indeed, only a few of participants explicitly cited rights to personal freedom and bodily autonomy as grounds for opposing vaccine mandates.

Recommendations for Transplant Program Vaccine Policies (Table 6)

Participants offered both implied and explicit suggestions for how transplant programs should design and implement vaccine policies. As noted above, many participants were reluctant to support a rigid application of pre‐transplant vaccine requirements, implicitly supporting a flexible approach. Moreover, most of participants mentioned being in favor of prioritizing vaccinated candidates for transplant as opposed to excluding non‐vaccinated patients from waitlists. A few participants further mentioned that vaccine requirements should be tailored to the medical needs of transplant patients, for example, that vaccines against respiratory infections should be made compulsory for lung transplant candidates.

Most participants thought that healthcare professionals should be responsible for informing patients about pre‐transplant vaccine requirements, rather than relying educational materials such as videos and brochures. Some identified transplant physicians as best suited to discussing vaccination given their position of authority. Others instead thought transplant nurses or social workers would be better able to navigate potentially sensitive issues around vaccine mandates, given their generally strong relationships with patients and their relatives. Many participants also mentioned that patients should be informed of vaccine requirements as early as possible during pre‐transplant assessment or even as soon as the topic of transplantation was broached, so they would have time to make an informed decision.

Participants also suggested ways of encouraging hesitant individuals to accept vaccination. Most participants thought there was a need to develop better educational strategies, some mentioning the value of risk and benefit data, especially in transplant contexts, as well as the importance of meeting patient needs. Some also mentioned the importance of patients feeling safe and comfortable asking questions when discussing vaccination with their healthcare providers. A few participants identified peer‐to‐peer interaction as useful for supporting transplant‐related decision‐making, with some suggesting that specific examples of transplant recipients who experienced severed COVID‐19 would powerfully illustrate the risks of non‐vaccination.

Discussion

Our study is, to our knowledge, the first to explore transplant patient perspectives on vaccine mandates. Previous studies have explored COVID‐19 vaccination coverage among transplant patients [39, 40, 41], as well as factors associated with vaccination uptake, such as age [28] and political affiliation [42]. Our study reveals that patients are acutely aware of the collective and ethical dimensions of transplantation, evaluating the stakes of mandatory vaccinations as part of a complex web of exchange and mutual obligation. This may be amplified in the Canadian context, where universal public healthcare is funded and managed as a collective good. Also, vaccination uptake among the Canadian population was high, with 81.1% of the general population who received at least one dose of COVID‐19 vaccination in June 2024 [43]. In the province of Quebec, a 2022 online survey of adults found that COVID‐19 vaccine coverage for at least two doses was 91.2% among all respondents, increasing to 94% for those living with a chronic disease and 98.4% for those 65 years or older [44].

Participants evoked more general issues associated with vaccine mandates, such as personal freedoms and their legal protection, the primacy of collective interests, and the value medico‐scientific rationality. However, across interview topics, participants often returned to the idea that vaccination policies in transplant settings should be guided by a distinctive set of moral and medical considerations.

Three overarching themes emerge from the results of our study: (1) Trust: Strong support for and adherence with vaccination linked to a high level of trust in transplant professionals and systems; (2) The privilege and obligations of transplantation: Vaccination is a moral responsibility, part of a web of mutual obligations associated with transplantation viewed as a privilege; (3) Ambivalent rights to care: The right to life‐saving care may sometimes apply to transplantation, which should not be denied on the basis of vaccination status alone. Our results do not reflect the polarization observed in society and the media regarding COVID‐19 vaccination.

The participants in our study, most of whom were transplant recipients, expressed high levels of trust in transplant professionals and procedures, as well as high levels of vaccine confidence and adherence. A strong majority were vaccinated against COVID‐19 despite moderate concerns about vaccine safety and efficacy, some reporting having accepted pre‐transplant vaccines regardless of whether they were mandatory and on the basis of trust in their transplant team. The observed high level of trust in the healthcare system, alongside the elevated rate of COVID‐19 vaccination among our patient population, is not unexpected. Organ transplantation inherently necessitates trust in institutional processes, particularly in the fair allocation of organs. Furthermore, the high vaccination coverage among our patients may itself be indicative of this trust, as previous studies have shown a positive association between institutional trust and vaccine uptake [45].

Our results also contrast with studies that have identified suboptimal levels of vaccine adherence and confidence among transplant recipients [26, 27, 28, 29]. Studies conducted in 2021–2022 report patient concerns about the risks of then‐new COVID‐19 vaccines, especially for transplant recipients for whom safety data was lacking. In our study, participants were generally confident that pre‐transplant requirements have a valid medical basis, and that their purpose is to protect transplant recipient and graft health. Many equated vaccine requirements with other waitlist criteria, and/or saw these as similarly oriented toward supporting post‐transplant outcomes. Other authors have argued that burdensome medical requirements are already part of the transplant process, establishing a precedent for mandatory vaccination [6, 46].

Participants also evoked moral responsibilities associated with transplantation to justify pre‐transplant vaccine mandates. They described transplantation as a complex set of social relations, in which the “gift” or privilege of receiving a donor organ incurred a strong sense of obligation and gratitude toward donors and their kin, as well as toward transplant services and broader societal investments in organ donation and healthcare funding. Honoring this gift by protecting their graft – including through vaccination – was said to be a way of showing respect for donors and their kin, but also toward fellow waitlisted candidates, for whom an irresponsible use of donor organs would be unjust. They thus identified the optimization of transplant outcomes, including through vaccination, as not just an individual health benefit but as a way of protecting scarce public goods.

Although support for mandatory pre‐transplant vaccination seems to be underpinned by a view of transplantation as a privilege, participants also evoked, albeit ambivalently, access to transplant waitlists or medically urgent transplants as a right. Many expressed discomfort with exclusionary or punitive applications of vaccine requirements. Some, for example, saw being unvaccinated as akin to past tobacco or alcohol use, arguing that it was unfair to deny someone a “second chance” at being healthy. When prompted with scenarios in which unvaccinated patients might die if they were refused a transplant, many suggested that vaccine requirements should be applied flexibly and with respect for the right to life‐saving treatment. Some also thought that inequitable access to vaccination would make pre‐transplant mandates discriminatory. This was not, however, a primary concern for most participant, as it has been in professional discussions of mandatory vaccination [6, 47, 48].

Some participants proposed that vaccination status be used as a prioritization rather than an exclusionary criterion. This might be seen as reconciling tensions between support for conditional access to the privilege of transplantation and a belief in a universal right to medical care. Although participants were invited to make recommendations on implementing vaccine mandates in transplant settings, their suggestions were in line with the view that persuasion rather than coercion should be the primary driver of vaccination. Thus, while almost all participants were clearly strongly in favor of pre‐transplant vaccination and advocated its energetic promotion, many expressed or implied hesitations about the potentially exclusionary, punitive and coercive dimensions of vaccine mandates. That being said, about half of participants consistently stated support for pre‐transplant vaccine mandates throughout the interviews.

For most participants in this study, vaccination should be considered a tie‐breaker between two otherwise similar candidates rather than a deal‐breaker that automatically excludes a patient from transplantation. A parallel can be drawn with alcohol use disorder and access to liver transplantation: Robert Veatch has argued that when two patients have the same allocation score, a modest negative weighting could be applied to the patient who used alcohol [49]. Similarly, treating vaccination as a deal‐breaker—completely denying transplantation to unvaccinated patients—may be straightforward to implement, but it is ethically problematic. [50] It raises broader concerns about the extent to which a patient's access to lifesaving treatment should depend on their previous health‐related behaviors. Conversely, using vaccination as a tie‐breaker is ethically less contentious, but it presents practical challenges. In real‐world allocation systems, it may be difficult to determine how much weight vaccination status should carry relative to other clinical and moral considerations.

Our study has several limitations. First, all participants were recruited from a single transplant center in Canada. Most were White and highly educated. In addition, more than one third of participants were lung transplant recipients who were significantly impacted by the COVID‐19 pandemic. Another major limitation is that most participants were transplant recipients; only five were transplant candidates. Transplant candidates may hold different views on vaccine mandates than transplant recipients. Vaccine mandates were also in place as a requirement for access to the lung transplant waiting list. We did not, however, find significant differences among perspectives according to organ type. Furthermore, most participants were vaccinated and expressed trust in public health recommendations. This may be a result of selection bias, as patients with these characteristics may have been more willing to participate in the study. As a consequence, we did not capture the perspectives of patients who were hesitant about vaccination or who distrusted the healthcare system. Even so, it is interesting to note that even among participants who were highly supportive of vaccination, we found significant hesitations about its imposition as a mandatory pre‐transplant requirement. Thus, our findings may offer valuable insights into how vaccination can be presented to patients during future public health emergencies.

Conclusion

This study, conducted among transplant recipients, demonstrates substantial support for vaccination. Vaccination was frequently perceived as an ethical responsibility inherent to organ transplantation, given that transplantation is regarded as a privilege. Endorsement of vaccination was closely associated with a high level of trust in the transplant system. Nevertheless, participants expressed reservations regarding the implementation of a strict vaccine mandate, as such a policy could potentially restrict access to life‐saving treatment for certain patients. Rather than mandatory vaccination, participants favored prioritizing vaccinated individuals for transplantation. Recommendations included developing educational tools, involving peers, introducing vaccine requirements early in the pre‐transplant assessment, and tailoring vaccination to patients’ medical needs. These recommendations may inform the design of future vaccination policies in the context of public health emergencies.
