Update on the Etiology and Pathogenesis of Erythrodermic Psoriasis
Authors: Yixuan Wang, Yanan Wang, Hongzhong Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractErythrodermic psoriasis(EP) is a rare, life-threatening variant affecting 75%-90% of the body surface area. Characterized by widespread erythema and potential systemic symptoms like fever and lymphadenopathy, it severely impairs patient quality of life. The pathogenesis of erythrodermic psoriasis is not fully understood. It is a multifactorial, multistep process suspected to result from an abnormal immune response induced by both genetic and environmental factors. Key contributors to erythrodermic psoriasis onset include specific gene polymorphisms, altered expression of adhesion molecules, dysregulated cytokine activity, and abnormal activation of T cell subsets. Additionally, imbalances in the skin microbiota and external factors, such as infections and medications, play important roles in disease onset and progression. Distinct from prior reviews that primarily emphasize clinical features and treatment, this review integrates recent mechanistic advances across genetic, immune, environmental, and microbiome domains to provide an updated, systems-level framework for understanding erythrodermic psoriasis and to highlight potential therapeutic implications.

Introduction

Erythrodermic psoriasis is a rare and life-threatening form of psoriasis that can involve approximately 75–90% of the body’s surface area.1 The prevalence of erythrodermic psoriasis among psoriasis patients ranges from 1% to 2.25%.2 Its clinical manifestations include widespread erythema, with or without scaling, along with possible systemic symptoms such as fever, lymphadenopathy, and joint pain. The exact pathophysiology of erythrodermic psoriasis is not fully understood. It is suspected to result from an abnormal immune response induced by both genetic and environmental factors. Clinically, erythrodermic psoriasis often develops from psoriasis vulgaris (PV) in response to triggers such as infections, rapid withdrawal or sudden changes in medication, alcohol use, emotional stress, and certain vaccines.3 Treatments for erythrodermic psoriasis are challenging, often based on clinical experience and patient comorbidities, due to the low disease incidence and limited clinical evidence. Various treatment options have been explored, including cyclosporine, acitretin, methotrexate, and newer biologics such as anti-tumor necrosis factor (TNF), anti-interleukin (IL)-17, and anti-IL-12/23 agents.4 This review examines the etiology and pathogenesis of erythrodermic psoriasis, focusing on genes, triggers, adhesion molecules, cytokines, lymphocytes, and skin microbiota.

While previous reviews have provided valuable insights into the clinical characteristics and management strategies of erythrodermic psoriasis, a detailed synthesis of its pathogenesis has been largely absent from recent literature. Therefore, this review aims to provide an updated and comprehensive synthesis of the pathogenesis of EP, incorporating recent findings that have emerged over the past few years.

Genetic Factors

Genetic factors play an important role in erythrodermic psoriasis. Studies have identified specific genetic polymorphisms associated with erythrodermic psoriasis.5 In a retrospective study involving 63 Chinese patients with erythrodermic psoriasis, the human leukocyte antigen HLA-C*0102 allele was identified as the most common variant, with a frequency of 34.4%.6 While this finding is significant, the modest sample size suggests the need for validation in larger cohorts. HLA-Cw1, which is more prevalent in certain Asian populations, has been associated with increased disease activity and a higher risk of developing hard-to-treat erythrodermic psoriasis.7 Comparisons between Chinese patients with erythrodermic psoriasis and those with plaque-type psoriasis have revealed differences in HLA-C and HLA-DRB1 alleles. Specifically, HLA-C0602, HLA-DRB112, and HLA-DRB107 alleles are less common in erythrodermic psoriasis patients than in plaque psoriasis patients.8 Additionally, a family with a heterozygous CARD14 gene mutation exhibited erythrodermic psoriasis, suggesting an association between this gene and the condition.9,10 Although this mutation elucidates a pathway involving IL-12 and IL-23 activation, its relevance to sporadic cases requires further investigation. Genetic analyses, including assessment of major histocompatibility complex Class I Cw*06,11 can aid in diagnosing erythrodermic psoriasis and guiding therapy choices, emphasizing the role of genetics in both the development and management of the disease. Collectively, these findings highlight the influence of genetic factors on the development and management of erythrodermic psoriasis. Importantly, genetic susceptibility may lower the threshold for disease destabilization, thereby predisposing susceptible individuals to environmental or iatrogenic triggers that precipitate erythroderma.

Environmental Factors

Triggers for erythrodermic psoriasis include abrupt withdrawal or sudden changes in medications, infections, emotional stress, vaccinations, and certain drugs. Risk factors associated with death or recurrence in erythrodermic psoriasis patients include pruritus and superficial lymphadenopathy.12,13 A case series study of 52 patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)-related skin conditions revealed that 9.6% had psoriasis.14 However, the interpretation of these findings is limited by the observational nature of the data and small sample sizes, precluding the establishment of a definitive causal relationship. COVID-19 can elicit immune responses that may lead to cytokine storms and systemic inflammation, similar to the mechanism observed in erythrodermic psoriasis.15 The increased burden of emotional stress during the pandemic has also likely contributed to an increase in psoriasis exacerbations. Several studies have indicated that the influenza vaccine, as well as the adenovirus, tetanus-diphtheria, and pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccines, may induce or exacerbate psoriasis.16–18 Notably, evidence supporting vaccine-induced erythrodermic psoriasis is largely confined to sporadic case reports. While proposed mechanisms include activation of the Th1 and Th17 immune pathways and vaccine-induced downregulation of angiotensin-converting enzyme 2, which can lead to increased levels of angiotensin 2, a factor associated with psoriasis development,19,20 these hypotheses remain to be fully validated through controlled experimental studies. Certain drugs can induce erythrodermic psoriasis. The medications most commonly implicated in drug-induced psoriasis include beta-blockers, lithium, antimalarials, interferon (IFN), imiquimod, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, terbinafine, tetracycline and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.21,22 For example, beta-blockers can reduce cyclic adenosine monophosphate levels in the epidermis, leading to excessive keratinocyte proliferation. While less common, isolated case reports suggest that other agents may also play a role. Bupropion, a medication used to manage depression, smoking cessation, and weight loss, has been associated with exacerbations of psoriasis and rare instances of erythrodermic psoriasis.23–25 Additionally, a case report has linked the combination of sofosbuvir and daclatasvir, drugs used in hepatitis C treatment, to erythrodermic psoriasis.26 Some drugs induce psoriasis by promoting keratinocyte overproliferation and affecting the IL-23/IL-17 axis. For example, imiquimod can induce psoriasis in most animal models by inducing IL-23/IL-17 axis-mediated deactivation of Toll-like receptor 7/8.27–29 Biologics targeting TNF-α, IL-23, and IL-17 may also disrupt immune signaling pathways through dysregulated cytokine production, IFN-α overproduction, and altered lymphocyte recruitment and migration, resulting in cytokine imbalances.30,31 In erythrodermic psoriasis, comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia contribute to disease pathogenesis and exacerbation.32 Hypertension has been strongly associated with an increased risk of psoriasis.33 Psoriasis patients also frequently exhibit metabolic syndrome, which includes dyslipidemia and obesity.34,35 Dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia, and elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels are more common in psoriasis patients than in controls.36,37 These metabolic abnormalities may exacerbate psoriatic skin inflammation by activating epidermal keratinocytes.Serum metabolic profiling of 20 EP patients and 20 matched healthy controls revealed a downregulation of glycerophosphocholines and bile acids in erythrodermic psoriasis patients, which may contribute to impaired skin barrier function and extensive skin inflammation.38 Collectively, these triggers and comorbid conditions may converge on immune activation, amplifying systemic inflammation. Accordingly, the following sections synthesize mechanistic evidence on adhesion molecules, cytokine networks, lymphocyte subsets, and microbiota perturbations that shape the EP inflammatory phenotype.

Immunological Factors

Adhesion Molecules

Adhesion molecules play key roles in the pathogenesis of erythrodermic psoriasis. Patients with erythroderma show elevated expression of adhesion molecules, including intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), and E-selectin.39 These molecules participate in leukocyte migration to psoriatic plaques, contributing to immune reactivity.40 Bressan et al41 investigated ICAM-1 expression across different psoriasis phenotypes using tissue microarray analysis on skin biopsies. Their cohort included 7 patients with erythrodermic psoriasis, alongside 13 pustular, 24 moderate-to-severe plaque, and 14 mild plaque psoriasis cases. Immunohistochemical staining for CD34 and ICAM-1 revealed that the highest mean ICAM-1 levels occurred in erythrodermic psoriasis. These high levels promote the migration of mononuclear inflammatory cells, enabling the movement of T lymphocytes into the dermis. Gupta et al42 reported that the number of blood vessels in psoriatic lesions was directly correlated with the severity of inflammatory infiltration. E-selectin contributes to erythrodermic psoriasis by mediating microvascular endothelium adhesion in neutrophils, monocytes, eosinophils, and certain T cell subsets.43 In erythrodermic psoriasis, serum levels of soluble E-selectin are correlated with lesion extent and can serve as markers of disease activity. Overall, elevated levels of adhesion molecules facilitate inflammation in psoriasis by promoting the adhesion and recruitment of T lymphocytes to lesion areas.44 The autologous mixed epidermal cell–T lymphocyte reaction (MECLR) is inhibited by blocking these adhesion molecules, highlighting their role in psoriasis pathogenesis.45 However, some researchers have suggested that plasma intercellular adhesion molecules contribute to the immunosuppressive state observed in erythrodermic psoriasis through abnormal cell adhesion mechanisms.46

Cytokines

These adhesion changes provide a mechanistic bridge to the cytokine networks that drive systemic and cutaneous inflammation in erythrodermic psoriasis. The plasmacytoid dendritic cell–interferon (pDC-IFN) pathway is generally considered dominant in erythrodermic psoriasis. Type I IFN-α is a major inflammatory cytokine implicated in the pathogenesis of this condition.47 Abnormal activation of pDCs leads to the production of large amounts of type I IFNs, resulting in chronic activation of myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs) and the production of TNF and IL-23. This mDC activation induces autoimmune Th17 and Tc17 cell migration into the epidermis, where these cells recognize epidermal autoantigens and produce cytokines such as IL-17 and IL-22, thus promoting the development and progression of erythrodermic psoriasis.47

In addition to the pDC-IFN pathway described above, studies also suggest broader cytokine dysregulation in EP, involving Th1/Th2-associated cytokines as well as Th17-related pathways. A study involving 16 erythrodermic psoriasis patients showed that levels of Th2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-10 were significantly higher in these patients than in PV patients or healthy controls.48 Notably, the ratio of Th1/Th2 cytokines was significantly lower in erythrodermic psoriasis patients than in PV patients.48,49 This specific cytokine milieu characterized by high levels of IL-4 and IL-10 points towards a Th2-skewed immune response, the cellular basis of which is detailed in the next section. Additionally, the release of IL-23, IL-12, and TNF from mDCs can stimulate Th17 cell activity. Cytokines such as IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22, IL-21, and IL-26, produced by Th17 cells, promote epidermal cell proliferation and IL-23 expression in keratinocytes, sustaining chronic inflammation. IL-23/IL-17 axis targeting with anti-IL-17A or anti-IL-23 therapies has shown efficacy with respect to improving skin lesions and systemic inflammatory comorbidities in erythrodermic psoriasis.50

In a case report, Kano et al51 reported a substantial increase in the IFN-γ production by CD8+ T cells in the peripheral blood of an erythrodermic psoriasis patient during the erythrodermic phase. Studies have shown that complete clearance of erythrodermic psoriasis is strongly associated with increased production of Th2 cytokines (IL-4 and IL-13) by CD4+ T cells and decreased production of IFN-γ by CD8+ T cells. In erythrodermic psoriasis, elevated IL-17A levels are correlated with disease severity. TNF-α, a key driver of the pro-inflammatory cytokine cascade, is overexpressed in erythrodermic psoriasis lesions, facilitating leukocyte recruitment to affected epidermal areas.52 The rapid systemic release of TNF-α may be linked to disease onset and severity.53 The cytokine tumor necrosis factor-related weak inducer of apoptosis (TWEAK) stimulates keratinocytes to produce CCL2, CCL5, CCL17, CCL22, and CXCL10,54,55 which recruit leukocytes to the skin and drive the inflammatory response involved in psoriasis pathogenesis.56 Wang et al57 examined 20 PV patients, eight pustular psoriasis patients, and eight erythrodermic psoriasis patients; they reported that TWEAK/Fn14 activation was greatest in erythrodermic psoriasis, which may explain the widespread erythema and intense inflammatory response characteristic of this condition. Overall, dysregulation involving cytokines and Th cell subsets plays a central role in the pathogenesis of erythrodermic psoriasis.

Lymphocytes

Building on the cytokine patterns summarized above, this section reviews the major lymphocyte subsets implicated in EP and their interactions. Advances in psoriasis research have demonstrated the importance of T cells in disease pathogenesis. Activated T cells stimulate keratinocyte proliferation that subsequently enhances T cell activity, establishing a vicious cycle of inflammation.58–60 While lymphocyte subsets implicated in psoriasis include regulatory T cells (Treg), cytotoxic T cells (Tc), and helper T cells (Th), their contributions operate as an interconnected network rather than in isolation.61–64 Th subsets coordinate immune polarization, and a key feature distinguishing EP from PV is the dysregulation of the Th1/Th2 balance.48 Unlike the classic Th1/Th17 dominance seen in PV, EP exhibits a distinct shift toward a Th2-dominant profile. This Th2 skewing explains the elevated IL-4 and IL-10 levels noted previously and suggests that humoral immunity plays a more prominent role in EP, whereas cellular immunity is relatively suppressed compared to PV.65–67 Th2 cells interact with B cell surface molecules and secrete IL-4 and IL-13, which bind to B cell surface receptors to induce immunoglobulin (Ig)E secretion.68 Although Th1 cytokines play auxiliary roles in the secretion of IgM, IgG, and IgA, the IFN-γ released by Th1 cells typically inhibit IgE secretion.69 This regulatory mechanism appears disrupted in erythrodermic psoriasis. Li et al,70 in a study of 48 PV patients and 16 erythrodermic psoriasis patients, found that the EP group had higher serum IgE levels compared with the PV group. This difference implies that unlike the classic Th1/Th17-driven pathology of PV, EP involves a Th2-skewed immune milieu with downstream B-cell activation and elevated IgE.71 This Th2 bias may broaden systemic immune activation and increase susceptibility to infection, while coexisting with ongoing Th17-related inflammation.

Despite this Th2 shift, the Th17 axis remains a critical component of the inflammatory network, operating in conjunction with other subsets. Considering the importance of Th17 cells in PV, their involvement in erythrodermic psoriasis pathogenesis has recently attracted interest. The results of a study by Moy et al72 suggest that Th17 and Th22 cells share overlapping functions in the development of erythrodermic psoriasis. Notably, after Th2 cells, Th17 cells are the most prominent T cell subset in tissue immunofluorescence-stained erythrodermic psoriasis skin lesions. This suggests a complex pathogenesis where Th2 dominance coexists with Th17 activity. Furthermore, a serum proteomics analysis of gene variations in erythrodermic psoriasis patients revealed multiple inflammatory pathway disruptions, primarily characterized by activation of the Th1/Th17 axis, minor shifts in the Th2 axis, and upregulation of the atherosclerotic pathway.73 The role of Tc cells in erythrodermic psoriasis remains unclear, but they are suspected to contribute to the pathogenesis of this specific psoriasis subtype.74

In addition to the helper and cytotoxic T cell subsets described above, TCRγδ(+) cells also play a significant role. Erythrodermic psoriasis is characterized by a strong proliferative response of TCRγδ(+) cells, which may indicate either a nonspecific immune response or a fundamental role in disease etiology.75 Given that erythrodermic psoriasis is frequently triggered by irritants, medications, and infections, TCRγδ(+) cells likely serve as a first line of defense, rapidly expanding in response to these stimuli. These cells are highly cytotoxic and produce key cytokines in psoriasis, such as IFN-γ.76 Epidermal hyperplasia in erythrodermic psoriasis is presumably related to growth factors produced by TCRγδ(+) cells that promote keratinocyte growth and proliferation, such as keratinocyte growth factor and epithelial cell-specific fibroblast growth factor.77 However, there is limited research regarding the impact of TCRγδ(+)-derived growth factors on epidermal hyperplasia in erythrodermic psoriasis. Further studies are needed to clarify whether the TCRγδ(+) cell proliferative response is a primary driver of the disease or a secondary effect of inflammation.78

Finally, B lymphocytes appear to contribute to the disease process, potentially driven by the Th2-dominant environment described above. Lu et al79 found that the number of CD19+ B lymphocytes in the skin lesions of erythrodermic psoriasis patients (n=7) was significantly higher than in non-lesional skin, suggesting a link between CD19+ B lymphocytes in lesions and the pathogenesis and activity of psoriasis. However, given the small sample size, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger cohorts to confirm the definitive role of B cells. Clinical evidence supports this link, as cases of erythrodermic psoriasis induced by B-cell chronic lymphocytic leukemia have been reported, with symptom relief occurring in conjunction with a decrease in peripheral lymphocytes.80 B lymphocytes play a key role in the Th2 immune response by promoting the maturation of dendritic cells, which then enhance Th2 activity.81 Therefore, the systemic accumulation of circulating malignant B lymphocytes may exacerbate T lymphocyte-driven skin inflammation and contribute to the transformation of plaque psoriasis to erythroderma through Th2 cytokine signaling. Furthermore, antibodies produced by B lymphocytes in psoriasis primarily target nuclei in the basal cell layer, but they also can target nuclei in both epidermal and dermal cells.82 This finding suggests that the immunological mechanisms in psoriasis involve structural alterations in nucleic acids or nuclear proteins within basal layer cells.82 There is speculation that B lymphocytes in erythrodermic psoriasis contribute to inflammation by activating and producing antibodies directed against skin cell nuclei, thus amplifying inflammatory processes within the disease. In summary, the pathogenesis of erythrodermic psoriasis is not driven by a single cell subset in isolation but rather by a complex, synergistic network. While the shift towards Th2 dominance distinguishes it from plaque psoriasis, this occurs alongside a persistent Th17 inflammatory axis. These T-cell responses are further amplified by TCRγδ(+) cells at the innate interface and sustained by a feedback loop involving B lymphocytes. Together, these interactions create a self-perpetuating inflammatory cycle that leads to the severe, systemic manifestations characteristic of this subtype.

Skin Microbiota

Beyond immune dysregulation, the skin microbiota may further modulate cutaneous inflammation in EP. The skin microbiota influences psoriasis pathogenesis by interacting with the immune system and triggering immune responses. Dysbiosis (ie, disruption of this microbial ecosystem) may contribute to the development and exacerbation of psoriasis.83,84 The human skin microbiota is complex, comprising multiple microorganisms; microecological imbalances are often reported in psoriasis, characterized by Th17-driven inflammation. Among psoriasis patients, the presence of Corynebacterium in the skin microbiota is negatively correlated with IFN signaling in the skin.85 Because of the inadequate skin barrier in erythrodermic psoriasis, Staphylococcus aureus is likely the most common pathogen infecting affected skin.86 Increased S. aureus colonization on erythrodermic skin can precede local infection and sepsis.87 Decreased expression of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) in keratinocytes and serum may contribute to the higher incidences of S. aureus infections and sepsis in erythrodermic psoriasis. S. aureus induces Th2 inflammation, promotes mast cell degranulation, and increases IgE production in atopic dermatitis. Its dominant colonization in erythrodermic psoriasis suggests a role for Th2/Th17-type inflammation in disease progression.88,89 Alterations in the skin microbiota may stimulate T cell populations and initiate immune cascade responses that lead to erythrodermic psoriasis. An understanding of the mechanisms by which skin microbiota and the immune system interact is essential for efforts to develop novel therapeutic strategies targeting erythrodermic psoriasis.

Comparison with psoriasis vulgaris

Although erythrodermic psoriasis often evolves from psoriasis vulgaris, their pathogenic mechanisms appear to differ in several aspects (Table 1). Genetically, while HLA-C*06:02 is the primary risk allele for psoriasis vulgaris, it is less frequently observed in erythrodermic psoriasis. Conversely, alleles such as HLA-C*01:02 and HLA-Cw1 are more strongly associated with erythrodermic psoriasis, indicating a unique genetic predisposition.6,8 Immunologically, psoriasis vulgaris is classically driven by the IL-23/Th17 axis with a strong Th1 component. However, erythrodermic psoriasis demonstrates a significant shift toward a Th2 immune response, evidenced by a lower Th1/Th2 ratio, elevated levels of Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-10), and increased serum IgE levels compared to psoriasis vulgaris.48,70 This suggests that humoral immunity plays a more prominent role in the erythrodermic subtype. Regarding inflammatory mediators, erythrodermic psoriasis exhibits a more intense inflammatory state, characterized by significantly higher expression of adhesion molecules like ICAM-1 and greater activation of the TWEAK/Fn14 pathway than observed in plaque-type lesions.41,57 Finally, in terms of skin microbiota, the extensive barrier disruption in erythrodermic psoriasis leads to a more severe dysbiosis. Conclusion

This review has provided a detailed discussion of the etiology and pathogenesis of erythrodermic psoriasis, examining the roles of genes, triggers, adhesion molecules, cytokines, lymphocytes, and skin microbiota in forming the pathogenesis network that leads to disease onset (Figure 1). Distinct from prior reviews that primarily emphasize clinical features and general management, this review integrates recent mechanistic advances across genetic, immune, environmental, and microbiome domains to provide an updated, systems-level framework for understanding erythrodermic psoriasis. Specific genetic backgrounds, such as HLA-C*01:02, HLA-Cw1, and mutations in the CARD14 gene, increase an individual’s risk of developing erythrodermic psoriasis,5–7,9,11 while external triggers including abrupt medication withdrawal or changes, infections, emotional stress, vaccines, and certain drugs can precipitate or exacerbate disease activity.39 Mechanistically, EP is characterized by coordinated immune dysregulation involving adhesion pathways, cytokine networks, and lymphocyte subsets, with a Th2-skewed profile occurring alongside persistent Th17-related inflammation. Finally, barrier disruption and microbiota dysbiosis including S. aureus predominance and reduced antimicrobial defenses may further amplify cutaneous and systemic inflammation.86

Despite these advances, our understanding of erythrodermic psoriasis pathogenesis faces several limitations. Most current data are derived from small-scale, retrospective studies or case reports due to the rarity of the disease. Consequently, high-level evidence from large, prospective, multicenter cohorts is scarce. To bridge these gaps, future research should prioritize high-quality mechanistic studies, potentially utilizing skin organoids or animal models, to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying these observations. Furthermore, the integration of multi-omics profiling, encompassing genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, will be critical for identifying specific biomarkers that differentiate erythrodermic psoriasis from other subtypes. Finally, translating these mechanistic insights into therapeutic strategies, particularly regarding the Th2/Th17 immune balance, may pave the way for personalized biological treatments. Thorough consideration of genetic, immune, environmental, and other factors is needed to understand the complexity of erythrodermic psoriasis and establish effective, targeted treatment options.
