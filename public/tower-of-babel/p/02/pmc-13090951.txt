The mental health toll of the Iran-Israel-US conflict on the Iranian diaspora
Authors: Shervin Shahnavaz, Farzaneh Badinlou, Kayvan Bozorgmehr, Zohreh Khazraeimanesh, Lars Lien, Karl Blanchet, Bernadette Nirmal Kumar
Published: 2026

Armed conflict leaves psychological scars far beyond borders, as seen by the mental health burden of war-torn and forcibly displaced populations.1,2 However, the burden borne by diasporas as they watch, grieve, advocate, and care from afar remains unrecognized. Recent evidence suggests that diasporas continuously exposed via family networks and digital media to others’ suffering are at risk for mental health problems.3 Extensive trauma related symptoms despite geographic distance are common among individuals in the diaspora.3,4

Common presentations among Middle Eastern diasporas include sleep disturbance; somatic complaints; worsening of pre-existing depression, anxiety, or trauma-related symptoms; moral distress and survivor guilt; and impaired functioning linked to compulsive news consumption and nocturnal monitoring of events.5

For Iranians abroad, the Iran-Israel-US conflict intersects with the earlier upheavals and sociopolitical situations in Iran, amplifying transnational stressors through identity and kinship ties.6

Parallel realities emerge: daily life in diaspora and the war in Iran, create a complex psychological landscape in which outward normalcy coexists with deep internal feelings of worry, grief, and anger, alongside hope and solidarity. The Iranian diaspora has mobilised across many countries, organising large-scale demonstrations that foster solidarity, shared identity, and emotional support. This helps transform feelings of helplessness into collective action and strengthens transnational ties.

This strong resilience, demonstrated through participation, enhances feelings of empowerment, meaning, and control, and helps alleviate distress. Nonetheless, the Iran-Israel-US conflict has deepened political divides, heightened tensions, and intensified rifts, adding emotional strain to the diaspora already under pressure. Consequently, it is common to experience exclusion, increased fear, moral distress, and polarisation between pro- and anti-war sentiments.

Disruptions and unreliable digital communication are a significant source of stress for the diaspora, as they rely on these channels to connect with loved ones in Iran. These uncertainties about the safety of family members intensify psychological strain and further increase worry, emotional exhaustion, and feelings of helplessness among Iranians living abroad. Furthermore, some members of the diaspora are experiencing stress due to increasingly restrictive migration rules in various countries. This combination of fears about their home countries and uncertain residency status significantly increases psychological strain and diminishes their well-being.7

In the short term, health and social care services should incorporate the mental health needs of diaspora communities into their overall response to the ongoing conflict. This includes evaluating the psychiatric and psychological impacts and ensuring that structured support is available for both patients and clinicians to reduce the risk of secondary or vicarious trauma. In the longer term, sustained investment is required to develop research and services that address the mental health needs of diaspora populations during times of instability, promoting, preventing, and treating them. Reducing psychological harm in the diaspora ultimately requires political solutions to ongoing conflicts. Without peace and de-escalation, the mental health burden resulting from these conflicts may extend to the diaspora and their networks, even affecting those without direct ties to the conflict zones. In our interconnected world, migration is an ongoing reality that shapes our future. The effects of regional wars reach far beyond the areas directly impacted, influencing the mental health of individuals in distant locations. These indirect effects require greater attention from healthcare professionals, researchers, and policymakers to effectively address the global health impacts of armed conflicts.
