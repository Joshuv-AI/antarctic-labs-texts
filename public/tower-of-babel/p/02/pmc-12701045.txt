Key results and possible future endeavors of the Russian health studies program
Authors: Bruce A. Napier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In 1994, the United States and the Russian Federation signed an agreement to cooperate in research on radiation effects, establishing the Joint Coordinating Committee for Radiation Effects Research (JCCRER). Over the past 30 years, the JCCRER has focused on understanding the health effects of chronic radiation exposure, particularly in the Mayak Production Association facilities and surrounding areas. The research has led to significant advancements in radiation protection, with projects categorized into public exposures and effects, and worker exposures and effects. Key projects include dose reconstruction, cancer risk estimation, and the development of dosimetry models. The collaboration has resulted in numerous scientific publications and has set a precedent for future international scientific partnerships. This article summarizes the intent and results of each of the projects performed under the JCCRER over the past 3 decades.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00411-025-01159-7.

Introduction

On January 14, 1994, representatives from the United States and the Russian Federation signed the Agreement between the Government of the United States of America and The Government of the Russian Federation on Cooperation in Research on Radiation Effects for the Purpose of Minimizing the Consequences of Radioactive Contamination on Health and the Environment. This agreement established the Joint Coordinating Committee for Radiation Effects Research (JCCRER) to further the understanding of health effects associated with chronic radiation exposure. The JCCRER was composed of six US and five Russian Federation federal agencies. The Department of Energy (DOE) was designated as the lead US agency and provided financial support and oversite of projects through the Russian Health Studies Program1. The Federal Medical Biological Agency (FMBA) was designated as the lead agency and served the same role for the Russian Federation. The primary focus of research over the past 30 years has been the radiation doses and effects to the workers in the Mayak Production Association facilities in the city of Ozersk and to the surrounding population in the Chelyabinsk and Kurgan oblasts. JCCRER projects led to significant scientific advancements in fields of study related to radiation protection.

Over the past 30 years of effort, each of the JCCRER projects has made significant progress in their primary objectives as well as in the supporting science. The various projects fall into two main categories – public exposures and effects (Direction 1) and worker exposures and effects (Direction 2). In addition, several supporting projects also provided important advances.

This paper presents an overview of the many projects undertaken over the past three decades in the DOE Russian Studies Program and suggests areas of possible future collaboration. Because of the current state of international relations, joint work on the projects has been halted, other than the finalization of several journal articles concerning already-completed work (although neither country has abrogated the actual JCCRER agreement). Below, the various primary joint projects under the JCCRER are listed in the order in which they were initiated under either Direction 1 or Direction 2, and additional projects supporting the main projects are listed last. Several of these projects are represented by articles included in this issue of the journal.

Public exposures and effects

Project 1.2а: physical preservation of the existing data

This project aimed to physically preserve the paper medical records and other records of the Urals Research Center for Radiation Medicine (URCRM) archive. The project formed a record register, determined the appropriate methods of preserving each type of record, developed a plan for systematic archiving of the records (microfilming or making magnetic-optical copies), and scanned and indexed the medical histories and outpatient records of persons examined in the URCRM Clinic over the period from 1951 to 2022 - in total, 102 000 records, 1.5 million pages - and microfilmed over 650 thousand pages.

Project 1.1: dose reconstruction for the populations in the Mayak region

The aim of this project, led at URCRM, was reconstruction of absorbed organ doses from all sources of exposure to the Techa River, East Urals Radioactive Trace (EURT), and offspring cohorts. The project developed the Techa River Dosimetry System (TRDS) – later updated to the Southern Urals Populations Exposed to Radiation (SUPER) dosimetry system. These dosimetry systems provide in-depth analysis of the available data on radionuclide releases to the Techa River and the atmosphere from chronic and accidental releases.

Project 1.1 developed models of radionuclide transport throughout the river system and adapted available models and data for atmospheric transport, using source terms for atmospheric iodine releases developed for the incomplete Project 1.4 (see below) (Napier et al. 2017; Eslinger et al. 2020).

For external dosimetry, Project 1.1 developed age-specific factors to convert the dose in air at 1 m above ground to dose in target organs for 23 organs considered in TRDS as well as for tooth enamel to support Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) tooth dosimetry (Shishkina et al. 2018a). For the first time, the methods of EPR dosimetry were developed for radiation situations with combined external gamma radiation and chronic internal exposure to bone-seeking radionuclides; the method is a combination of EPR dosimetry of tooth enamel, radiometry of dental tissues, and dosimetric modeling of dose formation in enamel from 90Sr incorporated in teeth (Shishkina et al. 2019).

The tooth results were used with historical tooth-beta-counter results to develop an original method of time-dependent strontium intake using in vivo measurements (Zalyapin et al. 2014). These, combined with extensive whole-body counter measurements, were used to derive a sex- and age-specific biokinetic model for strontium (Shagina et al. 2015).

To estimate bone marrow doses from the strontium intakes and retention, a model of the microarchitecture of the trabecular bone was developed (Zalyapin et al. 2018). This was accompanied by the development of a method for creating computational skeletal phantoms (Shishkina et al. 2018b). A family of computational phantoms has been developed and dose coefficients for 89,90Sr have been calculated (Degteva et al. 2021).

Novel methods of dose validation were developed. For the first time, biodosimetry has been applied for inhomogeneous chronic exposure of the body. The yield of radiation-induced translocations due to contributions to red bone marrow exposure to internal strontium and uniform whole-body exposure to external gamma sources was investigated. Two approaches have been developed to interpret the biodosimetry results: an empirical dependence of the translocation yield on the red bone marrow dose (Vozilova et al. 2014), and modeling of the formation and circulation of T-lymphocytes (Тolstykh et al. 2019).

All doses in the TRDS and SUPER have been estimated with estimates of uncertainty calculated as multiple replications using Monte Carlo techniques. The dose estimates consider shared uncertainties and unshared uncertainties included as Berkson (grouping) and Classical (measurement) error types, with individual autocorrelations. The dose estimates were saved for epidemiological studies as complete correlated cohort files with doses for ~ 70 years for 23 organs for 60,000 people (Napier et al. 2013).

Over the period 1994–2023, 159 papers have been published within the framework of Project 1.1, and several more are under preparation.

Project 1.2b: stochastic effects of environmental radiation exposure in population living near the Mayak production association

The primary aim of this project, also led at URCRM, was to estimate carcinogenic risk in case of chronic low dose rate human exposures in the populations around the Mayak facility. Over the past 30 years, several cohorts of exposed individuals have been established: the Techa River Cohort (people living along the river during the period of releases), the EURT cohort (those exposed to the fallout from the tank explosion of 1957), and the Techa River Offspring cohort. The project has arranged the data and improved the quality of data on the follow –up and collection of residence history, vital status, incidence and mortality data for the cohort members, extended the follow-up period for up to 70 years and expanded the catchment area. These changes resulted in an increase in the number of deaths and person-years, which increased the statistical power of the study and reduced the uncertainties of the risk estimates. Increase in the cohort size and in the number of cancer cases makes it possible to assess organ-specific cancer risks in future.

Direct estimates of the risk of solid cancer and leukemia incidence and mortality under predominantly low dose chronic exposure of unselected population have been calculated. The analysis of risk values was carried out at a new qualitative level, which is thanks to improvement of quality and completeness of epidemiological data; improvement of the dosimetric system with greater individualization of data; use of new calculation approaches (using corrected information matrix (CIM) methods, stochastic Monte Carlo doses (Stram et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2017) that make it possible to determine the level of uncertainty in risk estimates associated with dose uncertainties. The dose response for solid cancers and leukemias is well described by a linear model.

Recent analyses have used a cohort defined by combining all of the separate established cohorts. The combined cohort increases power for detection of any dose-response. A dose response seen in each original subcohort, with no significant differences in excess relative risk (ERR) over subcohorts. The average ERR estimates are roughly comparable to the Japanese Life Span Study, but with no significant sex differences. The ERR increases with increasing age at death. The resulting risk estimates do not support the point of view of a multiple reduction in effect under low dose or low dose rate exposure. The observed ratio with risk values in the Japanese cohort is close to 1 (Davis et al. 2015). The results of the project were used to improve the main recommendations of the ICRP (Publications 103 and 118), and various UNSCEAR reports (2019; 2024).

Over the period 1994–2023, 31 papers have been published within the framework of Project 1.2b, and others are in progress.

Worker exposures and effects

Project 2.4: Mayak worker dosimetry

The aim of this project was to estimate internal and external radiation doses to the workers of the Mayak facility. Work was conducted both witht the Mayak Production Association itself, until the year 2009 when the Mayak management ceased cooperation, and at the Southern Urals Biophysics Instituted (SUBI). The Mayak Worker Cohort under study, 25,757 workers, has the largest number of individuals and the highest chronic radiation exposures of any known population on Earth; approximately one-fourth of the exposed workers were women. Detailed health and exposure records are available, thereby facilitating radiation health effects research. The primary radionuclide for internal exposure is 239Pu. Dose estimates are based on bioassays of 239Pu in urine samples, a limited number of autopsies, and some whole-body count data, plus workers’ exposure histories and conditions. The calculations use a biokinetic model with parameters relevant to the conditions of exposure.

A refined biokinetic model of the behavior of plutonium in the body has been developed. Inhaled material is absorbed from the respiratory tract; a fraction containing the initially accumulated activity dissolves quickly, while the remaining fraction dissolves more slowly. A fraction binds with lung tissue where it is absorbed into blood much more slowly (Birchall et al. 2017a, b). Chelation therapy of workers modifies the retention of plutonium; this modification is included in the model (Sokolova et al. 2021).

Because not all workers have bioassay measurements, a Job Exposure Matrix (JEM) was developed for extrapolation of working conditions of certain workers provided with estimates of harmful exposure onto the rest of workers. Initial results of the JEM were provided to the team of Project 2.2 in 2019, but have not yet been used in a final analysis.

Although not yet incorporated in the dosimetry modeling, a novel Americium model has been developed.

As with the public doses, the worker doses were all estimated with uncertainties. The approach for internal doses is an advanced Bayesian model using multiple hyper-realizations (Zhdanov et al. 2017; Birchall and Puncher 2017). Most of the work on the external dose database was performed by Mayak PA. The results are detailed in the September 2007 special issue of the Health Physics journal and electronic annexes to it (Vasilenko et al. 2007). Spot estimates and their uncertainties are obtained for gamma and neutron related occupational exposure, along with the doses from medical diagnostic exposure. After 2010 the works were performed without the Mayak data and participation, mostly by the method of reverse engineering. A hyper model was developed for external dosimetry. The dataset obtained is consistent with the structure of internal dosimetry data including the doses from medical diagnostic exposure (Napier et al. 2016).

Several iterations of both internal and external doses have been prepared over the period of active development, in both deterministic and stochastic versions. A total of 88 individual journal articles have been published and more are in preparation.

Project 2.2: Mayak worker cancer mortality

the aim of this project was to obtain quantitative estimates of carcinogenic risks from both external exposure and internal exposure to plutonium based on analyses of data on the Mayak worker cohort.

This was the first study to demonstrate a statistically significant association between occupational exposure to plutonium and bone, lung, and liver cancer. The efforts also demonstrated the effects of external gamma exposure on lung, liver, other solid cancer, and leukemia rates.

In order to perform the analyses, the project developed novel statistical methods and risk models that use a combination of pre-monitoring Pu exposure surrogates and post-monitoring dose estimates for Pu risk estimation, use time-dependent effect modifiers for chronic exposures (age, age at exposure), and manage and use Monte-Carlo dose realizations to allow for dose uncertainty effects on risk uncertainties (Stram et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2017).

Leukemia mortality in the cohort shows no indication of Pu effects on non-chronic-lymphocytic-leukemia (non-CLL) leukemia rates, which is consistent with earlier findings. A quadratic external ERR dose response with attained age effect modification provides an ERR per Gy of 0.25 (95% CI 0,04; 0.7) at age 60 with a significant time since exposure effect with further modification by age at death.

For solid cancers, external doses show a significant linear dose-response with ERR 0.16/Gy with no sex effect (Sokolnikov et al. 2015).

For internal dose-related liver cancer mortality, there is a significant linear external dose effect with an ERR/Gy of 0.45 but a highly non-linear dose-response for Pu with a quadratic dose response ERR/Gy2 of 2.15.

For internal dose-related lung cancers, recent work including smoking adjustments resulted in a plutonium ERR/Gy of 3.5 for males and 8.9 for females; dose uncertainty increases upper confidence bounds (Stram et al. 2021).

The results of the project are presented in 31 peer-reviewed articles.

Project 2.8: Mayak worker tissue repository

the aim of this project was to establish and maintain a state-of-the-art tissue repository at the Southern Urals Biophysics Institute in Ozersk to serve as a resource for studies of the effects of protracted internal and external radiation exposure on human health. The samples have been catalogued, bar-coded, and preserved (Loffredo et al. 2017).

The biorepository facilitates a wide range of basic and molecular epidemiology studies designed to assess the relationship between radiation exposure and multiple health outcomes and cellular responses. Recent efforts have included studies based on the tissues in the repository. For instance, a recent study used whole exome sequencing to characterize somatic DNA mutations in primary liver tumors from this cohort to identify driver mutations and investigate the contribution of ionizing radiation to the mutational landscape of liver cancer (Goerlitz et al. 2019).

Forty six journal articles have been published concerning both the repository itself and the results of studies carried out using its resources.

Other supporting projects

Project 1.3: reconstruction of radionuclide contamination of the Techa river

This project attempted to develop a model of radionuclide transport in the Techa River to use as a means of back-calculating the aqueous emissions rates of various radionuclides. The approach was deemed to be insufficient by the Technical Steering Panel, and the project was terminated. The needed data were ultimately developed through a joint Mayak/URCRM team working through the International Science and Technology Center Project No. 2841 on Reconstruction of the Techa River Source Term as presented in Degteva et al. (2008).

Project 1.4: Ozersk Population Dose Reconstruction from Mayak Operations:

This project was intended to estimate thyroid doses to residents of the closed city of Ozersk resulting from radioiodine releases to the atmosphere from Mayak activities. The project completed a detailed temporal source term of radioiodine releases, as well as detailed information about historical farming and food distribution practices in the vicinity. However, the Mayak participants withdrew from the project in the year 2009 before the actual dose calculations could be performed for specific individuals residing in the city. The detailed information concerning the releases, as well as diets and food sources, was later incorporated into Project 1.1.

Project 2.1: metabolism and dosimetry of plutonium industrial compounds

This project conducted a joint analysis of the data collected by the U.S. Transuranium and Uranium Registries (USTUR) and the Dosimetry Registry at Mayak (DRIMA) on deceased people with occupational exposure to radiation to determine the inter comparability of data from the USTUR and DRIMA. The data were used to develop and improve dosimetry models.

The project showed that there was compatibility for liver and skeletal measurements between the organizations, and that uniform radiochemical methods, standards and equipment were used. The project illustrated that in vitro solubilities of industrial compounds were related to lung transportability (Khokhryakov et al. 2000). It was shown that liver diseases altered plutonium biokinetics and excretion (Suslova et al. 2006).

Project 2.3: Non-Cancerous effects of radiation exposures (Deterministic effects)

this project aim was to validate and analyze the data on acute and chronic effects of radiation, other than cancer, observed in a large number of workers at the Mayak facility. The project detailed clinical and blood count records on workers diagnosed with ‘Acute Radiation Syndrome’ and/or ‘Chronic Radiation Syndrome’, Plutonium pneumosclerosis, and showed that differential blood cell counts provided improved diagnostic classification (Claycamp et al. 2000).

Project 2.5: improved dosimetry and risk assessment for Plutonium-Induced lung disease using a microdosimetric approach

the aims of this project were to use archival tissues to measure microscopic distribution of plutonium using stereological and quantitative autoradiography, to quantify plutonium activity in different lung regions and tissues (e.g. conducting airways, parenchyma, pleura, scars, lymphatic tissue), and to investigate how non-uniform distributions might influence dose-related lung cancer risks. The project found that plutonium in lung tissues is not uniformly distributed, with over 40% of the Pu found in parenchyma, most highly concentrated in scars and regions of fibrosis (Romanov et al. 2003). It was determined that longer-term sequestration of plutonium may reduce radiation exposures to critical target cells and tissues (Guilmette et al. 2002).

Project 2.6: molecular markers of lung cancer

the aims of this project were to understand molecular mechanisms and identify biomarkers of lung cancer risk, to determine prevalence for promoter hypermethylation in silencing genes linked with the development of lung cancers (adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma), and identify new biomarkers, based on prevalence of gene methylation, using a cross-sectional study of cancer-free workers exposed to plutonium and an unexposed population. The project discovered a dose response for methylation of the p16 gene (cell cycle regulation) in plutonium workers, with greater methylation of the GATA5, a tumor suppressor gene, in adenocarcinomas from male Mayak workers compared to controls. Similar, but limited, observations were seen in females. It was concluded that inactivation of genes by methylation is important in cell transformation and could be important in the development of adenocarcinoma after plutonium exposures (Belinsky et al. 2004; Lyon et al. 2007).

Project 2.7: radiation biomarkers

the objectives of this project were to develop a biomarker-based dosimetry system for plutonium (≥ 0.3 kBq) and separately for gamma (≥ 30 cGy) exposures, identify stable biomarkers for past radiation exposures, and to identify and compare intra- and inter-chromosomal aberrations in plutonium and gamma ray exposed workers.

Chromosome painting techniques (mFISH, mBAND) identified stable intrachromosomal rearrangements in plutonium exposed workers (Hande et al. 2003). Significant excess of intrachromosomal aberrations in chromosomes 1,2 and 5 in plutonium workers compared to high gamma ray exposed reactor operators and non-Mayak workers not exposed to plutonium (Mitchell et al. 2004). The project concluded that “…stable complex chromosome translocations represent a sensitive, specific, long-lived, quantitative, low-background biomarker of densely ionizing radiation exposure in human populations exposed many years ago” (Hande et al. 2005).

Areas of possible future collaboration

The dosimetric projects for the Mayak Workers and Techa River residents have well developed results. Additional areas being worked on include advanced stochastic dosimetric phantoms for various age groups for strontium in bones for the Techa River doses and application of recently-developed pre-natal dosimetric models. Ongoing efforts to use historical tooth-beta counting data in the individualization of doses to Techa River residents could reduce the uncertainty for a large number of residents now modeled with village averages. The current modeling uses generic parameters for estimating direct exposure to radionuclide contamination of the riverbanks; an improved set of exposure time estimates for individuals in each riverside village has been under development but has not yet been fully implemented.

Expanded use of bioassay data for the workers could include fecal and urinary excretion plus additional efforts to incorporate americium monitoring results into the worker dosimetry. Because not all workers were provided with bioassay monitoring, the finalization of a Job Exposure Matrix would allow the addition of many worker-years of dosimetry results into the epidemiological analyses.

A large cohort of workers with external dose records and some bioassay data are available at the Siberian Chemical Combine in Seversk; a subtask of Project 2.4 could be expanded into a study parallel to that for the Mayak Production Association workers including not only plutonium exposures but also uranium exposures.

The efforts of Project 1.4 to estimate the thyroid doses to residents of Ozersk remain unfinished; the methods have been developed but a complete epidemiological analysis with individualized doses remains to be performed.

Conclusions

The Russian Health Studies projects were a success from the standpoints of collaboration, discovery, and partnership. The researchers collaborated effectively and delivered high-quality, innovative progress that advanced knowledge in radiation health and standards. This level of progress could not have been accomplished by either country alone; it was the collaboration and filling in the needs and gaps of the projects with each country’s strengths and resources that enabled such progress. The achievement of maintaining international collaboration for three decades sets a precedent and template for future international scientific collaborations.
