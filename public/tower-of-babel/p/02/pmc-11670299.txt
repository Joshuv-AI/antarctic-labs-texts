Unveiling interactions mediated by B vitamins between diatoms and their associated bacteria from cocultures
Authors: Cecilia Costas‐Selas, Sandra Martínez‐García, Jarone Pinhassi, Emilio Fernández, Eva Teira
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnveiling the interactions among phytoplankton and bacteria at the level of species requires axenic isolates to experimentally demonstrate their mutual effects. In this study, we describe the interactions among the diatoms Pseudo‐nitzschia granii and Chaetoceros tenuissimus and their associated bacterial species, isolated from surface water of a coastal upwelling system using coculture experiments. Microalgae growth was assessed in axenic monocultures or in coculture with each of their co‐isolated bacteria in the presence or absence of B vitamins. Pseudo‐nitzschia granii growth was limited by B‐vitamin supply, except when cultured with the bacteria Jannaschia cystaugens, which seemed to provide adequate levels of B vitamins to the diatom. Chaetoceros tenuissimus growth was reduced in the absence of B vitamins. Moreover, the growth of C. tenuissimus was stimulated by Alteromonas sp. and Celeribacter baekdonensis during the exponential growth. These results show a diversity of specific interactions between the diatoms and co‐isolated bacteria, ranging from allelopathy to commensalism. Understanding how interactions between phytoplankton and bacteria modulate the structure and function of marine microbial plankton communities will contribute to a greater knowledge of plankton ecology and improve our ability to predict nutrient fluxes in marine ecosystems or the formation of blooms in a context of global change.

INTRODUCTION

Diatoms (Bacillariophyceae) are microscopic, ubiquitous, eukaryotic phytoplankton responsible for about 20% of the photosynthesis on Earth (Armbrust, 2009; Nelson et al., 1995). They generate most of the organic matter that serves as the base of the marine food webs (Armbrust, 2009). Therefore, diatoms not only influence the biological pump and shape the carbon (C) cycle but also play pivotal roles in other elemental cycles such as nitrogen (N), silicon (Si), sulfur (S), and iron (Fe; Armbrust, 2009; Falkowski, 1994). A large portion of the diatom‐derived organic matter is assimilated and remineralized by bacteria (Buchan et al., 2014; Cho & Azam, 1990). Hence, diatoms and bacteria associations critically influence global C, S, and N cycles (Durham et al., 2015; Falkowski, 2012; Field et al., 1998). Despite their biogeochemical importance, there is still much to be understood about interactions among diatoms and bacteria, and their influence on biogeochemical cycling. Thus, the study of these interactions might contribute to a better understanding of diatom responses to environmental disturbances and could be a relevant research line for algal bloom formation (Buchan et al., 2014; Fuhrman et al., 2015; Ramanan et al., 2016; Seymour et al., 2017).

Diatoms are surrounded by the “phycosphere,” a volume in the proximity of the cells rich in organic matter in which bacterial growth and diversity are enhanced and the interactions between diatom and bacterial cells usually occur (Bell & Mitchell, 1972; Labeeuw et al., 2017; Ramanan et al., 2016). A variety of diatom–bacteria interactions have been described, spanning from mutualism and commensalism to parasitism and pathogenicity (Amin et al., 2012; Azam & Malfatti, 2007; Grossart et al., 2005; Joint et al., 2002; Labeeuw et al., 2017; Mayali & Azam, 2004; Meyer et al., 2017). Most of these interactions are mediated by bioactive molecules such as vitamins, hormones, or algaecides (Amin et al., 2015; Labeeuw et al., 2017; Meyer et al., 2017; Shibl et al., 2020).

Approaches based on coculture systems have long been used to study the interactions between microbial populations, including bacteria and phytoplankton (Amin et al., 2015; Cruz & Neuer, 2022). In brief, a coculture is a cultivation setup in which two organisms are grown with or without physical separation (Goers et al., 2014). A range of types of microbial interactions, from mutualistic to pathogenic, have been demonstrated using the coculture approach as a key way to experimentally probe the connection between two species. For example, Amin et al. (2015) demonstrated the beneficial interaction between Pseudo‐nitzschia multiseries and Sulfitobacter sp. SA11, with the diatom providing tryptophan to the bacteria that produce the auxin phytohormone indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA) promoting the diatom growth. In contrast, van Tol et al. (2017) described a marine bacterium able to inhibit diatom growth, and Paul and Pohnert (2011) described algicidal bacteria producing lytic proteases that are toxic for Skeletonema costatum, Conticriba weissflogii, and Phaeodactylum tricornutum. Cruz and Neuer (2022) also used coculture experiments to test if the aggregation and production of transparent exopolymeric particles (TEP) by Minutocellus polymorphus depended on the bacterial species included in the cocultures.

One of the most widely studied mutualistic interactions is that which occurs between bacteria that synthesize vitamins and the phytoplankton that require vitamins for survival (Croft et al., 2006; Grant et al., 2014; Helliwell et al., 2011). Over half of the phytoplankton population is auxotrophic for (unable to synthesize) at least one essential vitamin such as cobalamin (B12), thiamine (B1), or biotin (B7; Croft et al., 2005, 2006; Provasoli, 1974). Bacteria can synthesize these vitamins and sustain phytoplankton growth, and in exchange, bacteria obtain organic carbon. Recent studies have also revealed that many bacteria are also auxotrophic for B vitamins (Sañudo‐Wilhelmy et al., 2014). For example, Bittner et al. (2024) observed that 78% of the evaluated bacterial taxa were auxotrophic for B1, which could be provided by picocyanobacteria. There are many experimental studies demonstrating B‐vitamin trading among microbes. For example, the dinoflagellate Prorocentrum cordatum provides organic carbon and B3 vitamin to the bacteria Dinoroseobacter shibae, and in return, the bacteria provides B1 and B12 to the dinoflagellate (Wagner‐Döbler et al., 2010). Dinoroseobacter is known to provide B12 to Gephyrocapsa huxleyi (as Emiliania huxleyi) in exchange of organic C (Read et al., 2013; Wienhausen et al., 2017). Also, Thalassiosira profunda (auxotrophic for vitamin B12) gets vitamin B12 from Roseobacter pomeroyi, and in return, the bacteria obtains organic C (Croft et al., 2005; Helliwell et al., 2011; Seymour et al., 2017).

The Ría de Vigo is a coastal environment that is productive due to the upwelling pulses of nutrient‐rich waters intermittently occurring from March to September (Figueiras et al., 2002; Fraga, 1981). Previous studies in the area have revealed that the impact of bacteria on phytoplankton is variable and may shift from positive to negative and vice versa depending on the environmental conditions (Justel‐Díez et al., 2023; Prieto et al., 2016). More specifically, Costas‐Selas et al. (2024), using network analyses and antibiotic bioassays with natural communities from the Ría de Vigo, showed that diatoms tended to co‐occur with bacteria from the order Alteromonadales, which were more abundant when the overall impact of bacteria on phytoplankton was positive than when the effect was negative, suggesting positive interactions between these two groups. Although network analyses provide clues about potential specific interactions among microorganisms, only laboratory coculture experiments allow testing hypotheses about the existence, type, and mechanisms of biotic interactions.

In the present study, we carried out coculture experiments with two different species of phytoplankton, the diatoms Chaetoceros tenuissimus and Pseudo‐nitzschia granii, and their co‐associated bacteria, isolated from the Ría de Vigo (NW Spain). These two diatoms are ecologically and economically relevant species in coastal productive waters such as, for example, the Ría de Vigo, where they are particularly dominant during the summer stratification period (Figueiras et al., 2002). Pseudo‐nitzschia spp. produce domoic acid (DA), which may lead to human poisoning cases (Amnesic Shellfish Poisoning, or ASP), mass wildlife mortality, and widespread aquaculture closures on the Galician coast (Stewart et al., 1998; Trainer et al., 2012). Chaetoceros spp. is one of the most abundant and widespread genera of planktonic diatoms and one of the most abundant taxa of pelagic ecosystems (Booth et al., 2002; Malviya et al., 2016). High biomass of Chaetoceros spp. occurs during upwelling relaxation events in the Ría de Vigo and appears to support shellfish aquaculture due to their low sinking rates (Tilstone et al., 2000). Interestingly, the small (3–5 μm in diameter) solitary Chaetoceros tenussimus has been significantly associated with diverse bacteria in co‐occurrence networks in coastal waters in NW Spain (Joglar et al., 2021). Pseudo‐nitzschia granii lacks genes coding for the biosynthesis of B12 vitamin and also lacks the cobalamin‐independent methionine synthase (MetE) gene and, therefore, cannot grow without the external supply of cobalamin (Cohen et al., 2018; Ellis et al., 2017). Nevertheless, the mechanism for B12 vitamin provision remains unexplored. By contrast, to the best of our knowledge, there are no studies specifically reporting B‐vitamin auxotrophy for C. tenuissimus, yet many Chaetoceros species have been reported as auxotrophic for B12 vitamin (Croft et al., 2006).

Within this context, we hypothesized that specific interactions between the isolated diatoms and the co‐isolated bacteria will depend on the presence or absence of B vitamins. We expected that, under B‐vitamin‐deplete conditions, co‐isolated bacteria might provide B vitamins to the diatoms and stimulate their growth compared to axenic B‐vitamin‐deplete diatom cultures. Such beneficial effects might get diluted or even turned into a competitive relationship in a B‐vitamin‐replete medium.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Phytoplankton and bacteria isolation and culture maintenance

Pseudo‐nitzschia granii, Chaetoceros tenuissimus, and co‐associated bacteria were isolated from surface seawater (ca. 1 m). Seawater sample was collected using an acid‐cleaned plastic container close to Toralla island in the Ría de Vigo (42°12′09.4″ N, 8°47′51.8″ W) in October 2019. Phytoplankton isolates were obtained by serial dilution of seawater in a sterile f/2 + Si medium (Figure 1a). The isolated algae were grown in flasks with f/2 + Si medium (Guillard, 2005). Isolated cultures were maintained at 18°C and a 12:12 h light:dark diurnal cycle. Every 10 days, aliquots of cultures were transferred into new f/2 + Si medium to maintain healthy and exponentially growing cultures. Diatom growth was assessed by counting cell abundance using a Neubauer chamber (Nikon‐892800, Japan). A volume of 10 μL of diatom samples was loaded into the Neubauer chamber, and then counting was carried out in the central big square, which was split into 25 square widths of 0.2 mm. The following formula was applied: Concentrationcells⋅mL−1=Number of total cellsNumber of squares×10,000.

Bacteria were isolated by serially diluting aliquots of the nonaxenic monospecific algal cultures following the methodology from Amin et al. (2015; Figure 1b). In brief, diluted aliquots of diatom cultures were plated by spreading them onto Zobell agar plates (Figure 1c). Then, after 5–10 days of incubation, different bacterial colonies were isolated by streaking cells onto Zobell agar plates to isolate pure strains (Figure 1d). Pure isolated bacteria were transferred and maintained in liquid Zobell medium (Figure 1e).

Bacterial cultures were incubated and maintained at 18°C in a 12:12 h light:dark diurnal cycle. Every ~25 days, aliquots of bacterial cultures were transferred into new Zobell medium to maintain healthy and exponentially growing cultures. Bacterial growth was measured by absorbance (ONDA V‐10 plus, Giorgio Bormac, MO, Italy), calibrated with cell abundance obtained using 4′,6‐diamino‐2‐phenylindol (DAPI) staining and a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse TiU; Andersen, 2015). Isolated phytoplankton and bacteria strains were identified by 18S rRNA and 16S rRNA gene sequencing, respectively, as explained in detail in subsection Bacteria and phytoplankton classification.

Preparation of phytoplankton axenic cultures

Axenic algal cultures of Pseudo‐nitzschia granii and Chaetoceros tenuissimus were achieved by combining different methodologies (Amin et al., 2015; Shishlyannikov et al., 2011; Vu et al., 2018; Figures S1–S3 in the Supporting Information). To get axenic Pseudo‐nitzschia granii, approximately 25 mL of a mid‐exponential phase growing culture were collected and sonicated at low ultrasound intensity (40 kHz) for 40 s (Ultrasonic Cleaner, Branson 200; Figure S1a,b). Then, the diatom culture was gravity‐filtered onto a 10‐μm pore‐size polycarbonate membrane filter (Millipore; Figure S1c). Subsequently, cells were washed off from the filter by gentle shaking into sterile f/2 + Si medium containing a concentration of 350 ng · μL−1 of antibiotics mixture (per milliliter: 5000 units Penicillin, 5 mg Streptomycin, and 10 mg Neomycin; SIGMA; Figure S1d). Finally, after 10 days of incubation, the antibiotic‐treated cells were transferred to antibiotic‐free media (Figure S1e).

Axenic Chaetoceros tenuissimus was obtained using single‐cell sorting by flow cytometry that allows sorting and isolating of cells according to their physical and biological features (Marie et al., 2017). This technique was carried out with a FACSAriaIII flow cytometer with a laser emitting at 488 nm (Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA, USA). To this end, 2 mL of a mid‐exponential phase growing culture were collected (Figure S2a), sonicated in a low‐intensity ultrasound for 40 s, and sorted into 96‐well plates containing 250 μL of sterile f/2 + Si medium containing a concentration of 350 ng · μL−1 of an antibiotic mixture (5000 units Penicillin, 5 mg Streptomycin, and 10 mg Neomycin; SIGMA; Figure S2b,c). One thousand cells were sorted into the first three wells, 100 cells into the next three wells, and one cell into the remaining wells. After 10 days of incubation, living cells of C. tenuissimus were observed in wells with 100 and 1000 cells. The cells were transferred to a flask with 20 mL of f/2 + Si medium free of antibiotics (Figure S2d).

The axenic cultures were verified and routinely checked for the presence of bacteria by planting on Zobell agar plates, by microscopic inspection of DAPI‐stained cells under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse TIU inverted microscope), and by cell counting with flow cytometry (CytoFLEX S, Beckman Coulter, CA, USA; Andersen, 2015; Figures S1f, S2e, S3). Bacteria were not detected using any of the approaches.

Coculture experiments

A set of coculture experiments were carried out with axenic Pseudo‐nitzschia granii, anexic Chaetoceros tenuissimus, and their co‐isolated bacteria in the presence or the absence of B vitamins (B1, B12, and B7). Prior to the coculture experiments, duplicates of axenic P. granii and C. tenuisimus cultures were depleted of B vitamins. This was done by transferring aliquots of original axenic cultures to f/2 + Si medium without B‐vitamin supplementation and by maintaining the new cultures under the same conditions as described above. These transfers were repeated three times.

To examine the growth of Pseudo‐nitzschia granii with and without their co‐isolated bacteria and with and without B‐vitamin supplementation, eight treatments, in triplicate, were defined (Figure 2). The same experimental design was repeated with the axenic Chaetoceros tenuissimus strain (Figure 3).

All experiments were run in triplicate at 18°C and under a 12:12 h light:dark diurnal cycle over 11 days in f/2 + Si medium, with an initial diatom cell density of ~104 cells · mL−1 in early exponential phase. F/2 medium was prepared with filtered and autoclaved seawater from the Ría de Vigo. Joglar et al. (2021) reported consistently low values of B12 vitamin in nearby locations. Bacterium cells growing in a liquid Zobell medium and at the beginning of the exponential phase were inoculated at a final density of ~105 cells · mL−1 for the coculture experiments. A small amount of Zobell (150–250 μL, depending on the initial bacterial abundance) was transferred to the phytoplankton culture. Samples were collected daily for cell abundance. Cell abundance was determined by flow cytometry. Furthermore, every 2 days, 2 mL were collected from one of the triplicates (randomly selected), then fixed and stained with DAPI to check for potential bacterial contamination by fluorescence microscopy (Nikon Eclipse TiU).

Bacterial and phytoplankton cell abundance

Bacterial abundance and phytoplankton abundance were determined with a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA) with a laser emitting at 488 nm. Water samples (1.75 mL) were preserved with 1% (vol/vol) and 0.05% (vol/vol) of paraformaldehyde and glutaraldehyde solution, respectively, and stored at −80°C after being flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen. Prior to analysis, cells were stained with 10% (vol/vol) SYBR Green DNA fluorochrome (Gasol & Del Giorgio, 2000).

Exponential growth rate

The exponential growth rate (EGR) was calculated using datapoints from the time interval of exponential phase following the equation: EGR=lnNt/N0Δt.

In the equation, N 0 is the population size at the beginning of a time interval, N t is the population size at the end of the time interval, and Δt is the length of the time interval (t t – t 0).

Stains with DAPI

To stain samples with DAPI, 2 mL of culture samples were collected and fixed with formaldehyde (2% final concentration) for 2 h. Afterward, the samples were filtered through 0.2‐μm pore‐size polycarbonate filters in a 12‐funnel sampling manifold filter holder (Millipore). Cells retained in the filter were stained with DAPI solution (final concentration 1 μg · mL−1). The DAPI solution contains 5.5 parts of Citifluor (Citifluor), 1 part of Vectashield (Vector Laboratories), and 0.5 parts of phosphate‐buffered saline solution with DAPI stained preparation (50 μg · mL−1). The stained samples were examined with a Nikon Eclipse TiU inverted microscope (Nikon Instruments, Japan). The excitation wavelength for DAPI was 378/11 nm.

Bacteria and phytoplankton classification

For the identification of the isolated phytoplankton and co‐associated bacteria, ~10–20 mL of culture of each diatom and bacteria strain at mid‐exponential growth were filtered through 0.2‐μm pore‐size polycarbonate filters (Millipore) and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and conserved at −80°C. DNA from the bioassays, isolated phytoplankton, and bacteria strains was extracted with DNeasy® PowerWater® Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer's protocol. The DNA concentration was quantified using Qubit 2.0. fluorometer and the Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Thermo Fischer Scientific, MA, USA). The identification of isolated bacteria was assessed by sequencing the V4–V5 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene, while the taxonomic identities of the isolated phytoplankton by the V4 region of the 18S rRNA gene. To amplify the genes, F515/R926 universal primers were used for bacteria (Parada et al., 2016) and TAReuk454FWD1 and TAReukREV3 for phytoplankton (Logares et al., 2014). Both genes were sequenced using Illumina™ MiSeq platform (paired‐end reads, 2 × 250 bp) at Fasteris laboratory (Geneva, Switzerland).

The 16S rRNA and 18S rRNA gene sequences for taxonomic analysis were processed following the bioinformatic pipeline in Logares (2017). Raw reads were corrected by Bayes Hammer (Nikolenko et al., 2013) following the Schirmer et al. (2015) method. Subsequently, paired‐end reads were merged with PEAR (Zhang et al., 2014), and the longer sequences (>200 bp) were quality‐checked and dereplicated using USEARCH (Edgar, 2010). Operational taxonomic unit (OTU) abundances were acquired by mapping back reads to OTUs at 99% similarity. SILVA (release 138) and PR2BN+ databases were used to align and classify phytoplankton sequences, and only SILVA (release 138) was used for bacteria sequences.

Statistical analysis

To test normal distribution, the Shapiro–Wilk test (n < 50) and Kolmogórov–Smirnov test (n > 50) were carried out. The differences in cell abundances and bacterial abundances among treatments were analyzed by the Friedman test, which is the nonparametric equivalent to repeated measures analysis of variance (RMANOVA). To identify which pairs of treatments were significantly different, a Durbin‐Conover post hoc test was performed across all time points with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple hypothesis testing (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). Differences in the EGR between treatments were analyzed by ANOVA, and then post hoc tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction were conducted for pairwise comparisons of the treatments (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). All the univariate statistical tests were performed with the stats, nortest, and PCMRplus R packages (Gross & Ligges, 2015; Pohlert, 2014).

RESULTS

Coculture of axenic Pseudo‐nitzschia granii with bacteria

The cell abundance of Pseudo‐nitzschia granii differed significantly (Friedman test, χ2(7) = 191.81, p < 0.001) depending on the treatments (i.e., axenic growth or growth with different bacteria; Figure 4). The abundance of the axenic P. granii was significantly limited by B‐vitamin supply, since a significantly higher abundance of cells was present in the B‐vitamin treatment than in the B‐vitamin‐deplete treatment (Durbin‐Conover pairwise test, p < 0.01; Figure 4). The abundance of P. granii in coculture with the different bacteria and B‐vitamin supply was not significantly different from the abundance in the axenic B‐vitamin‐replete culture (Durbin‐Conover pairwise tests, p > 0.05; Figure 4). Interestingly, Jannaschia cystaugens significantly enhanced the diatom abundance (compared with the corresponding axenic control) in the treatment without B‐vitamin supply, while no significant differences were observed between the cell abundance of P. granii cocultured with J. cystaugens with and without B‐vitamin supply (Durbin‐Conover pairwise test, p > 0.05; Figure 4).

The EGR of Pseudo‐nitzschia granii was also significantly different between treatments (ANOVA, F 7,16 = 23.25, p < 0.05; Figure 5). In the treatments with B‐vitamin supply, P. granii had significantly higher EGR than in the corresponding B‐vitamin‐deplete treatments (post hoc test, p < 0.05; Figure 5). DISCUSSION

This study represents a step forward toward understanding the interactions between Pseudo‐nitzschia granii and Chaetoceros tenuissimus and their coexisting bacterial species. Previous studies of co‐occurrence network analysis derived from field samples, outside of the Ría de Vigo (Costas‐Selas et al., 2023) and from experiments, inside the Ria de Vigo (Costas‐Selas et al., 2024) have reported that Bacillariophyceae (such as Pseudo‐nitzschia sp. and Chaetoceros sp.) often tend to be positively associated with Rhodobacteraceae and Alteromonadales (Costas‐Selas et al., 2023, 2024). In contrast, in the network analyses, Pseudo‐nitzschia sp. displayed a co‐exclusion pattern with specific bacteria that was corroborated in coculture experiments in which the presence of the diatom excluded some bacterial species (i.e., Marinobacter sp. and Jannaschya cystaugens). Therefore, the present study adds support to our observations of the potential specific associations between phytoplankton and bacteria obtained from co‐occurrence network analyses in natural samples.

Pseudo‐nitzschia granii

Our results are in alignment with the previously described B12 auxotrophy of Pseudo‐nitzschia granii; however, this phenomenon extends beyond this genus (Croft et al., 2006; Ellis et al., 2017; Tang et al., 2010). B‐vitamin auxotrophy is widespread among other algal species; for example, diatoms such as Phaedactylon tricornutum or Gephyrocapsa huxleyi also show B12 auxotrophy (Croft et al., 2005; Helliwell et al., 2011). Also, many harmful algal bloom species are vitamin B1 and B12 auxotrophs, such as Scrippsiella trochoidea, Prorocentrum minimum, or Gymnodinium aureolum (Tang et al., 2010). Our data also suggest that the bacteria Althererythrobacter sp. is a likely auxotroph for at least one of the B vitamins included in this study (B1, B7, and B12). This agrees with previous studies that showed the genomes of these bacteria lack the core genes coding for cobalamin synthesis (Zheng et al., 2019). It is important to note that auxotrophy should be definitively confirmed by performing resupply experiments as previously described (Ellis et al., 2017; Tang et al., 2010).

Remarkably, our results strongly suggest that Jannaschya cystaugens might provide adequate levels of B vitamins to Pseudo‐nitzschia granii. This result is in accordance with previous molecular studies that showed that several members of the Roseobacter clade encode genes included in the vitamin B12 biosynthesis pathway (Luo & Moran, 2014; Sañudo‐Wilhelmy et al., 2014). Consequently, J. cystaugens may supply B12 in exchange for dissolved organic carbon (DOC) as has been observed in other associations (Durham et al., 2015; Sañudo‐Wilhelmy et al., 2014; Wagner‐Döbler et al., 2010). It is important to note that the B12 requirements of P. granii seem to be fulfilled even at relatively low (initial abundance 2.9 × 105 cells · mL−1) and even decreasing (final abundance 0.0 cells · mL−1) J. cystaugens abundances given that the result of the interaction was negative for this bacterial species. A recent study reported that the amount of intracellular cobalamin molecules in prototrophic bacteria largely varied from <1000 to >26,000 per cell (Sultana et al., 2023). B12 requirements per cell are highly variable in diatoms, ranging from 0.6 to 6800 nmol B12 · mol−1 C (Taylor & Sullivan, 2008), and thus, even at low bacterial densities, the provision of vitamin B12 molecules could be enough to support diatom growth. The negative impact of P. granii on J. cystaugens might be related to the potential for P. granii to produce polyunsaturated aldehydes (PUAs) or domoic acid (DA), which might inhibit and eventually lyse J. cystaugens cells, releasing intracellular metabolites such as B vitamins to the medium (Ribalet et al., 2008). However, a recent review questioned the allelopathic potential of DA on bacterial growth (Bates et al., 2018). Moreover, P. granii appears to have a full suite of cobalamin‐salvage genes (Coale et al., 2022; Cohen et al., 2018; Vancaester et al., 2020), which would allow the diatom to remodel degraded cobalamin compounds released as the bacterial cells lyse.

Marinobacter sp. did not stimulate the growth of Pseudo‐nitzschia granii in the B‐vitamin‐deplete medium, which is also in agreement with a recent report on the absence of B12 synthesis genes in a Marinobacter species (Xia et al., 2021). Interestingly, coculturing with P. granii was harmful for Marinobacter sp., and Jannaschya cystaugens (as discussed above), although it was favorable or neutral for Altererythrobacter sp., suggesting that the production of potential inhibitory substances by Pseudo‐nitzschia spp. (e.g., DA and PUAs), if present, could act in a selective manner (Bates et al., 2018; Guannel et al., 2011; Ribalet et al., 2008).

Chaetoceros tenuissimus

Chaetoceros tenuissimus growth was lower in the treatments without a supply of B vitamins than in those with them; this could be related to the potential impact of low B1, B12, and B7 availability on a wide range of cellular functions. B1 plays a crucial role in intermediary C metabolism because the active form of the vitamin is thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP); B7 is a coenzyme involved in carboxylase enzymes, including acetyl coenzyme A (CoA) carboxylase, which is required in fatty acid synthesis; and B12 is cobalt‐containing compound, which is used in amino acid synthesis and C resupply to the Krebs cycle (Croft et al., 2006; Dowling et al., 2012; Matthews et al., 2003; Schowen, 1998; Streit & Entcheva, 2003; Wildiers, 1901). Yet, in this case, the diatom grew relatively well without the addition of B vitamins. Chaetoceros tenuissimus may have developed alternative pathways for methionine synthesis such as the expression of cobalamin‐independent methionine synthase, MetE (Coale et al., 2022; Helliwell et al., 2011; Johnson et al., 2020), as has been observed for other Chaetoceros species (Ellis et al., 2017). Our results suggest that C. tenuissimus is not auxotrophic for any of the added B vitamins, yet more transfers in B‐vitamin‐deplete medium and further study would be necessary to fully corroborate their B‐vitamin requirements.

None of the accompanying bacteria seemed to provide B vitamins to Chaetoceros tenuissimus. Nevertheless, a Celeribacter baekdonensis strain appears to be able to produce B12 (Sultana et al., 2023), and Alteromonas sp. could provide B1 and B7 to the diatom, but not B12 since members from this genus appear to lack the genes for B12 synthesis (Sañudo‐Wilhelmy et al., 2014; Wienhausen et al., 2022). Although not significant, due to the variability of one of the replicas, Alteromonas sp. cell abundance was lower in the treatment without B vitamins. Even if the isolated bacteria were prototrophic for the required B vitamins, they could be unable to release the vitamin into the surrounding environment, as recently shown for many B12‐prototrophic bacteria (Sultana et al., 2023). By contrast, both Alteromonas sp. and Celeribacter baekdonensis significantly increased the EGR of C. tenuissimus, suggesting that the production of bacterial secondary metabolites, such as, for example, diketopiperazines (DKPs; cyclic peptides) or IAA, could have stimulated the diatom growth rate (Amin et al., 2015; Sittmann et al., 2021; Sultana et al., 2023). Interestingly, Sultana et al. (2023) also observed that Celeribacter baekdonensis did not provide B12 but stimulated the growth of Thalassiosira pseudonana.

Finally, the lower cell abundance of Celeribacter baekdonensis in the experiments with Chaetoceros tenuissimus in the presence of B vitamins may reflect nutrient competition with the algae toward the end of the experiment (when most nutrients in the medium would have been already consumed), as it reached higher abundances in the presence of B vitamins (Amin et al., 2012; Fuhrman et al., 2015; Joint et al., 2002).

CONCLUSIONS

The results of this study showed that the algae Pseudo‐nitzschia granii and the bacteria Altererythrobacter sp. appear to be auxotrophic for one or a combination of B1, B12, and/or B7, and the algae Chaetoceros tenuissimus likely has molecular strategies to thrive in the absence of an external supply of B vitamins. Bacterial growth was also influenced by the diatoms. Thus, P. granii could have produced antibacterial substances with a negative effect on J. cystaugens and Marinobacter sp. Conversely, Jannaschya cystaugens could provide adequate levels of B vitamins to P. granii in absence of an external supply. The growth of C. tenuissimus showed potential commensalistic interactions with Alteromonas sp. and Celeribacter during exponential growth, which turned into competitive interactions at the end of the experiments.

Our study moved forward to the next step in the investigation of microbial interactions among common and relevant species in a relevant productive coastal ecosystem by experimentally exploring specific interactions between Pseudo‐nitzschia granii and Chaetoceros tenuisimus and co‐isolated bacteria. Our results lay the groundwork for new ecological hypotheses, and the next steps will be to unravel the molecular basis of the mechanisms behind the observed interactions. In this effort, metatransciptomic, metaproteomic, and metabolomic approaches could help to identify the mechanisms involved and/or the metabolites exchanged in these phytoplankton–bacteria interactions. We believe that this information is relevant for gaining a comprehensive understanding of the role of biotic factors as driving forces of the complex structure and functioning of marine microbial communities.
