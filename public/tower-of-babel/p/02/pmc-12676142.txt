Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is a highly advanced noninvasive diagnostic tool used extensively for evaluating and monitoring cardiovascular disease. It is recognized as a key component in the diagnostic strategies outlined by major medical organizations, with two-thirds of the guidelines from the American Heart Association and the American College of Cardiology incorporating CMR.1 The 2021 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology chest pain guidelines specifically highlight 15 indications for using CMR in the context of chest pain syndromes, underscoring its importance in this area.2

Project Rationale

To enhance the widespread adoption and accessibility of CMR, maintaining its efficient use is essential. Several factors contribute to the efficiency of CMR, ranging from the process of image acquisition to the analysis of these images. Patient cooperation is also vital, particularly in adhering to breath-holding instructions during the scan, which can be challenging. Historically, CMR scans have been known for their lengthy duration, which can be a barrier to their use. Additionally, the availability of cardiac-capable MRI scanners and the presence of expert-trained personnel, such as cardiac-trained MRI technicians and imaging physicians, are crucial for the effective use of CMR. Addressing these factors can lead to more efficient use of CMR, making it more accessible to patients and health care providers alike. The aim of this project was to improve CMR efficiency by optimizing image acquisition time and clinical workflow using pathology-driven protocols and sequences tailored to the most likely clinical diagnosis while maintaining diagnostic accuracy.

Take-Home Message

•Streamlining the clinical workflow from patient preparation to standardized protocol-driven image acquisition and interpretation, collaboration between cardiology and radiology teams, and maximizing AI technology for remote patient scanning improves CMR efficiency, reduces barriers to patient access to CMR, increases patient throughput, and can enhance diagnostic accuracy, which leads to better patient outcomes.

Project Description

The intervention was initiated during August 2024 primarily at the University of Texas Medical Branch (UTMB), Galveston campus and was later expanded to 2 additional satellite campuses. Before every CMR scan, each patient's chart was reviewed by a level III fellowship-trained advanced cardiac imaging cardiologist, who noted the indication for the CMR study, the patient's clinical presentation and past medical history, and relevant laboratory and imaging tests (electrocardiograms, echocardiograms, nuclear cardiac imaging studies, computed tomography studies, and cardiac catheterizations).

A standardized pathology-based protocol using the Society of Cardiac Magnetic Resonance recommendations3 was provided to the MRI technologist, with real-time modifications as needed in the setting of suspected dual pathology. A pathology-based CMR protocol employs a structured approach to image acquisition and analysis. This protocol is tailored to specific cardiac conditions and uses a combination of noncontrast and contrast-enhanced sequences to prioritize image quality and diagnostic accuracy.4 Protocols were saved on the scanner.

Both in-person and remote supervision of image acquisition was performed by the advanced imaging cardiologist. Remote scanning across multiple sites was implemented using Phillips Radiology Operating Command Center (ROCC),5 a multivendor, multimodality, multisite, safe and secure remote imaging solution that enables imaging experts to seamlessly interact with scanner-facing technologists through chat, voice, and video at scan locations across the organization, all while concurrently accessing scanner console screens to view and edit examinations. CMR image postprocessing was performed in real-time using Suiteheart (Neosoft), an artificial intelligence–driven automated CMR software program that incorporates an intuitive, easy-to-use interface for reviewing and reporting CMR.6

The study period to observe a difference in scan duration before versus after the change in CMR protocol was January 1, 2024, to July 31, 2024 (preintervention), compared with September 1, 2024, to March 30, 2025 (postintervention). CMR scans were reviewed for time stamps in the picture archiving and communication system (PACS) that was stored in each patient's electronic medical record. The CMR series start time was recorded from the first image, which was either a 3-plane localizer, or from the first corresponding axial scout scan. The end time was obtained from the last image in the series. Scan duration was recorded as the time between the first and last acquired image. Because of the absence of localizer images in the PACS before the intervention, scan times starting from matching axial scout images were used as the start times for similar time comparison in both groups.

Project Deliverables

The primary outcomes of the project were the mean scan duration of CMR with and without contrast compared between the pre- and postintervention groups. Two additional desired outcomes of the project were an improvement in diagnostic image quality and an increase in the CMR volume as a direct result of shorter scan times, which would avail more patient scanning slots on the shared scanner and increase patient access at all 3 UTMB campuses

Project Outcome and Impact

After excluding incomplete series, 343 CMR scans were reviewed, including 293 scans with gadolinium contrast and 50 scans without contrast. There were 117 scans in the preintervention group versus 226 scans in the postintervention group. The characteristics of the scanned patients were similar across both groups, including age (56.2 ± 16.9 years), body mass index (29.5 ± 7.4 kg/m2), and sex (47.5% female, n = 163). This finding of significantly shorter postintervention scan times was observed in both male patients (61 ± 22.6 minutes postintervention vs 94.7 ± 32.6 minutes preintervention, P < 0.0001) and female patients (60.8 ± 21.9 minutes postintervention vs 94.8 ± 35.5 minutes preintervention, P < 0.0001). There was no significant difference in scan times with respect to patient sex, age, or body mass index. A 2-way ANOVA showed that scan type had statistically significant effects on scan time, with shorter mean scan time for CMR without contrast (52.9 ± 25.9 minutes postintervention vs 75.8 ± 44.05 minutes preintervention) compared with CMR with contrast (63.1 ± 21.2 minutes postintervention vs 97.4 ± 32 minutes preintervention) (F = 12.64, P = 0.0004). Our institutional data analysis using Current Procedural Terminology revenue codes (75557 and 75561) showed a 32% increase in monthly CMR scan volume (up to 23 examinations postintervention vs 18 examinations preintervention) at our main Galveston campus and a 200% increase at our Clear Lake campus (6 examinations postintervention vs 2 examinations preintervention). In addition, we launched a CMR service line at our League City campus, offering 12 CMR examinations a month.

Discussion

Studies have demonstrated that shorter CMR scan times can be achieved through techniques such as compressed sensing, artificial intelligence–assisted scans, and ultra-fast protocols. These methods effectively reduce scan time, enhance patient comfort, and increase throughput.7,8

Rather than relying on a one-size-fits-all approach, a pathology-driven CMR protocol customizes imaging sequences and acquisitions to align precisely with the differential diagnosis.9 This tailored method enhances diagnostic accuracy by focusing on the unique characteristics of different cardiac pathologies. For instance, in cases of suspected myocarditis, the protocol prioritizes T2-weighted imaging to assess myocardial edema and late gadolinium enhancement to detect fibrosis or scarring. The availability of the imaging physician to modify the imaging strategy in real time, especially when unusual findings are detected, ensures that each patient receives the most relevant and comprehensive evaluation. This collaboration also provides learning opportunities for the MRI technologist to appreciate cardiac pathology and progress toward independent level III CMR technologist competency.

Our intervention led to shorter CMR scan times, which led to higher patient throughput. A key component of this success was thorough comprehensive patient assessments to ensure optimal readiness for the procedure, minimizing delays and reducing the likelihood of repeat scans due to unforeseen pathologies. The availability of the imaging cardiologist to review and process images in real time allowed modification and termination of the study once the clinical question had been answered, thus saving time. The streamlined pathology-driven workflow directly enabled us to expand patient access to multiple campuses. CMR has been shown to affect patient management in various care settings up to 71.5% of the time,10 which can lead to lower costs and better outcomes. Although we did not objectively measure the changes in image quality or diagnostic accuracy between preintervention and postintervention, anecdotally both image quality and diagnostic accuracy were substantially improved.

Conclusions

Implementation of pathology-driven CMR scanning protocols, thorough prescan preparation, simultaneous supervision and image acquisition modifications by an advanced cardiac imaging cardiologist, and MRI technologist education on cardiac pathology successfully reduced CMR scan duration, increased patient volumes, and improved patient access.
