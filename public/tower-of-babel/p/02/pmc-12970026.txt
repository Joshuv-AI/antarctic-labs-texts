New Zealand Electronic Health Data for Cardiovascular Research: A Review
Authors: Anna E Møller, Rod Jackson, Andrew J Kerr, Susan Wells, Katrina K Poppe, Morten Schmidt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAotearoa New Zealand is among the few countries that routinely collect and utilize individual-level, linked electronic health data for cardiovascular research. This comprehensive health data infrastructure supports large-scale registry-based research, including observational studies and pragmatic randomized clinical trials embedded within registries.ObjectiveIn this review, we evaluate the scope and utility of New Zealand’s electronic health data for cardiovascular research. First, we present an overview of the demographics and epidemiological trends in cardiovascular disease within New Zealand. Second, we describe the primary health data sources used in cardiovascular research and assess their coverage, content, linkage capabilities, and data quality. Finally, we explore the future potential of these data assets to advance cardiovascular research, highlighting exemplar applications and key ethical, legal, and infrastructural considerations. We also compare New Zealand’s electronic health data with those of the Nordic countries and the United Kingdom, whose well-established registry systems offer valuable insights for informing New Zealand’s approach to registry-based research and suggest opportunities for future cross-country collaborative research.ResultsCardiovascular research in New Zealand is informed by a combination of nationwide administrative registries, clinical quality registries, and researcher-initiated registries, all linkable via a unique health identifier. These data sources provide comprehensive information on healthcare services, clinical practice, and broader social determinants such as socioeconomic conditions. While data quality and completeness vary, several collections, particularly large researcher-initiated cohorts, demonstrate high utility for observational studies, registry-enabled clinical trials, risk prediction modelling, and quality improvement initiatives. Additionally, New Zealand’s health data infrastructure demonstrates the potential for international cardiovascular research.ConclusionNew Zealand’s electronic health data represent a valuable resource for cardiovascular research, with applicability to both observational studies and clinical trials.Keywords: New Zealand, cardiovascular diseases, epidemiology, healthcare system, population health, data collection

Introduction

The combination of tax-supported universal healthcare, nationwide population-based registries, and individual-level data linkage using personal identifiers is rare, yet particularly valuable for registry-based research.1 In the Northern Hemisphere, such features are available in the Nordic countries and the United Kingdom (UK).1 In the Southern Hemisphere, this combination of features only applies to New Zealand.2 Still, whereas the Nordic countries, particularly Denmark, and the UK have a long tradition of reviewing health registries for their content, data quality, and research potential,3–6 New Zealand has only conducted registry-based research in recent decades. Thus, reviews of the range of electronic health data sources in New Zealand are virtually non-existent, and no overview of the health data available for cardiovascular research exists. We therefore reviewed existing health registry data available for cardiovascular research in New Zealand, providing an overview of their content, data quality, application, and future potential.

Demographic Profile

With an estimated population of 5.3 million in 2024,7 New Zealand is marked by significant ethnic diversity shaped by historical and contemporary immigration, primarily from Britain, the Pacific Islands, and more recently from South and East Asia.8 The population comprises six major ethnic groups: 68% European, 18% Māori (the indigenous population), 17% Asian, 8.9% Pacific Peoples, 1.9% Middle Eastern, Latin American, African, and 1.1% as other ethnicities.9 However, these proportions vary across age groups. In particular, the ethnic composition among adults and older individuals—those with the highest incidence and prevalence of cardiovascular disease (CVD), and therefore the primary focus of cardiovascular research—differs from that of the total population. Specifically, older age groups have higher proportions of individuals of European descent than other age groups.10 The ethnicity of New Zealand residents is strongly associated with socioeconomic status, depicting the evident inequity in the country.11 Residents of European and Asian descent generally have the highest average annual household equivalized disposable income, whereas Māori and Pacific peoples have the lowest annual income.12 Ethnic disparities in the health status of New Zealanders, particularly in cardiovascular health, are well documented.13

Cardiovascular Disease Burden

In Aotearoa New Zealand, CVD accounts for approximately 188,000 disability-adjusted life years, representing 14% of the total disease burden, and causes around 11,000 deaths annually.14 Ischemic heart disease is the leading contributor to health loss and the primary cause of death in the general population.15 Each year, the CVD burden imposes substantial costs on the healthcare system, estimated at NZ$3.3 billion.16 Moreover, the CVD burden among Māori is more than 1.5 times higher than that of non-Māori individuals, with an earlier age of onset.17,18 These disparities emphasize the importance of continued research in CVD, particularly through large-scale registry-based studies, given the complex, chronic, and multifactorial nature of the disease.

National Data Access and Linkage

Aggregated summary statistics of national health administrative data (eg, hospitalization rates and mortality) are publicly accessible.19 For more targeted and in-depth analyses, researchers must obtain Ethics Committee approval to access data in either an identifiable format or preferably via encrypted individual health identifiers.20 Most cardiovascular research conducted in New Zealand has used encrypted identifiers to facilitate linkage across multiple national health administrative registries (Figure 1).

A unique alphanumeric code from the National Health Index (NHI) is assigned to every individual upon entry into New Zealand’s health services (approximately 95% of the population)21 and is recorded at most healthcare contacts.22 The NHI contains key demographic and administrative information, including name, gender, address, geocode, New Zealand residency status, ethnicity, and dates of birth and death.23 This identifier enables deterministic linkage, ie, exact linkage of records at the individual level, across multiple national health administrative registries (described later in this review).22 For research purposes, the code is de-identified by encrypting the NHI and removing direct identifiers such as name and address.22

In 2011, Statistics New Zealand (Stats NZ) established the Integrated Data Infrastructure (IDI), a large research database comprising microdata on individuals and households linked using both deterministic and probabilistic methods.24,25 Probabilistic linkage compares demographic variables across records from different datasets and assigns a weighted probability that the records belong to the same individual.25 The IDI includes most national health administrative registries, the five-yearly National Census, data from other government agencies (ie, on education, housing, income and work, benefits and social services, the general population, and justice), as well as government-led questionnaire-based social and socioeconomic surveys.24,26 This resource provides longitudinal and linked data on all “ever residents” of New Zealand, supporting both whole-population studies and subgroup-specific studies across multiple sectors.24 The IDI is updated up to four times annually, incorporating new datasets and revising existing ones.24 However, the IDI does not include the NHI, which prevents direct linkage to researcher-initiated registries and other prospective studies, thereby limiting its utility in clinical research.27

Health Data for Cardiovascular Research

Personal health-related information on New Zealand residents is routinely recorded across several domains and can be categorized into three main types of data collections: (1) administrative registries, (2) clinical quality registries, and (3) researcher-initiated registries (Table 1). These data are generally sourced from hospitals, pharmacies, laboratories, disease cohorts, and administrative records on ethnicity and socioeconomic status. While general practice enrollment is identifiable, clinical data from general practices are not currently accessible, except through bespoke CVD risk assessment software such as the PREDICT platform28 or via direct request to Primary Health Organisations (PHOs). In the following sections, we elaborate on the data sources available since 2000, specifically examining their applicability to cardiovascular research.

Administrative Registries

Administrative registriesare typically collected for purposes other than research, such as accounting, service delivery, and policy monitoring. Nevertheless, these datasets play a crucial role in registry-based and clinical research, owing to their broad scope, consistency, and population-level coverage. Below, we describe the key administrative registries used for cardiovascular research in New Zealand, as presented in Table 1.

Hospital Data

The National Minimum Dataset (NMDS), sometimes referred to as the National Hospitalisation Dataset, comprises a nationwide collection of public and private hospital discharge data, forming the foundation for statistical analyses of national and provider-level trends.29 The dataset contains limited clinical information, including coded procedures and discharge data, for both short- and long-term inpatients, covering public hospitals since 1988 and private hospitals since 1997.29 Each public hospital in New Zealand is responsible for uploading coded discharge data to the NMDS within 21 days following the month of discharge, ensuring timely updates.29 In contrast, discharge data from privately funded events may be uploaded with a delay.29 NMDS data are synchronized annually with the Hospital events web tool, which provides interactive graphs and tables.30,31 In New Zealand, hospital discharge coding and the assignment of underlying cause of death follow the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision, Australian Modification (ICD-10-AM), and the World Health Organization’s ICD Rules and Guidelines for Mortality Coding.32

Additional hospital data, such as outpatient secondary events, emergency department visits, specialty clinic contacts, and non-attendance records for all doctor-run clinics, are stored in a separate dataset: the National Non-Admitted Patient Collection.33 This collection includes data from 1 July 2006 onwards and is continuously updated, with patient data submitted within 20 days following the month of the event.33

Mortality Data

The underlying cause of death is classified and recorded in the Mortality Collection, which contains data on all deaths and registerable stillbirths in New Zealand since 1988.32 Electronic registration data for deaths and stillbirths are obtained fortnightly from Births, Deaths, and Marriages,34 and matched with death certificates received daily and coroners’ reports received weekly.32 The Mortality data web tool links all-cause and cause-specific mortality data (for common causes of death) to demographic variables such as sex, age, and ethnicity.35 Unlike hospital discharge coding, which is the responsibility of individual hospitals, mortality coding is performed centrally by a single coding team, previously based at the Ministry of Health and now operating under Te Whatu Ora (Health New Zealand).32

Primary Care Enrollment Data

The Primary Health Organisation Enrolment Collection contains nationwide data on patient enrollment in primary care services.36 Established in 2005 for monitoring and research purposes, the dataset is updated monthly with data from all Primary Health Organisation Enrolment Registers.36

Prescription Data

The Pharmaceutical Collection, maintained by Te Whatu Ora (Health New Zealand), contains nationwide data on subsidized medications dispensed by community pharmacists since 1 July 1992.37 In New Zealand, the most prescribed medications are either fully or partially subsidized. Pharmacists submit reimbursement claims to the national health service upon dispensing each prescription, resulting in a largely complete dataset.37 However, newer unsubsidized medications may not be fully represented. The data primarily originate from the Sector Operations’ General Transaction Processing System, with additional drug-related information sourced from Stats NZ (ie, geographical data and census data), Sector Operations (ie, provider reference data), and the Pharmaceutical Management Agency (Pharmac) (ie, the pharmaceutical schedule and associated mappings).37 The pharmaceutical schedule includes details on government-subsidized pharmaceuticals, subsidy levels, supplier pricing, and access conditions.38 The Pharmaceutical Collection is updated monthly with data from Sector Operations and Pharmac.37 Notably, over-the-counter and in-hospital bulk purchases are not included.39

Laboratory Data

Laboratory test claims information is retrievable from the Laboratory Claims Collection, established in 2000 for monitoring purposes, specifically to support surveillance of primary care test subsidies.40 Most laboratory tests are fully subsidized, and laboratory organizations submit claims for virtually every patient tested in the community. While national laboratory claims data do not include actual test results, the VAREANZ (Vascular Risk Equity in Adult New Zealanders or Vascular Risk Equity in Aotearoa NZ) group (Table 1) has maintained copies of all NHI-linked biochemistry and hematology test results relevant to CVD. Specifically, test results have been maintained from the northern regional community laboratory providers since 2012, and from all other community laboratories across New Zealand from 2012 to mid-2024.41

New Zealand Virtual Diabetes Register

The Virtual Diabetes Register, established by the Ministry of Health in 200942 and now managed by Te Whatu Ora (Health New Zealand),43 is a tool used to estimate and monitor annual national and regional diabetes prevalences. It also supports clinical quality improvement efforts by integrating health data related to diabetes, including hospital inpatient and outpatient data derived from clinical records, laboratory test claims, and pharmaceutical dispensing data.42 The registry is updated annually at the end of March with data on patients identified as having diabetes who are alive and enrolled in a PHO during the preceding calendar year ending on 31 December.42 These data are made publicly available via the Virtual Diabetes Register web tool.44

Ethnicity Data

As ethnicity is self-identified and therefore considered a non-fixed variable, such data are collected in the context of each health event.45 Up to three ethnic group codes can be recorded for each contact with healthcare services in New Zealand.45 Notably, the data collection procedures used in the health sector mirror those employed in the NZ Census.45

Socioeconomic Data

The New Zealand Index of Deprivation (NZDep), established in 1995, serves as a key source of area-based socioeconomic deprivation data (via geocoding) for research purposes.46 The index is derived from Census data collected every five years since 1991 and reflects eight dimensions of deprivation: income, employment, communication (internet access), support, educational qualifications, home ownership, living space, and living conditions.46,47 Notably, the indicators used to construct each five-yearly index may vary over time, as their association with deprivation is time-dependent.48 Deprivation is measured on a decile scale from 1 to 10, with decile 1 representing the 10% least deprived areas and decile 10 representing the 10% most deprived areas.46

Clinical Quality Registries

Patient data, including demographics, clinical information, and outcomes related to specific diseases and procedures, are routinely collected through clinical records. These data are primarily used to monitor disease prevalence and, importantly, to support clinical quality assurance and patient safety initiatives.

Cardiac Inherited Diseases Registry New Zealand

In 2000, a multidisciplinary network of clinicians, the Cardiac Inherited Diseases Group, established the web-based Cardiac Inherited Diseases Registry New Zealand in Auckland.49 With the primary aim of investigating sudden unexplained death in the young, the registry received regional ethical approval in 2002 and national approval in 2009.49 This clinical and genetic registry contains data on probands and their families, facilitating family screening for inherited cardiac conditions.49 As of 2017, the registry included 2,749 registrants, comprising 941 probands with suspected inherited cardiac disease and their families, covering at least seven types of inherited heart conditions.49 Registry data, primarily entered manually by registry coordinators, include electrocardiograms, exercise and Holter test results, genetic test results, reason for referral, referral source, family pedigrees, and autopsy and pathology findings.49

New Zealand Cardiac Surgery Registry

Since 2015, the New Zealand Cardiac Surgery Registry has annually collected demographic, clinical, and outcome data on all patients undergoing cardiac surgery in the public health sector (ie, the five public hospitals performing cardiac surgery in New Zealand).50 The primary purpose of the registry is to improve quality of care for cardiac patients by benchmarking clinical performance against national and international standards.50 In addition, the registry provides valuable data on trends in cardiovascular treatment and post-operative complications.50

Researcher-Initiated Registries

Researchers in New Zealand have retrieved clinical data from electronic health records along with administrative health and demographics data from both primary and secondary care settings, generating a range of regional and national longitudinal clinical registries focused on cardiovascular conditions. These registries are described below in the order of their establishment.

PREDICT-CVD Cohort

The prospective PREDICT Cardiovascular Disease (PREDICT-CVD) open cohort study was established in 2002 by a research team at the University of Auckland.28 This cohort automatically enrolls primary care patients assessed for CVD risk in New Zealand general practices using the PREDICT software, a web-based CVD risk assessment and management decision support system.28

The PREDICT study has been implemented in approximately one-third of general practices in New Zealand and has facilitated a wide range of cardiovascular epidemiological research.28 Moreover, the PREDICT software has become an essential tool in routine general practice, assisting general practitioners with incident CVD risk assessment and management.28 The software is integrated with general practice electronic health records, enabling CVD risk factor data from patient records to auto-populate PREDICT data templates.28 Once all required variables have been entered, the software calculates the patient’s incident CVD risk and generates patient-specific management advice for the clinician, while simultaneously creating a research dataset for the PREDICT research group.28

The baseline cohort serves as the foundational population from which various sub-cohorts can be derived for specific research purposes.28 Existing sub-cohorts include a CVD sub-cohort (including patients with comorbid diabetes), a diabetes sub-cohort, and a non-CVD and non-diabetes cohort.28 With provider permission, PREDICT-CVD data are regularly linked to other health datasets, including drug dispensing records, laboratory test results, enrollments in PHOs, hospitalizations, and mortality data.28 As of late 2024, the cohort included over 600,000 individuals and has been used to derive multiple sex-specific CVD risk prediction equations,51,52 four of which have been implemented across New Zealand and Australia.53,54

ANZACS-QI Registries

The Aotearoa New Zealand All Cardiac Services Quality Improvement (ANZACS-QI) Programme—formerly known as the All New Zealand Acute Coronary Syndrome Quality Improvement Programme—aims to support appropriate, evidence-based management of cardiac disease, improve the quality of cardiac care, and standardize the clinical management of cardiac patients nationwide.55 The program utilizes both national administrative registries and more detailed procedure- and disease-specific ANZACS-QI registries.56

Since 2014, the Catheterisation Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (CathPCI) Registry has captured a mandatory dataset for all invasive coronary angiograms and percutaneous coronary procedures performed in New Zealand public and private hospitals.56 A separate Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) registry form is completed for the subset of patients with suspected ACS at admission who undergo coronary angiography.56 Data collected in these registries include patient demographics, admission and discharge ACS risk stratification, cardiovascular risk factors, diagnostic investigations and management, procedural details, inpatient outcomes, and medications prescribed at discharge.56 As of March 2025, the registry contained 213,254 coronary angiogram procedures, of which 101,332 involved patients presenting with ACS.57

The DEVICE Registry, also established in 2014, collects similar data for all permanent pacemaker and implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) procedures across New Zealand.58 By 2025, the registry comprised 26,622 pacemaker and 6602 ICD procedures.57

Since 2023, a web-based pilot Heart Failure Registry has been implemented in the Northern Region of New Zealand.59 A novel systematic sampling method is used to identify a representative cohort of patients hospitalized with heart failure using national administrative registries.59 Subsequently, electronic clinical records are reviewed, and data are entered into this web-based registry, which is currently being rolled out nationally.59

Based on the PREDICT web-based system, the ANZACS-QI registries have evolved into a large-scale research database, providing an electronic data infrastructure for nationwide clinical registry trials and a range of other research initiatives.56 The research and quality improvement arms of the ANZACS-QI Programme are part of the VAREANZ Programme (Table 1), utilizing linkage of registry data to national routine health administrative registries (eg, the NMDS, the Pharmaceutical Collection, and the Mortality Collection) held in the VAREANZ data collections.56 The quality improvement arm of the ANZACS-QI Programme is funded by Health New Zealand, while research activities are supported by separate research grants.60 The ANZACS-QI website provides further details on the operation and outputs of the program.60

VAREANZ Registries

In 2011, researchers from the PREDICT-CVD and ANZACS-QI programs (ie, the research arm of the ANZACS-QI Programme) received a 5-year Health Research Council program grant, enabling them to establish the Vascular Informatics using Epidemiology and the Web (VIEW) Research Programme to support PREDICT-CVD and ANZACS-QI research.61 The group subsequently received two additional consecutive 5-year program grants, with name changes reflecting shifts in research focus.61 The original VIEW Programme (2011) was followed by the VIEW2020 Programme in 2016, and the VAREANZ Programme in 2021.61

The VAREANZ group maintains the PREDICT-CVD cohort and ANZACS-QI registries, in addition to a newly established cross-sectional registry of patients aged 18 years and older, enrolled through all PHOs in New Zealand between 2023 and 2025.62 The latter includes CVD classifications and risk data. Individual-level data from each of these registries are linked to multiple annually updated national health administrative registries.63 The data holdings include information on adult patients, some of which (ie, the NMDS and the Mortality Collection) date back to 1988.63

Additionally, the VAREANZ group includes laboratory data from all biochemistry and hematology tests relevant to CVD, performed in all community laboratories in New Zealand from 2012 to mid-2024.41 These datasets have supported the development of CVD risk prediction and prognostic algorithms,64 as well as evaluation of treatment, service utilization, and outcomes in both the general population and specific CVD patient groups.63

Linkage across VAREANZ datasets enables the construction of national cohorts encompassing nearly all adult New Zealanders.63 These cohorts have been further linked to follow-up data, including all-cause mortality, fatal and non-fatal CVD events, and CVD medication dispensing records, supporting a wide range of cardiovascular research projects.63

Data Quality

The applicability of data relevant to epidemiological and clinical research depends on data quality, which is typically evaluated by assessing validity and completeness.6 Validity refers to the extent to which a variable, test, or tool accurately measures the intended condition or event, and is typically expressed as the positive predictive value. Completeness, on the other hand, refers to a dataset’s coverage of a specific condition or event within a predefined population, indicating the extent of missing data, and is typically expressed as sensitivity.6 Validity and completeness depend on the type of data collection, as outlined below and in Table 1.

Administrative Registries

Validity and Completeness

Systematic validation studies of administrative health data in New Zealand are limited. A quality assessment of the 2018 Census data, conducted by Stats NZ and the External Data Quality Panel, revealed substantial variation in the quality of socioeconomic variables.65,66 As a result, Census data were supplemented with information from alternative sources.67 In this context, the New Zealand Socioeconomic Index 2018 evaluated the impact of these supplemental data on the quality of NZDep data.68 According to this assessment, socioeconomic trends observed in the supplemental data generally aligned with those in the Census, suggesting minimal risk of bias introduced by the supplemental sources.68

High coverage rates have been documented for certain datasets. For instance, while individual-level pharmaceutical data were not consistently recorded in earlier years, more than 97% of dispensing records have been patient-linked since 2007.24 Similarly, although the Primary Health Organisation Enrolment Collection dataset was incomplete during its initial years, it has been operational with high coverage since 2008 and covered approximately 95% of the New Zealand population as of July 2025.69 In contrast, the Mortality Collection has recorded most deaths since the early 1960s.70 Socioeconomic data coverage, however, largely depends on the response rate to the five-yearly NZ Census, which was 88% in 2018.71

Data Linkage

Inconsistencies in linkage rates across ethnic groups have been documented, with lower linkage rates observed among Pacific and Asian New Zealanders compared with Māori and European New Zealanders.24 Therefore, cross-linked registry data should be used with caution, and potential limitations should be considered when conducting registry-based studies of Pacific and Asian New Zealanders. Despite this limitation, further probabilistic linkage of health datasets to data from other government agencies within the IDI enables studies that incorporate factors not routinely captured in health datasets.24 Although false-negative (missed) linkage rates in the IDI remain unmeasured, false-positive linkage rates appear to be low.25

Clinical Quality Registries

Clinical quality registries are generally assumed to possess high validity and completeness, as reported in other countries; however, formal validation studies remain limited.6,72 The completeness of the New Zealand Cardiac Surgery Register was assessed in 2018, demonstrating near-complete mandatory data capture (99% of registered patients).50 However, additional details regarding data quality have not been disclosed.

Researcher-Initiated Registries

Validity and Completeness

Recording in the ANZACS-QI registries and the PREDICT-CVD cohort depends on the availability of registry software at healthcare sites, and the practitioner’s decision to use the software.28 As of 2013, the ANZACS-QI software had been implemented in all 41 public hospitals in New Zealand, covering virtually all ACS admissions in the country.56 However, the ANZACS-QI Programme captures detailed patient-level data only for patients undergoing coronary angiography,56 accounting for approximately 60% of first-time ACS admissions to New Zealand hospitals.73 These patients are typically managed by cardiology services and form the target cohort for reporting key clinical performance indicators.73 Patients not registered tend to be older, more often women, and have more comorbidities and poorer outcomes.73 For patients not undergoing coronary angiography, the ANZACS-QI Programme therefore relies on more limited data from national administrative registries. As of 2015, the PREDICT software had been adopted by approximately one-third of general practices in New Zealand,28 with other CVD risk prediction tools used elsewhere.

To minimize input errors, data entered by clinicians are systematically validated at the point of entry into the registry platforms.28,56,63,74 Furthermore, the ANZACS-QI and PREDICT software systems include mandatory fields for key cardiovascular risk variables to ensure comprehensive data collection.56 Previously, height, weight, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) were non-mandatory to reduce clinician workload.28,56 These variables are now required in the ongoing PREDICT-CVD cohort study and are being collected in the VAREANZ primary care CVD registry.53 Other registry variables depend on individual practitioner decisions and may require time-consuming manual data entry.28,56

All ANZACS-QI registries aim for >95% data completeness. Completion rates are reported monthly to participating centers and to Health New Zealand.75 The ANZACS-QI Programme also employs independent clinical auditors to review a representative sample of registry forms from each participating center annually.56 When deficiencies are identified, education is provided, and the electronic forms and embedded definitions are reviewed and updated as needed.56

Beyond monitoring and audit, researcher-initiated registries are typically validated against the NMDS. For example, a validation study of the ANZACS-QI DEVICE Registry assessed the completeness of 2016 data using the NMDS as a reference.76 The study reported coverage rates of 86% for permanent pacemakers and 81% for ICDs, with similar capture rates across sex, age, and ethnic groups.76 Furthermore, the DEVICE Registry exhibits high rates of missing data for key clinical variables such as New York Heart Association (NYHA) Functional Classification and left ventricular ejection fraction.77 Similarly, completeness in other ANZACS-QI registries, including the CathPCI and ACS registries, is assessed biannually against the NMDS.75

Data Linkage

NHI linkage to administrative registries ensures near-complete registration of subsequent CVD events in both the ANZACS-QI registries and the PREDICT-CVD cohort. This reflects the fact that CVD events occurring within New Zealand almost invariably involve interaction with the healthcare system, which necessitates NHI registration through hospital admissions, prescription dispensing, or other clinical contacts.28 However, some key clinical variables, such as blood pressure and left ventricular ejection fraction, are missing from the VAREANZ national administrative registries, which do not routinely capture detailed clinical information.63,78

Research Potential

Most cardiovascular collections, including clinical quality registries and researcher-initiated registries, are ongoing and incorporate administrative health data. This integration provides continuous opportunities for designing registry-enabled clinical trials and conducting other epidemiological studies.28,56,63,74,77,79 In the following sections, we explore the potential of New Zealand health data for use in both national and international registry-based cardiovascular research.

National Perspective

The universal use of a unique alphanumeric identifier, the NHI, in New Zealand health records has enabled the establishment of a comprehensive network of deterministically linked administrative health data covering nearly all New Zealand residents. In addition to supporting nationwide clinical registries, these data are widely used to construct large study cohorts. To date, several research projects have been embedded within ongoing clinical registries, such as the ANZACS-QI registries, to facilitate data collection.

One example is the Multi-Ethnic New Zealand Study of Acute Coronary Syndromes (MENZACS), a prospective cohort study investigating predictors of recurrent events following first-time ACS.79 Embedded within the ANZACS-QI ACS and Cath Lab registries and linked to relevant administrative datasets, the study aims to develop multimodal risk prediction models for health outcomes in this subgroup of CVD patients.79 The cohort includes 2015 first-time ACS patients (aged >18 years) recruited from coronary care units at six New Zealand hospitals between 2015 and 2019.80 Blood samples were collected from all participants, assayed for biomarkers, including N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide, and stored for future ‘omic analyses.80

Another example is the Aotearoa New Zealand Rheumatic Heart Disease Registry, a contemporary cohort of patients diagnosed with moderate or severe rheumatic heart disease.74 Established in 2017 with follow-up through 2020, the registry aimed to develop a baseline cohort with high specificity for rheumatic heart disease, independent of ICD-10-AM discharge coding, to describe the demographics, care, and outcomes of affected individuals.74 Participants were enrolled from multiple data sources using NHI linkage to prevent duplication.74 Although the registry’s high specificity enabled accurate evaluation of rheumatic heart disease-related mortality and morbidity through linked datasets, this was offset by low sensitivity in capturing all cases nationwide.74

Demonstrating the research potential of probabilistically linked administrative datasets within the IDI, a previous study examined the impact of housing damage on CVD-related hospital admissions and mortality following a major earthquake in the Canterbury region of New Zealand.81

International Perspective

When evaluating New Zealand’s health data from a cardiology viewpoint in comparison with other countries, several parallels can be drawn with the Nordic countries and the UK (Table 2). Facilitated by a unique personal identifier assigned to all citizens at birth, upon immigration, or at entry into the healthcare system, the existence of regularly collected, well-organized, and linkable health data is rare on a global scale. Few countries share this feature, which enables lifelong follow-up of their citizens, including information on the date of death or censoring.1,2 Nonetheless, although the New Zealand’s health data systems encompass many strengths, they lack certain aspects when compared with those of the Nordic countries and the UK.

First, clinical datasets on cardiovascular conditions in New Zealand are predominantly initiated by researchers, who define various inclusion criteria for their cohort participants (eg, age and specific procedures). In contrast, the Nordic countries, particularly Denmark, Norway, and Sweden, maintain a broader range of nationwide clinical quality databases, each containing specific cardiovascular data that are routinely collected during daily clinical practice.6 This enables the capture of all patients at the national level with a specific diagnosis or condition.

Second, certain data relevant to cardiovascular registry-based research are currently not collected in New Zealand. For example, while Norway, Finland, and Iceland record contacts and diagnoses for their primary care patients,85,90,92 primary care data in New Zealand are limited to enrollment information.36 Similarly, Denmark and Finland record both prescription redemptions (at the individual level) and over-the-counter drug sales (aggregated),1 whereas New Zealand records redeemed prescriptions only.37 Yet, the ongoing PREDICT-CVD primary care cohort in New Zealand records clinical data on approximately one-third of adult primary care patients, including lifestyle factors.28 Similar data are available from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink in the UK,97 but not routinely available in the Nordic countries.1

Lastly, systematic quality reviews have been conducted on many of the Nordic1,6 and the UK202,203 registries, whereas New Zealand lacks such reviews of its data collections.

Cross-Country Studies

Constructing large, cross-national study cohorts is often challenged by conflicting privacy legislation across participating countries. However, the cross-border use of New Zealand health data is feasible under the Privacy Act 2020—specifically, Information Privacy Principle 12—which permits the disclosure of personal information to overseas entities under defined conditions.204 These include situations where the receiving party is subject to privacy laws that offer comparable safeguards, or where contractual agreements ensure adequate protection of the data.204

Despite this provision, we are not aware of any instances in which registry data from New Zealand have been pooled with international datasets for cardiovascular research. Nonetheless, other forms of cross-country collaboration are possible. For example, a comorbidity prediction model developed using a Danish cohort of patients with first-time myocardial infarction was subsequently validated in an independent New Zealand cohort, supporting its generalizability.205

Registry-Enabled Clinical Trials

Routine healthcare data can enhance both the feasibility and efficiency of clinical trials. While the use of registries in clinical trials is well established in countries such as Denmark, Sweden, and the United Kingdom,206 an increasing number of registry-enabled clinical trials are now being initiated in New Zealand (Table 3). Administrative registries can support various aspects of trial design, including feasibility assessment for sample size calculations, patient identification and recruitment, randomization, and the collection of baseline and outcome data. The High Flow Oxygen trial, a pragmatic, cluster-randomized, crossover trial, examined the association between high-flow supplementary oxygen and 30-day mortality in patients presenting with suspected ACS.207 This trial included all patients with suspected ACS (n=40,872) recorded in two national registries, the ANZACS-QI registry and the ambulance ACS pathway, during the designated study periods.207

The Text4HeartII trial, a two-arm, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial, evaluated the effectiveness of a 24-week Short Message Service-based intervention aimed at improving medication adherence and promoting lifestyle changes.208 During the recruitment period, 206 eligible patients were enrolled from two sites in Auckland. Routine clinical data from the ANZACS-QI registry were used to obtain baseline patient characteristics and detect outcomes.208

The BioMACS trial, a pragmatic, two-arm, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial, employed a biomarker-guided approach to risk stratification and management, with the goal of reducing recurrent clinical events following ACS.209 Conducted across five New Zealand sites, the trial utilized the ANZACS-QI registry to identify eligible post-ACS patients and national health data to support clinically driven outcome detection.209

Finally, the REDUCE-Acute Myocardial Infarction (REDUCE-AMI) trial exemplifies an international registry-based trial, with participating sites in New Zealand, Sweden, and Estonia.210 Using a parallel-group, open-label, randomized controlled design, the trial investigated the effect of beta-blockers in post-myocardial infarction patients with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction.210 Historical data from clinical quality registries were used to estimate event rates for sample size calculations, and eligible patients (n=5020) were identified, enrolled, and randomized directly within the registry system.210 Furthermore, baseline and follow-up data, including outcome detection and censoring information, were extracted from national administrative and clinical quality registries.210

Conclusions

New Zealand’s health data infrastructure provides a robust foundation for cardiovascular research. The universal NHI enables comprehensive linkage of individual-level administrative data across the healthcare system. This foundation is further strengthened by integrating large-scale clinical quality registries and researcher-initiated registries, supporting both observational studies and clinical trials.
