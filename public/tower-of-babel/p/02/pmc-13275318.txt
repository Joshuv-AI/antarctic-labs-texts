Rap1-mediated steric hindrance protects telomeres from MRX sensing
Authors: Stefano Mattarocci, Giordano Reginato, Elda Cannavo, Florian Roisné-Hamelin, Robert Gnügge, Alice Deshayes, Sabrina Pobiega, Olivier Alibert, Vincent Morin, Virginie Ropars, Johannes Mehringer, Vincent Geli, Philippe Cuniasse, Jean-Baptiste Charbonnier, Lorraine S Symington, Petr Cejka, Stéphane Marcand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTelomere capping largely depends on telomere length. Abnormally short telomeres are prone to activate DNA damage checkpoint, undergo unscheduled chromosomal fusions through nonhomologous end-joining (NHEJ) and be resected. All these processes are mediated by the Mre11–Rad50–Xrs2NBS1 (MRXMRN) complex. The response to telomere length is thought to correlate with the number of DNA-bound telomeric proteins but the mechanisms translating this number into functional protection remain unclear. Here, combining genetic and reconstitution biochemistry approaches, we explain why NHEJ inhibition and DNA resection suppression are proportional to the length of Rap1-bound arrays adjacent to DNA ends in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Our findings reveal that MRX has a capacity to distinguish short from long protein blocks at DNA breaks, which translates into the ability of Rap1 protein–DNA arrays to suppress MRX on the basis of their length. Unlike the previously described direct inhibition of MRXMRN by Rif2 and TRF2 iDDR, the process described here does not rely on direct protein–protein interactions. Instead, Rap1 inhibits MRX by steric hindrance, determined both by the length of DNA it shields and by the coverage density. These results explain how cells detect short telomeres, a crucial process for telomere length homeostasis and telomere-driven senescence.Subject terms: Double-strand DNA breaks, Telomeres, Genomic instability

Main

The Mre11–Rad50–Xrs2NBS1 DNA repair complex (MRX in S. cerevisiae, MRN in other eukaryotes and MR or SbcCD in bacteria) is a universally conserved early responder to DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs). This ATPase-nuclease complex is related to structural maintenance of chromosomes (SMC) proteins and specifically captures DSBs, subsequently activating DNA damage signaling through Tel1ATM and facilitating repair through both nonhomologous end-joining (NHEJ) and homologous recombination (HR) pathways1–6 (Supplementary Fig. 1). In yeast, MRX promotes Ku-dependent NHEJ by tethering DNA ends together to maintain their proximity for efficient repair7–9. Additionally, the Xrs2 subunit of MRX stimulates DNA ligation through a direct interaction with the Lig4 cofactor Lif1 (refs. 10–12), an interaction that is conserved between human homologs NBS1 and XRCC4 (ref. 10). In the context of HR, the nuclease activity of the MRXMRN complex is responsible for short-range resection of DSBs, particularly those blocked by proteins or secondary DNA structures13. Stimulated by phosphorylated Sae2CtIP, the MRXMRN complex exhibits a unique ability to cleave the 5′-terminated DNA strand endonucleolytically on the internal side of DSB-bound protein blocks, thus allowing DNA end resection to bypass DNA-bound protein obstacles. Consequently, MRXMRN is involved in the resection of Spo11-bound meiotic DSBs and facilitates the removal of nonproductive NHEJ complexes, such as Ku and its associated factors14–18.

However, chromosome ends must be protected against the promiscuous activities of MRXMRN to avoid deleterious chromosome fusions by NHEJ, resection or activation of DNA damage signaling. Stringent suppression of MRXMRN at telomeres is, therefore, crucial to maintain genome stability, although it remains unclear how it is achieved because of the unique ability of MRXMRN to bypass DNA-bound obstacles at DSBs. Telomeric DNA consists of short T+G-rich repetitive DNA sequences that are tightly bound by proteins in tandem, including Rap1 in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Taz1 in Schizosaccharomyces pombe and TRF1–TRF2 in mammals. Rap1, Taz1 and TRF2 are essential for protecting telomeres against fusion, resection and DNA damage signaling, partially by suppressing MRXMRN (refs. 19–27). The response to short telomeres is thought to correlate with the number of telomere-bound proteins21,28–32. However, how this number translates into functional telomere protection and telomere homeostasis has remained elusive.

A conserved mechanism for inhibiting MRXMRN at telomeres involves direct protein–protein interactions through a short motif termed as BAT (blocks addition of telomeres) or MIN (MRX-inhibitory motif)33. In budding yeast, this motif is within Rif2, a protein recruited to telomeres by Rap1 (refs. 34,35). The motif specifically binds the Rad50 ATPase subunit of MRX, triggers nonproductive ATP hydrolysis and prevents DNA end capture, disrupting most, if not all, MRX activities36–40. In fission yeast, Taz1 harbors a similar motif38, while, in mammals, an equivalent motif known as iDDR is found within TRF2 (refs. 19,24,26,41). Although the iDDR motif differs in sequence from its yeast counterparts and has a distinct evolutionary origin, it binds the same position on Rad50 and inhibits at least one MRN function—its endonuclease activity1,19,26. A similar Mre11–Rad50 inhibition was recently observed at telomeres in bacteria42. This recurrence of MRXMRN inhibition at telomeres underscores its functional importance.

Despite the conserved function of Rif2 in blocking MRXMRN, the phenotypes observed in S. cerevisiae cells lacking Rif2, in S. pombe cells lacking the MIN motif of Taz1 and in mammalian cells lacking the iDDR motif of TRF2 are notably modest24,26,38,39. In rif2∆ yeast cells, telomere fusions remain very rare, barely above the background observed in wild-type cells43,44. Steady-state telomere length increases by approximately one third because of the reduced repression of MRX–Tel1 kinase activity, which promotes telomere elongation by telomerase34,37,45–48. In contrast, loss of Rap1 from telomeres results in frequent telomere fusions and runaway telomere elongation, highlighting Rap1’s critical function to block NHEJ and limit telomere elongation through mechanisms independent of Rif2 (refs. 20,49–53). Consistent with this observation, previous work from D. Shore and colleagues demonstrated that telomeric repeats inserted at a DSB could inhibit MRX recruitment in cells expressing a truncated Rap1 protein unable to recruit Rif2 (refs. 21,23). Notably, this inhibition was observed with 250-bp telomeric repeats, close to the lengths typically seen in wild-type cells, but not with shorter 80-bp repeats. As the mechanism by which Rap1 inhibits MRX in this context and the relevance of this interplay for telomere maintenance remained unexplored, we assessed how Rap1 alone can protect telomeres against MRX activity.

Through a combination of genetic and biochemical approaches, we reveal here how Rap1 protein–DNA arrays inhibit MRX activities independently of Rif2 and other telomeric proteins. We show that elements of Rap1-dependent end protection observed in cells can be reconstituted in vitro with defined, purified components. We identify MRX’s DNA end-sensing activity as the key factor that determines the length of Rap1 arrays sufficient for end protection. MRX, hence, functions as a sensor of telomere length, which activates specific responses when telomeres get too short. Specifically, we found that tandem arrays of protein-bound DNA gradually inhibit MRX activation by steric hindrance in a length-dependent and density-dependent manner, solely influenced by the distance of the DNA-bound MRX from the DNA end. Overall, our findings elucidate a Rif2-independent mechanism that contributes to telomere end protection and telomere length homeostasis.

RESULTS

Suppression of MRX recruitment and resection activity depends on the number of Rap1 molecules flanking a DSB

To evaluate the ability of Rap1 to protect telomeres against MRX activity, we first examined whether Rap1 proteins bound in tandem close to a DSB could inhibit MRX recruitment. We used S. cerevisiae cells lacking Rif2, as well as Sir4, another telomere-associated protein recruited by Rap1 and contributing to telomere protection against promiscuous NHEJ through a yet-to-be-defined mechanism43,44. We used arrays of high-affinity Rap1-binding sites in tandem (Fig. 1a)30,54,55. These arrays, containing 4–10 Rap1-binding sites, were inserted adjacent to an I-SceI endonuclease recognition sequence, allowing us to monitor MRX recruitment at an induced DSB (Fig. 1a). We used chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)–qPCR to quantify the binding of the MRX subunit Xrs2 at the DSB. To avoid confounding effects from de novo telomere addition (Supplementary Fig. 2), we reversed the orientation of the inserted Rap1 sites relative to their native orientation at telomeres, such that the telomeres could not be extended by telomerase. Our assay also included an additional I-SceI site 1.2 kb away from the Rap1-inserted DSB, serving as an internal control for MRX recruitment. In this configuration, Rap1 arrays effectively reduced Xrs2 recruitment to the adjacent broken end (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 3a,b). The inhibition was modest (~2-fold) with four Rap1 sites but increased substantially with eight and ten sites (~4-fold and ~8-fold inhibition relative to no Rap1 site, respectively). To control for possible effects of increased distance between the broken site and the primers used to detect chromatin fragments, we included an array of eight mutant sites unable to bind Rap1 (refs. 54,55) (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 3a,b). This mutant array did not reduce Xrs2 interaction with the DSB relative to the absence of Rap1 sites, confirming that the observed effects were caused by Rap1 binding.

The inhibition of MRX binding by Rap1 arrays flanking a DSB suggests that MRX’s downstream activities, including 5′ end resection and promotion of NHEJ, could be consequently affected. To test this hypothesis, we estimated 5′ end resection using the I-SceI cleavage assay combined with Southern blotting, in which loss of the cut-fragment signal reflects resection and is largely MRX dependent (Supplementary Fig. 3c)39. Cells were arrested in G2/M (that is, when MRX-dependent resection is maximal) before and during I-SceI induction. Insertion of eight or ten Rap1 sites near the break stabilized DNA ends, with unresected fragments still detectable after 4 h of endonuclease expression (Fig. 1c,d). In contrast, breaks lacking Rap1 sites were fully resected, while a four-site Rap1 array conferred partial stabilization. These results indicate that tandem Rap1 sites effectively limit 5′ end resection in vivo in the absence of Rif2 in proportion to the number of sites, consistent with the observed inhibition of MRX binding at DNA ends by Rap1 (Fig. 1b)21,23.

To determine whether the Rap1-mediated inhibition of resection is because of a structural role resulting from DNA coverage or from a specific physical interaction with Rap1, we tested whether arrays bound by the unrelated bacterial LacI protein could similarly reduce 5′ end resection. We monitored resection of an inducible DSB flanked by 0–7 lacO sites, using qPCR to quantify the resection level (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 4a,b). In this assay, genomic DNA was isolated and digested with a restriction enzyme that cleaves a recognition site near the DSB56. An amplicon spanning this site was then analyzed by qPCR and resection extending beyond the site was quantified on the basis of the restriction resistance of single-stranded DNA. To isolate MRX activity from the long-range DNA end resection pathways, we deleted EXO1 and tagged Sgs1 and Dna2 with AID (auxin-induced degradation) tags (Supplementary Fig. 4c–f). In cells expressing LacI, resection past the arrays containing 3–7 lacO sites was decreased compared to cells lacking LacI, demonstrating that LacI binding can inhibit MRX-mediated resection in vivo. These findings indicate that MRX can be inhibited by tandem protein arrays above a certain length irrespective of the protein’s identity, supporting the conclusion that Rap1-dependent inhibition arises from physical occlusion of DNA, in contrast to the direct inhibition of MRX by Rif2 (refs. 36–40).

Rap1 arrays inhibit NHEJ in a length-dependent manner

As Rap1 depletion leads to frequent telomere fusions, we investigated whether Rap1 arrays can inhibit NHEJ. To this end, we monitored cell survival following continuous I-SceI endonuclease expression as a readout for NHEJ repair efficiency39,53. In this assay, survival in response to a DSB without adjacent Rap1 sites was around 1%, primarily because of fusion of the distal DSB ends (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 2; rif2∆ sir4∆ cells). In NHEJ-deficient cells (for example, lif1∆), survival dropped by four orders of magnitude, establishing the assay’s sensitivity range. Insertion of tandem Rap1 sites near the DSB inhibited NHEJ repair (Fig. 1f). With two Rap1 sites, survival was reduced approximately fivefold. This moderate inhibition is consistent with a contribution from the most end-proximal Rap1, through a restriction of Ku inward translocation53, although this effect is expected to be limited by the reversed orientation of the Rap1 sites relative to their native orientation at telomeres53. Increasing the number of Rap1 sites to four, six and eight led to progressively stronger inhibition with survival reduced ~100-fold, ~500-fold and ~5,000-fold, respectively, relative to DNA ends lacking Rap1 site. Arrays of eight Rap1 sites or more decreased survival to levels seen in NHEJ-deficient cells, reaching the assay’s sensitivity limit. In contrast, an array of 16 mutant sites defective for Rap1 binding54,55 did not affect survival, confirming that the reduction in cell survival and, hence, NHEJ inhibition depended on Rap1 binding (Fig. 1f).

To complement these findings, we used a second assay involving a single I-SceI site, where cell survival depends on NHEJ-mediated mutations that prevent further endonuclease cleavage39,57 (Fig. 1g). In this setup, Rap1 arrays near the DSB also inhibited NHEJ repair. The strength of the inhibition was similarly proportional to the number of Rap1 sites, reaching a maximum with 10–12 or more sites (Fig. 1g). These results indicate that tandem DNA-bound Rap1 molecules can protect against telomere fusion by NHEJ independently of Rif2 and Sir4. Our findings collectively suggest that DNA coverage by Rap1 broadly antagonizes MRX-dependent functions in proportion to the number of DNA-bound Rap1 molecules. While a few Rap1 molecules can partially inhibit MRX-dependent processes, arrays of 8–10 sites are necessary for stronger inhibition, highlighting the importance of DNA length covered by Rap1 in blocking resection and end-joining at DNA ends.

Rap1 arrays inhibit reconstituted MRX-dependent NHEJ in a length-dependent manner

The above genetic experiments revealed that the efficiency of the suppression of NHEJ and resection at DSBs bound by the telomeric protein Rap1 is proportional to the number of Rap1 sites, recapitulating what is occurring at short telomeres. Although these pathways that are aberrantly activated at short telomeres both depend on the MRX complex, the experiments could not determine whether it is MRX only or additionally other proteins that respond to the length of the Rap1 arrays. To understand the underlying mechanism, we set out to first reconstitute the MRX-dependent NHEJ reactions with purified recombinant proteins. Using a 520-bp-long dsDNA substrate with self-complementary 4-nt-long overhangs and recombinant Dnl4–Lif1, Yku70/80 (Ku), Nej1 and MRX (Fig. 2a,b), we observed efficient end-joining leading to DNA circularization—determined by appearance of T5 exonuclease-resistant products—and linear tandem joining of the DNA substrate (Fig. 2c). The reactions were ~5-fold stimulated by MRX (Fig. 2d), independently of the DNA overhang sequence (Supplementary Fig. 5a)58,59. We next constructed DNA substrates additionally flanked by two (~30 bp-long) or eight (~140 bp-long) Rap1-binding sites (Supplementary Fig. 5b) and we monitored the effects of the Rap1 arrays of various lengths on the reconstituted NHEJ reactions. As expected, Rap1 did not inhibit end-joining of DNA without the Rap1-binding sequence (Fig. 2e, left, and Supplementary Fig. 5c), excluding nonspecific effects. However, when two Rap1-binding repeats were present at both DNA ends, we observed a gradual decrease in DNA end-joining efficacy dependent on Rap1 concentration (Fig. 2e, middle). When a substrate with eight Rap1 repeats was used, the inhibition by Rap1 was much more pronounced (Fig. 2e, right), thus recapitulating the dependence on the number of Rap1 molecules observed in vivo (Fig. 1d,e). As expected, Rap1 was only inhibitory when added to the reactions before the NHEJ proteins (Fig. 2f). Using an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) approach, we observed that Rap1 binding to tandem arrays consisting of eight Rap1-binding sites entirely antagonized MRX binding to DNA (Supplementary Fig. 5d,e). Furthermore, using DNA substrates containing four Rap1 sites with flanking sequences of varying lengths lacking Rap1 sites favored the cobinding of MRX and Rap1 on the same DNA molecules, but only when the flanking sequences were ≥30 bp (Fig. 2g). These data are consistent with Rap1 antagonizing MRX–DNA binding only at sites where it is specifically bound.

To further substantiate these results, we next prepared a substrate with the Rap1 arrays present only on one side of a linear DNA substrate, adjacent to a ligatable (5′ phosphorylated) end (Fig. 3a). The other DNA end was not phosphorylated and, hence, not ligatable. Therefore, the DNA molecules can only be ligated in a head-to-head orientation. We observed end-joining in reactions containing Dnl4–Lif1, Ku and Nej1, which was strongly (~5-fold) stimulated by MRX (Fig. 3b). Importantly, we also observed a gradual decrease in DNA ligation efficacy with increasing length of the Rap1 arrays flanking the ligatable DSB end (Fig. 3c).

The genetic NHEJ experiments necessitated the use of an inverted Rap1 sequence, resulting in the opposite orientation of Rap1 compared to normal telomeres. Such a strategy was necessary to prevent the action of a telomerase, which would otherwise extend the short Rap1 arrays (Supplementary Fig. 2). In vitro, we instead primarily used the native orientation of Rap1; however, we note that flipping the Rap1 sequence resulted in the same inhibitory effect (Fig. 3d). The inhibition of MRX-dependent NHEJ by Rap1 is, thus, not dependent on its orientation with respect to the DNA end.

We next set out to investigate whether MRX is required for the length-dependent inhibition of NHEJ mediated by the Rap1 arrays. Although MRX is essential for NHEJ in cells, the in vitro reactions can be performed in the absence of MRX with a ~5-fold reduction in efficiency compared to the full reaction. This allowed us to gain insights into MRX function during this process. Excluding MRX from the NHEJ reaction mixture revealed one key difference (Fig. 3e,f). Rap1 bound 10 bp from the end inhibited the joining of DNA substrates with two or eight Rap1 arrays to a very similar extent, as expected53. In the absence of MRX, the Rap1-array-length-dependent effect was completely lost (Fig. 3e). The long array was even less efficient than the short one at a subsaturating Rap1 concentration (6.3 nM; Fig. 3f). The latter result suggests that some Rap1 sites, including the one closest to the DNA end, may be unoccupied when Rap1 availability becomes limiting.

MRX, thus, enables NHEJ of DNA substrates flanked by short Rap1 telomeric repeats, whereas it cannot promote NHEJ on substrates with long Rap1 repeats. More specifically, we show that MRX is directly responsible for the length dependency of NHEJ inhibition mediated by Rap1 arrays. The Rap1-mediated restriction of Ku inward translocation could account for the length-independent NHEJ inhibition observed in the absence of MRX53. Together, we demonstrate that NHEJ reactions catalyzed by defined components recapitulate the Rap1-array-length-dependent inhibition of NHEJ observed in genetic experiments. We identify MRX as the key factor that is responsible for the Rap1-array-length-dependent effect.

MRX inhibition by Rap1 depends on Rap1’s proximity to the DNA end

The inhibition of MRX by extensive protein–DNA coverage highlights the spatial constraints of MRX binding and activation near DNA ends. Shifting the blocking Rap1 array away from the end could allow us to estimate the minimal distance required for NHEJ and MRX machineries to productively bind the DNA ends. In the assays described above, the edge of the Rap1 arrays was positioned 11 bp away from the DNA end in the in vivo I-SceI assay and 10 bp away in the reconstituted NHEJ assay. Under those conditions, end-joining could be fully suppressed. To explore how distance impacts NHEJ and MRX assembly, we introduced variable linkers between the Rap1 arrays and the DNA ends in both setups. In the in vivo assay, introducing a 31-bp linker between arrays of 4–8 Rap1 sites and the DNA end reduced the inhibitory effect of Rap1 (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 6). Longer linkers further alleviated the inhibition, with NHEJ efficiency fully restored when an eight-site Rap1 array was positioned 101 bp away from the broken end. Notably, a shorter four-site Rap1 array required only a 31-bp linker for full restoration of NHEJ efficiency, suggesting a negative synergy between array size and distance when both factors become limiting. These results indicate that Rap1 inhibits NHEJ in cis with a limited range (<100 bp) and with a strength inversely proportional to the distance of Rap1 from the end. The gradual alleviation of NHEJ inhibition with increasing distance of Rap1 from the DSB implies that MRX frequently attempts to engage DNA internally (sometimes up to ~80–100 bp from the end) but fails to do so when encountering Rap1. Importantly, distances of 31 bp or more from the end have been shown to be sufficient to abolish inhibition arising from the restriction of Ku inward translocation mediated by the most end-proximal Rap1 (ref. 53), suggesting that, under these conditions, NHEJ inhibition is driven solely by Rap1-dependent MRX inhibition.

Similarly, in the reconstituted NHEJ assay, an eight-site Rap1 array beginning 30 or 50 bp from the DNA end did not inhibit end-joining, even with high Rap1 concentrations (Fig. 4b). This closely mirrors the in vivo results. The apparent threshold observed between 10 and 30 bp in the in vitro assay indicates that at least 30 bp of free DNA on each side of the break is a sufficient entry point for the NHEJ machinery, including MRX, to efficiently assemble on DNA ends. Together, these findings confirm that Rap1-mediated DNA coverage creates a physical block that prevents the assembly of the NHEJ machinery. The results demonstrate that the distance between Rap1 arrays and the DNA ends is also a critical determinant of their inhibitory effect, emphasizing that even modest shifts in the position of Rap1 arrays can have effects on MRX activity and NHEJ efficiency.

MRX inhibition by Rap1 depends on Rap1’s DNA coverage density

Our results point toward a mechanism where Rap1-mediated DNA coverage inhibits MRX binding by steric hindrance. This predicts that reducing Rap1 density by increasing the spacing between Rap1-binding sites could relieve MRX inhibition by creating space for MRX to access DNA. To test this hypothesis, we modified the Rap1 arrays used in the previous experiments. These arrays consisted of pairs of two closely spaced Rap1 sites (separated by 1 bp) with a constant gap of 6 bp between each pair. To assess whether increasing the spacing between Rap1 sites could restore MRX function, we extended the length of the intersite gaps to 10, 18, 35 or 60 bp while keeping the total number of Rap1 sites constant at eight (Fig. 5a). The modified arrays were inserted adjacent to the I-SceI site and the in vivo efficiencies of NHEJ and end resection were used as readouts for MRX function. We found that NHEJ remained strongly inhibited when the gaps were increased to 10 bp. However, increasing the spacing to 18 bp partially restored NHEJ efficiency (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Spacers of 60 bp were sufficient to relieve NHEJ inhibition, restoring NHEJ efficiency to the one observed with only two Rap1 sites. Similarly, increasing the gap size led to a progressive restoration of end resection (Fig. 5b). These results indicate that even short free DNA between Rap1-bound sites is sufficient to allow MRX to bind DNA near the end, further confirming our suggestion that MRX can also engage DNA internally to mediate NHEJ or resection.

The spacers of 35 bp and 60 bp within the Rap1 arrays contained one and two lacO sites, respectively (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 7b). To test whether filling these gaps with LacI binding could reestablish NHEJ inhibition, we expressed LacI in cells containing the lacO-containing Rap1 arrays. LacI expression caused a drop in NHEJ efficiency (~10-fold decrease in survival) compared to cells lacking LacI (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 7b). This effect was not observed in cells expressing LacI but lacking lacO-binding and Rap1-binding sites. These findings are consistent with our earlier observation that LacI arrays can block MRX-dependent resection (Fig. 1e) and confirm that continuous protein-mediated DNA coverage, rather than the identity of the protein itself, is the key determinant of MRX inhibition by protein arrays.

Alternatively, we asked whether the MRX inhibitor Rif2, recruited by Rap1 to spaced arrays, could enhance NHEJ inhibition. As shown in Fig. 5d, reintroduction of Rif2 resulted in stronger NHEJ inhibition irrespective of the spacer length, up to 60 bp. This indicates that Rif2-mediated MRX inhibition can compensate for the weakening of steric-hindrance-mediated inhibition caused by reduced Rap1 density. Lastly, we tested the impact of inserting a single spacer of 35 or 60 bp within the center of a dense eight-site array in the absence of Rif2. Both spacers partially restored NHEJ efficiency, although not to the level observed with a single four-site array (Fig. 5e). Similarly, a single spacer of 60 bp separating the two most distal Rap1 sites of an eight-site array reduced NHEJ inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 7c). These results suggest that a single spacer within tandem Rap1-binding sites can be sufficient to permit MRX recruitment and activity.

Rap1 site density and affinity contribute to end protection at native yeast telomeres

Native S. cerevisiae telomeres consist of variable tandem repeats of the sequence 5′-[TG]1–6-T-G2–3-3′. Within these sequences, the motif 5′-GGTGTGTGGGTGTG-3′ corresponds to the Rap1 consensus binding site (Fig. 6a). Additionally, three alternative motifs partially match this consensus, each containing a consensus mismatch that results in a reduction in affinity in vitro (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 8)53. The arrangement of theses motifs in native telomeres alternates in a partially stochastic manner, reflecting the variability in the priming position of S. cerevisiae telomerase during telomere synthesis53,60. To investigate the relative ability of these motifs to inhibit MRX activity, we generated eight tandem sites of each motif, inserted them adjacent to the I-SceI cleavage site and tested their capacity to inhibit NHEJ in vivo. Arrays composed of either consensus or alternative sites were bound by Rap1 in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 3a). However, only the array consisting of full-consensus Rap1 sites strongly inhibited NHEJ repair (Fig. 6b). This indicates that consensus Rap1-binding sites are more effective at antagonizing MRX at telomeres. This effect is consistent with steric hindrance arising from higher affinity and longer residence time of Rap1 on consensus sites. Because of the stochastic alternation between consensus and alternative Rap1-binding sites in native telomere sequences, individual telomeres may differ in their density of consensus Rap1 sites, with some telomeres exhibiting lower densities. This variability may result in reduced MRX inhibition and, consequently, a greater propensity for these telomeres to undergo NHEJ-mediated fusions in contexts where other end protection mechanisms are missing.

Source data

To test whether observed telomere fusions could be linked to the alternate telomeric repeat sequences, we reanalyzed previous sequencing data of NHEJ-dependent fusions between native telomeres53, extending the analysis to 200 bp from the fusion points and to alternative Rap1-binding sites. Fusions were PCR-amplified from cells lacking Rif2 and Sir4 and sequenced by Nanopore (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 9)53. The absence of Rif2 and Sir4 allowed us to focus on Rap1-mediated effects, as in the other assays presented in this study. We quantified the frequency of Rap1 consensus and alternative sites (their relative occurrence) as a function of their distance from the fusion points. This analysis compared fusions from isogenic Rap1-proficient (rif2∆ sir4∆) and Rap1-deficient (rif2∆ sir4∆ rap1-(∆)) cells. Strikingly, in Rap1-proficient cells, the frequency of full-consensus Rap1-binding sites was lower within ~100 bp of the fusion points compared to Rap1-deficient cells (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 10a). Conversely, alternative motifs were more frequent within the same region in Rap1-proficient cells. As controls, we analyzed the sequences of unfused telomeres from the same cells and found that the distribution of Rap1 sites followed the opposite trend, with a relatively higher frequency of consensus sites near telomere ends (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Figs. 10b and 11). This indicates that the lower frequency of consensus Rap1-binding sites seen in fusions from Rap1-proficient cells was not present among the bulk of telomeres and must have resulted from a selection process favoring the fusion. Therefore, we can conclude that, when Rap1 is bound to telomeres, a higher density of Rap1 consensus binding site in the end-proximal regions of the telomeres enhances protection against NHEJ-mediated fusions. These findings demonstrate that the density and binding affinity of Rap1 over the final 100 bp of telomeres are determinants of telomere end protection in vivo, highlighting the physiological relevance of our observations. We propose that Rap1-mediated DNA coverage in this region inhibits MRX binding through steric hindrance, thereby protecting telomeres from MRX-dependent pathways, including NHEJ.

Discussion

A fundamental aspect of telomere protection is the regulation of the MRXMRN complex, which stands at the apex of the cellular response to DSBs, with key roles in DNA repair, end resection and checkpoint signaling. While MRXMRN is indispensable for genome stability, its uncontrolled activity at telomeres can lead to detrimental outcomes, including excessive degradation, telomere fusions and inappropriate checkpoint activation, ultimately compromising chromosome integrity and cell division. Understanding how cells restrain MRXMRN at telomeres is, therefore, essential for elucidating fundamental principles of genome maintenance.

Here, we demonstrate that Rap1 protects yeast telomeres through a highly specific mechanism involving its direct binding to telomeric DNA sequences. Tandem arrays of Rap1-binding sites effectively protect DSB ends from end-joining and resection by preventing MRX end capture. This end protection distinctly depends on the number of Rap1 molecules bound and, thus, on the length of the Rap1 array. Protection is most effective with arrays containing at least 8–10 Rap1-binding sites (approximately 150 bp), whereas shorter arrays progressively fail to protect, leading to increased occurrence of end-joining, resection and MRX binding in proportion to the decrease in Rap1 occupancy (Fig. 7). Notably, this mechanism is strictly limited to telomeric DNA, thereby allowing efficient MRX-dependent repair of subtelomeric and internal DSBs61.

Proposed model for Rap1-mediated MRX inhibition by steric hindrance at telomeres.

Rap1-mediated DNA coverage on more than 150 bp (corresponding to 8–10 or more Rap1-binding sites) prevents MRX binding at telomeres. Shorter length or discontinuity in Rap1 coverage exposes telomeres to MRX end capture and, therefore, MRX activities. Rap1 turnover on DNA can be a key limiting step for MRX inhibition, as transient dissociation exposes free DNA for MRX to bind. This implies that MRX is highly proficient at scanning and capturing end-proximal accessible DNA, necessitating a continuous and stable protein–DNA coverage. The Rap1 DNA-binding domain (Rap1DBD) is shown in blue, Mre11–Rad50 is shown in orange, Ku is shown in cantaloupe and DNA is shown in gray. For simplicity, Xrs2 and other telomere components, including the MRX inhibitor Rif2, are not represented.

While Rap1 binding to telomeric DNA is highly sequence specific, the resulting protection does not depend on direct protein–protein interactions, as similar protection could be achieved using noncognate lacO–LacI protein arrays and mixed arrays of Rap1 and LacI. Therefore, we propose that the quasicontinuous binding of Rap1 along telomeric DNA creates a physical barrier that prevents MRX from engaging with chromosome ends. This steric hindrance mechanism differs from the previously characterized Rif2-mediated inhibition, which relies on a specific interaction with the Rad50 ATPase head19,36,38,39. While Rif2 directly interferes with MRX activity to block DNA binding, Rap1 sterically excludes MRX from telomeric DNA. Together, these two mechanisms establish a robust system that controls MRX functions at telomeres, preventing inappropriate repair while preserving telomere stability.

Protein-bound DNA ends are effectively recognized and resected by MRX14–16. Additionally, MRX cobinds with Ku, a prerequisite for NHEJ, indicating that MRX is not hindered by Ku at DNA ends. The remarkable ability of MRX to bypass the complexity and diversity of DNA ends is essential for efficient DSB repair and likely relies on the dynamic structures of Rad50 (ref. 4) (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, such adaptability poses a challenge for MRX control at telomeres. Our findings show how Rap1 overcomes this challenge by restricting MRX’s access to free DNA over a sufficient distance from the DNA end. Discontinuities in Rap1 DNA coverage, either between the arrays and the DNA end or within the arrays, greatly alleviate MRX inhibition, indicating that even short stretches of freely accessible DNA are sufficient for MRX to capture the DNA end. This aligns with structural studies of MRX–DNA complexes, which show that Rad50 contacts less than 20 bp of DNA when holding DNA ends1,4–6 (Supplementary Fig. 12). In these DNA-holding conformations, the extensive DNA–Rad50 interface is incompatible with additional protein binding at the same location. Any attempt by MRX to capture Rap1-bound DNA is, therefore, likely to destabilize the MRX complex. Furthermore, the transition from the open to the zipped DNA-holding conformation may not occur in the absence of direct Rad50–DNA contacts prevented by Rap1 binding. Beyond steric hindrance, dense Rap1 coverage also stiffens DNA54,62, potentially further contributing to MRX inhibition by making the distal border of the arrays less accessible for MRX capture. Interestingly, the same features required for MRX inhibition (length of covered DNA, protein density and tight binding) are also critical for stalling condensin-mediated loop extrusion54, suggesting a shared mechanistic step between these SMC and SMC-like complexes. Overall, our findings shed light on both telomere protection and MRX mode of action.

The physiological relevance of MRX inhibition through steric hindrance is multifaceted. In yeast, telomere lengths typically range from 150 to 450 bp in wild-type cells (mean: ~300 bp)63–65—just above the threshold for maximal MRX inhibition by Rap1. This length likely optimizes telomere end protection by ensuring the effectiveness of this pathway and, in synergy with other Rap1-mediated mechanisms, ensures the absence of telomere fusions in wild-type cells43,53. Conversely, at abnormally short telomeres, reduced MRX inhibition could trigger a compensatory response to promote their elongation, thereby contributing to telomere length homeostasis. Supporting this idea, very short telomeres below 100–150 bp are rapidly elongated by telomerase, a salvage pathway known to depend on MRX–Tel1 (ref. 32). Rap1 can regulate this salvage pathway through two mechanisms: directly by steric hindrance and indirectly by recruiting the MRX inhibitor Rif2, assuming that Rif2-dependent inhibition is also proportional to telomere length66,67. In addition, the frequency of Rap1 consensus sites tends to increase toward the ends of unfused telomeres in the absence of Rif2 (Supplementary Fig. 11). This suggests that synthesis of consensus sites by telomerase could more effectively reduce MRX binding, thereby limiting further elongation, an interpretation that would be consistent with steric hindrance-mediated MRX inhibition contributing to telomere length homeostasis near equilibrium in cooperation with other Rap1-mediated mechanisms.

Tandem repeats and extensive protein–DNA coverage are conserved features of telomeres from yeast to mammals, suggesting that the steric hindrance mechanism identified in yeast may also operate in other species. In mammals, continuous coverage of the end-proximal region of telomeres by TRF1 and TRF2 may itself contribute to telomere end protection and stability by limiting promiscuous MRN activities, acting in synergy with the inhibition mediated by TRF2 iDDR. Relevant to this hypothesis, yeast cells engineered to carry human-like telomeric sequences have been proposed to rely on a TRF1/2-like protein for telomere stability68–70. In contrast to yeast and germline cells, many human somatic cells lack telomerase, leading to progressive telomere shortening with successive division until one or more telomeres reach a critically short length71. This triggers constitutive MRN–ATM activation, ultimately inducing p21/p53-dependent senescence72,73. By restricting the proliferative capacity of somatic cells, telomere-driven replicative senescence functions as a potent tumor-suppressor mechanism and a potential contributor to aging. It would be relevant to determine whether the length-dependent activation of this pathway in humans is driven by a relaxation of an MRN inhibition similar to the one uncovered in yeast.

Purification of recombinant proteins

The MRX complex was purified from yMRnXf-tda1delta yeast cells (strain construction described below). For the production of the complex, yMRnXf-tda1delta cells were cultured in 50 ml of YPAR overnight. The next day, cells were inoculated in 2 L of YPAR and cultured shaking at 30 °C. Once the cell density reached 2 × 108 cells per ml, expression was induced with 20 g L−1 galactose for 3 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3,000g for 15 min, washed with cold 30 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5 with 1 M sorbitol and centrifuged at 3,000g for 15 min. The pellet was washed with lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10% glycerol and 300 mM NaCl) and centrifuged again at 3,000g for 15 min. The supernatant was removed and pellet volume was measured. The pellet was resuspended with 0.4 volumes of lysis buffer and supplemented with protease inhibitors (1:400 (v/v) protease inhibitory cocktail (P8340, Sigma), 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and 30 mg ml−1 leupeptin) and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol. The cells were then frozen dropwise in liquid nitrogen and lysed using a freezer mill (Sartorius) without thawing using the following program: ten cycles of 2 min each at 15 cycles per second, with 1 min of cooling time in between. The resulting powder was transferred into 50-ml tubes and stored at −80 °C until further processing.

The powder was resuspended with three volumes of buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10% glycerol, 400 mM NaCl, 1:400 (v/v) protease inhibitory cocktail (P8340, Sigma), 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 30 mg ml−1 leupeptin, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 20 mM imidazole and 0.1% NP40. The extract was clarified by centrifugation at 50,000g for 30 min at 4 °C. The clarified extract was incubated with 10 ml of preequilibrated Ni-NTA resin (Qiagen) for 1 h in rotation. The resin was separated by centrifugation at 1,000g for 2 min at 4 °C and washed once batchwise in wash buffer containing 30 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10% glycerol, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 20 mM imidazole and 0.1% NP40. The resin was separated from the supernatant, transferred to a plastic column and washed extensively with wash buffer. The resin was washed with buffer A (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10% glycerol, 100 mM NaCl and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol) to reduce the salt concentration and remove the detergent and eluted with buffer A supplemented with 250 mM imidazole. The relevant elution fractions as determined by Bradford assay were pooled, diluted five times with buffer A and loaded on a preequilibrated 1-ml HiTrap SP HP column (Cytiva) on an AKTA system (Cytiva) at 0.8 ml min−1. After loading, the resin was washed with buffer A and the protein was eluted with an 8-ml gradient to 100% buffer B (buffer A with 1 M NaCl). Fractions were analyzed by SDS–PAGE and pooled accordingly.

In vitro end-joining assays

The in vitro end-joining reactions were performed with 0.2 nM (in molecules) substrate in ligation buffer containing 60 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 5 mM dithiothreitol, 10 mM magnesium chloride, 1 mM ATP and 0.05 mg ml−1 BSA (New England Biolabs) in a final volume of 15 μl. The master mix was prepared on ice and aliquoted in the different tubes. The substrate was incubated with Rap1 (where indicated) for 5 min at 30 °C. Reactions were moved on ice, supplemented with yeast Ku complex and incubated for 3 min on ice. Afterward, Nej1, Dnl4–Lif1 and MRX were added to the reactions as indicated and the ligation was performed for 1 h at 25 °C. Reactions were stopped by adding 5 µl of 2% STOP solution (150 mM EDTA, 2% SDS, 30% glycerol and 1 mg ml−1 bromophenol blue) and 1 µl of 14–22 mg ml−1 Proteinase K (Roche) and incubating for 1 h at 50 °C. Ligation products were separated on 1.5% agarose gel. The gels were dried on DE81 chromatography paper (Whatman), exposed to phosphor screens and imaged with a Typhoon imager. Quantitation was performed using ImageJ. For the T5 treatment of the ligation products, end-joining reactions were performed as described above with the addition of an incubation step with 2 U of T5 exonuclease (New England Biolabs) for 45 min at 37 °C. For the order of addition experiments, ATP was omitted from the ligase buffer and proteins were added as indicated with incubation of 5 min at 25 °C in between additions. ATP was added just before incubation at 25 °C for 1 h. Afterward, reactions were processed as described above.

ChIP–qPCR method

ChIP was performed as previously described53. For each ChIP culture, 40 ml of cells (107 cells per ml) were fixed with 1% of formaldehyde for 5 min while rotating. The reaction was stopped by adding 2 ml of 2.5 M glycine. Fixed cells were then washed once with prechilled HBS buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.8 and 150 mM NaCl) and frozen in 600 µl of ChIP lysis buffer with protease inhibitors (50 mM HEPES pH 7.8, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP40 and 1% sodium deoxycholate). Cells were lysed using a Precellys homogenizer with zirconium and silica beads (BioSpec Products, 11079105z). After bead removal, the lysate was pelleted, resuspended in 300 µl of ChIP lysis buffer and sonicated using a Diagenod Bioruptor (high setting) for three cycles of 30 s on and 30 s off in a continuously cooled (4 °C) water bath. The sonicated samples were clarified at maximum speed for 30 min at 4 °C. Then, 10 µl of the 300-µl total volume was reserved as an input control, as described below.

Immunoprecipitation was performed by incubating the remaining lysate for 60 min at 4 °C with agitation using either anti-Myc (Sigma-Aldrich, 05-724) or anti-Rap1 (gift from D. Shore) antibodies. This was followed by a 120-min incubation with magnetic beads crosslinked to sheep anti-mouse or anti-rabbit antibodies at 4 °C with rotation. For the washing steps, the beads were collected using a magnet and washed as follows: first, in 1 ml of ChIP lysis buffer, before transferring to a new tube, and then sequentially in the following buffers: (1) 50 mM HEPES pH 7.8, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA and 0.03% SDS; (2) 50 mM HEPES pH 7.8, 1 M NaCl and 1 mM EDTA; (3) 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 250 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP40 and 0.5% sodium deoxycholate; and (4) 20 mM Tris pH 7.5 and 0.1 mM EDTA. Samples were eluted in 155 µl of elution buffer (buffer 4 + 1% freshly added SDS) and reverse-crosslinked in 140 µl of elution buffer at 65 °C for 16 h. The samples were then treated with proteinase K in TE buffer for 60 min at 37 °C, followed by DNA purification using the DNA cleanup kit (Thermo Scientific, K0831).

qPCR was performed using PowerSYBR green PCR master mix (Applied Biosystems, 4367659) on an Applied Biosystems machine. The program used for amplification was as follows: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 44 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. The percentage relative to the input control (IN) was calculated using the following formula: % IP/IN = (E)Ct(input) − Ct(IP), where E is in the PCR efficiency of the primers. The Ct(input) values were corrected for dilution using the formula Ct′ = Ct − logE(30).

In vivo NHEJ assay

To determine survival to I-SceI cleavage, cells were grown to saturation for 16 h at 30 °C in synthetic medium lacking uracil with glucose (2%) (108 cells per ml), diluted in water and spread on synthetic medium plates with galactose (2%). Colonies were counted after 3 days at 30 °C.

In vivo resection assay for Rap1 arrays

Cells grown to an optical density of 0.6 in synthetic glycerol lactate medium lacking uracil were arrested in in G2/M with 5 µg ml−1 nocodazole for 3 h at 30 °C. I-SceI expression was induced by galactose addition (3%) and time points were taken for DNA extraction. Genomic DNA was cut with StuI before gel electrophoresis separation (16 h, 80 V). Southern blots were performed with a mix of two 32P-labeled probes hybridizing TIM9 and ADE1. Signal quantification was performed using a Typhoon 5 imager and the ImageQuant software.

In vivo resection assay for LacI/lacO

We performed resection assays as previously described56. Briefly, we grew yeast cells exponentially in YPD (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone and 2% glucose) at 30 °C with vigorous shaking. To arrest the cultures in G2/M, we added 1% DMSO and 20 µg ml−1 nocodazole and continued culturing for 1.5 generation times (~2 h). Then, we depleted the AID-tagged proteins by the addition of 2 mM indole-3-acetic acid sodium salt and further culturing for 1.5 h. To induce DSB formation, we added 2 µM β-estradiol. We harvested cells at several time points by centrifugation, washed with TE (10 mM Tris and 1 mM EDTA, pH 8) containing 0.1% sodium azide and stored cell pellets at −20 °C. We extracted genomic DNA using the MasterPure yeast DNA purification kit (Lucigen), including RNase treatment. We diluted the genomic DNA extracts to 2.5 ng µl−1 in CutSmart buffer (New England Biolabs) and digested 100 and 150 ng with 5 U of RsaI and MseI (New England Biolabs), respectively. We evaluated DSB formation at the inserted MAT SrfI site and resection past the MseI sites 120 and 300 bp upstream, as well as the RsaI site (129 + x) bp downstream by qPCR. Per sample, we analyzed triplicates each containing 13 ng (un)digested genomic DNA, 300 nM of each primer and 1× SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR green supermix (Bio-Rad) in a total volume of 10 μl on a CFX384 real-time system (Bio-Rad) with the following protocol: 10 min of initial denaturation at 95 °C and 40 cycles of 10 s of denaturation at 95 °C and 1 min of annealing and extension at 58 °C. Supplementary Table 2 lists the qPCR primers used. We analyzed qPCR data with statistical software R (version 3.6.2) using custom scripts (available upon request).

We evaluated the lacO array size in all strains at the time of DSB induction by PCR using primers oRG46 and oRG745 and extracted genomic DNA as template to confirm that the lacO arrays had not contracted.

Telomere fusion analysis and Rap1 site mapping

Analysis of telomere sequences was performed as previously described53. Fused and unfused telomeres were amplified by PCR using DNA from three independent cell cultures. The resulting PCR products were pooled and subjected to Nanopore sequencing. Base calling was carried out by ONT Guppy software in ‘super accurate’ mode with the dna_r9.4.1_450bps_sup.cfg configuration file. Telomeric sequences were identified on the basis of the motif 5′-G0–3(TG1–3)≥3[N0–2(TG1–3)≥3]≥1T0–1-3′, which allows up to two noncanonical bases every three TG1–3 repeats. Only sequences containing at least 200 bp of TG1–3 repeats that met this quality threshold were retained for analysis. Fusion points were defined as the junction where a reverse C-rich telomeric sequence immediately follows a forward G-rich telomeric sequence. For unfused telomeres, 3′ ends were positioned at the transition from a forward G-rich telomeric sequence to a downstream poly(C) tract.

Rap1 consensus and alternative binding sites were identified using Seqkit. Distances to fusion points refer to the position of the base closest to junction within the Rap1 site. For unfused telomeres, distances to the 3′ end were calculated relative to the last base upstream of the poly(C) stretch. We used dedicated perl scripts to draw Rap1 site density plots. Because of the inherent error rate of Nanopore sequencing, some genuine sites were not detected and a smaller number of false-positive sites were introduced. Sequencing of the G-rich strand proved more accurate than that of the telomere C-rich strand, as evidenced by lower sequence divergence in the TG1–3 repeats and consequently higher apparent Rap1 site densities. Scripts are available from GitLab (https://gitlab.com/cnrgh/nanotel/rap1-tools).

Western blotting

We performed western blotting according to standard procedures described previously80. Briefly, we prepared protein extracts from 1.5 × 108 cells as described previously84. We ran 10-μl protein extracts on 9% polyacrylamide gels using the Mini-PROTEAN system (Bio-Rad), transferred on PVDF membranes using the iBlot 2 system, stained with Ponceau S solution and blocked with 3% milk in TBST (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl and 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.5). We used primary antibodies anti-FLAG (Sigma, F1804; 1:2,000 in TBST + 5% BSA + 0.05% sodium azide), anti-c-Myc (Santa Cruz, 9E10, sc-40; 1:1,000 in TBST + 5% BSA + 0.05% sodium azide) and anti-HA (Roche, 12CA5; 1:1,000 in TBST + 5% BSA + 0.05% sodium azide) to detect Sgs1–AID–6×FLAG and Dna2–AID–6×FLAG, OsTir1–9×Myc and LacI–NLS–3×HA, respectively. We used mouse IgGκ-binding protein conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (Santa Cruz, sc-516102; 1:5,000 in TBST + 1% milk) as secondary antibody and visualized using the SuperSignal West Femto maximum-sensitivity substrate (Thermo Scientific) and the Amersham Imager 600.

Pulse field gel electrophoresis (PFGE)

Yeast DNA embedded in agarose plugs was prepared as described previously55. About 1 × 108–2 × 108 cells were harvested, washed with 1 ml of 1% Triton, 250 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris pH 7.5 and frozen at −80 °C. Pellets were resuspended in 120 μl of 50 mM EDTA and 10 mM Tris pH 7.5, quickly warmed to 42 °C and mixed with 200 μl of prewarmed 1% agarose LMP. The suspension was distributed into 80-μl wells placed on a cool surface. The plugs were extruded and incubated overnight at 37 °C in 1 ml of 500 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris pH 7.5, 40 μg ml−1 of Zymolyase-100T (MP Biomedical, 32093). The following day, plugs were incubated 6 h at 53 °C in 1 ml of 500 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris pH 8.0, 1% N-laurylsarcosyl and 0.5 mg ml−1 proteinase K (Invitrogen, 100005393). Plugs were washed for 30 min three times in 1 ml of 50 mM EDTA and 10 mM Tris pH 7.5. PFGE was carried out in a 0.9% agarose gel in 0.5× Tris–borate–EDTA buffer at 14 °C with a CHEF DR III from Bio-Rad with a constant time of 20 s in 24 h (to resolve the whole karyotype). DNA was labeled with Gelred and the gel was scanned using a Typhoon 5 imager and the ImageQuant software.

SwitchSENSE

All switchSENSE experiments were performed on the heliX+ biosensor using ADP-48-2-0 heliX adaptor chips (Dynamic Biosensors). The chip was regenerated with a high-pH regeneration solution (HK-REG-1, Dynamic Biosensors) and freshly functionalized with the target DNA sequence (ELLA Biotech) hybridized to red adaptor strands (AS-1-Ra, Dynamic Biosensors) before each measurement or concentration determination. The experiments were performed in TE140 buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 140 mM NaCl, 50 µM EDTA, 50 µM EGTA and 0.05% Tween-20, pH 7.4) buffer. The kinetic interaction was tracked by fluorescence proximity sensing, where changes in the local environment of the reporter dye affect its fluorescence signal. A simultaneous measurement on a second detection spot without the target DNA sequence was recorded and used as a real-time control to monitor and subtract unspecific binding. In addition to this reference, a blank run (buffer only) was also recorded and subtracted to obtain the net interaction signal. The experiments were performed at a negative potential, a sampling rate of 1 Hz, a temperature of 25 °C and association and dissociation flow rates of 200 µl min−1 and 500 µl min−1, respectively. Experiment design, workflow and data analysis were performed with the heliOS software (Dynamic Biosensors). All data was generated at least in triplicates and the results were averaged.
