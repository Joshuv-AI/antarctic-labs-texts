Amplified local cooling effect of forestation in warming Europe
Authors: Yitao Li, Jun Ge, Hua Wu, Ronglin Tang, Yuanliang Cheng, Xiangyang Liu, Yi Wang, Wei Zhao, Caixia Gao, Si-Bo Duan, Qian Shi, Pei Leng, Enyu Zhao, Guangjian Yan, Xiaoning Song, Chenghu Zhou, Zhao-Liang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractForests exhibit local cooling or warming effects compared to adjacent openlands through biophysical processes. These temperature effects are predicted by earth system models to evolve in response to climate change. However, such temporal patterns remain unconstrained by observations and have not been detected in historical records. Here, by comparing the satellite observations of spatially nearby forests and openlands over the last two decades, we quantify temporal trends in local land surface temperature (LST) effects of forest change in Europe. During winter, the daytime warming effect of potential forestation weakens and reverses to cooling (−0.142 K/decade) with decreasing snow cover, as forests show less pronounced surface darkening trends than openlands. During summer, the daytime cooling effect intensifies (−0.188 K/decade) because forests remain more physiologically and hydrologically resilient to increasing soil dryness. These negative trends are broadly supported by state-of-the-art earth system models, though substantial inter-model variability persists. Given continued climate change, we emphasize the need to consider the dynamics of biophysical effects when comprehensive forest-related climate policies are formed.Subject terms: Climate-change impacts, Forest ecology

Introduction

Forests provide numerous benefits to humans and the planet, such as producing food and energy, reducing soil erosion, and increasing water availability in downwind areas1–4. Increasing forest cover through restoration practices plays a crucial role in addressing global warming by regulating the hydrological cycle and enhancing cloud formation5–7. In addition to the global impacts, forests exhibit local cooling or warming effects due to their unique biophysical characteristics that regulate surface energy and water fluxes7,8. The biophysical effects of forests on local climate have garnered particular attention in recent years, as they can significantly intensify or offset the climate mitigation effects of carbon sequestration9,10.

Using numerical models or multi-scale observations, previous studies have thoroughly investigated the impacts of re/afforestation, deforestation, and forest degradation on local land surface temperature (LST) and their biophysical mechanisms11–13. At night, forests warm the land surface compared to openlands through two mechanisms. First, forest canopies absorb solar radiation and store heat during the daytime, subsequently release this energy at night. Second, the nighttime land surface is typically cooler than the near-surface air, forming a stable boundary layer where downward atmospheric heat fluxes warm the land surface. Forests, with their greater surface roughness, can intensify turbulent mixing and enhance this heat exchange process14,15. During the daytime, forests exhibit two competing effects compared to non-forest vegetation: (a) the non-radiative cooling effect driven by higher evapotranspiration (ET) rates (when water supply is sufficient)16, and (b) the radiative warming effect resulting from lower albedo17. The net temperature response depends on the balance between these opposing effects18, which is predominantly governed by the background climatic or environmental conditions (e.g., temperature, snow cover, and soil moisture)19. For instance, during boreal winter when ET is rendered inactive by low temperatures, the dense forest canopies obscure bright snow, which remains exposed in openlands. This snow masking effect creates a strong albedo contrast between forests and openlands and causes forests to absorb more solar radiation compared to surrounding openlands, resulting in the local warming effect4. In warm and wet tropics, forests demonstrate pronounced local cooling effects due to their enhanced ET rates relative to other vegetation types20. Limited soil moisture in arid regions or during drought events can constrain forests’ evaporative advantage, enabling radiative warming to dominate. Consequently, the magnitude of forests’ cooling effect at the seasonal or annual scale may depend on the duration of sufficient water availability21.

Given the profound influence of background climate on the biophysical processes, it is reasonable to infer that the regulation of forests on local temperature will shift in response to rising CO2 concentration, global warming, or varying water availability. In recent years, studies have revealed the role of rising temperature, changing aerosols or internal climate variability in the temporal dynamics of the biophysical temperature effects22–25. However, these findings are based on model simulations, which are subject to inaccuracies in the physical parameterization scheme and representation of land-atmosphere interaction26,27. Besides, prior simulations only show significant changes under high-emission or other ideal scenarios22–25. On the other hand, observational studies that assess the biophysical effects of forestation are generally performed in a relatively static manner13,18,28. These studies focus on spatial, seasonal or diurnal heterogeneity of the biophysical effect over a specific year or study period but ignore the potential long-term variations. Overall, direct observational evidence of temporal dynamics in local temperature effects of forests is still lacking in historical records. Evaluating these dynamic patterns and their driving mechanisms is useful for constraining model results and forming better forest-related climate policies.

This study aims to fill the knowledge gap regarding the temporal variations (especially trends) in the local LST effects of potential forestation and the underlying biophysical mechanisms. It should be noted that the evaluated LST effects are local effects and should be treated as the reference for climate adaptation rather than global climate mitigation. We focus on Europe for the following reasons. (1) Both radiative and non-radiative processes play important roles in the local LST effects of European forests, facilitating the exploration of different biophysical mechanisms underlying the long-term variation of the forests’ LST effects. (2) Thanks to the dense distribution of weather stations, high-quality gridded meteorological data in Europe can help to explore potential links between changes in biophysical processes and the background climate. (3) Europe is a hotspot of climate change and forestation projects (3 billion trees to be planted in the European Union by 2030 according to the European Green Deal29), making our assessment more relevant and instructive. Our study aims to answer the following three questions: (1) What are the temporal patterns of the observed local LST effects of European forests in the recent two decades? (2) What climate factors drive these temporal patterns? (3) What is the biophysical mechanism behind this climatic control? To address these questions, we first use observations from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) and space-for-time method to estimate the local LST effects of forestation in Europe28. We then assess the temporal dynamics of the local LST effects and explore their relationships with different biophysical processes and potential climate drivers using statistical methods. To examine whether models can capture the observed temporal patterns, we evaluate the simulated trend of local LST effects of European forests in four state-of-the-art Earth system models against satellite observations. Our results could inform comprehensive assessments of climate effects induced by future forestation in Europe.

Results

Intensified local daytime cooling effects of forests

We first investigate the multi-year mean impacts of potential forestation on local LST in Europe winter and summer (ΔLSTw and ΔLSTs) (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Results show that forests exhibit a slight winter warming effect (0.14 ± 0.27 K, spatial mean ± standard deviation) compared to spatially nearby openlands, primarily driven by nighttime warming (0.25 ± 0.24 K), while the daytime signal remains negligible (0.01 ± 0.46 K). In contrast, forests exhibit strong land surface cooling in summer (−0.75 ± 0.41 K), as the daytime cooling (−2.11 ± 0.98 K) is minimally offset by a much smaller nighttime warming (0.25 ± 0.23 K). Our results confirm previous conclusions of the contrasting LST effects of mid-latitude forestation at seasonal and diurnal scales driven by different biophysical processes14,28.

We then focus on the temporal variations in ΔLSTw and ΔLSTs over the recent two decades. Our results show declining winter warming effects at the daily scale, with a trend of −0.052 K/decade (p = 0.07) (Fig. 1a). This decrease in the daily mean ΔLSTw is dominated by a negative trend of the daytime ΔLSTw (−0.142 K/decade, p = 0.04), while the nighttime ΔLSTw demonstrates only interannual variability with a negligible temporal trend (0.002 K/decade, p = 0.97) (Fig. 1a). Spatially, 81% of grids show negative daytime ΔLSTw trends, with only 10% of grids mainly concentrated in southeastern Europe showing significant trends (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1b).

During the summer nighttime, the warming effect shows an increasing trend of 0.040 K/decade (p = 0.01), but the magnitude is only about one-fifth of the daytime ΔLSTs negative trend (−0.188 K/decade, p = 0.01) (Fig. 1c). As a result, the daily mean cooling effects have been enhanced in the recent two decades (−0.083 K/decade, p = 0.01) (Fig. 1c). Spatially, about 89% of grids show negative daytime ΔLSTs trends, and 34% of grids (mainly distributed in central Europe) show significant negative trends (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1d). These results show that in both summer and winter, the daytime cooling effects of potential forestation have been significantly enhanced. Further analyses mainly focus on the biophysical mechanisms behind the negative trends in daytime temperature effects.

Impact of snow cover on daytime ΔLSTw dynamics

During winter, the sign and absolute value of ΔLSTw are generally dominated by the magnitude of the radiative process4,8,18. However, the negative trend in ΔLSTw could potentially arise from two mechanisms: (a) climate-driven alterations in net shortwave radiation absorption differences between forests and openlands (ΔNSRw), or (b) winter warming-induced enhancement in forests’ evaporative cooling advantage, represented by latent heat differences between the two land cover types (ΔLEw). To identify the dominant mechanism, we estimate the equivalent perturbations of the two processes on the surface energy balance (Supplementary Text 1).

Our analysis reveals that only ΔNSRw exhibits a statistically significant trend (−0.86 w m−2/decade, p = 0.05), whereas the ΔLEw trend remains non-significant (−0.02 w m−2/decade, p = 0.97) (Supplementary Fig. 3). Correlation analyses further support the greater importance of ΔNSRw affecting ΔLSTw dynamics, and confirm that this impact is mediated through modifications in the albedo difference (Δαw) between forests and openlands (Supplementary Figs. 4–6). Specifically, we show that forests are darker than openlands, as evidenced by negative Δαw values (−0.077, Supplementary Fig. 7). However, Δαw shows a positive trend (0.018 unitless/decade, p = 0.04) (Fig. 2a), indicating that the albedo contrast between forests and openlands has been diminishing. Spatially, Δαw trends are positive in 90% areas, and about 15% of grids (concentrated in central and eastern Europe) are statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2b).

The revealed positive Δαw trend can be traced back to the stronger albedo decrease in openlands (multi-year mean: 0.236; trend: −0.036 unitless/decade, p = 0.04) than in forests (multi-year mean: 0.159; trend: −0.017 unitless/decade, p = 0.03) from 2004 to 2023 (Fig. 2a). This observed divergence in surface darkening trends may be associated with snow cover (SCw) reductions (Supplementary Fig. 8), and differences in the snow-albedo feedback intensity in different land covers30–34. We confirm that the surface darkening is more susceptible to SCw decrease in openlands than forests, as evidenced by the difference in spatial regression slopes between albedo trends and SCw trends (0.47 vs. 0.22, Fig. 2c). The possible mechanism is that snow tends to be masked by tree canopies and retained for longer durations in forest ecosystems, resulting in a lower sensitivity of forest albedo to snowpack. The divergent responses of albedo to SCw are also supported by the temporal regression results, which suggest that openland albedo is about two times more sensitive than forest albedo (Supplementary Fig. 9). Notably, the used SCw is a function of snow mass (represented by the snow water equivalent)35, which is more governed by the macroclimate and thus shows no difference between forests and nearby openlands. These results indicate that although forest albedo tends to decline due to SCw loss, the relative darkening effect of forests and the additional solar radiation absorbed by forests (compared to adjacent openlands) have diminished in the recent two decades.

The positive Δαw trend induced by snow dynamics further controls the temporal change of ΔLSTw. Specifically, in those grids with more pronounced SCw reductions, the Δαw trends is larger and the negative daytime ΔLSTw trends are also stronger (Fig. 2d). Meanwhile, the temporal relationships between SCw and ΔLSTw suggest that in those years with lower snow contents, the daytime cooling effect of forests is also stronger (slope=1.08 ± 0.41), while the impact of SCw on the nighttime ΔLSTw is insignificant (slope=0.22 ± 0.20) (Fig. 2e). Overall, our statistical evidence supports the control of SCw on daytime ΔLSTw dynamics through weakening the albedo difference between forests and openlands. Notably, the observed ΔLSTw trend should not be attributed to structural or physiological changes in forests, but rather to the more pronounced albedo reduction in openlands in a warming world. Furthermore, though not yet observed, rising temperatures may exceed a critical threshold that enhances cold-season ET in the future. This may strengthen the evaporative cooling effect of forests under future warming conditions, especially in coniferous forests with year-round transpiration activity.

Dominant role of increased soil dryness in daytime ΔLSTs dynamics

In contrast to the winter warming effect, European forests show significant summer daytime cooling compared to nearby openlands (Supplementary Fig. 2), driven by non-radiative mechanisms18. Over the past two decades, Europe has experienced a pronounced increase in summer air temperature (ATs), exacerbating atmospheric and soil dryness. Specifically, we find a significant upward trend in vapor pressure deficit (VPDs) and a concurrent decline in surface soil moisture (SSMs). Additionally, we show an increasing trend in summer downward solar radiation (DSRs) and decreasing trends in precipitation (Ps) and wind speeds (WSs) (Supplementary Fig. 10). We hypothesize that these climate changes may differentially influence turbulent fluxes in forests and openlands, thereby contributing to the observed temporal dynamics of ΔLSTs. Notably, the climate variables may themselves be influenced by the presence of forests (e.g., soil moisture retention and evaporative cooling of trees). However, it should be emphasized that the climatic drivers used in our analysis are the grid means at the coarse resolution, reflecting large-scale patterns rather than microclimates. Our analysis tends to capture the different responses of forests and openlands to background climate change.

Temporal correlation analyses using VPDs, ATs, DSRs, Ps and WSs as the potential drivers reveal strong relationships between daytime ΔLSTs and climatic variables except WSs (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 11). However, after accounting for covariates, the partial correlations between the daytime ΔLSTs and DSRs, Ps, and ATs are greatly reduced and even reverse in sign. In contrast, VPDs maintains a predominant negative correlation with daytime ΔLSTs, with the mean partial correlation coefficient of −0.42 (Fig. 3a). Notably, we can also find strong positive partial correlations between SSMs and ΔLSTs (Fig. 3b) when replacing VPDs with the SSMs, the other factor describing the dryness stress. This is due to the strong coupling between SSMs and VPDs at the annual scale (r = 0.92, Supplementary Fig. 12). These findings raise the following question: whether variations in atmosphere or soil dryness are the primary factor governing changes in ΔLSTs over the past two decades?

To answer this question, we then use the binning regression method to further disentangle the respective contributions of VPDs and SSMs36. We conduct this analysis using the spatial samples of VPDs and SSMs trends with sufficient data for decoupling the two factors (Supplementary Fig. 13). We confirm the strong spatial correlation between SSMs and VPDs trends (r = −0.65) when all spatial samples are used. However, after stratifying either SSMs or VPDs trends into 10 quantile-based bins, correlations between the two variables show substantial attenuation (Fig. 3c), suggesting the two variables are decoupled. Then, we calculate the correlation between SSMs (or VPDs) trends and ΔLSTs trends in each VPDs (or SSMs) trend bin. We find the mean r between VPDs trends and ΔLSTs trends is only −0.11, whereas the mean r between SSMs trends and ΔLSTs trends reaches 0.40 (Fig. 3d). This result suggests that the change in SSMs is more likely to be the driver of ΔLSTs dynamics.

The critical role of SSMs is corroborated by non-linear statistical analysis using a Random Forest (RF) model (Supplementary Text 2). Variable importance analysis demonstrates that SSMs exerts a stronger influence than VPDs in predicting ΔLSTs dynamics. Contribution analysis also reveals that SSMs changes account for the largest portion (−0.152 K/decade, p < 0.05) of the simulated negative ΔLSTs trend (−0.181 K/decade, p < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 14), highlighting its dominant role in driving the observed enhanced forest cooling effect.

We also evaluate the sensitivity of summer latent heat (LEs) to VPDs in both forests and openlands using eddy covariance observations from European sites (Supplementary Text 3). The sensitivity analysis is based on the Penman-Monteith framework, which accounts for the influence of VPDs on surface resistance37. If VPDs is the main driver of enhanced forest cooling effect, we expect to observe greater positive LEs sensitivity in forests compared to openlands, in which case forests can produce stronger evaporative cooling effects in the context of rising VPDs. However, we find slightly higher positive sensitivity in openlands than forests, although the difference is not significant (Supplementary Table 1). This in-situ evidence, combined with our statistical results, suggests that VPDs may not explain the variation in daytime ΔLSTs, while the decline in SSMs appears to be the real driver.

We further conduct structural equation modeling (SEM) to elucidate the biophysical mechanisms underlying the impact of varying SSMs on daytime ΔLSTs dynamics (Fig. 4a). Our SEM incorporates two key biophysical mediators: the leaf area index difference (ΔLAIs) and latent heat flux difference (ΔLEs) between nearby forests and openlands. The path effect (mean ± standard error) is used to describe the strength and direction of the relationship. Following previous studies, we build SEMs at the grid scale and calculate the mean path effects of all models38,39. The analysis reveals three distinct pathways of SSMs affecting ΔLSTs:

SSMs → ΔLAIs → ΔLSTs. SSMs exerts a significant negative effect on ΔLAIs (−0.41 ± 0.15). Then, ΔLAIs demonstrates a negative effect on ΔLSTs (−0.19 ± 0.09). This pathway suggests that reduced SSMs can amplify the greenness difference in forests and openlands, thereby enhancing forests’ cooling advantage through improved convective heat dissipation.

SSMs → ΔLAIs → ΔLSTs. SSMs exerts a significant negative effect on ΔLAIs (−0.41 ± 0.15). Then, ΔLAIs demonstrates a negative effect on ΔLSTs (−0.19 ± 0.09). This pathway suggests that reduced SSMs can amplify the greenness difference in forests and openlands, thereby enhancing forests’ cooling advantage through improved convective heat dissipation.

SSMs → ΔLEs → ΔLSTs. SSMs shows a negative effect on ΔLEs (−0.25 ± 0.11), followed by a strong negative influence of ΔLEs on ΔLSTs (−0.54 ± 0.09). This pathway indicates that declining SSMs may impose less water limitation to forests compared to openlands, enabling forests to maintain stronger relative evaporative cooling through differential stomatal regulation (with greenness remaining constant).

SSMs → ΔLEs → ΔLSTs. SSMs shows a negative effect on ΔLEs (−0.25 ± 0.11), followed by a strong negative influence of ΔLEs on ΔLSTs (−0.54 ± 0.09). This pathway indicates that declining SSMs may impose less water limitation to forests compared to openlands, enabling forests to maintain stronger relative evaporative cooling through differential stomatal regulation (with greenness remaining constant).

SSMs → ΔLAIs → ΔLEs → ΔLSTs. SSMs-induced change in ΔLAIs subsequently enhances ΔLEs (0.62 ± 0.06), ultimately influencing ΔLSTs. This demonstrates that SSMs reduction amplifies evaporative cooling effects of forests by increasing the vegetation greenness contrast with openlands.

The pathway effects, representing the strength and direction of the mechanism, are quantified as the product of all constituent path coefficients (Fig. 4b). Results reveal that the second and third pathways (both mediated by ΔLEs) contribute equally to ΔLSTs variation (about 0.14 ± 0.06), while the impact first pathway is weaker (0.08 ± 0.05). These findings suggest SSMs can modulate ΔLSTs dynamics through multiple mechanisms, with forest evaporative cooling regulation emerging as the dominant process.

During summer, the vegetation greenness and ET rates are generally higher in forests than in adjacent openlands40. The negative effects of SSMs on ΔLAIs and ΔLEs revealed by the SEM analysis demonstrate that the decreased SSMs can amplify these greenness and ET differences between forests and openlands. Given the well-established constraint of soil moisture on vegetation growth41, we tend to attribute the negative effects of SSMs to forests’ stability and resilience to increasing soil dryness (i.e., lower sensitivity of forest greenness and ET to SSMs). Specifically, forests possess deeper root systems and enhanced soil moisture storage capacity, enabling them to sustain their cooling efficiency under climate change compared to openland vegetation.

Previous studies indicate that soil moisture depletion in Europe can exacerbate warming through land-atmosphere feedback mechanisms during heatwave events42,43. A comparative analysis of temperature responses across land cover types shows that openlands experience pronounced warming during long-lasting heatwaves, whereas forests exhibit moderate warming due to their conservative water-use strategies and sustained evaporative cooling44. Our results provide observational evidence that forests’ capacity to mitigate water deficit-induced warming persists not only during heatwave events but also across the interannual scales. However, it should be noted that our observational results represent the impact of soil moisture within a limited range under current climate regimes. If persistent warming or reduced precipitation drives soil moisture below a critical threshold, forests may lose their stability and the evaporative cooling capacity, thus failing to compensate for the warming effect induced by the dark canopy.

Evaluation of ΔLSTs and ΔLSTw trends in four CMIP6 models

To examine whether the observed trends of the biophysical LST effects of European forests can be reproduced by Earth System Models (ESMs), we use the output from the simulation of the historical period in the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) archive. The simulated effect of forests is isolated by a similar method for observational data, using differences between the forest and openland tiles (forest minus openland) within each model grid cell in four state-of-the-art ESMs that provide subgrid information (CESM2, CNRM-ESM2-1, GFDL-ESM4 and UKESM1-0-LL).

The multi-model mean suggests widespread declining trends of daily mean ΔLSTw (−0.068 K/decade, p < 0.01) and ΔLSTs (−0.054 K/decade, p < 0.01) over Europe during 1985–2014 (Fig. 5a, d). These trends align well with the observed ones (Fig. 1a, c), and the discrepancy in magnitudes can be partly attributed to differences in the period and spatial coverage of the observation and simulations (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16). We should emphasize that the current models perform better in simulating negative trends in ΔLSTw than in ΔLSTs, as evidenced by the large intermodel differences in ΔLSTs trends simulated by four models. Specifically, the ΔLSTw trend values are similar across the four models (Fig. 5a). While for ΔLSTs, CESM2 simulates a significant declining trend of −0.133 K/decade, which dominates the multi-model mean signal. CNRM-ESM2-1, GFDL-ESM4 and UKESM1-0-LL simulate much weaker trends of −0.042, −0.018, and −0.027 K/decade, respectively (Fig. 5d). In addition, differences are found in the multi-year mean ΔLSTw or ΔLSTs from the four models (Fig. 5a, d), which can be attributed to the biases in surface energy partitioning and albedo responses to land cover changes45.

We further evaluate the model representation of biophysical processes behind the negative trends in ΔLSTw. Observational results indicate that the decreasing trend in the openland albedo is 2.10 times greater than that in the forest albedo, which is close to the multi-model mean of 1.94 (Fig. 5b). Moreover, we confirm that the observed slope (−5.51 ± 1.64) between Δαw and ΔLSTw can be well reproduced in the multi-model mean (−5.57 ± 1.39) (Fig. 5c). These findings demonstrate that the present model can produce reliable results in simulating the impact of snow dynamics on albedo and LST variations across vegetation types. However, considerable discrepancies exist across different models in these two relationships. For the summer results, we conduct partial correlation analysis between ΔLSTs and climate variables (Fig. 3a). The wind speed is excluded here considering the model data availability. Among all four models, only CNRM-ESM2-1 and UKESM1-0-LL capture the dominant role of SSMs on ΔLSTs dynamics (Fig. 5e). CESM, despite simulating the most pronounced negative trend in ΔLSTs, failed to reproduce the crucial role of SSMs. Overall, our comparison results suggest that while the multi-model ensemble can capture negative trends in ΔLSTw and ΔLSTs, discrepancies persist between observations and simulations (particularly in summer), along with intermodel differences in biophysical process representation. Our results are informative for improving current models for better simulations of local climate effects of European forests and their temporal dynamics in a warming world.

Discussion

Previous observational studies focus on the spatial, diurnal or seasonal heterogeneity of biophysical LST sensitivity to forest cover change or the mechanisms behind these patterns8,13,28,46. These studies are performed in a relatively static manner, using the results of a single year or the mean of a period, ignoring potential annual temporal variability. Here, our satellite evidence shows the emergent trend in the biophysical temperature effects of forestation in Europe over the last two decades (Fig. 1). Our results suggest that previous observational assessments may underestimate the daytime cooling effect of forests in the changing world. At least in the European region, the temporal dynamic of biophysical effects should be taken into consideration. Meanwhile, prior model-based studies report responses of the biophysical effects to climate change in high-emission or ideal scenarios22,23,47. Here, we have detected robust trends in historical records, despite the limited climate change during the study period. This implies that the sensitivity of the biophysical effect to climate change may be much stronger than previously thought, which merits the attention of researchers in the fields of climate change and earth system modeling.

Since we estimate the biophysical LST effects based on fixed and stable pixels with minimum disturbance, the negative trends can be solely attributed to background climate changes. We link the temporal change of the winter LST effect to the radiative process, represented by Δαw, and further attribute the negative ΔLSTw trend to the decreasing SCw. Specifically, the albedo of openland is about two times more sensitive to SCw than that of forests. Thus, the diminished SCw could result in a more pronounced reduction in openland albedo but have a limited impact on forest albedo, thereby weakening the relative radiative warming effect of forests compared to openlands. As a result, the relative contribution from non-radiative processes (i.e., higher heat exchange efficiency in the rougher forested land surface) may become more dominant, leading to attenuated daily warming effects of forestation in European winter (Fig. 1a, b). Meanwhile, the ET-related mechanism should also be noted, although not detected in our current analysis: future climate warming may enhance the winter evaporative advantage and the resultant cooling potential of forests. These mechanisms suggest that forestation in mid- to high-latitude regions may exhibit a similar reduced warming effect or even reversed net cooling in a warming world. The obtained relationships between SCw and biophysical factors of forests are useful for making reasonable predictions or constraining model simulations of future forest-snow interactions. In addition, previous studies have suggested that high latitudes and altitudes are considered unsuitable for forest restoration, as the positive radiative forcing driven by the albedo effect surpasses the negative feedback driven by the carbon sequestration48,49. Our satellite evidence implies that the timing of tree growth and future climate change should be considered when prioritizing the geolocation of forestation practices. In the context of global warming, the snow cover in winter or spring is projected to maintain a decreasing trend, which may significantly reduce the biophysical radiative forcings of forests. This factor warrants consideration when mapping forestation potential for climate mitigation purposes in a changing world.

In summer, the biophysical mechanisms behind the enhanced cooling effect of potential forestation are more complicated than those in winter. The magnitude of the evaporative cooling effect is influenced by both vegetation structure and meteorological conditions50. Using statistical methods, we find that declining SSMs is the predominant factor contributing to both the interannual variation and the negative trend in ΔLSTs (Fig. 3, Supplementary text 2 and 3). Path analysis shows that SSMs affects ΔLSTs dynamics through two mechanisms (Fig. 4). First, we show ΔLAIs is modulated by SSMs, implying that as the soil becomes drier, the vegetation structure difference between forests and openlands becomes more evident. The higher ΔLAIs could boost the cooling efficiency of forests through stronger direct heat dissipation and evaporative cooling. The impact of SSMs on vegetation growth has been well-documented: increasing dryness pressure could trigger stomatal closure, inhibit photosynthesis, and increase vegetation mortality51,52. Our results confirm a stronger negative effect of SSMs on non-forest vegetation than on forests53. The potential reason is that forest ecosystems with high species richness and deep roots are more stable and resilient to dryness54,55. Second, even under constant vegetation conditions, less soil water supply directly amplifies ΔLEs and enhances the cooling effect of forests. These findings are consistent with prior research56,57, indicating that soil moisture imposes stronger limitations on ET in grasslands and croplands relative to forest ecosystems. This differential response is evidenced by both the greater sensitivity of and the higher critical soil moisture thresholds in non-forest ecosystems. This phenomenon may be attributed to the capacity of forests to maintain ET through deep soil water extraction, thereby exhibiting greater resilience to warming-induced surface soil moisture deficits compared to openlands. Overall, the revealed impacts of SSMs on ΔLSTs essentially reflect the diverse vegetation physiological and hydrological responses to climate change.

In addition to temperature, European forests can also affect the hydrological process. In winter, forest cover delays or accelerates the melting of snowpack compared to the adjacent openlands, depending on the background climate and canopy density58. The revealed increasing daytime cooling effect favors the retention of winter snow in forests and further increases streamflow from snowmelt in the following season. As a result, forests may buffer the impact of global warming on fresh-water availability59, plant phenology60 and food production61 through altering the snow melting process in Europe. During summer, forests maintain high ET, which further promotes precipitation in downwind areas and accelerates the water cycle process62. The enhanced cooling effect is achieved at the expense of water loss through transpiration, which may increase the pressure on terrestrial water availability and pose threats to ecosystem productivity63 and freshwater resources64. The vegetation-climate feedback can promote precipitation through local moisture recycling and thus have a positive effect on water availability, which partially offsets the direct negative effect of ET on water availability65. Considering the drying or wetting trend in the future, the impact of potential forestation in Europe should be discussed according to the specific background climatic conditions due to the trade-off between greater cooling effects and less water availability66.

Potential caveats or issues should be noted when interpreting our remote sensing-based results. First, the observed negative trends in ΔLSTw and ΔLSTs reflect variations in LST difference when comparing forests and openlands, rather than the inherent capacity of existing forests to reverse warming trends. These patterns may originate from either modifications in forest characteristics or changes in the adjacent openland used as reference. Fundamentally, the negative trends emerge because forests exhibit a weaker temperature response than openlands in a warming climate, and this divergence stems from distinct biophysical and physiological properties between forest and openland vegetation. Meanwhile, the adopted “space-for-time” method provides a priori estimates of the local temperature effects67. The influence of forests on regional cloud formation and subsequent downward radiation represents a significant yet overlooked mechanism in current assessments68. This omission may lead to a systematic underestimation of forests’ net cooling effect6. In addition, forests can exert more complicated effects on climate through multiple processes, such as changes in large-scale atmospheric circulations7. Coupled models can effectively estimate these effects, notwithstanding the considerable uncertainties.

The enhanced local cooling effect implies that planting trees in proper areas remains a promising local solution against the risk of warming, especially in highly populated regions. Nonetheless, tree restoration is not a panacea for climate change. Although our results highlight the negative biophysical feedback to the warming trend in forests, this feedback is unable to offset the warming trend driven by rising atmospheric CO2 (Supplementary Text 4). Therefore, reducing greenhouse gas emissions and developing clean energy remain fundamental to limiting climate change. We also note that the role of increasing atmospheric CO2 on vegetation physiology is not considered in this study. The CO2 fertilization effect can enhance vegetation leaf area69, boost transpiration70, and cool the land surface71. Meanwhile, the CO2 physiological forcing also stimulates stomatal closure72, reduces transpiration73 and increases local temperature74. These two opposing mechanisms have diverse effects on forest and non-forest vegetation and affect forest cooling efficiency. The stomatal closure-driven negative effect may overwhelm the LAI-driven positive effect75. Thus, ignoring the physiological response of vegetation may result in the overestimation of the forest cooling effect (especially in summer) under high atmospheric CO2 concentration conditions.

Finally, the evaluated LST is the thermal radiative temperature of the land surface (vegetation foliage, canopy, and soil), which is biologically relevant and has advantages in describing energy, water, and carbon fluxes76. However, in climate change assessments, the screen height air temperature, rather than the LST, is a more widely used metric in terrestrial regions77. Converting LST effects to corresponding air temperature effects is of great importance but remains challenging78. Nonetheless, our LST-based results are useful for model improvement and informing the increasing climate benefits of European forests.

Overall, our findings provide solid satellite evidence that the cooling effects of forest conversion from openlands in Europe have been amplified in the last two decades. This temporal variation pattern may be even more evident when different forest age dynamics are considered79. Our results challenge the previous view that biophysical effects are marginally time-dependent in the recent two decades and advocate that temporal dynamics should be considered in biophysical effect assessments. Moreover, the revealed dominant role of snow cover and soil moisture deficit in controlling biophysical sensitivity emphasizes the importance of accurate parameterization of related processes in land surface models or vegetation dynamics models (e.g., snow masking effects and climatic constraints on vegetation leaf area), which are directly relevant to the projected future climate effects of forests and forming forest-based climate policies.

Methods

Satellite data

Satellite observations are adopted to estimate the potential biophysical effects of forestation. The satellite data used in this study are divided into two categories: MODIS products and other auxiliary land surface data. All satellite data are processed and output with a spatial resolution of 1 km.

LST data are obtained from the MYD11A1 dataset, which is retrieved by the split-window algorithm76. The MYD11 LST provides daily instantaneous observations at ~1:30 PM (daytime) and 1:30 AM (nighttime), corresponding to the daily maximum and minimum temperatures. Observations with LST errors greater than 2 K are filtered out according to the quality control flag. All high-quality observations are used to aggregate the summer (June, July, and August) and winter mean LST (December, January, and February) for both daytime and nighttime. We generate summer and winter LST from 2004 to 2023. It is worth noting that winter LST for a given year is averaged from the January and February LST of that year and the December LST of the previous year. For example, the 2023 winter LST is the mean value from December 2022 to February 2023. This aggregation principle is applied to all seasonal variables. We calculate the daily mean LST of summer and winter through the weighted combination of daytime and nighttime LST for both seasons80.

LE data are derived from the MOD16A2GF (MOD16A2 Gap-filled) dataset, with an original resolution of 8-day and 500 m. We select all observations retrieved by the main algorithm (Penman-Monteith model) and convert the LE unit to W/m². Then, all available records are spatially and temporally aggregated to 1 km seasonal means from 2004 to 2023. Leaf area index (LAI) data are obtained from the MCD15A3H dataset with a 4-day and 500 m resolution. All LAI values retrieved by the main Look-up-Table (LUT) method are aggregated to 1 km seasonal means from 2004 to 2023. The albedo data are from the MCD43A3 dataset, which contains daily 500 m black-sky and white-sky albedo for the shortwave band. We average the black-sky and white-sky albedo and then aggregate them to 1 km seasonal means from 2004 to 2023. Yearly land use/land cover data from 2004 to 2023 are derived from the MCD12Q1 dataset, with an original spatial resolution of 500 m. The land cover results under the International Geosphere-Biosphere Program (IGBP) classification scheme are used. The 500 m land cover maps are spatially aggregated to a 1 km scale based on the majority type.

Other auxiliary land surface data include forest loss data from the Global Forest Change (GFC) dataset81, impervious surface data from the Global Artificial Impervious Area (GAIA) dataset82, and digital elevation model (DEM) from the GMTED2010 dataset. The GFC dataset provides annual forest loss masks from 2000 to 2023 with 30 m spatial resolution based on time series analysis. All layers from 2004 to 2023 are composited, and a mask of forest loss during the study period is generated. The composited map is then spatially aggregated to 1 km resolution to match the MODIS data. The aggregated values range between 0 to 1, indicating the proportion of 30 m pixels where forest loss has occurred. GAIA data document the impervious surface mask from 1985 to 2018 with a spatial resolution of 30 m. Similar to the GFC datasets, the data from the last year (2018) are aggregated to a 1 km resolution impervious surface percentage map for further analysis. The DEM data of GMTED2010 has a spatial resolution of ~7.5 arc-seconds. We aggregate the original DEM to a 1 km spatial resolution for further analysis.

Reanalysis data

Meteorological variables are obtained from the monthly ERA5-Land reanalysis dataset83 with a spatial resolution of 0.1°. ERA5-Land dataset has been widely used for analysing the interaction between climate and terrestrial ecosystems. The used variables include 2 m air temperature (AT), 2 m dewpoint temperature (DT), precipitation, downward shortwave radiation, wind speed, surface soil moisture and snow cover. We aggregate these climatic variables into winter and summer means with a spatial resolution of 0.5°. Here, near-surface soil moisture data of layer 1 (0–7 cm depth) are used for analysis, as this part of the water can be utilized by herbaceous or woody vegetation, thereby containing the water limitation information for both forests and openlands. Observed biophysical effects of forests on LST

This paper explores the temporal dynamics of forests’ biophysical processes under a changing climate. To ensure a sufficient sample size for robust results, we employ a space-for-time substitution strategy to estimate the a priori potential effect, rather than using the space-and-time approach for the a posteriori actual effect67,85. Specifically, we assume that all 1 km pixels within a 0.25° grid (comprising 30×30 pixels) share the same background climate12–14,28,86,87, and the potential effect of forests on the LST (ΔLST) can be estimated by the difference between the mean LSTs of forested areas (LSTf¯) and non-forest openlands (LSTo¯) within the grid:

According to the MCD12Q1 land cover product, forest pixels should belong to evergreen needleleaf forests (ENFs), deciduous broadleaf forests (DBFs), evergreen broadleaf forests (EBFs), and mixed forests (MFs). Openland pixels include grasslands (GRA) and croplands (CRO). To minimize the potential impact of direct human activities, such as anthropogenic heat emissions and land use/land cover changes, on the ΔLST time series, we apply additional filtering criteria to the potential samples: (a) the land cover type remains unchanged during the study period; (b) the forest loss area should be less than 5% during the study period based on GFC data; and (c) the impervious surface percentage should be less than 5% according to GAIA data. For each 0.25° grid, we first determine the less dominant land cover type (forest or openland) based on the count of valid samples, then apply a ± 100 m elevation threshold around its mean elevation to select comparable samples from the other type. We calculate ΔLST only if both the forest and openland sample sizes exceed 5 pixels. We also discard ΔLST calculations for grids where the mean elevation exceeds 500 m to avoid potential bias induced by mountainous terrain. The resultant mapping of the biophysical LST effect is spatially aggregated from the 0.25° scale to the 0.5° scale to match the meteorological data and mitigate the impact of potential outliers.

We calculate ΔLST for both winter and summer from 2004 to 2023. The criteria ensure that the forest and non-forest pixels remain consistent and stable during the study period, allowing us to attribute the temporal dynamics of ΔLST primarily to background climate change. By substituting the target variable, we also calculate the albedo, LE and LAI difference between forest and non-forest areas.

Simulated biophysical effects of forests on LST

To obtain the simulated biophysical effects of forests on LST over the historical period, we use the output from the historical simulation of the CMIP688. The historical simulation is forced by externally imposed and time-varying natural (e.g., solar variability and volcanic aerosols) and anthropogenic (e.g., greenhouse gases, aerosols, and land use/land cover changes) forcings. The last 30-year (i.e., 1985–2014) simulation output is used to calculate the biophysical effects of forests and their trends. Here, we choose the last 30 years period because it is close to our observational period (i.e., 2004–2023). Moreover, the 30-year time series can be considered long enough to minimize the influence of internal climate variabilities on the long-term trend.

A few models that participate in the historical simulation report the subgrid information following the data reporting protocol of the Land Use Model Intercomparison Project (LUMIP)89. Specifically, these models provide surface diagnostic variables outputs on four subgrid tiles, including primary and secondary land (psl, including trees, grasslands, barrens and vegetated wetlands), cropland (crp), pastureland (pst) and urban land, in each model grid cell. To create forest and openland comparisons within a grid cell for further analysis, we only retain the grid cells with the ratio of the tree cover fraction to the psl fraction exceeding 10%; this threshold is consistent with that used by the Food and Agriculture Organization to define forest cover90. For these selected grid cells, the psl tile can be considered as a forest tile. The crp and pst tiles are combined into an openland tile to keep consistency with the satellite observations. Since the forest and openland tiles in a grid cell are forced by the same atmospheric inputs (e.g., downward radiation, air temperature and precipitation), the background climate is the same between the two tiles within a grid cell. As such, differences in surface diagnostic variables (e.g., LST) between the two tiles are solely attributed to differences in land cover45,91. Four models (CESM2, CNRM-ESM2-1, GFDL-ESM4 and UKESM1-0-LL) are used for the subgrid analysis, as they provide the required output for the historical simulation. CESM2 and GFDL-ESM4 each have only one member that provides the subgrid output, and the available one (r10i1p1f1 for CESM2 and r1i1p1f1 for GFDL-ESM4) is used. CNRM-ESM2-1 and UKESM1-0-LL each have more than one member, and the first one (r1i1p1f2 for CNRM-ESM2-1 and r2i1p1f2 for UKESM1-0-LL) is used for the sake of keeping consistency with other models as well as saving computational cost.

Statistical analyses

We use the Theil-Sen estimator and Mann-Kendall test to analyse temporal trends92 (scipy and pymannkendall packages in Python). The Theil-Sen estimator is a non-parametric method for robust estimation of the slope by using the median value of a range of possible slopes. The Mann–Kendall test, which is also nonparametric, statistically assesses whether a temporal trend is monotonically upward or downward.

Correlation analyses are employed to examine the potential influence of given drivers on the target variables (numpy package in Python). These analyses are based on either spatial or temporal samples. Temporal correlation is conducted at both pixel and regional scales. For pixel-scale correlation, we calculate the spatial mean and standard deviation of correlation coefficients to represent regional results. For regional-scale correlation, we first calculate the mean value for the study area and then determine the correlation coefficient. In addition, partial temporal correlation analysis is used to explore the relative impacts of multiple climate drivers on ΔLSTs at the pixel scale (pingouin package in Python). The partial correlation can determine whether a climate variable exerts an independent effect on the target variable beyond its covariation with other climate drivers. For instance, the partial correlation can isolate the association between ΔLSTs and SSMs while accounting for the influence of other covariates (e.g., temperature and precipitation).

We also conduct spatial correlation analyses using trend values to examine whether long-term changes in the independent variables can induce the trend in the target variable. These analyses are conducted using either complete spatial samples or binned subsets of the data. The latter, referred to as the binning regression method, is used to disentangle the contribution of covarying VPDs and SSMs on ΔLSTs. Specifically, we first stratify either SSMs or VPDs trends into 10 percentile bins (0–10th, 10th–20th,…, 90th–100th) and then assess their correlation (Fig. 3c). Subsequently, we evaluate the correlation between SSMs (or VPDs) trends and ΔLSTs trends within each corresponding VPDs (or SSMs) trend bin (Fig. 3d). This binning approach can effectively decouple the two factors, allowing us to identify the real dominant driver by comparing the resulting correlation coefficient.

We use SEM to reveal the biophysical mechanism underlying the ΔLSTs dynamics (semopy package in Python). The SEM is designed based on previous studies93,94. Specifically, we assume that climate variables influence the temporal dynamics of ΔLSTs through three pathways: (P1) climate change affects ΔLSTs via ΔLAIs; (P2) climate change affects ΔLSTs via ΔLEs; and (P3) climate change affects ΔLAIs, which further alters ΔLEs and then ΔLSTs. For P1 and P3, the impact of climate variables on ΔLAIs arises from the diverse sensitivities of vegetations to climate change. Specifically, climate changes can differently promote or inhibit forest and non-forest vegetation growth, which further affects ΔLSTs through convective heat dissipation (P1) and the evaporative cooling effect (P3). Climate variables also directly modulate ΔLEs even when the vegetation greenness remains constant. This is due to inherent differences between different vegetation types44, resulting in diverse evaporative responses to climate change and consequently affecting ΔLSTs (P2). It should be noted that all drivers used in SEM (SSMs, ATs, DSRs, WSs and Ps) represent grid-level mean values, which primarily reflect background climatic and environmental conditions. These variables are unlikely to be influenced by internal variations within the grids (ΔLEs and ΔLAIs).

In practice, SEM is performed at the grid level. We use all the data within a 3 × 3 spatial moving window for each 0.5° grid to ensure a sufficient sample size for establishing a robust model. Notably, we first normalize the input data and ΔLSTs by subtracting the 20-year means for each grid to exclude the impact of spatial variability. SEM is conducted in those windows where the sample size is larger than 120. The data are normalized using the Z-score method before modeling. Following the previous study38, the goodness-of-fit index (GFI) is employed to evaluate the applicability and effectiveness of each model. We retain the models with GFI >0.9 and calculate the mean path values along with the standard errors. The contributions of pathways P1, P2, and P3 are measured by their path effects, which are calculated by multiplying the contained path values. Uncertainties are estimated based on error propagation (error_propagation package in Python).
