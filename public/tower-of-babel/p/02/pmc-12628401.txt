Adequacy of pharmacological prescription in patients at very high risk of fracture within the FLS in Spain: analysis of the REFRA FLS-SEIOMM Registry

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is a skeletal disorder characterized by low bone mass and deterioration of bone tissue microarchitecture, resulting in decreased bone strength and an increased susceptibility to fractures. OP represents a growing burden on healthcare and social care systems worldwide [1–3]. Osteoporotic fractures are associated with diminished quality of life, increased morbidity and mortality, and extensive healthcare resource utilization. Consequently, the primary goal of OP treatment is fracture prevention. Despite the availability of multiple effective agents [4], a substantial therapeutic gap persists, with 73% of women and 63% of men in Europe lacking adequate fracture prevention measures. In Spain, these figures are similarly high at 67% for women and 60% for men [5].

As a chronic condition, OP requires precise, individualized risk fracture assessment before therapeutic decisions are made. To aid this, various cost-effective tools have been developed to identify patients most in need of treatment. Age, female sex, low bone mineral density (BMD), fall propensity, and prior fractures are well-established risk factors in these assessments [6, 7].

Following an initial fracture, the likelihood of subsequent fractures either at the same site or elsewhere increases significantly, particularly within the first two years, a phenomenon known as imminent fracture risk [8]. In women with a first fracture, the risk of sustaining fracture within the first year is estimated to be five times higher than in those without a prior fracture. Therefore, initiating treatment promptly in these patients is essential, ideally using drugs known for their rapid and potent antifracture efficacy. Additionally, it is critical to establish a long-term treatment plan that includes sequential therapy [9].

Recently, clinical guidelines for OP treatment from various scientific societies have undergone updates [10–12]. These latest versions emphasize fracture risk stratification, with a consensus on several points: recent fractures signify higher risk; simplified risk assessment facilitates categorization into low, high, and very high risk of new fractures; individualized treatment strategies should be tailored to each risk category; and early intervention following the initial fracture, using agents with a rapid effect, is encouraged to mitigate the risk of new fractures [12–15]. However, these guidelines vary somewhat in the criteria they use to classify high- and very high-risk fracture. There is consensus that patients at very high risk should begin treatment with an osteoanabolic agent, followed by an antiresorptive agent, to sustain BMD improvements and provide a rapid and lasting reduction in fracture risk [16–19].

The Spanish Society for Bone and Mineral Metabolism Research (SEIOMM) has developed specific guidelines for managing high- and very high-risk patients, aligning with other international recommendations in prioritizing fracture prevention [20].

FLS provides a structured, multidisciplinary approach to identifying, assessing, treating, and monitoring patients with fragility fractures, aiming to reduce the treatment gap and prevent recurrent fractures. These programs have demonstrated effectiveness in lowering new fracture rates, enhancing treatment prescription, and improving adherence. Furthermore, FLS has been shown to be cost-effective [21, 22]. In Spain, SEIOMM, in line with the International Osteoporosis Foundation’s “Capture the Fracture” initiative, is leading secondary fracture prevention efforts, promoting FLS development through the national REFRA-FLS SEIOMM registry [23].

The primary aim of this study is to describe the characteristics of patients at high and very high risk for new fractures treated in FLSs and evaluate the alignment of their pharmacological treatment with SEIOMM guideline recommendations. A secondary objective is to determine the incidence of new fractures after one year of follow-up and identify factors associated with these fractures in these two patients’ groups.

Material and methods

Design and patients

This study is an observational, retrospective, registry-based, multicenter cohort study. The cohort was derived from the SEIOMM REFRA-FLS registry, which includes individuals aged 50 and older who experienced at least one fragility fracture within the previous year. Patients were managed across 16 nationally distributed Fracture Liaison Services (FLS) (Supplementary Fig. 1) and were recruited from January 2015 to April 2024.

Flow chart of patients participating in the study. All patients were classified as high-risk fracture (HRF) or very high-risk fracture (VHRF) and had bone mineral density and lateral spine radiographs

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study protocol received approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Hospital del Mar (Barcelona), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study Setting

All FLS adhering to a standardized protocol aligned with International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF) recommendations [23]. Each FLS met the following criteria: (a) active status with IOF best practice certification, overseen by a designated coordinator [24]; (b) full coordination with primary care centers in their catchment area; and (c) comprehensive data collection using the SEIOMM REFRA-FLS Registry.

Eligible fractures included those of the hip, vertebrae (thoracic or lumbar), and other common fragility sites (proximal humerus, distal radius, and pelvis). Patients with underlying bone lesions, advanced cognitive impairment, or conditions associated with limited life expectancy were excluded.

Patient Recruitment and Data Collection

The study population included patients who consulted an emergency department for an episode of fragility fracture and were referred to the FLS. Additionally, nurse case managers actively reviewed hospital patient lists (including admissions, emergency department visits, and outpatient visits) to identify eligible patients.

Upon arrival at FLS units, a research nurse verified inclusion criteria and referred eligible participants to the FLS coordinator, who provided information about the study and obtained written informed consent.

Data were collected from medical records and included in the SEIOMM REFRA-FLS Registry. The registry documented sociodemographic data, fracture characteristics, comorbidities, prescribed medications, and lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking and alcohol). Fall risk was assessed by the number of falls in the previous year and the “Up and Go” test, with low risk defined as fewer than 2 falls in the previous year and an “Up and Go” result of ≤ 20 seconds; higher fall risk was otherwise indicated. Additionally, the FRAX tool estimated the 10-year fracture risk [25]. Patients underwent baseline evaluations including BMD assessment, morphometric fracture identification via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), and lateral spine radiographs, along with targeted laboratory testing. Functional status was assessed for Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) using (1) the Barthel Index, (2) instrumental activities via the Lawton Index [26], and (3) 10-year life expectancy based on comorbidities using the Charlson Index [27].

Based on this comprehensive assessment, patients received individual recommendations on lifestyle modifications and treatment, coordinated with primary care.

Study population

Of the 7161 participants evaluated in the REFRA-FLS registry units, 1413 had one year of follow-up (returning for an in-person visit) and sufficient baseline data for classification (BMD and lateral spine X-ray) as either very high risk or high risk of fracture, according to SEIOMM criteria [20]:

Adherence (taking at the right times) and persistence (taking continuously, without forgetting) by means of two instruments: Self-reported compliance test and Morisky-Green test [28]. Falls and any new fractures were documented, with radiological confirmation for fracture occurrence.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized using absolute and percentage frequencies, while numerical variables were described by median and interquartile range. The normality of numerical variables was evaluated using the Jarque-Bera test.

Comparisons between risk and refracture groups were made using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney test for numerical variables. Treatment groups were compared with Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for numerical variables.

To assess the association between refracture occurrence during follow-up and key covariates, a multivariable logistic regression model was used. The final model covariates were selected using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to minimize overfitting. Results are presented as Odds Ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) and p-values. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2).

Results

Sociodemographic clinical and lifestyle characteristics

A total of 1413 patients were included in this study. Table 1 presents their sociodemographic, clinical, and lifestyle characteristics. All participants had experienced a fragility fracture within the past year and were followed for at least one-year post-fracture. Among these, 1034 patients (73.2%) met the criteria for the HRF category, while 379 (26.8%) were classified as VHRF. Most patients in both groups were female, representing 86.7% of the HRF group and 85.2% of the VHRF group. The median age was significantly higher in the VHRF group (76 years vs. 72 years in HRF, p<0.001), with a larger proportion of patients aged over 80 (33.5% in VHRF vs. 18.3% in HRF; p<0.001). In the VHRF group, the index fracture was primarily located in the hip or vertebrae (81.5% vs. 45.5% in HRF; p<0.001).

Body Mass Index (BMI) was slightly lower in the VHRF group (p<0.001), with 6.9% of VHRF patients having a BMI < 20 kg/m2, compared to 3.3% in the HRF group (p<0.001). There were no significant differences between the groups in smoking or excessive alcohol consumption. Notably, nearly 50% of patients in both groups were at risk of falls, with approximately 26.8% of the study population experiencing two or more falls in the year before their fracture.

Previous fracture prevalence was significantly higher in VHRF patients (34.6% vs. 23.1% in HRF; p<0.001). FRAX® calculations indicated a higher 10-year risk for major osteoporotic fractures (75.8% in VHRF vs. 44.2% in HRF; p<0.001) and hip fractures (80.3% in VHRF vs. 49.1% in HRF; p<0.001). In the HRF group, previous fractures were distributed as follows: 13.1% hip, 13.1% vertebra, and 73.7% other sites. In the VHRF group, previous fractures were more common in the vertebrae (39.7%), followed by other sites (52.7%) and the hip (7.6%) (p<0.001). As expected by classification criteria, bone mass values in the VHRF group were ≤ −3.5 T-score in the femoral neck or vertebra in 51.7% of patients (p<0.001) (Table 1).

There were no significant differences in comorbidities or bone metabolism influencing concomitant treatments among groups, with thyroid medications being the most commonly used (Supplementary Table 1).

Regarding functional status, patients in the VHRF group showed moderate dependence in Activities of Daily Living (ADLs), with a median Barthel Index of 85, compared to mild dependence in the HRF group with a score of 95. In instrumental activities, assessed by the Lawton Index, the VHRF group had moderate dependence (score of 5), while the HRF group demonstrated greater autonomy with a score of 8.

Recommended treatment

The time elapsed from the index fracture to the initiation of treatment was 106.5±80.7 days for the HRF group and 120.7±93.1 days for the VHRF group (p=0.005). No significant differences were observed between different types of fractures.

Most patients received calcium and vitamin D supplements, as shown in Table 1, alongside lifestyle recommendations that included guidance on avoiding harmful habits, dietary advice, physical activity, information about osteoporosis, and fall prevention strategies. 15% of patients in the HRF group and 18.3% in the VHRF group had received prior treatment for OP (p=0.151).

Of the study participants, 1222 (86.5%) initiated treatment with either antiresorptive or osteoanabolic agents. This percentage was significantly higher in the VHRF group (89%) compared to the HRF group (84%) (p<0.05) (Fig.2). Among the HRF patients, oral bisphosphonates, primarily alendronate, were the most commonly prescribed antiresorptive agents (54.2%), followed by zoledronic acid and denosumab (23.8%) and osteoanabolic agents such as teriparatide and romosozumab (7.3%). In contrast, the VHRF group predominantly received parenteral antiresorptives (zoledronic acid and denosumab; 34%), followed by oral bisphosphonates (33.2%) and osteoanabolic agents, mainly teriparatide (23.35%) (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

As shown in Table 2, osteoanabolic drugs were more frequently prescribed to younger patients and those with vertebral fractures, while antiresorptive drugs were primarily used for patients with hip or other types of fractures (p<0.001). Drug choice is also correlated with fractured location. For fractures of the hip, spine, or other sites, oral bisphosphonates were prescribed in 27.9%, 18.9%, and 53.3% of cases, respectively. First year of follow-up: adherence to treatment, new fractures, and associated factors

During the first year of follow-up after initiating treatment, 84 patients (5.9%) sustained a new fracture, with 61 cases (5.9%) in the HRF group and 23 cases (6.1%) in the VHRF group. The incidence of new fractures was similar between men and women (5.4% and 6.3%, respectively), with no significant difference based on the type of drug received (6.7% in those on antiresorptives vs. 6.6% in those on osteoanabolics). No significant differences were observed among patients who experienced a refracture and those who did not after one year of follow-up; in the HRF group, the time to refracture was 112.1±76.7 vs 95.1±77.9 days (p= 0.109), and in the VHRF group, it was 115.2±88.3 vs 123.7±100.4 days (p=0.068).

The time to initiation of antiosteoporotic treatment was similar between patients who experienced a refracture and those who did not, after one year of follow-up. In the HRF group with refracture, the time to initiation of treatment was 112.1 ± 76.7 versus 95.1 ± 77.9 days (p = 0.109) in the non-refracture group, and in the VHRF group with refracture, it was 115.2 ± 88.3 versus 123.7 ± 100.4 days (p = 0.068).

Sociodemographic characteristics, clinical details, and lifestyle factors for patients with and without new fractures are summarized in Table 3. Significant differences were observed in fall risk, 10-year hip fracture probability per FRAX®, and functional status, as assessed by the Lawton and Barthel indices. In the HRF group, high fall risk was notably prevalent among those who experienced new fractures, observed in 72% compared to 41% of patients without new fractures (p<0.001). Additionally, 41% of patients with new fractures had experienced two or more falls in the year before the index fracture, compared to 26% of those without a refracture (p=0.017). In the VHRF group, multiple falls were also more common among patients with new fractures (43.5% vs. 22%, p=0.035) (Table 3).

In the HRF group, patients with new fractures had a higher 10-year fracture probability and significantly lower Lawton and Barthel index scores than those without new fractures (p<0.05) (Table 3).

It should be noted that in neither of the HRF and VHRF study groups did we find differences in the treatments received when comparing the refractured and non-refractured groups (Table 3).

Multivariate analysis, using refracture as the dependent variable and 10-year hip fracture probability and prior falls as independent variables, identified having 2 or more falls per year as the only significant predictor of new fractures within one year (OR 1.829, 95% CI 1.102-2.974, p=0.018) (Supplementary 4)

Antiresorptive drugs were the most frequently prescribed in both groups, regardless of refracture status. Among patients who sustained new fractures, 87.2% received antiresorptive therapy, while 12.8% received osteoanabolic treatment. Among those without refractures, these rates were 86.8% and 13%, respectively.

Adherence to treatment was assessed exclusively at one year of follow-up, showing a high and similar percentage in the HRF (89.9%) and VHRF (90.4%) groups, with no significant differences by drug type (oral antiresorptive 92.9%, parenteral antiresorptive 93.3%, osteoanabolic 93.4%) or by refracture occurrence (87.5% in those with new fractures vs. 90% in those without) (Tables 2 and 3).

Regarding the relationship between new and index fractures, new fractures occurred in 6% of patients with an initial hip fracture, 5.7% with an initial vertebral fracture, and 6.3% with fractures in other locations, with no significant difference between HRF and VHRF groups. The distribution of new fracture locations was 17.3% vertebral, 16.0% hip, and 88.7% at other sites, with no significant variation between the HRF and VHRF groups.

Discussion

In this study, we found that, based on SEIOMM criteria, one in four patients evaluated in FLS Units across our country can be classified as VHRF. Population studies on patients aged 50 and older with a diagnosis of osteoporosis (OP) show variability in risk classifications: 1.8–20% for HRF and 8.2–16% for VHRF, with the VHRF rate reaching up to 51.6% in patients with hip fractures [25, 29–31]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in risk stratification processes, OP management across healthcare settings, and disease progression stages. The FLS model includes a comprehensive patient assessment that considers factors such as prior fractures, recent fracture type, radiographic evaluation for asymptomatic vertebral fractures, and BMD. This approach supports more accurate stratification of imminent fracture risk.

In our cohort, patients in the VHRF subgroup were older (one-third were over 80 years), had a higher prevalence of previous fractures, lower BMI, significantly elevated 10-year risks for major OP and hip fractures (assessed by FRAX®), and, in many cases, vertebral fractures compared to the HRF group. Unlike other clinical guidelines [10, 15, 32], the SEIOMM guidelines do not use FRAX for risk stratification, due to concerns about FRAX’s potential underestimation of non-hip fracture risk in the Spanish population [33, 34]. Given our findings, incorporating at least the FRAX hip fracture probability in future editions of the SEIOMM guidelines could be beneficial for patients without available BMD measurements as it was found to correlate consistently with VHRF classification in our study.

Our results also demonstrate that FLS Unit implementation contributes to high treatment initiation rates [35], with 84% of HRF patients and 89% of VHRF patients receiving treatment. The therapeutic approach was primarily focused on potent, parenteral antiresorptive agents (zoledronic acid and denosumab) in up to 34% of VHRF patients, while osteoanabolic therapy was selected for 23% of these cases. Recently, the IOF and the European Society for Clinical and Economic Aspects of Osteoporosis, Osteoarthritis, and Musculoskeletal Diseases (ESCEO) recommended risk classification strategies for HRF and VHRF patients [14].

The rationale behind more precise risk characterization is to better target osteoanabolic interventions for patients at very high risk and to enhance cost-effectiveness. Osteoanabolic treatments, such as teriparatide, abaloparatide, and romosozumab, have been prioritized for this purpose, in alignment with several international guidelines [36]. In our study, osteoanabolic treatment was most frequently used for vertebral fractures, accounting for up to 54.7% of cases, indicating that treatment choice was influenced by fracture location. A recent study highlights the crucial role of bone-forming agents in managing severe OP, particularly in patients with high fracture risk (HRF), multiple vertebral fractures, recent fragility fractures, or insufficient response to antiresorptive therapies [37]. Hip fracture patients, generally older with more comorbidities and a higher fall risk, were more frequently treated with potent parenteral bisphosphonates [38, 39]. The lower initial use of osteoanabolics may also reflect the study’s timeline, as many patients were enrolled prior to guideline publication and before the approval of romosozumab and abaloparatide in the therapeutic arsenal. Additionally, real-world clinical practice often varies from guideline recommendations [40].

Research indicates that new fracture risk peaks within the first two years after an index fracture, a period known as “imminent fracture risk.” This is particularly relevant to FLS-managed patients, all of whom have experienced recent fractures. The risk of refracture within the first year was similar between the two study groups (5.8% in the HRF group and 6.0% in the VHRF group). Although a higher refracture rate might have been expected in the VHRF group, it is important to consider that a greater proportion of VHRF patients received more potent treatments, such as zoledronic acid, denosumab, or anabolic agents, compared to the HRF group. This suggests that these treatments may have contributed to reducing the refracture rate in the VHRF group. Furthermore, it is likely that VHRF patients with refracture who did not survive to attend a follow-up visit one year later could result in an underestimation of refractures in this group.

Furthermore, multivariate analysis revealed that the primary factor influencing refracture occurrence was fall risk, which was higher in patients who experienced a refracture, regardless of their classification. In both groups, the refracture rates were lower than those reported in previous studies [41, 42], suggesting that the treatment and follow-up strategies applied to these patients were appropriate in both groups.

On the other hand, we observed that in the VHRF group, patients with a new fracture had a better functional status, which makes us think that these patients have greater physical activity so it increases their risk of falls.

Although guidelines suggest that patients with recent fractures should initiate treatment with osteoanabolics or denosumab due to their rapid, potent antifracture effects and greater bone mass gains compared to oral bisphosphonates [16, 17, 43], we found no significant difference in new fracture incidence based on the medication received. Several factors may explain these findings, including the early initiation of potent antiresorptives (such as zoledronic acid and denosumab) and the high treatment adherence observed among patients (60% adherence on the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale and 93% by self-report).

An important finding in both HRF and VHRF groups was the significant association between new fractures and fall frequency. Our multivariate analysis identified that experiencing two or more falls annually was a significant predictor of fractures during follow-up. Similar findings have been reported in other studies [40]. While previous fractures and low BMD are well-established predictors of fractures, most of these fractures result from low-impact trauma, often due to falls. Recent updates in the FRAX tool (FRAXplus) now incorporate fall history as a correction factor for fracture risk estimation, with proposed multipliers based on fall frequency [44].

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size and follow-up duration may be insufficient to fully investigate the effects of each treatment on fracture prevention. Second, this study was conducted across 16 FLS Units within the REFRA-SEIOMM registry, a subset of the 98 IOF-recognized FLS Units in Spain. Third, patients had to survive at least one year to be included in the study and thus frail patients that refractured within a year and did not survive were not eligible. Finally, the initiation of the REFRA registry preceded the publication of SEIOMM and other guidelines recommending osteoanabolics as the preferred first-line treatment for VHRF patients, possibly explaining their use in only 23% of our VHRF patients. Furthermore, romosozumab and abaloparatide were only recently approved in Spain. Finally, the registry includes a high proportion of hip fractures, which historically received lower indications for osteoanabolic treatment until the recent approval of romosozumab and abaloparatide in Spain.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that 26.8 % of patients seen in FLS units fall into the VHRF category. Sequential treatment—starting with osteoanabolic therapy followed by an antiresorptive agent, as recommended by current guidelines—may represent an effective approach for these high-risk patients. However, our findings suggest that, at least during the first year, the risk of falling, rather than the specific choice of antiosteoporotic therapy, is more strongly associated with the likelihood of sustaining new fractures. The adherence achieved through structured FLS care is linked to a lower incidence of fractures in osteoporotic patients.
