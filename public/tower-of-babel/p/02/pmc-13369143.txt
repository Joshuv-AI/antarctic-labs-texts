The mediating roles of psychological resilience and social support in the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress in adolescents
Authors: Mehmet Sıddık Vangölü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to examine the mediating roles of psychological resilience and perceived social support in the‍ association between phubbing and psychological distress among adolescents. The sample consisted of high school adolescents. Participants completed the General Phubbing Scale, Depression Anxiety Stress Scale 21 (DASS-21), Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS), and Brief Resilience Scale (BRS). The findings indicated phubbing was‍ positively associated with psychological distress. In addition, psychological resilience and perceived social‌ support demonstrated significant indirect associations in the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress‌. Specifically, higher levels of phubbing were associated with lower psychological resilience and reduced perceived social support, which‌, in turn, were related to‌ elevated psychological distress.‍ These findings suggest that psychological resilience‍ and social support may serve as protective resources in the context of problematic digital interaction behaviors among adolescents. The results highlight the potential importance of developing intervention programs aimed at strengthening psychological resilience and enhancing social support to reduce the association between phubbing and psychological distress among‌ adolescents.

Introduction

For many adolescents today, being ignored by someone who is focused on their phone has become an increasingly common social experience. Although smartphones have made communication easier, their widespread use may also weaken the quality of face-to-face interactions. The widespread use of smartphones in recent decades has produced many benefits1. Smartphones have facilitated social interaction by enabling people to communicate regardless of time and place2. However, as these digital technologies become increasingly integrated into daily life, they may weaken face-to-face interactions3. Phubbing refers to being preoccupied with one’s phone while ignoring others during social interaction4. It has also been described as a modern form of social exclusion in which individuals focus on their smartphones rather than participating in conversation5. This behavior presents a negative communication style that may adversely affect interpersonal relationships and psychological well-being6. Phubbing is particularly significant during adolescence, as this is a sensitive developmental stage in which peer acceptance, social belonging, and identity development are especially prominent. Therefore, experiences of being ignored may have stronger psychological implications for adolescents7. Phubbing does not only affect the person being ignored; it may also reshape the phubber’s own social interactions. Reduced face-to-face interaction may be associated with weakened social bonds and superficial relationships8. Over time, such interactional patterns may be associated with higher levels of psychological distress.

Adolescence is a developmental period often associated with heightened psychological distress9. Adolescents who engage in phubbing may experience weakened social connections due to reduced face-to-face interaction, which may, in turn, be associated with psychological distress10, adjustment problems and other mental health difficulties11,12. In this context, phubbing is a significant risk factor for adolescents13. Although previous research has addressed the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress in adolescents14,15, the protective mechanisms underlying this relationship remain unclear. Studies conducted in Türkiye have also shown that problematic digital behaviors are associated with lower well-being and difficulties in adolescents’ social relationships16–18. This study aims to contribute to the literature by exploring the roles psychological resilience and social support as protective mechanisms in the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress among adolescents. Clarifying this relationship is important because identifying protective factors may inform early prevention and support strategies for adolescent mental health. Phubbing can be considered a form of covert social exclusion that may lead adolescents to feel ignored or excluded during face-to-face interactions. Repeated experiences of this kind may undermine adolescents’ sense of belonging and self-esteem over time, thereby increasing psychological distress19.

The relationship between phubbing and psychological distress

Psychological distress is defined as the presence of symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and stress20. Since adolescence is a developmental period characterized by intense emotional fluctuations and be linked to greater psychological vulnerability, these symptoms may appear more frequently and intensely during this stage21. In this study, psychological distress is conceptualized as a broader psychological construct comprising depression, anxiety, and stress. These symptoms are particularly common during adolescence9. Phubbing, among adolescents, may be one factor associated with these symptoms22. Adolescents whose social relationships are weakened by phubbing23,24 may experience depression22, anxiety14, and adjustment problems3. Previous research suggests a reciprocal relationship between phubbing and psychological distress14. Indeed, a new model25 suggests that risky digital activity may be associated with depressive mood in adolescents through reciprocal pathways, thereby creating create negative developmental cycles. Empirical studies supporting this model have demonstrated a relationship between phubbing and psychological distress14,26,27. In addition, Ni et al.28, who synthesized data from 52 studies involving 19,698 individuals, reported correlations between phubbing and variables such as attachment anxiety, attachment avoidance, depression, and loneliness. Although these findings provide insight into the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress in adolescents, identifying protective factors may help clarify how this negative reciprocal cycle can be reduced. Therefore, identifying protective factors in the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress is crucial.

The mediating role of psychological resilience and social support

According to the Displacement Hypothesis29, individuals increasingly spend time using electronic and digital technologies instead of engaging in social interactions or physical activities. Phubbing may replace the time adolescents could otherwise dedicate to face-to-face social interactions and meaningful relationships30. Over time, this may weaken social bonds and contribute to psychological distress31. In this context, perceived social support may function as a protective interpersonal resource, whereas psychological resilience may serve as an internal resource that helps mitigate potential negative outcomes. Therefore, psychological resilience and perceived social support may be considered not only related variables but also mediating mechanisms that help explain how phubbing is associated with psychological distress by weakening individual coping resources and interpersonal support. Phubbing may weaken social support resources by reducing the time individuals devote to social interactions32,33. Decreased social support, in turn, may limit individuals’ capacity to cope with stress, leading to psychological distress, psychological resilience, by contrast, may function as an internal resource that facilitates individuals’ adaptation to such negative processes.

Psychological resilience is particularly important during adolescence, a period market by rapid emotional, cognitive, and social changes. It plays a critical role during adolescence, when individuals develop identity, emotional regulation, and stress management skills34. Psychological resilience is considered a protective mechanism in the face of stressors and encompasses two fundamental elements: exposure to challenging life experiences, and, the capacity to adapt despite such experiences35. Research has shown that psychological resilience serves a protective function in challenging situations36–39. Conversely, individuals with low psychological resilience may be more sensitive to interpersonal difficulties and more vulnerable to negative social experiences such as peer rejection and isolation40. Therefore, psychological resilience may reduce the negative psychological associations of stressful experiences41. In other words, psychological resilience may function as a protective factor in the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress in adolescents.

Perceived social support is a critical protective factor during adolescence, as individuals in this age group often rely on family, friends, and their close social environment to cope with stress. As another protective mechanism, social support, refers to social resources provided by others and is closely related to the function and quality of social relationships42. According to the social support buffering hypothesis, perceived social support may mitigate the negative effects of certain risk factors43. Accordingly, social support may be significantly associated with lowers levels of negative outcomes, such as stress and depression44. Indeed, previous studies have shown that social support may protect adolescents against depression37,45, anxiety46, and PTSD47. Based on these findings, perceived social support may be associated with lower psychological problems.

Current study

Previous studies have reported strong associations between phubbing and psychological distress in adolescents11,12,14,15. Identifying protective mechanisms that may reduce psychological distress in this relationship is therefore critically important. Recent research highlights the potential of psychological resilience36,38 and social support17,37,45 as protective factors in challenging situations. However, despite extensive literature on psychological resilience and social support, gaps remain in understanding the roles of these structures in the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress among Turkish adolescents. This study is based on the assumption that phubbing may be associated with lower perceived social support by reducing social interactions, and that psychological resilience may help explain the link between phubbing and psychological distress. This issue is particularly important in the Turkish context, where family ties, peer relationships, and social belonging play central roles in adolescent development. Furthermore, studies conducted in Türkiye show that problematic digital behaviors may be negatively associated with well-being.

The present study has several unique contributions. First, it simultaneously examines both internal (psychological resilience) and external (perceived social support) protective mechanisms within a single model. Second, whereas previous studies have largely focused on direct associations, this study investigates the underlying processes through a mediation framework. Third, this research provides evidence from a Turkish adolescent sample, contributing to the limited literature in this cultural context. Accordingly, this study aims to address a significant gap in the literature by examining psychological resilience as an individual resource, and perceived social support as an environmental resource, in the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress among Turkish adolescents. Beyond examining statistical associations, this study seeks to clarify the underlying processes linking phubbing to psychological distress. Specifically, phubbing may be linked to adolescents perceived social connections and internal coping resources, which in turn may be associated with higher levels of psychological distress. In this context, the present study addressed the following hypotheses. The proposed mediation model is shown in Fig. 1.

H1: Phubbing will be positively associated with psychological distress (depression, anxiety, and stress).

H2: Phubbing will be negatively associated with perceived social support.

H3: Phubbing will be negatively associated with psychological resilience.

H4: Psychological resilience will mediate the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress.

H5: Perceived social support will mediate the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress.

Method

Participants and procedure

The sample consisted of 522 high school students aged between 13 and 18. Among the participants 277 were girls and 245 were boys. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling procedure. Before the commencing the research process, ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Social and Human Sciences at Bitlis Eren University (Approval No: E-84771431-050.01.04-107762). In addition, the necessary permissions were obtained from the Ministry of National Education and the relevant school administrations. Data were collected using an online data collection method via Google Forms. During the data collection process, participants were informed about the purpose of the research, the principles of voluntariness, and the principles of confidentiality. Both parental consent and the informed consent of adolescents were obtained for participants to take part in the research. Participants were clearly informed that they could withdraw from the research at any time and that their responses would be kept confidential. The data collection process took approximately 10 min, and participants were not offered any financial or academic incentives.

Measures

General phubbing scale

The scale originally developed by Chotpitayasunondh and Douglas48 was adapted into Turkish culture by Gavcar et al.16 to measure the general level of phubbing. The scale consists 15 items across four sub-dimensions: NP: Nomophobia (1, 2, 3, and 4); IC: Interpersonal Conflict (items 5, 6, 7, and 8); SI: Self-Isolation (items 9, 10, 11, and 12); PA: Problem Acknowledgment (items 13, 14, and 15). There are no questions to be scored in reverse in the scale. Higher scores indicate greater levels of phubbing, whereas lower scores reflect lower levels of phubbing. In the present study, the calculation was based on the total score. The scale demonstrated strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.94 in the original study and 0.89 in the current sample.

Depression anxiety stress scale 21 (DASS-21)

The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21, originally developed by Lovibond and Lovibond49, was adapted into Turkish by Sarıçam50. The scale consists of 2‍1 item distributed across three sub-dimensions: Depression, Anxiety, and Stress. The instrument is rated on a 4-poin‍t Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (‍Never) to 3 (Always) and does‌ not include any reverse-scored items. Higher scores on each subdimension indicate higher levels of depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms.‌ The scale demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficient reported in‍ the original study as 0.87 for the Depression scale, 0.85 for the Anxiety subscale, and 0.81 for the Stress subscale. In the current study, the corresponding Cronbach’s alpha‌ values were 0.82, 0.81, and 0.76, respectively.

Multidimensional scale of perceived social support (MSPSS)

MSPSS is a scale consisting of 12 items. It includes 3 groups related to the source of support, each consisting of 4 items. These are; family, friends and special people. A high score indicates that the perceived social support is high51. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.89 in the original study and 0.87 in the current study.

Brief resilience scale (BRS)

The Brief Resilience Scale was developed by Smith et al.52 to assess psychological resilience. The scale‍ is a self-report instrument consisting of six items rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale. Following the reverse coding of negatively worded items, higher scores indicate greater levels of psychological resilience. Th e Turkish adaptation of the scale was conducted by Doğan53. The scale demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.83 reported in the original study and 0.72 in the current sample.

Data analysis

First, we examined the skewness and kurtosis values to determine whether the normality assumptions were met. We then used Pearson correlation analysis to determine the relationships between the variables. Gender was included as a covariate in all mediation analyses. To test the mediation hypotheses, PROCESS Macro Model 4 (Version 5.0) for SPSS v27 was used with 5000 resamples and 95% confidence intervals54–56. In this analysis, the Errors-in-variables (EIV) regression method proposed by Hayes et al.56 was preferred over the classical OLS regression approach. This method incorporates measurement errors of independent and mediating variables into the model, ensuring more reliable path estimates54. When the EIV approach is activated, the standard errors for the paths are calculated using the heteroscedasticity-sensitive HC3 correction54,56. Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess Common Method Bias (CMB). To assess the potential presence of Common Method Bias (CMB), Harman’s single-factor test was conducted. All measurement items were entered into an unrotated exploratory factor analysis using Principal Axis Factoring. The results indicated that a single factor accounted for 18.58% of the total variance, which is well below the recommended threshold of 50%. This finding suggests that common method variance is unlikely to be a significant concern in the present study.

Table 3 presents the unstandardized and standardized regression coefficients for the mediation model examining the indirect effects of social support and resilience in the association between phubbing and psychological outcomes (depression, anxiety, and stress). Heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors (HC3) were used in all analyses.

Results indicated that phubbing significantly and negatively predicted social support (B = -0.142, β = -0.160 p < .01), accounting for a relatively small proportion of the variance in social support (R² = 0.02). Similarly, phubbing was a statistically significant negative predictor of resilience (B = -0.023, β = -0.136, p < .001), although the amount of variance explained was limited (R² = 0.03). These findings suggest that while higher levels of phubbing are associated with lower perceived social support and resilience, the explanatory power of these associations is modest and should be interpreted with caution.

With respect to the outcome variables, phubbing remained a significant positive predictor of depression (B = 0.081, β = 0.298, p < .001), anxiety (B = 0.067, β = 0.263, p < .001), and stress (B = 0.091, β = 0.369, p < .001). At the same time, both mediators showed significant negative associations with all psychological outcomes. Specifically, social support negatively predicted depression ( B = − 0.088, β =-0.290, p < .001), anxiety (B = − 0.042, β =-0.290, -0.147, p < .001), and stress (B = − 0.043, β =-0.290,-0.156, p < .01), while resilience also exhibited negative associations with depression (B = − 0.497, β =-0.290, -0.315, p < .001), anxiety ( B = − 0.356, β =-0.239, p < .001), and stress ( B = − 0.389, β =-0.270, p < .001). Finally, all mediation analyses were performed after controlling for gender, and the significance of the mediation model was preserved even after this adjustment.

The overall models accounted for a moderate proportion of variance in the outcome variables, explaining 19% of the variance in depression (R² = 0.19), 20% in anxiety (R² = 0.20), and 16% in stress (R² = 0.16), and were statistically significant based on HC3-adjusted F statistics (p < .001). Taken together, these results provide evidence for indirect pathways linking phubbing to psychological distress via social support and resilience. However, given the relatively small effect sizes observed in the associations between phubbing and the mediators, these indirect effects should be interpreted as limited in magnitude rather than strong or decisive. Although phubbing retained a significant direct effect on depression, anxiety, and stress, the inclusion of social support and resilience was associated with a reduction in these coefficients, suggesting a partial and modest mediating role.

Table 4 presents the standardized indirect effects of phubbing on psychological distress through social support and resilience, estimated using a bootstrapping procedure. Indirect effects were considered statistically significant when the 95% bootstrap confidence intervals did not include zero.

For depression, the total indirect effect of phubbing was significant (effect= 0.024, [0.0111, 0.0381]). Examination of the specific indirect paths showed that both mediators made significant contributions to this effect. The indirect effects through social support (effect= 0.012, [0.0044, 0.0221]) and through resilience (effect= 0.011, [0.0023, 0.0227]) were statistically significant, indicating that lower social support and resilience partially explained the association between phubbing and depression.

With respect to anxiety, the total indirect effect of phubbing was also significant (effect= 0.014, [0.0056, 0.0242]). Both indirect pathways were significant, including the path via social support (effect= 0.006, [0.0013, 0.0124]) and the path via resilience (effect = 0.008, [0.0016, 0.0164]). These results suggest that phubbing indirectly contributes to higher anxiety levels indirectly by undermining social support and resilience.

Similarly, for stress, the total indirect effect was significant (effect= 0.016, [0.0073, 0.0264]). The indirect effects through social support (effect= 0.006, [0.0017, 0.0121]) and resilience (effect= 0.010, [0.0026, 0.0190]) were both statistically significant, suggesting that lower social support and resilience mediated the association between phubbing and stress.

Overall, the bootstrap analyses provide strong evidence that social support and resilience act as significant parallel mediators in the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress. Because all confidence intervals excluded zero and direct effects remained significant (see Table 4; Fig. 2), the findings support a mediation model, in which phubbing has both direct and indirect associations with depression, anxiety, and stress.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the mediating roles of psychological resilience and social support in the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress among adolescents. The findings showed that phubbing was associated with psychological distress among adolescents. Specifically, phubbing was positively associated with depression, anxiety, and stress. The findings contribute to the literature by highlighting the links among phubbing, psychological distress, psychological resilience, and social support, and the unique structure that emerges in this area of analysis. Despite statistical significance, the relatively low explained variance in the mediators (R² = 0.02–0.03) suggests that the practical impact of phubbing on psychological distress through social support and resilience is limited. These results suggest that phubbing should not be viewed solely as problematic technology use habit among adolescents. Rather, pubbing appears to be a multifaceted social phenomenon that also affects adolescents’ interpersonal experiences and shapes their psychological processes.

This direct association between phubbing and psychological distress may reflect feelings of social exclusion, being ignored, and worthlessness in adolescents57. Phubbing may threaten adolescents’ basic psychological needs, particularly those related to belonging and acceptance58. Thus, it may be associated with higher levels of depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms in adolescents14. When the findings are evaluated in this context, it becomes clear that phubbing cannot be explained solely by individuals’ problematic digital habits, but rather as a multifaceted phenomenon affecting the developmental processes of adolescents as a source of social stress30.

One of the original contributions of this study is the identification of psychological resilience as a mediating mechanism in the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress. When psychological resilience is understood as individuals’ ability to maintain functioning and psychological integrity in the face of negative experiences59 it may function as a protective psychological resource during adolescence, a period where repetitive and everyday social stressors like phubbing have a greater impact60. Thus, adolescents with higher psychological resilience may respond more adaptively to the negative experiences associated with phubbing and may develop more adaptive coping strategies41. Based on this finding, psychological resilience is not only effective in coping with traumatic events but also emerges as an important protective factor against phubbing and its negative consequences, such as psychological distress, which are significant stressors that impair daily and social functioning61.This finding suggests that adolescents with higher psychological resilience may view phubbing experiences as temporary and manageable situations rather than interpreting them as personal rejection62. This may reduce the emotional impact of such experiences.

The role of perceived social support in the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress contributes to a clearer understanding of this association. This mediating role suggests that phubbing and its psychological associations should not be evaluated independently of their social context63. Social support may be considered an important relational resource in individuals’ efforts to understand and cope with stressful situations, especially during adolescence, a vulnerable period64. For adolescents exhibiting phubbing behavior, the social support they receive despite reduced face-to-face interaction may help reduce the negative psychological processes associated with decreased face-to-face interaction and psychological distress22. Thus, the findings support the hypothesis that support from close others and the broader social environment may have a protective role in adolescents’ mental health in the digital context65. Family support, close friendships, and a positive school climate can mitigate the negative associations of phubbing by providing adolescents with emotional security, a sense of acceptance, and alternative sources of belonging66. In this context, social support can be considered a relational resource that helps explain the association between phubbing and adolescents’ psychological distress.

When these findings are considered holistically, it can be said that individual resources such as psychological resilience and interpersonal experiences such as social support form a dynamic structure that regulates the relationship between phubbing and psychological distress67,68. These results indicate that evaluating the mental health implications of phubbing solely in terms of technology use is insufficient, as broader psychosocial processes also play an important role. These findings offer significant practical implications. School counselors can develop psychoeducational programs focused on healthy technology use, peer communication, and emotion regulation skills. Family-based interventions can strengthen supportive communication within the home. Furthermore, programs aimed at increasing psychological resilience may help adolescents cope more effectively with daily social stressors.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, psycho-educational programs focusing on healthy technology use, peer communication, and emotion regulation skills may be developed in schools. Families can be provided with training on mindful communication, supportive parenting, and digital awareness. Furthermore, interventions aimed at strengthening psychological resilience may help adolescents cope more effectively with daily social stressors.

Limitations and conclusions

The findings of the present study provide a comprehensive overview of the association of phubbing behavior with psychological distress among adolescents, but certain limitations should be considered. First, the sample of this study consisted solely of adolescents. Future research should examine whether these associations differ across different age groups. Second, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to predict the long-term associations between phubbing on mental health. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the long-term associations among these variables. Third, the study relied on self-report measures. Future studies could address this limitation by using multiple data sources, behavioral indicators, or qualitative and mixed-method designs. Another limitation of this study is that significant potential confounding factors that could influence the observed relationships, such as age, socioeconomic status, and daily smartphone screen time, were not controlled for.

In conclusion, this study showed that phubbing behavior is associated with higher psychological distress among adolescents, while psychological resilience and perceived social support emerged as protective mechanisms. The findings indicated that lower levels of phubbing were associated with more favorable psychological outcomes, including lower anxiety and stress symptoms. These findings suggest that phubbing is not only a digital behavior but also a potential risk factor for negative psychological distress among adolescents, with possible implications for the quality of their interpersonal relationships.

One of the key contributions of this study is the identification of psychological resilience and social support as protective mechanisms explaining this relationship. The findings showed that higher levels of phubbing were associated with lower levels of psychological resilience and perceived social support, whereas higher psychological resilience and perceived social support were associated with lower levels of psychological distress. Furthermore, mediation analyses revealed that part of the association between phubbing and psychological distress occurred through psychological resilience and social support. This indicates that individual resilience and social resources may play an important role in adolescents’ responses to digital social stressors.

These findings emphasize that focusing solely on reducing risk behaviors is insufficient in efforts to protect and strengthen adolescent mental health; such efforts should also support the development of individual resources such as psychological resilience and strengthening social support networks. School-based psychoeducational programs, interventions aimed at strengthening family and peer relationships, and supportive practices to be associated with psychological resilience have significant potential in mitigating the negative associations of phubbing. Future studies that test causal relationships using longitudinal designs and comparatively address the function of protective mechanisms in different cultural contexts may make significant contributions to the literature.
