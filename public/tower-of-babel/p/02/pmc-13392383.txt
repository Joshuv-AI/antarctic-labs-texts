An agent-based platform for simulating the impact of chlamydia vaccine in the US population
Authors: Qi Deng, Grégoire Ranson, Nicola Luigi Bragazzi, Valerie Bosch Castells, Sandra S Chaves, Edward Thommes, Jianhong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChlamydia trachomatis (CT) remains the most reported bacterial sexually transmitted infection in the United States (US), underscoring the urgent need for an effective vaccine. An agent-based model calibrated to the US National Survey of Family Growth (NSFG) and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) datasets was developed and used to study the impact of vaccine on the CT and all-cause pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) burden. The model was further validated against independent epidemiological data. Here we show that a conventional vaccine with 50% efficacy and 60% coverage can achieve a 64.2% [95%CI: 43.0–68.1] reduction in chlamydia prevalence and a 14.3% [95%CI: 10.1–29.4] reduction in the prevalence of women with one or more lifetime episodes of all-cause PID. Prioritizing vaccination among individuals aged 15–24 yielded the greatest impact, even with moderate vaccine performance, highlighting the importance of age-targeted, gender-inclusive strategies. These findings provide quantitative evidence to support the design and implementation of chlamydia vaccination programs and highlight the value of agent-based modeling frameworks for evaluating complex intervention strategies in sexually transmitted infections.Subject terms: Bacterial infection, Epidemiology, Vaccines

Introduction

Chlamydia trachomatis (CT) is the most frequently reported bacterial sexually transmitted infection (STI) worldwide1. In 2023, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported 1.6 million cases of chlamydia, which represents two-thirds of all STIs reported that year. Nonetheless, chlamydia is mainly asymptomatic (>80%) at any infection site2–5, and reporting cases from surveillance data are likely to underestimate the prevalence of the disease6. The data are also likely to be biased by opportunistic screening practices because of the focus on young women and at-risk groups in the population (like man who have sex with men (MSM) and sex workers)7. Untreated infections can lead to severe reproductive health outcomes, especially in women, as infection can ascend the reproductive tract, causing pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) that may lead to ectopic pregnancy and infertility8.

Some countries have implemented CT control programs using the ‘test and treat’ approach for asymptomatic at-risk groups9, but evidence of their effectiveness in reducing infection prevalence or preventing PID is limited. Despite screening recommendations, uptake remains suboptimal, and some scientists suggest that these programs might detect transient self-resolving infections10. While CT has not shown significant antimicrobial resistance (AMR)11,12, this has been described in association with other pathogens like gonorrhea13. Routine screening in low-prevalence populations may not be cost-effective14–16, leading some health agencies to consider more targeted approaches for high-risk groups or behavioral interventions13,14.

The development of a vaccine against CT represents a promising avenue to enhance existing control17,18. Several vaccine candidates are under development in preclinical and early clinical trials19–21. Thus, a flexible, user-friendly modeling platform would be particularly timely as a tool to simulate CT transmission in a population and to investigate how pharmacological interventions can mitigate the disease burden. The value of such a platform would be further amplified if it were built in such a way as to be readily extensible to other sexually transmitted diseases. Mathematical modeling studies can be a useful tool for evaluating the impact of vaccines17,22–24. Recently, Makhoul et al.17 examined the potential population-level impact of a hypothetical chlamydia vaccine in the US using a deterministic compartmental framework. They demonstrated that a moderately efficacious CT vaccine could substantially reduce chlamydia prevalence. Such compartmental models provide valuable insights into forming chlamydia epidemiology and vaccination policy, particularly for large-scale projections.

In our study, we focused on questions that require explicit representation of individual infection histories, dynamics of sexual partnerships and the accumulation of long-term sequelae. Addressing these outcomes requires tracking individuals over time and linking transmission events to downstream health consequences, which is more naturally represented within an agent-based modeling (ABM) framework25–27. We therefore develop an ABM to simulate CT transmission dynamics and evaluate candidate vaccination strategies under realistic scenarios. For this analysis, we will explore the impact of a chlamydia vaccination program on two main outcomes: 1) CT infection rates in the community (males and females), and 2) PID rates in the overall female population. We implement different vaccination scenarios, varying vaccine efficacy, uptake and recommendations in people aged 15–44 years (Table 1). We explore the potential benefit of the vaccine in reducing the risk of infection and CT transmission and test the impact of population prioritization.

Results

Vaccine efficacy (VE) and vaccine coverage (VC) required to reduce prevalence of chlamydia infection in the community

An ongoing vaccination campaign with a moderately efficacious vaccine (VE = 50%) and moderate coverage (VC = 60%) resulted in chlamydia prevalence decreased by 64.2% [95%CI: 43.0–68.1] relative to the baseline scenario without vaccination (Fig. 1a, c), while PID prevalence declined by 14.3% [95%CI: 10.1–29.4] (Fig. 1b, c). Our simulations suggested that an efficacy of at least 80% with 60% coverage, or alternatively 50% efficacy with 90% coverage, is necessary to achieve reductions in chlamydia prevalence exceeding 80%. The greatest reductions in both chlamydia and PID burden, however, are observed when both VE and VC exceed 80%. Under these conditions, chlamydia prevalence declines to near-zero levels, highlighting the potential for disease elimination with a high-performing vaccine and widespread implementation.

Impact of VE waning rates

A vaccine with rapid waning rates (decay rates of (1 year)−1 or (5 years)−1) may have limited long-term effectiveness (Fig. 2), assuming a fixed VC of 60%. In contrast, vaccines with slower waning rates, such as (10 years)−1 or longer, are associated with substantially greater reductions. Specifically, a vaccine with a 10-year decay rate achieves a 60.8% [95% CI: 50.8–75.9] reduction in chlamydia prevalence, although this was 33.7% [95% CI: 14.5–48.0] less reduction compared to a vaccine with constant VE. We adopt a 10-year half-life assumption for VE decay in all subsequent vaccination scenario analyses. Under this assumption, vaccine-induced immunity decreases by half every decade and can be considered to have almost disappeared approximately 40 years after vaccination.

Impact of vaccination scale-up under waning immunity

Figure 3a shows the resulting population-level effective immunity over time, defined as the average individual-level vaccine protection across the population. Under immediate coverage, effective immunity rises sharply following vaccine introduction but subsequently declines as vaccine-induced protection wanes. In contrast, gradual scale-up scenarios generate slower accumulation of population immunity, with peak effective immunity occurring later and at lower levels as scale-up duration increases. These patterns illustrate the competition between immunity gains from vaccine rollout and losses due to waning protection. Immediate coverage achieves the largest relative reduction in chlamydia prevalence (59.0% [95% CI: 45.9–72.1]), followed by 5-year (52.5% [95% CI: 31.1–73.9]) and 10-year scale-up scenarios (51.9% [95% CI: 27.7–76.1]) (Fig. 3b). In the subsequent scenario analyses, we adopt the immediate scale-up assumption to provide clearer comparison across vaccine characteristics and targeting strategies.

Impact of different vaccine mechanisms

In the absence of vaccination, chlamydia prevalence remains around 2 to 2.5% (Fig. 4a). The most pronounced reduction is observed when the vaccine reduces both susceptibility to infection and infectiousness, resulting in the theoretical elimination of CT infection within 20 years. Vaccines that target either susceptibility or infectiousness alone also led to significant reductions in CT infections and PID, though the decline is slower. Figure 4b presents chlamydia and PID prevalences at year 20 following vaccine implementation. Notably, there is no statistically significant difference between the prevalences obtained in the susceptibility-reduction-only versus the infectiousness-reduction-only scenarios. The simulation predicts an 83.7% [95% CI: 75.1–92.3] reduction in chlamydia prevalence and a 21.6% [95% CI: 16.3–26.9] reduction in PID prevalence in the general population under the dual-mechanism vaccine.

Impact of target population prioritization

Our simulations indicate that prioritizing vaccination for females only or for older adults (ages 25–44 years) leads to relatively modest reductions in disease burden (Fig. 5a). Notably, even when vaccine coverage among these groups reaches 90%, the reduction in chlamydia prevalence (22.6% [95% CI: 12.6–47.5]) is comparable to that achieved by vaccinating only 60% of young individuals aged 15–24 years (23.2% [95% CI: 15.0–57.5]). Given that achieving 60% vaccine coverage is generally more feasible than achieving 90% coverage, targeting younger populations may represent a more efficient strategy. The most substantial reductions are observed when vaccination targets both males and females across the broader 15–44-year age range. Vaccinating 60% of both males and females aged 15–44 years led to a 61.2% [95% CI: 46.9–75.5] reduction in chlamydia prevalence, and increasing coverage to 90% achieved a reduction of 84.6% [95% CI: 76.5–91.7]. Similar but less pronounced patterns are observed for PID outcomes, reflecting the cumulative nature of PID development (Fig. 5b). Under the most comprehensive strategy (90% vaccination of both sexes), PID prevalence decreases by 23.4% [95% CI: 22.0–24.8] after 20 years. To complement prevalence-based comparisons, we calculate the number of vaccinations needed to avert one chlamydia infection (VNAI) under each prioritization strategy (Supplementary Table 6, Supplementary Fig. 5). Vaccination strategies targeting younger individuals (ages 15–24 years) are consistently the most efficient and robust, with a median VNAI of 3.7 and a mean of 6.6 at 60% coverage. In contrast, programs prioritizing older adults (ages 25–44 years) required substantially more vaccinations per infection averted and exhibited much greater uncertainty, with a median VNAI of 5.1, a mean of 18.0, and a wide uncertainty interval (2.6–113.6) at 60% coverage. Vaccinating both females and males yields a lower median VNAI (4.2) than vaccinating females only (4.7), with a narrower uncertainty interval. This advantage becomes more pronounced at higher coverage levels: at 90% coverage, the median VNAI decreases to 3.1 under gender-neutral vaccination compared with 3.5 for female-only strategies, alongside a substantially reduced upper tail of the distribution.

Discussion

Previous agent-based modeling studies have provided important insights into chlamydia transmission and control, including sexual network models of screening and reinfection dynamics14,25, analyses of male screening9, partner notification strategies27, and vaccine impact assessments26. Our model builds on these works but extends them in several critical ways.

First, we incorporate greater population heterogeneity, including heterosexuals, MSM, and FSW, with dynamic relationship patterns. Second, we explicitly track infection histories and lifetime all-cause PID prevalence, allowing direct estimation of vaccine effects on the broader reproductive health. Third, we perform a rigorous three-step model calibration: (i) sexual network calibration to the NSFG data, (ii) chlamydia prevalence calibration across age and sex to the NHANES data, and (iii) all-cause PID calibration across age to the NHANES data. Fourth, we conduct model validation using epidemiological datasets that are independent of the calibration targets. This validation demonstrates that the model can reproduce multiple age- and time-related features of CT infection and PID without direct calibration to these outcomes, thereby increasing confidence that the model captures key mechanisms governing disease progression and long-term sequelae. We believe that these extensions support the use of the model for evaluating vaccination scenarios and provide complementary insights into the potential long-term public health impact of chlamydia vaccination.

Our simulations show that a vaccine against CT infection can significantly reduce both CT cases and all-cause PID in the US population, even with a moderate efficacy (50%) and coverage (60%), respectively. This result underscores the value of vaccine candidates currently under development, even if they do not include sterilizing immunity19,20,28, consistent with evidence that natural protective immunity to CT infection is partial and wanes over time29. In the context of persistent transmission despite decades of screening efforts, a vaccine offering partial protection could provide a critical new tool in chlamydia control. Further, our analyses also reveal the flexible tradeoff between VE and VC. We show that high coverage can partially offset lower efficacy, and vice versa. For example, either 80% VE with 60% VC or 50% VE with 90% VC achieved reductions in CT prevalence exceeding 80%. This finding has important implications for public health programs, suggesting that tailored strategies may be viable in different settings depending on logistical constraints, vaccine hesitancy, and health system capacity.

A key determinant of vaccine impact is the duration of protection. For example, a vaccine with a 10-year half-life reduced prevalence by nearly 60%, whereas one with a 1-year half-life achieved only a 14% reduction. These results reinforce the importance of designing vaccines with long-lasting immune responses and suggest that boosters may be needed to maintain population-level benefits if durability is limited. Mechanistically, we find that vaccines that reduce both susceptibility to infection and infectiousness among infected individuals have the greatest impact, with the potential to reduce CT prevalence to zero. While most vaccine evaluations focus on susceptibility alone, our results demonstrate that incorporating reduced infectiousness can dramatically amplify public health benefits17,26,30,31. This suggests that clinical trials should measure not only infection endpoints but also bacterial load and transmission potential, and that vaccine developers should consider immune responses capable of lowering pathogen shedding.

The scale-up scenarios further highlight the interaction between vaccination rollout and waning immunity. Our results show that slower rollout in coverage leads to delayed and attenuated reductions in CT prevalence, although long-term prevalence levels converge once target coverage is reached. These findings underscore that population-level impact depends not only on the eventual coverage achieved but also on the pace of vaccine deployment. This is particularly relevant for adult vaccination programs, which typically lack school-based delivery infrastructure and face substantial logistical and behavioral constraints. Consistent with real-world experience from adult hepatitis A/B vaccination in the US32, our results suggest that vaccine durability and sustained rollout efforts will be critical determinants of the public health impact of a chlamydia vaccine.

Targeting vaccination to specific subgroups also affects overall program efficiency. We show that prioritizing younger individuals aged 15–24 years, even at moderate coverage levels, is more effective than high-coverage programs focused on older adults or females only. This is consistent with chlamydia epidemiology and suggests that adolescent or early young adult vaccination, analogous to current HPV vaccination strategies, would be an efficient and impactful policy. Including both males and females in vaccination programs further enhances impact, highlighting the importance of gender-inclusive approaches. Across prioritization programs, the median VNAI over a 20-year horizon ranged from 3 to 5 (Supplementary Table 6, Fig. 5). Vaccination strategies targeting younger individuals or adopting gender-neutral approaches were consistently more efficient and robust. Increasing coverage from 60% to 90% further reduced both the median and mean VNAI and the upper tail of the distribution, particularly for gender-neutral strategies. These estimates are closely aligned with previously reported values17.

Despite substantial reductions in chlamydia incidence, declines in lifetime PID prevalence were more modest. It is important to recall, though, that this is a reduction in all-cause PID, with non-CT-associated PID modeled as a baseline PID background. In our simulations, the CT attributable fraction of PID is 37.9% [95%CI: 17.6–60.4], consistent with epidemiological reports33,34. We nevertheless chose all-cause PID reduction as our outcome due to its greater relevance for assessing the overall public health burden and the potential impact of a CT vaccine on healthcare needs. Indeed, it is critical to remember that PID is strongly associated with other significant reproductive health issues in women, including infertility and ectopic pregnancy. PID-related tubal damage can obstruct the passage of eggs or sperm, leading to these severe outcomes. Thus, the projected reduction in PID cases indirectly implies a potential reduction in the burden of infertility and ectopic pregnancy. Furthermore, while not modeled here, it is important to note that CT infection can also contribute to infertility in men35,36.

Several previous deterministic modeling studies have also examined the impact of a hypothetical chlamydia vaccine in the US. In particular, Makhoul et al.17 observed sustained reductions in chlamydia burden across a wide range of vaccine efficacy, duration of protection and coverage assumptions. Our findings are broadly consistent with these conclusions. At the same time, our study extends prior work by using an agent-based framework that explicitly captures individual-level heterogeneity, reinfection histories, waning immunity, and gradual vaccine scale-up. This allows direct assessment of long-term PID burden alongside prevalence reduction.

Our study has several limitations. The agent-based model simplifies sexual networks, maintaining a constant 20,000-agent population where 55-year-olds are replaced by 15-year-olds, without considering other demographic changes. The age range (15–54) balances computational feasibility with epidemiological relevance, as US data indicates minimal sexual activity below 1537 and minimal CT transmission above 5438. Although key aspects of partnership formation and dissolution were informed by empirical data, the sexual network structure necessarily relies on structural assumptions. In particular, the agent-based interaction rules may generate heterogeneity in cumulative partnership exposure and concurrency over time. While such heterogeneity is plausible, CT transmission is characterized by substantial general-population exposure rather than strong concentration within narrowly defined core groups. Network heterogeneity is known to amplify the apparent population-level impact of interventions such as vaccination and may therefore contribute to the magnitude of vaccine effects observed in some scenarios. The results presented should thus be interpreted as scenario-based projections under plausible network assumptions, rather than precise quantitative forecasts for the US population. We do not explicitly model vaccination’s effect on CT infection duration17,26; instead, incorporating it within vaccine efficacy against infectiousness. Transmission probabilities remain constant across individuals and time, omitting variations due to condom use, partner notification, treatment behaviors, or biological heterogeneity. Regarding PID risk in untreated CT infections, available data only support a one-time probability4,31, without evidence on risk changes with repeated untreated episodes. Our fixed-risk assumption for untreated cases may underestimate the cumulative PID burden. These aspects warrant future research.

In conclusion, this modeling study strongly supports the development and deployment of a chlamydia vaccine as a valuable addition to current control strategies. Even partially effective vaccines with moderate uptake could meaningfully reduce the burden of infection and PID, and by extension, other associated sequelae in women. Optimizing vaccine characteristics, especially durability and the ability to reduce infectiousness, and targeting both males and females will be essential to achieving maximal public health impact. Our findings provide actionable insights for researchers, policymakers, and funders, and underscore the need for continued investment and collaborative efforts to translate vaccine development into real-world disease reduction and improved public health outcomes for both women and men.

We emphasize that the modeling framework developed here can be readily adapted to other sexually transmitted infections; the sexual network simulation component is by itself agnostic about the STI being transmitted, and thus, in principle, other diseases, or indeed, combinations of diseases, could be “dropped in”.

Methods

Ethical approval was not required for this study as it did not involve human participants or the collection of individual-level identifiable data. The study was based on publicly available, de-identified datasets (NSFG and NHANES) and a simulation-based modeling framework.

Our ABM tracks 20,000 sexually active agents, aged 15 to 54 years, with equal numbers of males and females over time. The age distribution of the simulated population mimics that of the US population within the corresponding age range39. Each agent is characterized by a set of attributes, including age, sex, sexual behavior (heterosexual, female sex workers (FSW) or MSM), preferred number of concurrent partners (pnp), vaccination status, CT infection status and age-specific immunity level against CT infection. We did not consider male sex workers as they constitute a marginalized subgroup of the overall group40 and women who have sex with women, as they contribute very little to STI transmission41. The values and distributions of model parameters, along with their sources, are provided in Table 2. Note that parameters sampled from normal distributions are truncated at lower bounds to prevent non-physical negative. For example, the duration of treated chlamydia infection was sampled from a normal distribution with a mean of 2 weeks and a variance of 2 weeks, with a lower bound of 1 week26. The model simulation operates with a time step of one week. The algorithmic description of our model implementation is provided in Supplementary Methods. At each simulation step, the model executes three main operations: the removal and addition of agents, the formation and dissolution of dynamic relationships, and the transmission of disease.

Agent removal and addition

To maintain a dynamic yet constant population size, each agent ages by one week at every simulation time step. Once an agent reaches 55 years of age, they are immediately removed from the network, and any relationships involving that agent are dissolved. Simultaneously, a new 15-year-old agent is introduced into the network to replace the departing individual. The new agent is assigned a sex that balances the female: male ratio over time. The sexual behavior of new agents is randomly assigned in accordance with the predefined distribution of characteristics.

Upon entering the population, new agents have no existing relationships but are immediately available to form them. These incoming agents are considered naïve, meaning they are neither infected nor vaccinated. It is assumed that all agents retain consistent sexual behavior throughout their lifetime.

Dynamic relationship formation and dissolution

The formation and dissolution of relationships are strongly related to agents’ age, sexual behavior and predefined partnership preference. In the model, heterosexual individuals form partnerships exclusively with agents of the opposite sex, while MSM form partnerships exclusively with other MSM individuals. FSW can establish relationships with any heterosexual male. Let pij denote the probability that agent i forms a relationship with agent j. This probability is assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution centered around the age difference between the agents. in which ∆ is the age difference between agents and is defined based on the relationship type: for homosexual pairs ∆ = |agei − agej |; for heterosexual pairs ∆ = agemale − agefemale − μ. The sex workers are likely to have sexual relationships with people of a wide age range. Thus, if either agent is a sex worker, the probability of relationship formation is set to 1, regardless of age. This formulation ensures that: (1) agents of a similar age are more likely to form a relationship; (2) male agents are likely to form a relationship with females who are younger than themselves. This aligns with the observed facts in the US42.

Once a relationship is formed, it is probabilistically designated as either long-term or short-term. The duration of relationships is geometrically distributed, with long- and short-term relationships having different average values; the duration of each partnership is then stochastically drawn from the appropriate distribution. Partnerships involving FSW are treated separately: they are always modeled as short-term encounters, with durations drawn from a normal distribution (mean 2 weeks, variance 2 weeks). In reality, FSWs may maintain longer-term relationships, which are not explicitly represented in the model. However, given the relatively small size of the FSW population and the focus of our analyses on population-level vaccination effects, this simplifying assumption is unlikely to materially influence the main conclusions. At each simulation step, the model decrements the duration between the partners by one week. When the duration reaches zero, the relationship is dissolved. Following dissolution, both agents enter a gap period during which they are temporarily ineligible to form new relationships. This ensures a realistic turnover rate in partnerships and mimics social network stability. An exception is made for sex workers, who do not experience a gap period between partnerships. While most agents prefer to maintain monogamous relationships, a minority (5% of the population) engage in concurrent partnerships, with each participating agent allowed up to three simultaneous partners.

Disease transmission

We consider the rates of chlamydia infection in the general population of both males and females. Initially, a proportion of agents is assumed to be infected with chlamydia based on the age- and sex-specific prevalence estimates in the US43. Then, at each simulation step, chlamydia transmission occurs through sexual contact. The model has Susceptible - Exposed - Infected - Recovered – Susceptible (SEIRS) transmission dynamics. In this model, susceptible agents are those who are not currently infected with chlamydia and lack immunity, rendering them at risk of acquiring infection upon exposure. A susceptible agent becomes infected following sexual contact with an infectious partner, with the per-contact probability of transmission determined by the direction of transmission: from an infected male to a susceptible female, the probability is βm→f, whereas from an infected female/male to a susceptible male, the probability is βf/m→m.

The frequency of sexual contact is relationship-type specific: on average, long-term partnerships involve 1.75 sexual encounters per week, short-term partnerships involve 3 weekly encounters26, and partnerships with FSWs include 10 encounters per week. Following infection, susceptible agents transition to the exposed state, during which they are not yet infectious. After an incubation period of two weeks26, exposed agents move into either the asymptomatic infected state with a probability of 85% or into the symptomatic infected state4,5. Symptomatic individuals have a weekly probability of seeking treatment, while the asymptomatic agents have a chance to be screened and detected. Infected agents who are detected and treated are assumed to recover within two weeks26. Those who remain undiagnosed and untreated experience prolonged infection durations, which are randomly drawn from a normal distribution with a mean of 52 weeks and a variance of 52 weeks. A lower bound with 45 weeks was applied to reflect the typically prolonged duration of untreated infection44,45. Recovered agents acquire partial and temporary natural immunity, meaning their susceptibility to reinfection after exposure is reduced relative to susceptible agents. The magnitude of this reduction is modeled by stochastically drawing from a uniform distribution spanning 20% to 80%. This assumption is consistent with estimates reported by the work46, which inferred a mean reduction in susceptibility of 67% [95% uncertainty interval: 24%–88%] based on US data. Following recovery, individuals enter a refractory period representing temporary post-infection immunity, before gradually returning to the fully susceptible state. The average refractory duration after recovery defines the mean length of this post-recovery immune period and is modeled as a stochastic process with a mean of 6 weeks following natural recovery and 2 weeks following treatment, consistent with26. This representation of partial, waning immunity acting through reduced susceptibility is consistent with prior mathematical modeling studies of CT, which suggest that long-lasting or complete immunity is incompatible with observed reinfection patterns and endemic persistence46,47.

Female agents have an age-dependent weekly probability (Pbase) of developing all-cause PID independent of CT infection. For treated CT-infected women, studies indicate PID risk increases by 55% after initial infection, an additional 17% following second infection, and 35% after third or subsequent infections, compared to uninfected women30, on top of Pbase. Untreated CT-infected women face a one-time 16% probability of developing PID4,31, independent of Pbase or infection history. Model calibration varied Pbase to match U.S. female population PID prevalence estimates48, confirming Pbase is well below 1%, with untreated CT-infected individuals having substantially higher overall PID risk than treated ones.

Using the calibrated and validated model, we evaluate various vaccination scenarios’ impact on chlamydia prevalence in males and females, and all-cause PID in US females (Table 1). Vaccination targets individuals under 44 years, regardless of infection history, until the simulation end. Vaccine coverage is the proportion of the target population vaccinated. For example, a coverage level of 60% implies that 60% of the targeted population currently has vaccine protection. The model doesn’t account for multiple doses or boosters. Each scenario was simulated using 20 independent stochastic realizations to account for the random variability inherent to the ABM. To assess whether this number of simulations adequately captures stochastic uncertainty, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using 10, 20, 50 and 100 simulations. Across these analyses, mean outcome trajectories and relative differences between scenarios were highly consistent, without altering qualitative conclusions or scenario rankings (Supplementary Fig. 4). Based on this convergence behavior and the substantial computational cost of the model, we therefore report results based on 20 simulations per scenario.

The base scenario systematically examined different combinations of vaccine efficacy (VE) and coverage (VC) for substantial chlamydia and PID reduction. It assumed constant vaccine protection (i.e., susceptibility reduction to infection) for uninfected individuals. Recognizing vaccine-induced immunity may wane over time due to immune memory degradation or pathogen evolution20, we extended this to include time-varying VE with fixed VC, modeling exponentially declining immunity. In addition, to assess the impact of a gradual uptake of the vaccine while considering wanning of vaccine-induced immunity, we conducted a scenario analysis in which VC gradually increases to 60% over 5 or 10 years, while VE exponentially declines from an initial value of 80%.

While vaccines often aim for sterilizing immunity, many provide partial protection by reducing susceptibility, disease severity, or infectiousness28,49. For a potential chlamydia vaccine, protection could reduce susceptibility (as in previous scenarios) and lower infectiousness among vaccinated infected individuals, potentially by decreasing bacterial load via vaccine-induced immune responses26,28,49. We simulated three scenarios where vaccines: (1) reduce susceptibility only, (2) reduce infectiousness only, or (3) reduce both, administered to individuals aged 15–44 years. Finally, we explored targeted vaccination strategies by age and sex versus broader population approaches. The Supplementary Tables 1–6 summarize relative reductions in chlamydia and PID prevalence under each scenario. Although simulations were conducted in a finite population, demographic turnover through agent aging and replacement ensured continuous population renewal and reduced the likelihood of stochastic extinction unrelated to intervention effects. Importantly, extinction was not observed in isolated or sporadic simulation replicates across all scenario’s runs. When near elimination occurred, it did so consistently across all stochastic realizations within a given scenario.
