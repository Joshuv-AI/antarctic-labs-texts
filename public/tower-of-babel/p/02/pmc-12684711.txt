A cardiac event triggers an endocrine investigation: a case report of primary hyperaldosteronism revealed by postpartum cardiomyopathy

Introduction

Postpartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) is a pregnancy-related pathology that is characterized by the development of heart failure (HF) secondary to left ventricular systolic dysfunction towards the end of pregnancy. The diagnosis of PPCM is based on exclusion; thus, it is necessary to eliminate other potential aetiologies. This case serves to emphasize the significance of considering endocrine disorders as potential aetiologies for HF in young postpartum women. Healthcare providers must consider the potential impact of various endocrine disorders on the structure and function of the cardiac muscle, as undiagnosed endocrine pathologies in young patients may lead to catastrophic complications.

Case presentation

A 41-year-old mother of three presented to the obstetrics department during her 7-month twin pregnancy with hypertension [blood pressure (BP) 180/110 mmHg] and proteinuria of 0.87 g per 24 h. She was diagnosed with preeclampsia. An early delivery was indicated with an emergency caesarean section. Both foetuses died due to intrauterine foetal distress. Shortly after labour, the patient developed orthopnoea and bilateral pedal oedema. Physical examination revealed normal heart sounds, systolic murmurs in the mitral and tricuspid areas, along with pulmonary rales and peripheral oedema. The electrocardiogram showed a regular rhythm, no evidence of ventricular hypertrophy or conduction abnormalities. Chest radiography cardiomegaly and pulmonary oedema. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction of 40%, concentric LV hypertrophy, and increased right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) (>55 mmHg).

The diagnosis of acute HF was made, and the patient was managed with intravenous diuretics and potassium supplementation. Forty-eight hours later, she was transferred to the cardiology department for further investigation of acute HF during pregnancy.

The biological assessment after the acute episode was unremarkable except for hypokalaemia. Given the clinical presentation and peripartum context, PPCM was initially suspected. However, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed features suggestive of hypertensive cardiomyopathy. This unexpected result prompted further evaluation with abdominal computerized tomography (CT) angiography, which revealed an adrenal adenoma (Figure 1).

Adrenal angioscan image: right adrenal adenoma measuring 16 × 9 mm.

The diagnosis of primary hyperaldosteronism was made based on an elevated aldosterone/renin ratio (309; normal < 64), elevated plasma aldosterone, and suppressed plasma renin activity values. A laparoscopic adrenalectomy was subsequently performed (Figure 2). One-year follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic, with normal BP and normal cardiac function on echocardiography. She was no longer on any medication.

Piece of adrenalectomy: morphological appearance compatible with adrenocortical adenoma. No histological evidence of malignancy.

Discussion

Question 1: when can a heart failure flare-up be considered postpartum cardiomyopathy?

The diagnosis of PPCM should be considered in young postpartum women presenting with HF. According the guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology, PPCM is defined by three criteria:

Heart failure secondary to left ventricular systolic dysfunction with an LVEF of <45%

Despite ongoing research, the exact pathophysiology of PPCM remains unknown. However, it is believed to result from a multifactorial process, involving increased oxidative stress, microvascular dysfunction, inflammation, environmental factors, and genetic predisposition. Notably, preeclampsia supports rather than excludes the diagnosis of PPCM.2

In the present case, PPCM was initially suspected due to several risk factors, such as chronic hypertension, twin pregnancy, maternal age over 30, African ancestry, and preeclampsia. However, further investigations including cardiac MRI findings, persistent hypokalaemia, and the discovery of an adrenal adenoma, as well as the results of the aldosterone/renin ratio test, pointed towards an alternative diagnosis: primary hyperaldosteronism.

This case highlights the importance of considering secondary causes of cardiomyopathy in the peri- and post-partum period. It is interesting to note the severe presentation of primary hyperaldosteronism in this case.

Question 2: what is the relationship between primary hyperaldosteronism and pregnancy?

Primary aldosteronism (PA), first described by Litynsky and later by Jerome Conn in 1955, is a form of hypertension caused by the overproduction of aldosterone by the adrenals.3 Primary aldosteronism is characterized by suppressed renin levels due to autonomous aldosterone secretion, resulting in elevated BP levels, hypokalaemia, and hypernatremia.4 During pregnancy, several hormones, such as oestrogen and progesterone, increase, leading to changes in the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS). As a result, the aldosterone level increases 10-fold, leading to plasma volume expansion and increased cardiac output. These effects are counterbalanced by the vasodilatory and natriuretic actions of progesterone, which prevents the development of hypertension in most pregnant women.5

In patient with PA, this homeostatic balance is disrupted, and the elevated levels of aldosterone have adverse effects on the heart. They are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events and mortality, particularly when aldosterone secretion is inappropriate for renin levels and sodium intake.6

In our case, the aldosterone/renin ratio could not be assessed in post-partum period due to expected hormonal fluctuations, which could have led to false-positive results. The test was therefore postponed until hormonal stabilization has achieved, allowing for a more accurate and reliable interpretation.

Question 3: what are the hormonal changes during pregnancy?

Oestrogen and progesterone: During pregnancy, oestrogen stimulates hepatic synthesis of angiotensinogen, the precursor of angiotensin I. Both oestrogen and progesterone increase the secretion of renin and angiotensin-converting enzyme. It leads to increased secretion of angiotensin II, which stimulates the glomerulosa and increases aldosterone levels. As a result, the level of angiotensin II is multiplied by three during pregnancy.

Renin: During pregnancy, two sources of renin should be considered: renal and uterine. While the mechanisms regulating uterine renin production remain unclear, it is known that renin activity significantly increases during pregnancy. In addition to the kidney, the uterus, ovaries, and placenta produce pro-renin. The physiological role of elevated pro-renin has yet to be discovered, as there is no evidence that it is converted to renin in the vessels.

RAAS: The renin–angiotensin system undergoes major adaptations during pregnancy, and the aldosterone level increases, reaching 10 times higher concentration levels. Moreover, this increase is due to increased renin and angiotensin levels.5 Aldosterone causes fluid overload and increased cardiac output. It leads to plasma volume expansion due to continued sodium retention by the kidney on the one side and peripheral vasodilation on the other side to maintain a high rate of placental perfusion for substrate supply and balance foetal fluid volume.4 Despite this increase, pregnant women are generally normotensive due to the antagonistic effect of progesterone (a competitive inhibitor of aldosterone in the distal convoluted tubule). The physiological effects of the increase in aldosterone are thus attenuated during pregnancy.

Pathophysiological role of aldosterone in PA: The excess of aldosterone in PA stimulates endothelial dysfunction and inflammatory cell infiltration and promotes atherosclerotic plaque development and plaque instability, arterial stiffness, and calcification. At the cardiac level, aldosterone increases cardiac inflammation, fibrosis, and myocardial hypertrophy. As a clinical consequence, elevated aldosterone levels are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events and mortality, particularly when aldosterone secretion is inappropriate for renin levels and sodium intake, as in PA.

In our case, the excess aldosterone, combined with pregnancy-related hormonal changes, appeared to have pathological effects on cardiomyocytes. This contributed to the development of an episode of acute HF.

Overall, we should keep in mind that PPCM should always be considered a diagnosis of exclusion in women presenting with HF due to left ventricular systolic dysfunction. To avoid overdiagnosis, careful evaluation of pre-existing cardiac conditions is essential, particularly cardiomyopathies, hypertensive heart disease, and valvular disorders. While severe preeclampsia may lead to HF through diastolic dysfunction, PPCM should only be diagnosed in the presence of systolic dysfunction, and LVEF must be <45%, with ventricular dilatation that may or may not be present.7 In our case, the presence of concentric LV hypertrophy associated with reduced ejection fraction was more consistent with hypertensive cardiomyopathy related to excess aldosterone, which showed significant improvement following adrenalectomy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case serves to emphasize the significance of considering endocrine disorders as potential aetiologies for HF in young postpartum women. It is imperative for healthcare providers to consider the possibility of disorders such as PA in the differential diagnosis, as not all cases of postpartum HF are necessarily attributed to postpartum cardiomyopathy.
