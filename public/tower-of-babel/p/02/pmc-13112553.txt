Conduction Disturbances After TAVR
Authors: Hüseyin Ayhan, İdris Yakut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: conduction disturbances, permanent pace maker, transcatheter aortic valve replacement

Conduction disturbances (CD) remain among the most frequent and clinically relevant complications following transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). Despite substantial advances in device design and implantation techniques, the occurrence of new-onset atrioventricular block or bundle branch block continues to influence postprocedural management, length of hospitalization, and long-term outcomes.

In this issue, Kesriklioglu et al1 describe the case of a 76-year-old man who presented with syncope and alternating bundle branch block 6 days after TAVR. The patient's admission ECG showed atrial fibrillation with a wide QRS complex compatible with left bundle branch block (LBBB), tachy-brady, and pauses. In fact, retrospective analysis of a routine 48-hour, 12-lead Holter recording obtained post-TAVR and prior to discharge demonstrated sequential conduction changes, progressing from LBBB to a narrow QRS complex, followed by incomplete RBBB and ultimately complete RBBB, findings consistent with alternating bundle branch block.1 The present case provides a compelling clinical illustration of the dynamic and often unpredictable nature of post-TAVR CD. Such observations reinforce the concept that post-TAVR rhythm assessment must extend beyond static findings and incorporate dynamic monitoring to guide timely, individualized device therapy.

The contemporary analysis by Nuche et al2 provides a comprehensive synthesis of the multifactorial mechanisms underlying conduction abnormalities after TAVR, integrating baseline patient characteristics, anatomical substrates, and procedural determinants. Importantly, the authors highlight that CD are rarely attributable to a single factor. Instead, they emerge from a complex interplay between pre-existing conduction system vulnerability, the spatial relationship between the aortic valve complex and the His–Purkinje system, and procedural variables such as valve type, implantation depth, and oversizing.

To conceptualize this multifaceted pathophysiology, Figure 1 summarizes the 3 principal domains contributing to post-TAVR CDs: baseline electrocardiographic features reflecting intrinsic conduction system vulnerability, anatomic characteristics influencing susceptibility to mechanical injury, and procedural factors related to valve selection and implantation technique. These insights provide essential context for interpreting the present case and highlight the need to integrate electrical, structural, and procedural considerations into post-TAVR clinical decision-making.

A Framework for Understanding Post-TAVR Conduction Disturbances

TAVR = transcatheter aortic valve replacement.

Pre-existing RBBB represents one of the strongest predictors of advanced CD following TAVR. Contemporary data demonstrate that more than half of patients with baseline RBBB develop high-grade atrioventricular block after the procedure, with the vast majority of events occurring within the first 3 days, most commonly during valve deployment. Nearly half of these patients require permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation during the index hospitalization, and early recovery of atrioventricular conduction is uncommon, particularly in those with persistent intraprocedural block.3 These observations underscore that RBBB identifies a high-risk TAVR population in whom procedural planning, extended rhythm surveillance, and individualized pacing strategies are essential to optimize outcomes. New-onset LBBB is a frequent CD following TAVR and remains a relevant clinical concern today. Patients developing persistent LBBB experience a significantly higher risk of delayed PPM implantation, with the greatest vulnerability observed within the first year after the procedure.4

The expanding adoption of TAVR has intensified the need for safe, efficient postprocedural care pathways that minimize hospital stay without compromising patient outcomes. Recent evidence highlights remote monitoring as a pivotal enabler of this paradigm shift. The studies suggest that integrating telehealth-based clinical assessment with targeted rhythm monitoring may redefine post-TAVR care.5,6 Kesriklioglu et al1 were unable to detect alternating bundle branch block because the patient was discharged before they could evaluate the 48-hour 12-lead Holter monitor. Although pacemaker implantation is not the preferred method, it should be performed if the indication arises, even on the same day if necessary. Otherwise, it can lead to more catastrophic consequences. In our own clinical series, after observing cases presenting with asystole, we performed PPM without the patient leaving the TAVR table when necessary.7

Post-TAVR Pacemaker Dependence

Beyond the binary presence of PPM implantation after TAVI, accumulating evidence underscores that the degree of pacemaker dependence and cumulative right ventricular pacing burden are central determinants of downstream clinical outcomes. Longitudinal data demonstrate that a substantial proportion of patients receiving a pacemaker after TAVR recover intrinsic conduction over time, with only one-third remaining pacemaker-dependent during follow-up.8 Post-TAVR pacing should be conceptualized as a dynamic physiological state rather than a static complication. This evolving perspective provides a critical framework for interpreting emerging data on pacing strategies and reinforces the need to align procedural planning, monitoring, and pacemaker selection with long-term ventricular synchrony and clinical prognosis. As data regarding left bundle branch pacing increase, it will be seen that pacing dependence and pacing cardiomyopathy are less common in TAVR, and PPM results in less adverse outcomes in TAVR.9

In conclusion, the real issue is not whether the patient has a pacemaker or not; the real issue is determining whether the patient needs a pacemaker before any harm occurs, and doing so flawlessly.
