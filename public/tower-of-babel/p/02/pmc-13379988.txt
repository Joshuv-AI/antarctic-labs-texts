Associations of parental physical activity and physical fitness with motor competence, physical fitness, anthropometry, and 24-hour movement behaviors in preschool children: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Loreto Alonso-Martínez, Robinson Ramírez-Vélez, Michael Duncan, Mikel Izquierdo, Alicia Alonso-Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhether parental physical activity (PA) and physical fitness (PF) are associated with preschoolers' motor competence (MC), fitness, and 24-h movement behaviors (MB) remains uncertain. We examined associations of parental moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) and PF with children's anthropometry, MC, PF, and 24-h MB in mother–child and father–child pairs. In this cross-sectional study in public schools in Pamplona (Spain) and nearby municipalities, 161 children aged 4–6 years (80 girls), 90 mothers, and 81 fathers were assessed. Parental MVPA was measured using wrist-worn accelerometers; parental PF was assessed via upper- and lower-body strength, cardiorespiratory fitness, and body composition by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). Exposures were categorized into tertiles. Child outcomes included anthropometry (fat mass, BMI z-score, waist circumference [WC]), MC (MOBAK-KG object-control [OC] and self-movement [SM]), PF (PREFIT: handgrip strength, 4×10-m shuttle run, standing long jump, and cardiorespiratory fitness), and 24-h MB (accelerometer-derived total PA, light PA, MVPA, sedentary time, and sleep across six consecutive days). Neither parental MVPA nor PF was significantly associated with children's anthropometry, MC, or PF in either dyad (all p > 0.05). However, maternal MVPA was inversely associated with child sleep duration (β = −0.59 h·night−1, 95% CI [−0.98, −0.20], p = 0.004). Conversely, higher maternal PF (moderate vs. low) was associated with a more favorable child MB profile, including greater sleep duration (β = 0.41 h·night−1, 95% CI [0.01, 0.81], p = 0.050), higher total PA (β = 60.70 min·day−1, 95% CI [18.85, 102.58], p = 0.006), higher light PA (β = 32.80 min·day−1, 95% CI [1.65, 64.02], p = 0.042), higher MVPA (β = 27.88 min·day−1, 95% CI [7.22, 48.55], p = 0.010), and lower sedentary time (β = −52.70 min·day−1, 95% CI [−102.48, −2.86], p = 0.042). For the father–child dyad, higher paternal MVPA was positively associated with child total PA (β = 44.10 min·day−1, 95% CI [1.62, 86.70], p = 0.045) and light PA (β = 37.77 min·day−1, 95% CI [6.82, 68.73], p = 0.019). These exploratory findings suggest that prioritizing child- and setting-level strategies has the potential to provide equitable, skill-rich movement opportunities, while parental engagement could be better fostered through facilitative routines rather than focusing solely on parental MVPA or fitness.

Introduction

Motor competence (MC), the capacity to execute fundamental locomotor and object-control (OC) skills, is a foundational marker of early neuro-motor development and a proposed upstream determinant of children's health trajectories.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Competent movement supports engagement in active play, facilitates later participation in sport and recreation, and is associated with healthier weight status and physical fitness (PF).1,2 In parallel, early PF and 24-h movement behaviors (MB) (physical activity [PA], sedentary behavior [SB], and sleep) are associated with cardiometabolic health and cognitive outcomes early in life,3 making the preschool period a key developmental window for prevention-focused pediatric research. Although PA promotion remains a cornerstone of public health strategies,4, 5, 6 sustained inactivity across the life course remains prevalent,7, 8, 9 underscoring the need to identify actionable determinants of healthy movement development from the earliest years.

Parents are central agents in young children's movement environments, shaping opportunities for active play through role modelling, co-participation, and daily routines. However, evidence linking parental factors to children's movement outcomes is inconsistent and often small in magnitude, with substantial heterogeneity across study designs, measurement methods, and parent–child dyads. Systematic reviews highlight that parental influences on children's PA vary by the type of parental influence examined and by child age, and that associations are frequently attenuated when objective measures are used.4, 5, 6,8,9

Importantly, movement-related outcomes in early childhood should not be treated as parallel endpoints without a developmental rationale. MC and PF are related but distinct constructs that reflect children's movement capabilities. While MC refers to the quality and mastery of the movement, expressed through locomotor and OC skills, PF represents the body's physiological capacity to support movement, such as cardiorespiratory endurance and muscular strength. Their associations with MB (e.g., moderate-to-vigorous PA [MVPA] and SB) may vary across developmental stages. Evidence syntheses on young children suggest that MC and PF are associated with multiple health indicators, yet the magnitude—and sometimes the direction—of these associations differ by age and context.10, 11, 12 Developmental models further propose a dynamic (potentially bidirectional) sequence linking MC, PA, PF, and weight status over time, implying that in preschoolers, parental correlates may relate more proximally to children's time-use behaviors than to children's competence or fitness, which are also shaped by maturation and learning opportunities.13,14

Despite the increasing interest in the family-level determinants of child health, key gaps remain. First, relatively few studies have concurrently assessed both parents and preschool children using device-based PA monitoring and then related these exposures to children's MC and PF measured using standardized field-based tests.7,15 Second, parental PF, which may better reflect broader health behaviors and household routines than MVPA alone, remains underexamined as a correlate of preschool children's movement profiles. Third, father–child dyads are frequently underrepresented, limiting inference on potentially meaningful differences between maternal and paternal pathways.7

Therefore, the objective of this cross-sectional dyadic study was to test whether objectively measured parental MVPA and PF are associated with preschool children's (1) MC (primary outcome) and (2) PF, anthropometry, and 24-h MB (secondary outcomes), examining mother–child and father–child dyads separately and evaluating effect modification by child sex. Based on developmental theory, we hypothesized that parental MVPA/PF would show stronger associations with children's MB than with children's MC or PF in the preschool years.

Methods

Participants and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study in public schools in Pamplona (Spain) and nearby municipalities. We recruited 161 preschool children (80 girls, 81 boys; mean age 5.13 ± 0.62 years), 90 mothers (41.1 ± 4.55 years), and 81 fathers (42.71 ± 4.95 years). Regarding demographic background, the majority of the sample was Caucasian (71%), while 29% were Hispanic. According to national classifications, the participating families were predominantly of medium-to-high socioeconomic status. All 161 children provided valid anthropometric, MC, PF and MB data. After excluding cases with incomplete parental data, 90 child–mother dyads (92 available; 2 excluded) and 81 child–father dyads (84 available; 3 excluded) were retained for analysis. Within the maternal set, all 90 mothers contributed to the MVPA models, whereas the PF models included 85 mothers because five had incomplete fitness assessments that precluded computation of the PF composite. Thirty children had data from both parents and therefore contributed to both the maternal and the paternal models.

Child inclusion criteria were enrolment in the 2nd or 3rd grade of preschool education and absence of a diagnosed health problem. Parents were eligible if their child participated and had no condition that precluded completion of PF testing. This work forms part of the Observatorio de Actividad Física en Escolares project (academic years 2022–2025) (https://observatorioactividadfisica.es). Written informed consent was obtained from schools and families prior to data collection.

Testing followed a station-based protocol. Each MOBAK-KG and PREFIT item was administered at a designated, clearly marked station by a trained evaluator who remained at that station throughout to minimize familiarity bias and enhance methodological consistency. Small groups of 3–5 children rotated through all stations. Tasks were explained and demonstrated; no practice trials were allowed. Total administration time was approximately 45 min.

Measurements

Children: Trained assessors followed the CDC–NHANES procedures.16 Height was measured to the nearest millimet using a SECA 213 stadiometer (Seca, Hamburg, Germany). Weight was measured barefoot in light clothing using a Tanita DC-430MAS scale (Tanita Corp., Tokyo, Japan; 100 g precision). BMI was calculated as kg/m2 and converted to age- and sex-specific z-scores using WHO reference data.17 WC was measured to the nearest millimeter at the umbilicus with a SECA 201 calibrated tape (Seca, Hamburg, Germany). MC was evaluated using the MOBAK-KG test battery, which covers OC (throwing, catching, bouncing, and dribbling) and SM (balancing, rolling, jumping, and running) domains. Each domain has a maximum score of eight points, resulting in a combined score ranging from 0 (lowest) to 16 (highest). Test procedures and scoring are described in full detail elsewhere.18 PF was evaluated using the PREFIT battery.19 Following manual guidelines, upper-body strength was measured across four alternating trials (right–left–right–left) using a handgrip (HG) strength dynamometer (TKK-5001 Grip-A, Takei, Tokyo, Japan) with a fixed 4.0 cm grip span - the validated optimal distance for preschoolers.20 Lower-body strength was assessed using the standing long jump (SLJ) from a standing start, with the best of three attempts recorded. Speed–agility was measured using the 4 × 10 m shuttle-run test, with the fastest of two trials recorded. CRF was determined using an adapted 20 m Course-Navette test with 0.5 km h−1 stage increments, recording the total number of completed laps.15 Each fitness variable was standardized into an age- and sex-specific z-score (z = (value – mean)/SD) based on reference values from the PREFIT manual.21 To align directionality before computing an overall fitness composite score (the average of the HG, SLJ, speed–agility, and CRF z-scores), the speed–agility z-score was multiplied by −1 so that higher values uniformly represent superior performance across all PF components.22,23 PA, SB, and sleep were assessed using triaxial GENEActiv accelerometers worn on the non-dominant wrist for six consecutive days (sampling frequency 87.5 Hz). Wearing the GENEActiv monitor on the wrist has demonstrated moderate-to-high validity for assessing PA in both preschoolers and adults, with wrist placement significantly improving participants’ compliance compared to waist-worn and thigh-worn protocols.24,25 According to Roscoe, these accelerometers showed an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.75 for LPA and MVPA, and 0.99 for SB, in preschool-aged children, compared with laboratory-based oxygen consumption values.24 Moreover, when comparing GENEActiv (wrist-worn) with activPAL (thigh-worn) in healthy ambulatory adults, the results showed a significant correlation between the devices, with only a slight underestimation of SB by the GENEActiv accelerometer (−3.44 min).25 Data were downloaded with GENEActiv software (v3.3) and processed in R using the GGIR package.26 Inclusion required ≥600 min of waking wear time and an average sleep duration ≥200 min across the six days. Intensity thresholds for preschoolers were SB < 56.3 mg; light PA 56.3 to 191.5 mg; moderate PA 191.6 to 695.7 mg; vigorous PA ≥ 695.8 mg.27 These cut-points are based on wrist-worn ENMO calibration thresholds27 and are widely used in studies using pediatric populations. Total PA was calculated as the sum of light, moderate, and vigorous PA.

Parents: Height and weight were measured using a SECA 799 electronic column scale. Whole-body composition (fat mass %, lean mass %, visceral fat volume, cm3) was determined using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA; Lunar DPX, GE, Madison, WI).28 Appendicular lean mass (ALM) was calculated as the sum of lean mass in the arms and legs and indexed to height (ALMI, kg·m−2).29 PF was assessed across multiple components. Upper-body strength was measured as maximal isometric grip strength using a digital dynamometer (TKK-5101, Takei Scientific Instruments, Tokyo, Japan) in four alternating trials (right–left–right–left), with the mean value used for analysis. Lower-body strength was assessed using a dynamic leg-extension test on a Smart Strength eGym machine (eGym GmbH, Munich, Germany) to determine the one-repetition maximum (1RM). CRF was evaluated through a symptom-limited incremental cardiopulmonary exercise test on an Excalibur cycle ergometer (Lode BV, Groningen, Netherlands), beginning at 25 W and increasing by 25 W every 3 min until participants could no longer maintain a cadence of 50–60 RPM or limiting symptoms appeared. Heart rate, Borg RPE, and respiratory gas exchange (V˙ O2, V˙ CO2, minute ventilation, and respiratory exchange ratio) were continuously monitored, with maximal oxygen uptake (V˙ O2peak) (mL·kg−1·min−1) serving as the primary CRF outcome. A composite parental PF z-score was calculated as the mean of standardized z-scores from HG, leg-extension strength, and V˙ O2peak. Parents wore the same wrist-worn GENEActiv devices on the same six days as their children. Adult intensity thresholds were SB < 45.8 mg; light PA 45.8 to 193.1 mg; moderate PA 193.2 to 418.2 mg; vigorous PA ≥ 418.3 mg.27 These cut-points are based on validated ENMO thresholds for wrist-worn accelerometry27 and are commonly used in adult studies with GENEActiv devices. Total PA was calculated as the sum of light, moderate, and vigorous PA. Because not all dyads included both parents, valid wear time was summarized separately for dyads with maternal versus paternal data. In dyads with maternal data, children provided M = 6.60 days (SD = 0.43), and mothers provided M = 6.58 days (SD = 0.54) with a child-mother dyad average of M = 6.59 (SD = 0.43). In dyads with paternal data, children provided M = 6.73 days (SD = 0.40), and fathers provided M = 6.65 days (SD = 0.67), with a child-father dyad average of M = 6.69 (SD = 0.46) of valid accelerometer wear data.

Ethical clearance

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before enrolment, all parents or legal guardians were informed of the purpose of the project and signed informed consent forms. The study protocol was approved by the Public University of Navarra Ethics Committee (approval number: PI-020/19).

Statistical analysis

Before the primary analyses, we explored two alternative operationalizations of the parental exposures. First, we examined composite parent–child categories that combined each child's total PA or PF with the corresponding parental value (e.g., “low child–low parent”, “low child–moderate parent”); however, several cells were too sparse for robust modelling, containing as few as n = 8 in the maternal analyses and n = 6 in the paternal analyses. Second, we classified participants according to compliance with World Health Organization 24-h movement recommendations, which revealed a marked imbalance (children: 82% met vs. 18% unmet; mothers: 20% vs. 80%; fathers: 39.5% vs. 60.5%). Because both approaches produced unstable or highly unbalanced groups, these observations motivated the tertile-based operationalization used in the primary analyses.

To maximize statistical power, we categorized parental MVPA and PF into tertiles (analyzed separately for mothers and fathers): low, moderate, and high MVPA; and low, moderate, and high PF (based on the PF composite z-score). The use of tertiles was selected to form clear and comparable groups that help identify associations and differences between groups, instead of analyzing MVPA and PF as continuous variables. This method allows researchers to detect potential non-linear or threshold effects that might not be visible when assuming a linear relationship between the continuous variable and the outcome. As shown in the sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S1), these alternative models did not significantly alter the primary findings.

Generalized linear models (GLMs) were fitted with dummy coding and adjustment for child age and sex. GLMs were chosen because they allow for the simultaneous modelling of variables with different distributions. All variables met parametric assumptions, as verified by visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Continuous outcomes were modelled using a Gaussian distribution with an identity link, reported as β and 95% CI, representing the expected change in the dependent variable for each unit increase in the predictor. Count outcomes were analyzed using a Poisson regression with a log link, reported as exp(β) and 95% CI of exp(β), indicating the multiplicative change in the dependent variable for each unit increase in the predictor. Analyses were performed using jamovi version 2.3.28, (The jamovi project, Sydney, Australia). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-sided). Because multiple outcomes were examined and no correction for multiplicity was applied, p-values are reported without adjustment, and the corresponding associations are interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Maternal and paternal exposures were modelled in separate sets of GLMs (maternal MVPA, n = 90; maternal PF, n = 85; paternal MVPA and PF, n = 81); the mother–child and father–child estimates were never pooled into a single model. Because 30 children had data from both parents, these children contributed to both the maternal and the paternal models; within any individual model, however, each child appeared only once, so the independence of observations required by the GLMs was preserved. As the maternal and paternal models share this subset of children, estimates from the two dyad types are reported and interpreted separately rather than combined.

To address the prespecified question of effect modification by child sex, the four primary OC models were re-fitted with a child-sex × parental-exposure interaction term (maternal MVPA, maternal PF, paternal MVPA, and paternal PF), retaining the Poisson regression with log link and the full covariate set. The Wald test of the interaction term was used to assess whether the parental association differed between boys and girls, and sex-stratified incidence-rate ratios were derived from the same models for interpretation. To contextualize the magnitude of the observed sex gap, adjusted boy-vs-girl incidence-rate ratios for OC were compared, on the natural-log scale, with the standardized mean differences reported in the most recent meta-analysis of fundamental motor skills in children aged 3 to 6 years. Because the dyadic sub-samples (n = 81–90) afford limited power to detect interactions, these analyses are reported as exploratory (Supplementary Table S2). Sensitivity analyses using continuous parental exposures and recommendation-based classifications yielded patterns of association consistent with the primary tertile-based models (Supplementary Table S1); additional model diagnostics are provided in the Supplementary materials.

Results

Child-mother dyad - MVPA

Maternal MVPA (Table 1) was not significantly associated with children's anthropometric outcomes (fat mass, BMI z-score, and WC; all p > 0.05). Child age was positively associated with fat mass (β = 0.43, 95% CI [0.09, 0.77]; p = 0.016). For MC outcomes, maternal MVPA was not associated with MOBAK OC, SM, or total MOBAK (all p > 0.05). In contrast, both age and sex predicted OC performance (Age: exp(β) = 1.68, 95% CI [1.38, 2.07]; p < 0.001. Sex [boys vs girls]: exp(β) = 1.68, 95% CI [1.14, 2.51]; p = 0.010), and age predicted SM (exp(β) = 1.60, 95% CI [1.35, 1.91]; p < 0.001) and total MOBAK (exp(β) = 1.64, 95% CI [1.43, 1.87]; p < 0.001). Maternal MVPA was not associated with children's PF (HG, speed–agility, SLJ, or CRF; all p > 0.05). Age was consistently associated with better PF (HG: β = 2.17, 95% CI [1.43, 2.91]; p < 0.001. Speed–agility: β = 0.24, 95% CI [0.14, 0.33]; p < 0.001. SLJ: β = 13.90, 95% CI [8.00, 19.79]; p < 0.001. CRF: β = 9.61, 95% CI [5.11, 14.1]; p < 0.001). Neither maternal MVPA nor child sex were significantly associated with children's PF outcomes. For MB outcomes, maternal MVPA was inversely associated with sleep duration (β = −0.59 h·night−1, 95% CI [−0.98, −0.20]; p = 0.004). Age was positively associated with TPA, LPA, and MVPA (all p ≤ 0.001) and negatively associated with sedentary time (β = −45.81 min·day−1, 95% CI [−69.6, −22.0]; p < 0.001), indicating that older children were less sedentary and more active. Child-mother dyad - PF

When maternal PF was considered (Table 2), higher maternal PF (moderate vs low) was associated with more favorable MB in children: greater sleep duration (β = 0.41 h·night−1, 95% CI [0.01, 0.81]; p = 0.050), higher TPA (β = 60.70 min·day−1, 95% CI [18.85, 102.58]; p = 0.006), higher LPA (β = 32.80 min·day−1, 95% CI [1.65, 64.02]; p = 0.042), and higher MVPA (β = 27.88 min·day−1, 95% CI [7.22, 48.55]; p = 0.010), alongside lower sedentary time (β = −52.70 min·day−1, 95% CI [−102.48, −2.86]; p = 0.042). Maternal PF was not associated with children's anthropometric, MC, or PF outcomes. Age remained positively associated with most variables (all p < 0.001), except for WC and sleep duration.

Sex differences in object-control skills and interactions with parental exposures

Boys outperformed girls in OC skills with a moderate-to-large effect (mean MOBAK OC score, boys vs. girls: descriptive Cohen's d ≈ 0.73-0.75; full descriptive statistics in Supplementary Table S2). After adjustment for child age and parental exposure, the boy-vs-girl incidence-rate ratio for OC was 1.61-1.72 across the four sex × parental-exposure interaction models (Supplementary Table S2; all p < 0.001). The natural-log of these ratios (≈0.48-0.54) lies within the range reported by the Zheng et al.30 meta-analysis for children of comparable age (pooled SMD ≈ 0.48-0.81 at ages 4–6), indicating that the sex gap observed in our sample is consistent with the broader literature rather than a sample-specific artefact. By contrast, no significant sex effect was detected for SM skills, total MOBAK score, or any individual PF item, confirming that the sex gap in this preschool sample is OC-specific.

In the four exploratory sex × parental-exposure interaction models for OC, none of the interaction terms reached statistical significance (maternal MVPA × sex, p = 0.681; maternal PF × sex, p = 0.665; paternal MVPA × sex, p = 0.416; paternal PF × sex, p = 0.485; full output in Supplementary Table S2). Sex-stratified estimates were directionally consistent across boys and girls, with overlapping confidence intervals. These analyses do not support the existence of a differential parental influence on OC skills by child sex in this preschool age range, although the limited size of the dyadic sub-samples constrains the power to detect interactions of small-to-moderate magnitude.

Discussion

This study examined the associations between parental MVPA and PF and preschool children's anthropometry, MC, PF, and 24-h MB. Interpreted through the Stodden developmental model, our findings suggest that the coupling between movement capability (MC/PF) and MB (e.g., MVPA, sedentary time, sleep) is still emerging in early childhood. This supports the premise that relationships among competence, fitness, and weight status are developmentally contingent rather than uniform across ages.13,14 In this context, it is notable that parental MVPA and PF were not significantly associated with children's anthropometry, MC, or PF in the present preschool sample. The clearest parent-related signals were instead observed for children's time-use behaviors, with higher maternal PF associated with a more favorable MB profile and greater paternal MVPA associated with higher child total PA and light PA. This pattern supports that parental attributes act most proximally on children's daily routines and movement opportunities (i.e., the behavioral “input” layer). Stronger downstream links to competence, fitness, and adiposity likely manifest later, as children gain autonomy and activity becomes increasingly skill- and fitness-dependent.13,14 Within this developmental frame, child sex emerged as a domain-specific rather than a global differentiator. Boys outperformed girls in OC skills, matching meta-analytic benchmarks for this age range. Conversely, no comparable sex effects emerged for SM skills or PF. Furthermore, all sex × parental-exposure interactions for OC were not significant.

Child factors as primary determinants

Across models, child age and sex accounted for the largest proportion of variance, underscoring that preschool differences in MC, PF, and MB are strongly shaped by maturation, practice exposure, and early learning contexts. Older children demonstrated higher MC and PF and accumulated more MVPA, consistent with expected developmental trajectories in the preschool years.15,31 Sex differences were also evident, with boys outperforming girls in OC skills and several fitness components, mirroring established patterns in this age group.32,33 In our adjusted primary models (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3, Table 4), the boy-vs-girl OC incidence-rate ratio ranged from 1.34 to 1.68, equivalent on the standardized scale to the pooled effect of approximately 0.48 reported by Zheng et al.30 across 38 studies of 3- to 6-year-olds, and the absence of any equivalent sex difference for self-movement skills or for individual fitness items argues that the gap is skill-specific rather than reflective of a broader motor-capability divergence. These age- and sex-related differences likely reflect developmental gains in neuromotor control, attentional capacity, and task familiarity that influence both competence and performance in early childhood.34 In addition, early gendered opportunities (e.g., differential exposure to ball skills) may contribute to observed sex differences, suggesting schools and caregivers can play an important role by providing intentionally inclusive, skill-rich movement experiences.35 Viewed through a developmental lens, the predominance of child age and sex over parental MVPA/PF in explaining MC and PF does not imply that parents are irrelevant; rather, it suggests that in preschoolers the parent–child linkage may operate more through contextual facilitation and routine structuring than through parents’ own MVPA volume or fitness per se—consistent with prior reviews reporting weak or inconsistent parent–child PA associations in early childhood.8,9

Limited influence of parental MVPA and PF

Neither maternal nor paternal MVPA or PF was associated with children's capability-related outcomes (MC and PF) or anthropometry (e.g., body fat, BMI z-score, WC), whereas the most consistent parent-related signals were observed for behavioral time-use outcomes (children's TPA/LPA and the overall 24-h MB profile). This capability–behavior dissociation is compatible with developmental models positing that, in the preschool years, links between MB and movement capability are still consolidating and may be more strongly shaped by maturation and learning contexts than by parental MVPA or fitness alone.13,14 Our findings also align with syntheses reporting weak or inconsistent parent–child PA associations in early childhood, particularly when PA is measured objectively.8,9 Importantly, parental MVPA volume may be an imprecise proxy for the proximal family processes that structure preschoolers' movement opportunities (e.g., co-participation, transportation to activities, access to outdoor play, and limits on recreational screen time). In preschoolers, whose days are often substantially organised by childcare settings, variations in MC and PF may reflect exposure to skill-rich practice and instruction rather than parental fitness per se. The null anthropometry findings are also plausible in this age group because adiposity-related differences may require longer cumulative exposure to family behaviors, may show restricted variability in predominantly medium-to-high socioeconomic contexts with access to safe play and structured routines, and can be influenced by multiple unmeasured determinants (e.g., diet, sleep regularity, and childcare policies) that dilute observable parent MVPA/PF correlations at this developmental stage.

Contextual and socio-ecological considerations

From a socio-ecological perspective, the small and selective parent–child associations observed in this study may reflect the fact that preschool children's movement development is shaped by multiple, interacting contexts beyond parental MVPA and PF. In our sample, the predominance of medium-to-high socioeconomic status and likely access to safe play spaces and structured preschool provisions may have constrained variability in both exposures (parental MVPA/PF) and outcomes (child anthropometry and capability measures), thereby attenuating detectable associations. In addition, daily routines for preschoolers are often strongly influenced by childcare and school environments, such as time allocated for outdoor play, staff engagement, availability of equipment, opportunities for skill-rich practice, and classroom norms regarding sedentary time, which may exert a stronger proximal influence on children's 24-h MB and motor-skill learning than parental behaviours measured outside those settings.

These findings support an emphasis on setting-level and systems-level levers to promote early movement development. Interventions may have greater impact when they target modifiable environments that reach all children, including high-quality, developmentally appropriate physical education, structured and unstructured active play opportunities, equipment and space that afford locomotor and OC practice, active breaks that interrupt prolonged sitting, and policies that protect time for daily outdoor play.36,37 At the same time, family-focused strategies remain relevant when they address proximal practices, for example, establishing consistent routines, facilitating outdoor play, encouraging co-play, and limiting recreational screen time, rather than assuming that increasing parents' MVPA or fitness alone will translate into improvements in children's MC or PF. Finally, replication in more socioeconomically diverse samples and in settings with varying preschool provision will be important for determining whether these patterns persist across contexts and for identifying the ecological conditions under which stronger parent–child linkages emerge.

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of this study was the use of high-quality, validated, and objective measures across all key assessment areas. These include the MOBAK test for MC, the PREFIT battery for PF, device-based accelerometry for MB, and laboratory-based assessments of parental PF (including cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness metrics). This rigorous methodology offers a unique and robust approach compared with previous studies, which often relied on less reliable or self-reported data. Additionally, the use of blind evaluators who were unaware of the children's identities across testing stations minimised familiarity bias and improved internal validity. Finally, integrating both maternal and paternal data within a single analytical framework provides a comprehensive understanding of the influences of early familial movement, an aspect seldom explored in preschool populations.

However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design, although a necessary first step in understanding parental influence on children's health behaviours, precludes causal inference and limits the ability to determine the directionality of parent–child associations.38 In addition, because 30 children had data from both parents and thus contributed to both the maternal and the paternal models, the two sets of estimates are not statistically independent; we therefore interpreted the maternal and paternal models separately and did not perform formal maternal-versus-paternal comparisons. The relatively homogeneous SES of participating families may restrict generalizability to more socioeconomically diverse or disadvantaged populations. Although public schools should represent a varied sample, families that choose to participate tend to have higher health literacy, resources, and higher SES status.39,40 Although wrist-worn accelerometers have been validated and are widely accepted as reliable tools for assessing PA, wrist placement and age-specific cut-points can lead to misclassification of some movement intensities in preschoolers. Thus, the non-significant results between parents and children might have been attenuated by the lower sensitivity of the wrist-worn devices.41,42 Future studies using compositional data analysis may better capture the 24-h activity spectrum. Uneven compliance with WHO PA recommendations—high among children but lower among parents—could also have reduced between-subject variability and attenuated associations.43 Although we tested sex × parental-exposure interactions for the OC outcome, the dyadic sub-samples (n = 81–90) provided limited power to detect interactions of small-to-moderate magnitude; the absence of significant interaction effects should therefore be interpreted as a failure to reject the null rather than as positive evidence of homogeneity, and warrants confirmation in larger, more diverse cohorts. Lastly, potential residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as childcare attendance, parental screen-time policies, and neighbourhood built environment, along with the risk of type I error from multiple comparisons, should be considered when interpreting these findings.

Practical implications and future research

From a practice and policy perspective, our findings suggest that, during the preschool years, the most efficient levers to improve children's movement development are likely setting- and child-centered strategies implemented in preschools and community environments, rather than programs that rely primarily on changing parents' MVPA or fitness. Given developmental models in which the coupling between movement behavior and movement capability is still consolidating in early childhood,13,14 priorities should include daily opportunities for skill-rich active play, intentional instruction and practice of locomotor and OC skills, and repeated exposure to a range of movement intensities across the day. Particular attention should be paid to OC skill opportunities for girls. In our sample, the adjusted boy-vs-girl OC incidence-rate ratio was approximately 1.66 (range 1.34–1.68 across models), a magnitude consistent with the pooled meta-analytic SMD of 0.48 reported by Zheng et al.30 across 38 studies of 3- to 6-year-olds, and aligning with evidence that the OC gap widens with age in early childhood (SMD: 0.27 at age 3 → 0.81 at age 6).30 Three observations argue that this gap reflects modifiable exposure rather than fixed capability. First, no equivalent sex difference was detected for self-movement skills or for any PF item in our models, suggesting a skill-specific rather than a general motor-capability divergence. Second, our sex × parental-exposure interaction models for OC were uniformly non-significant, indicating that the observed gap is unlikely to be driven by sex-differentiated parental MVPA or fitness and is therefore more plausibly shaped by setting-level practice opportunities. Third, structured fundamental-movement-skill interventions delivered in childcare and kindergarten produce large effects on OC (pooled SMD = 1.36 in Wick et al.'s meta-analysis10), substantially exceeding the magnitude of the observed sex gap and demonstrating empirically that the gap is responsive to environmental modification. Recent evidence further suggests that the quality of the play context — not its quantity — moderates equity: boys, but not girls, accrue OC benefit from unstructured outdoor play, implying that girls require deliberately structured, instructor-supported practice to access comparable skill gains.44 Equitable access to equipment, intentional inclusion in ball-skill activities, and gender-balanced teacher prompting therefore constitute concrete, evidence-based levers for narrowing the OC gap in the preschool years.32,33,35

Preschools can translate these findings into pragmatic action using a small set of scalable steps. First, embed brief, structured “skill snacks” (≈10–15 min·day−1) within routine classroom schedules to practice fundamental movement skills with progression (e.g., throwing/catching, jumping/landing, hopping, balance). Second, optimize the play environment by ensuring varied and developmentally appropriate equipment (e.g., different-sized/weighted balls, targets, low obstacles, balance elements) that increases affordances for both locomotor and OC practice and encourages self-directed skill exploration. Third, reduce prolonged sitting by implementing short active breaks (≈2–5 min) at regular intervals (e.g., hourly), which simultaneously decreases sedentary time and provides repeated movement exposures. Fourth, strengthen home–school continuity with simple take-home activity prompts (e.g., toss-and-catch, hopscotch, mini–obstacle courses) that emphasize co-play and enjoyment, supported by brief instruction cards that guide parents on how to adapt and progress activities.10, 11, 12 Collectively, these strategies target the contexts most likely to shape preschoolers’ daily movement experiences and provide equitable opportunities for skill acquisition.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs to determine whether parent MVPA and PF become more strongly associated with children's MC, PF, and adiposity as children gain autonomy and activity becomes increasingly skill-dependent later in childhood.13,14 Studies using time-synchronized dyadic accelerometry (or proximity-aware sensors) could quantify co-activity patterns and clarify whether associations reflect shared time and settings rather than independent parent or child behaviors. Analytically, compositional data approaches and isotemporal substitution models could better estimate how reallocating time among sleep, sedentary behavior, LPA, and MVPA relates to downstream capability outcomes. Finally, intervention research should move beyond parental MVPA prescriptions alone and test family components that explicitly target facilitation mechanisms (e.g., routines, access, co-play, transport, and screen-time rules), ideally embedded within preschool-based programs to maximize reach and reduce socioeconomic inequities in movement opportunities.

Conclusion

In this preschool sample, child age and sex accounted for the greatest variability across anthropometric, MC, PF, and 24-h MB metrics, underscoring the dominance of early developmental and biological influences. Conversely, parental MVPA and PF were not materially associated with children's movement capabilities or anthropometry. The modest associations that emerged were primarily behavioral and exploratory: higher maternal PF was linked to more favorable child 24-h MB profiles, while higher paternal MVPA was linked to increased child PA. Since these exploratory findings suggest that parental physical traits are not direct drivers of early child capability, intervention strategies may be most efficient when focusing on child-centered, setting-level initiatives within schools and communities. Future longitudinal research is needed to determine whether family-level influences strengthen as children gain autonomy, and whether engaging parents through environmental facilitation rather than structural restrictions yields greater behavioral alignment.
