A parallel investigation of trauma exposure, maladaptive appraisals and posttraumatic stress reactions in two groups of trauma-exposed adolescents
Authors: Andrea Undset, Tine K Jensen, Grete Dyb, Tore Wentzel-Larsen, Silje M Ormhaug, Synne Stensland, Ines Blix
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Maladaptive appraisals, such as thoughts about experiencing a permanent and disturbing change and about being a fragile person in a scary world, are associated with posttraumatic stress reactions (PTSR) for trauma-exposed children and adolescents. Less is known about what puts young people at risk for developing such appraisals, and the differential relationship between the types of appraisals and PTSR.Objective: The primary aim was to examine the role of age, gender and exposure to potentially traumatizing events (PTEs) for the levels of maladaptive appraisals. The secondary aim was to investigate the association between the levels of maladaptive appraisals (appraisals of a permanent change and appraisals about a scary world) and PTSR.Methods: We investigated these aims in two samples: survivors after the terror attack at Utøya island in Norway in 2011 (N = 165, mean age 17.65, SD = 1.19) and adolescents referred to treatment after mainly interpersonal trauma (N = 152, mean age 15.08, SD = 2.18). The aims were investigated using linear regression and partial correlation.Results: In the terror-exposed sample, female gender was significantly associated with higher levels of scary-world appraisals, witnessing or learning that someone close were exposed to physical violence was significantly associated with more permanent-change appraisals, and a higher number of PTEs was significantly associated with higher levels of both types of appraisals. For the clinical sample, we found no significant associations between exposure to PTEs, gender, age, and the level of maladaptive appraisals. Both types of maladaptive appraisals were highly associated with PTSR in both samples, and there was no significant difference in the strength of the associations between the types of appraisals and PTSR.Conclusions: The results have implications for identifying adolescents at risk for developing harmful maladaptive appraisals. Both scary-world appraisals and permanent-change appraisals were strongly associated with PTSR in both groups of trauma-exposed adolescents.KEYWORDS: Maladaptive appraisals, posttraumatic stress, adolescents, trauma, child post traumatic cognitions inventory

Introduction

Many children and adolescents are exposed to a potentially traumatizing event (PTE) (McLaughlin et al., 2013; Stensland et al., 2013). About 16% of trauma-exposed children and adolescents develop posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Alisic et al., 2014). To explain why some, but not all, develop persistent reactions after trauma, cognitive theories of PTSD suggest that psychological adjustment is affected by the way the individuals interpret and give meaning to the potentially traumatic event and their reactions to the trauma (Dalgleish, 2004; Ehlers & Clark, 2000).

According to Ehlers and Clark’s cognitive model of PTSD (2000), maladaptive appraisals such as ‘I will never get over this’ play a key role in the development and maintenance of posttraumatic stress reactions (PTSR). In line with their model, there is now a considerable number of studies supporting that maladaptive appraisals and PTSR after trauma are highly associated in both child and adult populations (Gómez de la Cuesta et al., 2019; Mitchell et al., 2017). Moreover, longitudinal studies indicate that initial maladaptive appraisals predict subsequent PTSR in children and adolescents exposed to different types of PTEs, such as single-event trauma (Hiller et al., 2019; Meiser-Stedman et al., 2019), war trauma (Palosaari et al., 2013), and young people in out-of-home care exposed to maltreatment (Hiller et al., 2021). Thus, identifying the factors associated with maladaptive appraisals could contribute to understanding the pathways to persistent trauma reactions.

Only a handful of studies have investigated factors associated with maladaptive appraisals in trauma-exposed children and adolescents, and of these, some have found that the type of PTE may play a role. In one study of trauma-exposed children, Meiser-Stedman and colleagues (2009b) found that children who had experienced or witnessed an assault reported more maladaptive appraisals compared to children who had experienced motor vehicle accidents. Interpersonal PTEs were also associated with more maladaptive appraisals in the study conducted by Ponnamperuma and Nicolson (2016). They investigated associations between PTEs and maladaptive appraisals in a trauma-exposed Sri Lankan adolescent sample, where half the participants lived in areas highly impacted by the 2004 tsunami. Those who reported that their worst experience was sexual or physical abuse or witnessing domestic violence, had more maladaptive appraisals than those who identified a non-interpersonal PTE as their worst experience (Ponnamperuma & Nicolson, 2016).

Exposure to multiple types of PTEs may also be associated with the level of maladaptive appraisals. De Haan and colleagues (2017) reported that maltreatment-exposed children who experienced five or more types of maltreatment such as domestic violence, emotional abuse, neglect, and physical and sexual abuse, tended to exhibit higher levels of maladaptive appraisals than those who reported exposure to fewer types of maltreatment. Similarly, Ponnamperuma and Nicolson (2016) found that adolescents who had experienced multiple types of PTEs reported higher levels of negative appraisals than those who had been exposed to a single event.

Child characteristics such as gender could also impact the level of maladaptive appraisals after trauma. Some studies have reported higher levels of maladaptive appraisals among girls (de Haan et al., 2017; Diehle et al., 2015; Meiser-Stedman et al., 2009b), while others found no such associations (Ponnamperuma & Nicolson, 2016). In most studies with children and adolescents, the level of reported maladaptive appraisals does not seem to differ between age groups (Diehle et al., 2015; Meiser-Stedman et al., 2009b; Ponnamperuma & Nicolson, 2016). In summary, interpersonal PTEs, exposure to more types of PTEs and female gender have been associated with higher levels of maladaptive appraisals.

Studies have shown that trauma-related maladaptive appraisals can be divided into sub-categories. For adults, most studies have investigated maladaptive appraisals about the self, the world, and self-blame (Foa et al., 1999; Gómez de la Cuesta et al., 2019). For children and adolescents, Meiser-Stedman and colleagues (2009b) found that maladaptive appraisals can be grouped into thoughts about a ‘permanent and disturbing change’ (permanent-change appraisals) and thoughts about being a ‘fragile person in a scary world’ (scary-world appraisals). Permanent-change appraisals and scary-world appraisals are often highly correlated (Diehle et al., 2015; Lobo et al., 2015; Pfeiffer et al., 2017), yet, only a few studies have investigated these two types of appraisals separately. One exception is a study by Meiser-Stedman and colleagues (2009b), who found that girls reported more scary-world appraisals. Thus, there is limited knowledge about possible differential factors associated with elevated levels of permanent-change appraisals and scary-world appraisals.

Certain types of appraisals may also have a stronger association with PTSR than other types of appraisals, however, studies investigating the relative impact of permanent-change appraisals and scary-world appraisals for PTSR in children and adolescents are scarce. In Gómez de la Cuesta and colleagues’ meta-analysis (2019), there was no significant difference in the strength of the association between permanent-change appraisals and PTSR, and between scary-world appraisals and PTSR. However, Meiser-Stedman and colleagues found evidence that permanent-change appraisals, but not scary-world appraisals, mediated the relationship between 2–4 week and 6-month symptoms of PTSD for children and adolescents admitted to an emergency department following a motor vehicle accident or assault (Meiser-Stedman et al., 2009a).

It is possible that factors associated with maladaptive appraisals and what type of appraisals are associated with distress may differ between trauma-exposed groups. Interestingly, De Haan and colleagues (2019) found indication of a mediating role of maladaptive appraisals between risk factors and symptom levels of PTSD for children exposed to accidental trauma, but not for children exposed to interpersonal trauma. Investigating such relationships in different populations can be complex, as it is challenging to distinguish between the effect of variables such as trauma exposure, gender and age from other differences between the samples. Despite these limitations, taking differences between trauma-exposed populations into account may enhance our understanding of how to prevent persistent reactions.

In the present study, we aim to investigate whether the types and numbers of PTEs, as well as gender and age, are associated with maladaptive appraisals in trauma-exposed adolescents. Additionally, we aim to investigate the associations between permanent-change appraisals, scary-world appraisals and PTSR. This study contributes to the current knowledge by investigating these aims in two trauma-exposed groups: survivors after the terrorist attack at Utøya Island in Norway (terror-exposed sample), and adolescents mainly exposed to multiple interpersonal trauma, who had developed clinically significant symptoms of PTSD and were referred to treatment (clinical sample). Based on previous research, we hypothesize that exposure to an interpersonal PTE, multiple types of PTEs and being female are associated with higher levels of maladaptive appraisals. We also hypothesize that permanent-change appraisals and scary-world appraisals are associated with PTSR. We did not formulate any specific hypothesis regarding differences in the strength of the associations between the type of maladaptive appraisals and PTSR across the two samples.

Methods

Procedure

Previous papers provide a detailed description of the data collection for the Utøya study (Dyb et al., 2014; Glad et al., 2021; Stene & Dyb, 2016) and the Norwegian RCT study on TF-CBT (Jensen et al., 2014). The two studies were both approved by the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics (Utøya study; document 551224, 2011/1625, 2014/246, TF-CBT study; 2009/11493 S-07442). Participants who were between the ages of 10 and 19 at test point were included in the present study. The aims, hypotheses, and analytic plan were pre-registered at Open Science Framework (OSF) (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/B3HS5).

Participants

Terror-exposed sample. On 22 July 2011, a terrorist attacked the Labour Party’s youth organization’s summer camp at Utøya Island, Norway. The shooting attack lasted for approximately one hour and twenty minutes, and 69 of the 564 mostly young people on the island were killed. Everyone on the small island was exposed to severe trauma, and most survivors lost someone close to them in the attack (Dyb et al., 2014).

The survivors of the terror attack and their parents were invited to participate in an open cohort-study, across four study waves between 2011 and 2020 (Glad et al., 2021). The inclusion criteria in the open-cohort study were exposure to the terror attack, thus, the cohort includes survivors with varying levels of posttraumatic stress reactions. Data was collected through semi-structured interviews, conducted by trained interviewers, mainly health care workers. Informed consent was given by the participants prior to the interview, and for participants younger than 16 years, consent was given by their parents.

In the present study, we included data from the second round of interviews (T2), 14–15 months after the terror attack, as maladaptive appraisals and PTEs were not measured at T1. All survivors who were 13 years or older, and currently lived in Norway, were invited to participate in the Utøya study at T2. For the present study, 165 survivors (mean age 17.7 (range 14–19), 51.5% female) who were present either at the island (n = 163) or on the landside (n = 2) during the attack, were included.

Clinical sample. The participants in the Norwegian RCT study of TF-CBT were between the ages of 10 and 18. They were referred to the outpatient child and adolescent mental health services by their primary physician or Child Welfare Services between April 2008 and February 2011. The inclusion criteria for the TF-CBT study were exposure to trauma and having developed clinically significant symptom levels. All participants had experienced at least one traumatic event and had at least one symptom in each of the three PTSD symptom criteria (reexperiencing, avoidance and hyperarousal) according to DSM-IV (APA, 1994). Additionally, only patients with a score of 15 or above on the Child PTSD Symptom Scale were eligible for participation. This is above the recommended cutoff of 11 established for this measure by Foa and colleagues (2001), for discriminating between children with high and low levels of PTSR. Children and adolescents with acute psychosis, suicidal behaviour or a need for an interpreter were excluded from the study. Informed consent was given by both the adolescents and their parents, and the data collection was conducted by a clinician (Jensen et al., 2014).

For the present study, 152 adolescents (mean age 15.1 (range 10–18), 80.3% female) were included. The data included for the present study were baseline data prior to treatment start.

Measures

Maladaptive appraisals. In both studies, the Child Post-Traumatic Cognitions Inventory (CPTCI; Meiser-Stedman et al., 2009b) was used to measure maladaptive appraisals. CPTCI is a 25-item scale, where responses are given on a 4-point scale with the following responses: 1 (don’t agree at all), 2 (don’t agree a bit), 3 (agree a bit), or 4 (agree a lot). The scale consists of two subscales, ‘Permanent and Disturbing Change’ (CPTCI-PC) with 13 items, and ‘Fragile Person in a Scary World’ (CPTCI-SW) with 12 items. The sum scores range from 25–100 (CPTCI-total), 13–52 (CPTCI-PC) and 12–48 (CPTCI-SW). In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha was high for both the terror-exposed sample (CPTCI-total α = .93, CPTCI-PC α = .90, CPTCI-SW α = .85) and the clinical sample (CPTCI-total α = .92, CPTCI-PC α = .88, CPTCI-SW α = .85).

Posttraumatic stress disorder. For the terror-exposed sample, symptoms of PTSD over the past month were measured using the University of California at Los Angeles PTSD Reaction Index (UCLA PTSD-RI; Steinberg et al., 2004) . The UCLA PTSD-RI is a 20-item scale with responses recorded on a 5-point scale. Three items have two alternative formulations, and the highest score of the two formulations is used to calculate the total score. The 17 items that make up the total score correspond to the DSM-IV (APA, 1994) criteria for PTSD. Responses are rated 0 (never), 1 (two times a month), 2 (1–2 times a week), 3 (2–3 times each week), and 4 (almost every day). The sum score ranges from 0–68 and demonstrated good internal reliability in the present study (α = .89).

For the clinical sample, symptoms of PTSD over the past two weeks were measured using the Child PTSD Symptom Scale (CPSS; Foa et al., 2001). The scale consists of 17 items recorded on a 4-point scale, measuring the 17 DSM-IV (APA, 1994) symptoms for PTSD. Responses are 0 (not at all), 1 (once a week or less), 2 (2–4 times a week), and 3 (5 or more times a week). The sum score ranges from 0–51 and internal reliability was relatively high in this study (α = .75).

Potentially traumatizing experience (PTE). For the terror-exposed sample, PTEs were measured by twelve questions, based on Costello and colleagues (2002), asking if the participant had ever experienced any of the mentioned events, such as ‘sudden (unexpected) death of someone close’ or ‘been exposed to violence (beaten up or hurt)’. Responses were ‘no, never’, ‘yes, before 22 July 2011’ and ‘yes, after 22 July 2011’, thus, the questionnaire specifically asked for experiences that were not related to the terrorist attack. The responses indicating whether the event had happened before or after the terrorist attack were collapsed, so those responses used in the analysis only included an indication of lifetime exposure to a particular event.

For the clinical sample, PTEs were measured by an adapted version of the Traumatic Events Screening Inventory for Children (TESI-C) (Jensen et al., 2014; Ribbe, 1996). It consists of twelve questions, assessing lifetime exposure to any of the mentioned types of events, such as ‘have you ever seen someone fight or attack someone else? With a weapon or knife?’ with responses ‘yes’ and ‘no’. Note that it is the total exposure to PTEs that is included in this measure, not the index trauma identified for the treatment the participants received.

For the present study, the items measuring PTEs in the two studies were compared, and those types of events only included in one of the studies were excluded. Exposure to terrorism was not measured for the clinical sample, thus, exposure to terrorism is not included in the analyses. The trauma categories ‘disaster’ and ‘severe accidents’ were collapsed into one group due to only 15 participants reporting to have experienced a disaster. Finally, six categories of PTEs were included: (1) severe illness / death of someone close, (2) disaster / severe accident, (3) physical violence / threats of physical violence to oneself, (4) someone close exposed to physical violence, (5) sexual assault, and (6) frightening / painful medical procedures at the hospital. The responses were coded 1 (yes, for having experienced such an event), and 0 (never experienced such an event). Due to lack of coherence in measures among the two samples, the data on the following PTEs were not included in the analyses and in the sum score of the number of experienced PTEs for the clinical sample: witness violence (experienced by anyone, also strangers), kidnapping and a general category for other PTEs. For the terror-exposed sample, bullying, neglect, painful separation from family or partner and a general category for other PTEs were not included.

A variable for exposure to interpersonal trauma was included for a comparison of the samples, and interpersonal trauma (yes = 1, no = 0) was defined as having been exposed to one or more types of potentially traumatic interpersonal events, including physical violence to oneself or a close person, or sexual assault. Finally, a sum score was computed for the number of PTEs, including exposure to severe illness / sudden death, disaster, accident, physical violence (self-experienced), physical violence (family / close person), sexual assault, and hospitalization.

Statistical analyses

There were 325 participants between 10 and 19 years who participated in the two studies. Two participants from the clinical sample were excluded due to having experienced none of the trauma types included in this study. In addition, two participants from the clinical sample and four participants from the terror-exposed sample were excluded due to missing data for more than half of the items on the main outcome variable, maladaptive appraisals. Finally, 165 participants from the terror-exposed sample and 152 participants from the clinical sample were included in the analyses Figure 1.

Flowchart for the two samples.

The sum scores for CPSS, UCLA PTSD-RI, CPTCI-Total, CPTCI-PC, and CPTCI-SW were computed using the half-rule approach described by Fairclough (2010, p. 50). For participants who had responded to at least half the items on a scale, the mean of the valid responses was calculated and then multiplied by the number of items for that scale. Thus, the sum scores will fall within the correct range for the scale, also for participants who have skipped one or more items. There were 0.5% missing items for maladaptive appraisals and PTSR in the terror-exposed sample, 0.003% missing in the clinical sample, and no participant had more than two items missing for any of these scales.

For comparisons of the means, the original scales for PTSD (CPSS and UCLA PTSD-RI) were transformed to scores ranging from 0–100. Independent samples t-tests (two-tailed) were performed to examine the mean of CPTCI-Total, CPTCI-PC, CPTCI-SW, PTSD, the mean number of trauma types, and the mean age of the two samples. Differences between the two samples in frequencies of gender, exposure to interpersonal trauma, and the six categories of PTEs were examined using the chi-square test for independence.

First, we examined factors associated with elevated levels of maladaptive appraisals. Within each sample, we ran six multiple linear regression models: we examined two models of predictor variables for each of the three outcome variables (CPTCI-total, CPTCI-PC and CPTCI-SW). In the first model, age, gender, and the six types of PTEs were included as predictor variables. In the second model, age, gender and the number of trauma types were included as predictor variables. For interpretability of the regression coefficients, the sum scores for CPTCI-total, CPTCI-PC and CPTCI-SW were transformed to scales ranging from 0–100.

Second, we investigated the relationship between the types of appraisals and PTSR. Partial correlations between CPTCI-total, CPTCI-PC, CPTCI-SW and PTSR were investigated within each sample, controlling for age and gender. In addition to the planned analyses, we calculated the 95% and 99% confidence intervals (Bias corrected and accelerated: BCa) for the partial correlations. To investigate whether there were differences in the strength of the relationships, we also calculated the 95% and 99% confidence intervals for the difference between all correlation coefficients, both within and between the samples. To calculate the confidence intervals, we used 10,000 bootstrap samples utilizing the R-package boot (Canty & Ripley, 2024; Davison & Hinkley, 1997). Finally, we utilized the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) which controls for the false discovery rate in the regression analyses, and thus provides p-values adjusted for multiple testing.

As sensitivity analyses, we investigated the association between each of the six types of PTEs and maladaptive appraisals separately, only adjusted for age and gender. We ran three separate linear regression analyses for each of the six types of PTEs within each sample, with the outcome variables CPTCI-total, CPTCI-PC, and CPTCI-SW. The results of the sensitivity analyses are reported in the supplementary material.

Of the variables that were included in the regression models, there was 3.7% missing data for the terror-exposed sample and 3.3% for the clinical sample. Missing values were handled using multiple imputation in the R-package mice (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011) with 200 imputations. All analyses were performed in IBM SPSS 25 and R (R Core Team, 2022).

Regression analyses

The regression models for the terror-exposed sample, with maladaptive appraisals (CPTCI-total, CPTCI-PC, CPTCI-SW) as the outcome variables, are presented in Table 3. Witnessing or learning that a close person was exposed to physical violence was significantly associated with a higher total score of CPTCI and higher scores on CPTCI-PC. Self-experienced or threatened physical violence was significantly associated with higher scores on the CPTCI-PC subscale. The number of PTEs the adolescents have been exposed to was significantly associated with higher scores on both the total and the subscales of CPTCI. Female gender was in both models significantly associated with higher total scores on CPTCI and the CPTCI-SW subscale. There was no significant association between age and maladaptive appraisals in any of the models.

After applying the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) for adjusting for multiple testing, none of included predictor variables in model 1 were significantly associated with CPTCI-total. The results of the sensitivity analyses are presented in the supplementary material. In the terror-exposed sample, having experienced severe illness or the sudden death of someone close was significantly associated with a higher total score of CPTCI, and the CPTCI-PC subscale, when the other types of PTEs were not adjusted for. Both self-experienced or threatened violence and witnessing or learning that someone close was exposed to violence, were significantly associated with higher scores on CPTCI-total, CPTCI-PC, and CPTCI-SW when the other types of PTEs were not adjusted for.

Correlations between maladaptive appraisals and posttraumatic stress reactions

The partial correlation analyses with 95% and 99% confidence intervals are presented in Table 5. The subscales of CPTCI were highly correlated in both the terror-exposed sample (.81) and the clinical sample (.74). Both subscales correlated highly with the total scale, for CPTCI-PC, the correlation was .96 (p < .001) for the terror-exposed sample and .94 (p < .001) for the clinical sample, for CPTCI-SW, the correlations were .95 (p < .001) for the terror-exposed sample and .92 (p < .001) for the clinical sample. Furthermore, the correlations between CPTCI-total, CPTCI-PC, CPTCI-SW and PTSR were significant and high in both samples. The confidence intervals for the difference between the correlations indicated that there was no significant difference in the strength of the association between permanent-change appraisals and PTSR, and scary-world appraisals and PTSR in any of the samples. However, there was evidence for a higher correlation in the terror-exposed sample for both permanent-change appraisals and PTSR and scary-world appraisals and PTSR than in the clinical sample.

Discussion

In this study, we examined factors associated with elevated maladaptive appraisals, and the relationships between permanent-change appraisals, scary-world appraisals and PTSR. We partly found support for our first hypothesis in the terror-exposed sample: that female gender, interpersonal PTEs (physical violence, but not sexual assault) and multiple types of PTEs are associated with higher levels of maladaptive appraisals. There were no significant associations between any of the predictor variables and maladaptive appraisals in the clinical sample. As predicted by our second hypothesis, both permanent-change appraisals and scary-world appraisals were highly associated with PTSR in both samples.

Interpersonal trauma was defined as exposure to physical violence or sexual assault. In line with previous studies which have found an association between interpersonal trauma and elevated maladaptive appraisals (Meiser-Stedman et al., 2009b; Ponnamperuma & Nicolson, 2016; Vasileva et al., 2022), physical violence was associated with elevated levels of maladaptive appraisals in the terror-exposed group. We found no significant association between exposure to sexual assaults and maladaptive appraisals, which is at odds with previous findings (Ponnamperuma & Nicolson, 2016; Vasileva et al., 2022). We can only speculate why there was no significant association between sexual assault and maladaptive appraisals in the present study, however, it should be mentioned that only 8% of the terror-exposed group reported exposure to sexual assaults, which could have impacted the possibility of detecting significant relationships in this sample.

Experiencing more types of PTEs was significantly associated with higher levels of both types of maladaptive appraisals in the terror-exposed sample, a finding that echoes previous studies in the field (de Haan et al., 2017; Ponnamperuma & Nicolson, 2016). Ehlers and Clark (2000) suggest that exposure to several potentially traumatic events can reactivate memories and emotional responses from previous events and give additional negative meaning to the trauma exposure, such as ‘I am the type of person that brings out the worst in people’ (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). Again, we did not find a significant association between the number of PTEs and the level of maladaptive appraisals in the clinical sample.

The present study adds to the small number of studies that have investigated the two types of maladaptive appraisals: permanent-change appraisals and scary-world appraisals, separately for children and adolescents. Previous studies have found high correlations between the two types of appraisals (Lobo et al., 2015; Pfeiffer et al., 2017), and this was also the case for both samples in the present study. However, the finding that witnessing or learning that a close person was exposed to physical violence was significantly associated with more permanent-change appraisals in the terror-exposed sample extends previous findings and suggests that while the types of appraisals are highly correlated, they may be associated with different risk factors in some trauma-exposed populations.

Previous studies have reported an association between female gender and higher overall levels of maladaptive appraisals (Diehle et al., 2015; Lobo et al., 2015; Pfeiffer et al., 2017). As in the study by Meiser-Stedman and colleagues (2009b), female gender was significantly associated with a higher level of scary-world appraisals in the terror-exposed sample, but not with permanent-change appraisals. These findings indicate that appraisals about being a fragile person in a scary world, such as ‘I can’t cope when things get tough’ or ‘I have to watch out for danger all the time’ may be especially prevalent for girls. As girls have a higher risk of developing PTSD after trauma (Alisic et al., 2014), it is important to disentangle gender-specific risk factors for elevated trauma reactions. The increased risk of developing PTSD for girls has been associated with an elevated risk of exposure to high-impact traumatic events such as sexual assaults, gender differences in coping strategies and appraisals of the traumatic event, and factors related to biological sex, such as differential biological responses to stress and trauma among females and males (for a review, see Olff et al., 2007). These factors may also contribute to an elevated risk for females of experiencing oneself as fragile or that the world is threatening. Other gender-based differences, such as gender roles, may also play a role (Christiansen et al., 2022). For example, girls may report more scary-world appraisals due to a higher degree of congruence with existing gender roles for girls, and thus, it is also possible that these thoughts could be underreported by boys.

In summary, we found that experiencing more different types of PTEs was significantly associated with a higher level of maladaptive appraisals, witnessing or learning that someone close was exposed to physical violence was significantly associated with a higher level of permanent-change appraisals, and female gender was significantly associated with a higher level of scary-world appraisals in the terror-exposed sample.

Notably, we did not find any significant relationships between the predictor variables and the level of maladaptive appraisals in the clinical sample. Although one should be careful when interpreting non-significant findings, one could speculate whether these differences between the samples may be due to the fact that one of the samples exclusively included trauma-exposed adolescents who had clinically significant and persistent posttraumatic stress symptoms, of whom the majority had been exposed to interpersonal trauma. The clinical sample was included in the study based on inclusion criteria involving elevated levels of posttraumatic stress reactions, in combination with help-seeking and referral to specialist health services. The terror-exposed sample had all been exposed to the same extreme trauma and agreed to participate in the Utøya study, but there were no inclusion criteria based on the severity of their posttraumatic stress reactions. This difference may introduce sampling biases, and thus, the samples should be compared with caution. Furthermore, posttraumatic stress reactions were measured using different instruments in the two samples, which may further complicate comparisons across samples.

Maladaptive appraisals were measured using the same instrument in the two samples. There were significantly higher levels in the clinical group than in the terror-exposed sample and in other comparable studies (e.g. de Haan et al., 2017; Diehle et al., 2015; Meiser-Stedman et al., 2009b), including the clinical sample reported in the study by Diehle and colleagues (2015) where the mean level was 47.3, compared to 63.3 in the present study. However, similarly high levels (64.3) were reported by a subsample with PTSD in the study by Meiser-Stedman and colleagues (2009b). Whereas the variation in scores for maladaptive appraisals was comparable between the two samples in the present study, it may be that the included predictor variables in our study differentiate better between the level of appraisals in samples with great variation in symptom levels than in samples where all participants report high levels of PTSD symptoms.

In line with our second hypothesis, there was a high and significant association between maladaptive appraisals and PTSR in both groups included in this study. There was no significant difference between the strength of the association between permanent-change appraisals and PTSR nor scary-world appraisals and PTSR in any of the samples. This finding is consistent with the meta-analysis by Gómez de la Cuesta and colleagues (2019), where the authors found no significant difference in the strength of the effect size of the two types of appraisals for PTSR. As noted by Gómez de la Cuesta and colleagues (2019), both permanent-change appraisals and scary-world appraisals entail maladaptive appraisals about the self, which have been particularly associated with elevated posttraumatic stress reactions in adults.

The association between maladaptive appraisals and PTSR was significantly stronger in the terror-exposed sample than in the clinical sample, which may seem puzzling given that the levels of both maladaptive appraisals and PTSR were higher in the clinical sample. Treatment studies have found indications that a reduction in maladaptive appraisals works as a mechanism for symptom reduction in populations with clinically significant symptom levels (Kleim et al., 2013; Pfeiffer et al., 2017; Wiedemann et al., 2020), including studies with the clinical sample that participated in this study (Jensen et al., 2018; Knutsen et al., 2018). It could be that the significantly stronger association between maladaptive appraisals and PTSR in the terror-exposed sample rather emphasizes the important role of maladaptive appraisals in the psychological adjustment of adolescents exposed to single-event mass trauma such as terrorism.

Strengths and limitations

Both the Utøya study and the TF-CBT study had a high response rate and there was little missing data. A parallel investigation of these samples provided a unique possibility to examine pathways to persistent reactions and recovery across trauma-exposed populations. This study also has some important limitations, as there are substantial differences between the studies and samples, which makes it challenging to conclude why we observed different relationships. First, the clinical sample was included in the TF-CBT study based on the inclusion criteria of having clinically significant PTSD symptoms. In contrast, the terror-exposed sample included adolescents with varying levels of posttraumatic stress reactions. Second, exposure to PTEs was measured by different measures in the two studies, which could affect the prevalence. For example, the item measuring exposure to witnessing or learning that someone close was exposed to physical violence had a broader definition in the Utøya study than in the TF-CBT study (see Figure 2). Third, since we did not have data on repeated exposure, the role of chronic or enduring exposure to the same type of trauma is not reflected in our models. Furthermore, there could be additional differences between the groups that we could not control for, which had an impact on the observed relationships in this study. For example, the terror-exposed sample is largely representative of the general youth population in Norway (Stensland et al., 2018), and there may be differences between the groups on variables such as the economy and living situation that may have played a role. However, due to differences in how the demographic variables were measured, we did not include these variables. As the data were collected between 2009 and 2012, we used DSM-IV criteria for PTSD. We do not know whether the results would have been different if posttraumatic stress reactions were assessed according to the DSM-5 criteria. Moreover, posttraumatic stress reactions were measured with different scales that are reported on different timescales, two weeks for the clinical sample and the past months for the terror-exposed sample. This may have implications for the reporting and recall of reactions. Finally, the included data were cross-sectional, thus, we could not examine temporal relationships between the variables.

Conclusion

After exposure to potentially traumatizing events, some children and adolescents report elevated levels of maladaptive appraisals. We found that among adolescents exposed to terrorism, having experienced more types of PTEs, exposure to physical violence and female gender were significantly associated with more maladaptive appraisals. More studies are needed on factors associated with elevated maladaptive appraisals in adolescents exposed to severe interpersonal trauma and for adolescents with clinically significant symptom levels. Both permanent-change appraisals and scary-world appraisals were related to PTSR in both samples of trauma-exposed adolescents. As maladaptive appraisals are strongly associated with posttraumatic stress reactions, there is a need to further elucidate the pathways involved in the development of elevated maladaptive appraisals in trauma-exposed adolescents.
