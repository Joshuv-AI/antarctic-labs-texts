Stability control of suspended monorail vehicles based on PID and quadratic programming allocation
Authors: Yongzhi Jiang, Dong Wang, Renxiang Chen, Qunsheng Wang, Zhengwei Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuspended monorail vehicles are susceptible to coupled lateral–vertical vibrations under multi-source disturbances, such as track irregularities and fluctuating crosswinds, which can degrade operational stability and passenger comfort. To address this issue, this study develops an actuator-constrained hierarchical control framework for suspended monorail vehicles. The main methodological contribution is a vehicle-specific constrained control-allocation procedure. This procedure links measurable lateral–vertical acceleration feedback to physically bounded multi-actuator forces through sensitivity-based actuator configuration, data-identified control-effectiveness mapping, and QP-based allocation. Specifically, a high-fidelity vehicle dynamics model calibrated using measured data is subjected to track irregularities and Simiu-spectrum fluctuating wind loads. Sobol sensitivity analysis is then used to determine the actuator installation locations and acting directions, and a local control-effectiveness matrix is identified through single-actuator excitation simulations and linear regression. In the inner loop, a genetic-algorithm-optimized fixed-parameter PID controller and a fuzzy self-tuning PID controller are designed to generate virtual control commands. In the outer loop, a quadratic-programming-based allocation problem distributes these commands among the actuators while enforcing amplitude constraints. An LQR-QP controller is also introduced as a classical linear optimal-control benchmark. Under identical disturbances and actuator constraints, the fuzzy self-tuning PID-QP controller achieves the best overall performance among the compared methods. Relative to the uncontrolled case, it reduces the lateral and vertical acceleration RMS values by 21.15% and 20.80%, the corresponding acceleration peaks by 28.54% and 32.42%, and the RMS values of the lateral and vertical stability indices by 6.55% and 6.84%, respectively. These results indicate that the proposed hierarchical cooperative control framework can effectively improve the lateral and vertical stability of suspended monorail vehicles while satisfying actuator amplitude constraints.Keywords: Suspended monorail vehicle, Hierarchical control, Genetic algorithm, Fuzzy self-tuning PID, Quadratic programmingSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Suspended monorail vehicles offer strong gradeability, low noise, relatively low construction cost, and short construction periods, and are therefore increasingly used in urban public transport and scenic-area transit systems1. Owing to their suspended configuration, however, these vehicles are particularly sensitive to external disturbances during operation. Forced excitation from track irregularities and random lateral loading from fluctuating crosswinds may act simultaneously, inducing pronounced lateral sway and vertical vibration and thereby degrading operational stability and ride comfort2. Unstable vehicle motion can also compromise operational safety3. Consequently, vibration suppression and stability improvement under multi-source disturbances have become key challenges in the dynamic design and control-system development of suspended monorail vehicles.

Substantial progress has been made in the dynamic modeling of suspended monorail vehicles, the analysis of wind-induced responses, and performance improvement strategies. Yang et al.4 established a vehicle–track-beam coupled dynamic model to evaluate the influence of wind loads on system responses and proposed parameter-optimization and speed-control strategies for suppressing lateral sway under turbulent winds. Bao et al.5 developed a fully coupled wind–vehicle–bridge model and revealed the significant effects of turbulent crosswinds on dynamic response and ride comfort through aerodynamic calculations in non-uniform wind fields, highlighting the importance of coupling effects in vibration analysis. He et al.6 systematically investigated the effects of span, deflection-to-span ratio, and track irregularities on the dynamic performance of the vehicle–track system, and provided recommendations for optimizing key geometric parameters. Regarding operating-condition optimization, Jiang et al.7 constructed a multi-parameter and multi-objective optimization platform to reduce wheel-load reduction, tipping risk, and wear during curve negotiation; Bao et al.8 investigated the effects of vehicle height, speed, and curve radius on curving performance using a rigid–flexible coupled vehicle–bridge model; and Zhang et al.9 established a quantitative relationship for the critical lateral force from the perspective of guide-wheel preload, providing theoretical guidance for parameter selection under safety and comfort constraints.

Research on vibration suppression and operational-stability control for monorail vehicles has also continued to develop. Yildiz et al.10 proposed semi-active magnetorheological dampers as secondary suspension elements and compared robust and adaptive control strategies for suppressing roll, nodding, and vertical vibration during curve negotiation. Zhou et al.11 combined global sensitivity analysis with factor modeling to investigate shimmy in single-axle straddle-type monorail vehicles and identified the key parameters governing stability. Zhou et al.12 developed a dynamic suspension model with time-delay compensation for magnetorheological dampers and validated it through hardware-in-the-loop tests. Song et al.13 proposed an active-suspension retrofit scheme based on a high-degree-of-freedom vehicle model and closed-loop feedback control for shimmy suppression. Zhou et al.14 further established a vehicle dynamic model with magnetorheological dampers and proposed adaptive fuzzy semi-active lateral control combined with predictive current control based on an inverse damper model.

Despite these advances, several issues remain insufficiently addressed for suspended monorail vehicles. Existing studies have mainly focused on dynamic-response analysis, suspension-parameter optimization, and semi-active vibration suppression, whereas active control strategies for coupled lateral–vertical ride-stability responses under combined track irregularities and fluctuating crosswinds remain limited. Moreover, actuator configurations are often assumed rather than selected according to their influence on vehicle responses, and the relationship between actuator forces and coupled acceleration outputs is rarely identified and incorporated into a constrained allocation framework. These limitations may reduce the physical feasibility and reproducibility of the control-design process. Therefore, the present study does not aim to develop a new generic PID or optimization algorithm; instead, it establishes a reproducible, actuator-constrained, vehicle-specific control-design procedure for suppressing lateral–vertical coupled vibration in suspended monorail vehicles.

Advanced control approaches, including model predictive control, sliding-mode control, and fault-tolerant control, have been widely investigated for complex dynamic systems subject to uncertainties, disturbances, and actuator faults. Saadatinezhad et al.15 proposed a fault-tolerant model predictive control strategy for load–frequency control in a multi-area power system with tie-line failures, demonstrating the ability of predictive control to handle structural changes and maintain system reliability. Alizadeh et al.16 developed a fault-tolerant sliding-mode control method for an unmanned bicycle robot by integrating sliding-mode control, fault detection, and fault estimation, thereby improving robustness under actuator faults. Alizadeh et al.17 further proposed a super-twisting fast non-singular terminal sliding-mode control strategy for a 3-DOF robotic arm, improving trajectory-tracking accuracy and robustness under model mismatch and external disturbances.

In contrast, proportional–integral–derivative (PID) control remains attractive for engineering applications because of its simple structure, high reliability, and ease of implementation. Samaroo et al.18 proposed a semi-active suspension combining dynamic vibration absorbers with PID-controlled magnetorheological dampers to mitigate vibration in in-wheel-motor-driven electric vehicles, thereby improving comfort and stability. Wang et al.19 designed a distributed PID strategy optimized by particle swarm optimization to suppress congestion and instability in mixed traffic. Ram et al.20 proposed a robust PID-tuning method for quarter-car suspension systems to improve response speed and robustness. Zhou et al.21 developed an online adaptive PID-tuning strategy based on a BP neural network to adapt to time-varying nonlinear road conditions. Li et al.22 applied fractional-order theory to semi-active suspension PID control and verified its advantages in precision and vibration reduction. Liu23 combined LQR and PID control for autonomous collision avoidance, improving response speed and stability. Lv et al.24 proposed fuzzy PID tuning based on the Beluga Whale Optimization Algorithm to improve anti-lock braking performance. These studies demonstrate the engineering adaptability and extensibility of PID-based strategies for nonlinear, time-varying, and uncertain systems.

Based on the above discussion, this study addresses the lateral–vertical coupled vibration of suspended monorail vehicles under multi-source disturbances, including track irregularities and fluctuating crosswinds. The control strategy is selected according to three engineering requirements. First, the controlled variables should be directly related to ride stability and measurable during operation; therefore, the lateral and vertical accelerations at the carbody center of mass are selected as feedback signals. Second, the inner-loop controller should have a simple structure and low online computational burden because onboard sensing, controller sampling time, and reliability requirements constrain real-vehicle implementation. PID-type controllers are therefore adopted as the basic feedback regulators. Third, the actuator forces must satisfy amplitude limits, and multiple actuators must operate cooperatively; accordingly, a QP-based outer-loop allocation layer is introduced to map the virtual lateral–vertical control demands to feasible actuator forces. On this basis, a hierarchical cooperative control framework with an “inner-loop feedback–outer-loop allocation” structure is developed. Suspended monorail vehicle model

Dynamic model of the suspended monorail vehicle

The suspended monorail vehicle consists of a carbody, bogies, bolsters, and suspension components, and is supported and guided through wheel–rail contact with a box-type guideway beam. The wheelset typically includes driving wheels, guide wheels, and stabilizing wheels. The guide and stabilizing wheels provide lateral guidance and stability, whereas the driving wheels move along the bottom surface of the guideway beam. The driving wheels are connected to the gearbox through bearings, and the gearbox is connected to the bogie through rubber bushings, enabling the transmission of wheel–rail interaction forces and traction to the bogie. To improve stability and comfort, suspension elements such as air springs, anti-roll dampers, and lateral and vertical dampers are installed between the bogies and the carbody.

The vehicle dynamic model provides the basis for vibration-response analysis and control-strategy development. In this study, a high-fidelity suspended monorail vehicle dynamic model is established in Universal Mechanism (UM). The model consists of one carbody, two bogies, and two bolsters; each bogie is equipped with four driving wheels, four guide wheels, and four stabilizing wheels. Data-driven high-fidelity model

Although the high-fidelity multibody model preserves the physical structure of the suspended monorail vehicle, uncertainties remain in several model parameters. If these uncertainties are ignored, the simulated responses may deviate from the actual vehicle behavior. Therefore, measured vehicle data are introduced to calibrate the high-fidelity model.

In this study, the calibrated model is obtained through a data-driven correction process based on the physical multibody model25. This procedure does not replace the vehicle dynamics model with a black-box surrogate. Instead, measured data are used to update key parameters in the physics-based model, enabling the model to reproduce the dynamic behavior of the actual vehicle more accurately while retaining its original mechanical structure and nonlinear coupling characteristics.

The calibration process consists of three steps. First, a baseline high-fidelity model is built in UM according to the vehicle structure, suspension layout, tire configuration, and nominal design parameters. Second, dynamic-response data are collected from the experimental vehicle under real operating conditions. The measured lateral and vertical acceleration signals of the carbody are processed according to the ride-performance evaluation procedure, and the corresponding lateral and vertical Sperling indices are obtained. Third, selected model parameters are iteratively updated, and the simulated Sperling indices are compared with the measured values until the model response reaches an acceptable agreement with the test data. Controller design

To improve the lateral–vertical stability of the suspended monorail vehicle under track irregularities and fluctuating crosswind loads, a hierarchical control framework with an inner-loop feedback controller and an outer-loop control-allocation layer is developed. The inner loop generates virtual acceleration-suppression commands according to the measured lateral and vertical acceleration errors of the carbody, whereas the outer loop distributes these virtual commands to multiple actuators through constrained quadratic programming.

The overall control design includes four main parts: the control problem formulation, the GA-optimized fixed-parameter PID controller, the fuzzy self-tuning PID controller, and the QP-based actuator allocation strategy. The rationale for adopting the proposed PID–QP hierarchical architecture is discussed first, followed by the detailed controller formulation.

Rationale for the proposed control architecture

The proposed control architecture is designed according to the dynamic characteristics of suspended monorail vehicles and the practical constraints of onboard implementation. Under combined track irregularities and fluctuating crosswinds, the vehicle exhibits coupled lateral and vertical vibration responses. Since the deterioration of stability is directly reflected in the lateral and vertical accelerations of the carbody, these two measurable acceleration signals are selected as feedback variables.

PID-type controllers are adopted in the inner loop because of their simple structure, low computational burden, and ease of real-time implementation. However, a conventional fixed-parameter PID controller may not provide sufficient adaptability under random and time-varying disturbances. Therefore, two PID-based controllers are considered in this study. The GA-optimized fixed-parameter PID controller provides an offline-tuned baseline with reproducible parameters, while the fuzzy self-tuning PID controller further adjusts the PID gains online according to the acceleration error and its variation rate. In this way, the fuzzy self-tuning mechanism improves the adaptability of the controller to different vibration states without requiring an accurate online prediction model.

The outer-loop allocation layer is introduced because the virtual control commands generated by the inner loop cannot be directly applied to the vehicle. In practice, the control forces are produced by multiple actuators installed at different locations and acting in different directions, and each actuator is subject to amplitude constraints. Therefore, the control-allocation problem is formulated as a constrained quadratic-programming problem, in which the tracking error between the desired virtual control demand and the acceleration response generated by actuator forces is minimized while satisfying actuator force limits. This hierarchical structure separates feedback regulation from actuator-force allocation, allowing the controller to improve lateral–vertical stability while maintaining physical feasibility of the actuator inputs.

The controller uses the lateral and vertical accelerations at the vehicle center of mass as the primary feedback signals, with the objective of regulating them close to zero and thereby improving the Sperling stability index. For the two channels, lateral and vertical PID feedback structures are designed separately. Genetic-algorithm-based parameter optimization

A genetic algorithm (GA) is used to optimize the PID gains offline, yielding a fixed-gain controller that serves as a stable and reproducible baseline. Manual PID tuning is difficult because the suspended monorail vehicle model involves nonlinear wheel–guideway interactions, coupled lateral–vertical vibration responses, and stochastic external disturbances. Gradient-based tuning is also impractical because each evaluation of controller performance is obtained from a time-domain simulation of the high-fidelity vehicle model rather than from an explicit analytical objective function. Inner loop II: fuzzy self-tuning PID controller

Suspended monorail vehicles exhibit pronounced time-varying characteristics under excitations such as track irregularities and fluctuating crosswinds. A single fixed-parameter PID controller may perform well near the operating condition used for tuning, but its performance can deteriorate when the vibration amplitude, disturbance intensity, or coupling relationship between the lateral and vertical responses changes. Large acceleration errors require stronger proportional action for rapid correction, rapidly varying errors require increased derivative action to suppress overshoot and oscillation, and small residual errors require appropriate integral action to improve steady-state suppression. These requirements cannot be fully satisfied by a single set of fixed PID gains.

Therefore, a fuzzy self-tuning PID controller is introduced with the GA-optimized gains as initial values. The fuzzy mechanism uses the acceleration error and its rate of change as inputs and updates the PID gains online according to the instantaneous vibration state. Compared with model-based adaptive control or MPC, fuzzy self-tuning PID does not require an accurate online prediction model and imposes a lower computational burden. It is therefore suitable for engineering applications with limited measurable feedback signals while improving adaptability to random and time-varying disturbances.

Membership functions and fuzzy rule-base design

To represent different magnitude levels of the error signals and the corresponding gain adjustments, several fuzzy sets are defined for each input and output variable. A symmetric seven-level linguistic set is adopted, where NB, NM, NS, ZO, PS, PM, and PB denote Negative Big, Negative Medium, Negative Small, Zero, Positive Small, Positive Medium, and Positive Big, respectively. The membership functions follow a robust “trapezoidal-at-the-ends and triangular-in-the-middle” layout. Trapezoidal membership functions are used at both extremes to improve fault tolerance and saturation robustness in large-error regions, whereas triangular membership functions are used in the middle to provide finer resolution for small-to-moderate errors and to preserve approximately linear adjustment behavior. By appropriately selecting the centers and widths of the membership functions, the normalized error and error-rate inputs are mapped to linguistic values in the fuzzy domain, while the fuzzy outputs determine the magnitude and direction of the PID gain adjustments.

Outer loop: quadratic-programming-based control allocation

The inner-loop controller outputs the desired control effort in the lateral and vertical directions, whereas physical actuation is realized through forces generated by multiple independent actuators installed at different positions and acting in different directions. The control system is therefore an over-actuated allocation problem: two primary stability-related control demands must be realized by several actuator forces. Because a direct one-to-one mapping is unavailable, simply assigning the same command to all actuators may lead to inefficient force distribution, actuator saturation, or undesirable coupling effects.

The outer loop therefore maps the virtual control demand from the inner loop to physically feasible actuator forces. This allocation process is naturally formulated as a constrained optimization problem. The QP formulation is selected because its objective function directly minimizes the tracking error between the desired lateral–vertical virtual control demand and the response generated by actuator forces, while the box constraints explicitly represent actuator amplitude limits. In addition, the resulting optimization problem is convex and computationally efficient, making it suitable for real-time implementation. Thus, the QP layer does not replace the feedback controller; instead, it functions as a constraint-handling and actuator-coordination module. The allocation problem is formulated as a convex QP problem with bound constraints to coordinate multiple actuator inputs under amplitude limits.

GA-PID-QP performs better because of offline gain optimization, but its fixed parameters cannot adapt to time-varying vibration states. The different behaviors of the three controllers can be explained in terms of model dependence, parameter adaptability, and actuator feasibility. LQR-QP uses a local linear model identified around the selected operating condition. It therefore provides an efficient linear optimal-control benchmark, but its performance may decrease when the actual vehicle response deviates from the identified local model because of nonlinear coupling, random wind excitation, or large-amplitude vibration. GA-PID-QP does not rely on an online vehicle model and has a simple implementation structure. Its gains are optimized offline using the high-fidelity simulation model, which improves the baseline control effect relative to manually selected gains. However, because the gains remain fixed during operation, the controller cannot actively adjust its damping and correction intensity when the disturbance level or vibration state changes.

Fuzzy self-tuning PID-QP combines the advantages of the above two approaches. It retains the simple acceleration-feedback structure of PID control while introducing online gain correction according to the error magnitude and error variation rate. When the acceleration error becomes large, the proportional action is strengthened to improve response speed. When the error changes rapidly, the derivative action is increased to suppress overshoot and oscillation. When the error is small but persistent, the integral action helps reduce residual vibration. Therefore, the fuzzy self-tuning mechanism provides better adaptability to time-varying lateral–vertical coupled vibrations. Meanwhile, all three inner-loop controllers share the same QP allocation layer, ensuring that the generated virtual control demands are converted into actuator forces within the prescribed amplitude limits. This explains why the hierarchical architecture improves vibration suppression while maintaining actuator feasibility.

Overall, the proposed hierarchical control framework can coordinate multiple actuators under amplitude constraints. The outer-loop QP allocation ensures physically feasible actuator forces, whereas the fuzzy self-tuning PID inner loop improves adaptability to random time-varying disturbances. The results verify the effectiveness of the proposed fuzzy self-tuning PID-QP strategy for improving the lateral and vertical stability of suspended monorail vehicles.

This comparison is intended to evaluate the proposed actuator-constrained hierarchical allocation framework with different inner-loop controllers, rather than to claim the universal superiority of fuzzy self-tuning PID-QP over MPC, sliding-mode control, or other robust control strategies. Although more advanced controllers may provide stronger robustness under broader uncertainties, they usually require more detailed prediction models, higher online computational cost, or additional robustness design. In contrast, the proposed PID-QP framework emphasizes engineering simplicity, measurable acceleration feedback, and explicit actuator feasibility for suspended monorail vibration suppression.

Engineering implementation and limitations

The proposed hierarchical control framework has potential engineering applicability for suspended monorail vehicles because it relies on measurable lateral and vertical accelerations at the carbody center of mass, which can be obtained using acceleration sensors or an inertial measurement unit. The inner-loop PID-type controller generates virtual acceleration-suppression commands based on these feedback signals, while the outer-loop QP allocation layer distributes the virtual control demand to multiple actuators under explicit amplitude constraints. Therefore, the proposed framework provides a physically feasible control strategy for improving lateral–vertical stability under track irregularities and fluctuating crosswind disturbances.

Compared with unconstrained control strategies, the QP-based allocation layer prevents infeasible control commands from being directly applied to the physical actuators. In addition, the fixed-parameter PID and fuzzy self-tuning PID controllers have relatively simple feedback structures, which is beneficial for onboard implementation. The fuzzy self-tuning mechanism further improves adaptability to time-varying vibration states without requiring a highly complex online prediction model. These characteristics indicate that the proposed control framework has practical potential for engineering application in suspended monorail vehicles.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. First, the current actuator configuration is mainly determined according to vibration-suppression performance and the sensitivity of different installation positions, while economic optimization has not yet been incorporated. Seven actuators are adopted in the present study to achieve better lateral–vertical stability improvement under the research and development conditions of the test vehicle. However, the number, installation position, weight, power consumption, maintenance cost, and reliability of the actuators should be further optimized before commercial application. In future work, a multi-objective optimization strategy considering both control performance and economic feasibility will be introduced to obtain a more cost-effective actuator configuration.

Second, although the inner-loop PID-type controllers have low computational complexity, the overall control process still involves online QP-based actuator-force allocation. Moreover, the calibrated high-fidelity vehicle model and the identified control-effectiveness relationship are used to support the design and validation of the control strategy. If the control-effectiveness matrix or data-driven high-fidelity model is updated online under varying operating conditions, the computational burden of the onboard controller may increase. Therefore, real-time solver efficiency, sampling-time constraints, and hardware computational capability should be further evaluated before practical implementation.

Third, the present study is based on numerical simulations using a calibrated high-fidelity vehicle model. Although measured data are used to improve the agreement between the model and the actual vehicle, discrepancies between the calibrated model and the real vehicle are still inevitable. These discrepancies may arise from uncertain suspension parameters, nonlinear wheel–guideway contact characteristics, sensor noise, actuator dynamics, and changes in operating conditions. Such model–vehicle mismatch may affect the actual control performance. Future work will further improve the fidelity of the data-driven model by incorporating more experimental data under different operating conditions and will verify the proposed control framework through hardware-in-the-loop tests and real-vehicle experiments.

Finally, actuator nonidealities, such as time delay, bandwidth limitation, dead zone, friction, saturation dynamics, and force-tracking error, are not fully considered in the current simulation. The disturbance cases considered in this study mainly include track irregularities and fluctuating crosswinds under selected operating conditions. Therefore, the generalization ability of the proposed method under broader operating speeds, wind conditions, track irregularity levels, sensor noise, and actuator degradation still requires further investigation.

Conclusions

This study developed a vehicle-specific hierarchical control framework for suppressing lateral–vertical coupled vibrations of suspended monorail vehicles under track irregularities and fluctuating crosswinds. By combining acceleration feedback with QP-based actuator allocation, the proposed framework converts virtual ride-stability control demands into physically feasible multi-actuator forces under amplitude constraints.

This study developed a vehicle-specific hierarchical control framework for suppressing lateral–vertical coupled vibrations of suspended monorail vehicles under track irregularities and fluctuating crosswinds. By combining acceleration feedback with QP-based actuator allocation, the proposed framework converts virtual ride-stability control demands into physically feasible multi-actuator forces under amplitude constraints.

The actuator configuration and allocation model were determined through Sobol sensitivity analysis and control-effectiveness identification, rather than empirical assumptions. On this basis, GA-PID-QP and fuzzy self-tuning PID-QP controllers were constructed, where the former provides a reproducible fixed-gain baseline and the latter improves adaptability through online gain adjustment.
