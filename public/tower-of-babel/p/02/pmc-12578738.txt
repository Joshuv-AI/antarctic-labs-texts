Pyrethroid and organochlorine susceptibility in Aedes aegypti populations from eastern black sea region, Türkiye: traces in the kdr gene region of the species origin
Authors: Murat Öztürk, Fatih Şaban Beriş, Hilal Bedir, Muhammet Mustafa Akiner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aedes aegypti (Linnaeus 1762) is a significant public health concern in both its native and invaded regions because of the transmission of arboviruses. Target-site mutations in the voltage-gated sodium channel (vgsc) gene, commonly referred to as knockdown resistance (kdr), represent the most extensively studied mechanism underlying pyrethroid resistance. Therefore, we aimed to assess possible pyrethroid and organochlorine resistance levels, knockdown resistance, and mutation types known to contribute to the resistance profile in the Black Sea region of Türkiye in 2020. Bioassay results showed that all field populations, as well as the Bora Bora laboratory strain, were susceptible and predominantly carried wild-type genotypes. For kdr, all samples were wild type for the V410L, L982W, S989P, I1011V/M, L1014F, V1016I/G, and T1520I mutations. The mutant allele was detected in only one specimen in the Pazar population, where it was found in a homozygous state at position F1534C from ten specimens. Novel mutations in the vgsc gene region, with unknown implications for resistance, were determined at positions A1498T, R1599C, P1611L, and P1615S in the studied populations. Two different intron types were detected in the kdr gene (in domain 2) between nucleotide positions 3111 and 3228 and within an intron region (coded as type-1 and type-2). These findings indicate that, Ae. aegypti populations in the Eastern Black Sea region remain susceptible to pyrethroid and organochlorine insecticides. Genetic analyses indicate that these populations likely originated from Asia, with evidence of two distinct lineages. Notably, this study represents the first molecular and phenotypic assessment of insecticide resistance in Ae. aegypti populations conducted in Türkiye.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00436-025-08538-5.

Introduction

Aedes aegypti (Linnaeus 1762), also known as the yellow fever mosquito, is the main vector of dengue fever, yellow fever, Zika, and chikungunya viruses. There is no effective vaccine or drug for these arboviral diseases (Saavedra-Rodriguez et al. 2018). The main current strategy for preventing diseases includes controlling adult Ae. aegypti populations. Unfortunately, intensive and long-term insecticide application has caused insecticide resistance in Ae. aegypti populations in many areas of the world (Marcombe et al. 2012).

Four major mechanisms are associated with insecticide resistance: metabolic resistance, target-site resistance, penetration and behavioural resistance (IRAC 2025). Metabolic resistance involves the degradation or sequestration of the different insecticide groups by three major enzyme groups: esterases, glutathione-S-transferases, and monooxygenases (Akıner et al. 2024; Kılıçaslan et al. 2022). Target-site resistance is caused by single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in target genes such as acetylcholinesterase (ace-1), the GABA receptor, and the voltage-gated sodium channel (vgsc) (Kamgang et al. 2020). In general, vgsc mutations change the structure of the protein and inhibit or reduce its binding of pyrethroids and organochlorines (Ayres et al. 2020). More than 50 SNP mutations in the vgsc gene have been detected in different insect species and confirmed to be responsible for the knockdown resistance (kdr) (Rinkevich et al. 2013; Dong et al. 2014). At least eleven sodium channel mutations (V410L, G923V, L982W, S989P, I1011M/V, V1016G/I, T1520I, F1534C, and D1763Y) identified in Ae. aegypti have been linked to pyrethroid resistance (Brengues et al. 2003; Du et al. 2016; Ayres et al. 2020; Enayati et al. 2024). Among these, V1016G, V1016I, and F1534C mutations have been extensively investigated and shown to be strongly related to pyrethroid resistance (Kasai et al. 2014).

Ae. aegypti was reported on Türkiye's Mediterranean and Black Sea coasts in the 1920 s (Merdivenci 1984). By the 1950 s, the species had disappeared. However, it was observed sporadically in some parts of Türkiye in 1961, 1984, 1992, 1993, and 2001 (Schaffner and Mathis 2014). In 2015, it was detected again in the northeastern Black Sea region, including the provinces of Artvin, Rize, and Trabzon (Akıner et al. 2016). It has been known in this area since then (Demirci et al. 2021).

Targeted control measures against Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus in Türkiye, Ae. aegypti in Türkiye were conducted primarily, initiated in 2016 following its initial detection, to prevent the establishment and spread of the species. Control of the adults was carried out with neonicotinoids and pyrethroids, while larval control used with insect growth regulators and Bacillus thuringiensis var. israelensis (Bti). The control efforts targeting Ae. aegypti populations in Artvin, Rize, and Trabzon achieved a reduction in population size of 37% for adults and 61% for larvae. However, the resistance status of Ae. aegypti was not reported in this study (Akıner et al. 2018). There have been no large-scale studies of the control/eradication of Ae. aegypti in that area since then. In addition, the use of chemicals in the region has been prohibited due to the organic farming strategy for the tea plantations in the area (Akıner et al. 2018). The resistance types and mutations that occur because of insecticide selection pressure can provide information about the species’ resistance profiles and possible origins. For example, S989P, L982W, and V1016G kdr mutations have been found only in Asia, while G923V and I1011M kdr mutations have been found only in America (Fan et al. 2020). Therefore, the data obtained may contribute to the discussions about the origin of the species. Apart from the kdr mutations, two different intron types located between codons 1011 (exon 20) and 1016 (exon 21) in the vgsc genomic region of Ae. aegypti were determined and identified as groups A and B (Martins et al. 2013; Saavedra-Rodriguez et al. 2018; Scott 2019; Cosme et al. 2020). Group A exists with the I1011M kdr mutation while group B is associated with the F1534C kdr mutation (Martins et al. 2013, 2013; Kawada et al. 2016; Chung et al. 2019).

The purpose of this study was to determine the potential levels of insecticide resistance and the specific types of kdr at the target-site, as well as to interpret the possible origin of the species based on the resistance profile of Ae. aegypti in Türkiye.

Materials and methods

Mosquito collection and laboratory rearing for bioassays

A total of 12,247 mosquito specimens were collected between March and September 2020 from four semi-urban coastal districts in the Eastern Black Sea Region of Türkiye: Pazar and Ardeşen (Rize Province), and Arhavi and Hopa (Artvin Province) (Fig. 1). Sampling sites were selected from used tyre piles.Fig. 1The map of the mosquito collection areas from the Black Sea region of Türkiye (The figure was made ArcMap 10.4)

The map of the mosquito collection areas from the Black Sea region of Türkiye (The figure was made ArcMap 10.4)

The immature stages of Ae. aegypti were primarily collected using two standard methods: ovitrap surveillance and larval dipping. A total of 8,086 eggs, comprising both Ae. albopictus and Ae. aegypti, were obtained from ovitraps; following laboratory rearing and morphological identification, 293 specimens were confirmed as Ae. aegypti. Similarly, 4,161 larvae were collected from natural and artificial breeding sites, of which 572 were identified as Ae. aegypti after rearing to the adult stage (Table 1). Ovitrap sampling was conducted at each site using one ovitrap per location, following the protocol described by Medlock et al. (2018). Each ovitrap was deployed in the field for five days, after which the traps were retrieved and transported to the laboratory. Collected egg were air-dried at room temperature for 24 h and subsequently counted under a stereomicroscope. All eggs were then subjected to a standardized hatching protocol and reared to adulthood under controlled insectary conditions (27 ± 1 °C, 75 ± 5% relative humidity, and a 14:10 h light: dark photoperiod). During the larval development phase, individuals were fed with TetraMin® fish food (Tetra, Melle, Germany). Larval sampling was performed concurrently using the larval dipping method, employing dippers and Pasteur pipettes, targeting water-holding used tyres. To enhance species detection probability and genetic diversity, sampling was intentionally conducted across multiple tyre sites. All collected larvae were transported to the laboratory and maintained under the same rearing conditions described above until adult emergence. The adults were identified according to Schaffner et al. (2001) and used for bioassay insecticide testing and molecular analysis. Fifteen randomly selected field-collected Ae. aegypti specimens were stored at –20 °C for subsequent molecular analyses for per site. A colony was established using no fewer than 20 additional Ae. aegypti specimens for bioassay experiments. Adult females were provided a 10% sucrose solution as mosquito feed and with defibrinated bovine blood to fulfill their gonotrophic needs.

Insecticide bioassays

Three- to five-day-old and non-blood-fed female adult mosquitoes from the F1 or F2 (to reach the required number of specimens for the experiment) progeny were used for the bioassays. The Bora-Bora strain of Ae. aegypti was used in bioassays as a control for each insecticide. The bioassays were performed with WHO (World Health Organisation) test tube kits using insecticide-impregnated papers with concentrations of DDT (Dichloro diphenyl trichloroethane) (4%), dieldrin (4%), permethrin (0.75%), deltamethrin (0.05%), λ-cyhalothrin (0.05%), cyfluthrin (0.15%), and etofenprox (0.5%) (WHO 2016, 2022). Each assay was carried out with 20 female individuals. The tests were conducted in five replicates for each insecticide with two control groups (PY (Pyrethroid) control: insecticide-free silicone oil test paper; OC (Organochlorine) control: insecticide-free Risella oil test paper provided with the test kit) at the same time. Tests were repeated three times on three consecutive days to avoid any impact of the conditions. The mosquitoes were transferred to the resting tubes after one hour of exposure and provided 10% sugar feed with sucrose-soaked cotton. The mortality rate was calculated and recorded 24 h after an hour of exposure. Mortality rate was calculated as (number of dead mosquitoes/number of total sample) * 100. Insecticide resistance rates were classified according to WHO (2016) (mortality < 90% is resistant, mortality 90–97% is possible resistance, mortality ≥ 98% is susceptible).

Molecular assays of kdr mutations

The GeneJET Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific®) was used to extract DNA from whole mosquito bodies according to the manufacturer's recommended protocol. A total of 45 female Ae. aegypti adults (comprising ten mosquitoes from each locality and five mosquitoes from the Bora Bora laboratory strain (insecticide-free for 25 years) used as control) were used for molecular studies. All products were visualised in a 1.5% agarose gel, and all positive samples were stored at −20 °C until the PCR studies.

kdr mutation assays were performed using three different primer sets, including seven kdr mutations that were described previously. The first primers were forward, 5′-AGACAATGTGGATCGCTTCC-3′ and reverse, 5′-GGACGCAATCTGGCTTGTTA-3′ and were used to investigate the presence of point mutations at S989P, I1011M/V, L1014F and V1016G/I (Kawada et al. 2014). The second primer set was forward, 5′-GAGAACTCGCCGATGAACTT-3′ and reverse, 5′-GACGACGAAATCGAACAGGT-3′ for the T1520I and F1534C point mutations (Kasai et al. 2014). The last primer set was forward, 5′-GATAATCCAAATTACGGGTATAC-3′ and reverse, 5′-TTCTTCCTCGGCGGCCTCTT-3′ for the V410L point mutation (Saavedra-Rodriguez et al. 2018). The PCR mixture was prepared using Taq DNA Polymerase with Standard Taq Buffer (New England, Biolabs, M0273S) with a final volume of 25 µL, according to the manufacturer. The PCR conditions were performed in the previously referenced conditions using a T100™ BioRad® thermal cycler (Kasai et al. 2014; Kawada et al. 2014; Saavedra-Rodriguez et al. 2018). All PCR products were visualized in 2% agarose gels (Supplementary 1). Positive samples, 10 µL of amplicons for each gene region were transferred to new PCR tubes. Primer pairs (forward and reverse) used in the amplification of each gene region were provided in a minimum of five µL for each sample and were sequenced (Macrogen Inc. (Amsterdam)) as reverse and forward (GenBank accession number: PQ623049-88; PQ576197-276).

The detection of knockdown resistance (kdr)-associated point mutations in Ae. aegypti populations was performed by aligning the obtained nucleotide sequences with reference sequences available in the GenBank database (accession numbers EU399181).

In addition, previously characterized kdr mutation primers for V410L (Saavedra-Rodriguez et al. 2018), V1016I and F1534C (Harris et al. 2010), were used for the detection of point mutations in the vgsc gene. The resulting sequences were translated into protein sequences, and the presence of amino acid substitutions corresponding to known resistance mutations was confirmed.

Data analysis

The insecticide bioassay results were recorded and analysed using Microsoft Excel 2020. Differences in mean mortality rates among mosquito populations, individual insecticides, and insecticide classes were statistically evaluated using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) in SPSS software (version 27.0). The raw DNA sequences obtained from three primer sets were quality-trimmed and aligned bidirectionally (forward and reverse) using MEGA 7 software. Heterozygous individuals were identified by comparing forward and reverse sequence chromatograms, and the presence of polymorphic sites was confirmed by inspecting the corresponding chromatographic peaks. The kdr mutations were determined by aligning them with previously detected specific primer sequences used for detecting point mutations. The kdr mutations were converted to amino acids using the MEGA 7 software, and these mutation points were determined. Phylogenetic analyses of the obtained sequences were combined using Sequence Matrix v1.7 for each gene region, and the PAUP 4.0 partition homogeneity was tested. Results of the partition-homogeneity test (P < 0.05) indicated that trees of all three replicated gene regions reflect the same underlying phylogeny. The 1000 repeat ML tree was performed from the combined sequences obtained using MEGA 7 using Tamura-3 model. The haplotypes of the sequences were determined with the DNASP software, and the median-joining tree was revealed with Network 10.2.

Results

Insecticide bioassays

A total of 11100 Ae. aegypti females were used for the bioassay tests (2220 individuals from each population). All four of the populations evaluated were susceptible or possibly resistant to pyrethroid and organochlorine. The mortality rate ranged from 95.33% to 100%. All populations were susceptible to cyfluthrin, λ-cyhalothrin, and etofenprox. However, three populations (Ardesen, Arhavi, and Hopa) were detected as possibly resistant to permethrin. The population from Pazar was susceptible (Table 2, Supplementary 2). All populations screened were possibly resistant to deltamethrin. All the populations evaluated were possibly resistant to DDT. The lowest mortality rate against dieldrin was observed in the Ardeşen population (mortality rate 96.88%), and the population was classified as possibly resistant, while the others were determined to be susceptible. The Bora Bora laboratory strain was susceptible to all insecticides used (Table 2, Supplementary 2). New mutation type and their positions

Two different intron types were detected in the kdr gene between nucleotide positions 3111 and 3228 and within an intron region (coded as type-1 and type-2) in domain 2. This intron is located between exon 20 and exon 21 and referred to as intron 20 that is immediately adjacent to codons 989, 1011, and 1016 (Fig. 2). Sequence data were analyzed with BLASTn in the NCBI GenBank database. The highest similarity rate was between 98.8–99.2%. Type-1 intron regions were present in each population and closely related to the Indian samples (GenBank accession numbers KM677309 and KM677310). Type-2 was detected in two samples (four samples in total) from the Pazar and Ardesen populations and found to be closely related to samples from Pakistan (GenBank accession number MT707209 and MT707210), Iran (GenBank accession number MZ773475) and India (GenBank accession number KM677327-34).Fig. 2Intron and exon regions and point mutations in vgsc gene of Aedes aegypti from Türkiye

The A1498T mutation was highly associated with the P1611L and P1615S mutations and was found in both intron types. Our results showed the presence of the F1534C kdr mutation in combination with the A1498T, P1611L, and P1615S mutations. Its presence was determined exclusively in type-2, but this combination was only found in one individual of F1534C, and needs to be confirmed with more samples. The R1599C mutation did not demonstrate any connection to other mutation types (Table 3).

Phylogenetic analysis of Ae. aegypti

The ML phylogenetic tree revealed a division of Ae. aegypti into two lineages. The lineages were grouped according to the ML phylogenetic tree into two groups (Type-1 and Type-2). Type-1 represented a large group and included samples from all populations. Type-2 represented a smaller group in comparison to type-1 and included only the Pazar and Ardesen samples (Fig. 3). Thirteen haplotypes were obtained from combined sequences. The H2 and H8 haplotypes were determined as the dominant haplotypes, and haplotype 2 was determined as the ancestral lineage. Discussion

Ae. aegypti is the main vector of many arboviral diseases, including yellow fever, dengue fever, and chikungunya, and is of great public health importance (Ahebwa et al. 2023). Therefore, mosquito control studies have focused on this species in subtropical and tropical countries (Mores et al. 2020). Insecticide resistance, as in many other countries, is a major problem in Türkiye and leads to the ineffectiveness of control measures (Kılıçaslan et al. 2022). However, insecticide resistance studies in Türkiye have focused on other species (Culex sp., Anopheles sp., Aedes albopictus), and there is no existing research on insecticide resistance in Ae. aegypti (Yavaşoglu et al. 2019; Kılıçaslan et al. 2022; Akıner et al. 2024). Therefore, this study holds considerable importance as it represents the first study addressing insecticide resistance in Ae. aegypti in Türkiye. Pyrethroids have been frequently preferred in control and eradication initiatives since the late twentieth century (Nkya et al. 2013). DDT and other organochlorines were frequently used to control and eradicate Ae. aegypti in the years following World War II in Türkiye and many Mediterranean countries (Reiter 2010; Ganushkina et al. 2016). Resistance has been detected to a range of insecticides in all geographical areas where it is present (Jangir and Prasad 2023). In this study, we investigated the potential levels of resistance to pyrethroid and organochlorine, as well as the molecular basis of kdr associated with this resistant status.

Control operations were initiated to eradicate/control Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus ((Skuse 1895) (Diptera: Culicidae)) populations in Türkiye, with pyrethroid being extensively used in the operations over two seasons (Akıner et al. 2018). Bioassay results showed that all populations were susceptible or possibly resistant to pyrethroids and organochlorines, although there was an extensive usage of pyrethroid group insecticides during two seasons. Results suggest that, Ae. aegypti may be in the early stages of developing resistance against pyrethroids. Cyfluthrin and λ-cyhalothrin were used during the control operations, and the populations were found to be susceptible to these insecticides. Although our study results did not support a high level of resistance against pyrethroid and DDT, high levels of resistance were determined in Iranian Ae. aegypti populations (Sanei-Dehkordi et al. 2024). The establishment of Ae. aegypti populations in Iran was reported later than in Türkiye. Given the geographical proximity of the Turkish Iranian border and the spatial closeness of the study area, it is plausible that these two Ae. aegypti populations originate from the same genetic lineage. However, the pyrethroid resistance profiles observed in our study diverge significantly from those previously reported in Iran, implying the potential role of region-specific selective pressures or independent introduction events. Furthermore, there remains a significant knowledge gap regarding insecticide resistance in Ae. aegypti populations from the Caucasus region and the potential invasion points or maritime ports that may serve as entry pathways. Although the presence of pyrethroid-susceptible populations has been reported in Malaysia, Colombia, and Thailand (Thanispong et al. 2008; Ocampo et al. 2011; Leong et al. 2019), the resistance patterns observed in those studies are not fully congruent with our findings in terms of insecticide classes and insecticide types used.

The eastern Black Sea populations of the species exhibit a lower sensitivity to organochlorines than to pyrethroids. This may be due to the control practices in the species’ original areas. Some studies suggest that the Turkish and Caucasian Ae. aegypti populations may be ancient remnants (Kotsakiozi et al. 2018). Historically, organochlorine insecticides were extensively used in the Caucasus region during Soviet-era eradication campaigns targeting Ae. aegypti populations. (Ganushkina et al. 2016). The low susceptibility to DDT and dieldrin, both organochlorine compounds, may be associated with this scenario as the developed resistance can be maintained over time through balancing selection without insecticide pressure (Hedrick 2007). It has been reported that invasive species may experience a serious loss of genetic variability due to the founder effect, and that invasion usually occurs by transporting a few eggs/adults to new sites (Rai 1991; Djiappi-Tchamen et al. 2021; Öztürk and Akıner 2023). Considering the invasive status of Ae. aegypti, the limited variation observed in insecticide resistance can be more plausibly interpreted because of recent colonization events and associated genetic bottlenecks (Öztürk and Akıner 2023). The bioassay results and mutation screening analyses revealed relatively low variance across individuals and populations. While founder events may initially lead to elevated heterozygosity due to admixture or the presence of divergent alleles introduced during colonization, our sequence data revealed a notably low level of heterozygosity. This observation suggests that the population may have undergone a genetic bottleneck followed by a period of demographic and genetic stabilization. Given that Ae. aegypti was first reported in the region in 2015, it is plausible that the species has since established a stable population with reduced genetic variation, potentially due to limited gene flow, directional selection, or drift in a relatively isolated setting.

In the kdr mutation screenings, no mutations were detected in any of the populations (except the Pazar population) in the resistance-related mutation sites (V410L, L982W, S989P, I1011V/M, L1014F, V1016I/G, T1520I, and F1534C). The resistance alleles in 1534 were only present in the Pazar population, and their frequency was relatively low (0.1). These findings suggest that mutations in the kdr gene region likely represent standing genetic variation that exists at low frequencies under natural conditions. Nevertheless, exposure to insecticides exerts strong selection pressure, favoring individuals harboring such mutations and leading to a significant increase in the frequency of resistance alleles over time (Kılıçaslan et al. 2022). This SNP resistance allele is widespread throughout the world and has been reported in Asia, America, Africa, and Europe. The F1534C mutation type was present generally in areas where other mutation types were absent (Fan et al. 2020). Failure to detect this mutation type in the Hopa, Ardeşen, and Arhavi populations may be attributed to limitations in sampling effort or sample size. On the other hand, the lower sensitivity to organochlorine in the adult’s assays may be due to the presence of this mutation in the Pazar population. It should be considered that insecticide resistance is a complex process related to genetic, physiological, behavioural, and ecological factors (Karunaratne et al. 2018).

Our results revealed the presence of four novel mutations (A1498T, R1599C, P1611L, and P1615S) located within domain III of the voltage-gated sodium channel. Although the functional consequences of these mutations remain to be experimentally validated, plausible hypotheses can be proposed based on their domain localization and insights from comparative structural analyses. Notably, the A1498T and R1599C mutations are positioned near the T1520I and F1534C mutations, which have a previously determined relationship with pyrethroid resistance. This close, spatial localisation raises the possibility that these novel variants may similarly influence ion conductance or disrupt insecticide-binding affinity. Previous molecular modeling studies have demonstrated that even conservative amino acid substitutions within critical domains of the sodium channel can markedly alter pyrethroid-receptor interactions (Du et al. 2013; O’Reilly et al. 2006). Furthermore, it is conceivable that these mutations induce conformational alterations in the three-dimensional structure of the channel protein, thereby modulating its interaction with insecticide molecules. Such structural perturbations could underlie an emergent resistance phenotype (Ahoua Alou et al. 2010). Further high-resolution modeling and functional assays will be essential to elucidate the mechanistic role of these novel variants.

Our results also showed two different intron types in the vgsc gene. These types were previously found in Taiwanese and African populations. Type-1 was associated with the S989P, V1016G, and D1763Y kdr mutations, while type-2 was associated with the F1534C kdr mutation (Kawada et al. 2016; Chung et al. 2019). Our results supported this scenario, and we found that the F1534C kdr mutation was associated with intron type 2. Although our results showed two types of introns, the location of the introns differed from previous findings. Kawada et al. (2016) and Chung et al. (2019) reported that introns were located between the S989P and V1016G mutation points. However, our results showed the intron types to be located after V1016G. On the other hand, our sequence data showed high similarity to the determined samples obtained from GenBank (Kawada et al. 2016; Chung et al. 2019).

Both mutation types and these new intron types may be phylogenetically important because type-2 was common along the Indian Ocean coasts of several countries of the Asian continent, as indicated by GenBank: India (KM677307-8, KM677323-34, MW013056.1), Saudi Arabia (KY626192, KY626195, MN997336, MN997342), Nepal (LC485541), Vietnam (MG257775), Malaysia (MK005569-71, MT237395-419), Taiwan (MK495876), Sierra Leone (MK977832-38), Pakistan (MT707210), and Iran (MZ773475.1)). Type-1 has been found worldwide. In previous studies, a specific variation was identified for the ITS2 gene region both in the study area and around it (Shaikevich et al. 2018; Öztürk and Akıner 2023). A network and ML tree revealed the existence of two different lineages belonging to Ae. aegypti in the region. Öztürk and Akıner (2023) indicated two lineages in the phylogenetic study with three different gene regions (ND4, ND5, and COI). Our results with a different gene region related to kdr resistance also supported this situation.

The resistance profile of the Ae. aegypti populations in the Black Sea region do not match the resistance profiles for the local species (Culex pipiens L., 1758 (Diptera: Culicidae) or invasive species (Ae. albopictus) in the region (Kılıçaslan et al. 2022; Pichler et al. 2022). Although the use of agricultural insecticides is officially prohibited in the Eastern Black Sea region in Türkiye, the extent of unauthorized or individual-level insecticide application for either vector control or agricultural purposes remains unclear, except for limited data available from surveillance and control studies in the area (Akıner et al. 2018). The resistance profiles observed in Ae. aegypti populations may be partially shaped by the origin and introduction history of the invasive lineage. As the species is likely of Asian origin, it is possible that the founding populations introduced into the region had not previously been exposed to strong insecticide selection pressures, or that resistance levels reflect genetic bottlenecks associated with the colonization process. Alternatively, current resistance levels may reflect genetic bottlenecks and founder effects associated with the colonization process. Although the patterns revealed by bioassays, kdr mutation screenings, and intron typing suggest an Asian origin, this inference remains inconclusive. Therefore, definitive confirmation of the geographic origin and associated resistance mechanisms necessitates further high-resolution molecular and phylogeographic analyses.
