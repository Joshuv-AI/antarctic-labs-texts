Cervical microbial dysbiosis in single HPV16-positive HSIL
Authors: Mengxin Du, Lingyun Wei, Mengyu Geng, Wenzhen Wang, Lin Wang, Xiaochun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious studies on high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) have typically categorized human papillomavirus (HPV) infection status broadly as positive or negative without distinguishing specific subtypesgenotypes, which may obscure microenvironmental features associated with single HPV16 infection. Therefore, this study characterized the cervical microbiota in 35 single HPV16-positive HSIL women compared to 38 healthy controls using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. The HPV16-positive HSIL group exhibited significant increases in microbial richness and evenness. Compositional alterations included a reduction in Firmicutes and an increase in Fusobacteriota, a decrease in Lactobacillus, and an increase in Sneathia (P < 0.05). Functional prediction revealed significant differences in multiple KEGG pathways and COG functional categories between the two groups (P < 0.05). These findings indicate that single HPV16-positive HSIL is characterized by a dysbiotic microbiota with increased α-diversity, Lactobacillus depletion, and Sneathia enrichment, providing a foundation for exploring these microbes as potential diagnostic biomarkers or therapeutic targets. Introduction

Cervical cancer represents a significant malignant threat to women’s health worldwide. It ranks fourth in incidence and mortality among female malignant tumors globally1,2. Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV)is established as the primary risk factor for the development of both cervical precancerous lesions and invasive cervical cancer3. HPV16 is considered the highest-risk subtype due to its potent carcinogenic potential and the highest prevalence, accounting for approximately 61% of cervical cancers4. The progression from HPV infection to cervical cancer involves a series of precancerous stages, among which high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) represents a critical precancerous condition. Particularly when associated with HPV16, HSIL carries a substantial risk of progression and may develop into invasive cervical cancer if not treated in a timely manner5,6.

In recent years, the association between the cervical microbiota and HPV infection as well as cervical lesions has emerged as a research focus7. Numerous studies have confirmed that cervical microbiota dysbiosis is closely associated with persistent HR-HPV infection and the progression of cervical lesions8. However, most previous studies investigating microbiota alterations related to HPV infection have grouped all HPV genotypes together without distinguishing specific genotypes9–11. Given the significant differences in biological characteristics and carcinogenic risk among various HPV genotypes, this analytical approach has potential limitations. Pooling HPV16, which carries the highest carcinogenic risk, together with other genotypes may dilute the specific microbiota signals associated with HPV16.

Currently, the cervical microenvironment in individuals with HSIL associated with single HPV16 infection remains poorly characterized. To address this research gap, the present study focused on patients with both single HPV16 infection and histopathologically confirmed HSIL, using HPV-negative healthy women as controls. By employing 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, we systematically compared the differences in cervical microbiota diversity, taxonomic composition, and functional predictions between the two groups. This study aimed to characterize the cervical microbial features of the single HPV16-positive HSIL population, with the goal of identifying potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets for early detection and intervention in this high-risk group.

Results

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, this study ultimately enrolled 38 healthy women (Healthy group) and 35 women with single HPV16-positive HSIL (16HSIL group). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age, body mass index (BMI), age at first sexual intercourse, number of sex partners, number of pregnancies, number of deliveries, or smoking habits (Table 1).

The cervical microbial community structure and abundance from the HPV16-positive HSIL and healthy groups

Absence of contamination was confirmed by the negative control gel electrophoresis images (Supplementary Figs. S1, S2). A total of 558 ASVs (19.2%) were shared between the HPV16-positive HSIL and healthy groups. The healthy group contained 891 unique ASVs (30.7%), while the HPV16-positive HSIL group contained 1454 unique ASVs (50.1%) (Fig. 1a). Species abundance and evenness across samples were assessed using rank–abundance curves (Fig. 1b). Notable differences were observed between the healthy and HPV16-positive HSIL groups. The curve for the HPV16-positive HSIL group spanned a wider range along the x-axis, reflecting a greater number of species and higher overall abundance. Additionally, the curve declined gradually, particularly at higher species ranks, indicating the presence of multiple moderately abundant species and a more uniform distribution of community members. In contrast, the healthy group exhibited lower species abundance and reduced evenness.

Diversity of cervical microbiota in the HPV16-positive HSIL and healthy groups

Normalization of sequencing depth across samples is shown in the rarefied ASV table (Supplementary Table S2). The rarefaction curves (Supplementary Figs. S3–S6) plateaued, indicating that the sequencing depth was adequate to cover the microbial diversity. To compare the microbial community richness and evenness between the two groups, we calculated four alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Observed features, Shannon, and Pielou evenness) (Fig. 2). For species richness, the 16HSIL group exhibited a significantly higher Chao1 index (median: 104, IQR: 118.21) compared to the Healthy group (median: 64.9, IQR: 58.75), as determined by the Kruskal-Wallis test (P = 0.024). Consistently, the Observed features count was also significantly greater in the 16HSIL group (median: 82.1, IQR: 97.6) than in the Healthy group (median: 54.95, IQR: 55.05) (P = 0.034).Regarding community evenness and diversity, the Shannon index was significantly higher in the 16HSIL group (median: 1.65, IQR: 1.9) than in the Healthy group (median: 1.3, IQR: 1.46) (P = 0.048). Similarly, the Pielou evenness index confirmed that the 16HSIL group (median: 0.42, IQR: 0.38) possessed significantly greater species evenness than the Healthy group (median: 0.33, IQR: 0.37) (P = 0.043). These results collectively indicate that the 16HSIL group harbors a microbial community with both higher richness and more uniform species distribution compared to the Healthy group.

Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) and Nonmetric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) were conducted to evaluate β-diversity between the two groups. PCoA1 and PCoA2 explained 36.55% and 17.66% of the total variation, respectively (Fig. 3a). The NMDS analysis yielded a stress value of 0.181, indicating good representation of the dissimilarities in low-dimensional space (Fig. 3b). Visualization of both PCoA and NMDS plots revealed substantial overlap between the two groups, suggesting similar overall microbial community structures. To statistically assess the differences in microbial communities, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on Bray-Curtis distances was performed. The analysis revealed no statistically significant difference between the two groups (R²= 0.024, P = 0.013).

Taxonomic distribution of cervical microbial communities at the phylum, genus and species levels

In this study, we systematically compared the taxonomic composition of microbial communities between the Healthy and 16HSIL groups at the phylum, genus, and species levels. At the phylum level, the Firmicutes_D was the most dominant phylum in both groups, followed by Actinobacteriota. Compared to the Healthy group, the relative abundance of Firmicutes_D was significantly lower in the 16HSIL group (81.02% vs. 64.22%, p = 0.035), while the abundance of Fusobacteriota was markedly increased (0.39% vs. 4.30%, P = 0.016, Fig. 4a,d). At the genus level, although Lactobacillus remained the most dominant genus in both groups, its relative abundance was significantly reduced in the 16HSIL group (79.46% vs. 62.05%, P = 0.016). In contrast, the abundance of Sneathia was significantly increased in the 16HSIL group (0.38% vs. 4.10%, P = 0.003, Fig. 4b,e). At the species level, both groups were dominated by Lactobacillus iners and unclassified Lactobacillus. Compared with the Healthy group, the 16HSIL group showed a trend towards increased abundance of Lactobacillus iners, though the difference was not statistically significant (40.99% vs. 43.13%, P = 0.766). The relative abundance of unclassified Lactobacillus decreased significantly in the 16HSIL group (33.78% vs. 14.87%, P<0.01). In contrast, the relative abundance of Sneathia vaginalis, a bacterium associated with dysbiosis, was substantially elevated in the 16HSIL group (0.0047% vs. 2.33%, P<0.01, Fig. 4c,f).

Identification of potential biomarkers for the cervical microbiota

To characterize the microbial composition differences between the 16HSIL and Healthy groups, an Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis was performed. The phylogenetic cladogram (Fig. 5a) visually represents these community differences across phylogenetic levels, with node sizes proportional to taxonomic abundance and connecting lines indicating evolutionary relationships. In our analysis, Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) scores exceeding 3.0 were considered statistically significant. Positive LDA scores indicate taxa enriched in the Healthy group, whereas negative scores signify enrichment in the 16HSIL group (Fig. 5b). The signature phylum for the Healthy group was Firmicutes_D (LDA = 5.91), while that for the 16HSIL group was Fusobacteriota (LDA = 4.63). The signature genus for the Healthy group was Lactobacillus (LDA = 5.90), while that for the 16HSIL group was Sneathia (LDA = 4.61). The signature bacterial species for the Healthy group was unclassified Lactobacillus (LDA = 5.53). The signature bacterial species for the 16HSIL group were Dialistersp001553355 (LDA = 3.63) and Sneathia vaginalis (LDA = 4.37).

LEfSe analysis of cervical microbiota. (a) Cladogram representing the taxonomic hierarchy from phylum to species. The diameter of each node is proportional to the taxon’s relative abundance. (b) Histogram of LDA scores (log10) for taxa with significant differences (p < 0.05, LDA score > 3.0).

Function prediction for cervical microbiota in the HPV16-positive HSIL and healthy groups

To investigate the differences in predicted functional potential of the microbial communities between the HPV16-positive HSIL and Healthy groups, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 6a) and COG functional annotation (Fig. 6b) were performed using PICRUSt2. This predictive analysis revealed significant functional divergence between the two groups. The two groups showed significant differences in several KEGG pathways related to Metabolism, Cellular Processes, and Environmental Information Processing. Specifically, the HPV16-positive HSIL group exhibited significant downregulation in Carbohydrate metabolism, Lipid metabolism and Signal transduction, and exhibited significant enrichment in the Cell growth and death (P < 0.05). In the COG functional analysis, significant differences were observed between the HPV16-positive HSIL group and the Healthy control group across multiple functional categories(P < 0.05). These differences were primarily associated with functions such as DNA-binding transcriptional regulator, ABC-type amino acid transport systems, and Fatty acid-binding protein.

Discussion

This study employed 16S rRNA gene sequencing to investigate the changes in cervical microbiota during critical stages of cervical precancerous lesions. Potential confounding factors related to sex hormones, such as menopausal status, were taken into account, and the study cohort was exclusively composed of reproductive-age female. Diagnoses were confirmed by histopathology rather than cytology. Furthermore, secretions were collected directly from the cervix rather than the vagina to accurately characterize microbial changes at the lesion location.

Our study revealed significant dysbiosis of the cervical microbiota in patients with single HPV16-positive HSIL compared to healthy controls, characterized primarily by increased alpha diversity, decreased relative abundance of Lactobacillus, and significant enrichment of the genus Sneathia. Functional prediction suggested a downregulation of Carbohydrate metabolism pathways and an upregulation of pathways related to Cell growth and death. These findings provide novel insights into the cervical microenvironment associated with HPV16-positive HSIL. In this study, 16HSIL group exhibited significantly increased alpha diversity, but no significant difference in beta diversity. This result may suggest that while the overall structure of the microbial community is similar between the two groups, the internal composition has shifted, characterized by diminished Lactobacillus dominance and an increased proportion of various low-abundance species, leading to higher species richness and evenness. This state of partial dysbiosis might be a characteristic microbial feature of the HSIL stage.

As the core microbiota of the cervix, Lactobacillus exerts protective effects by producing lactic acid, maintaining an acidic environment, and generating antimicrobial peptides15,16. A depletion of Lactobacillus in the cervicovaginal microbiota is associated with elevated risks of persistent HR-HPV infection and progression to cervical cancer17. In this study, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus in the 16HSIL group was significantly lower than that in the Healthy group, which is consistent with previous studies18–20. However, it is noteworthy that Lactobacillus remained the most dominant genus even in the 16HSIL group. This finding suggests that in the HSIL stage, the overall structure of the microbial community has not yet undergone complete restructuring, but rather exists in a state of partial dysbiosis characterized by diminished Lactobacillus dominance and the initial proliferation of opportunistic pathogens. This may represent an intermediate stage in the evolution of the cervical microbiota from a healthy state to a cancerous state.

At the species level, Lactobacillus iners showed an increasing trend in 16HSIL group, although this did not reach statistical significance. L. iners produces only L-lactic acid, rather than the more protective D-lactic acid, and is unable to produce hydrogen peroxide, making it difficult to maintain a low pH and an antimicrobial environment. Therefore, a vaginal microbiota dominated by L. iners is less stable and more susceptible to dysbiosis21. This study did not assess vaginal pH or local inflammatory cytokine levels, so it remains unclear whether these factors are associated with changes in the abundance of L. iners and its role in HPV16-positive HSIL. Future studies could incorporate clinical and immunological parameters to further explore these associations.

In our study, Sneathia was identified as the only opportunistic pathogenic genus with significantly increased relative abundance in the HPV16-positive HSIL group, with Sneathia vaginalis being the primary contributing species. This finding stands in contrast to previous studies that grouped all HPV infections together, which generally reported an increase in multiple potential pathogens such as Gardnerella, Prevotella, Atopobium, and Sneathia in HSIL patients18,22–24. This discrepancy suggests that the enrichment of Sneathia may represent a specific microbial characteristic in the progression of HPV16 infection to high-grade lesions. Previous studies have confirmed that Sneathia can induce cervical epithelial cells to produce pro-inflammatory cytokines and the oncogenic metabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG), which promotes genomic instability by disrupting epigenetic regulation25. Bridy et al. found that HPV16-positive cervical epithelial cells exhibit greater resistance to the cytotoxicity of Sneathia and support its prolonged survival and proliferation26. This suggests that HPV16 may provide a microenvironment conducive to the persistent colonization of Sneathia, indicating a potential ecological synergy between the two. However, this speculation requires further experimental validation. In our study, PICRUSt2 functional prediction revealed that Carbohydrate metabolism pathways were significantly downregulated in 16HSIL group, which may be related to the metabolic characteristics of Sneathia. This bacterial genus lacks a complete phosphotransferase system (PTS) and multiple polysaccharide-degrading enzymes, resulting in a limited capacity for carbohydrate utilization27. However, these findings require further validation through metagenomic or metabolomic approaches. Notably, the association between Sneathia and HPV infection and cervical lesions is not unique to Asian populations. The enrichment of Sneathia in the cervical-vaginal swabs of patients with HPV positivity, CIN, or cervical cancer has been reported in multiple studies across diverse geographical regions26,28,29. This suggests that the link between Sneathia and HPV infection and cervical lesions may be a cross-ethnic phenomenon. However, its abundance and clinical significance may be modulated by genetic background, environmental factors, and overall microbial community structure. Future multicenter and cross-ethnic studies are warranted to further elucidate its role in HPV-related cervical lesions.

This study provides a foundation for the development of cervical microbiota-based biomarkers or therapeutic targets for HSIL, yet certain limitations exist. First, the lack of an HPV16-positive non-HSIL control group makes it impossible to distinguish whether the observed microbiota alterations are attributable to HPV16 infection itself or associated with HSIL lesions. Future studies should include a control population of HPV16-positive individuals with normal histology or low-grade lesions. Second, multiple comparisons were not corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) in the screening of differential microbiota, posing a risk of false-positive findings. Although LEfSe analysis (LDA > 3.0) was employed in this study to enhance the robustness of the results, the positive findings concerning certain low-abundance taxa should still be interpreted with caution and validated in future studies with larger sample sizes using more stringent statistical correction methods. Third, no a priori power analysis was conducted. Due to the relatively small sample size, this study may have limited statistical power to detect differences in β-diversity and low-abundance taxa between groups. We will expand the sample size in future studies to further validate the current findings.

Conclusion

This study revealed significant alterations in the cervical microbiota structure of patients with single HPV16-positive HSIL, characterized by increased α-diversity, reduced abundance of Lactobacillus, and a marked enrichment of Sneathia.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

This study recruited women of reproductive age who attended the gynaecology outpatient department or underwent routine health examinations at the physical examination center of Shanxi Bethune Hospital between November 2024 and May 2025. All participants underwent HPV genotyping and cervical thinprep cytologic test (TCT) before enrollment. HPV genotyping (fluorescence PCR method) simultaneously detected and distinguished 21 HPV genotypes, including 15 high-risk types (HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68) and 6 low-risk types (HPV 6, 11, 42, 43, 44, 81). Women who tested positive for HPV16 additionally underwent cervical biopsy, and the decision to proceed with cervical conization was based on the biopsy results. Eligible participants were consecutively enrolled and divided into two groups based on their test results and subsequent pathological diagnoses:

Healthy group: negative for all 21 tested HPV genotypes, with no intraepithelial lesions or malignancy on TCT.

16HSIL group: single HPV16-positive and histopathologically confirmed as HSIL by colposcopy-directed cervical biopsy.

Cervical secretion samples from women in the 16HSIL group were collected following colposcopy-directed biopsy confirmation of HSIL, prior to any other treatment. All women with HPV16-positive HSIL underwent cervical conization in accordance with standard diagnostic and treatment guidelines. Based on postoperative pathological findings, those with positive margins, lesions upgraded to invasive carcinoma, or pathological downgrading (less than HSIL) were excluded. Ultimately, only participants whose postoperative pathology still indicated HSIL with negative margins were included in the analysis (Fig. 7).

Flow diagram of this study.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Aged between 25 and 45 years, (2)Having sexual experience, (3)Not using hormonal contraceptives, (4)Non-pregnant, non-lactating, and premenopausal.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Use of antibiotics, interferon or probiotics within the past month or vaginal intercourse or vaginal lavage within 72 h, (2) History of vaginitis, urinary tract infections, or sexually transmitted diseases (chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomoniasis, syphilis, or genital herpes), (3) Previous treatment history of cervical lesions or hysterectomy, (4) History of previous HPV infection or anti-HPV treatment, (5) History of hepatitis, (6) HIV positivity, (7) Systemic diseases (diabetes, autoimmune disorders or malignancies), (8) Previous HPV vaccination.

All subjects provided written informed consent voluntarily. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Bethune Hospital and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (approval number YXLL-2025-226).

All participants were required to complete a questionnaire, including age, body mass index (BMI), age at sexual debut, number of sex partners, gravidity, parity, and smoking habits. Sample collection was performed uniformly by the same gynecologist. Sampling was performed during the mid-follicular phase of the participants’ menstrual cycle. Participants were positioned in the lithotomy position. Using a sterile vaginal speculum to expose the cervix, a disposable swab was gently rotated within the cervical canal at least 10 times. Once the disposable swab has fully absorbed the cervical mucus, remove it carefully to avoid touching the vaginal wall. The disposable swab was then immediately placed into a sterile centrifuge tube containing Amies transport medium (Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd.) and transported to the laboratory on ice and stored at -80℃ until extraction.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted from all samples using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (D5635-02) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). The size of the DNA fragments was determined by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration was assessed with the Nanodrop 2000 (ThermoFisherScientific, Inc., USA). PCR amplification of the V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was performed using universal primers 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). The PCR amplification was performed under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 98℃ for 5 min; 28 cycles of denaturation at 98℃ for 30 s, annealing at 53℃ for 30 s, and extension at 72℃ for 45 s; followed by a final extension at 72℃ for 5 min. Negative controls were included during amplification to monitor for contamination.

Following confirmation of successful amplification by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, the products were purified with the Agencourt AMPure XP nucleic acid purification kit (Beckman Coulter, Inc., USA). Libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Inc., USA). The amplicon libraries were detected for fragment size distribution and quantified using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Inc., USA) and the ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Inc., USA), respectively. Finally, DNA libraries with concentrations above 2 nM were considered qualified and underwent paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., USA) by Shanghai Biotree Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Bioinformatics analysis

After removing primer sequences from the raw sequencing data using Cutadapt (v4.9), the DADA2 (v1.16) pipeline was employed for filtering, denoising, merging, and chimera removal to obtain amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and their abundance profiles30. Following quality control, a total of 4,729,154 high-quality sequences were obtained from 73 samples, with an average sequencing depth of 64,784 ± 16,428 reads per sample (range: 41,260–98,269 reads). To ensure comparability across samples, rarefaction was applied to normalize all samples to the minimum sequence count (41,260 reads per sample). For details of the sequencing depth for each sample, please refer to the Supplementary Table 1. The rarefied ASV table was used for subsequent analyses. Taxonomic annotation is based on the Greengenes2 database (Release 2024.09). This database employs the nomenclature system of the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB).

Within-sample microbial community richness and evenness were assessed using the Chao1, Observed features, Shannon, and Pielou evenness indices, with group differences evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on the Bray-Curtis distance matrix were performed to evaluate compositional differences between samples. PERMANOVA was used to test the significance of differences between groups. Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was employed to identify differentially abundant taxa between the two groups. Briefly, the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was first used to detect taxa with significant differences between groups, followed by linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to estimate the effect size. Taxa with LDA scores > 3.0 and P < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Functional prediction analysis was performed using PICRUSt2 software based on the Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) database and the Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) databases, and the Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare differences in potential microbial community functions between the two groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of general clinical data was performed using SPSS 25.0. Continuous variables were first tested for normality. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between two groups using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared between two groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described as frequencies (percentages) and compared between groups using the chi-square test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
