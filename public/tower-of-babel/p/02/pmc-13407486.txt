Double Gallbladder with Intrahepatic Location: A Rare Anomaly Managed by Combined Laparoscopic and Open Cholecystectomy
Authors: Kethineni Uma Maheswara Rao, Rahul Bharti Saxena, Radhika Narayan, Md Zahid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGallbladder duplication is an uncommon congenital anomaly (1 out of 4,000 autopsies) that poses significant diagnostic and surgical challenges. In the present case, a 70-year-old female presented with right hypochondrial pain, dyspepsia, and nausea for one month. Ultrasound revealed a bifid gallbladder with calculi in one pouch and a normal common bile duct. Intraoperatively, two separate gallbladders were found, one positioned normally and the other intrahepatic. Laparoscopic excision of the first was completed, but conversion to open surgery was required because of the unclear anatomy and the intrahepatic location of the second. Both specimens were confirmed histologically as gallbladders with chronic cholecystitis. Recovery was uneventful, and the patient remained symptom-free at the six-month follow-up. Thus, gallbladder duplication, especially with intrahepatic positioning, can complicate a laparoscopic dissection. Preoperative imaging and intraoperative vigilance are crucial to avoiding bile duct injury.Keywords: Gallbladder, Congenital abnormalities, Cholecystitis, Laparoscopic cholecystectomy, Case reports

INTRODUCTION

Gallbladder duplication is an uncommon congenital anomaly with an estimated incidence of 1 in 4,000 individuals.1,2 The anomaly was first described by Blasius in 1674 and later systematically classified by Boyden in 1926.1 Harlaftis et al.2 then refined the classification into three types: (I) split primordium group (septated, V-shaped, and Y-shaped), (II) true duplication with separate cystic ducts (H-shaped and trabecular variants), and (III) triple gallbladder.2,3

Most duplicated gallbladders remain asymptomatic and are incidentally diagnosed. On the other hand, they may predispose to gallstone disease, chronic cholecystitis, or rarely carcinoma, and they can significantly increase the risk of bile duct injury during cholecystectomy.4-6 Preoperative imaging with ultrasound often fails to establish the diagnosis, whereas Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) and computed tomography (CT) cholangiography provide better anatomical delineation.7,8 Nevertheless, duplication is only confirmed intraoperatively in many reported cases.5,6

The present case is unique in demonstrating true duplication of the gallbladder, with one gallbladder located intrahepatically, draining via a small posterior duct directly into the liver. Intrahepatic duplicated gallbladders are extremely rare, and surgical management is technically challenging. This case underscores the importance of intraoperative vigilance, readiness to convert from laparoscopic to open surgery when the anatomy is unclear, and the necessity of removing both gallbladders to prevent recurrence. This paper adds to the limited pool of evidence on gallbladder duplication, highlights the pitfalls in preoperative diagnosis, and emphasizes strategies to avoid major bile duct injury.

CASE

A 70-year-old woman presented with complaints of pain in the right hypochondrium, dyspepsia, and nausea for one month. She had no history of fever, vomiting, or jaundice. She reported a similar episode six months prior. The patient had no comorbidities such as diabetes or hypertension.

On examination, she was hemodynamically stable with a pulse of 94/min and blood pressure of 132/86 mmHg. No pallor, icterus, hepatosplenomegaly, or ascites were noted. An abdominal examination revealed a soft, non-tender abdomen, and the hernial orifices were intact.

1. Investigations

Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count and liver function tests, were within the normal limits (total bilirubin, 0.5 mg/dL; direct bilirubin, 0.1 mg/dL; indirect bilirubin, 0.4 mg/dL; aspartate aminotransferase [AST], 32.8 U/L; alanine aminotransferase [ALT], 24 U/L; alkaline phosphatase [ALP], 85.2 U/L; white blood cell [WBC] count, 8.7×109/L; hemoglobin, 11.8 g/dL; platelet count, 413×109 /L; red blood cell [RBC] count, 4.6×106/μL; hematocrit [HCT], 37.4%; mean corpuscular volume [MCV], 81 fL; mean corpuscular hemoglobin [MCH], 25.5 pg; mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration [MCHC], 31.6 g/dL). Ultrasound of the abdomen revealed a bifid gallbladder with calculi in one pouch and a normal common bile duct.

2. Management

The patient was planned for a laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Intraoperatively, two completely separated gallbladders were identified. The normally placed gallbladder was dissected and removed laparoscopically (Fig. 1A, 1B).

(B) Intraoperative image of duplicated gallbladders. The white arrow indicates the dissected bed of the normal gallbladder. While attempting to dissect the intrahepatic gallbladder, the cystic duct could not be clearly delineated because of its intrahepatic location. Accidental opening of the gallbladder fundus released mucus and calculi (Fig. 2). Conversion to open surgery was performed considering the unclear Calot’s anatomy and the risk of bile duct injury. Intraoperatively, a posterior communication draining into the liver directly via a small bile duct was identified. An open cholecystectomy was performed safely.

Intraoperative image of the intrahepatic gallbladder. The white arrow highlights multiple calculi within the lumen.

A 32F drain was placed in the subhepatic space. Postoperatively, the drain output was bile-stained serous fluid for three days, after which it ceased. The drain was removed, and the patient was discharged in stable condition.

3. Follow-up

At 15 days, she was asymptomatic with good wound healing. On the six-month follow-up, the patient remained healthy without any recurrence of symptoms.

4. Histopathology

Both specimens confirmed gallbladder tissue with all layers intact and features of chronic cholecystitis (Fig. 3A, 3B).

Histopathological section of (A) the intrahepatic gallbladder showing intact mucosal lining with features of chronic cholecystitis (H&E stain, magnification ×100), (B) the normally positioned gallbladder showing mild chronic inflammation and pyloric metaplasia (H&E stain, magnification ×400).

DISCUSSION

Gallbladder duplication poses unique diagnostic and operative challenges. Its incidence is estimated at 1 in 4,000.1,2 Embryologically, it results from abnormal budding of the hepatic diverticulum.4 The Boyden and Harlaftis classifications remain the most widely applied.1-3

A preoperative diagnosis remains difficult. Ultrasound may suggest duplication, but it is often insufficient to clarify the ductal anatomy.7,8 In the present case, both gallbladders were closely adherent to each other, and the normally positioned gallbladder had to be separated during surgery to confirm that the structure represented a true double gallbladder rather than a bifid fundus. This close anatomical relationship likely contributed to the difficulty in detecting the anomaly on preoperative ultrasonography. The duplicated structure may appear as a single gallbladder or be mistaken for a septate lumen when both gallbladders are in close proximity or share adhesions. Hence, even with meticulous scanning, ultrasonography may occasionally fail to identify such variants, underscoring the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and considering intraoperative anatomical reassessment when findings are atypical.

MRCP is considered the gold standard for precisely delineating cystic ducts and their drainage. At the authors’ institution, preoperative MRCP or CT cholangiography is selectively performed in patients with atypical ultrasonographic findings, suspected choledocholithiasis, or prior biliary interventions. In this case, the initial ultrasound suggested a bifid gallbladder with a normal common bile duct, and there were no biochemical or clinical indicators of ductal obstruction. Hence, an MRCP was not obtained preoperatively. MRCP could have provided better anatomical delineation and increased the educational value of this report. On the other hand, the absence of such imaging did not adversely affect the surgical outcome, and the intrahepatic position and anomalous drainage were identified only during surgery. In previously reported cases (Table 1),3,5,8-20 most duplications were diagnosed preoperatively with MRCP, CT, or enhanced intraoperative imaging such as indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence.9,12,14,18 In some cases, however, the anomaly was detected intraoperatively,3,8,10,15 underscoring the importance of vigilance when encountering atypical anatomy.

The main risk in duplicated gallbladders is iatrogenic bile duct injury. Achieving the critical view of safety is essential, but this may be practically challenging in intrahepatic or trabecular variants.11 In such cases, adjuncts like IOC or ICG can clarify the anatomy. Several reports highlight the value of intraoperative cholangiography (IOC) in reducing the risk of ductal injury.5,12,14,16,19 ICG imaging has gained popularity in recent years, particularly in robotic procedures.12,14,18

The literature reports that laparoscopic cholecystectomy was feasible in most cases.3,5,8,9,12,13,16,19 Robotic approaches were also successful in selected patients.12,14,18,20 Nevertheless, conversion to open cholecystectomy was required in difficult cases, especially with intrahepatic gallbladders or unclear anatomy.10,15,20 These findings were consistent with this case, where conversion to open surgery was essential for a safe dissection.

Most duplicated gallbladders exhibit benign pathology such as chronic cholecystitis or gallstones.5,6 On the other hand, reports of different pathologies in each gallbladder exist,6 reinforcing the recommendation to excise both gallbladders even if only one appears diseased.12,16,19 Rare instances of carcinoma arising within one of the duplicated gallbladders have been reported, supporting the recommendation to excise both gallbladders even if only one appears diseased.21,22

This case contributes to the limited literature on gallbladder duplication, with the unique feature being an intrahepatic gallbladder. The strength of this report lies in its detailed intraoperative description, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and the surgical decision-making that prioritized patient safety. Histopathological confirmation of chronic cholecystitis in both specimens further strengthens the validity of the diagnosis. As a single case, the generalizability of these findings is limited. The absence of advanced preoperative imaging, such as MRCP or CT cholangiography, restricted detailed anatomical delineation, and the relatively short follow-up period prevented an assessment of the long-term outcomes.

Based on this experience, it is recommended that surgeons maintain a high index of suspicion for a duplicated gallbladder when atypical biliary anatomy is encountered on imaging or intraoperatively. Preoperative MRCP or CT should be considered whenever feasible to define the anatomy. Intraoperative adjuncts such as cholangiography or indocyanine green fluorescence can help prevent bile duct injury. Surgeons should maintain a low threshold for conversion to open surgery when the anatomy is unclear. Finally, both gallbladders should be excised, even if only one appears diseased, to prevent recurrence and exclude the possibility of a malignant transformation.

Gallbladder duplication, especially with an intrahepatic component, is a rare but surgically significant anomaly. Preoperative imaging may not always establish the diagnosis, and intraoperative vigilance is critical. Excision of both gallbladders and readiness to convert to open surgery remain the key strategies to prevent bile duct injury and ensure safe outcomes.
