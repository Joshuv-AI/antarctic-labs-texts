Ovarian Fibrosis Through Comparative Analysis of Collagen Architecture
Authors: Caroline J Aitken, Lanna Kadhim, Alyssa Murray, David A Landry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOvarian fibrosis is a known pathology of reproductive aging, becoming a growing concern for infertility and complex ovarian diseases. In research, mouse and human ovary samples are utilized, though distinct differences between species warrant validation of architectural phenotypes to accurately define its pathology. Using picrosirius red staining under polarized light microscopy, this study characterize fibrillar collagen distribution and organization across defined ovarian regions, specifically the cortex and medulla, in aging human ovary and the equivalent region in the aging mouse ovary. Our results reveal cortex‐specific collagen accumulation with anisotropic organization in aging human ovaries, in contrast to the diffuse, isotropic collagen deposition observed in the whole ovary of aging mice. Our findings support age‐associated ovarian fibrosis as the accumulation and/or cortical anisotropic organization of fibrillar collagen within the ovary.Keywords: collagen, comparative histology, extracellular matrix, ovarian fibrosis

Introduction

Fibrosis is the excessive buildup of collagenous extracellular matrix (ECM), which can progressively cause significant scar tissue and loss of organ function (Henderson et al. 2020). Ovaries are particularly susceptible to fibrosis due to aging and subsequent inflammation (Wu et al. 2022), causing immune cell shifts and myofibroblast activation (Landry et al. 2022). Since ovarian fibrosis entails damage to ovarian tissue structure and function, its implications with infertility and other ovarian diseases are a growing concern (Landry et al. 2020). Additionally, compelling evidence suggests that ovarian fibrosis contributes to ovarian cancer permissiveness and colonization, providing an optimal metastatic niche (Fujimoto et al. 2024). With few mechanistic studies on ovarian fibrosis, current research utilizes mouse models and human ovary samples to understand its pathology.

Mouse models are valuable for studying fibrosis as they recapitulate the dynamic ECM structures and cellular communications involved (Padmanabhan et al. 2019). However, given the distinct structural and cyclical differences between mouse and human gonads, comparisons between histological phenotypes in mouse and human fibrosis require further investigation. Since fibrosis research relies heavily on mouse models, human comparisons are crucial for adequate interpretation of research findings and translational relevance (Padmanabhan et al. 2019). This report aims to delineate species‐specific phenotypes of ovarian fibrosis and highlight differences that will assist further research in this field.

Results

Fibrillar collagen thickness, accumulation, and organization were assessed in pre‐ and postmenopausal human ovaries at two defined depths: the cortex (50 µm below the ovarian surface epithelium, OSE) and the medulla (1 mm below the OSE), and in spatially analogous sub‐epithelial and inner regions of young and aged mouse ovaries (Figure 1A). Collagen architectural were assessed using Picrosirius Red (PSR) staining under polarized light microscopy (POL‐PSR), which enables visualization of fiber thickness and organization: thick, tightly packed fibers appear orange to red, whereas thin, loosely organized fibers appear green to yellow (Dayan et al. 1989). POL‐PSR analysis revealed an age‐associated increase in fibrillar collagen deposition in both species (Figure 1B), characterized by predominantly thin fibers in mice and thicker fibers in humans (Figure 1C). In aged mice, collagen accumulation increased significantly across all ovarian regions, whereas in humans, collagen accumulation was restricted to the cortex of postmenopausal ovaries, with no change observed in the medulla.

By examining fibrillar collagen organization, we identified anisotropic (aligned) collagen restricted to the cortical region of postmenopausal human ovaries (Figure 2). Differences in collagen organization were visualized using gray‐scaled POL‐PSR images and quantified using orientation histograms (Figure 2A,B) and coherency indices between regions (Figure 2C–F). In mice, age‐associated ovarian fibrosis did not result in anisotropic organization in any region. In contrast, human ovaries exhibited significantly higher cortical anisotropy compared to the medulla in postmenopausal women, whereas premenopausal ovaries displayed lower cortical anisotropy relative to the medulla (Figure 2F). While medullary collagen coherency remained unchanged with menopausal status, cortical collagen coherency was significantly increased in postmenopausal compared with premenopausal women.

(B) Orientation histograms showing distribution of collagen fiber angles (pronounced peaks denote dominant alignment directions, while flatter distribution indicates isotropic organization). Fiber coherency visualized and quantified by measuring the saturation of local gradients in mouse (C) and human (E) ovaries. High saturation (values near 1.0) indicates parallel fiber alignment, whereas low saturation (values near 0.0) indicates perpendicular or disorganized fibers. Bar graphs represent the comparison of coherency measurements within the ovarian cortex and medulla or their equivalent regions in mice (D) and humans (F) (n = 3). Data are means ± SEM. Statistical differences were assessed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001). Scale bar 200 µm.

Discussion

PSR staining under polarized light provides a robust and reliable representation of the fibrillar collagen matrix (Junqueira et al. 1979; Landry et al. 2022). While birefringence colors have historically been interpreted as markers of collagen type (Junqueira et al. 1979), accumulating evidence indicates that PSR‐POL signals primarily reflect collagen fiber thickness and packing rather than molecular composition (López De Padilla et al. 2021), supporting its use as a sensitive and efficient tool to assess collagen architecture.

Collagen accumulation and fibrillar architecture differed substantially between species, with humans showing cortex‐restricted, thicker fibers and mice displaying a diffuse distribution of thinner fibers. This pattern is consistent with prior reports of ovarian cortex‐dominant fibrosis in humans (McCloskey et al. 2020) and the more generalized stromal fibrosis in mouse ovaries (Amargant et al. 2020). This cortical confinement of thicker collagen fibers has also been reported in ovarian biopsies from pregnant women, showing increased fiber thickness in the cortex compared with the cortico‐medullary junction (Grosbois et al. 2023). These differences likely reflect species‐specific regulation of collagen organization, consistent with distinct collagen networks reported between mice and humans (Wang et al. 2015).

Beyond collagen abundance, only human postmenopausal ovaries exhibited increased anisotropic cortical collagen organization. This finding aligns with prior reports describing highly anisotropic cortical collagen following menopause using second harmonic generation microscopy (McCloskey et al. 2020) and digital collagen analysis platforms (Vaishnav et al. 2025), which did not examine medullary organization.

Ovarian fibrosis likely differs between mice and humans because of differences in ovulation frequency, cortical cellularity, immune environments, and the duration of reproductive aging (Park et al. 2021). While mouse models recapitulate key aspects of ovarian aging (Marchante et al. 2023) and core pathway of fibrosis (Martínez et al. 2014; Walkin et al. 2013), their spatial presentation remains distinct. Frequent ovarian cycles, multiple ovulations, rapid stromal repair, and lower stromal density in mice likely contribute to the diffuse collagen pattern. Whereas infrequent ovulation, prolonged exposure to postmenopausal hormonal changes, and higher baseline cortical collagen in humans favor a cortex‐specific anisotropic organization. These species‑specific patterns are consistent with broader across fibrosis models in humans and mice.

Importantly, several mouse studies demonstrate that metabolic conditions such as obesity accelerate ovarian fibrosis (Umehara et al. 2022), raising parallels with obesity‑associated ovarian dysfunction in women, but without clear evidence of cortical anisotropy in humans. Likewise, PCOS and premature ovarian insufficiency (Çakır Gündoğdu et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2026), conditions associated with ovarian fibrosis in both humans and mouse models, underscore conserved fibrotic responses across species similar to aging, although their spatial organization within the ovary has not been systematically compared. These comparisons highlight an important translational question: to what extent do mouse models accurately reflect human ovarian fibrosis, particularly regarding regional collagen architecture? By directly visualizing structural differences between species, our findings help refine how mouse models should be interpreted when used to study human ovarian fibrosis.

Conclusion

This study provides comparative evidence of regional differences in fibrillar collagen accumulation and organization between mouse and human ovaries with aging. While mouse models provide valuable mechanistic insight, their limitations must be recognized when extrapolating to human ovarian fibrosis, particularly with respect to collagen subtype regulation and matrix organization during aging. Future studies integrating comparative proteomic and extracellular matrix analyses of mouse and human ovaries will be critical to identify specific collagen subtypes, structural ECM components, and molecular pathways involved, thereby clarifying the origin and evolution of age‐associated ovarian fibrosis. Such comparative approaches will also be essential for interpreting findings from experimental mouse models and assessing potential therapeutic intervention strategies.

Materials and Methods

This study was performed under a protocol approved by the Animal Care Committee of the University of Ottawa and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care. Mice were housed under controlled environmental conditions with 12‐h alternating light/dark cycles, with free access to water and food. Young (6 months old) and aged (18 months old) female C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. Mice were euthanized based on age, and ovaries were collected for histological analysis. Human ovarian tissues from three premenopausal (age 35–45) and three postmenopausal (age 55–60) women were obtained in accordance with the Ottawa Health Science Network Research Ethics Board protocol 20180168‐01H and generously donated by Dr. Barbara Vanderhyden (Vaishnav et al. 2025). For both mouse and human ovaries, three biological samples per group (n = 3) were analyzed. One representative histological section per ovary was selected for analysis based on established regional collagen patterns previously validated in larger datasets (McCloskey et al. 2020; Landry et al. 2022).

Histopathologic assessment of ovaries was performed using 5 μm sections of formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissue. Slides were stained with PSR and were imaged at 20× under bright‐field (PSR) and polarized light (POL‐PSR) using a Zeiss AXIO Imager M2 with a linear polarizer (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany; CBIA core Facility at the University of Ottawa).

Picrosirius Red selectively binds fibrillar collagens, and under polarized light produces birefringence proportional to collagen fiber thickness, packing, and alignment. Thus, PSR‑POL provides a semi‑quantitative assessment of collagen architecture rather than collagen subtype composition. For fibrillar collagen quantification, polarized images were analyzed using FIJI (ImageJ). We used our previously published protocol (Landry et al. 2022) to determine the value of each hue pixel to obtain a histogram of hue frequency and saturation from the resolved 8‐bit images. We used the following hue ranges: red 2–9 and 230–256, orange 10–38, yellow 39–51, and green 52–128. The hue between 129 and 229 consists of non‐birefringent tissue and is labeled as no collagen. The number of pixels within each hue range was determined and expressed as a percentage of the total image area in pixels. For each section, a 200 × 200 µm ROI was analyzed to enable direct architectural comparison between species. For orientation and coherence measurements, images were analyzed using the OrientationJ plugin to generate a coherency map and calculate the directional coherency coefficient (Fonck et al. 2009). The coherency coefficient reflects the degree of local collagen fiber alignment, with higher values indicating increased anisotropic organization.
