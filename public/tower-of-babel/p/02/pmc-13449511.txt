The influence of interpersonal competence on smartphone addiction: the moderating role of football participation
Authors: Chen Zhong, Huarui Huang, Yi Zheng, Ning Ma, Yizhou Shui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmartphone addiction poses a significant threat to adolescent mental health. According to the Compensatory Internet Use Theory and the Stress Buffering Hypothesis, this study investigated whether sports participation moderates the relationship between interpersonal competence and smartphone addiction, and whether this effect differs between football and individual sports.MethodsA total of 2,435 primary and secondary school students in China participated in this survey. Data were collected using the Interpersonal Competence Questionnaire, the Athlete Participation Scale, and the Smartphone Addiction Scale. Correlation and moderation analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 and PROCESS 4.2 (Model 1 and 3).ResultsInterpersonal competence was significantly negatively correlated with smartphone addiction (r = -0.192, p < 0.001). While no significant overall moderating effect of sports participation was observed (b = -0.027, p = 0.152), a three-way interaction analysis showed that a significant moderating effect of sports participation level emerged specifically among students participating in football (b = -0.059, p = 0.007). This moderating effect was not significant for participation in individual sports (b = 0.065, p = 0.118).ConclusionInterpersonal competence negatively predicts smartphone addiction, and football participation uniquely moderates this relationship, with the moderating effect strengthening as participation levels increases. These findings suggest that interventions should prioritize socially interactive team sports, such as football, to address adolescents’ social deficits and reduce addiction risks.Keywords: Interpersonal competence, Smartphone addiction, Football participation, Moderating effect

Background

By December 2024, the number of smartphone internet users in China had reached 1.105 billion, with adolescents accounting for 16.7% of this population [1]. Adolescence is a period characterized by immature psychological development and limited self-discipline. If adolescents engage in inappropriate smartphone use during this stage and become absorbed in the online world, they are at high risk of developing smartphone addiction [2]. Moreover, smartphone addiction has been shown to correlate significantly with negative psychological outcomes, including reduced sleep quality [3, 4], depression [3, 5] and heightened anxiety levels [6].

Interpersonal competence, a key ability enabling individuals to establish and maintain healthy social relationships, play a significant role in maintaining mental health [7], particularly during the critical period of adolescent socio-emotional development [8–10]. The Compensatory Internet Use Theory explains a certain behavior. Negative life situations can trigger motivation that leads individuals to browse the internet [11]. Interpersonal competence deficits, as a form of negative social situation, may lead adolescents to rely on smartphones for online social interaction due to the immediacy and anonymity, ultimately resulting in smartphone addiction [11, 12]. Previous studies have confirmed that the interpersonal competence of adolescents is related to their levels of smartphone addiction [13]. Specifically, individuals with weaker interpersonal competence tend to depend more heavily on virtual social interactions, thereby increasing the risk of addiction [14–16].

According to the Stress Buffering Hypothesis, supportive resources can buffer stress-related health outcomes. They operate by changing how individuals interpret stressors and by supplying supplementary coping strategies [17]. Sports participation is an important positive behavioral resource and an external support for stress responses [18]. The social connections and sense of group belonging that adolescents gain through sports can help alleviate interpersonal stress [19], and to some extent, reduce the perceived threat of interpersonal predicaments caused by insufficient interpersonal competence [20], thereby preventing smartphone addiction that results from a lack of real-world social interaction [21]. A regular exercise habit may lead adolescents to view sports as an alternative to smartphones for venting interpersonal conflicts [22]. Furthermore, the real-world social networks formed through sports reduce the need for dependence on virtual social interaction [23], exerting a positive moderating effect on digital media use behaviors, and consequently lowering the potential risk of smartphone dependence [24].

Team sports offer adolescents great social opportunities compared to individual sports, enabling them to acquire more supportive resources [25, 26]. Among team sports, football stands out for its high level of cooperation, rich social interaction, and widespread popularity among adolescents [27], and has emerged as an important activity influencing adolescents’ distal psychological development outcomes [28]. However, little research exists on how football participation varies in its impact on adolescents’ interpersonal competence and smartphone addiction. Although existing studies have compared the differential effects of football versus individual sports on smartphone addiction [29], they have not yet further examined interpersonal competence, a core variable closely related to football participation. The essential characteristic that distinguishes football from individual sports lies in its high degree of interpersonal collaboration, rather than merely physical energy consumption or exercise skill practice [26]. Therefore, if football shows a unique moderating effect on smartphone addiction compared to individual sports, this effect must be achieved through the key variable of interpersonal ability. The collaborative platform established by football creates intensive peer interaction opportunities for adolescents, significantly enhancing their social skills and group integration abilities [30]. It effectively alleviates the social pressures adolescents face in real life, redirecting their attention away from the virtual online world toward active encouragement in real-world activities [31–33]. Therefore, compared with individual sports, football can provide more social support resources, more effectively buffer adverse distal psychological development outcomes, and thus exert a stronger mitigating effect on smartphone addiction [29].

Previous studies have shown that sports participation plays an important role in improving interpersonal competence and reducing smartphone addiction [29, 34]. However, the existing literature has two major limitations. First, most studies treat sports participation as a whole, failing to distinguish the unique effects of different types of sports. Second, while a study have made a distinction between football and individual sports [29], the role of interpersonal competence—a core variable central to football participation—has not been explored in depth. Thus, it remains unclear whether football uniquely moderates the relationship between interpersonal competence and smartphone addiction. To address this gap, the present study integrates the Compensatory Internet Use Theory and the Stress Buffering Hypothesis. Methods

Participants and procedure

This study employed purposive sampling to select 162 schools across 13 provinces within China’s seven major geographic regions for questionnaire surveys. A total of 2,451 in-school primary and secondary students were recruited as research subjects. Among them, 16 dropped out midway through the survey. Consequently, the final sample comprised 2,435 participants. Among them, 1,510 participants (62%) engaged in football, all of these subjects had received formal football training and were required to participate in football practice at least three times a week. The remaining 925 participants (38%) engaged in individual sports such as swimming, tennis, athletics, or other individual sports activities. This group was strictly required to be students who had not participated in any formal football training.

The study was carried out in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shaanxi Normal University (No. 202516087). The principles of voluntariness, confidentiality, and non-harmfulness were strictly adhered to throughout the study. Prior to participation, the purpose, content, and precautions of the survey were fully explained to the participants, and written informed consent was obtained. In this study, informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians. With permission from each school, questionnaires were administered to classes of adolescents. Each questionnaire was coded and entered anonymously, and all data and information were kept confidential and were not disclosed to any external parties, except for the purpose of scientific research.

Measures

Interpersonal competence questionnaire

This study employed the Interpersonal Competence Questionnaire developed by Buhrmester et al. as the measurement tool [35]. This scale has undergone rigorous reliability and validity testing among Chinese adolescent populations [36]. The questionnaire comprises 15 items. Participants self-rated using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “very poor” (1) to “very good” (5). Total scores ranged from 15 to 75, with higher scores indicating stronger interpersonal competence. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale in the present study was 0.950.

Athlete participation scale

This study employed the Athlete Participation Scale developed by Agbuga et al. as the measurement instrument [37]. The scale was translated into Chinese and validated through confirmatory factor analysis, demonstrating sound reliability and validity indicators among Chinese adolescents [38]. The scale comprises 13 items. Participants self-rated responses using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “completely disagree” (1 point) to “completely agree” (5 points), yielding total scores between 13 and 65 points. In this study, the scale achieved a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.987.

Smartphone addiction scale

This study employed the Smartphone Addiction Scale-Short Version (SAS-SV) developed by Kwon et al. as the assessment tool [39]. This version has undergone cross-cultural adaptation among Chinese adolescents and demonstrated acceptable structural validity and reliability indices [40]. The scale comprises 10 items measured using a 6-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly Disagree” (1 point) to “Strongly Agree” (6 points). Higher scores indicate more pronounced tendencies toward smartphone addiction. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.933.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 and the PROCESS macro version 4.2 for SPSS [41]. All continuous variables were mean-centered prior to analysis. First, descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency distribution) for demographic variables and Pearson correlation among the main study variables were computed using SPSS. To account for the heterogeneity associated with sport type (football vs. individual sports), this study employed PROCESS macros. First, a simple moderation analysis (PROCESS Model 1) was conducted to examine the moderating role of sports participation in the relationship between interpersonal competence and smartphone addiction. Subsequently, a multiple moderation analysis (Model 3) was performed to test whether this relationship was jointly moderated by sports participation and football participation(football vs. individual sports).

Results

Common method bias

This study conducted Harman’s single factor test to test for common methods bias. The first principal component explained 30.20% of the variance, remaining below the 40% threshold. These results indicate that no significant common method bias existed in this study [42].

Discussion

This study draws on the Compensatory Internet Use Theory and the Stress Buffering Hypothesis to examine the relationship between interpersonal competence and smartphone addiction, with a particular focus on the moderating role of football participation. The results indicate that interpersonal competence negatively predicts smartphone addiction, whereas the overall moderating effect of general sports participation is not significant. When sport type (football vs. individual sports) was introduced as a categorical variable, a three-way interaction model revealed that the moderating effect was significant only among adolescents who participated in football. The findings highlight the unique educational value of football, extends the application of the Compensatory Internet Use Theory and the Stress Buffering Hypothesis to the fields of sports and smartphone addiction, and provides practical implications for educators.

The findings of this study indicate that interpersonal competence exert a significant negative predictive effect on adolescents’ levels of smartphone addiction. Consistent with the Compensatory Internet Use Theory [11]. Adolescents with weak interpersonal competence often struggle to establish peer connections. When individuals struggle to satisfy emotional needs in real-world social interactions, they may turn to virtual spaces to compensate for real-world deficiencies [43]. Moreover, interpersonal relationships are associated with negative emotions such as stress, loneliness, and anxiety [44–46]. These negative emotions constitute key triggers of smartphone addiction [47]. Due to insufficient interpersonal competence, the probability of developing negative psychological states increases in adolescence [48], which, in turn, further exacerbates the occurrence of smartphone addiction [49]. Therefore, interpersonal competence is negatively associated with smartphone addiction.

When sports types are not differentiated, the moderating model show that sports participation does not have a significant moderating effect on the relationship between interpersonal competence and smartphone addiction. This finding highlights the complexity of sports-based interventions: Their are instead shaped by multiple factors, such as the type of sports, social context, individual psychological experiences, and broader cultural factors [50, 51]. From the perspective of the Stress Buffering Hypothesis, the potential value of exercise lies in its ability to alleviate interpersonal stress by providing supportive resources, thereby preventing the development of smartphone addiction [17, 18, 23]. However, this type of supportive resource is easily diluted. Some sports rely more on individual development and provide limited social support [52, 53]. As a result, adolescents engaged in such sports may find it difficult to receive timely emotional support or practical assistance when facing interpersonal difficulties. Moreover, competition is inherent in many sports. For some adolescents, sports may evoke pressure rather than enjoyment. leading to frustration and distress due to poor performance [54]. Consequently, general sports participation may not provide enough support to reduce interpersonal stress. The positive effects of exercise on psychological outcomes are not related to external indicators (such as the frequency and duration of exercise), but are closely related to the internal psychological experiences during the exercise process [27, 55, 56]. Different sports provide different psychological experiences [26]. If the types of exercise are not finely distinguished, those specific psychological experiences that were originally effective in buffering stress and inhibiting addiction are likely to be diluted in the overall average effect. This may explain why sports participation failed to exert a significantly moderating effect in the present study.

The three-way interaction model revealed significant differences across sport types in moderating the relationship between interpersonal competence and smartphone addiction. Specifically, a significant moderating effect of sports participation was observed only under the condition of football participation, and this effect was more pronounced among adolescents with higher levels of football participation. Previous studies have confirmed that team sports are more conducive to the development of interpersonal competence compared to individual sports [57]. Team sports influence the self-efficacy and emotional regulation abilities of adolescents through interpersonal interactions [58], in order to counteract negative behaviors [59, 60]. As a team sport, football offers unique advantages in providing social opportunities. It reduces adolescents’ social anxiety during play and strengthens social skills [21, 61]. thereby helping to build lasting friendships with teammates [62]. Additionally, they are more likely to perceive stressors as challenges rather than threats and seek external help when needed [27, 63]. The cooperative nature of football helps adolescents develop a tendency to trust others and reduce their level of self-centeredness, while its competitive nature provides them with a safe outlet for venting interpersonal stress. Both mechanisms may contribute to reducing smartphone addiction.

In contrast, individual sports lack the inherent features of team sports, such as high-frequency social interaction, a sense of trust and belonging, and mechanisms for buffering interpersonal conflicts. Consequently, they may not provide sufficient alternative supportive resources to effectively buffer smartphone addiction as an adverse distal psychological development outcome [29].

This study offers several insights for teacher education and adolescent mental health research. First, school educators should prioritize the cultivation of adolescents’ interpersonal competence to prevent smartphone addiction arising from social isolation. Second, football participation serves as an effective intervention strategy, not only fosters interpersonal relationships among adolescents but also reduces their dependence on smartphone. Therefore, when designing and implementing sports intervention programs, priority should be given to highly social team activities like football to more effectively promote the coordinated development of adolescents’ mental health and social adaptation skills.

This study is the first to examine the moderating role of sports participation in the relationship between interpersonal competence and smartphone addiction among adolescents across different sports. However, several limitations exist. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference [64]. Therefore, interpretations of moderation analysis results from cross-sectional data should be approached with caution. Future research should employ longitudinal or hierarchical linear models to test moderation effects. Second, data were collected via self-report, making results susceptible to common method bias [42, 65], future studies should utilize multiple assessment methods. Finally, although this study compared football with “individual sports,” the latter category is relatively broad and does not distinguish among specific types (e.g., swimming, tennis, athletics). This aggregation may obscure differential effects across various forms of individual sports. Future research should subdivide individual sports to examine their potentially heterogeneous moderating effects.

Conclusion

Football participation uniquely moderates the negative predictive relationship between interpersonal competence and smartphone addiction, with this moderating effect strengthening as football participation increases. This study extends the application value of the Compensatory Internet Use Theory and the Stress Buffering Hypothesis in to sports domain, and expands the explanatory boundaries of the Stress Buffering Hypothesis to smartphone addiction. They also highlight the unique value of football for adolescents’ smartphone addiction. These findings suggest that Adolescents should prioritize team-based outdoor sports, such as football, to improve interpersonal competence, reduce the risk of smartphone addiction, and support healthy mental development.
