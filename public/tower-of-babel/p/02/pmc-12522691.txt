Aberrant alternative splicing in alcoholic hepatitis: regulatory roles of RBPs and sQTLs
Authors: Jinjiang Zhao, Jie Cheng, Shaohua Liang, Wei Guo, Beibei Zhao, Huipeng Zhang, Xianghuang Mei, Ning Zhang, Meifang Sheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlternative splicing (AS) plays a critical role in gene regulation, contributing to transcriptomic complexity and disease progression.MethodsIn this study, we investigated the regulatory effects of AS in alcoholic hepatitis (AH) using transcriptomic data from 41 samples (Normal: 21; AH: 20) from three projects (PRJNA597328, PRJNA600011 and PRJNA656197) from the NCBI database.ResultsA total of 1,061,774 transcripts were identified, of which 42.23% were novel and not annotated in the reference genome, highlighting the diversity of transcriptomic variation in AH. Furthermore, we identified 1,655 aberrant alternative splicing events (AASEs) associated with 1,170 genes, 487 of which were significantly correlated with 22 differentially expressed RNA-binding proteins (RBPs). Additionally, 4,745 splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTLs) were identified in AH, mapped to 2,438 genes enriched in the regulation of some metabolic processes. Notably, several susceptibility sQTL locis were located within known alcoholic liver disease (ALD) associated genes, including GAB1, TJP2 and RCAN1. These findings suggest that AS, through its regulation by RBPs and genetic variants (sQTLs), significantly influences key biological processes in AH, including fatty acid degradation and ferroptosis, processes that are central to the clinical manifestations of AH.ConclusionsThis study provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying AH, emphasizing the importance of AS and its regulatory factors in disease progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04339-3.

Introduction

AH is a severe and potentially fatal condition of acute-on-chronic liver failure triggered by prolonged heavy alcohol consumption. It is marked by the sudden onset or exacerbation of jaundice, impaired liver function, and signs of hepatic decompensation, such as ascites, encephalopathy, and bleeding due to portal hypertension [1]. Pathophysiologically, AH is driven by the effects of alcohol both within and outside the liver. In hepatocytes, alcohol is metabolized by non-inducible enzymes (alcohol dehydrogenase) and inducible enzymes (CYP2E1) into the toxic metabolite acetaldehyde. Acetaldehyde induces liver damage by inhibiting immune cell activity, leading to hepatocyte necrosis and apoptosis. Concurrently, alcohol exerts harmful effects on the gut, ultimately triggering liver inflammation through multiple mechanisms [2, 3]. Accumulating evidence indicates that alterations in gene expression are associated with the severity of AH, highlighting the importance of conducting in-depth investigations at the molecular level [4, 5].

AS is a critical mechanism in gene expression regulation. Through AS of exons and introns in precursor mRNA, AS generates multiple distinct mRNA isoforms, thereby enhancing the coding potential of the genome. This process is widespread among eukaryotes and is a key factor in the development of various diseases. For instance, Chen et al. identified a novel splice variant of the forkhead-box protein P1 (FOXP1) that causes FOXP1 syndrome [6]. Similarly, Xie et al. demonstrated that Polypyrimidine Tract Binding Protein 1 (PTBP1) promotes bladder cancer metastasis by regulating AS to upregulate the MEIS2-L isoform and enhances cancer cell proliferation by increasing the expression of the PKM2 isoform [7]. Duriez et al. reported that liver injury disrupts AS mechanisms and increases hepatitis B virus splicing events [8]. Furthermore, growing evidence has been uncovering the link between AS and liver diseases [9]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that specific AS isoforms have potential as biomarkers [10], and splicing regulators offer promising targets for therapeutic intervention in liver diseases [11].

Alternative splicing events (ASE) play a critical role in gene expression regulation by producing diverse mRNA isoforms through different splicing patterns. This process enhances protein diversity and functional complexity. Among the most common AS are mutations at genomic splice sites. Studies have identified 29 distinct splice site mutations of the p53 gene across 12 types of cancer [12–14]. These mutations often result in the loss of nearby exons, with over half of these exon losses leading to truncated proteins that alter functionality and contribute to disease progression [15]. While ASEs encompass all naturally occurring AS variations, AASEs specifically refer to those linked to pathological processes. A growing body of evidence has demonstrated that AASEs play pivotal roles in the onset and progression of various diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disorders, and neurodegenerative conditions, by altering protein structure, function, and expression levels [16, 17]. Identifying sQTLs, particularly in cancer tissues, is crucial for understanding how genetic variation contributes to tumorigenesis and progression. Several studies have already conducted genome-wide sQTL mapping across various human tissues, such as whole blood and brain [18–21]. These large-scale transcriptomic analyses, utilizing high-throughput genotyping and deep RNA sequencing, have revealed the widespread presence of sQTLs across the genome. This research provides new insights into the complexity of gene regulation, showing how genetic variation can influence splicing mechanisms, gene expression, and ultimately human disease. In addition, RBPs play an essential role in splicing regulation. For example, Feng et al. highlighted the heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A1 (hnRNPA1), a critical RBP associated with neurodegenerative diseases and cancer. hnRNPA1 plays significant roles in gene transcription, mRNA translation, and stability, making it a potential therapeutic target [22]. Extensive research has elucidated the diverse roles of RBPs in the pathogenesis and progression of liver diseases [23, 24]. However, to date, no studies have specifically investigated the impact of RBPs on AS in AH.

In this study, RNA-seq data from 21 normal samples and 20 AH patient samples were collected to systematically identify ASEs, leading to the detection of 1,655 AASEs. Furthermore, we analyzed the correlative roles of RBPs on these AASEs and identified sQTLs. Our analysis revealed 22 RBP genes that were significantly associated with AASEs and 4,745 sQTLs. These results provide a comprehensive characterization of ASEs in AH, elucidate the regulatory network of AASEs, and offer potential targets and insights for future therapeutic strategies in AH treatment research.

Materials and methods

Data collection and processing

We obtained transcriptome data for 41 liver samples from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). These transcriptome data contain six projects (accession numbers: PRJNA597328 [25], PRJNA600011 [26, 27], PRJNA656197). These datasets included 21 normal liver samples and 20 AH patient samples (Supplementary Table S1). Raw transcriptomic data were processed for downstream analyses. First, we converted the downloaded SRA files into FASTQ format using the fastq-dump tool (v2.11.3) from the SRA Toolkit (v3.0.5) (https://github.com/ncbi/sra-tools). Next, we removed adapter sequences and low-quality reads using fastp (v0.23.4) [28] with default parameters. Subsequently, the cleaned reads for each sample were mapped to the human reference genome GRCh38.p14 (GCF_000001405.40) using hisat2 (v2.2.1) [29] with default parameters. Finally, sorted BAM files were generated using the sort function of samtools (v1.19.2) [30] with default parameters.

Transcript identification and differential gene expression analysis

To identify transcripts across all samples, we processed the sorted BAM files generated by hisat2 and samtools alongside the gene annotation file using StringTie (v1.3.6) [31] with default parameters for transcript assembly for each sample (command: stringtie sample.sort.bam -G reference.gtf -o sample.out.gtf). The assembled transcripts were then merged into a unified reference transcriptome using the StringTie merge function. This merged transcriptome was subsequently used for remapping with STAR (v2.7.10b) [32] using the –quantMode TranscriptomeSAM option to facilitate transcript-level quantification with RSEM (v1.3.3) [33] (rsem-calculate-expression –paired-end –fragment-length-max 1000 –estimate-rspd –forward-prob 0.5). The merged transcriptome was analyzed for coding potential using gffcompare (v0.12.6) [34] with default parameters. We used gffread [34] to discard redundant transcripts with "-N -M -K". Transcripts assigned a class code of " = " were considered reference transcripts, those with a class code of "u" were considered as intergenic transcripts, and all other class codes were considered as novel transcripts. Gene expression levels were quantified using featureCounts (v2.0.6) [35] (-p -B -C -t exon -g gene_id). To minimize batch effects introduced by differences among samples from multiple projects, we applied the ComBat function from the sva R package [36] to adjust the normalized count matrix. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between normal and AH samples were identified using the DESeq2 (v1.42.0) [37] package in R (v4.3.3). Additionally, we incorporated project information as a covariate in the DESeqDataSetFromMatrix function to further account for batch-related variation. DEGs were defined based on the criteria of padj < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange|> 1. A total of 2949 DEGs were identified (Supplementary Table S2).

Identification of AASEs

ASEs were identified using the generateEvents module (-e SE SS MX RI FL -f ioe) of SUPPA2 (v2.3) [38], based on the annotation file of the assembled master transcript. Subsequently, the percent spliced in index (PSI) values for each ASE were quantified using SUPPA2’s psiPerEvent module with default parameters, leveraging the transcript-level TPM expression matrix. ASEs with PSI > 0.1 in at least 10 normal samples and 10 AH patient samples were retained for further analysis. In our study, we operationally defined AASEs as those showing statistically significant differences in PSI values between AH and normal control samples. These AASEs may lead to altered protein isoforms that contribute to the pathogenesis of diseases such as AH. To identify AASEs, the diffSplice module of SUPPA2 was employed with the parameters “–nan-threshold 0.8 -m classical -gc”, evaluating PSI differences for each ASE between normal and AH samples. ASEs with a P-value < 0.05 and |delta-PSI|> 0.1 were classified as AASEs [39].

Construction of a correlation-based RBP-ASE network

A total of 1,236 RBPs were utilized for analysis in this study (Supplementary Table S3) [40]. Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) were calculated for all RBP-AASE pairs to assess whether changes in the expression levels of differentially expressed RBPs influenced the PSI values of specific AASEs. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. RBP-AASE pairs with an absolute PCC > 0.6 and an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly correlated [41]. The significant RBP-AASE splicing regulatory network was visualized using Cytoscape [42].

sQTL analysis

We performed SNP calling on the transcriptome data from 41 samples using GATK (v4.5) [43] to identify genetic variants. Briefly, reads were aligned with STAR using the “–twopassMode Basic” option. The resulting BAM files were processed with GATK AddOrReplaceReadGroups to add @RG information, and PCR duplicates were removed using MarkDuplicates. To reduce alignment artifacts, we retained properly paired reads using samtools (“view -f 0 × 2”). Considering RNA-seq splicing features, GATK SplitNCigarReads was applied to split reads into exonic segments and hard-clip intronic overhangs. SNP and indel calls were generated for each sample with GATK HaplotypeCaller using “–dont-use-soft-clipped-bases” and a minimum confidence threshold (-stand-call-conf 20.0). GVCF files from all samples were combined with CombineGVCF and jointly genotyped with GenotypeGVCFs. Variants were filtered using GATK VariantFiltration (-window 35 -cluster 3) to improve sensitivity and specificity, and SelectVariants was applied to retain only biallelic SNPs. Finally, VCFtools (–max-missing 0.9 –maf 0.05) was used to remove sites with > 10% missing data or minor allele frequency (MAF) < 5%.

To explore the regulatory relationships between SNPs and ASEs in AH, we used fastQTL [44] (–cov covariance.txt -W 100000 –normal) to identify sQTLs. We retained ASEs with PSI > 0.1 in at least 30% of the samples. Following the method described by Yamaguchi et al. [45], PSI values of all ASEs across samples were quantile normalized to the average empirical distribution for each ASE, and subsequently subjected to inverse normal transformation across samples. To account for potential confounding due to batch effects among samples, we applied the probabilistic estimation of expression residuals (PEER) method [46] to infer hidden factors, and included 10 PEER-derived hidden factors as covariates in the sQTL analysis. In addition, to further control the effect of population structure on the discovery of QTLs, we included 3 genotype principal components (PCs) based on sample size bins [47]. The positional information of ASEs was combined with their peer-corrected PSI values to create the phenotype file for fastQTL, while the SNP VCF file served as the genotype file. sQTLs located within a 100 kb region of the AS sites were defined as cis-sQTLs. P-values were adjusted using the ‌false discovery rate‌ (FDR) correction method, and cis-sQTLs with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered significant.

Enrichment analysis

The R package clusterProfiler (v4.8.3) [48] was used to perform GO, KEGG, and PFAM enrichment analyses on the genes in which the sQTLs were located. Visualization of the results was carried out using ggplot2 (v3.5.1) [49]. Annotation files for GO, KEGG, and PFAM were generated using Eggnog-mapper [50], KOBAS [51], and HMMER [52] with default parameters, respectively. UpSet plots were created with the R package UpSetR (v1.4.0) [53], and Manhattan plots were generated using the R package CMplot (v4.5.1) [54].

Results

Identification and characterization of transcripts

A total of 1,061,774 transcripts were identified from the RNA-seq data of 21 normal and 20 AH samples. Transcript quantification showed similar expression distributions across samples, indicating good sequencing quality suitable for downstream analyses (Supplementary Figure S1). ierarchical clustering heatmap revealed distinct expression patterns between AH and Normal samples (Supplementary Figure S2). Based on gene annotations in the reference genome and class codes provided by gffcompare, these transcripts were categorized into three groups: reference transcripts, novel transcripts, and intergenic transcripts. Among them, reference transcripts accounted for 15.99% (169,755 transcripts), novel transcripts constituted the largest proportion at 42.23% (448,374 transcripts), and intergenic transcripts made up 41.78% (443,645 transcripts), with the latter being potential lncRNAs (Fig. 2A). The number of transcripts decreased as their length increased, with most reference transcripts falling within the 1 kb range. In contrast, novel transcripts had a higher proportion of transcripts longer than 2 kb (Fig. 2B). The number of transcripts also basically decreased with an increasing number of exons. Reference transcripts with two exons were more abundant compared to novel transcripts, but those with more than two exons were fewer in number than their novel transcript counterparts (Fig. 2C). These findings indicate that novel transcripts not only dominate in quantity but also exhibit longer lengths and more exons, suggesting a more complex structural profile. While transcript expression levels varied across chromosomes, the distribution of expressed transcripts within different samples was relatively concentrated (Fig. 2D). Chr1-Chr12 and ChrX express more transcripts than other chromosomes. Identification of AASEs in AH

AASEs can profoundly influence gene expression and protein diversity, thereby contributing to disease pathogenesis. To comprehensively understand ASEs in AH, we used SUPPA2 to identify 63,964 ASEs with PSI > 0.1 in at least 10 normal and 10 AH samples. These ASEs were categorized into seven types: skipping exon (SE), alternative 3' splice sites (A3), alternative 5' splice sites (A5), retained intron (RI), alternative first exons (AF), alternative last exons (AL), and mutually exclusive exons (MX). Among these, MX events were more tissue-specific, whereas A3 and RI events were more shared across samples. Interestingly, all seven ASE types were shared in AH samples with similar frequencies as those in normal samples (Fig. 3A). By comparing the PSI values and significance of these ASEs between normal and AH samples, we identified 1,655 AASEs (|∆PSI|> 0.1, p-value < 0.05) (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S4). AF had the highest number of AASEs (489 events), affecting 351 genes, while MX had the fewest (31 events), associated with 26 genes. The other AASE types ranged between 102 and 377 events (Fig. 3C). PSI variation differed across the seven AASE types: MX showed the highest ∆PSI, whereas SE and A3 were the lowest (Fig. 3D). Across the 41 samples (except AH_1_1), each had exceeding 1,200 AASEs with similar type proportions (Fig. 3E). In general, the number of AASEs in normal samples is slightly more than that in AH samples. Interestingly, some genes were influenced by only one AASE type, while others were regulated by multiple AASEs. For example, of the 351 genes influenced by AF-type AASEs, 282 were exclusively regulated by AF (e.g., NPR3). Similarly, 252 of the 330 genes influenced by SE-type AASEs were only regulated by SE (e.g., OXR1). Conversely, some genes were affected by multiple ASE types, such as AFDN, which was regulated by six ASE types (excluding MX and AL), and FGFR2, which was regulated by AL and MX (Fig. 3F-G). RBP correlation of AASEs in AH

RBPs are key post-transcriptional regulators that can modulate AS patterns. To uncover the influence of RBPs on AS in AH, we conducted Pearson correlation analyses between differentially expressed RBPs and AASEs. Initially, 23 differentially expressed RBP genes were identified (Supplementary Figure S3, Supplementary Table S5). Correlation analysis revealed that 22 RBPs were significantly associated with 487 AASEs (PCC > 0.6 and p.adjust < 0.05), forming 1261 RBP-AASE pairs (Supplementary Table S6). Based on these associations, we constructed an RBP-AASE splicing network in AH (Fig. 4A). The network includes 487 AASEs across 371 genes. These genes are enriched in pathways such as ferroptosis, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, and endocrine resistance (Supplementary Figure S4). Interestingly, the regulatory capacity of the 22 differentially expressed RBPs varies significantly (Fig. 4B). PEBP1 exhibited the strongest correlative influence, impacting 149 AASEs across almost all types. HSPA7 and DMGDH regulated 143 AASEs spanning all seven ASE types, respectively. Conversely, ASS1, PSMC1, MRPS24, and PCDH20 regulated fewer than ten AASEs each. Among these, AKAP1 correlated with only one AF event. These correlations suggest that PEBP1, HSPA7 and DMGDH likely play pivotal roles in splicing regulation in AH. Identification of sQTLs

Genetic variations can alter the splicing of pre-mRNA by creating or disrupting splice sites or other cis-regulatory splicing elements, thereby influencing the inclusion of exons or the choice of splicing sites in the final processed mRNA [12, 55, 56]. Recently, these genetic regulatory mechanisms of AS have been identified as key links between genetic variants and diseases [57]. To elucidate the genetic regulation of AS in AH, we performed sQTL analysis with 314,563 SNPs and the PSI matrix of 95,471 ASEs identified from 41 samples. 4,745 sQTLs were identified, with their distribution across chromosomes ranging from 50 to 591 per chromosome. These sQTLs were significantly associated with 3,453 ASEs (Supplementary Table S7), and the number of significantly associated ASEs varied by chromosome. Chr1 and Chr12 had the highest numbers of significantly associated ASEs, with 591 and 374 respectively, while Chr22, Chr18, ChrX, Chr13 and Chr21 each had fewer than 100 significantly associated ASEs (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, AF was the most frequently significantly associated ASE type among those influenced by sQTLs. Among these sQTLs, the two most strongly regulatory SNPs: chr1:186,384,317 (located in ODR4) and chr1:186,296,294 (located in PRG4), each significantly associated 21 and 20 ASEs. Additionally, two SNPs located within RBPs, chr1:186,325,857 (located in TPR) and chr17:29,111,451 (located in MYO18A), significantly associated 17 and 9 ASEs, respectively (Fig. 5B). Four thousand, seven hundred forty-five sQTLs were located in 2,438 genes, which were subjected to enrichment analysis to better understand their functions. GO enrichment revealed significant associations with regulation of some metabolic processes, including regulation of lipid metabolic process, regulation of cellular ketone metabolic process, and regulation of fatty acid metabolic process (Fig. 5C). Disruption of hepatic lipid metabolism under ethanol exposure is a hallmark of AH, which is characterized by steatosis and lipotoxicity [58, 59]. KEGG pathway enrichment highlighted associations with Fatty acid degradation, Ubiquitin mediated proteolysis, and Ferroptosis (Fig. 5D). Previous studies have established that ferroptosis is closely linked to the progression of alcohol-related liver disease [60–62]. Additionally, Pfam domain enrichment identified significant associations with structural domains such as AMP-binding, SH3_9, and WD40 (Fig. 5E). In the Reactome database, these genes were primarily enriched in Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier (SUMO)–related pathways, including SUMO E3 ligases SUMOylate target proteins, SUMOylation, SUMOylation of DNA damage response and repair proteins, and SUMOylation of ubiquitinylation proteins (Supplementary Table S8). These findings offer a resource for the molecular mechanisms underlying AH.

sQTLs located in the key genes associated with AH

sQTLs are among the primary causal mechanisms underlying genome-wide association study (GWAS) loci. Identifying the impact of genetic variants on AS can help pinpoint susceptibility genes associated with complex traits, such as cancer [45, 57]. In this study, the top some most significant sQTL susceptibility gene loci were chr12-124,974,451, chr4-143,353,003, chr18-58,061,220, chr9-69,225,129, chr20-13719398, chr2-241,309,357, chr12-16,440,840, chr21-34,521,673, chr21-34,521,676, chr21-34,521,677, and chr3-47,428,994. These variants were mapped to genes including DEAH-Box Helicase 37 (DHX37), GRB2 associated binding protein 1 (GAB1), NEDD4 like E3 ubiquitin protein ligase (NEDD4L), tight junction protein 2 (TJP2), ESF1 nucleolar pre-rRNA processing protein homolog (ESF1), high density lipoprotein binding protein (HDLBP), microsomal glutathione S-transferase 1 (MGST1), regulator of calcineurin 1 (RCAN1), and SREBF chaperone (SCAP) (Fig. 6A). Among these, DHX37, a 42,305 bp gene with 27 exons, harbored the sQTL chr12:124,974,451 in the intronic region between exons 7 and 8 (Fig. 6B). This sQTL was associated with five ASEs, including one A3, one SE, and three MX events (Fig. 6C). Similarly, the sQTL chr4:143,353,003 was located in GAB1, a gene with 14 exons, and was associated with two AF events only. In addition, we performed protein domain analysis on the alternative splicing isoforms of genes corresponding to these significant top sQTLs (Supplementary Table S9). The 16 splicing isoforms related to the DHX37 gene all contained the ubiquitin-related pfam domain. Discussion

AH is a clinical syndrome characterized by the rapid onset of jaundice and liver failure in patients with chronic heavy alcohol abuse. Alcohol-induced liver damage is associated with various factors, including the production of reactive oxygen species, dietary fat intake, adipose tissue, fatty acid transport proteins, hepatic lipid synthesis, and cytokines. Studies have shown that the overexpression of the steroid response binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) and the induction of the glucose-responsive transcription factor carbohydrate-responsive element-binding protein are associated with alcohol-related steatosis [64–66]. Additionally, other regulatory factors such as the liver X receptor (LXR) [67] and the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) [68] have also been implicated in causing steatosis. Recent research has revealed a connection between alcoholic steatohepatitis (ASH) and epigenetic changes, highlighting differential alcohol-induced hepatic epigenetic gene expression. The etiology of ASH involves a complex interplay of genetic, epigenetic, and non-(epi) genetic factors [69]. AS, a critical epigenetic modification, has been widely linked to various diseases [70]. However, the impact of AS on AH remains largely unexplored. Therefore, analyzing AS in AH is of significant importance for understanding the mechanisms underlying the development of this condition.

In this study, we integrated RNA-seq data from 20 AH samples and 21 normal samples obtained from the NCBI database, and identified a total of 1,061,774 transcripts. The human genome annotation GENCODE (hg38) includes 412,034 transcripts. Thus, the number of transcripts identified in our study is approximately 2.58 times greater than the annotated transcript count. This discrepancy may be attributed to various factors, including repetitive regions in the human genome, large variations in GC content, isoform length diversity, and the complexity of AS [31, 71]. As noted by the developers of StringTie [31], transcriptomic data reflect biological reality, and it is difficult to definitively determine which predicted transcripts are false positives. Although comprehensive human genome annotations are available, they still do not fully represent all genes and transcripts that exist in vivo. While some incorrectly assembled or redundant transcripts that span multiple genes can be manually filtered out, researchers should be aware that additional false-positive transcripts may persist. These should ideally be validated using PCR experiments before further functional analysis. Current tools still face challenges in thoroughly eliminating such artifacts, which may introduce potential biases into downstream differential expression or functional analyses. Therefore, the development of more accurate algorithms and the integration of multi-omics validation strategies are needed to improve the accuracy of transcriptome assembly in future studies. On the other hand, although batch effects were corrected in this study, and clear separation between AH samples and normal samples was observed after correction (except for AH_1_1) (Supplementary Figure S2), researchers should remain cautious about potential biases caused by residual batch effects. Experimental validation is still required to further confirm the novel transcripts identified in this study.

RPBs play an important role in regulating AS. In cancer, aberrant AS and dysregulated RBPs can lead to abnormal expression of cancer-related genes, thereby driving tumor initiation and progression [72–74]. Interestingly, while multiple studies in fatty liver disease have consistently reported downregulation of SRSF3 accompanied by splicing changes in canonical targets [75], our analysis of AH samples revealed that SRSF3 expression remained stable and its target exons did not undergo systematic shifts. Such inconsistency may reflect etiologic heterogeneity between metabolic steatosis and alcohol-driven injury, as well as differences in liver cell-type composition, both of which could obscure factor-specific splicing patterns in bulk RNA-seq. In addition, we constructed a regulatory network involving 22 differentially expressed RBPs and 487 AASEs. Notably, PEBP1, HSPA7 and DMGDH were found to significantly correlate with a substantial number of AASEs. Studies have shown that the interaction between PEBP1 and Periostin may activate autophagy through protein disulfide isomerase, thereby preventing ALD [76]. These findings suggest that PEBP1-regulated AASEs may be involved in pathways relevant to liver pathology, and it is plausible that some of these splicing changes could influence disease progression toward liver failure. However, further studies are required to determine the specific functional consequences of these PEBP1-associated splicing events.

sQTL studies investigate the association between genetic variations in the genome and disease, identifying key loci linked to disease susceptibility and progression. In this study, we identified 4,745 sQTLs in AH. These genes are located in 2,438 genes. KEGG enrichment analysis of these genes revealed pathways related to ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis. Numerous studies have demonstrated that ubiquitination regulates various physiological and pathological processes and is involved in hepatic inflammation and lipid metabolism [77, 78]. It was reported that ubiquitin-specific protease 22 (USP22) contributes to the pathogenesis of ALD by deubiquitinating BRD4, thereby promoting inflammation and exacerbating ALD. Among the several susceptibility sQTL loci identified in this study, one is located in NEDD4L. Previous research has shown that downregulation of NEDD4L impairs ubiquitin-mediated degradation, leading to the upregulation of CHOP and activation of its downstream apoptotic pathway, which aggravates hepatocyte apoptosis, inflammation, and fibrosis in liver tissue [79]. Additionally, other significant susceptibility sQTL loci identified in this study are located in genes associated with liver inflammation. For instance, an sQTL (chr4:143,353,003) is located in GAB1. Studies have shown that microRNA-200a suppresses cell invasion and migration in hepatocellular carcinoma by directly targeting GAB1 [80], and mice with liver-specific GAB1 deletion exhibit defective liver regeneration phenotypes in response to partial hepatectomy, characterized by impaired hepatocyte proliferation [81]. Another sQTL (chr9:69,225,129) is located in TJP2, whose complete loss—along with ZO-2—has been linked to severe liver disease [82]. Moreover, mutations in TJP2 disrupt the proper localization of CLDN1 within the hepatic lobule, impairing tight junction integrity and thereby damaging surrounding hepatocytes [82, 83]. Furthermore, an sQTL (chr21:34,521,677) is located in RCAN1, an endogenous inhibitor of calcineurin (CaN). Recent studies have reported that RCAN1 expression is selectively downregulated in fibrotic liver tissue, whereas its overexpression promotes apoptosis of activated hepatic stellate cells and alleviates liver fibrosis [84, 85]. It is important to acknowledge that the sQTL analysis in this study was conducted based on a relatively small sample size, primarily due to the limited availability of liver tissue samples from patients with AH. As a result, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and further experimental validation is necessary to confirm the functional relevance of the identified sQTLs. The future accumulation and release of transcriptomic data from additional AH liver samples will be instrumental in constructing a more comprehensive and robust sQTL landscape. Moreover, because the SNPs in this study were called from RNA-seq data, the total number of variants available for sQTL analysis was relatively limited compared with whole-genome sequencing or large-scale SNP calling approaches. Consequently, future sQTL studies based on larger sample sizes should carefully consider and evaluate the potential impact of linkage disequilibrium. Nevertheless, despite this limitation, our study still provides a resource for understanding the genetic regulation of splicing in AH and offers a foundation for future research in this field.

In summary, the comprehensive identification and analysis of ASEs in AH conducted in this study provide a resource. Researchers can leverage these data to further explore key splicing events, isoforms, and regulatory factors associated with the development and progression of AH. This will help to elucidate the role of AS in the pathogenesis of AH.
