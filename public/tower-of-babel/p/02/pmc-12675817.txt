Evaluating Pedicled Groin Flap Reconstruction in Hand Degloving Injuries: A Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Saleh Abualhaj, Yazan S Al-Zamer, Lina Al-Shadfan, Mosleh Abualhaj, Ali aloun, Basel Joseph Massad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of pedicled groin flap coverage in such injuries and identify factors associated with postoperative complications and flap viability.MethodsA retrospective review of 23 patients who underwent pedicled groin flap reconstruction for finger or hand degloving injuries between 2015 and 2024 was conducted. Patient demographics, surgical details, complication rates, aesthetic outcomes, and functional recovery were analyzed.ResultsThe cohort had a mean age of 27.9 years, with a male predominance (73.9%) and a high smoking rate (73.9%). Most defects involved the fingers (87%), with a mean defect size of 30.6 cm2 and a mean flap size of 72.0 cm2. The mean wound healing time was 32.5 days, and the average hospital stay was 3.6 days. Flap success was achieved in 91.3% of the cases. Postoperative complications occurred in 34.8% of the patients, including partial flap necrosis (26.1%), wound dehiscence (13.0%), and infection (4.3%). Intraoperative venous congestion was observed in 39.1% of the cases and was more frequent among patients who developed complications (P = .001). Other variables showing statistically significant differences between patients with and without complications included smoking status (P = .037), additional procedures performed (P = .037), and smaller defect dimensions (P = .011 for width; P = .032 for size). Aesthetic outcomes were rated as good or neutral in over 90% of the cases. Functionally, 91.3% of the patients experienced minimal-to-mild disability, with a mean QuickDASH score of 18.1 (SD = 14.1). Patients with complications had higher disability scores (mean: 27.9 vs 12.9; P = .012).ConclusionsGroin flap reconstruction remains a reliable and accessible option for managing hand degloving injuries, particularly in resource-limited settings, offering acceptable functional and aesthetic outcomes despite a relatively high rate of secondary procedures.Type of study/level of evidenceTherapeutic III.Key words: Clinical outcome, Degloving injury, Functional outcome, Groin flap, Hand reconstruction

Degloving injuries of the hand and fingers are devastating soft tissue traumas typically caused by high-energy mechanisms, such as industrial or vehicular accidents, that exert intense shearing forces on the skin and subcutaneous tissues.1 These injuries often result in extensive skin avulsion, exposing tendons, bones, and neurovascular structures and pose considerable challenges in terms of both limb salvage and functional restoration.1,2 Although reimplantation or revascularization of the avulsed tissue is considered the ideal reconstructive strategy, it is often unfeasible because of the nonviability of the degloved skin.3

In such scenarios, regional flaps remain a cornerstone in reconstruction. The groin flap, first introduced by McGregor and Jackson in the 1970s, has become a widely used option in hand surgery.4,5 Its enduring relevance stems from its dependable vascular anatomy, ease of harvest, low donor site morbidity, and the ability to cover large defects with pliable tissue.5

The primary goals of reconstruction following degloving injuries extend beyond mere wound coverage—they aim to restore hand function, preserve range of motion, and achieve an acceptable cosmetic result to support psychological and social reintegration.6 Given the critical role of the hand in daily activities, employment, and quality of life, even minor limitations in dexterity or disfigurement can lead to notable disability or distress.7

Despite its established role, there is a paucity of data specifically evaluating its outcomes in the context of hand and finger degloving injuries, particularly regarding long-term functional and aesthetic results. Therefore, this retrospective multicenter case series evaluates outcomes of pedicled groin flaps in 23 patients with traumatic hand and finger degloving injuries, focusing on functional and aesthetic results and factors affecting recovery. It represents the largest study of its kind in Jordan.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective, multicenter case series aimed to evaluate the outcomes of groin flap reconstruction in patients with degloving injuries of the hand, conducted across multiple tertiary care centers in Jordan between January 2015 and June 2024. Degloving injuries were defined as circumferential or near-circumferential avulsions of the skin and subcutaneous tissue, often exposing tendons, bones, or neurovascular structures.8

Study population

All patients who sustained hand degloving injuries and underwent groin flap reconstruction as the primary coverage technique during the study period were included. A comprehensive census sampling method was employed to capture all eligible cases. Inclusion criteria comprised the use of a pedicled groin flap for soft tissue coverage in degloving injuries, with a minimum follow-up duration of 6 months. Patients were excluded if alternative reconstructive methods were the primary approach or if postoperative records were insufficient to evaluate outcomes. All procedures were performed exclusively by hand surgeons, consistent with standard practice in Jordan.

Surgical technique

Groin flap reconstruction was performed using a pedicled flap based primarily on the superficial circumflex iliac artery axis. The flap was designed over the groin region, with the lateral border at the anterior superior iliac spine and the medial edge extending toward the femoral vessels. The incision was made along the inguinal crease, and subfascial dissection was carried out to elevate the flap, taking care to preserve the vascular pedicle throughout.

Following flap inset, the affected limb was immobilized using a surgical stitch between the affected hand and the abdominal wall, combined with a support sling to maintain optimal positioning and prevent tension on the pedicle. Flap perfusion was monitored regularly by the surgical team and trained nursing staff, using clinical parameters such as flap color, temperature, capillary refill time, and turgor.

Intraoperative or postoperative venous congestion was managed conservatively in most cases through limb elevation, gentle flap massage, and warming. In some cases, additional measures such as releasing tight dressings or temporary use of medicinal leeches were applied. No surgical re-explorations were required in this cohort.

Pedicle division was performed under regional or general anesthesia between 14 and 24 days after surgery (mean: 16 ± 3 days), after clinical confirmation of adequate neovascularization and flap integration. Division was performed with careful dissection and hemostasis to minimize complications.

Data collection

Clinical and surgical data were retrospectively extracted from patient files, including electronic medical records, surgical notes, surgical logs, and follow-up clinic documentation. A uniform data abstraction form was used to record patient demographics (age, sex, smoking status, and comorbidities), injury characteristics (mechanism, location, and defect size), surgical details (flap design, dimensions, pedicle length, and intraoperative complications), and any adjunct procedures (eg, tendon grafting, flap thinning, and pseudo-syndactyly release).

The size of the defect and flap was determined intraoperatively by measuring the length and width of the soft tissue loss and the flap using a sterile ruler. The defect and flap areas were then calculated in square centimeters (cm2) by multiplying these dimensions.

Key outcome variables included flap viability (complete survival vs partial/total failure), time to wound healing, duration of hospitalization, and postoperative complications such as venous congestion, necrosis, or infection.

Intraoperative venous congestion was identified based on clinical signs, including flap discoloration characterized by a dusky or purplish hue, increased flap turgor, and impaired capillary refill. These signs indicated impaired venous outflow.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi version 2.5.4. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) summarized baseline and outcome data. Comparative analyses between groups (eg, with vs without complications) were conducted using χ2 or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables and independent t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables. A two-tailed P value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of Al-Balqa Applied University, with additional approvals obtained from participating institutions. Given the retrospective nature of the study and the anonymization of data, the requirement for informed consent was waived. All patient data were deidentified and handled in accordance with ethical and privacy guidelines.

Results

Demographics, surgical details, and outcomes

This case series included 23 patients who underwent groin flap reconstruction for finger or hand defects at the following three tertiary centers: (1) a public sector hospital (seven cases, 30.4%), (2) a military hospital (four cases, 17.4%), and (3) a private sector hospital (12 cases, 52.2%). The mean age of the patients was 27.9 years (range: 14–53 years), with a male predominance of 73.9%. All patients were free of comorbidities, although 73.9% were smokers. Most defects were located on the fingers (87%) and primarily on the left side (56.5%). All finger cases involved the distal and middle phalanx levels, with some cases preserving the proximal phalanx. Additionally, approximately half of the patients had preservation of the volar aspect of the finger. Nearly all cases were primary defects (95.7%). The mean defect size was 30.6 cm2, whereas the average flap size was 72.0 cm2. Most flaps (73.9%) were designed using the tubular rotation technique. Additionally, 73.9% of the cases required supplementary procedures. The average wound healing time was 32.5 days, and the hospital stay averaged 3.6 days. Donor site complications were rare (4.3%). The mean duration of follow-up was 2.0 years (SD: 1.24), with a range of 1–6 years, and the flap success rate was high at 91.3% (Table 1).

Demographics, Surgical Details, and Outcomes of the Cases

Clinical, aesthetic, and functional outcomes

Among the 23 patients who underwent groin flap reconstruction for finger and hand defects, complications were relatively common. Intraoperative venous congestion was the most frequent issue, observed in 39.1% of the cases. Postoperative complications occurred in 34.8% of the patients, with partial flap necrosis reported in 26.1%. Wound dehiscence was seen in 13% of cases, whereas wound infection requiring treatment was identified in one patient (4.3%; Fig. 1).

Intraoperative and postoperative complication bar chart.

Aesthetic and functional outcomes following groin flap reconstruction were generally favorable. Based on the Likert scale, 43.5% of the patients rated their aesthetic outcome as good, whereas 47.8% reported a neutral experience and 8.7% perceived the outcome as poor. Vancouver scar scale assessments revealed that most scars were either mild (39.1%) or normal (26.1%), with fewer cases rated as moderate (30.4%) or severe (4.3%; Fig. 2).

Aesthetic outcomes of degloving cases.

Functionally, as measured by the QuickDASH score, 91.3% of the patients reported either minimal (39.1%) or mild disability (52.2%), whereas only 8.6% experienced moderate-to-severe disability. The mean QuickDASH score was 18.1 (SD = 14.1), indicating overall acceptable functional recovery (Fig. 3).

Functional outcomes of degloving cases.

Risk factors of postoperative complications

In the univariate analysis of factors potentially associated with postoperative complications following groin flap reconstruction, several variables demonstrated statistically significant differences between patients with and without complications. Smokers and those who underwent additional procedures were more frequently observed among patients experiencing complications (P = .037 for both). Smaller defect width (P = .011) and overall defect size (P = .032) were also more common in the complication group. Venous congestion was notably more prevalent in patients with complications (87.5%) compared with those without (13.3%; P < .001). Functional outcomes, assessed by the QuickDASH score, were worse among patients with complications (mean score: 27.9) than those without complications (mean score: 12.9; P = .02).

Although not reaching statistical significance, trends toward differences were noted in sex distribution (P = .056), flap width (P = .068), and aesthetic outcomes based on the Likert scale (P = .090), which may hold clinical relevance. Other demographic and surgical variables, including age, body mass index, defect location, flap dimensions, surgical time, and wound healing duration, did not differ considerably between the two groups. Additionally, no notable trend was observed between the number of cases contributed by each center and clinical outcome. Despite the differences in sector affiliation, all centers adhered to comparable surgical techniques and perioperative management protocols, resulting in consistent outcomes across institutions (Table 2).

Discussion

Despite the growing preference for free flaps in complex hand injuries, pedicled flaps like the groin flap remain practical and effective, especially in resource-constrained settings where microsurgical expertise and equipment are limited.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Their reliable vascular anatomy, ease of harvest, and ability to provide timely coverage make them valuable alternatives in acute trauma care, as emphasized by Bajantri et al.17 Additionally, pedicled groin flaps are often chosen in patients with contraindications to microsurgical reconstruction, such as coagulation disorders or comorbidities, further supporting their ongoing relevance in diverse clinical scenarios.18

Our study supports the reliability, functional utility, and acceptable aesthetics of pedicled groin flaps for hand degloving injuries, particularly in health care systems lacking microsurgical options. We observed a high flap survival rate of 91.3%, a short average hospital stay of 3.6 days, and a mean wound healing time of 32.5 days. Functionally, only 8.6% reported moderate-to-severe disability, with a mean QuickDASH score of 18.1. Most patients rated their aesthetic outcomes as neutral or good, with a few reporting poor results. These findings confirm the groin flap as a dependable, efficient, and cosmetically acceptable reconstruction method.

Our flap success rate of 91.3% aligns with the previously reported rates for upper limb groin flaps, reflecting the flap’s reliable vascular anatomy, meticulous surgical technique, and comprehensive perioperative care.19 On comparing our results with those of Hussain et al’s20 free groin flap series with eight patients, we found that the demographic characteristics were similar, but their hospital stay was longer (5 days), and the overall complication rate was lower (25% vs 34.8%).20 Our higher complication rate may reflect differences in flap type and resource limitations, highlighting trade-offs in flap choice.

Our cohort demonstrated a mean wound healing time of 32.5 days, consistent with findings in recently published literature.21 Our complication rates—partial necrosis (26.1%), wound dehiscence (13%), and infection (4.3%)—are lower than some prior reports with rates as high as 38% for flap loss and 32% for infections.4 Such differences may be attributed to variations in injury severity, surgical expertise, and the institutional standards for perioperative management.

Venous congestion was observed intraoperatively in 39.1% and was considerably associated with postoperative complications. This aligns with Boissiere et al, who identified venous congestion as a major challenge because of dependent flap positioning and limited early venous drainage, underscoring the need for vigilant monitoring and management.22 To help prevent venous congestion, careful flap design that preserves adequate venous outflow is essential, along with avoiding excessive tension or twisting of the vascular pedicle during inset. Intraoperative monitoring of flap perfusion and ensuring optimal positioning to minimize dependent drainage further contribute to reducing this risk. Implementing these measures can improve flap viability and overall surgical outcomes.23

Factors associated with complications included smoking, smaller injury width and defect size, and additional procedures. Smoking, a well-known modifiable risk factor impairing wound healing, has been linked to increased surgical site infections and reoperations, as confirmed by large database studies.24, 25, 26, 27 Flap width also appeared more critical than length for vascular reliability, emphasizing the preoperative optimization of flap design.23

Functional and aesthetic outcomes were favorable overall. The mean QuickDASH score of 18.1 compares well with that of prior studies reporting higher disability scores. Notably, patients with complications had notably worse functional outcomes, suggesting that complications negatively impact recovery.28,29

Our retrospective study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Although, to the best of our knowledge, this manuscript represents one of the largest case series focused on groin flap reconstruction for hand degloving injuries, the relatively small sample size still limits the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, because of this limited sample size, multivariable analyses adjusting for potential confounders were not performed. As a result, the associations identified in the univariate analysis should be interpreted with caution, and larger studies are warranted to validate independent risk factors for postoperative complications in this patient population. Furthermore, as a retrospective observational study, our work provides a comprehensive overview of groin flap utilization but does not include a direct comparison with alternative reconstructive methods, which limits our ability to draw conclusions about the relative efficacy or superiority of the groin flap technique. An additional limitation of this study is the relatively short follow-up period, which may not capture late cosmetic changes such as flap thickening or bulkiness, particularly in patients who experience weight gain.

Despite these limitations, the strength of our multicenter design and the use of validated, objective outcome measures—namely the QuickDASH and Vancouver Scar Scale—enhance the robustness and clinical relevance of our findings and contribute meaningfully to the existing literature on groin flap reconstruction for hand degloving injuries.

Future research should emphasize prospective, comparative studies and systematic tracking of secondary procedures and their outcomes and adopt standardized outcome measures to better inform clinical decision making and optimize patient care.
