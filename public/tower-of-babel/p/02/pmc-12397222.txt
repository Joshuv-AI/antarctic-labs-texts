Unique plastisphere viromes with habitat-dependent potential for modulating global methane cycle
Authors: Xue-Peng Chen, Dong Zhu, Shu-Yue Liu, Ming-Ming Sun, Mao Ye, Lu Wang, Da Lin, Tian-Lun Zhang, Matthias C. Rillig, Yong-Guan Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plastispheres, novel niches in the Anthropocene, harbor microbial communities with unique functional signatures. As the most abundant biological entity on Earth, viruses are key regulators of microbial community composition and metabolism. However, little is known about viral communities and their functions in the plastisphere. Here, we investigate the composition and functional profile of plastisphere viral communities through microcosm experiments combined with global plastisphere metagenomics data. We find that the plastisphere recruits a distinct viral community with 86.9% novel viral operational taxonomic units compared to control substrates. The plastisphere viral community modulates host methane metabolism through auxiliary metabolic genes and distinctive interactions with hosts. These auxiliary metabolic genes for methane cycling are prevalent in global plastisphere viral communities. Notably, the plastisphere microbiome adopts the life history strategy of copiotrophs in the nutrient-poor water environment, making the water plastisphere a potential hot spot for methane emission compared to the soil plastisphere. Our phage transplantation experiments reveal that lysogenic viruses significantly contribute to enhancing the methanogenic capacity of microorganisms and promoting methane emission of the water plastisphere. Overall, we decipher the role of viruses in the plastisphere and reinforce the necessity of incorporating viral contributions when assessing the effects of plastisphere communities on global biogeochemical cycles.

Introduction

Plastic contamination is widespread in different compartments of the Earth, received increasing research attention1. Plastic enters ecosystems and accumulates in large quantities via different pathways, such as atmospheric deposition and agricultural activities (mulching, amendments and irrigation)2. Paddy ecosystems, agricultural ecosystems with intermittent irrigation, have become potential reservoirs of plastic due to sewage irrigation and mulch application3,4. Plastic in ecosystems provides a persistent substrate that can be colonized by a diverse array of microorganisms and that supports microbial biofilm growth. The resultant ecosystem is referred to as the plastisphere. To date, most studies have targeted bacteria and archaea and demonstrated that the plastisphere is a unique ecological niche with significantly different community composition and function in comparison to natural environments5. However, as the most abundant biological entities on Earth, the community composition and function of viruses in the plastisphere have remained unexplored.

Viruses are central members of “microbial dark matter” and are becoming increasingly recognized for their important ecological roles through their interacting with hosts. Viruses can exploit the cellular machinery of hosts for reproduction by adopting lysogenic or lytic life strategies6. From an ecological perspective, viruses in a microbial community can mediate host community composition through the “Kill-the-Winner” and “Piggyback-the-Winner” ecological models7. Furthermore, viruses that infect microbial hosts can “hijack” or supplement host metabolism through auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs), thereby playing a direct or indirect role in biogeochemical cycling of elements8,9. For instance, virus-encoded AMGs associated with glycoside hydrolase, such as endomannanase with functional activity, have been demonstrated to contribute to complex carbon degradation in soil ecosystems10,11. Viruses carrying AMGs for dissimilatory sulfur metabolism infect sulfur metabolizing microbes and alter sulfur metabolism within host cells in surface soil recovering from fire, significantly contributing to the sulfur cycle in terrestrial ecosystems12. In addition, the role of AMGs is also well documented for viruses from aquatic ecosystems that encode diverse AMGs for photosynthesis13, phosphate metabolism14, carbon fixation15, and ammonia oxidation16. However, compared with these studies of viral communities in natural environments, little is known about how viruses in the plastisphere regulate host population dynamics and metabolic potentials, and how they could affect key ecosystem functions.

Methane is a potent greenhouse gas contributing to global warming17,18. Methane cycling is largely driven by microorganisms, with microbial methanogenesis producing about 69% of the total methane released into the atmosphere19. Inundated paddy ecosystems are not only a sink of plastic, but also one of the major sources of methane, accounting for 10–25% of global emissions20–22. The plastisphere, as a new niche in the paddy ecosystem, could selectively recruit a distinct microbial community, which may alter biogeochemical fluxes of methane23. Moreover, viruses carrying AMGs might participate in methane cycling by modulating host methane metabolism in soil and freshwater lake ecosystems24,25. Some viruses encode AMGs responsible for aerobic methane oxidation, with the potential to compensate host methane metabolism in deep-sea hydrothermal vents26. Despite these recent advances, the contributions of plastisphere viruses to the ecosystem functions of methane cycling remain enigmatic. Especially in inundated paddy ecosystems that encompass different types of environments (i.e., soil and water environments), it is also still unknown whether there are differences in the ecological role of viruses between the soil and water plastisphere.

Arsenic contamination and methane emissions co-occur in paddy ecosystems21,27. The coupled metabolism of methane and arsenic has great potential in paddy ecosystems. Arsenic pollution could not only affect methane production28,29, but also couple the anaerobic oxidation process of methane in paddy soils30,31. Further, as a key pollutant in paddy soils, arsenic could alter soil microbial diversity and structure profoundly, thereby affecting the biogeochemical cycling of elements in paddy ecosystems32,33. However, the effects of arsenic contamination in paddy soils on viral communities are still poorly understood. The viral community is susceptible to environmental stimuli, including in the context of global change34. For example, drought and pollutants affect the viral community composition and modify virus-host symbiotic or antagonistic relationships35,36. Thus, the effects of arsenic pollution, a typical global change factor in paddy ecosystems, on the viral community may lead to fundamental shifts in ecologically relevant functions of the overall microbial community. Considering the interference of arsenic pollution and understanding how microorganisms of the plastisphere and the viruses that infect them might alter methane metabolism in paddy ecosystems is crucial to inform efforts to mitigate microbially driven methane emission and climate warming.

Life history strategy is the means by which microorganisms achieve successful reproduction in various environments or given a certain set of constraints, which could determine their response to environmental stimuli and their metabolic potential37,38. One common application of life history theory is the copiotroph-oligotroph framework39. It is a direct descendant of r-K selection theory and offers a potential organizing principle for describing the complexity of microbial systems. This life history framework defines copiotrophs and oligotrophs, and emphasizes the environmental resource condition dependence of microbial life history traits40. The traits responsible for the adaptations to different environmental conditions that underlie the spectrum of adaptive strategies include not only genomic traits (i.e., genome size, codon usage bias, and 16S ribosomal RNA gene copy number) but also functional attributes (i.e., carbohydrate metabolism, environmental sensing, and energy metabolism)40–42. For example, a study has shown that the genomes of neutral pH-adapted copiotrophs are smaller and enriched with functional genes for energy metabolism and membrane transport, while the genomes of acid-adapted oligotrophs are larger and enriched with genes for cell motility, secretion system, and signal transduction41. For the plastisphere under different scenarios, despite previous work has revealed its distinct functional features due to differences in environmental physicochemical properties43, the life history traits of those communities remain largely unknown. Therefore, in the inundated paddy ecosystem with heterogeneous environmental conditions, combining life history strategy to explore the microbial communities of the soil and water plastisphere would contribute to comprehensively understanding the ecological roles of microbial hosts and viruses in different environments and their differentiation.

In this work, we wish to uncover the community of plastisphere viruses by complementing laboratory studies with global data, elucidating the role of viruses in the biogeochemical cycling of methane. To this end, we conduct a long-term microcosm experiment of paddy ecosystems to explore the community composition and functional signatures of viruses in the plastisphere, while considering the impact of arsenic pollution (a typical global change factor in the paddy ecosystem) stress on viral communities. To further verify the ecological effects of viruses on methane cycling, we conduct phage transplantation and collect metagenomic data of the global plastisphere to mine and examine the AMGs of viral communities. For the soil and water plastisphere in the paddy ecosystem, we hypothesize that the viruses and their hosts adopt distinct life history strategies in response to the heterogeneous environmental conditions, and that the viruses play diverse regulatory roles in host methane metabolism, thereby resulting in different ecological functions of methane cycling in the soil and water plastisphere. Our study targets the viral community and provides new insights into the unique ecosystem functioning of the plastisphere, increasing our understanding of the specific ecological effects and microbial risks of plastic pollution.

Results

Unique prokaryotic community composition and function of the Plastisphere

The prokaryotic community structure in plastisphere, especially the water plastisphere, was significantly distinct from that in control substrates (PERMANOVA: SP vs. SG, p = 0.036; WP vs. WC, p = 0.026; WP vs. WG, p = 0.033; Supplementary Fig. 1a). At the phylum level, Proteobacteria was the most dominant in all substrates (71.2%–82.7%). However, compared with the soil control substrates (SC and SG), the relative abundance of Proteobacteria was decreased in the soil plastisphere (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Similar change was observed in the water plastisphere relative to the water ceramic. Notably, the prokaryotic communities of the control substrates and plastisphere in soil and water environments were significantly different by 9 biomarkers and 10 biomarkers, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Positively affected taxa in the plastisphere included Chloroflexi, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Planctomycetes and negatively affected Gemmatimonadetes and Proteobacteria (LDA Score > 4). These species had a significant impact on the differences in prokaryotic communities between the plastisphere and the control substrates.

A principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) revealed separation of the prokaryotic community function between the plastisphere and control substrates (PERMANOVA: SP vs. SC, p = 0.033; SP vs. SG, p = 0.015; WP vs. WC, p = 0.024; Supplementary Fig. 1d), further indicating the uniqueness of the plastisphere community. To investigate differences in Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways between plastisphere and control substrates, KOs were grouped into BRITE hierarchy level 3 pathways. At BRITE hierarchy level 2, 12 of 18 pathways (e.g., nucleotide metabolism, metabolism of other amino acids, energy metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and substance dependence) with significant difference between plastisphere and control substrates in soil were metabolism and human diseases related, and 5 of 6 metabolism-related pathways were more abundant in the soil plastisphere (Supplementary Fig. 1e). Conversely, among the 12 pathways with significant difference between plastisphere and control substrates in water, 8 were more abundant in the water control substrates.

Plastisphere contained novel and diverse virus pools

In total, 13,872 viral contigs and 3687 viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) were obtained from all samples, including 357 high-quality vOTUs (completeness > 90%). The classification analysis found that 80.2% of vOTUs could not be attributed to any known family (Supplementary Data 1), similar to the reported rate of novelty for the viromes from other ecosystems6,44. To put the plastisphere viral community into a global context, the gene-sharing network was constructed to investigate the relationship between the vOTUs dataset in this study and other databases (Supplementary Data 2). Viral communities from the soil, water, and plastisphere clearly fell into 631 viral clusters (VCs) (Fig. 1c). Notably, only 2.1% of vOTUs could be clustered with taxonomically known viruses in NCBI RefSeq database, while only 15 vOTUs (2.4%) clustered with viruses detected in previous studies. Moreover, only 13.1% of the plastisphere vOTUs clustered with control substrates vOTUs from the microcosm experiment and the collected global data (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 3). These results indicated that 86.9% of the plastisphere vOTUs were novel compared to control substrates and isolated references.

Viral communities were significantly different (PERMANOVA: SP, R2 = 0.34, p = 0.003; WP, R2 = 0.27, p = 0.047) between the plastisphere and control substrates (viral analyses of similarity (ANOSIM): SP, R = 0.51, p = 0.007; WP, R = 0.28, p = 0.045; Supplementary Fig. 3). The comparison of the average community dissimilarity confirmed that the soil plastisphere viral community difference was lower than that of soil; in contrast, the water plastisphere displayed an opposite effect, with significantly higher viral community difference compared with the water (Mann–Whitney U test: U = 744, p = 0.002, cliff’s delta = 0.453, 95% CI [0.134, 0.687]; Supplementary Fig. 4a). Besides, the richness (Chao1 and ACE index) was significantly lower in the plastisphere viral communities (Supplementary Fig. 4b), whereas the nucleotide diversity (π value) were significantly higher in the plastisphere viral communities (Mann–Whitney U test: S vs. SP, U = 26978, p = 0.0005, cliff’s delta = −0.1767, 95% CI [−0.2734, −0.0764]; W vs. WP, U = 23011.5, p = 5.59 × 10−9, cliff’s delta = −0.2977, 95% CI [−0.3890, −0.2007]; Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data 4). The D’ value used to quantify the degree of homologous recombination was significantly smaller in plastisphere viral population than in soil and water (Mann–Whitney U test: S vs. SP, U = 34160, p = 0.002, cliff’s delta = 0.163, 95% CI [0.060, 0.262]; W vs. WP, U = 32690, p = 1.32 × 10−9, cliff’s delta = 0.32, 95% CI [0.22, 0.42]; Supplementary Fig. 4c). The pN/pS values of the soil plastisphere and water plastisphere viral populations were significantly higher than that of soil and water (Mann–Whitney U test: S vs. SP, U = 3.88 × 108, p = 5.32 × 10−29, cliff’s delta = −0.05, 95% CI [ − 0.06, −0.04]; W vs. WP, U = 1.54 × 108, p = 3.76 × 10−5, cliff’s delta = −0.02, 95% CI [ − 0.04, −0.01]; Supplementary Fig. 4d), respectively. There were 289 (1.2%), 549 (1.6%), 242 (2.0%), and 562 (2.2%) genes with pN/pS > 1 detected in soil, soil plastisphere, water, and water plastisphere viromes, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5). More than half of these annotated and positively selected genes were related to DNA replication, repair, and metabolism, and possessed abnormally high pN/pS values.

Interactions between viruses and prokaryotes in the plastisphere

The prokaryotic communities in the plastisphere were significantly distinct from those in natural environments (PERMANOVA: S vs. SP, FDR = 0.0012; W vs. WP, FDR = 0.0012; Supplementary Fig. 6a). The water plastisphere prokaryotic community was significantly more diverse (i.e., higher Richness, Chao1, and ACE indexes) and more even (i.e., higher Shannon, Simpson, and Pielou J indexes; Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). Moreover, the prokaryotic nucleotide diversity (Mann–Whitney U test: S vs. SP, U = 22720, p = 0.005, cliff’s delta = 0.165, 95% CI [0.048, 0.278]; W vs. WP, U = 14159, p = 2.56 × 10−5, cliff’s delta = −0.25, 95% CI [ − 0.35, −0.13]) and degree of recombination (Mann–Whitney U test: S vs. SP, U = 3195, p = 2.38 × 10−16, cliff’s delta = −0.61, 95% CI [−0.72, −0.47]; W vs. WP, U = 13492, p = 0.004, cliff’s delta = 0.193, 95% CI [0.063, 0.317]) in water plastisphere were significantly higher than in water, which was the opposite for the comparison of soil plastisphere and soil (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e). Notably, the Shannon diversity index of prokaryotic functions (KO diversity) significantly reduced (Mann–Whitney U test: U = 52, p = 0.038, cliff’s delta = 0.625, 95% CI [−0.095, 0.916]; Supplementary Fig. 6f) in water plastisphere with higher taxonomic diversity (Mann–Whitney U test: U = 0, p = 0.0002, cliff’s delta = −1, 95% CI [−1, −0.9522]) compared to water. The mismatch between taxonomic and functional diversities may be attributed to the changes in microbial genome size41. The average genome size of water plastisphere was significantly lower than that of water (Mann–Whitney U test: U = 64, p = 0.0002, cliff’s delta = 1, 95% CI [0.9522, 1]; Supplementary Fig. 7a). The prokaryotic genome size was positively correlated (R = 0.67, p = 2.60 × 10−5) with KO diversity, but negatively correlated (R = –0.63, p = 0.0001) with taxonomic diversity, which further showed that a relatively low diversity microbial community with large genomes could support a higher diversity of functional genes than a high diversity microbial community with small genomes (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). In addition, the GC content significantly increased in the plastisphere prokaryotic genome (Mann–Whitney U test: S vs. SP, U = 0, p = 0.0002, cliff’s delta = −1, 95% CI [ − 1, −0.9522]; W vs. WP, U = 0, p = 0.0002, cliff’s delta = −1, 95% CI [ − 1, −0.9522]; Supplementary Fig. 7b). The density curve of the predicted minimal doubling time was shown in Supplementary Fig. 8a. After removal of metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) with doubling time > 5 h due to model inaccuracies at slower growth rate45, the average doubling time was longer in soil plastisphere and water plastisphere (Mann–Whitney U test: S vs. SP, U = 3702, p = 0.184, cliff’s delta = −0.120, 95% CI [−0.287, 0.055]; W vs. WP, U = 5183, p = 7.27 × 10−5, cliff’s delta = −0.30, 95% CI [−0.43, −0.15]) than in soil and water, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8b and Supplementary Data 8). Further, there was significant positive correlation (SP: R = 0.44, p = 6.71 × 10−5; WP: R = 0.47, p = 1.45 × 10−5) between MAGs relative abundance and minimal doubling time in the plastisphere, indicating that the microbial community adapted to new niches through strategies that probably include slow growth (Supplementary Fig. 8c–f).

The 378, 102, and 44 vOTUs were linked to the dominant bacterial phyla Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota, and Actinobacteriota, respectively (Fig. 1d). Moreover, 409 vOTUs were associated with several host bacterial phyla, indicative of polyvalence (Supplementary Data 5). The 50 vOTUs distributed in Myoviridae, Siphoviridae, Podoviridae, and Inoviridae were linked to 11 host phyla, which formed 177 virus-host pairs in all samples (Fig. 2a). There were more virus-host pairs in the soil plastisphere microbial community than in soil. By contrast, the water plastisphere had fewer virus-host pairs than water, mainly caused by the Inoviridae-Patescibacteria pair, the Inoviridae-Verrucomicrobiota pair, the Podoviridae-Proteobacteria pair, the Podoviridae-Patescibacteria pair, the Siphoviridae-Bacteroidota pair, and the Siphoviridae-Patescibacteria pair (Supplementary Fig. 9). Similarly, the average proportion of lysogenic viruses separately decreased and increased (Mann–Whitney U test: S vs. SP, U = 2.86 × 106, p = 2.50 × 10−7, cliff’s delta = −0.08, 95% CI [−0.12, −0.05]; W vs. WP, U = 3.248 × 106, p = 0.016, cliff’s delta = 0.039, 95% CI [0.007, 0.071]) in soil plastisphere and water plastisphere relative to soil and water (Fig. 2b), which was also supported by the relative abundance of proviruses, lysogenic prokaryotes, and lysogenic marker genes (Supplementary Fig. 10). With respect to the community composition, viruses and hosts (beta-dissimilarity; SP: R = 0.57, p = 3.80 × 10−6; WP: R = 0.44, p = 6.00 × 10−4) correlated positively with each other only in the plastisphere (Fig. 2d, e). Overall, we detected 2717 defense systems in 13 bacterial phyla in all samples, which corresponded to 88 types and 3 known defense mechanisms “nucleic acid degradation”, “abortive infection”, and “DNA synthesis inhibition” (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Data 9). The abundance and diversity of antiviral defense systems increased in soil plastisphere in comparison with soil, whereas fewer antiviral defense systems were observed in water plastisphere relative to water, which was consistent with the results of virus-host linkages and life strategies.

Contribution of prokaryotes and viruses to methane cycling in the plastisphere

For methane metabolism of prokaryotes, the key gene families (mcr and mtr) related to methanogenesis were significantly enriched (One-way ANOVA: soil environment, d.f. between groups = 3, d.f. within groups = 12, p = 0.003, η2 = 0.672; water environment, d.f. between groups = 3, d.f. within groups = 12, p = 0.002, η2 = 0.819) in the water plastisphere and reduced in the soil plastisphere compared with corresponding control substrates (Supplementary Fig. 11). Similarly, the relative abundance of these genes increased in water plastisphere compared with the water, whereas it significantly decreased (t-test, p < 0.05) in the soil plastisphere compared with the soil (Fig. 3a). There were about 61.9% of genes involved in aceticlastic methanogenesis and methylotrophic methanogenesis having significantly higher relative abundances in water plastisphere than in water (t-test, p < 0.05). Meanwhile, similar changes were obtained in the functional genes for central methanogenic pathways (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Central methanogenic pathway that contained gene families shared by the three methanogenesis pathways: hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis, aceticlastic methanogenesis, and methylotrophic methanogenesis, was fundamental to methanogenesis. Methanogenesis was one part of the carbon cycle, which was affected by carbon catabolism. The 23 specific genes coding for glycoside hydrolases (GHs) were classified into five types of genes related to the typical carbohydrate hydrolysis (xylan, pectin, chitin, starch, and xyloglucan), which represented organic carbon decomposition. These genes were significantly enriched (t-test, p < 0.05) in the water plastisphere prokaryotic community and significantly reduced (t-test, p < 0.0001) in the soil plastisphere prokaryotic community compared with corresponding controls (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Prokaryotic genes associated with anaerobic oxidation of methane were more abundant in water plastisphere (Fig.3a and Supplementary Fig. 12b). The pmo and mmoC, as the marker genes of aerobic methane oxidation, showed increased abundances in water plastisphere compared to water, whereas significantly lower (t-test, p < 0.05) relative abundance of pmo was detected in soil plastisphere than in soil (Supplementary Fig. 14). Additionally, about 46.9% and 55.5% of the functional genes for aerobic oxidation of methane to carbon dioxide and serine cycle were significantly enriched (t-test, p < 0.05) in plastisphere prokaryotic communities, respectively. In the serine cycle, the relative abundances of genes (ppc, mdh, mtkAB, mcl) related to glyoxylate and acetyl-CoA synthetases were significantly increased (t-test, p < 0.05) in the plastisphere.

Consistently, the typical methanogens and aerobic methanotrophs of prokaryotic communities were more abundant in water plastisphere than in water, and less abundant in soil plastisphere than in soil (Supplementary Fig. 15). Among all MAGs, the only methanogens belonging to Methanoregulaceae were found in water plastisphere samples, which encoded the genes for the complete hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis metabolic pathway (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Data 10).

Significant and positive correlations were observed between pmoABC/mcrABG gene families and narBGY/nirKS/nrfA gene families in prokaryotic communities (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Data 11). The relative abundances of genes for denitrification were significantly reduced and increased in the soil plastisphere and water plastisphere, respectively (t-test, p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 18). There were 8 MAGs affiliated with Pseudomonadaceae having potential for coupling methane and nitrogen cycling process, which mainly distributed in plastisphere samples (Supplementary Fig. 16). Further, the average minimal doubling time of these MAGs (~1.7 h) was lower than that of 91.3% MAGs, and their relative abundances increased in plastisphere compared with natural environments, which suggested that Pseudomonadaceae could adapt the new niches through growing rapidly. The biomarker, Methylophilaceae, encoded the genes involved in C1 compounds oxidation to carbon dioxide and denitrification, and was significantly more abundant in the plastisphere than in natural environments (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Fig. 17).

In terms of viral communities, there were 186 vOTUs linked to methylotrophs, forming 3006 virus-host pairs in all samples (Supplementary Data 5). Methylotrophs are active in the oxidation of C1 compounds and are involved in the global methane cycling46. Notably, the number of virus-methylotroph pairs in the plastisphere was higher than that in natural environments (Supplementary Fig. 19a). For the metagenomic contigs of methanotrophs, 152 vOTUs, predominantly lysogenic viruses, were predicted to interact with them (Supplementary Fig. 19b and Supplementary Data 6). These vOTUs had higher abundance in the plastisphere compared with corresponding controls, especially the water plastisphere (Supplementary Fig. 19c). Based on the new integrated phage–host prediction method (iPHoP)47, 100 vOTUs were found to be associated with a total of 58 hosts, including methanogens, methanotrophs, and methylotrophs, which spanned eight bacterial and one archaeal genera (Supplementary Fig. 19d and Supplementary Data 7).

A total of 12 putative AMGs related to carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), ammonia assimilation, and methane cycling were identified in 41 vOTUs (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Data 11). Most of these vOTUs (51.2%) carried AMGs annotated as glycosyltransferase family 2 (GT2). There were 14 vOTUs encoding 9 AMGs associated with aerobic oxidation of methane to carbon dioxide (frmA, fdwB, and mgsA), serine cycle (glyA, gpmB, serA, and eno), and RuMP cycle (fbaB and fbp), which were more abundant in the water plastisphere than in water. Notably, 10 of the 14 vOTUs were inferred as lysogenic viruses. The host of one vOTU carrying AMGs of both eno and frmA was methylotrophs, and this virus was enriched in the water plastisphere compared to the water.

Based on the KEGG database, the 447 genes for methane cycling, 389 genes for nitrogen cycle, and 724 genes annotated as glycoside hydrolases (catabolism) were detected in 200 vOTUs, 166 vOTUs, and 724 vOTUs, respectively (Fig. 3d). In the context of methane cycling, the viruses encoded a series of genes that were involved in aerobic oxidation of C1 compounds to carbon dioxide. The genes related to the conversion of carbon dioxide to N5-methyltetrahydromethanopterin in the hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis pathway were also identified in viral genomes. Further, consistent with the result of the microbial community, the genes for aerobic oxidation of methane and C1 compounds to carbon dioxide and the serine cycle were more abundant in the plastisphere. In the water plastisphere, the relative abundances of genes for methanogenesis, glycoside hydrolases, and denitrification were increased compared with water. These AMGs-carrying vOTUs were dominated by lysogenic viruses (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Data 12). Lysogenic viruses accounted for 90.8%, 90.2%, and 85.3% of the total viruses with AMGs related to methane cycling, nitrogen cycle, and carbon catabolism, respectively. According to the results of virus-host linkages, the 51 vOTUs encoding genes for methane cycling were hosted by methylotrophs, which interacted with each other more closely in the plastisphere (Supplementary Fig. 20a). The hosts of two vOTUs carrying AMGs for C1 compounds oxidation (mgsC, mxaL) were predicted to be methylotrophs in the default database of iPHoP. Moreover, the 35 vOTUs encoding AMGs for aerobic oxidation of methane to carbon dioxide and cell material were linked to methanotrophs. These vOTUs were dominated by lysogenic viruses (32/35) and enriched in the water plastisphere (Supplementary Fig. 20b).

In the microcosm experiment 1, virus-encoded AMGs involved in methane cycling, including methanogenesis and methane oxidation, were enriched in the water plastisphere compared to the water control substrates (Supplementary Fig. 21). Conversely, the total abundance of AMGs related to methane cycling in the soil plastisphere was lower than that in the soil substrates. These results further elucidated the uniqueness of plastisphere communities relative to control substrates and the regulatory potential of the water plastisphere viral community in the methane cycling.

To more directly evaluate the methane metabolism potential of the plastisphere microbial community in water, anaerobic incubations and methane flux measurements were conducted on the water plastisphere. The average methane concentration of the group containing biofilm-attached plastic pieces (W_WP) was significantly higher (one-way ANOVA, d.f. between groups = 2, d.f. within groups = 9, p = 2.74 × 10−7, η2 = 0.965) than that of the group containing only water and the group containing plastic pieces without biofilm (Supplementary Fig. 22a). The result indicated that the methanogenic capacity of the microbial community in water plastisphere was significantly enhanced, which made the water plastisphere a hot spot of methanogenesis in the water environment. Meanwhile, in the phage transplantation experiment, the contribution of viruses from the water plastisphere to microbial methanogenesis was validated by comparing the treatment groups with bacteria and lysogenic viruses (BLV) or viruses (BV) against three controls: bacteria only (B), bacteria with autoclaved lysogenic viruses (BLV0), and bacteria with autoclaved dead viruses (BV0). Methane emissions in both the BLV and the BV groups were significantly higher than in the control groups (one-way ANOVA, d.f. between groups = 4, d.f. within groups = 15, p = 5.93 × 10−13, η2 = 0.982). The BLV group showed the highest methane emissions among all groups (Supplementary Fig. 22b). These results suggested that lysogenic viruses from the water plastisphere microbes significantly enhanced the methanogenic capacity of the microbes and played a key role in promoting methane emission of the water plastisphere.

Discussion

As a potential hallmark of the Anthropocene, the plastisphere received increasing research attention. To date, most studies have targeted bacteria and archaea, and very few have focused on viruses. In this study, the plastisphere was validated as a novel ecological niche for microorganisms by testing the compositional and functional differences between the plastisphere and control substrates. Then, we further studied the viral community in the plastisphere from paddy ecosystems by employing viral metagenomes, and our results showed that the plastisphere harbored a unique viral community. This was supported by the observation that viruses in the plastisphere were mostly novel compared to both the control substrates and the NCBI RefSeq in our study. The composition, taxon diversity, and microdiversity of plastisphere viral communities were largely different from those of the other environmental viral communities. Richness of plastisphere viral communities was significantly lower than that of other environments. However, at the viral population level, genes in the plastisphere experienced stronger selection pressure and a higher degree of recombination, which drove viral genetic dynamics to generate a higher microdiversity. Higher microdiversity could provide advantages for viral evolution and adaptation to the plastisphere environmental conditions48,49. Microdiversity reflected the intra-population genetic variation in viral populations, which could alter virus-host interactions and evolutionary dynamics for the genotype, thereby having an impact on key ecosystem functions. Collectively, these findings suggested that the unique viral community in the plastisphere may perform some specific functions50,51, which emphasized the need for further comprehensive studies in the plastisphere. The novel viruses encompassed by this study could serve as a community resource for characterizing the unknown fraction of the plastisphere.

Accumulating evidence suggests that the interactions between viruses and prokaryotes are key to regulating the structure and function of microbial communities in natural ecosystems52. In the plastisphere, distinctive interactions of viruses and prokaryotes were observed in this study. This point was supported by a positive correlation between the viral community and the prokaryotic community in beta-dissimilarity. Besides, the lifecycle of viruses was remarkably changed in the plastisphere. Compared to the ambient environment, the viruses in the water plastisphere tended to have a lysogenic lifestyle and were mainly linked to dominant bacteria, which supported the Piggyback-the-Winner hypothesis7. Similar to the deep-sea environment53, the lysogenic strategy favored by viral communities may play a key role in reprogramming host metabolism and aiding host adaptation to new niches. The strategy highlighted that the virus tended to integrate into the host genome instead of lysing the hosts for replication despite high host abundance. The integrated proviruses in host genomes would protect the hosts from reinfection through superinfection exclusion mechanism, which may explain the decreased virus-host associations in the water plastisphere compared to water environment. The pressure of viral infection on hosts would result in the evolution of host defense systems. Thus, a lower abundance of antiviral defense systems was observed in the water plastisphere microbial community with less virus-host linkage compared to the water environment. Virus-host interactions played a central role in mediating the function of the host community in ecosystems54. The distinct virus-host interactions in the plastisphere implied a specific functional role of viruses in modulating host metabolism.

We found that microbial functions connected to methane cycling were altered in the plastisphere relative to ambient environments. Some studies have indicated that altered microbial functions in the plastisphere could threaten natural ecosystem functioning43,55,56. In our microcosm experiment, the plastisphere recruited a novel microbial community with significantly distinct functional features compared with the control substrates, thus likely affecting the normal functioning of the whole ecosystem. The water plastisphere exhibited higher functional potentials for methane cycling than the ambient environment, while the soil plastisphere had an opposite effect. The key gene families for methanogenesis (i.e., hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis, aceticlastic methanogenesis and methylotrophic methanogenesis), central methanogenic pathways, and methane oxidation were enriched in the water plastisphere. More abundant GHs genes targeting diverse substrates in the water plastisphere would subsequently provide more small-molecule organic acids for the methanogenesis pathway. Notably, we observed that methane oxidation could be coupled with nitrogen reduction. Compared with ambient environments, increased abundances of genes for methane oxidation and denitrification indicated that the coupling process was enhanced in water plastisphere. The water plastisphere could thus be a hot spot of methane cycling activity. The increased abundance of methanogens and aerobic methanotrophs in the water plastisphere also supports this notion. For the soil plastisphere, although the microbial metabolic potential for methanogenesis and methane oxidation decreased, most of the genes for aerobic oxidation of C1 compounds to carbon dioxide and the serine cycle were significantly enriched. Thus, the carbon assimilation in the soil plastisphere could be enhanced through C1 compounds oxidation and the serine cycle.

The difference in microbial methane metabolism between the soil plastisphere and the water plastisphere may be attributed to the difference in microbial life history strategies. Many studies have suggested that the life history strategies of microorganisms determine their metabolic potential37,38. To advance our understanding of differences in the life history strategies of microbial communities between soil plastisphere and water plastisphere compared to the respective ambient environments, we summarized the structure and function features of prokaryotic and viral communities (Fig. 6). Firstly, compared to ambient environments, the soil plastisphere and water plastisphere microbial communities were differentiated in methanogenic potential with the differentiation of taxon diversity. The water plastisphere had a smaller average prokaryotic genome, higher microdiversity of prokaryotic community, higher methanogenic potential, less antiviral systems, fewer virus-host linkages, and a high proportion of lysogenic viruses than the water environment, while the soil plastisphere microbial community showed the opposite pattern. According to previous studies, the life history strategies of soil plastisphere and water plastisphere microbial communities corresponded to that of oligotrophic and copiotrophic communities41. Thus, the differentiation in life history strategies between soil plastisphere and water plastisphere could be attributed to the different physicochemical properties of different ecosystems, especially for the difference in resources. Soils are usually rich in nutrients for microorganisms, and although the plastisphere could adsorb organic matter, it may still be a “nutrient desert island” compared to soil environment57. However, in the relatively nutrient-poor water environment, the plastisphere could serve as a “nutrient oasis” within these deserts5,43,58. These results validated our initial hypothesis that the plastisphere microbial communities adopted different life history strategies in the soil and water environments, and thus, the soil and water plastisphere exhibited different ecological effects on methane cycling.Fig. 6Conceptual framework of the life history strategies of the plastisphere microbial community in soil and water environments, and schematic model of virus critical role in prokaryotic methane metabolism.Compared to the ambient environments, the water plastisphere microbial community has higher methanogenic potential with higher taxon diversity, while the soil plastisphere microbial community is the opposite. In the plastisphere, viruses interacted with prokaryotes associated with methane cycling more closely and had greater potential to modulate methane cycling. Viruses of the plastisphere, especially the water plastisphere, not only formed more linkages with methylotrophs but also tended to interact with methanotrophs compared with natural environments. We anticipated that the interaction may impose important impacts on microbial methane metabolism, particularly in the plastisphere. A number of studies have demonstrated that viruses frequently carry AMGs to regulate the corresponding biogeochemical cycling processes in different habitats59. For instance, some viruses in deep sea, polar, and desert ecosystems have been found to carry AMGs for carbon metabolism, such as pmoC and glnA, which regulate host carbon loss in resource-limited conditions60. In the plastisphere, we found that plastisphere viruses encoded multiple AMGs involved in methane cycling, including fwd, fae, and fdhB that had been detected in previous studies19,61, further illustrating the great potential of plastisphere viruses in modulating methane cycling. For the unique viral community of plastisphere, virus-encoded AMGs for methane cycling were enriched in the water environment and reduced in the soil environment compared to the corresponding control substrates. This finding suggested that the regulation of host methane metabolism by viruses using AMGs may vary depending on the habitats in which they dwell, as also supported by the previous study in water and sediment62. Meanwhile, in the comparison between the plastisphere and the environment, the abundances of AMGs related to methane cycling exhibited the same trend as that in the microbial community. These viruses encoding AMGs for methane cycling interacted with methylotrophs and methanotrophs more closely in the plastisphere. These results implied the effect of plastisphere viruses on host methane metabolism through AMGs. As shown in Fig. 6, the viruses played an auxiliary role in shaping the distinct functional features of the water plastisphere by modulating host methane metabolism, including methanogenesis, methane oxidation, carbon catabolism, and the coupling process of methane oxidation and nitrogen reduction. These vOTUs carrying AMGs were inclined towards a lysogenic lifestyle. This lifestyle allowed the viral genomes to persist within bacterial hosts, augmenting host and viral fitness by AMGs in the plastisphere. Through the validation of phage transplantation experiment, it was determined that lysogenic viruses significantly contributed to enhancing the methanogenic capacity of microorganisms and promoting methane production in the water plastisphere, likely attributed to the “batten down the hatches” strategy adopted by the viruses62. Specifically, these viruses compensated host methanogenic metabolism through enriched AMGs related to methanogenesis and close interaction with prokaryotes associated with methane cycling in the water plastisphere. Additionally, the expression of AMGs encoded by lysogenic viruses after insertion into the host genome could confer the host an advantage in resource acquisition, thereby driving host adaptation to new niches6,63. This was supported by the enriched AMGs related to carbon catabolism in the water plastisphere viral community. Carbon catabolism would generate small-molecule organic acids, which provide substrates for methanogenesis. Therefore, the lysogenic viruses from the water plastisphere enhanced the carbon catabolism of the hosts by the enriched AMGs and subsequently promoted host methanogenesis. Although the phage transplantation experiment has limitations in accurately quantifying the contribution of lysogenic viruses in microbial communities, it is an effective and widely used method for qualitative assessment64–66, thus providing reliable evidence for our findings.

Remarkably, these elements of virus-mediated methane auxiliary metabolism were widespread in the global plastisphere, considering that plastics expand the global microbial niche, which may alter the global carbon cycle43. We found that the AMGs involved in methane cycling, including methanogenesis and methane oxidation, were prevalent in the plastisphere viral community at a global scale. Although differences in the type and abundance of specific AMGs encoded by the plastisphere viruses existed between each country, they were dominated by genes for the serine cycle. These virus-encoded AMGs for methane cycling were enriched in the water plastisphere compared to the water control substrates, while the opposite pattern was observed in the soil plastisphere relative to the soil control substrates. Furthermore, for the plastisphere in different types of ecosystems, there were differences in the preference of functional genes encoded by viral communities, which was also verified in our experiment. Compared with the soil plastisphere, more abundant genes for central methanogenic pathways existed in the water plastisphere, indicating that the water plastisphere had a greater risk of becoming a methane source in the global environment. In summary, the global plastisphere viruses could participate in methane cycling by encoding AMGs and played an indirect role in shaping the distinct ecological functions of the plastisphere by reprogramming host metabolism. These insights reinforced the so far limited knowledge of viral contribution to methane metabolism and perhaps climate warming.

Finally, we found that the plastisphere viral community and virus-mediated microbial methane metabolism were sensitive to global change, specifically chemical pollution. In paddy ecosystems, As contamination is a global environmental problem and closely linked to methane cycling. Previous studies reported that As could affect methane production in paddy soils28, and couple with anaerobic oxidation of methane67. Meanwhile, as a key pollutant in paddy soils, As could alter microbial community characteristics profoundly32, thereby affecting the biogeochemical cycling of elements in the paddy ecosystem33. In the present study, contamination mainly affected the microbial methane metabolism potential through the viral community. This was because exposure had no significant impact on the taxon and microdiversity of plastisphere prokaryotic communities, but significantly affected the composition and increased the microdiversity of the plastisphere viral communities. Moreover, plastisphere viruses interacted with hosts more closely under As stress. The metabolic potentials for the serine cycle in the water plastisphere and aceticlastic methanogenesis in the soil plastisphere were also affected after As contamination. In plastisphere viral communities, the AMGs for serine cycle (eno) and aceticlastic methanogenesis (cooF) exhibited a variation consistent with that in microbial communities, and the AMG sequences could encode structurally intact proteins and perform their function. Combined with the results of virus-host linkages, it could be speculated that As contamination affected the microbial methane metabolism potential through viral communities. These findings further highlighted the critical role of the viral community in shaping the special functional features of the plastisphere, especially in the context of global change.

In conclusion, our study provides new insights into the unique ecological effects of the plastisphere from the perspective of viruses by combining global plastisphere data with those from an experiment. This study corroborates the critical role of viruses in shaping the distinct ecological features of plastispheres, especially in the context of global change. Moreover, we have supplemented our knowledge about the differentiation of plastisphere microbial communities in different ecosystems by considering effects on microbial life history strategies, further expanding our knowledge of the roles of the plastisphere community in ecosystems. In particular, comparing water and soil plastispheres gives rise to contrasting life history signatures, likely explained by the very different environmental context of plastic in water and soil. The water plastisphere has a greater risk of becoming a hot spot of methane emission with the auxiliary role of viruses compared to the soil plastisphere, which deserves more attention in future studies. Our study highlights the necessity of quantifying the viral contribution to the ecological effects of the plastisphere, which is of importance for more accurate prediction of the impact of plastic pollution on biogeochemical cycles and global change.

Methods

Experimental design and sample collection

Bulk soil samples (sandy loam) were collected from Zhangxi paddy field (depth: 0−20 cm) in Ningbo, China (29°48′36′′ N, 121°20′2′′ E), with a pH of 7.37, organic carbon of 21.21 g kg−1, and total nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium content of 2.29, 0.8, and 18.84 g kg−1, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). After mixing, air drying and sieving through a mesh (2 mm), the soil was divided into two parts for the control group and the As treatment group. For the As treatment group, the soil was mixed with 2Na2HAsO4·7H2O (ACS reagent, ≥98%, Shanghai, Sigma-Aldrich Trading Co. Ltd) solution to yield a final concentration of ~ 30 mg kg−1 As in soil68. This As concentration was commonly found in the paddy fields with mild As contamination69.

To systematically test the novelty of plastisphere communities and decipher the plastisphere viral communities, two independent microcosm experiments were performed in this study. The microcosm experiment was designed as described in our previous study70, simulating the paddy field under submergence conditions. Specifically, the above two types of soil were placed into boxes (80 × 50 × 23 cm) and covered with deionized water. The height of the soil layer and water layer was 15 cm and 10 cm, respectively. Four replicates were set up per group and preincubated for 14 d in an indoor environment at 25 ± 1 °C. Then, Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) pieces (200 × 80 × 0.5 mm), one of the common plastics detected in soils71–73, were cleaned with 75% ethanol and sterilized in deionized water in turn, and were vertically inserted into the soil (25 PET pieces per microcosm). Each PET piece was divided into two parts (10 cm in the soil and 10 cm in the water) from top to bottom by the soil-water interface, which would form the water plastisphere (WP) and soil plastisphere (SP) during incubation, respectively.

Microcosm experiment 1: novelty of plastisphere communities

For comparison, the control substrates (i.e., ceramic pieces and glass sheets) were cleaned and placed into boxes of the control group in the same way as the PET pieces above (6 ceramic pieces and 6 glass sheets per microcosm). Ceramic pieces and glass sheets are common non-plastic, chemically inert hard substrates in natural environments and are frequently used in control experiments with plastics74,75. After 30 d of cultivation, the control substrate and plastic samples (4 samples of each substrate per microcosm) from the control group were collected.

Microcosm experiment 2: composition and functions of plastisphere viral communities

At the end of the cultivation period (200 d), the soil, water, and plastic samples of the control group (CS, CW, CSP, and CWP, respectively) and the As treatment group (AS, AW, ASP, and AWP, respectively) were collected.

For the plastisphere samples from one microcosm, PET pieces were randomly taken and divided into the soil plastisphere samples and water plastisphere samples with sterile scissors according to the soil-water interface. Then, these two types of plastisphere samples were separately pooled together and washed three times with sterilized deionized water for further analysis. The ceramic and glass samples in soil (SC and SG) and water (WC and WG) were treated in the same way. Notably, samples from microcosm experiment 1 and microcosm experiment 2 were collected and analyzed independently.

Metagenomic sequencing and analysis

To analyze the dissimilarity of microbial community from different substrates, the control substrate and plastic samples in the microcosm experiment 1 were used for deep metagenomic sequencing. A total of 32 samples representing natural environment (soil and water environment) and new niche (plastisphere) from different treatment groups in the formal experiment were also used for metagenomic sequencing to analyze changes in microbial community functional potential. The DNA was extracted using a PowerSoil DNA isolation kit (MoBio Laboratories Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext UltraTM DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and were sequenced on the NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) on an S4 flow cell using 150 bp paired-end reads. Trimmomatic (v0.36)76 was used for adapter clipping and quality trimming of raw reads. The trimmed reads from each sample were assembled into contiguous sequences (contigs) using Megahit (v1.2.9)77, and short contigs (<1000 bp) were discarded for all downstream usages. Taxonomic classifications of metagenomic contigs were assigned using geNomad v 1.5.1 (https://github.com/apcamargo/genomad). To focus on prokaryotes, the potential eukaryotic contigs were detected and removed using EukRep (v0.6.7)78. Average genome size of each sample was evaluated by using MicrobeCensus pipeline79. GC% was calculated by using Quast v5.2.080.

These assembled contigs were binned using MetaBAT2 (v2.12) with default parameters81. Bin completeness, contamination and strain heterogeneity were evaluated by CheckM (v1.2.0)82. Bins scoring ≥50% completeness, <10% contamination, and <10% strain heterogeneity were retained as MAGs. Taxonomy of the MAGs was assigned using GTDB-Tk (v2.1.1)83 with the R207_v2 reference package. Then, all MAGs were dereplicated using dRep100 (v2.2.3)84 to create a non-redundant MAG dataset. The resulting maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was visualized and refined using ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online). Minimal cell doubling time was estimated by gRodon v2.3.085 based on codon usage bias patterns in each MAG with >10 ribosomal proteins.

Genes of the contigs and MAGs were predicted by Prodigal (v2.6.3)86. For functional annotation, the protein-coding sequences were separately compared against the KEGG database87, CAZymes88, methane cycling database (McycDB)89, and nitrogen cycling database (NcycDB)90 by kofamscan (v1.2.0), HMMER (v3.1b2)91, and DIAMOND. Abundances of genes and MAGs were quantified with Salmon (v1.1.0) and were expressed in the form of transcripts per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (TPM)92.

Amplicon sequencing

The microbial community structure was assessed by 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene amplicon sequencing using the same DNA as for metagenomics. The V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primer set 515 F/806 R (515 F: 5′-GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′; 806 R: 5′-GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). The PCR amplification was performed using protocols suggested by the Earth Microbiome Project93. The paired-end sequencing (250 bp) was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform. The Qiime2 was employed for processing of reads94. DADA2 was used to filter, denoise, and remove chimeras from reads95. After quality control, the paired-end reads were merged and clustered into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) at a 100% similarity level. To obtain taxonomic information of the ASVs, representative 16S rRNA genes were aligned against the SILVA database (v138.1)96 using the RDP classifier (v2.2) (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu).

Viral metagenomic sequencing and analysis

For virus DNA extraction, virus-like particles (VLPs) from various types of samples were first purified and concentrated by filtration and centrifugation following the previously described protocol97,98. The VLPs suspension was used for viral DNA extraction by Takara MiniBEST Viral RNA/DNA Extraction Kit Ver.5.0. The DNA samples were sent for TruSeq library construction and paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform. The raw data processing and the following assembling were the same as the metagenomic analysis described above.

Potential single-contig viral genomes were identified and screened using a combination of DeepVirfinder (v1.1)99, Virsorter2 (v2.0)100, and VIBRANT (v1.2.1)101. Only contigs longer than 5 kb were retained on following criteria: (i) DeepVirfinder score ≥ 0.9 and p < 0.01, (ii) high confidence level (score ≥ 0.9 or score ≥ 0.7 but have hallmark genes) of VirSorter2 (–keep-original-sequence), (iii) both identified by DeepVirfinder score ≥ 0.7 and p < 0.05 and VirSorter2 score ≥ 0.5. (iv) DeepVirfinder score ≥ 0.7 and p ≤ 0.05 and VirSorter2 score ≥ 0.5 were further screened using VIBRANT (virome module). Then, the identified viral contigs were clustered into the vOTUs at 95% average nucleotide identity and 85% aligned fraction using MUMmer and were estimated the completeness and contamination using CheckV v1.0.1 (database v1.2)102. The vOTUs dataset was mapped against the reference sequences to obtain the viral abundance, and read counts were normalized to per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM) using Bwa (v0.7.12)103. Prodigal was used to predict viral genes using the meta option. Genes were annotated using eggNOG-mapper (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/) based on GO (Gene Ontology)104, KEGG, and Pfam (the Protein Families)105 databases with default parameters. Virus-encoded AMGs were identified by DRAM-v (v1.2.0)106 combined with VIBRANT. Abundances of AMGs were quantified with Bwa and were expressed in the form of RPKM. Phyre2 (v2.0) was used for searching the secondary and tertiary structural homology and modeling the protein structure of AMGs (http://www.sbg.bio.ic.ac.uk/phyre2/html/page.cgi?id=index). Viral taxonomy annotations were assigned using HMMER by querying protein sequences against the HMM models from the VOGDB database (https://vogdb.org/). To complement viral classification, the vOTUs were clustered into VCs that were roughly equivalent to ICTV (International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses) prokaryotic viral genera using vConTACT2 (v0.9.17)107. The shared network formed by vConTACT2 was visualized by Cytoscape v3.8.0 to show the overlap of viruses from plastisphere with viral genomes from control substrates (this study and the collected global metagenomic data), human gut108, deep-sea sediment109, the Antarctic Ice Shelf110, and Refseq111 database. For the microcosm experiment 1 data, viral genomes were recovered from metagenome assemblies and analyzed as above.

Virus-host linkage analysis

The 3687 vOTUs were putatively linked to 587 host MAGs using three in silico methods where virus-host linkages were predicted based on: sequence similarity between CRISPR spacers in prokaryotic genomes and viral genomes, tRNA sequence similarity, and nucleotide sequence homology of prokaryotes and virus. CRISPR spacers were recovered from the prokaryotic genomes by CRISPR recognition tool (CRT, v2.1)112 and were compared against viral genomes using BLASTn (v2.9.0 + ) with the thresholds of ≥95% identity, ≤2 single nucleotide polymorphisms, and e-value ≤ 10−10. In parallel, tRNA genes in viral contigs were annotated using tRNAscan-SE (v2.0.9)113 and were blasted against the prokaryotic genomes using BLASTn (>95% sequence identity). Moreover, the viral genomes were aligned with prokaryotic genomes based on shared genomic regions through BLASTn. The matches that hit ≥ 50 bitscore, ≥70% nucleotide identity, ≥2500 bp match coverage, and e-value < 0.001 were retained114. Given the high recognition of the three host prediction methods, the viral hosts obtained by each method were combined to analyze the host range comprehensively. To maximize host prediction, virus-host linkages were further predicted based on the annotated metagenomic contigs with the above three methods. Additionally, the automated command-line pipeline iPHoP (v1.3.2) was employed to complement the analysis of virus-host linkages with its default database (confidence score threshold of 90)47.

DefenseFinder server (https://defense-finder.mdmparis-lab.com/) was used to systematically detect antiviral defense systems in the microbiome based on MacSyFinder models115, which classified the defense mechanisms as previously suggested116. The virus lifestyle (lysogenic or lytic) was inferred by Deephage (v1.0) using 0.6 as the cutoff117. Remaining vOTUs were predicted to be lysogenic or unclassified depending on if they contained the lysogenic marker proteins (transposase, integrase, excisionase, resolvase, and recombinase proteins)62. The lysogenic marker proteins were downloaded from Pfam and were identified by “hmmscan” from HMMER. Additionally, CheckV and PhiSpy (v2.3.0)118 were used to detect proviruses in prokaryotic genomes according to known virus signatures.

Taxon diversity and microdiversity analyses

Taxon diversity estimates include the alpha-diversity and beta-diversity indexes. The alpha-diversity and beta-diversity calculations were performed using vegan (v2.6-4)119 in R (v4.2.2) based on the 16S ASVs dataset and the vOTUs dataset, respectively. For beta-diversity analysis, a principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix was performed using the “vegdist” function of vegan. Statistical differences between sample groups were assessed with permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 random permutations using “adonis2” from vegan. P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons by the Benjamini–Hochberg method (FDR)120. ANOSIM (vegan) was utilized to test the magnitude of dissimilarity between microbial communities. Population microdiversity metrics, including nucleotide diversity (π) (genome-level), linkage disequilibrium measures (D’), and selection measures (pN/pS), were calculated by inStrain v1.5.3 using the representative MAGs or vOTUs dataset121.

Data acquisition and de novo assembly

In-house 19 plastisphere metagenomic data were combined with 141 publicly available sequencing samples that were retrieved by using the keywords “plastisphere” and “metagenome”, which contained 62 soil plastisphere samples and 98 water plastisphere samples in total. Meanwhile, the 22 control substrate samples, such as pinewood pellets, wooden strips, ceramic, rock, and glass beads, from the above retrieved literatures were also included in the analysis. The sequencing reads were downloaded from National Center for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Archive database or Genome sequence Archive database. Details of all samples used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 13. In the subsequent analyses, plastisphere samples collected from the same study and site were considered replicates of each other. Data processing and analyses were performed as described above. The world map showing the distribution of collected samples and the composition of AMGs involved in methane cycling was constructed using ggplot2 (v3.5.1) and scatterpie (v0.2.4) in R.

Incubation experiments for methane production potential

Anaerobic incubation experiments and methane flux measurements were conducted to assess the methane production potential of the water plastisphere microbial community. Water samples were collected from the control group of the microcosm experiment and divided into three groups with different treatments: (i) original water samples (W); (ii) water samples containing PET pieces without biofilm attached (W_P); (iii) water samples containing PET pieces with biofilm attached (W_WP). The PET pieces without biofilm attached were just the PET pieces cleaned with 75% ethanol and sterilized in deionized water. The PET pieces with biofilm attached were the water plastisphere collected from the above microcosm experiment after cultivation. Each group was set up in acid-washed and sterilized serum vials with four replicates. The headspace of each vial was flushed with Ultra High Purity N2 for 20 min (replacing the headspace 20 times) to establish an anaerobic headspace. Vials were incubated at 25 °C for 30 d.

Methane concentrations were measured via headspace equilibration and gas chromatography. At the end of the incubation period, headspace gas samples were collected after shaking the incubation vials for 2 min to reach equilibration. Samples were subsequently analyzed for methane concentrations using a Shimadzu GC-2014 gas chromatograph (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with a flame ionization detector.

Phage transplantation experiment

The bacterial and viral suspensions of the water plastisphere were extracted from 20 water plastisphere samples suspended in 4.0 L saline magnesium (SM; per L containing 5.8 g NaCl, 2 g MgSO4·7H2O, 50 mL 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 7.5)). The suspension was vortexed at a maximum speed for 3 min, shaken at 250 rpm for 2 h, and then centrifuged at 4 °C for 20 min. The obtained supernatant was filtered through a 2-μm cellulose acetate filter, and further filtered and concentrated using tangential flow filtration (TFF) (Vivaflow 200, Sartorius, Germany) with pore sizes of 0.22 μm and 100 kDa. The retentates (> 0.22 μm) were used as bacterial suspensions, and the filtrates (100 kDa–0.22 μm) were used as viral suspensions and no-virus suspensions (<100 kDa). The bacterial suspensions were incubated with mitomycin-C (MC) at a final concentration of 1 μg mL−1 and shaken at 200 rpm for 24 h in the dark. Subsequently, MC-induced lysogenic viruses were collected and concentrated (100 kDa–0.22 μm) using the TFF system. The obtained lysogenic viral suspensions were used for the following experiments.

Experiments were conducted with 20 mL autoclaved water samples from the control group of above microcosm experiment in 120-mL serum vials. To provide energy for the activities of the viruses and hosts during the culture period, glucose solution (7.5 mg mL−1) was added into each vial. The vials had the headspace flushed with N2 for 20 min (replacing the headspace 20 times). Experiment treatments consisted of (i) bacterial suspensions with no added virus (B), (ii) bacterial suspensions mixing with autoclaved lysogenic viral suspensions (BLV0), (iii) bacterial suspensions mixing with autoclaved viral suspensions (BV0), (iv) bacterial suspensions mixing with lysogenic viral suspensions (BLV), and (v) bacterial suspensions mixing with viral suspensions (BV). The virus-to-bacteria ratio was maintained at 10:1. All treatments were incubated at 25 °C with four replicates. At the end of the incubation period (30 d), headspace methane concentrations were measured as described above.

Statistical analyses

Statistics and data visualization in this study were primarily performed using R and Origin (v2021b). Line Discriminant Analysis (LDA) Effect Size (LEfSe) was performed using microeco (v1.5.0)122 in R to identify biomarkers that were statistically different among groups. Statistical significance of differences was determined using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test, t-test, and one-way ANOVA test in SPSS (v26.0). Stamp analysis was carried out to decipher the differences in genes for CAZymes between groups123. Pearson correlation was applied to evaluate the association between virus-host taxa based on the beta-diversity. The KO diversity was calculated based on the KEGG-annotated metagenomic results, and Pearson correlations were used to assess the correlations among prokaryotic average genome size, taxonomic diversity, and KO diversity. The relationship of prokaryotic minimal doubling time and MAGs abundance was also evaluated by calculating Pearson correlation scores. Spearman’s rank correlation between denitrification process and methane oxidation process at the gene level were performed. For both correlation and difference tests, associations with a p-value ≤ 0.05 were considered significant.
