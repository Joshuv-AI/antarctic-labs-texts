A straightforward tendon transfer technique for extensor pollicis longus tendon rupture
Authors: ChungTing Liu
Published: 2025

Introduction

The extensor pollicis longus (EPL) tendon plays a critical role in thumb extension and retropulsion, facilitating fine motor functions essential for daily activities. Rupture of the EPL tendon is a relatively uncommon but clinically significant injury that can lead to substantial functional impairment. The incidence is most frequently observed following distal radius fractures, as well as in association with inflammatory arthropathies, and as a complication of chronic steroid use or repetitive strain [1].

Anatomically, the EPL tendon traverses the third dorsal compartment of the wrist, passing over Lister’s tubercle, a bony prominence of the distal radius that acts as a fulcrum. This unique course renders the tendon susceptible to attritional wear or ischemic compromise, particularly in the setting of compromised vascular supply or mechanical irritation [1].

Diagnosis of EPL tendon rupture is often delayed due to subtle clinical findings in early stages. Early recognition and surgical intervention, including tendon transfer or grafting, are essential to restore thumb function and prevent long-term disability.

Traditionally, extensor indicis proprius (EIP) tendon transfer has been the most widely adopted surgical method for the treatment of EPL tendon rupture. Although EIP tendon transfer provides reliable restoration of thumb extension, it is not without limitations. Reported drawbacks include donor site morbidity, decreased independent extension strength of the index finger, and the need for a longer surgical exposure and operative time [2]. These disadvantages highlight the necessity of exploring alternative tendon transfer techniques. In this context, the extensor pollicis brevis (EPB) tendon offers a viable option due to its anatomic proximity to the EPL, smaller incision requirement, and the potential to simplify the surgical procedure while preserving the function of the index finger. Moreover, the EPB provides superior thumb abduction and extension owing to its radial vector. Among the thumb extensors, the EPB may therefore represent the most favorable combination of movements when considered as a single-tendon transfer option [3].

We conducted a pilot study to evaluate the feasibility and early outcomes of EPL to EPB tendon transfer.

Materials and methods

This retrospective observational study was conducted from 2019 to 2024. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The inclusion criteria were patients with EPL tendon rupture following distal radius fracture, and those treated with EPL to EPB tendon transfer. The exclusion criteria were patients with a history of prior thumb tendon injuries or deformities, concomitant hand or wrist conditions that could affect thumb function, previous surgery involving the EPB or other extensor tendons, or incomplete follow-up data.

This was a consecutive case series and a total of eight patients were included in the study, with a mean age of 66 years (range, 46–78 years). All patients were female. The overall mean interval from fracture to tendon rupture was 44.4 days (range, 32–62 days). Hand dominance was recorded for all patients and all injuries involved the dominant hand.

In every case, the fracture line involved or was adjacent to Lister’s tubercle. Three patients had previously undergone open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) for the distal radius fracture, while the remaining five were managed conservatively. Among the conservatively managed cases, three exhibited linear fractures and two showed radial height shortening. In the operatively treated group, sonography and radiographs revealed no evidence of screw protrusion or associated tendon irritation.

Patients were evaluated at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Outcome measures included grip strength, thumb range of motion (ROM), and pain assessment using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) [4]. The functional Kapandji score was also recorded to assess thumb opposition [5]. All functional assessments were compared to the contralateral, uninjured hand. Grip strength on the affected side was measured and adjusted for hand dominance, assuming a 10% difference between dominant and non-dominant hands [6]. The thumb ROM was measured with the forearm in a neutral position, the wrist stabilized on a flat surface, and adjacent joints supported to minimize compensatory motion. Patient satisfaction was assessed using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied, 5 = very satisfied) [7] at the final follow-up visit, which occurred at 12 months postoperatively.

Diagnosis of EPL tendon rupture was made clinically and confirmed by ultrasonography. All tendon transfer procedures were performed by the same surgeon using a standardized surgical technique.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed under general anesthesia. A 3-cm longitudinal incision was made over the dorsal aspect of the first metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint, centered along the EPB tendon. The dorsal sensory branches of the radial nerve and accompanying dorsal veins were carefully identified and protected throughout the procedure.

The distal stump of the EPL tendon was located and retrieved through the incision (Fig. 1). The insertion of the EPB tendon at the base of the proximal phalanx was exposed. No significant anatomical variations were observed across patients, facilitating consistent tendon transfer. The distal EPL stump was then gently pulled to achieve full extension of the thumb at both the interphalangeal (IP) and MCP joints. The proper tension of the transfer was judged intraoperatively by positioning the wrist in neutral and ensuring that the thumb IP joint could be fully extended without undue tightness, while still allowing adequate flexion. This balance was confirmed by passively moving the thumb through its motion arc before final fixation.

Two small longitudinal incisions were made on the EPL tendon proximal to the MCP joint. The EPB tendon was woven through these slits, creating a tendon-to-tendon overlap between the EPL and EPB tendons (Fig. 2) [8]. Multiple horizontal mattress sutures were placed at the overlapping segment to secure the tendon weaving and ensure stable coaptation (Fig. 3). Any redundant portion of the EPL tendon was excised. Postoperatively, a thumb spica splint was applied to maintain the thumb in full extension and protect the tendon transfer during the initial healing phase.

Rehabilitation protocol

Postoperatively, the thumb was immobilized in a full-time extension thumb spica splint for 4 weeks. The thumb IP and MP joints were maintained in full extension, the wrist positioned in approximately 30 degrees of extension, and the thumb placed midway between radial and palmar abduction.

At 4 weeks post-surgery, both active and passive ROM exercises were initiated. Progressive strengthening and more intensive ROM exercises were introduced beginning at 8 weeks, although activities involving sudden or forceful thumb flexion were strictly avoided.

By 12 weeks postoperatively, unrestricted use of the wrist and thumb was permitted, with full restoration of functional activities.

Results

The mean operative time of tendon transfer was 27.1 min (range, 20–35 min), with minimal intraoperative blood loss. The mean length of the surgical incision was 3.1 cm (range, 2.7–3.5 cm). All patients tolerated the procedure well, and none required hospital admission.

All patients were evaluated at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. The mean grip strength of the unaffected hand was 23.1 kg (range, 19–28 kg). The grip strength of the affected hand was 19.0 kg (range, 16–24 kg) at 3 months, 20.1 kg (range 17–24 kg) at 6 months, and 20.4 kg (range, 17–24 kg) at 12 months. Compared with the unaffected side, the affected hand regained 82.3% (range, 77.0% − 87.6%) of grip strength at 3 months, 87.1% (range, 83.9% − 90.3%) at 6 months, and 88.2% (84.6% − 91.8%) at 12 months postoperatively. All patients in this study sustained injuries to their dominant hand. The dominance-adjusted recovery rates of grip strength were 74.0% (range, 69.2% − 78.8%) at 3 months, 78.4% (range, 75.5% − 81.3%) at 6 months, and 79.4% (range, 76.1% − 82.7%) at 12 months.

The mean ROM of the thumb IP joint was 80.0° (range, 75°−85°) at 3 months, 88.8° (range, 80°−90°) at 6 months, and remained unchanged at 88.8° (range, 80°−90°) at 12 months postoperatively. The mean ROM of the thumb MCP joint was 45.0° (range, 40°−50°) at 3 months, all improved to the full ROM of 50.0°at 6 months, and remained stable at 12 months. No further improvement in IP joint ROM was observed beyond 6 months. The mean functional Kapandji score was 8.6 (range, 8–9) at 3 months, with all patients achieving the full score of 10 at both 6 and 12 months postoperatively. All patients reported a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain score of 0 at 3, 6, and 12 months. All patients expressed satisfaction Likert scale 5 (very satisfied) with the outcome at the 12-month postoperative visit.

No intraoperative or postoperative complications, such as infection, tendon re-rupture, donor site morbidity, or complex regional pain syndrome, were observed in our series.

Discussion

According to our study, the EPL to EPB tendon transfer technique demonstrated reduced surgical complexity, a smaller incision, and shorter operative time in the treatment of EPL tendon rupture. Postoperative outcomes, including grip strength, thumb ROM, pain relief, and functional recovery, were excellent.

The EPL to EPB tendon transfer was first introduced in 1972 for the management of EPL tendon rupture in patients with rheumatoid arthritis. This procedure was proposed for three main reasons: [1] it preserves the EIP tendon, which may later be required for reconstruction of the finger extensors; [2] it restores thumb extension in an abducted rather than adducted position; and [3] it is technically less demanding to perform. In the original report, satisfactory results were achieved in 39 of 40 cases, although 9 patients exhibited suboptimal IP joint function [9]. More recently, Kastanis et al. presented a case series of eighteen patients with spontaneous EPL rupture treated with EPB transfer, and also demonstrated favorable functional outcomes at one-year follow-up [10].

EPL to EPB tendon transfer is indicated in patients with confirmed EPL rupture, particularly following distal radius fractures, when restoration of thumb extension is required. It is especially valuable in cases where the EIP tendon is unavailable or where minimizing donor site morbidity and surgical complexity is desirable. Contraindications include severe soft tissue compromise, local infection, insufficient EPB tendon function, or patient noncompliance with postoperative rehabilitation.

Traditionally, EIP tendon has been the preferred donor for EPL reconstruction because of its similar excursion and line of pull. However, EIP transfer is associated with certain drawbacks, including donor site morbidity, loss of independent index finger extension, and the need for a more extensive surgical exposure [2]. In contrast, the EPB tendon offers several advantages: (1) its proximity to the EPL allows for a shorter and more straightforward surgical approach, reducing operative time; (2) harvesting the EPB avoids functional compromise of the index finger, thereby preserving hand dexterity; and (3) in many patients, the EPB provides adequate excursion and strength to restore isolated thumb extension, as observed in our series. Although anatomical variations of the EPB exist, intraoperative assessment enables determination of its suitability as a donor [11]. Collectively, these factors suggest that EPB tendon transfer is a viable alternative to conventional EIP transfer in selected patients.

Nevertheless, limitations of the EPB tendon transfer must be acknowledged. The EPB demonstrates considerable anatomical variability and may be absent, hypoplastic, or exhibit limited excursion, reducing its reliability as a donor. Moreover, the EPB is generally a weak thumb extensor that contributes primarily to MCP rather than IP joint motion, which may restrict its capacity to fully restore EPL function in cases requiring strong or sustained extension [11]. Third, careful intraoperative tensioning is critical because of its smaller caliber compared with the EPL. Finally, long-term outcomes regarding durability and resistance to fatigue are not yet well established, and comparative data with the conventional EIP transfer remain limited.

Among the various surgical options, extensor indicis proprius (EIP) tendon transfer remains the most commonly employed technique for reconstructing ruptured EPL tendons [2]. According to the literature, complete recovery of index finger ROM was achieved in all patients following EIP tendon transfer in a long‑term case series. Furthermore, most of patients regained the ability to extend the thumb against resistance. The mean IP joint ROM of the thumb was − 5° ± 4° in extension and 67° ± 15° in flexion. The mean QuickDASH score was 16 ± 14. These findings indicate favorable long‑term functional recovery and patient‑reported outcomes following EIP to EPL tendon transfer [12, 13]. However, reported surgical incision lengths for EIP transfer range from 3 to 5 cm, and operative times vary across studies [2]. In our series, the EPL to EPB transfer required a mean incision length of 3.1 cm and a mean operative time of 27.1 min, while achieving outcomes comparable to those reported for conventional EIP transfer.

In our series, although IP joint ROM improved from 80° to 88.8°, it did not reach the expected normal range, in contrast to the near-complete recovery observed in the MCP joint. This incomplete recovery indicates that the transfer may not fully restore the thumb’s anatomic motion arc, even when functional outcomes are favorable. Clinically, the residual deficit may be negligible in most daily activities, since key functions such as grip, pinch, and object manipulation largely depend on composite thumb motion rather than isolated IP joint excursion. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge this limitation when counseling patients, particularly those whose occupations or hobbies demand fine motor precision or maximal thumb flexion-extension.

Complete detachment of the EPB may theoretically reduce independent MCP joint extension strength. However, in our series, no functional deficit or weakness in thumb MCP extension was observed postoperatively. This suggests that the transfer preserves functional thumb motion despite loss of the native EPB insertion.

In our study, the mean interval from distal radius fracture to EPL tendon rupture was 44.4 days (range, 32–62 days). However, this interval may vary considerably and delayed presentations are possible. According to a study by Laane et al., the incidence of EPL tendon rupture following distal radius fracture was 0.4%, with a mean interval of 3.7 months between the initial injury and tendon rupture. Notably, only a minority of cases were associated with mechanical irritation from prominent dorsal screws, indicating that other etiological factors, such as vascular compromise or attritional degeneration, may contribute to the pathogenesis of EPL rupture [14]. Therefore, extended clinical follow-up after distal radius fractures is recommended to monitor for this rare but significant complication.

In our study, the fracture line involved or was adjacent to Lister’s tubercle in all cases. Previous reports similarly highlighted the association between fracture patterns around Lister’s tubercle and subsequent EPL tendon rupture [15–17]. These findings suggest that EPL tendon rupture is relatively common in distal radius fractures involving Lister’s tubercle. Therefore, careful assessment of thumb IP joint extension should be routinely performed in patients with this fracture pattern.

This study has several limitations. First, the measurement of thumb ROM is inherently subjective, which may introduce observer bias. Second, the retrospective observational design limits the ability to establish causal relationships. Third, the follow-up period in this study is relatively short. While our results demonstrate favorable early outcomes, longer-term follow-up is necessary to fully evaluate the durability and potential late complications of EPL to EPB tendon transfer. Fourth, the sample size was relatively small, and all patients were elderly females with dominant-hand injuries. This severely limits the generalizability of our findings. Future studies with larger sample sizes and prospective, randomized controlled designs are required to confirm and expand upon our results.

Conclusion

EPL to EPB tendon transfer appears to be a feasible option for restoring thumb extension in elderly patients with EPL rupture. Early functional outcomes were satisfactory, with good restoration of thumb motion and grip. However, further studies with larger sample size and longer follow-up are needed to confirm the efficacy and generalizability of this procedure.
