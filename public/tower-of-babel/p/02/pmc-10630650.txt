Sanitization at different times after floor egg collection for hatchability, microbiological quality, and performance of broilers
Authors: Claudio M.A. Franco, Itallo C.S. Araújo, Tainá S.B. Lopes, Thayna F. Gonçalves, Bruno T.A. Costa, Lorena S. Sousa, Isadora M.S. Araújo, Marcelo R. Souza, Leonardo J.C. Lara
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In this study, the effects of egg type and sanitization time on eggshell and yolk sac microbiological quality, hatchability, and broiler performance at 7 and 35 d of age were evaluated. A total of 3,440 eggs from a commercial flock Cobb500 were randomly assigned in a 2 × 2 factorial scheme, separated by the type of egg (clean nest or floor) and eggs sanitization at 30 or 120 min after collection, totaling 4 treatments. After hatching, 312 male chicks were randomly selected and distributed according to treatment. Egg type and timing of eggshell sanitization did not influence the microbiological quality of the eggshell surfaces or hatchability (P > 0.05). The number of contaminated unhatched eggs was higher in the floor eggs group (P ≤ 0.05). There were no interactions between the studied factors or individual effects on feed intake, body weight gain, or feed conversion ratio (P > 0.05) in birds at 7 and 35 d of age. Floor eggs sanitized after 120 min had reduced productive factor compared to birds from clean nest eggs. It was concluded that the incubation of floor eggs sanitized 30 or 120 min after collection did not influence hatching results. However, a delay in the sanitization of floor eggs can increase the contamination of the chick yolk sac and consequently reduce the viability of broilers at 35 d. Floor eggs should therefore be sanitized 30 min than 120 min after collection.

INTRODUCTION

Floor egg incubation has recently been studied due to the high demand for day-old chicks in world (Costa et al., 2022; Perić et al., 2022). These eggs are more prone to contamination from dirt on the shell surface, making them more susceptible to microbial contamination (Van den Brand et al., 2016; Costa et al., 2022). Deeming et al. (2002) presented evidence that higher contamination in floor eggs may reduce their hatchability, owing to the elevated rate of infection found in the yolk sacs of dead embryos during incubation. However, studies on the effect of egg categories on hatchability, progeny quality, and productive performance are scarce. According to Sheppard and Duncan (2011), flocks of young breeders can account for up to 20% of floor eggs. Nonetheless, the authors concluded that neglecting to incubate these eggs could result in considerable losses in production costs.

Floor eggs are generally sanitized in broiler breeder farms along with cleaned nest eggs within 120 min of egg collection. The hatching egg sanitization procedure should be performed to reduce the microbial contamination of eggshells and bacterial infection in developing embryos and neonatal chicks (Clímaco et al., 2018; Melo et al., 2019). Therefore, efficient cleaning and sanitization programs are fundamental to good performance in the poultry industry, especially in antibiotic-free broiler production. Few studies have examined the appropriate interval between laying and sanitizing fertile eggs to reduce microbial load on the shell surface and minimize internal egg contamination.

Studies on the effect of egg category on hatchability, progeny quality, and production performance of broilers for up to 35 d are still scarce. The aim of this study was therefore to investigate the effects of the interval between eggs collection and sanitization of clean nest and floor eggs using paraformaldehyde (3 g/m3) on the microbial counts on the eggshell surface and newly hatched chick yolk sacs, in addition to evaluating the incubation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

All procedures used in the experiments of this study received prior approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of the Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais, Brazil (protocol number 85/2020).

Eggs Collection and Experimental Treatment

The eggs were collected from a broiler breeder farm in Carmo do Cajuru, Minas Gerais, Brazil (20.1851°S, 44.7711°W). The treatments were arranged in a completely randomized 2 × 2 factorial design with 2 egg types (clean nest or floor) and 2 sanitization cycles (30 or 120 min after collection), totaling 4 treatments.

A total of 3,344 eggs were collected on the same day from a 62-wk-old Cobb500 commercial breeder flock. Half of the sampled eggs (1,672) were collected directly from the floor, and the remaining eggs were collected from automatic nests. The laying group-nests were commercial automatic nests (1.15 × 0.50 × 0.50 m, width × depth × height) (Tecnoesse) was used 1 individual nest per 6.5 hens. There were 49,262 hens and 4,478 roosters in the breeders’ house and the photoperiod was set to 16L:8D during the experimental period. In the week when the experiment was carried out, the percentage of floor eggs collected in the facility was 6.5 ± 0.5%. Prior to collection, all the eggs from the previous day were removed to ensure that “sleeping” eggs were not used in the experiment. Eggs were collected from hatching trays with the large end facing upward. Five samplings were performed on the same day with an interval of 2 h between them and sanitization was carried out 30 or 120 min after each collection. The floor eggs were selected as described by Costa et al. (2022). Clean nest eggs were those collected from the nest, and which did not show any dirty. Floor eggs were those collected from the breeders house floor, which did not show any dirty or that showed slight dirt (litter and feces) were cleaned with the aid of a small knife, following the protocols of the breeder farm. All egg collectors used disposable gloves, and between each collection, the gloves were changed; those who collected the floor eggs did not handle the nest eggs and vice versa.

The fumigation compartment has a volume of 15.3 m3. The concentration of paraformaldehyde used for fumigation was 3.0 g/m3 for a total of 46 g (Melo et al., 2019). The application time was standardized to 25 min for burning and chemical agent action, followed by 20 min for exhaustion before opening the chamber. This configuration ensured that the gas circulated throughout the compartment for a time considered optimal for the agent to work. The temperature and relative humidity in the fumigation chamber were set from 24.5°C to 26°C and 75 to 90%, respectively. For the automatic control of temperature and humidity, a CASP automation panel was connected to a Mondial NUA-02 room humidifier in the optimal range of paraformaldehyde action. After sanitization, all eggs were stored on hatching trays in an air-conditioned room (22°C) per 24 h.

Microbial Evaluation of Eggshells

After sanitization, the eggs were collected for microbiological analysis. In total, 128 eggs (64 clean nest eggs and 64 floor eggs) were used for microbiological analyses of the shell surface after treatment. The eggs were distributed into 2 groups of 16 eggs each and subdivided into 4 replicates. Gloves were worn during collection, and the eggs were placed in sterile plastic bags (Whirl-Pak) in a pool of 4 eggs per bag and identified according to the treatment. All collected eggs were refrigerated at 4°C and transported to the laboratory for microbiological analyses (Melo et al., 2019; Costa et al., 2022).

The microorganisms on the eggshell were counted according to the methodology described by Melo et al. (2019) and carried out 24 h after collection (storage temperature from 19.1°C to 22.2°C). For the analysis, each bag containing a pool of 4 eggs was reopened in a sterile environment and 250 mL of 0.1% phosphate-buffered saline solution (PBS) was added. The bag was closed and the eggs were manually scrubbed for 5 min to remove bacterial cells from the surface of the shells. Then, 25 mL of the rinse solution was serially diluted in 0.1% PBS on the decimal scale. Then, 100 µL of the diluted samples were plated on 80 plates for growth of total aerobes (PCA) (Oxoid Ltd., Basindstone, Hampshire, UK) up to a dilution of 10−2 for clean nest eggs and 10−3 for floor eggs to count aerobic mesophilic microorganisms. Also, 100 µL of the diluents were plated on 64 MacConkey agar plates (BD DifcotmTM, Sparks, MD) to count 10−2 Enterobacteriaceae. All plates were incubated aerobically at 37°C for 48 h and the colonies were counted, registered, and the results were calculated in log10 CFU/poll of 4 eggs.

Incubation

After 24 h of storage at the breeder farm (22°C), eggs were transported to a hatchery located in the city of São Sebastião do Oeste, MG (20.2780°S, 45.0035°W). In the hatchery, the correct position of the eggs (air chamber upward) was ensured, and the eggs were distributed according to the treatments in 44 incubation trays (11 per treatment), with a capacity of 76 eggs per processing, totaling 836 eggs per treatment. After the conditioning in the trays, the eggs were stored for 48 h in the egg room at average temperature of 18°C and average humidity of 72%. Before entering the setter, the eggs underwent preincubation, which consisted of previously keeping them at 26°C for 12 h. The eggs were then incubated together (Peebles and Brake, 1987) at 37.4°C (99.4°F) with 58% of relative humidity (RH) in a CASP 125E multistage incubator (Amparo, São Paulo, Brazil), with a capacity of 124,476 eggs. The eggs were rotated 45°, 24 times daily (for 18 d).

The 44 trays containing the experimental eggs (76 per tray) were identified and equally distributed at 3 different positions inside the 4 trolleys (3 upper, 5 middle, and 3 lower positions). The other spaces of incubation trolleys were occupied with other trays containing eggs from the same broiler breeder batches to maintain the environmental conditions of the incubator within required technical standards.

At 18 d of incubation, the eggs that showed embryonic development were transferred to specific hatching baskets and kept in a single trolley and sent to the hatcher with a capacity of 20,736 eggs (CASP 21E, Amparo, São Paulo, Brazil). The hatcher conditions were between 98.0°F (36.6°C) and 96.7°F (36°C) and RH from 78 to 83%.

After 504 h incubation, all hatched chicks in each basket were counted, and hatchability in relation to the total number of eggs set (%) and hatchability vs. fertile eggs (%) were calculated. For all incubation variables 11 trays with 76 eggs was considered the repetitions.

The chicks were graded according to hatchery in first-class, second-class, or eliminated (Costa et al., 2022). All hatched chicks were classified according to general condition (degree of dehydration, mobility, reflex, and activity) and the condition of the navel (well healed or open) was checked also, all the chick were feather sexed. Male chicks were counted and vaccinated (subcutaneously against Marek, New Castle, and Gumboro disease). Then, newly hatched chicks were separated according to the treatments and kept in a chick box in the dispatch room at an ambient temperature of 24°C.

Residue Analysis

Residue analysis was performed thrice during the incubation: after 12 d, during the 18-d transfer, and at the end. In the first 2 stages, candling was done to remove infertile and dead eggs. Finally, at the end of the incubation, all unhatched eggs from the 11 trays of each treatment were evaluated. According to Hubbard (2015), unhatched eggs were broken and their contents were investigated to determine the embryonic stage and cause of embryo death. Three phases of embryonic mortality were considered: initial: from 1 to 7 d of development; intermediate: from 8 to 14 d and final: from 15 to 21 d (Hubbard, 2015). In addition, infertile eggs, external pecked eggs containing live or dead chicks, and eggs with evidence of bacterial or fungal contamination were also counted.

Microbial Evaluation of the Yolk Sac and Cecum

A microbiological assessment of the yolk sac was performed according to the method described by Costa et al. (2022). Twelve saleable male chicks per treatment (experimental unit) were randomly selected and brought to the laboratory for microbiological analysis of the yolk sac. Each chick was an experimental unit. Then, 24 h posthatching, the chicks were euthanized by cervical dislocation to aseptically collect the yolk sac. One gram of yolk sac content per chicken was serially diluted in 0.1% PBS. A 100 μL aliquot was plated on 80 plates PCA agar for counts of aerobic mesophilic microorganisms (PCA) (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK) and 64 MacConkey plates agar (BD Difcotm, Sparks, MD) for counts of Enterobacteriaceae. After 48 h of aerobic incubation at 37°C, colonies were counted on each plate. The results were recorded as log10 CFU/yolk sac.

To count the lactic acid bacteria in the cecum, analyses were performed on 12 chicks per treatment. After euthanasia, the cecum was aseptically removed, and part of the luminal content of each bird was recovered in a preweighed empty tube, which was weighed again to determine the weight of the sample collected. Then, 0.1% PBS was added to the tubes to allow a 10−1 dilution. Centesimal dilutions of up to 10−7 were prepared with 0.1% PBS. A volume of 100 µL of each dilution was plated on Man, Rogosa, and Sharp agar (MRS, Acumedia, Lansing, MI). The plates were aerobically incubated at 37°C and the counts of lactic acid bacteria were performed. The results are expressed as log10 CFU/yolk sac.

Broiler Performance

A total of 312 male first-class chicks (78 from each treatment) were randomly selected and distributed in a completely randomized design in a 2 × 2 factorial scheme, separated by egg type and duration of sanitization, for a total of 4 treatments. The chicks were separated into transport boxes and identified according to the treatment. On the same day, chicks in each treatment group were randomly selected and housed in 4 transport boxes, totaling 78 chicks per box. Boxes containing chicks were sent immediately to the Animal Metabolism Laboratory at UFMG in Belo Horizonte, Minas Gerais, Brazil. The chicks were transported in trucks as described by Costa et al. (2022). Inside the laboratory, the chicks waited for 20 h, totaling 24 h of holding, simulating the placement time used by several companies. The air temperature inside the laboratory was 30°C (±1) and with 65% relative humidity.

The chicks were housed in a 15 m2 poultry rearing room. It had 24 metallic cages of 1 m2 without litter, equipped with 50 cm feeders and 1 nipple drinker per cage. Upon the arrival of the chicks, the air conditioning was adjusted to 32°C. The room has an air renewal system. From housing to the end of the experiment, the birds were offered water and mash ad libitum, formulated to meet all production stages in accordance with Cobb Vantress (2018), and distributed daily until the end of the experiment. Starter (3,050 kcal ME/kg, 22.0% CP and 1.18% dLys) and Finisher (3,150 kcal ME/kg, 19.5% CP, and 1.05% dLys) diets were fed for 1 to 21 and 22 to 35 d, respectively. The ambient temperature followed the recommendations of the strain manual (Cobb Vantress, 2018) and the light program used was 6D:18L throughout the rearing phase. The temperature and humidity were measured daily using a conventional digital thermometer, along with the chick mortality.

The chicks were weighed and distributed into 6 replicates (cages) with 13 birds each (13 bird/m2), with an average initial weight of 46.8 g. Total feed intake, body weight, body weight gain, feed conversion ratio, and viability were evaluated at 7 and 35 d. The production factor (PF) for the experimental period was calculated using the following formula: (daily weight gain (kg) × viability (%)/feed conversion ratio) × 100. All the dead chicks were necropsied every day. Postmortem analysis revealed the main lesions in different organs. The lesions were noted to establish high-quality diagnostic hypotheses.

Statistical Analysis

All data were first evaluated for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test (5%). Means were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the R software (2014). Averages were compared using Tukey's test at P ≤ 0.05. The mean performance of each pen was used as the experimental unit.

The statistical model used to test the effect of treatments was:Yij=μ+T1i+T2j+Yij+εij,in which Yijk is the quantitative response variable; μ is the overall mean; T1 is the effect of treatment (“i” = clean nest eggs or floor eggs); T2 is the effect of treatment (“j” = 30 or 120 min after collection); Y is the effect of factors “i”; “j” and ɛ is the random error.

DISCUSSION

This study is the first to evaluate the interactions between egg type and sanitization time after egg retrieval. However, these factors did not affect the primary incubation parameters. Though eggs collected from the litter presented higher contamination than eggs collected from the nest, the result was expected due to the presence of microorganisms in broiler breeders house litter (Costa et al., 2022). Greater contamination of floor eggs and the time of sanitation are factors that determine broiler chicks performance. Broiler chicks from floor eggs sanitized for 120 min after the collection had lower viability, which compromised production efficiency at 35 d.

The total counts of aerobic mesophilic microorganisms on the eggshell surface were ≤5.0 CFU/mL for all treatments. According to De Reu et al. (2005), under appropriate hygiene in a production environment, the counts of total aerobic mesophilic microorganisms should be below 5.0 CFU/mL log10. Costa et al. (2022) found that the counts of these microorganisms on the eggshell surface of floor eggs before sanitization with paraformaldehyde were around 6.51 CFU/mL log10. Our results prove that fumigation can reduce the counts of total aerobic mesophilic microorganisms on the surface of the floor shells and clean nest eggs. Mechanical cleaning of eggshells, which is a standard procedure on broiler breeder farms where the experiment was conducted, may also have contributed to the low levels of eggshell contamination.

The yolk sac contents of chicks sanitized 120 min after collection showed greater contamination. We can contextualize a possible gradual microbial proliferation during the incubation of the sanitized floor eggs 120 min after collection, as the process consists of adequate conditions for microbiological growth, particularly temperature, humidity, and nutrient availability. According to Cortés et al. (2004) and Clímaco et al. (2018), the bacterial count on the eggshell surface increases during incubation; consequently, this factor may be a probable cause of yolk sac contamination.

In addition, these treatments did not affect fertility or hatchability. Furthermore, intermediate mortality (8–14), final mortality (15–21), live pecks, and dead pecks were unaffected by the treatment. These results confirm the findings of Costa et al. (2022), who did not report differences in the hatchability of floor eggs compared to clean nest eggs. In contrast, Van den Brand et al. (2016) and Ahamed et al. (2019) found lower hatchability of floor eggs compared to clean nest eggs; however, a comparison of the results of these studies is difficult because the authors did not report the interval between collections, egg selection procedures, and the type of sanitization.

Residual analysis of unhatched eggs revealed higher contamination of floor eggs. At the time of laying, the eggs are heated due to the temperature of the hen and appear moist due to the deposition of the cuticle, which makes them more prone to eggshell contamination, especially when placed in environments with a high microbiological load, such as house litter (Hameed et al., 2014). Shortly after oviposition, the process of egg cooling begins with the production of a natural flow of air from the surface of the shell to the interior of the egg through the eggshell pores. In this way, the simultaneous carrying of microorganisms occurs (Scott and Swetnam, 1993). Van den Brand et al. (2016), Ahamed et al. (2019), and Costa et al. (2022) observed that floor eggs were more contaminated during incubation than clean nest eggs. These results align with the findings of the present study.

Taking into account the factors studied, initial embryonic mortality was lower for clean nest eggs sanitized 120 min after collection than for floor eggs sanitized at the same time. These results indicate the need to sanitize eggs, particularly floor eggs, in the shortest possible time. Araújo and Albino (2011) recommended sanitizing eggs no later than 30 min after laying, regardless of category. The shorter time for sanitization reduced the contamination of the eggshell, and consequently, the entry of microorganisms into its interior. Eggs that remain in dirty environments such as poultry house litter can further increase contamination (Costa et al., 2022).

Regarding broiler performance, the treatments, egg type, and time of sanitation did not affect feed intake, body weight gain, or feed conversion ratio at 7 and 35 d. Similar results were reported by Van den Brand et al. (2016) and Costa et al. (2022), who studied broiler performance on the floor and clean nest eggs. However, clean nest eggs showed higher viability during the placement of up to 7 d of life compared to floor eggs, regardless of the time of sanitization. During the first week, chicks from floor eggs were apathetic, stunted, and later died. Necropsy revealed a picture characteristic of sepsis and omphalitis. The higher percentage of aerobic mesophilic microorganisms and enterobacteria isolated from the yolk sac content of chicks originating from floor eggs may have been the cause of the high initial mortality and consequent low viability in the first 7 d of life. According to Almeida et al. (2016), laboratory diagnosis of first-week mortality due to initial contamination of the yolk sac, navel, or both, characterized as omphalitis, is very common in broiler production.

At 35 d, at the end of the experiment, broilers from floor eggs sanitized for 120 min after collection showed lower viability than birds from the other treatments. From the eighth day onward, birds in this treatment group showed signs of prostration and apathy. After death, signs suggestive of sepsis, including pericarditis, perihepatitis, aerosacculitis, and peritonitis were observed at necropsy. These findings explain the higher mortality rate of birds in this egg category, and hence the direct impact on the viability during the experimental period.

In contrast, broiler chickens from floor eggs sanitized 30 min after collection showed higher viability at 35 d than those from floor eggs sanitized 120 min after collection, and the numbers were similar to those of broiler chickens from clean nest eggs, regardless of the time of sanitization. This can be explained by the sanitization applied in this treatment since 30 min between collection and sanitization was sufficient to positively reduce the mortality rate at 35 d. Therefore, 30 min of sanitization of this type of egg after collection resulted in a reduction in microbiological contamination, which had a positive effect on the viability of broiler chickens.

The result for PF, in the same way, was affected when the broiler chickens came from floor eggs sanitized 120 min after the collection. PF was lower than that of the other treatments and was mainly influenced by the lower viability observed at 35 d. The current study increased the risk when floor eggs were sanitized 120 min after collection, which is enough to render the fumigation ineffective. Therefore, the time elapsed between this interval was crucial for the reduced viability and inferior PF results. Barnett et al. (2004) and Khabisi et al. (2012) concluded that it is possible for floor eggs, which have higher levels of shell contamination with the presence of cracks, to have increased chances of penetration of microorganisms before and during incubation, affecting mortality, embryonic growth, and broiler performance.

CONCLUSIONS

Sanitization of eggshells should be performed within 30 min of collection to achieve productive results for broiler chickens. Therefore as a routine in the breeder's farm, a greater number of collections for egg sanitization should be encouraged at shorter intervals to guarantee the quality of fertile eggs.
