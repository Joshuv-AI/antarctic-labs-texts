Inverse association between hydroxychloroquine use and depressive symptoms in systemic lupus erythematosus: a single-center cross-sectional study
Authors: Xiaoqin Wang, Leilei Yang, Bingjie Gu, Qijie Ren, Dinglei Su, Nan Che
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHydroxychloroquine serves as a foundational medication in the treatment of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), exhibiting multiple roles including immune modulation and cardiovascular protection. However, current research on its effect on depressive symptoms in SLE patients remains limited and yields conflicting findings.MethodsA single-center cross-sectional study was conducted at the Nanjing First Hospital, Nanjing Medical University between November 1, 2022 and December 30, 2023. A total of 106 adult Chinese patients meeting SLE diagnostic criteria were enrolled. Severity of depressive symptoms was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), alongside demographic, clinical, pharmacological, and laboratory data collection. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0, incorporating univariate and multivariate linear regression analysis.ResultsA total of 53.8% of the studied SLE patients exhibited depressive symptoms. Of these, mild symptoms were present in 31.2% of the patients, and a further 22.6% were burdened with moderate-to-severe symptoms. Univariate analysis indicated that hydroxychloroquine use was associated with lower PHQ-9 scores (Z = -2.253, p = 0.024), a finding corroborated by multivariate analysis showing a significant inverse association (B = -2.095, β = -0.192, p = 0.012). Meanwhile, in contrast to the negative correlation between sleep duration and depressive symptoms (B = -0.728, β = -0.231, p = 0.004), fatigue showed a positive correlation (B = 4.589, β = 0.401, p < 0.001). No significant associations were found between PHQ-9 scores and the levels of multiple cytokines (including interleukin (IL)-2, IL-4, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and interferon (IFN)-γ), inflammatory markers (erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP)), or daily steroid dose.ConclusionsThis study reveals an inverse association between hydroxychloroquine and SLE-related depressive symptoms. However, whether this association implies that the use of HCQ can reduce the risk of depression or alleviate depressive symptoms in SLE patients requires verification through rigorously designed prospective cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41927-026-00634-y.Keywords: Hydroxychloroquine, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Depression, Depressive symptom, Patient Health Questionnaire-9

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by autoantibody production, immune complex deposition and multisystem involvement, including the skin, joints, kidneys, and nervous system [1]. Neuropsychiatric manifestations, collectively termed neuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus (NPSLE), encompass a wide range of neurological and psychiatric symptoms, such as cognitive dysfunction, seizures, psychosis, and mood disorders, notably depression [2]. The prevalence of depression among SLE patients varies, with studies reporting rates between 27% and 43.2%, significantly higher than in the general population [3–5]. The comorbidity of SLE and depression presents a complex clinical challenge, as their synergistic interaction complicates disease management and can exacerbate physical symptoms, compromises patients’ quality of life, and heightens the risk of cardiovascular disease and suicide [6–8].

The pathogenesis of depression in SLE remains incompletely understood, with multifaceted risk factors implicated in its development. Existing research has predominantly focused on identifying associations between specific demographic, clinical, and therapeutic variables and depression in SLE patients, though findings have been heterogeneous and often conflicting [8]. Disease-related factors under scrutiny include global disease activity indices, specific organ damage, severe pain, chronic fatigue, dermatological and musculoskeletal manifestations, and prolonged glucocorticoid therapy—all of which may contribute to psychological distress through direct pathophysiological mechanisms or indirect impacts on quality of life [4–6, 9–11]. Notably, the influence of non-glucocorticoid agents, particularly the immunomodulatory mainstay hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), on SLE-associated depression has yet to be fully explored. This highlights the need to better understand the pharmacological modulators of neuropsychiatric symptoms in SLE, with HCQ being a notable candidate for further investigation.

HCQ is universally endorsed as first-line therapy for SLE by major international guidelines [12, 13], which emphasize its broad-spectrum benefits spanning disease control, organ protection, attenuation of damage accrual, and enhanced survival [14, 15]. Mechanistically, HCQ exerts immunomodulatory effects by inhibiting signaling of Toll-like receptors (TLR) and autophagy-driven interferon-α production, key drivers of SLE pathogenesis, while also demonstrating anti-thrombotic and lipid-lowering properties that mitigate vascular complications [15–18]. Given its established role in reducing systemic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and thrombotic risks, HCQ’s potential to ameliorate SLE-related neuropsychiatric symptoms, including depression, merits rigorous exploration. This is particularly pertinent given emerging evidence linking SLE-associated inflammation to neuropsychiatric comorbidities, suggesting HCQ’s pleiotropic mechanisms may extend to improving mental health outcomes in this population.

Current research on the association between HCQ and depression risk remains limited and yields conflicting findings. A multinational network cohort study comparing HCQ with sulfasalazine (SSZ) in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients found no significant increase in depression risk with short-term or long-term HCQ use, suggesting a reassuring safety profile in this population [19]. However, a recent systematic review analyzing HCQ-induced psychiatric adverse effects highlighted potential risks of short-term depressive symptoms, particularly in vulnerable subgroups such as those with pre-existing psychiatric conditions or SLE [20]. Given HCQ’s multifaceted therapeutic benefits in SLE, clarifying its role in depression outcomes is critical to optimizing risk-benefit ratios in clinical practice. This study aims to address this knowledge gap by integrating comprehensive clinical, pharmacological, and laboratory data to elucidate the impact of HCQ on depression in SLE patients, thereby providing deeper insights into the therapeutic effects of HCQ on SLE and evidence-based strategies for optimizing the clinical management of patients with SLE.

Methods

Study design and patient enrollment

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Rheumatology and Immunology, Nanjing First Hospital, from November 1, 2022, to December 30, 2023. A total of 106 adult Chinese patients meeting SLE diagnostic criteria were enrolled. All participants were diagnosed with SLE according to either the 1997 American College of Rheumatology’s revised criteria for SLE classification [21] or the 2019 European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology SLE classification criteria [22]. The inclusion criteria were: (1) Chinese citizens aged ≥ 18 years, (2) voluntary participation in the survey, and (3) ability to independently complete the PHQ-9. The exclusion criteria included: (1) a history of severe psychiatric disorders, (2) previous use of psychiatric medications, (3) pregnancy or lactation, (4) a history of malignancy, and (5) disability caused by conditions other than SLE.

Data collection

Data from each enrolled patient were categorized into four sections. Section Ⅰ included demographic information such as gender, age, marital status, education level, disease duration, and sleep duration. Section Ⅱ covered clinical data such as comorbidities, including other autoimmune diseases (AID), thyroid disorders (e.g., hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), infections, other chronic conditions (e.g., hypertension, coronary artery disease, stroke), existence of fatigue, interstitial lung disease (ILD), and pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). SLE disease activity, assessed with the SLE Disease Activity Index-2000 (SLEDAI-2k) and clinical SLEDAI (cSLEDAI) which is the score of SLEDAI-2k based solely on clinical manifestations, excluding laboratory parameters [23], and medications, including corticosteroids represented as prednisone equivalents, biologic agents such as belimumab and telitacicept, immunosuppressants encompassing cyclophosphamide (CTX), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), cyclosporin A (CsA), tacrolimus (TAC), azathioprine (AZA), and leflunomide (LEF), as well as HCQ, were recorded. Section Ⅲ documented laboratory results, including (1) counts of white blood cell (WBC), platelet, neutrophil, lymphocyte, and hemoglobin level; (2) erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP); (3) serum cytokines including interleukin (IL)-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and interferon (IFN)-γ; (4) antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and anti-extractable nuclear antigen (ENA) autoantibody profiles, including autoantibodies against SSA, SSB, RNP/Sm, histones, ribosomal P protein, and centromere protein B (CENP-B); (5) antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs), including anticardiolipin antibodies (ACA) and anti-β2-glycoprotein 1 antibodies (anti-β2GP1); and (6) immunoglobulin (Ig) G and complement components C3 and C4. The assessment of depressive symptoms was conducted in Section IV with the standardized self-report PHQ-9, which required approximately 1–3 min to complete.

Assessment of depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the PHQ-9, a reliable instrument validated in primary care settings [24]. This tool comprises nine items aligned with the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3). The total score, ranging from 0 to 27, reflects the severity of depressive symptoms, with established cut-offs indicating no (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), and severe (≥ 15) symptom levels. Accordingly, participants were categorized into two groups based on PHQ-9 scores: a score of five or higher indicated the presence of depressive symptoms, while a score below five indicated their absence.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM, USA). Data normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range (IQR)] for normally and non-normally distributed variables, respectively. Categorical data are summarized as numbers and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the independent t-test (normal distribution) or Mann-Whitney U test (non-normal distribution) for continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Associations with depressive symptoms were evaluated using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation analysis based on data distribution. Variables showing a significant association (p < 0.05) in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate linear regression model.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of enrolled patients with SLE

Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical profile of the 106 enrolled SLE patients. The cohort was predominantly female (89.6%, n = 95). The most represented age group was 50–59 years (31.1%), and disease duration was highly variable, with 45.3% of patients having a history of 2–10 years. Most patients (79.2%) reported short sleep duration (0–7 h/night). According to SLEDAI-2k scores, 43.4% of patients having a score greater than 6, indicating moderate to severe disease activity, and 56.6% having a score of 6 or less, indicating mild activity or remission [25, 26]. Laboratory findings revealed a high prevalence of low complement levels (90.6%), followed by thrombocytopenia (30.2%) and leukopenia (15.1%). Assessment with the PHQ-9 indicated that 53.8% of patients had depressive symptoms, which were mild in 31.2% and moderate-to-severe in 22.6%. HCQ was utilized by 65.1% of patients at daily doses of 0.2–0.4 g, and 39.6% received other immunosuppressive agents.

As shown in Table 2, higher depressive symptom scores were significantly associated with female sex (Z = -1.995, p = 0.046), being married (Z = -2.451, p = 0.014), presence of another autoimmune disease (AID) (Z = -3.434, p < 0.001), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) (Z = -2.308, p = 0.021), fatigue (Z = -6.824, p < 0.001), and interstitial lung disease (ILD) (Z = -2.843, p = 0.004). In contrast, HCQ use was associated with significantly lower scores (Z = -2.253, p = 0.024).

Analysis of continuous variables (Table 3) revealed significant positive correlations between PHQ-9 scores and age (r = 0.400, p < 0.001), WBC count (r = 0.195, p = 0.046), and neutrophil count (r = 0.207, p = 0.034). A significant negative correlation was observed with sleep duration (r = -0.333, p < 0.001) and platelet count (r = -0.224, p = 0.022). No significant associations were found with disease duration, education level, hemoglobin, IL-2, IL-4, IL-10, TNF-α, IFN-γ, ESR, CRP, C3, C4, IgG, or prednisone equivalent dose.

Identification of independent factors for depressive symptoms in SLE patients

Variables showing significance in the univariate analysis were included in a multivariable linear regression model to identify independent factors associated with depressive symptoms. Due to significant collinearity between WBC and neutrophil counts (r = 0.953, p < 0.001), the latter was excluded.

As shown in Table 4, longer sleep duration (B = -0.728, β = -0.231, p = 0.004) and HCQ use (B = -2.095, β = -0.192, p = 0.012) were independent factors associated with lower PHQ-9 scores. In contrast, higher fatigue levels were significantly associated with more severe depressive symptoms. (B = 4.589, β = 0.401, p < 0.001). Given the potential overlap between depressive symptoms, fatigue, and sleep disturbance, an additional multivariable linear regression analysis was performed excluding both fatigue and sleep duration. In this adjusted analysis, HCQ use remained significantly and inversely associated with PHQ-9 scores (B = -2.744, β = -0.251, p = 0.004) (Supplementary Table 1). Other variables, including gender, age, marital status, WBC count, platelet count, T2DM, and ILD, were not significantly associated with PHQ-9 scores in the primary analysis. The final model, with an adjusted R² of 0.460, explained 46.0% of the variance in depressive symptom severity.

Correlation between weight‑adjusted HCQ dose and PHQ-9 scores

To examine whether SLE-related depressive symptoms are associated with HCQ exposure relative to body weight, the weight-adjusted HCQ dose (mg/kg) was calculated. Among the 64 patients with complete weight and dosing data, the mean HCQ dose was 5.12 ± 1.19 mg/kg. As PHQ-9 scores were non-normally distributed, Spearman’s rank correlation was used and demonstrated no significant association between the weight-adjusted HCQ dose and PHQ-9 scores (ρ = − 0.080, P = 0.529) (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study identified sleep duration, fatigue, and HCQ use as independent correlates of depressive symptoms in SLE patients. Specifically, shorter sleep and greater fatigue were associated with more severe symptoms, whereas HCQ use was linked to milder symptoms. Clinically, these findings have significant implications for the management of SLE-related depressive symptoms, suggesting that addressing sleep disturbances, fatigue, and optimizing HCQ use may be crucial components of a holistic treatment approach.

Our results align with previous studies reporting the adverse effects of sleep disturbances and fatigue on depression in SLE patients [27–30]. Poor sleep quality, as measured by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), significantly elevates depression levels in SLE patients [27]. Specifically, PSQI dimensions such as subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep disturbances, and daytime dysfunction have been found to correlate with depression in SLE patients [28]. Furthermore, depression serves as a significant predictor of fatigue in SLE patients, reflecting a bidirectional relationship between these two conditions [31]. Psychological factors, such as depression, anxiety, and stress, are well-established in exacerbating fatigue in SLE patients [30]. This creates a vicious cycle where sleep disturbances exacerbate fatigue, which in turn further impairs sleep quality, necessitating comprehensive treatment approaches that address both symptoms. This emphasizes the need for integrated care strategies that consider the complex interplay between these factors.

Our study also revealed a significant inverse association between HCQ use and depressive symptoms in patients with SLE; however, correlation analysis showed that the weight-adjusted HCQ dose was not significantly associated with PHQ‑9 scores, suggesting that the severity of depressive symptoms is independent of the HCQ dose. This finding adds to the existing body of evidence supporting the benefits of HCQ in SLE management. This is particularly significant given the high prevalence of neuropsychiatric comorbidities in SLE and the need for comprehensive treatment strategies that address both physical and mental health outcomes. The mechanisms underlying the observed inverse association between HCQ and depressive symptoms in SLE are likely multifactorial. Primarily, HCQ’s established immunomodulatory properties, such as inhibiting TLR signaling and IFN-α production [18], may be linked to the mitigation of neuroinflammation—a key contributor to depressive pathogenesis that is prominent in SLE [32–34]. By reducing systemic immune activation, HCQ might indirectly attenuate these neuroinflammatory processes, thereby correlating with fewer depressive symptoms. Additionally, HCQ’s anti-thrombotic and lipid-lowering effects could promote cerebrovascular health, potentially supporting better emotional regulation. Finally, through effective control of SLE disease activity and prevention of organ damage, HCQ may improve overall quality of life, which in turn is associated with a lower burden of depressive symptoms. These plausible pathways warrant further investigation to elucidate the link between HCQ and mood outcomes in SLE.

The interpretation of our findings is subject to several limitations. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data constrains causal inference between the identified factors and depressive symptoms. Second, the generalizability of the results may be limited by the modest sample size and the potential for residual confounding from unmeasured variables, including medication adherence and socioeconomic status. Although disease activity (SLEDAI) and glucocorticoid dose are clinically relevant factors in SLE, they did not reach statistical significance in our preliminary univariate analysis. Given the modest sample size (n = 106) and the inclusion of multiple covariates (n = 11) in the final model, we prioritized parsimony to avoid overfitting and maintain statistical stability. Consequently, these variables were not forced into the final multivariate model. This approach, while methodologically reasoned, may have omitted potential subtle associations between disease activity, steroid exposure, and depressive symptoms. Furthermore, the use of self-reported data for sleep and fatigue introduces the possibility of measurement bias. Lastly, the use of weight‑adjusted HCQ dose as a surrogate may not accurately reflect individual serum HCQ concentrations, which are influenced by variable pharmacokinetics; thus, the observed lack of association with depressive symptoms could be partly attributed to this limitation in exposure assessment. Future research employing longitudinal designs, larger and more diverse samples, objective measures of sleep, fatigue and serum HCQ concentrations, and thorough adjustment for contextual confounders would help clarify these relationships.

Conclusions

This study identifies shorter sleep duration and greater fatigue as notable correlates of depressive symptoms in SLE, while also observing an independent inverse association between HCQ use and symptom severity. These findings suggest that HCQ may offer broader benefits in this population; however, given the cross-sectional design and sample size, the nature of this relationship warrants further clarification. Prospective, controlled studies are needed to determine whether HCQ exerts a favorable effect on depressive symptoms related to SLE.
