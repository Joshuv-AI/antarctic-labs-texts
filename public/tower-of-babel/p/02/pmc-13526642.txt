Mechanical detection of sub-band mobilities of two-dimensional electron gas on reduced SrTiO3(001) surface
Authors: Akash Gupta, Marcin Kisiel, Rémy Pawlak, Ernst Meyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) in reduced strontium titanate offers a versatile platform for oxide electronics, yet its dissipation mechanisms under field driven charge fluctuations remain poorly understood. Here, we combine low-temperature atomic force microscopy with scanning tunneling spectroscopy to probe the force and dissipation responses of a mechanical oscillator interacting with the STO 2DEG. The observation of Rydberg-like image potential states by tunneling experiments confirm the 2DEG formation, while dissipation spectroscopy reveals bias-dependent peaks linked to local electrostatic gating and charge redistribution within the 2DEG energy sub-bands. Our analysis identifies electron exchange between oxygen vacancies and the 2DEG as a dominant mechanism responsible for AFM dissipation peaks. These features are quantitatively explained by variations in quantum capacitance as carrier density is tuned by electric fields. Under magnetic fields, dissipation peaks obey the Kohler’s rule, allowing extraction of carrier mobilities in each sub-band. Our results establish a non-invasive AFM-based methodology for quantifying energy losses in quantum oxides, providing new insights into charge dynamics relevant for spintronic applications.

Introduction

Strontium titanate SrTiO3(001) (STO), a semiconductor with a wide electronic band gap (~3.2 eV), is a paradigm example of quantum paraelectric perovskite oxide1, exhibiting a high dielectric constant at low temperatures without undergoing a ferroelectric transition. Absent in the pristine form of STO due to quantum fluctuations, a long-range ferroelectric order can be introduced through chemical doping, stress2,3, electric fields4, or oxygen vacancy engineering5. When strongly reduced6–11 or interfaced with LaAlO312–14, the interfacial band bending can lead to the formation of a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) near its surface without affecting its bulk insulating property15. The 2DEG yields a series of gate-tunable quantum phenomena including insulator to metal, insulator to superconductor transitions16,17, magnetism7,18, and Rashba interaction19,20. STO has thus garnered a significant interest for the diverse physical properties emerging at its interface controllable with external stimuli21. This intimate connection between the STO atomic configuration, its electronic properties and exotic electrostatic response makes this material a prime candidate for novel electronic applications in nanostructured materials. However, the atomic scale understanding of how energy may dissipate when fine tuning the 2DEG occupancy is lacking in the literature22,23.

The plethora of oxygen-deficient STO reconstructions depends on subtle stoichiometric variations at the surface introduced by thermal treatment or cleaving procedures24,25. Annealing STO at T = 1050 °C in ultra-high vacuum conditions (UHV) leads to the oxygen-deficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sqrt{5}\times \sqrt{5}$$\end{document}5×5 reconstruction, consisting of a lattice of oxygen vacancies VO26, around which each dangling bond with neighboring Ti atoms hosts two electrons. First-principles calculations showed that the VO complexes act as an effective quantum dot27, which induces localized states in the semiconducting gap of STO28. At high density, the unpaired electrons of the vacancies delocalize and contribute to the formation of the surface 2DEG6,7,9–11,14,29,30, leading to ferroelectricity by the formation of polar nanodomains and ferromagnetism7,14,31. Angle-resolved photo-electron spectroscopy (ARPES) has assigned to the 2DEG two light electron sub-bands (about −180 meV and −90 meV) and a heavy sub-band (≈ − 40 meV) below EF, as a result of a surface confinement by an inherent field on the order of 108 V m−1 (0.1 V nm−1)6,8. In this context, it is reasonable to assume that the tip of an AFM used as a local gate, applying a voltage of a few volts across a tip-sample gap of a few nanometres, may electrostatically vary the carrier density within the STO’s 2DEG and generate energy dissipation at the local scale.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) operated in non-contact mode is a powerful tool to not only visualize in real space oxide surfaces32,33, but also quantify extremely delicate forces between tip and sample27,34 with respect to the local electronic density of states (LDOS) of the sample. Acting as a local gate, the AFM technique combined with scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) has enabled single-electron charging in individual QDs35, the control over the charge-state of adatoms36, vacancies27,37, and single molecules38–40. Through capacitive coupling between the tip’s electric field and the surface, depletion or accumulation of carriers into states located near the Fermi level EF can be locally controlled, yielding substantial energy dissipation Γ of the oscillator when the coupling with the substrate is due to non-conservative interaction forces27,41–43. Combined with tunneling spectroscopy, the position in energy of the gated electronic states can also be determined with respect to EF. Using field-effect resonant tunneling (FERT) spectra, image potential states (IPS) lying above the vacuum level can also be probed via the tip-induced electric field44–47. The spectra reveal a Rydberg series of surface image states bound by the crystal image potential—a hallmark of a surface 2DEG.

In this work, we detect the force and dissipative responses of a mechanical oscillator coupled to the 2DEG of reduced STO under external electric and magnetic fields. Using tunneling spectroscopy, we characterize the local density of states induced by oxygen vacancies at the reduced STO surface as well as the three 2DEG sub-bands near EF. The metallic nature of the STO’s 2DEG and its electrostatic response to the tip field is further confirmed by probing well-defined IPS states in FERT spectra. At larger distances of the order of a few nanometers, force spectroscopy enables to deplete or accumulate carriers within the 2DEG by capacitive coupling, leading to prominent force (dissipation) kinks (peaks). By applying a normal magnetic field, the force/dissipation response as a function of measured force evolves in a quadratic manner (in agreement with Kohler’s rule of magneto-resistance46,48), which allows an estimate of the electron mobilities of the 2DEG. At B = 0.43 T, a discontinuity appears in the force/dissipation spectra, suggesting spin polarization of the 2DEG heavy sub-band18. Dissipation spectroscopy thus offers an efficient and completely non-invasive way for analyzing gate-tunable metal oxide interfaces, of considerable importance for electrically and magnetically tunable spintronic functionalities.

Despite the large amounts of vacancies, the STO surface retains a large band gap Eg = 3.25 eV, as determined by the differential conductance dI/dV measurements of Fig. 1c. The valence band maximum and conduction band minimum (CBM) are respectively located at −3 eV and 0.5 eV similar to refs. 27,49. In the inset of Fig. 1c, the dI/dV spectra show three resonances denoted as OA, OB, and TP, which are attributed to occupied electronic states from oxygen vacancies as described in ref. 26,27. At about −0.7 eV below EF, OA is the shallower in-gap state due to the oxygen vacancy VO state, which occupancy can be controlled by external tuning of chemical potential27. At about −1.3 and −1.1 eV, the “Tanaka” peak TP and OB are also characteristic signatures of the strongly reduced STO surface.

Let us now discuss the 2DEG of the reduced STO surface. To compare with previous ARPES results7,18, we characterize the DOS near EF using dI/dV spectra (Fig. 1e). We observed three reproducible occupied features near Fermi energy, denoted E1 at ~ − 40 meV, E2 ~ − 87 meV and E3 ~ − 112 meV, which we assign to the surface 2DEG sub-bands based on their energies and their consistency with previous reports on reduced STO surface. Their respective positions in energy are consistent with previous ARPES data7,18, supporting the assignment of E1 to the heavy carriers sub-band with effective mass m* = 10 me (me is the free electron mass) and E2 and E3 to the light carrier sub-bands with effective masses m* = 0.7 me. We also stress that the presence of those states has been reproduced on many reconstructed patches of the surface (Fig. S2), however we note that their exact energies depend on the reduction level of the surface and the local density of oxygen vacancies. To unambiguously confirm the 2DEG’s presence, we also performed FERT spectroscopy at the vicinity of the surface (Fig. 1d). The IPS originating from the Rydberg series of surface states confined between the surface and crystal image potential are the hallmark of surface states (i.e., 2DEG) at metallic surfaces44,45. Experimentally, we acquired dZ/dV spectroscopic measurements near the STO surface by a continuous sweep of the tip-sample voltage Vs while keeping the tunneling current constant with the STM feedback loop (see “Methods”). When Vs exceeds the local work function (ϕ), resonant tunneling between tip and sample occurs as soon as the Fermi level of the tip aligns with the IPS states. This leads to the series of peaks in the FERT spectra, as exemplary shown in Fig. 1d, which position can be described from a quasi-classical approximation by the equation: 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{e}}}{V}_{{{\rm{n}}}}=\phi +{\left(\frac{3n\pi \hslash {{\rm{e}}}E}{\sqrt{2{m}_{{{\rm{e}}}}}}\right)}^{2/3}$$\end{document}eVn=ϕ+3nπℏeE2me2/3 where Vn is the sample voltage for the nth IPS, ϕ is the local work function of the sample, me is the free electron mass, and E is the electric field.

In Fig. 1d, the IPS resonances are marked with their index numbers n = 1...9 (see details in Supplementary Information S3). Note also that the IPS oscillations in FERT spectra are homogeneous across the surface, meaning that we observed no or minor alterations near defects and domain boundaries (Fig. S3b, c). IPS are also completely absent on pristine (non-reduced) STO surface (Fig. S3a in Supplementary Information S3). The resonance denoted IS corresponds to an interfacial state, whereas CBM and VL are the conduction band maximum and vacuum level, respectively. A quantitative estimation of the local work function of the sample is obtained from Eq. 1 by plotting the voltage position Vn of the IPS states as a function of n2/3 (orange squares in Fig. 1f). By fitting the linear regression, we extract the ϕ value corresponding to the y-intercept equal to about 4.2 eV, a value in good agreement with previous works50. Altogether, the observation of oxygen-vacancy states and IPS in tunneling spectra confirms the emergence of the 2DEG in our sample.

To better understand the origin of the mechanical dissipation, we model the system schematized in Fig. 2a with three capacitances (Ctip, Csub, and CQ), which represent the geometrical tip-sample capacitance, the substrate (background) capacitance, and the quantum capacitance exerted by the 2DEG, respectively. While Ctip and Csub are well-established to describe the parabolic lineshape of Δf(V) spectroscopic measurements, CQ reflects how the 2DEG localized at the vicinity of the STO surface can accommodate additional charges at a given quantum level51. This capacitance is expressed as CQ = ρe2 = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{{m}^{* }{e}^{2}}{\pi {\hslash }^{2}}$$\end{document}m*e2πℏ2 with ρ, e, and m* are the electronic DOS, the elementary charge, and the effective mass of the quantum state, respectively. For a 2DEG, the density of states as a function of energy ρ(E) can be approximated with a Heaviside step function, whose step positions correspond to the energies of the 2DEG sub-bands E1, E2, and E3 and step heights directly derive from the in-plane effective masses of the corresponding sub-bands. As an approximation, CQ is modeled using a simplified 2DEG density of states with step-like sub-bands, assuming spin degeneracy and effective mass taken from ARPES data6,18. Our modeling neglects the disorder broadening, mass anisotropy, and the orbital/spin structure of the STO surface 2DEG. Since E1 is the heaviest band as compared to E2 and E3, the CQ(E) ∝ ρ(E) function, shown in Fig. 2b has a large step associated to E1 and two smaller and equal steps for E2 and E3, respectively.

To account for the interaction forces acting on the cantilever, the electrostatic force between tip and sample plotted in Fig. 2c is obtained by considering CQ(E) in the equation introduced by Stomp et al.35. Discussion

Scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS) measurements on oxygen-deficient STO reveal the presence of a metallic 2DEG, evidenced by in-gap surface states (E1, E2, and E3) and field emission resonances—so-called IPS—which are characteristic signatures of metallic behavior on the otherwise insulating oxide. On such a surface, the electric field (0.1 V nm−1) introduced by a non-contact AFM tip is capable to locally modifying the confinement of the 2DEG, thus providing access to dynamic charge processes and enabling the detection of discrete charging events of the 2DEG. The presence of oxygen vacancies is essential. Their role in producing large dissipation peaks during AFM measurements has already been demonstrated in previous studies27,28, where several charging scenarios have been proposed, including electron exchange between a single vacancy and the conduction band, between vacancies themselves, and between vacancies and the surface 2DEG (see Fig. 5 in ref. 27). Our measurement supported the latter scenario. The strong agreement between the experimentally observed charging peaks and the simulated dissipation spectra (Fig. 2), which incorporate the STS energy levels of the surface 2DEG, suggests that the dominant mechanism involves electron exchange between oxygen vacancies and the 2DEG. In this scenario, the AFM dissipation response arises primarily not from the change of the vacancy’s charge state, but from variations in the quantum capacitance of the de-localized 2DEG. The charge originates from vacancy states (quantum dot-like electron emitters) and the observed dissipation response is governed by the electronic compressibility or quantum capacitance CQ of the 2DEG, with the vacancies acting merely as electron sources or sinks. Using the average <CQ > = 140 aF (Fig. 2b) and tip projected “active” surface area \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S=\pi {\frac{d}{2}}^{2}$$\end{document}S=πd22, where d = 5 nm is the tip-sample distance46, we can estimate a sheet capacitance C2D = 700 μF cm−2, which for 2DEG energy width ΔE = 0.1 eV (Fig. 1e) yields a carrier density N ≈ 4 × 1014 cm−2. That places the STO 2DEG firmly in the Fermi liquid regime57, yet many-body renormalization remains strong18. In particular, King et al.18 reported that pronounced orbital ordering, spin-orbit coupling, and many-body interactions are crucial for STO 2DEG electronic structure. The electrostatic potential that defines the 2DEG lifts the t2g orbital degeneracy present in the bulk STO through orbital mixing, resulting in the coexistence of light, circular dxy sub-bands and heavy, elliptical dxz/yz band. While a conventional Rashba splitting at the bottom of the dxy band is relatively small, the orbitally enhanced Rashba interaction can increase significantly - by an order of magnitude—near avoided crossings with the heavy txz/yz band. Moreover, heavy dxz/yz sub-band couples very strongly to phonons8 over a wide frequency range, resulting in substantial bandwidth renormalization, as compared to the more conventional electron-phonon (el-ph) coupling leading to kinks in the light dxy sub-band dispersion. Thus, the stronger el-ph interaction increases the effective mass and scattering rate of the heavy sub-band, possibly resulting in enhanced energy dissipation of Γ1 on the order of 10–20 meV per cycle, compared with a few meV per cycle dissipation of the light Γ2,3 (see Figs. 2 and 3).

While the dissipation peaks consistently occur at the same tip-sample interaction force or electric field independently on the tip-sample voltage (Fig. 3f), the dissipation behavior under the external B-field differs significantly. Specifically, Δf associated with each of the Γ1, Γ2, and Γ3 charging peak exhibits a parabolic dependence on B (Fig. 4a). In a single relaxation time approximation and a weak-magnetic field limit, the magnetoresistivity follows Kohler’s rule, R(B)/R(B = 0) ≈ 1 + (μB)2 —where μ is the carrier mobility-consistent with previous results for 2DEG on liquid helium58. Under these assumptions, the observed Δfn(B) for the dissipation peak marked by n is expected to follow a parabolic dependence (see Supplementary Material 8 for details): 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {f}_{n}(B)\approx \Delta {f}_{n}(B=0)\cdot \left(1-\frac{3}{2}{{{\rm{\mu }}}}_{n}^{2}{B}^{2}\right)$$\end{document}Δfn(B)≈Δfn(B=0)⋅1−32μn2B2 where Δfn(B = 0) is the frequency shift at zero magnetic field. Fit of the experimental data with the formula Eq. 2 allows for determining the carrier mobilities corresponding to the individual Γ1, Γ2, and Γ3 sub-bands, related to E1, E2, and E3 energy in gap states. The heavy-electron Γ1 band exhibits lower mobility values as compared to the lighter Γ2, Γ3. The extracted values are summarized in Table 1 and range from 2500 to 4800 cm2 V−1 s−1, consistent with previously reported high-mobility values of an electron gas confined at the LaAlO3/SrTiO3 interface at low temperatures13. The relatively small difference in sub-band extracted mobilities as compared to the much larger difference in their effective mass indicates that the effective relaxation times are also sub-band dependent.

We will make one remark before closing. A tiny discontinuity is observed around B = ± 0.43 T for the heavy Γ1 sub-band (Fig. 4a), accompanied to slight increase of mobility (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{\mu }}}=\frac{e\tau }{{m}^{* }}$$\end{document}μ=eτm*) for ∣B∣ > 0.43T. Within the single relaxation time approximation, this observation implies a reduced scattering rate 1/τ. It is possibly due to the heavy character of the dxz/yz band, which exhibits larger spatial delocalization as compared to its light dxy counterparts and consequently, its wave functions enclose more Vo vacancies18. We note that the heavier effective mass refers to the in-plane band structure dispersion, whereas the larger spatial extension is related to confinement of the wave function in the real space, particularly along the surface - normal direction. Thus, the coupling between the carriers and localized Vo moments might be significant, particularly due to relatively large orbital angular momentum (0.7 ℏ) near the heavy-band crossings, which enhances the interaction with Vo magnetic moments. Thus, at low B-fields, the random magnetic moments of individual surface vacancies Vo (or oxygen-vacancy clusters) remain unpolarized and might act as fluctuating transverse magnetic fields contributing to the spin scattering events. An external B-field polarizes Vo moments, and once aligned, they present almost no transverse component, which leads to a reduction of the spin-dependent scattering of 2DEG and a slight increase of μ above B = 0.43 T.

AFM mechanical dissipation combined with STS measurements enables the characterization of electron dynamics and determination of band-selective mobility within the surface 2DEG of oxygen-deficient STO. The observed mobility change in the B-field arises from suppression of scattering in the surface 2DEG, possibly due to vacancy moment alignment, highlighting the 2DEG and defect interplay. The dissipation spectroscopy offers an efficient and completely non-invasive tool for analysis of correlated oxide interfaces, of considerable importance for electrically and magnetically tunable spintronic functionalities.

Methods

Sample preparation

The experiments were performed in two independent ultra-high vacuum (UHV) chambers, both operating at a low temperature of T = 4.5 K. A commercially available SrTiO3 crystal from MTI corporation was sputtered with Ar+ ions and annealed by direct current heating to T = 950 ℃ as determined by an infrared pyrometer. This process leads to the removal of surface contaminations and hydrocarbons from the surface. The oxygen-deficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sqrt{5}\times \sqrt{5}$$\end{document}5×5)R26. 6∘ reconstruction of SrTiO3(001) is obtained by annealing at T ≈ 1050 ℃ by passing current through the sample under UHV conditions for 20 min.

Low temperature STM/AFM experiments

The STM/AFM experiments were performed using a low-temperature microscope operated at T = 4.8 K in ultrahigh vacuum (p ≈ 1 × 10−10 mbar). The force sensor is a quartz tuning fork based on a qPlus design59 operated in the frequency-modulation mode (resonance frequency f0 ≈ 25 kHz, spring constant k ≈ 1800 N m−1, quality factor Q ≈ 14,000, and oscillation amplitude A ≤ 1 Å). The bias voltage was applied to the tip. STM images are taken in constant-current mode. The tip mounted to the qPlus sensor consists of a 25 μm-thick tungsten wire, shortened and sharpened with a focused ion beam. pAFM spectroscopy

The B-field dependent experiments have been performed in a separate low-temperature pendulum atomic force microscopy (pAFM) system. Due to its low spring constant k = 0.1 Nm−1 and high Q = 7 × 105, the force sensor in the pAFM geometry offers enhanced force and dissipation sensitivity. We used a highly n-doped silicon cantilever (ATEC-Cont from nanosensors with resistivity ρ = 0.01–0.02 Ω cm). The pAFM sensor is suspended perpendicularly to the surface, meaning that the tip vibrational motion is parallel to the sample surface. The oscillation amplitude A was kept constant by means of a phase-locked loop feedback system at 5 nm. Cantilevers were annealed in UHV up to 700 ℃ for 12 h to remove surface contaminants from both the cantilever and tip. It is also known that this long-term annealing leads to negligible amounts of localized charges on the probing tip. To compensate for the CPD between the tip and the sample, the voltage was applied to the sample. The magnetic field was applied out of the surface plane, along the pAFM cantilever axis.
