Physiological and molecular investigation of nanosilica mediated drought tolerance in cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.)
Authors: Mohammad Esmaeil Ameri Bafqi, Amir Mohammad Naji, Heshmat Omidi, Amir Bostani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.) is widely recognized as an anticancer and antidiabetic agent. While the effects of drought stress on plants are well documented, there is a lack of comprehensive research on the use of nanosilica as a new strategy to mitigate them. In this study, two Iranian landraces (Isfahan and Semnan) were cultivated and treated with three concentrations of foliar nanosilica (0, 4, and 6 mM nSi) under three levels of drought stress (60, 40, and 20% of field capacity, FC). The treatment significantly improved plant physiological and biochemical responses, with the most pronounced benefits observed at 4 mM nSi under mild drought (60% FC). This optimal treatment increased total chlorophyll content up to 18.57 mg g− 1 FW and essential oil yield (EOY), reaching 8.22 kg ha− 1 with 2.81-fold compared to the mild drought stress without foliar nSi application (1.56 kg ha− 1). Expression of limonene synthase (LS) increased 3.6-fold, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase 1 (PAL1) increased 3.3-fold, and aldehyde dehydrogenase family 3 member H1 (ALDH3H1) peaked under moderate drought combined with 4 mM foliar nSi application. Concomitantly, nSi elevated osmoprotectants, with proline content rising to 5.20 µmol mg− 1 FW under severe drought. Our findings establish that nSi, particularly at a 4 mM concentration under mild water deficit, activates an integrated tolerance program in cumin, stabilizing photosynthesis and improving the allocation of resources toward enhancing essential oil yield.

Introduction

Cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.) is cultivated in many Middle Eastern countries like Iran, India, China, Tunisia and is widely recognized in traditional medicine for its anticancer and antidiabetic properties1. Drought is a major environmental stress that significantly reduces agricultural crop yields and affects key physiological processes, such as photosynthesis, translocation, cell expansion, division, the storage and transport of nutrients2,3. Plants use enzymatic and nonenzymatic antioxidant systems to neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced under stress4. Plants employ three distinct strategies to survive under drought stress conditions, avoidance, escape and tolerance5.

One approach for improving plant drought tolerance involves the use of compounds and elements that can influence plant physiological responses, thereby mitigating the effects of drought6. Silicon dioxide nanoparticles (Nanosilica) have been shown to significantly increase drought tolerance and improve physiological traits in plants7. Nanosilica-treated plants maintain better water status, with higher relative water content and improved leaf water content, photosynthetic parameters, better gas exchange characteristics and increased transpiration efficiency7,8. Nanosilica application leads to a more balanced production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and enhanced activity of antioxidant enzymes such as peroxidase, catalase, and superoxide dismutase7,9. Studies have shown that nanoparticles can influence plant gene expression and metabolite synthesis. Wang et al.10, reported that SiO2 upregulated the expression of the JA biosynthesis genes OsPLD, OsLOX, and OsOPR and the signaling pathway gene OsJAZ, as well as SA-dependent defense pathway-related genes (OsICS, OsPAL1, and OsWRKY45). Similarly, PAL1 activation, which enhances phenolic compound and lignin production, has been observed in wheat, where nSi improved cell wall integrity and oxidative stress defense11. silver nanoparticles can upregulate genes involved in the biosynthesis of phenolic compounds and flavonoids, increasing their antioxidant capacity (Chavez et al., 2024). Investigating the role of nanosilica (nSi) could reveal new strategies for enhancing drought tolerance and metabolite production12,13.

Profiling key stress-responsive genes not only reveals critical biomarkers for tolerance but also identifies potential metabolic bottlenecks and regulatory hubs for future biotechnological manipulation. This approach is fundamental for advancing our understanding of plant stress physiology and developing strategies for crop improvement under challenging climatic conditions. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, limonene synthase, and aldehyde dehydrogenase are examples of stress-responsive genes that are associated with essential oil production in the medicinal plant Cumin. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) is a pivotal enzyme in the phenylpropanoid pathway, catalyzing the deamination of phenylalanine to trans-cinnamic acid. This committed step initiates the biosynthesis of a vast array of phenolic compounds. These metabolites are integral to plant defense, fulfilling roles in antioxidant activity, ultraviolet photoprotection, and the reinforcement of structural integrity under stress conditions. Under drought stress, PAL gene expression is typically upregulated, facilitating the accumulation of flavonoids and other antioxidants that scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitigate oxidative damage to cellular membranes14,15. Limonene synthase (LS) catalyzes the cyclization of geranyl pyrophosphate to limonene, a monoterpene and a principal constituent of cumin essential oil. Limonene contributes to the characteristic aroma of cumin and exhibits documented antimicrobial and antioxidant properties. The expression of the limonene synthase gene is responsive to environmental cues, including drought stress16. The aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) enzyme superfamily functions in aldehyde detoxification and metabolism. Under drought stress, enhanced ROS generation leads to the formation of reactive aldehydes, which can induce cytotoxicity. ALDH enzymes oxidize these deleterious aldehydes to their corresponding, less toxic carboxylic acids, thereby playing a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and underpinning stress tolerance17.

To date, no studies have explored the application of nSi foliar spray for modulating gene expression and secondary metabolite profiles in cumin plants under drought conditions. This lack of data represents a significant gap in optimizing cumin production under stress. This research investigated the effects of drought stress in conjunction with foliar nSi application on gene expression and essential oil production in cumin. Therefore, the primary objectives of this study were: (1) to analyze the impact of foliar nSi application on the expression of stress-responsive and secondary metabolite-related genes (PAL, LS, ALDH) in cumin under drought stress; (2) to assess the subsequent changes in essential oil yield; and (3) to evaluate some physiological and biochemical indicators of drought tolerance. We hypothesized that foliar nSi application would upregulate the expression of the targeted biosynthetic genes, leading to an enhanced accumulation of protective secondary metabolites and essential oils, thereby improving the physiological resilience and overall performance of cumin plants subjected to drought stress.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and design

A factorial experiment based on a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications was conducted in Aveh, Iran (Central Province, 34° 79′ N, 50° 42′ E), with detailed site characteristics provided in Table 1. Each replication consisted of four rows, each 2 m long, with 25 cm spacing between rows. This study aimed to investigate the effects of foliar nanosilica and drought stress on the physiological and biochemical responses and to perform a molecular study on cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.). Drought stress was applied at three levels (60, 40, and 20% of field capacity, [FC]), representing mild, moderate, and severe degrees of water availability, respectively, and foliar application of nanosilica (nSi) was applied at three levels (0 [control], 4, and 6 mM). The nanosilica dose range was chosen based on previous studies18–21 and was selected to span from non-exposed control to a high, yet sub-toxic level that allows for the detection of clear, dose-dependent biological effects, consistent with toxicology guidelines for silica nanoparticles. Intermediate doses (4 mM) improve the resolution of the concentration-response relationship within a range. Additionally, two Iranian cumin landraces, Isfahan and Semnan, were also included in the study.

Treatments and crop management

In the fall, land preparation began with deep plowing (up to 30 cm to break up soil compaction and improve drainage), followed by initial leveling. Soil texture and nutrient analyses were then conducted. The land was left undisturbed until spring, when favorable conditions allowed for additional land preparation according to the design plan of drought treatments and nanosilica spray levels (20–30 nm, Iranian Pioneers of Nanosilica Materials Co., Vakilabad, Mashhad, Iran) supplemented with Jonobgan ionic foliar spray soap as a surfactant (Kerman Zamin Co., Kerman, Iran), which was applied at two growth stages: after the six-leaf stage (late March) and before flowering (mid-April). Control plants were sprayed at the same time points with distilled water containing the same foliar soap to ensure that any observed effects were consistent with nanosilica rather than the surfactant. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed that the nanosilica used in this study had diameters of approximately 30 nm, as reported by the supplier. Drought stress was induced after the six-leaf stage, once plants were established. The soil moisture levels were monitored using the pressure plate method, which involves preparing soil samples, saturating them under specific pressures, and drying them to measure the soil moisture percentage. After the moisture contents of the soil samples at field capacity and the permanent wilting point (PWP) were calculated and the apparent specific mass of the soil was determined, the volumetric water content at FC and PWP was calculated for each drought level. For physiological trait measurements, plant samples were collected from the plots one week after the second foliar spray and frozen for subsequent analysis.

Proline content

Free proline content was determined using the method described by Bates et al.23. For this purpose, 10 ml of a 3% sulfosalicylic acid solution was added to fresh seedlings (0.5 g) in a porcelain mortar and extracted. The extract was then filtered through filter paper. Two milliliters of ninhydrin reagent and 2 ml of acetic acid were added to 2 ml of the filtered extract. The mixture was incubated in a water bath at 100 °C for 1 h. After the samples were cooled on ice and the temperature of the tubes was lowered, 4 ml of toluene was added to each sample, and all samples were spun for 30 s. Finally, after separation of toluene from the lower solution (two-phase separation of the samples), the absorbance of the extract above the tube was read at a wavelength of 520 nm via a spectrophotometer, Proline concentration was then determined by comparison with a standard curve.

Protein concentration

The protein concentration was quantified using the Bradford assay24 with a spectrophotometer. This method is based on the binding of Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 dye to proteins, resulting which shifts in the dye’s absorbance maximum from 465 nm to 595 nm. The intensity of the color change is proportional to the protein concentration in the sample. A standard curve was generated using known concentrations of a reference protein, such as bovine serum albumin (BSA), to accurately determine the protein concentration in the experimental samples. The absorbance of the samples was measured at 595 nm via a spectrophotometer, and the protein concentration was calculated on the basis of the standard curve.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)

The hydrogen peroxide content was calculated via the proposed method of Alexieva et al.25. Frozen leaf tissue (0.25 g) was homogenized in 5 mL of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid using a mortar and pestle. In the following step, the obtained solution was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for five minutes, and the supernatant was separated. Then, 250 µl of the obtained solution, along with 250 µl of 100 mM phosphate buffer and 500 µl of sodium iodide (KI), were combined and mixed. Finally, the absorption rate of the sample at a wavelength of 390 nm was recorded via a spectrophotometer, and from the standard hydrogen peroxide curve, the amount of this compound was calculated.

Malondialdehyde (MDA)

A thiobarbituric acid (TBA) assay was used to determine the malondialdehyde (MDA) content as described b Senthilkumar et al.26. Briefly, ~ 0.1 g of leaf tissue was homogenized in 1 ml ice-cold 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (0.5 ml) was mixed with 0.5 ml of 0.5% TBA in 20% TCA and incubated at 95 °C for 30 min, then cooled on ice. Absorbance was read at 532 nm and corrected for non-specific absorbance at 600 nm. Malondialdehyde content was calculated using the extinction coefficient and expressed as nmol malondialdehyde per mg protein.

Essential oil yield (EOY)

The seeds were ground into a powder using a mechanical grinder. The essential oil from each sample was subsequently extracted via hydrodistillation using a Clevenger apparatus for 2 h. The extract was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, and the essential oil yield was calculated based on the dry weight of the seeds.

Gene expression and RNA isolation

Sampling was performed 24 h after drought stress induction, and the collected samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Sampling at 24 h after drought was selected because many drought responsive secondary metabolite biosynthesis genes, including PAL and ALDH family members, show strong induction in the early-mid phase of stress (typically 12–24 h), when defense and phenylpropanoid pathways are maximally activated but before severe damage occurs27–30. RNA extraction was performed using the TRIzol reagent method. The quantity and quality of the extracted RNA were performed via NanoDrop and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis in 1× TAE buffer. Due to the high sensitivity of RNA to RNase contamination and its rapid degradation, all electrophoresis equipment was treated with 1% SDS before use, washed with water and absolute ethanol, dried, and finally rinsed with DEPC-treated water. Electrophoresis was carried out at a constant voltage of 90 V for 1 h.

cDNA synthesis

Initially, 1 µg of RNA, along with 1 µL of oligo(dT)18 primer and 1 µL of dNTP mixture, was added to a 0.2 mL microcentrifuge tube, and the volume was adjusted to 10 µL with DEPC-treated water. The mixture was incubated at 70 °C for 5 min to allow primer annealing. A master mix, prepared according to Table 2, was subsequently added to the microtube, followed by incubation at 40 °C for 1 h to synthesize the first strand cDNA. Finally, to inactivate the reverse transcriptase (RT), the tube was incubated at 85 °C for 5 min.

Primer design

For this purpose, SRA sequences of Cuminum cyminum L. available in the NCBI database were retrieved. These sequences were assembled from the corresponding reads using the software MEGAHIT Galaxy (Version 1.2.9 + galaxy1). Carrot (Daucus carota), a closely related species within the Apiaceae family, was selected as a reference. Consequently, the sequences of the target genes identified in carrots were aligned with the final assembled sequences obtained from cumin. Finally, primers for the target genes were designed using Geneious Prime 2021.1.1 (Table 3). In this study, the actin gene was selected as the endogenous control based on established literature and its widespread use as a stable internal control across abiotic stresses (heat, cold, salt, and drought) and hormone stimuli. It was ranked highest overall by geNorm and NormFinder for normalization31,32.

qRT‒PCR

A Bioer Real-Time PCR System and SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Ampliqon, Denmark) were used for qRT-PCR. Each reaction was run in three technical replicates. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 62–65 °C (depending on the gene) for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s. Following amplification, melting curve analysis was performed from 58 °C to 95 °C (increment of 2 °C every 5 s) to confirm the specificity of the amplification product (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., 2021), with the assumption of fixed treatment effects. The means of the main factors were compared using Fisher’s protected LSD test at P < 0.05, and their interactions were compared with the LSMeans procedure. Graphs were generated using Microsoft Excel 2019 and JMP Pro. Results

The results in Table 4 confirm that nanosilica (nSi) had a significant effect on the response variable, with a high Logworth value and a P-value less than 0.05. The landrace played a strong but not dominant role in controlling the response variable, likely due to genetic differences in drought tolerance and resource allocation. Root development and water infiltration are influenced by soil bulk density, which is an important controlling variable. The drought × nanosilica interaction was significant, with a P-value near 0.05, indicating that nSi efficacy can vary under drought conditions, potentially through improved drought tolerance. By contrast, the three-way interaction (nanosilica × drought × landrace) was not significant, indicating that these factors together do not produce a synergistic effect beyond the individual or two-way interactions.

The expression of the limonene synthase (LS) gene increased, reflecting enhanced terpenoid biosynthesis and suggesting a protective role against abiotic stress. Gene expression analysis

LS gene expression was highest in the Semnan landrace under the 4 mM nSi treatment combined with drought stress at 40% FC (Fig. 4B). The fold change was 3.6 under the 4 mM treatment and 3.31 under the 6 mM treatment; despite the decrease, no statistically significant difference was detected between the two concentrations. A similar trend was observed for the Isfahan landrace (Fig. 4A). Specifically, the 4 mM nSi treatment under 40% FC drought stress showed the highest expression level (4.843). This value did not differ significantly from that of the 6 mM nSi treatment under 40% FC drought stress (4.612) nor from the 4 mM and 6 mM nSi treatments under 60% FC drought stress, which yielded values of 4.564 and 4.025, respectively.

PAL1 gene expression was highest under the 6 mM and 4 mM nSi treatments combined with mild drought stress (60% FC), with values of 7.761 and 7.378, respectively (Fig. 4C,D). These levels represented 3.33- and 3.1-fold increases in expression compared with the control treatment (no nSi application).

ALDH3H1 gene expression was highest under the 4 mM nSi treatment combined with moderate drought stress (40% FC). A similar trend was observed in both landraces, with Isfahan showing the highest expression under the same treatment. Increasing the nSi concentration from 4 mM to 6 mM resulted in a reduction in gene expression.

Prolin content

The proline content was significantly (P < 0.01) influenced by the drought stress level, foliar nSi application, and their interaction (Fig. 5A). The highest proline content was observed under severe drought stress (20% FC) combined with foliar nSi application (6 mM) in the Isfahan, where it reached 5.20 µmol mg−1 FW. In contrast, the lowest proline content (1.84 µmol mg−1 FW) was recorded under mild drought stress (60% FC) without foliar nSi application (control) in Isfahan. However, as drought stress intensified, proline accumulation increases significantly, particularly in plants subjected to relatively high levels of foliar nSi application.

Protein

The foliar nSi applications and their interactions are presented in Fig. 5B. The highest protein content was detected in Isfahan under mild drought stress (60% FC) with 4 mM foliar nSi application, reaching 12.47%. In contrast, the lowest protein content (4.14%) was recorded in the Semnan under severe drought stress (40% FC) without foliar nSi application (control). However, under mild drought stress (60% FC), the protein content decreased as foliar nSi application decreased in both landraces.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)

Figure 5C shows the response of H2O2 to nSi and drought stress. The highest H2O2 content (301.97 µmol.g−1 FW) was observed in Isfahan under severe drought stress (20% FC), without foliar nSi application (control). In contrast, the lowest H2O2 content (102.94 µmol.g−1 FW) was recorded in Semnan under mild drought stress (60% FC) with 6 mM foliar nSi application. Under mild drought stress (60% FC), the H2O2 content decreased with decreasing foliar nSi application in both landraces. Interestingly, under severe drought stress (20% FC), the H2O2 content increased in Semnan when the foliar nSi application was reduced, reaching 224.82 µmol.g−1 FW with 4 mM foliar nSi application.

Malondialdehyde (MDA)

The degree of MDA accumulation varied markedly across drought stress levels, nSi applications, and plant varieties (Fig. 5D). Under moderate drought (40% FC), the highest MDA levels observed in Isfahan (6.45 nmol ml−1) and Semnan (7.31 nmol ml−1) without foliar nSi application (control). These findings suggest intensified lipid peroxidation under moderate stress. Under moderate drought (40% FC), the MDA content decreased slightly in Isfahan (3.75 nmol ml−1 at nSi 4 mM) and Semnan (4.97 nmol ml−1 at nSi 4 mM), indicating various differences in the oxidative stress response. The maximum MDA content occurred in Semnan under moderate drought without foliar nSi application (7.31 nmol ml−1), which was statistically comparable to that with nSi 4 and 6 mM (4.97 and 3.94 nmol ml−1, respectively).

Total chlorophyll

Under moderate drought (40% FC), the highest total chlorophyll levels were observed in Isfahan (13.43 mg g−1 FW at nSi 4 mM) and Semnan (13.37 mg g−1 FW at nSi 4 mM), as shown in Fig. 5E. These findings suggest that moderate drought combined with high foliar nSi application (nSi 4 mM) enhances total chlorophyll retention. Under severe drought (20% FC), total chlorophyll levels remained relatively stable in Isfahan (13.66 mg g−1 FW in the nSi foliar control) but decreased slightly in Semnan (10.8 mg g−1 FW in the nSi foliar control), indicating varietal differences in chlorophyll preservation under stress.

Essential oil yield

The essential oil yield (EOY) was significantly influenced by the drought stress level, foliar nSi application, and their interaction (Fig. 5F). The highest EOY (8.22 kg ha−1) was observed in Semnan under mild drought stress (60% FC), with the highest foliar nSi application (6 mM). In contrast, the lowest EOY was observed in Isfahan under severe drought stress (20% FC) without nSi foliar treatment (control), with a value of 1.68 kg ha−1. Under mild drought (60% FC), both varieties presented significantly greater EOY when combined with foliar nSi treatments (4 or 6 mM). In Isfahan, the EOY increased from 2.20 kg ha−1 (control) to 8.15 kg ha−1 (6 mM). Severe drought (20% FC) drastically reduced EOY across both landraces, but foliar nSi application (4 or 6 mM) partially mitigated this effect.

Correlation analysis

Correlations were assessed using Pearson’s method (Fig. 6). A strong positive correlation was observed between PAL1 and LS expression (p < 0.0001), also, between LS and EOY. ALDH3H1 and H2O2 (p < 0.0001) were also significantly positively correlated. This correlation also occurred between ALDH3H1 and proline content. Both proline accumulation and ALDH3H1 activity are stress-responsive mechanisms. A weak but notable correlation observed between ALDH3H1 and EOY. Also, there were a positive correlation between total chlorophyll content and EOY.

Discussion

Overall treatment effects and interactions

The ANOVA results demonstrate that nanosilica exerts a primary and statistically significant influence on the measured trait in Cumin, consistent with the widely reported central role of nanosilica (nSi) in improving plant performance under drought via both physiological modulation and improved soil-plant water relations34–39. The moderate, non-dominant effect of landrace suggests that genotypic differences contribute to variation in the response variable, likely through inherent differences in root architecture and the ability to exploit changes in soil physical properties such as water-holding capacity and penetration resistance, traits known to interact strongly with Si-based amendments under water deficit36–39. The near-significant drought × nSi interaction indicates that the magnitude of nSi benefits depends on soil moisture status, supporting evidence that nSi is most effective under stress by enhancing root growth, water uptake, membrane stability, and antioxidant defenses, thereby improving drought tolerance rather than merely boosting growth under optimal conditions34,40–42. Similar interaction patterns between drought and nSi have been observed in coriander, faba bean, maize, and hemp, where nSi treatments mitigated yield and biomass losses and enhanced water use efficiency under reduced field capacity36,43,44. In contrast, the non-significant three-way interaction (nSi × drought × landrace) implies that, although landraces differ and nSi efficiency changes with drought level, there is no strong evidence that any particular cumin landrace exhibits a uniquely synergistic response to nSi under specific drought intensities. This agrees with broader reports that Si and nSi-mediated drought tolerance mechanisms (improved soil water availability, root hydraulic conductance, and ROS control) are largely general across genotypes and species rather than strongly genotype specific34,37–39. Overall, these findings position nSi as the main controllable factor, with drought level modulating its effectiveness and landrace exerting a secondary, additive rather than synergistic, influence on the response.

Physiological and agronomic responses

Increasing nSi application (e.g., from no nSi foliar treatment to nSi 4 mM) under mild drought stress (60% FC) significantly increased chlorophyll in both landraces (e.g., Isfahan: 9.85 mg g−1 FW to 18.57 mg g−1 FW; Semnan: 12.1 mg g−1 FW to 17.55 mg g−1 FW). nSi improves photosynthetic efficiency45 by stabilizing the chlorophyll content and enhancing stomatal regulation, as observed in Oryza sativa by Zhang et al.46.

Under varying treatments, essential oil yield (EOY) in both landraces clearly followed a stress mitigation pattern, mild drought (60% FC) combined with higher nSi doses (4–6 mM) maximized EOY, whereas severe drought (20% FC) without nSi caused the sharpest decline. The eightfold difference between the best (Semnan, 60% FC with 6 mM nSi, 8.22 kg ha−1) and worst (Isfahan, 20% FC, control, 1.56 kg ha−1) treatments highlights both genotypic variation and the strong alleviating effect of foliar nSi on drought induced reductions in oil productivity. A similar interactive trend has been reported in coriander, where moderate drought combined with foliar Si nanoparticles significantly enhanced EO percentage and EO yield relative to well-watered or severely stressed plants without Si-NPs47. Likewise, in hemp and feverfew, mild-to-moderate water limitation coupled with nSi led to higher essential oil content or yield than either severe drought or non-stressed controls without nSi, indicating that nSi can partially offset drought damage and even exploit moderate stress as a stimulus for secondary metabolite synthesis48–50. Therefore, the results are consistent with a broader body of evidence showing that optimal nSi doses under mild drought create a favorable balance between growth maintenance and stress induced stimulation of essential oil biosynthesis, while under severe drought nSi mainly plays a protective rather than yield enhancing role.

Biochemical stress markers

The interaction between drought stress and foliar nSi application revealed that proline accumulation was more pronounced in Isfahan than in Semnan under severe drought stress (20% FC) with 6 mM foliar nSi application. The increase in proline content under drought conditions aligns with the findings of numerous studies that reported similar findings51. Additionally, research has indicated that proline accumulation is one of the mechanisms used by plants to prevent the adverse consequences of water stress52.

The results revealed that the highest protein content in Isfahan was achieved under mild drought stress (60% FC) with 4 mM foliar nSi application. Additionally, in Semnan, the highest protein content was observed under the same conditions, with a value of 10.18%. These results suggest that the response of protein content to foliar nSi application is highly dependent on the severity of drought stress and the variety of the plant. Drought stress often leads to reduced protein synthesis due to limitations in water availability, which affects cellular functions and can lead to the degradation of existing proteins. The application of nSi may help mitigate oxidative stress by increasing antioxidant enzyme activity45. This reduction in oxidative damage allows for better maintenance of cellular integrity and function, promoting protein synthesis. a review by Mustafa et al.53 highlighted that nanoparticles can increase osmolyte accumulation and improve antioxidant activities, which are crucial for maintaining protein levels during drought stress. Similarly, studies on other crops have shown that metal-based nanoparticles, such as ZnO and TiO2, can significantly improve protein content by increasing growth parameters and metabolic processes54.

The highest H2O2 content in Isfahan was achieved under severe drought stress (20% FC), with the highest foliar nSi application (6 mM), whereas in Semnan, the highest H2O2 content was observed under the same drought stress but with 4 mM foliar nSi application. The observed increase in H2O2 under severe drought conditions with nSi application may indicate an adaptive response. While high levels of H2O2 can be damaging, they can also activate antioxidant defense systems, leading to increased expression of genes involved in stress tolerance55. The variation in H2O2 content between the Isfahan and Semnan landraces suggests genetic differences in their responses to drought and nSi treatments.

Under moderate drought conditions, the combination of water deficit and external stressors such as nSi can lead to an overproduction of ROS, resulting in lipid peroxidation and subsequent MDA accumulation. This finding is consistent with findings from other studies that reported increased MDA levels under drought stress as a result of increased oxidative damage56.

Gene expression pattern

The expression patterns of LS, PAL1, and ALDH3H1 in Cuminum cyminum L. under drought and nanosilica (nSi) application indicate a coordinated, nSi mediated activation of defense and detoxification pathways. The highest LS transcript levels at 4 mM nSi under 40% FC in both Semnan and Isfahan landraces, with no significant difference from 6 mM, suggest that moderate drought combined with an optimal nSi dose maximizes gene activation but that the response is not strictly dose dependent, consistent with Si induced modulation of stress responsive gene expression reported in other crops57–59. The strong induction of PAL1 at 4–6 mM nSi under mild drought (60% FC) aligns with the central role of PAL in the phenylpropanoid pathway and secondary metabolite-based defense in cumin, where PAL up regulation under stress is closely associated with increased phenolic accumulation60. The 3.1 to 3.36 fold PAL1 increases relative to the control support the idea that nSi enhances phenylpropanoid mediated protection, similar to Si driven boosts in phenolics and flavonoids observed in wheat and lentil under drought58,59. Likewise, maximum ALDH3H1 expression at 4 mM nSi under 40% FC, followed by a decline at 6 mM, indicates an optimal concentration for stimulating aldehyde detoxification and maintaining cellular redox balance, in line with broader evidence that Si improves antioxidant capacity and ROS detoxification under water deficit. The similar response patterns in both cumin landraces suggest that this nSi mediated transcriptional reprogramming is robust across genotypes, supporting the broader concept that nSi enhance drought tolerance via regulation of secondary metabolism, osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant systems in arid zone crops57–59. These results are consistent with those of Ranjbar et al.61 who observed that drought stress significantly influences the expression of LS in cumin. The increase in ALDH3H1 activity observed under both nSi and drought conditions highlights its role in detoxifying aldehydes produced during stress, further supporting plant resilience. This finding aligns with findings from other studies that have demonstrated the importance of ALDH in stress tolerance mechanisms across various plant species62.

A proposed model of nanosilica mediated drought tolerance

Taken together, the results indicate that nSi mediates drought tolerance in cumin through an integrated mechanism that combines improved water relations with activation of biochemical and transcriptional defenses. First, nSi enhances chlorophyll content and likely stabilizes photosynthetic membranes under water deficit, supporting carbon assimilation when drought would normally suppress photosynthesis and protein synthesis. Second, nSi strongly induces PAL1 and LS, thereby boosting phenylpropanoid and terpenoid metabolism; this supports lignin deposition, phenolic and essential oil accumulation, and associated structural and chemical defenses that help maintain cell wall integrity and protect cellular components under drought. Third, the coordinated up regulation of ALDH3H1 and proline, together with controlled H2O2 accumulation, points to an nSi mediated strengthening of osmotic adjustment and detoxification systems that limit oxidative and aldehyde damage during water deficit.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that foliar nSi application significantly upregulated key stress-responsive genes, including limonene synthase, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase 1, and aldehyde dehydrogenase family 3 member H1, under drought stress in cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.). These genes play key roles in enhancing antioxidant capacity, secondary metabolite production, and detoxification processes. The increased activity of ALDH3H1 under drought and nSi co-treatment underscores its importance in mitigating oxidative damage, which is a hallmark of drought stress. The coordinated upregulation of LS, PAL1, and ALDH3H1 highlights the integration of secondary metabolite pathways under nanosilica-mediated stress tolerance. nSi likely acts as a priming agent, eliciting early gene expression and enhancing stomatal function and nutrient uptake, thereby indirectly boosting stress-responsive pathways. To our knowledge, this is the first report establishing a direct correlation between nSi application and increased essential oil yield (EOY) in cumin under drought conditions. The strong association between LS and EOY underscores the role of terpenoid biosynthesis in stress adaptation and economic yield. The retention of total chlorophyll under moderate drought with nSi application is another significant finding. Chlorophyll preservation is critical for maintaining photosynthetic efficiency and energy production under stress conditions. The genotypic differences in chlorophyll retention, with Semnan showing higher levels than Isfahan under control and moderate drought conditions, further emphasize the role of genetic factors in the stress response. The significant increase in EOY under nSi application is associated with upregulation of LS, which drives terpenoid biosynthesis. This study identified 4 mM nSi under mild drought (60% FC) as the optimal treatment for maximizing EOY and stress tolerance. This concentration and stress level effectively activated stress-responsive pathways without inducing toxicity or overloading the plant’s metabolic resources.
