Prophylactic Knee Bracing in College Football Players Is Associated With a Low Rate of Anterior Cruciate Ligament Tears
Authors: David Riopelle, Christian Cruz, Russ Romano, Alexander Weber, Seth Gamradt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo analyze the annual incidence of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries in National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA) college football offensive linemen utilizing prophylactic knee bracing at a single institution from 2002 to 2023.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study on prospectively collected data from an institutional NCAA football injury database. We retrospectively analyzed data between 2002 and 2023 to assess the incidence of ACL injury in NCAA football players, comparing offensive linemen wearing bilateral prophylactic knee braces (PKBs) to the overall injury rates of all positions on the team. Injury rate per 1,000 athlete-exposures was calculated for each group with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and the difference between groups was calculated with a significance level set at P < .05.ResultsThere was an estimated total of 52,844 offensive linemen athlete-exposures and 298,320 all-position athlete-exposures in the cohort. There were 8 total ACL injuries in offensive linemen wearing bilateral PKBs over a period of 22 years, compared to 58 in all other positions, with an offensive linemen ACL injury rate of 0.151 per 1,000 athlete-exposures (95% CI, 0.030-0.273) versus an overall ACL injury rate of 0.194 per 1,000 athlete-exposures (95% CI, 0.145-0.243). There was a difference of 0.043 ACL tears per 1,000 athlete-exposures in favor of the offensive linemen, which did not reach significance (P = .501).ConclusionsIn this study over a 22-year span, we found a low overall incidence of ACL tears among NCAA football offensive linemen wearing bilateral PKBs, with a lower, although not statistically significant, rate of ACL injury compared to players at all positions.Level of EvidenceLevel III, retrospective comparative study of prospectively collected data.

Lower extremity injuries make up the majority of athletic injuries across all National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA) sports, significantly contributing to missed playing time, injury burden, and overall health care costs.1 More specifically, knee ligamentous injuries are common NCAA football injuries both during the season and in the off-season, with the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) being one of the most commonly injured articular structures.2, 3, 4 NCAA football had the highest annual incidence of ACL injury across 15 major collegiate sports from 1988 to 2004 and again from 2004 to 2013, with data from the NCAA Injury Surveillance Program showing an overall rate of ACL injury of 0.17 per 1,000 athlete-exposures, with an exposure defined as participation in a practice or game.1,4 National Football League (NFL) data from 2015 to 2018 revealed that ACL tears not only are common but also result in a much higher number of player-days lost per season than any other lower extremity injury.5 Furthermore, college ACL injury rates are not decreasing, despite the increased focus on injury prevention in recent years.4 The total number of annual college football players has increased by nearly 20,000 since the year 2000, per the NCAA sports participation database. As this participation continues to increase, ACL injuries can be expected to follow without improved injury prevention strategies.6

Rigid knee braces have been in use since the 1960s for the prevention and mitigation of knee ligamentous injuries in football and other sports. Prophylactic knee bracing, defined by the American Association of Orthopaedic Surgeons in 1985, utilizes “off-the-shelf” braces for use in sport prior to injury with a stable knee.7 Braces typically consist of lightweight, hinged lateral and medial bars, secured with a sleeve or elastic straps. The braces theoretically resist varus, valgus, and rotational forces on the knee joint, supplementing the knee’s natural stiffness for contact and noncontact stresses.8 Currently, prophylactic knee bracing within American football remains controversial, with conflicting and inconclusive evidence of its utility for preventing ACL and other knee injuries.7,9,10 For example, 1 historic study commonly cited from Hewson et al.11 in 1986 showed no change in injury rates between braced and nonbraced college football players. However, a more recent 2023 study on NFL players found a significantly reduced rate of major knee injury in players wearing prophylactic knee braces (PKBs).10 This study also found that the overall utilization of prophylactic bracing decreased from 16.3% to 5.6% by the end of the study period, further highlighting the confusion surrounding whether these braces prevent injury and should be worn by players.10 There is a paucity of updated literature to guide young players and training staff, and given the prevalence of ACL and other knee injuries, there is a continued need for research into the prophylactic potential of these knee braces.

We sought to contribute to the growing fund of literature on PKB usage and knee injury prevention potential, providing data to guide athletes and sports departments on appropriate use of these braces. The purpose of the present study was to analyze the annual incidence of ACL injuries in NCAA college football offensive linemen utilizing prophylactic knee bracing at a single institution from 2002 to 2023. We hypothesized that NCAA college football offensive linemen utilizing bilateral PKBs would have a low overall ACL injury rate compared to all positions on the team.

Methods

Institutional review board approval was obtained for this study. From 2002 to 2023, our research group prospectively collected player injury data from the institution’s NCAA Division I football team for all positions year-round. It has been standard since the start of data collection for all offensive linemen to wear bilateral PKBs during practice and games. Injury data during this period were compiled into a database for research and analysis, updated continuously. All recorded instances of ACL tears, as diagnosed by the team physician based on physical examination and/or confirmatory imaging, in all players were identified and extracted. Other variables extracted from the data set for our analysis included year of injury and position type (offensive lineman vs other position). Activity was extracted using a string search for “game” or “practice.” Position was extracted using a string search for “off” and “line.” No demographic data were collected in the database as this was not part of the initial collection protocol. We excluded athletes with ACL injuries sustained outside of football, as well as athletes with incomplete data in the data set. There were no other exclusion criteria.

In our data analysis, each “practice” or “game” equated to 1 athlete exposure per player. As we did not have discrete data on exactly how many players from each position played in each practice and game in the 22-year study period, we estimated the number of offensive linemen and other-position players who participated in each event based on approximations from the team’s professional medical training staff. For each game, we estimated that, on average, 7 total offensive linemen played, so there were 7 offensive linemen exposures per game. We estimated that 28 other-position players participated, resulting in 28 other exposures, for a total of 35 athlete-exposures per game. For each practice, we estimated that 15 offensive linemen participated for 15 exposures and 70 other-position players participated for 70 exposures, for a total of 85 exposures per practice. Each year consisted of 11 games and 155 practices. We computed the mean proportion of ACL injuries per athlete-exposure per year and converted this to the rate per 1,000 athlete-exposures. Rates were calculated overall for all positions and for the subsample of offensive linemen, reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The difference between these groups was then calculated with a 2-sample t test, with the significance level set to P < .05.

Results

From 2002 to 2023, there were an estimated 2,402 overall (practice and game) athlete-exposures per year for offensive linemen using the methodology outlined above, totaling 52,844 offensive linemen athlete-exposures. For all positions, there were an estimated 13,560 athlete-exposures per year, totaling 298,320 all-position athlete-exposures. One instance of ACL injury in a single athlete was excluded as it was due to a non-athletic-related injury. Four other injury events were missing injury type (i.e., it was unknown if these were ACL injuries or other injuries), so these were also excluded from the analysis. There were a total of 8 instances of ACL tears among offensive linemen wearing bilateral PKBs over the study period, compared to 58 total ACL tears for all positions. Yearly breakdowns of these exposures, events (i.e., ACL tears), and calculated injury rates per 1,000 exposures are shown in Table 1. Over the entire study period, the offensive linemen ACL injury rate was 0.151 per 1,000 athlete-exposures (95% CI, 0.030-0.273), and the all-position ACL injury rate was 0.194 per 1,000 athlete-exposures (95% CI, 0.145-0.243), resulting in a difference between these groups of 0.043 injuries per 1,000 athlete-exposures (P = .501). Discussion

In this study, we found a low overall incidence of ACL injuries in offensive linemen wearing bilateral PKBs. The offensive lineman injury rate was 0.151 per 1,000 exposures, while the all-position injury rate was 0.194 per 1,000 athlete-exposures. These findings are based on our institution’s experience from 2002 to 2023 with NCAA football player ACL injuries overall and in offensive linemen wearing bilateral PKBs. While the ACL injury rate was lower for offensive linemen weaking PKBs compared to the entire team, this difference did not reach significance (P = .501). It is possible that, given the rarity of ACL injuries in general in our population, this study may be underpowered to detect a true difference in injury rate between offensive linemen and the rest of the team.

The most recent reported data from the NCAA Injury Surveillance Program in 2016 show an NCAA football overall ACL injury rate of 0.17 per 1,000 athlete-exposures, which is also higher than our calculated injury rate for offensive linemen in bilateral PKBs.6 While these values cannot be directly compared due to differences in data collection and calculations (i.e., because we estimated our overall exposures), our lower rate of injury in offensive linemen compared to this national average suggests that bracing may be beneficial for the prevention of severe ligamentous knee injury. These data add to the fund of literature analyzing the use of prophylactic knee bracing for the prevention of ACL injuries in football players, specifically offensive linemen.9,10

Anterior cruciate ligament injuries are among the most common injuries in college sports, especially NCAA football.1, 2, 3, 4 Prophylactic knee bracing in football players for knee injury prevention has been controversial since it gained popularity in the 1980s.7 A randomized controlled trial from West Point in 1986-1987 randomized football players between wearing a brace and not wearing one during the football season and found that bracers had a lower overall knee injury rate, lower rate of MCL tears, and lower rate of ACL tears, although the study was underpowered to detect a significant difference in ACL tears.9 Of note, this study also found that the efficacy of bracing for injury prevention did depend on position, with defensive players significantly benefiting from PKBs while offensive players showed no benefit. Our study also had a lower absolute rate of ACL tears in offensive linemen using PKBs compared to the entire team, although it also seems to be underpowered to detect a significant difference. More recently, a study analyzing prophylactic knee bracing in NFL offensive linemen from 2014 to 2020 found a significantly reduced rate of major knee injury in players wearing prophylactic knee braces, with ACL tears in 1 of 154 (0.65%) bracers versus 18 of 1,407 (1.28%) nonbracers (P = .04).10

As mentioned earlier, Ackerman et al.10 also found that the overall utilization of prophylactic bracing in the NFL decreased from 16.3% to 5.6% by the end of their study period from 2014 to 2020. One possible explanation for the reduced use of knee bracing in the NFL and otherwise is the perceived notion that these knee braces hinder overall performance (speed, strength, etc.). Studies on the biomechanical and performance differences in braced versus nonbraced knees show inconsistent conclusions.7 For example, Ewing et al.12 analyzed lower extremity muscle activation in braced versus nonbraced knees and found that the hamstrings and vastus muscles exhibited greater peak muscle forces with bracing, without a change in contraction timing, which may partially explain enhanced knee stability in braces. Houston and Goemans,13 on the other hand, showed that knee extension velocity was slower with bracing than without, resulting in higher blood lactate levels. Greene et al.14 revealed that some knee braces result in reduced speed and agility while others do not. As the landscape of college and professional sports continues to change, these contradictions in the literature point toward a need for continued study and updates on the effectiveness of knee bracing in athletes. In the present study, we show a very low ACL injury rate in offensive linemen using prophylactic knee braces over a period of 22 years, lower than the overall injury rate for the entire team but with a difference that did not reach significance. Future research should include randomized controlled trials that directly compare injury rates in braced versus nonbraced knees in the modern football era, examining specific injury rates for other positions on offense and defense. Given the high prevalence of ACL tears in college and professional football, as well as in other collegiate and professional sports, research and development of these potential preventative measures is important for the health and safety of athletes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective database study, we were limited to data reported in our data set over the collection period, with 4 players having incomplete injury data that needed to be excluded. While we could infer that all offensive linemen in the 22-year period wore PBKs by our institution’s standard practices, we did not have information on whether or not other position players wore PKBs, limiting our overall data analysis capabilities. The decision to wear knee braces for these other positions was more individualized and specific to each player’s situation, which could not be controlled for here. The study analyzed only male football players from a single institution, with a focus on offensive linemen, limiting overall generalizability. Given the low rate of ACL injury overall in general, our study was likely underpowered to detect a difference between the groups, if one exists. Additionally, we were not able to compare our linemen’s ACL injury rates in PKBs to linemen injury rates without PKBs because all our linemen wear PKBs by convention. An additional limitation is in our definition of “athlete-exposure,” defining exposure broadly as any instance a player played in a practice or game at all, rather than how many plays these players were involved in that would actually put them at risk. Some players naturally play more than others and some not at all, introducing significant granularity to the concept of an “exposure.” Given the continuous nature of practices and games, in which a player could be injured in several ways during drills and plays, the exact number for exposures would be difficult to quantify with precision. As has been done with the historical NCAA data presented, this is simplified with considering each practice and game as a single exposure for each player, streamlining data collection and analysis but sacrificing precision. Furthermore, in our study, due to limited data in our data set on the exact number of players participating in each practice and game from each position, we instead used estimations from our professional medical athletic staff for number of athlete-exposures per practice and game, which may not accurately reflect the actual number of exposures in which the players were at risk of knee injury. Because of this, we are not able to directly compare our values for injuries per athlete-exposure to the historic data sets.

Conclusions

In this study over a 22-year span, we found a low overall incidence of ACL tears among NCAA football offensive linemen wearing bilateral PKBs, with a lower, although not statistically significant, rate of ACL injury compared to players at all positions.
