Cerebellar hyperperfusion following endovascular obliteration of dural arteriovenous fistula: illustrative case
Authors: Kohei Chida, Yasukazu Katakura, Kei Kitakami, Masahiro Yabuki, Wataru Yanagihara, Kenya Miyoshi, Yosuke Akamatsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDCerebral hyperperfusion after reconstructive surgery can develop intracerebral hemorrhage. However, there is no previous report of cerebellar hyperperfusion after surgical treatment for dural arteriovenous fistula (DAVF). In this report, the authors describe a case of cerebellar hyperperfusion following endovascular obliteration of DAVF.OBSERVATIONSA man in his 70s had a progressing cognitive disturbance. Preoperative angiography showed a high-grade DAVF at the right sigmoid sinus with occlusion of the ipsilateral internal jugular vein. Since the superior sagittal sinus (SSS) was also occluded and the left transverse sinus (TS) exhibited severe stenosis, the bilateral cerebellum exhibited severe venous congestion. Preoperative brain perfusion SPECT showed severely reduced cerebrovascular reactivity to acetazolamide in the bilateral cerebellum. Transvenous transluminal angioplasty of the left TS and SSS and coil embolization of the right sigmoid sinus were performed. While the patient did not develop new neurological deficits, postoperative brain perfusion SPECT showed increased cerebral blood flow in the bilateral cerebellum up to 210% compared to that before the endovascular treatment. Blood pressure was strictly controlled, and then cerebellar hyperperfusion recovered on postoperative day 7.LESSONSCerebellar hyperperfusion can develop after surgical treatment for DAVF. Therefore, careful evaluation of preoperative hemodynamics, even in the posterior fossa, may be important. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26342

Surgical treatment for dural arteriovenous fistula (DAVF) can resolve venous ischemia.1 Evidence from previous research suggests that impaired autoregulation caused by prolonged ischemia may lead to vasoparalysis, thereby contributing to cerebral hyperperfusion following blood flow restoration.2 Cerebral hyperperfusion is generally characterized by symptoms of unilateral headache, pain in the face or eyes, seizures, and focal neurological deficits, with intracerebral hemorrhage as the most serious complication.2,3 In addition, postoperative hyperperfusion sometimes leads to diffuse damage in the cerebral cortex and white matter, even if no symptoms are evident.4–6 Whereas several reports have described this phenomenon after carotid reconstructive surgery3,7 or bypass surgery for chronic cerebral ischemia (including moyamoya diseases),8 cerebral hyperperfusion following surgical treatment for DAVF appears rare.9–11 Furthermore, no previous reports have described cerebellar hyperperfusion after surgical treatment for DAVF. In this report, we describe a case of cerebellar hyperperfusion following endovascular obliteration for DAVF.

Illustrative Case

A man in his 70s with a medical history of hypertension and dyslipidemia presented with a history of cognitive disturbance progressing over a period of several months. Diffusion-weighted imaging and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) imaging revealed hyperintense areas in the right temporal lobe and basal ganglia (Fig. 1A). MRA revealed hyperintensity in the venous sinuses of the posterior fossa due to arteriovenous shunting (Fig. 1B). High-grade DAVF located at the right sigmoid sinus was confirmed on cerebral angiography. The feeders for the arteriovenous fistula (AVF) originated from the right external carotid artery branches, including the petrosal branch of the middle meningeal artery and the stylomastoid artery and mastoid branches of the occipital artery (Fig. 2A). As the right internal jugular vein (IJV) was occluded, retrograde venous reflux involved the ipsilateral transverse sinus (TS), contralateral TS, superior sagittal sinus (SSS), and straight sinus with multiple cortical venous refluxes of both supra- and infratentorial veins toward the temporo-occipital lobe and cerebellar hemisphere bilaterally (Fig. 2A and B). The SSS was also occluded at the occipital region and the left TS exhibited severe stenosis (Fig. 2A). During the venous phase of angiography, the cortical veins of the right temporo-occipital lobe and bilateral cerebellum appeared tortuous and dilated, with severe venous congestion draining into the TS, whereas the vein of the right frontoparietal lobe drained via the diploic vein and superficial temporal vein due to occlusion of the SSS (Fig. 2B and C). The lesion was classified as Cognard type IV based on ectatic dilatation of multiple cortical veins. Brain perfusion SPECT using N-isopropyl-p-[123I]-iodoamphetamine (IMP) at rest and acetazolamide challenge was performed as described previously.12 Cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) to acetazolamide was calculated as follows: CVR (%) = [(blood flow measured after acetazolamide ­challenge – resting blood flow)/resting blood flow] × 100. This investigation demonstrated reduced cerebral blood flow (CBF) in the right cerebral hemisphere, right cerebellar hemisphere, and left cerebellar hemisphere, with values of 19.7, 19.2, and 26.4 mg/100 g/min, respectively (Fig. 3A). CVR was severely reduced in the same areas, with values of 7.6%, −9.1%, and −9.1%, respectively (Fig. 3B). We planned transluminal angioplasty of the left TS and SSS and coil embolization of the right sigmoid sinus. Prior to performing the venous approach, a 4-Fr Simmonds catheter (Medikit) was placed into the right external carotid artery to visualize the condition of the DAVF. Using the contralateral jugular vein approach, a 6-Fr ASAHI FUBUKI Dilator Kit (Asahi Intecc) was placed in the left IJV. Via this guiding catheter, a 6-Fr Esperance distal access catheter (Japan Lifeline) was advanced with a Phenom 17 microcatheter (Medtronic) and Chikai 14 black microguidewire (Asahi Intecc). After guiding the 6-Fr Esperance to the left TS–sigmoid sinus junction, an Rx-Genity 5 mm/20 mm (Kaneka Medix) was guided to the stenotic lesion of the left TS. After transluminal angioplasty with Rx-Genity, the 6-Fr Esperance distal access catheter was advanced to the right sigmoid sinus via the confluence of sinuses. The microcatheter was then guided to the end of the occluded portion of the right sigmoid sinus for coil embolization, which was just proximal to the shunt point via the distal access catheter. Microcoils were then deployed for embolization of the right sigmoid sinus. After complete occlusion of shunt flow was observed on angiograms, a distal access catheter was guided just proximal to the occluded region of the SSS. The microguidewire could not pass through the occluded lesion, so we used a Radifocus guidewire 0.035-inch (Terumo). Once the Radifocus guidewire passed through the lesion, a microguidewire was then advanced following the Radifocus guidewire to the frontal part of the SSS. Transluminal ­angioplasty was performed from the frontal part to the occipital part of the SSS. Finally, complete occlusion of shunt flow and recanalization of the occluded SSS were observed on the angiograms (Fig. 4A and B). The patient did not develop new neurological deficits after treatment. Although postoperative MRI revealed no new lesions and no residual DAVF (Supplementary Fig. 1), brain perfusion SPECT revealed increased CBF in the cerebellum and basal ganglia bilaterally up to 210% and 207%, respectively, compared to those before endovascular treatment (Fig. 5A). As the patient did not develop any symptoms due to the phenomenon, systolic blood pressure was controlled below 110 mm Hg by intravenous administration of nicardipine without sedation. Brain perfusion SPECT performed 7 days after the endovascular treatment revealed that hyperperfusion had ­recovered (Fig. 5B). Cognitive disturbance improved with rehabilitation, and the patient was discharged 1 month after treatment with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0. Discussion

Observations

We reported a case of bilateral cerebellar hyperperfusion following endovascular obliteration for DAVF.

In the present case, the patient showed progression of cognitive disturbance for a couple of months. Since the symptoms seemed to have resulted from venous congestion of the supratentorial regions,14 embolization of the fistula would not have been sufficient as treatment. Transluminal angioplasty using a balloon catheter of the severely stenotic TS and occluded SSS was thus performed simultaneously in the present case.

Only 4 cases of cerebellar hyperperfusion after surgical treatment have been reported on the basis of hemodynamic study findings.15–18 In all 4 cases, surgical treatment involved revascularizations for steno-occlusive vertebral artery, and cerebellar hyperperfusion developing following surgical treatment for DAVF does not appear to have been reported previously. Cerebral hyperperfusion was originally defined as an increase in CBF of the cerebral hemisphere by at least 200% compared to levels before carotid endarterectomy. While no distinct definition of cerebral hyperperfusion has been devised other than after carotid endarterectomy, the 4 mentioned cases of cerebellar hyperperfusion after revascularization for steno-occlusive vertebral artery revealed an approximately 200% increase in CBF.15–18 In the present case, since CBF increased more than 200% in the cerebellum bilaterally, cerebellar hyperperfusion was defined as present. Furthermore, one of those cases demonstrated preoperative severe hemodynamic insufficiency by 123I-IMP SPECT with acetazolamide challenge,15 consistent with the findings of the present case. A case of cerebral hyperperfusion following DAVF obliteration has been reported.10 In that case, the area of postoperative hyperperfusion showed that CVR decreased to acetazolamide preoperatively, consistent with the present case. Previously, the severity of DAVF has been classified based on cortical venous reflux and occlusion of the involved sinus.1,19,20 Recently, however, the severity of the lesion has been demonstrated based on the cerebral hemodynamics in the involved area. Kawaguchi et al. measured CBF by brain perfusion SPECT in 18 patients with DAVFs and concluded that a decrease in CBF was related to cortical venous reflux and occlusion of the involved sinus.21 A PET study of 10 patients with DAVF revealed that cortical venous reflux and delayed parenchymal circulation correlated with increased cerebral blood volume (CBV), decreased CBF, and a mild elevation in oxygen extraction fraction.22 Furthermore, a study that measured CBF and CBV by brain perfusion SPECT with acetazolamide challenge in 20 patients with DAVF demonstrated that the reduction in CBF correlated strongly with the pathological findings of venous congestive encephalopathy on MRI, and the loss of CVR correlated closely with irreversibility of the lesion.23 A separate PET study of 8 patients with DAVF revealed that symptomatic patients tended to exhibit more pronounced hemodynamic compromise than asymptomatic patients, and reported that these alterations are comparable to those found in patients with occlusive carotid artery diseases.24 According to these reports, the cerebral hemodynamics of patients with DAVF, even in the cerebellum, should be impaired similar to those of patients with cerebral steno-occlusive artery diseases. Cerebellar hyperperfusion could therefore occur in a patient with severe progression of DAVF.

The simultaneous procedures—coil embolization of the right sigmoid sinus and transluminal angioplasty of the left TS and SSS—might have resulted in the dramatic change to cerebral hemodynamics and contributed to hyperperfusion bilaterally in the cerebellum and basal ganglia. In addition, CVR of the right cerebral hemisphere also seemed to be decreased, whereas cerebral hyperperfusion did not occur in that area. This might be because brain damage was evident in the right temporal lobe and basal ganglia on preoperative MRI, which could impair metabolism in the ipsilateral cerebral cortex due to a remote effect and might have led to overestimation of the CVR.25 Finally, few reports have described hyperperfusion following surgical treatment for DAVF, probably because imaging evaluations have not been performed. Since previous investigators have suggested that hyperperfusion following surgical treatment for DAVF can lead to irreversible neuronal damage,10 careful evaluation of preoperative hemodynamics should be performed to predict hyperperfusion following DAVF with hemodynamic insufficiency.

Lessons

Cerebellar hyperperfusion can develop after surgical treatment for DAVF, which is associated with severe hemodynamic insufficiency. Careful evaluation of preoperative hemodynamics may therefore be important, even in the posterior fossa.
