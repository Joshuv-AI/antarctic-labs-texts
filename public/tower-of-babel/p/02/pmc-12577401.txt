Socioeconomic disparities in urological cancers in iran: a systematic analysis for the Global Burden of Disease study 2019
Authors: Alireza Khajavi, Fatemeh Haj Hashemi, Fatemeh Khatami, Roghayeh Koohi Ortakand, Maryam Noori, Ramin Rahimnia, Seyed Mohammad Kazem Aghamir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study examined the socioeconomic disparities across Iran’s provinces regarding health measures for urological cancers, including bladder, kidney, prostate, and testicular cancers.MethodsWe analyzed the age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and death rates for 31 Iranian provinces using data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 (2000–2019). Socioeconomic status was evaluated using principal components analysis, and health inequalities were measured with the concentration index (CI).ResultsFrom 2000 to 2019, there was an increase in the incidence and prevalence of all urological cancers and the mortality of prostate cancer. However, the mortality/incidence ratios for prostate and bladder cancers decreased. The highest CIs, 0.26 for prevalence and 0.24 for incidence were recorded for testicular cancer in 2000–2004, which decreased to 0.18 and 0.17 in 2015–2019. No remarkable inequality was found in kidney cancer measures or the death rates of bladder and prostate cancers. Persistent inequality was observed in testicular cancer death rates (CIs of 0.16 and 0.12 in 2000–2004 and 2015–2019). The mortality/incidence ratio for testicular cancer showed minor inequalities favoring the poorer provinces.ConclusionAge-standardized incidence and prevalence rates for bladder, prostate, and testicular cancers, as well as testicular cancer death rate, were higher in the better-off provinces. Poorer provinces had higher mortality/incidence ratios for testicular cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25054-4.Keywords: Urological cancers, Socioeconomic, Inequality, Iran, GBD

Introduction

As the world’s 17th largest country with an area over 1.64 million km2, socioeconomic disparities play a significant role in shaping the Iran’s diverse landscape. Iran’s provinces display noticeable differences in terms of the economic development, sociocultural dynamics, healthcare infrastructure, and health outcomes [1–3]. Amidst these regional variations, noncommunicable health outcomes, particularly urological cancers, are of great concern. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, these neoplasms accounted for 7.8% of the disability adjusted life of years attributed to noncommunicable diseases in 2021, in the case of Iran [4]. They engage organs in both genders, affecting the kidneys, bladder, testes, and prostate, and causing economic burdens for the health system and the individuals [5]. Besides the differences between countries in terms of occurrence and outcomes of cancers, there are also diversities across the variety of socioeconomic characteristics, such as educational level, financial status, and risk factors related to life-style, work environment, residential area, and environmental pollution [6, 7]. Urological cancer epidemiology has attracted global attention [8–10] and numerous studies have also been reported on these cancers’ determinants and concequences in the case of Iran [5, 11, 12]. While remarkable investigations are reported on the association between socioeconomic status and urological cancers’ incidence and prevalence [13–15], there remains a significant gap with regard to examining the intricate interplay of socioeconomic factors with urological cancers across Iran’s 31 provinces.

This study seeks to address this crucial knowledge gap by conducting a comprehensive analysis of socioeconomic disparities and inequalities related to the incidence, prevalence, and mortality of urological cancers across Iran’s provinces. Our investigation is supported by extensive data from the GBD study, offering valuable insights into the epidemiology of these cancers. We specifically examine the socioeconomic distribution of urological cancers, focusing on kidney, bladder, prostate, and testicular cancers.

Methods

Data sources

This study utilized secondary data from two sources. The Global Burden of Diseases Study 2019 provides estimates of incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates per 100,000 population, for 204 countries from 1990 to 2019 [16]. Among other diseases, GBD reports these health measures for urological cancers, including bladder, kidney, prostate, and testicular cancers. GBD estimates are generated through an ongoing global research project based on advanced modeling techniques, led by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, which has been active since the 1990 s and is updated regularly [17]. The data is publicly available at the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) repository. In addition to national data, GBD reports include assessments for provinces and states in several countries, including Iran. For this study, we obtained age-standardized health measures for urological cancers, as this allows for comparability of estimates at the sub-national level across Iran’s provinces. Additionally, we calculated the mortality-to-incidence ratio as an indicator of the health system’s performance and cancer healthcare disparity [18].

The second data source was the Statistical Center of Iran (SCI). Since the 1980 s, SCI has conducted annual cross-sectional surveys known as the Household Income and Expenditure Survey (HIES) [19]. These surveys, with annual sample sizes exceeding 100,000 participants, provided provincial-level data on mean age, literacy rates, marriage rates, family size, and car ownership. Additionally, with reference to the national censuses conducted by SCI in 1996, 2006, 2011, and 2016 and using the growth rates and interpolation/extrapolation approaches, annual estimates of population density and urbanization at the provincial level were obtained [20].

PCA Estimation

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a standard dimension reduction method that extracts a smaller set of components from a group of possibly correlated variables [21]. As a recognized tool for constructing socioeconomic status (SES) indicators, PCA provides a weighted average of demographic and asset ownership variables, in which the weights are obtained from the interconnection structure [22]. PCA was applied to seven variables including mean age, literacy rate, marriage rate, family size, car ownership, population density, and urbanization. The SES indicator created through PCA accounted for 55.9% of the information from these variables. This indicator was scaled to a range between 0 and 1, to facilitate comparing the provinces.

Given changes in the number and composition of provinces over the past decades, and the lack of detailed data on these divisions in the 1990 s, the provincial SES data from 2000 to 2019 were merged with GBD data to form the final study period. Throughout this paper, measures for prostate and testicular cancers are reported for men, while measures for bladder and kidney cancers are reported for the entire population.

Statistical analysis

The concentration index (CI) was used to identify socioeconomic differences across Iran’s provinces regarding urological cancer incidence, prevalence, mortality, and mortality/incidence ratio, with SES as the ranking variable [23]. CI has been extensively used to measure the amount of cocioeconomic inequality in health related variables [24, 25]. CI ranges from − 1 to 1, where values closer to −1 indicate a bias toward the poor, values closer to 1 indicate a bias toward the rich, and values near zero indicate no inequality.

The analyses were conducted using Stata version 14.

Results

Based on the PCA’s findings, Iran’s provinces were categorized into four socioeconomic strata, moving from the least developed provinces (Q1 including Sistan and Baluchistan, Kohgiluyeh Boyer-Ahmad, Ilam, Hormozgan, Lorestan, Khuzestan, Kurdistan, and West Azarbayejan), to the lower mediate ones (Q2 including Golestan, North Khorasan, Kermanshah, Chahar Mahaal Bakhtiari, Ardebil, Zanjan, Kerman, and Bushehr), to the upper mediate ones (Q3 including Hamadan, South Khorasan, Fars, East Azarbayejan, Khorasan-e-Razavi, Qazvin, Markazi, and Gilan), and finally the most developed ones (Q4 including Mazandaran, Qom, Semnan, Isfahan, Yazd, Alborz, and Tehran). The equivalent SES scores are reported in the Appendix Table S1.

Age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates per 100,000 population and mortality/incidence ratios of prostate cancer

This figure indicates apparent increases in the incidence and prevalence and to a lesser extent, increases in the mortality and decreases in the mortality/incidence ratios of prostate cancer, nationally and in all socioeconomic strata. Notably, the most developed provinces have constantly had the highest prevalences and lowest mortalities and mortality/incidence ratios, indicative of benefiting from more sophisticated caring systems. The largest increase of 89% occurred in prevalences (from 98.6 in 2000 to 186.2 in 2019), and the largest decrease of 28% was for the mortality/incidence ratios (from 67.8 in 2000 to 48.5 in 2019) both in the case of the least developed provinces.

Figure 2 presents Iran’s trends of testicular cancer, interms of age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates per 100,000 population and mortality/incidence ratios. While increases in the incidence, prevalence, and mortality of testicular cancer occurred nationally and in all socioeconomic strata, mortality/incidence ratios witnessed minor decreases. Again, the most developed provinces constantly had the highest incidences, prevalences, and mortalities and lowest mortality/incidence ratios. The largest increases of 12 folds were for the incidence and prevalence (from 0.3 to 3.6 and 2.6 to 32.0, in 2000 and 2019, respectively), in the upper intermediate provinces.

Figure 3 shows Iran’s trends of bladder cancer, in terms of age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates per 100,000 population and mortality/incidence ratios. Accordingly, bladder cancer’s incidence and prevalence increased over the years, mortality/incidence ratios demonstrated obvious declines, and mortalities had almost no change, nationally and in all socioeconomic strata. The most developed provinces constantly had the highest incidences and prevalences and lowest mortality/incidence ratios. As the largest rise, prevalence witnessed an increase of around 1.6 times over 20 years, in all socioeconomic strata. The largest decline of 25% in mortality/incidence ratios was obtained for the lowest socioeconomic stratum, from 59.5 in 2000 to 44.4 in 2019.

At last, age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates per 100,000 population and mortality/incidence ratios of kidney cancer are shown in Fig. 4. It shows increases in the incidence, prevalence, and mortality, and no change in mortality/incidence ratios. However, the socioeconomic divisions converge to a large extent and seem to have little or no impact on these measures. The only notable differentiation is the 1.8 times increase in prevalence in the least developed provinces, from 9.0 in 2000 to 16.2 in 2019.

Age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates per 100,000 population and mortality/incidence ratios of kidney cancer

Next, the concentration index was calculated for the health measures of incidence, prevalence, death rates, and mortality-to-incidence ratio. Figures 5 and 6 depict the findings, over four periods 2000–2004, 2005–2009, 2010–2014, and 2015–2019. They indicate the persistent inequality of bladder, prostate, and testicular cancers in terms of incidence and prevalence measures, meaning the better off experiencing the higher rates. Specifically, the most sizable inequalities with CIs of 0.26 and 0.24 in the period 2000–2004 were recorded for prevalence and incidence of testicular cancer, which reduced to 0.18 and 0.17 in the period 2015–2019, respectively. No inequality in any of the measures of kidney cancer and death rates of bladder and prostate cancers was detected. There was also a persistent inequality in testicular cancer death rates (CIs of 0.16 and 0.12 in the periods 2000–2004 and 2015–2019). Finally, the mortality/incidence ratio showed minor inequalities toward the poor, again the most notably in terms of testicular cancer, meaning the higher mortality/incidence ratios for the poor. The observations from Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4 generally support these inequalities.

Discussion

The careful exploration of socioeconomic differences regarding urological cancers across the expanse of Iran’s 31 provinces uncovers a tableau of intricate insights. Rooted in the bedrock of data sourced from the Global Burden of Disease study, our analysis unravels the higher incidence and prevalence rates of bladder, testicular, and prostate cancers, among the well-off provinces. These provinces also owned lower mortality-to-incidence ratios of urological cancers, especially testicular cancer.

Reviewing the research on Iran, an individual-level study in Tehran considered the association between socioeconomic status and the incidence of bladder and prostate cancers. They assessed socioeconomic status through applying PCA on assets and house features including years of education, house ownership, room per person, area per capita, having independent bath, kitchen, toilet, car, landline, cell phone, freezer, and computer. They reported higher incidences of bladder and prostate cancers in the better-off districts [16]. Also, a study in Asian countries indicates a positive association of development level with the incidence of prostate cancer [26]. Our findings are in line with these reports.

A cohort study in Taiwan reported higher and lower incidences of kidney and bladder cancers among high-income people [15]. A study in the United Kingdom indicated that well-off people had a higher chance of prostate cancer incidence, while no difference was found in terms of prostate cancer death [27]. A study on aggregated data indicates the positive association of development with urological cancer incidence and negative links with the mortality-to-incidence ratio [28]. These conclusions are again similar to our findings.

At the global level, an inequality assessment of urological cancers based on the data of the United Nations Human Development Index and the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) demonstrates higher incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates and lower mortality-to-incidence ratios in more developed countries. A recent review of urologic oncology shows the remaining disparities in the receipt of screening, diagnosis, and treatment of urologic cancers [29].

Regarding the socioeconomic impacts on urological cancers, previous studies have mentioned factors such as income level, occupation status, and residencey [15]. In general terms, the linkage of socioeconomic factors with cancer incidence and survival has been reported for a variety of cancers, in both developed and underdeveloped circumstances [30], while the poor countries experience higher levels of inequality [31]. This association could be attributed to the differences in access to diagnosis and healthcare services and the expertise of medical practitioners. We observed higher incidence and prevalence rates of bladder, testicular, and prostate cancers, among the well-off provinces, which could be attributed to the higher willingness to go under under diagnostics, higher access to screening tests, and the higher financial ability to pay for the treatment. Another explanation could be provided regarding the age distribution. The well-off provinces generally have higher proportion of aged population, and the urological cancers’s incidences are reportedly higher among the elderly [32]. On the other hand, lower mortality-to-incidence ratios of urological cancers in the well-off provinces could be due to high-quality medical care and better health awareness.

Implications for public health policy

The implications stemming from our findings are far-reaching and pivotally influence the contours of public health policies and interventions across Iran. Recognizing the multifaceted urological cancer burdens borne by various provinces fosters the formulation of finely tuned strategies, tailor-made to accommodate the distinct demands of each region and mitigate socioeconomic impediments that obstruct equitable cancer care. Investments in healthcare infrastructure, the inception of early detection initiatives, and the dissemination of cancer education campaigns must be channeled toward the most deprived provinces. Additionally, socioeconomic development and abating poverty can exert a ripple effect on cancer outcomes. Elevated living standards are often concomitant with healthier lifestyles and augmented access to healthcare, thereby synergistically influencing cancer care trajectories. Moreover, provinces with limited economic resources often grapple with elevated cancer incidence rates, delayed diagnoses, and attenuated access to advanced therapeutic modalities. It underscores the imperativeness of accessibility within economically disadvantaged regions, with the overarching goal of calming disparities in cancer outcomes.

Trajectory of future research

The trajectory of future research compels inspecting the determinants and mechanisms that undergird these disparities. Subsequent investigations ought to scrutinize the ramifications of interventions and policies aimed at ameliorating inequalities and enhancing cancer outcomes.

Urological cancers cast a considerable shadow upon Iran’s public health landscape, with kidney, bladder, testicular, and prostate cancers ranking as significant contributors to the nation’s oncological burden. These malignancies take a toll not solely upon the physical health of individuals but also upon healthcare resources and economic development. A comprehensive understanding of the regional variations in the prevalence and outcomes of these cancers is indispensable for the formulation of evidence-based public health strategies and the judicious allocation of resources to where they are most acutely required. The spectrum of socioeconomic factors exerts a profound influence on health outcomes, and urological cancers are no exception. Access to healthcare, the complexities of lifestyle choices, and environmental exposures all converge with socioeconomic status to engender disparities in cancer incidence, survival rates, and therapeutic outcomes. The exploration of these determinants and inequalities is an imperative facet of addressing the multifaceted challenges posed by urological cancers.

Conclusion

In summation, our scrutiny of socioeconomic disparities and inequalities across Iran’s provinces in the context of urological cancers casts a spotlight on regional disparities and socioeconomic determinants. We endeavor to contribute to the formulation of more equitable and efficacious strategies for averting, diagnosing, and addressing urological cancers within the Iranian landscape. The quest for ameliorated cancer outcomes necessitates an enduring commitment to addressing the disparities that persist across a variegated populace and geographical topography.
