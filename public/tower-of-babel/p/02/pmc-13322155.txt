Power, Duration, and Compliance: Reframing Risk of Ventilatory-Induced Lung Injury With the Risk-Adjusted Mechanical-Power Score
Authors: Lada Lijović, Paul Hilders, Tomislav Radočaj, Mladen Širanović, Paul Elbers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Static thresholds for mechanical power (MP) may not prevent ventilator-induced lung injury because risk depends on exposure duration and the underlying respiratory compliance. We aimed to quantify how MP intensity and exposure duration interact with respiratory compliance to predict oxygenation changes consistent with acute respiratory distress syndrome worsening or 14-day mortality.Design:A retrospective analysis of 2 large intensive care datasets.Setting:ICUs in the Netherlands and the United States from 2003 to 2016 and 2008 to 2019, respectively.Patients:Mechanically ventilated adults with oxygenation levels consistent with moderate to severe acute respiratory distress syndrome.Interventions:None.Measurements and Main Results:Time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models stratified by respiratory compliance estimated the hour-specific associations of immediate exceedance and cumulative time above MP thresholds with the primary outcome. Estimated effects were integrated into a risk-adjusted mechanical-power score. Among 2150 mechanically ventilated acute respiratory distress syndrome patients risk from MP exposure was dictated by respiratory compliance: in higher-compliance lungs, risk followed a dose-response pattern, with immediate hazard beginning at 10 J/min (hazard ratio = 1.04) and cumulative harm amplifying significantly over time. Conversely, for low-compliance patients, risk was confined to a narrow power band (11–20 J/min) without evidence of cumulative harm. With risk-adjusted MP score as a predictor of outcome eXtreme Gradient Boosting yielded an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.863.Conclusions:A single “safe” MP threshold is insufficient for guiding ventilation; the risk of lung injury is governed by a dynamic interplay of power intensity, duration, and the patient’s respiratory compliance. The risk-adjusted MP score unifies these factors into a time-varying, clinically interpretable metric that warrants prospective validation for personalized ventilator management.

KEY POINTS

Question: This study aimed to quantify how mechanical-power intensity and exposure duration interact with respiratory compliance to predict acute respiratory distress syndrome progression or 14-day mortality.

Findings: In this retrospective analysis of a large cohort, the risk from mechanical power was dictated by respiratory compliance; in patients with higher compliance, risk began at 10 J/min (hazard ratio = 1.04) and amplified with prolonged exposure, whereas for low-compliance patients, risk was confined to a narrow power band (11–20 J/min) without evidence of cumulative harm.

Meaning: A single safe mechanical-power threshold is insufficient for guiding ventilation, as the risk of lung injury is governed by a dynamic interplay of power intensity, duration, and the patient’s respiratory compliance.

The burden of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) in critically ill patients remains high with mortality hovering at 30–40%, and despite improved understanding of it heterogenous causes and pathophysiology, treatment is largely supportive, with mechanical ventilation as the cornerstone of care. Paradoxically, ventilation exceeding safe levels can itself worsen ARDS via ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI), prompting widespread adaptation of low tidal volumes and limited driving pressures (1).

Research in the last decade has brought us closer to understanding that root mechanism behind VILI is an energy-driven process with irreversible tissue injury arising from intolerable tidal parenchymal strain (2, 3). Therefore, the concept of mechanical power (MP) has emerged as an important metric to guide safe mechanical ventilation (4, 5). MP is a unifying metric quantifying the rate of inflation energy delivered to the respiratory system incorporating tidal volume, airway pressures, flow and respiratory rate (6).

However, MP quantifies total rather than only dissipated energy per unit-time delivered to the respiratory system. Further, not all delivered energy may be equally harmful. Widely distributed low-intensity loss from airway flow resistance and baseline tissue viscoelasticity is noninjurious. High-intensity, localized loss, arising from cyclic recruitment-derecruitment under evaluated surface tension (atelectrauma) and from overdistension leading to structural failure (volutrauma) results in injury (7). Consequently, experiments aiming to find injurious MP thresholds by analyzing large ICU cohorts yielded inconsistent results ranging from 12 to 17 J/min (8–10).

Furthermore, baseline respiratory mechanics may modulate risk. In low-compliance lungs, stress and strain concentrate at the interfaces between regions with different mechanical characteristics, generating local stress and amplifying injury, in inverse proportion to the size of the ventilable compartment (11, 12). Observational studies have shown that MP normalized to compliance is a stronger predictor of mortality than power alone (13). Recent research has been focused on how individual components and mechanical characteristics affect VILI, but no prior work has systematically examined how intensity, duration, and individual lung compliance interact to shape the hazard of ARDS progression (14).

In this study, we analyze high-resolution ventilator data from 2 broad ARDS cohorts to quantify time-varying hazards of MP thresholds across compliance strata. First, we identify which mechanical-power thresholds are most predictive of adverse outcomes. Second, we model how the time-varying hazard evolves with cumulative hours above each threshold, using Cox models with time-varying covariates. Third, we assess how baseline respiratory compliance alters both the magnitude and temporal trajectory of risk. Finally, to translate these insights into a clinically useful tool, we distill each patient’s exposure history into a risk-adjusted mechanical-power (RAMP) score—a single scalar integrating intensity, duration, and compliance-weighted log-hazard ratio. By capturing not only how much power but also how long and in whom it causes harm, our work aims to better inform personalized ventilator strategies that minimize VILI.

METHODS

This study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines. Patient data were extracted from publicly available databases, eliminating the need for further ethics review. Amsterdam University Medical Centers Database (AmsterdamUMCdb) is a European ICU database with 1 billion clinical data points from a 32-bed mixed surgical–medical academic ICU and a 12-bed high-dependency unit, covering 23,106 admissions of 20,109 unique patients between 2003 and 2016 (15). Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) is a North American ICU database with 85,242 patients covering 94,458 admissions between 2008 and 2019 (16).

Data Preprocessing

To ensure accurate identification of invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) across databases, we implemented a unified series of custom structured query language queries, mitigating pitfalls associated with relying solely on procedure codes or single-stream device data (17), described in Supplement Methods (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H950).

From master list of oxygen-therapy episodes, first IMV episode per patient was selected. Final IMV cohort included patients with at least 48 hours of IMV who spent at least 75% of ventilation time in purely controlled mode.

Ventilatory parameters during the first 48 hours of IMV were extracted hourly. When multiple values occurred within an hour, we used their mean, and missing hourly values were forward filled for up to 4 hours. When not directly recorded, respiratory compliance was calculated hourly as tidal volume divided by driving pressure (Pplateau – PEEP) in volume-controlled ventilation. When plateau pressure (Pplateau) was unavailable, as in pressure-controlled modes, driving pressure was defined as pressure above PEEP, corresponding to peak inspiratory pressure (Ppeak). We computed each patient’s time-weighted mean compliance across initial 48 hourly measurements and dichotomized the cohort into “high” or “low” compliance based on the cohort median.

For each patient, we captured all laboratory and vital sign measurements from the 48 hours following the start of their first ventilation episode. If multiple values were available, minimums and maximums were used based on clinical relevance (e.g., minimum mean arterial pressure or maximum Paco2), and forward filled based on reasonable clinical assumptions (allowed forward fill of 4 hours for mean arterial pressure or 12 hours for hemoglobin, e.g.). Missing values were then median imputed. We flagged the presence of sedation, neuromuscular blockade, vasopressor use, or prone positioning which were represented as binary indicators. Outliers were cleaned based on clinically informed boundaries.

Outcomes

To longitudinally assess respiratory function and detect changes in oxygenation consistent with ARDS severity, Pao2/Fio2 ratios were analyzed for each patient throughout the initial 48 hours and in the subsequent 14 days after initiation of IMV. Arterial Pao2 and Fio2 measurements were extracted and paired if taken within 1 hour of each other. If extubation occurred, Fio2 was estimated from available nasal cannula or oxygen mask flow rates. Baseline ARDS category was established from lowest Pao2/Fio2 ratio during the first 48 hours of IMV, using Berlin definition thresholds: severe (< 100), moderate (100–200), mild (200–300), or no ARDS (> 300). Only patients with moderate and severe ARDS were included. Subsequently, Pao2/Fio2 ratios over the remaining 14-day period were monitored for a “worse” transition, defined as at least one hierarchical shift to a higher ARDS severity category relative to 48-hour baseline (moderate to severe). The earliest timestamp of first worsening event was captured. For each patient, a binary 14-day mortality outcome was defined for death occurring between 48-hour ventilation mark and 14 days following that mark. Following the logic that severe ARDS has no further stage progression and death at moderate ARDS is a possibility, 2 variables, transition to worse ARDS or death within 14 days were then merged into one outcome variable by earliest timestamp; patients were followed from the 48-hour mark until the earliest of: first worsening in ARDS category, death, ICU discharge, or day 14.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables are presented as median (Q1–Q3) and compared with the Mann-Whitney U test; qualitative data as n (%) and compared with the χ2.

To evaluate the impact of MP exposure on the 14-day outcome, we performed a separate time-dependent Cox analyses, stratified by 48-hour time-weighted respiratory compliance (19). Each subgroup was analyzed independently. A range of MP thresholds were tested, for each specific threshold and each patient 2 primary time-dependent covariates were created at each hour: 1) immediate exposure, representing the risk associated with exceeding the threshold in that hour, and 2) cumulative exposure, capturing the total number of hours a patient spent above the same threshold from the start of ventilation. The association between time-dependent covariates and the outcome was analyzed using time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models fitted for each threshold over immediate and cumulative exposure. To control confounding, a 2-step process was used to select baseline covariates. First, pairwise correlations among candidate predictors were examined and 1 candidate from each pair with absolute correlation coefficient greater than 0.6 were excluded as an outcome-agnostic redundancy screen. Subsequently, set of baseline covariates were selected using a Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression with the outcome as the response and included as adjustment variables in the final model. The combined effect at each threshold and hour was summarized as the product of the immediate and cumulative hazard ratios (HRs) and displayed in a heatmap. To derive a single-value summary of each patient’s exposure, we computed RAMP score integrating intensity (thresholds crossed) and duration (hours above threshold), weighted by the per-hour log-hazards from the time-varying Cox models and summed across thresholds. Detailed explanations and formulas are available in Supplement Methods (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H950).

Logistic regression, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Random Forest with five-fold cross-validation and 70–30 train-test split, with RAMP score as an additional variable, were performed to assess the predictive value of models for ARDS progression or death, and results presented as ROC curves. All tests were 2-tailed with p value of less than 0.05 considered significant. Analyses were performed in Python’s (SciPy, Version 1.9.1; distributed by Anaconda, Austin, TX), and graphical representations were generated with Matplotlib, Version 3.2.2 (Matplotlib Development Team, San Francisco, CA) (20).

From 23,106 AmsterdamUMCdb and 94,548 MIMIC-IV ICU admissions, we identified 14,447 and 28,645 first IMV episodes. Restricting to episodes greater than or equal to 48 hours reduced these to 3,947 and 9,321; requiring greater than 75% time in controlled modes further narrowed the pool to 1425 and 1088. Applying Berlin Pao2/Fio2 criteria for moderate or severe ARDS within the first 48 hours yielded 1321 Amsterdam and 829 MIMIC-IV patients, who were combined into a single analytic cohort of 2150 patients (Fig. 1).

Among patients with respiratory compliance above the cohort median (“high-compliance”), cumulative exposure to MP was neutral up to 17 J/min (HRs 0.999–1.001; all p > 0.16) and became harmful once the threshold reached 18 J/min: each additional hour above the cutoff increased hazard by roughly 0.3–4.4% (HR 1.003 at 18 J/min, p = 0.016, to HR 1.044 at 35 J/min, p < 0.001). The instantaneous effect of being above the threshold in a given hour was already deleterious from 10 J/min onward–HR 1.04 (95% CI, 1.01–1.08; p = 0.026) at 10 J/min–and climbed almost monotonically to HR 1.38 (95% CI, 1.14–1.67; p = 0.001) at 35 J/min, remaining significant for most cutoffs greater than or equal to 10 J/min (notably borderline around 21–25 J/min).

In low-compliance patients, the direction of the cumulative term was reversed: HRs were consistently below 1 across nearly all, indicating a small but statistically detectable reduction in hazard per additional hour above the cutoff, with only a few nonsignificant points (11–13 J/min). Immediate exceedance increased risk only across a middle band of thresholds–11–20 J/min–where HRs ranged from 1.05 to 1.10 (all p ≤ 0.011); at higher cutoffs, estimates widened and lost significance. DISCUSSION

In this large, high-resolution ARDS cohort, we found that the injurious effects of MP depend not only on the magnitude of power delivered but also on the duration of exposure critically shaped by the patient’s baseline lung mechanics. First, we observe immediate risks–even a single hour spike with preserved compliance of 10 J/min increases HR by ~4% (HR 1.04), whereas a 1-h spike to 15 J/min raises it by ~6% (HR 1.06) and to 20 J/min by ~9% (HR 1.09). In low‑compliance lungs, the first‑hour penalty for 11–20 J/min is similar (HR 1.05–1.10). Second, the cumulative risk from prolonged exposure diverged dramatically between the groups. For patients with preserved compliance, the hazard amplified over time, with the HR after 24 hours at 20 J/min reaching 1.18 and climbing as high as 3.7 at 35 J/min, although this number is driven by low sample size. In contrast, the cumulative hazard in the low-compliance group paradoxically drifted back toward baseline by 24–48 hours—a pattern likely reflecting early non-survivor depletion or evolving lung recruitment. We confined analysis to first 48 hours of ventilation when lung injury is most dynamically evolving and ventilator settings are less driven by imaging and reactive management, and outcomes to 14 days to minimize confounding from later clinical events (21, 22).

These hour-by-hour, compliance-adjusted insights refine and extend prior work on MP thresholds, from the original 12 J/min boundary for histologic and inflammatory injury in animal models to the higher risk-associated cutoffs (12–22 J/min) reported in human observational studies (9, 21, 23–25). Our findings help explain the variability in these reported thresholds; a single cutoff is unlikely to perform well in a mixed cohort where the cumulative risk for high- and low-compliance patients diverges so dramatically. By using high-resolution data and stratifying by lung mechanics, we move beyond the search for a single “safe” value and instead demonstrate that risk of MP is dynamic, context-dependent.

The finding of divergent cumulative effects across compliance strata should be viewed as complementary, rather than contradictory to prior work emphasizing vulnerability of low-compliance lungs, and can be interpreted through the physics of tissue damage (26). Established theory proposes 2 primary mechanisms for VILI: the concentration of energy at stress risers on the boundaries of collapsed and open tissue, leading to acute atelectrauma; and the progressive loading and sequential failure of microelements from repetitive, subcritical strain over time (27). Both pathways could be dominant in different compliance states. The higher immediate HR in lower compliance lungs, which paradoxically attenuates with cumulation, is consistent with the stress riser model, where intense initial damage is applied unevenly at the boundaries between collapsed and aerated tissue. Once this initial damage has occurred, higher pressures applied might be helpful due to full recruitability of the collapsed segments (28). Attenuation of cumulative risk does not suggest lower compliant lungs are safer—it points to a temporal injury profile, one likely dominated by early atelectrauma and confounded by non-survivor depletion. In contrast, the amplifying, time-dependent risk in higher-compliance lungs aligns with the progressive loading model, where fatigue damage accumulates over thousands of cycles (28). We demonstrate that the propensity to injure might not simply be a function of total energy or the primary stretching force alone, but a dynamic interaction where the lung’s mechanical state dictates which physical injury pathway dominates.

Previous analyses have shown that mean MP normalized to respiratory system compliance strongly predicts mortality (29). Extending this concept, we tried to capture both the intensity and temporal dynamics in compliance-stratified framework with RAMP score. It integrates per-threshold log-hazard curves over time and compliance strata into a single log-hazard–scale score: it ranked among the top 20 predictors in XGBoost and added incremental discrimination beyond conventional covariates, although all variables were largely overshadowed by the Pao2/Fio2 ratio by importance in the model. In direct comparison, XGBoost outperformed other models, suggesting underlying non-linear relationships between the predictors. We are not aware of machine learning models predicting ARDS progression, but models predicting occurrence and mortality in general have lower predictive power (30, 31).

Our study has several limitations. Foremost, as an observational analysis, it establishes associations rather than causality, thus high MP may act as a marker of underlying disease severity rather than the direct cause of injury. This study is retrospective, subject to selection bias, unmeasured confounding, and center-specific practice patterns, and we have not externally validated RAMP in an independent cohort. Although our ventilator data are higher resolution than prior work, gaps required imputation that may miss brief power spikes or introduce bias. The MP and compliance formulas rely on simplifying assumptions (constant resistance, linear compliance, no auto-PEEP) that may not hold in heterogeneous ARDS lungs, and even this high granular data are still insufficient for very fine tuning, for example, for patient-trigger breaths during volume-control ventilation. Due to lack of radiological data, full Berlin criteria could not be used to verify ARDS; Pao2/Fio2 may be influenced by non-pulmonary factors and should be interpreted with caution. 48-hour compliance strata and threshold choices–whereas physiologically motivated–are somewhat arbitrary and may not generalize to all patient subgroups or ventilator strategies. Finally, our Cox models assume a constant per-hour log-linear hazard increment, potentially oversimplifying the non-linear kinetics of injury and repair.

In sum, our high-resolution, compliance-stratified analysis demonstrates that VILI is governed by a dynamic interplay of MP magnitude, exposure duration, and baseline lung mechanics–rather than any single “safe” threshold. Immediate power spikes elevate risk even in compliant lungs, whereas cumulative exposure may follow divergent pathways of microelement fatigue vs stress-riser injury in different compliance states. Based on these data, a target of MP less than 10 J/min is prudent but understandably not always feasible; in high-compliance lung, when there is space for strategic adjustments, we should keep in mind that MP of 18 J/min applied for as short as 2 hours may represent an alert for accumulating lung damage. By integrating these effects into the RAMPS metric, we provide a unified, time-varying gauge that outperforms traditional models in predicting ARDS progression and mortality, that should be prospectively validated.
