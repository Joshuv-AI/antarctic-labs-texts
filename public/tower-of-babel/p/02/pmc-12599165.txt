Molecular features influencing clinical outcome of advanced HER2-positive gastric cancer receiving trastuzumab plus chemotherapy
Authors: Yu Lei, Danxi He, Jiqing Si, Junrong Yan, Minchi Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLess than half of the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive gastric cancer (GC) patients respond to trastuzumab plus chemotherapy, and the outcomes are unsatisfactory. Understanding the underlying mechanisms remains crucial for identifying patients who are more likely to benefit from treatment.Patients and methodsWe performed targeted DNA sequencing on paired pre-treatment and progressive tumour tissues from 22 HER2-positive advanced GC patients undergoing first-line treatment with trastuzumab and chemotherapy. Clinicopathological and genomic characteristics were assessed for the correlation with clinical outcomes.ResultsA performance status (PS) of 0–1 was associated with improved progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) than a PS of 2. Poorly differentiated tumours exhibited shorter PFS than moderate or moderate-poor ones. Pre-treatment amplification of MYC or TOP2A gene was association with increased PFS, and suggested a potential benefit for OS. Patients with higher tumour mutation burden (TMB) experienced significantly worse PFS, while higher chromosome instability (CIN) appeared to be correlated with longer PFS. Compared to non-responders, responders had a higher CIN but similar TMB and intratumoural heterogeneity (ITH). PS and MYC amplification emerged as independent factors related to PFS according to multivariate survival analysis. Additionally, after treatment, TMB significantly increased in non-responders, while CIN significantly decreased in responders.ConclusionsPre-treatment MYC amplification and PS were independently associated with clinical outcomes in HER2-positive advanced GC patients treated with first-line trastuzumab plus chemotherapy. Dynamic post-treatment changes in TMB and CIS provide valuable insights into the relationship between therapeutic response and distinct evolutionary trajectories.Keywords: Gastric cancer, HER2, trastuzumab, next-generation sequencing, molecular features

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks fifth globally in both incidence and mortality, and most patients are diagnosed in the advanced stage, resulting in a poor prognosis [1]. Chemotherapy is currently the standard treatment for unresectable or metastatic GC, but its effectiveness is quite limited. Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) is a transmembrane protein encoded by the ERBB2 gene and serves as the most critical target in GC treatment [2]. Approximately, 20% of newly diagnosed GCs are HER2-positive [3]. Trastuzumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting HER2, has been demonstrated in clinical researches and practice to enhance survival benefits when combined with chemotherapy [4–7]. However, approximately, 30–40% of patients with HER2-positive GC do not respond to trastuzumab plus chemotherapy, and the overall efficacy and clinical outcomes remain unsatisfactory. In particular, the relatively short progression-free survival (PFS) remains a prominent clinical challenge [3,4,6–8]. Therefore, exploring the potential clinicopathological and molecular markers to identify people who are more likely to benefit from treatment is still an important clinical issue for improving overall GC management.

Previous reports identified several potential key factors that may influence the response to trastuzumab and the clinical outcomes in HER2-positive GC: (1) adverse clinicopathological features, such as diffuse Lauren subtype, visceral metastasis and abnormal levels of haemoglobin, lactate dehydrogenase and C-reactive protein prior to treatment [8,9]; (2) HER2-related concerns, including intratumoural HER2 heterogeneity, loss of HER2 positivity, heterodimers and acquired ERBB2 mutations [10–15]; and (3) molecular mechanisms associated with specific gene alterations and genomic characteristics [8,16–18].

In terms of molecular aspects, Xu et al. [8] performed whole-exome sequencing (WES) on tumour tissue samples before and when progressive disease (PD) of trastuzumab combined with chemotherapy and found several response or PFS-related biomarkers, including pre-treatment chromosomal instability (CIN), NOTCH1 gene deletion and tumour mutation burden (TMB), as well as acquired ERBB4 and FAT1 mutations. Hino et al. [18] used targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) and found that, among patients with HER2-positive advanced esophagogastric and gastric cancer (AGC) treated with trastuzumab combined with chemotherapy, a higher pre-treatment HER2 copy number (CN) as well as the absence of co-amplification of other genes in the HER2 signalling pathway were associated with long PFS of >24 months. In contrast, the presence of co-amplification involving genes in the HER2 pathway other than ERBB2 significantly affected PFS. However, mutations in TP53, PTEN/PIK3CA and ERBB2, as well as amplifications of other genes such as CCNE1 and CCND1, were not significantly correlated with PFS. Janjigian et al. [16] conducted a prospective tumour-targeted NGS analysis in patients with metastatic esophagogastric cancer and found a strong correlation between NGS-quantified ERBB2 amplification levels and PFS during first-line trastuzumab treatment. Specifically, patients with ERBB2 amplification levels in the top quartile experienced significantly prolonged PFS. Patients with concurrent alterations in the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)–RAS–PI3K/AKT pathway genes showed significantly shortened PFS, suggesting a potential contribution to intrinsic trastuzumab resistance. Multivariable analysis revealed that ERBB2 levels and co-alterations in the PI3K pathway were independent factors associated with differences in PFS. However, alterations in cell cycle-related genes were not associated with response differences. Meanwhile, the study also explored mechanisms of acquired resistance and identified that the selection of non-amplified ERBB2 clone was a recurrent mechanism underlying the trastuzumab resistance in esophagogastric cancer, primarily manifested as loss or significant reduction of HER2 expression or ERBB2 amplification. Additionally, secondary alterations in ERBB2, such as deletion of ERBB2 exon 16, as well as concurrent mutations leading to activation of the RAS or PI3K pathways, such as activating mutations in KRAS and PIK3CA, were also found to potentially contribute to both intrinsic and acquired resistance to trastuzumab in these patients. Complementary evidence supporting the role of PI3K pathway and ERBB family alterations in resistance comes from a study by Wang et al. [17], which utilized targeted DNA sequencing of plasma-derived circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA). Specifically, they found that pre-treatment detection of PIK3CA/PIK3R1/PIK3C3 and ERBB2/ERBB4 mutations was associated with worse PFS. In recent years, TMB, CIN score (CIS) and intratumour heterogeneity (ITH) have emerged as important biomarkers for predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy across various cancers. Elevated CIS and ITH correlated with poorer PFS or reduced response [19–23], whereas higher TMB was associated with better outcomes [24–26]. However, the clinical significance of TMB, CIS and ITH before and after trastuzumab-based chemotherapy in HER2-positive GC remains unclear due to limited available data, warranting further investigation. Additionally, ctDNA-based liquid biopsy is currently the most mainstream method for dynamically monitoring resistance to therapy in clinical practice [17,27–29]. However, compared to tumour tissues, ctDNA has lower sensitivity in detecting gene alterations, particularly in identifying gene CNV, which are more likely to yield false negatives. Therefore, tissue biopsy remains the more reliable method for investigating tumour genomic characteristics. In summary, relatively high-depth targeted DNA sequencing based on tumour biopsies remains crucial and valuable for exploring biomarkers related to treatment.

In this study, we collected tumour tissue samples before treatment with trastuzumab combined with chemotherapy and at the time of PD from 22 advanced GC patients with HER2-positive, as determined by immunohistochemistry (IHC) or fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH). Targeted NGS covering 425 cancer-related genes was performed. In addition, we gathered pre-treatment clinicopathological information and comprehensively analysed the clinicopathological and molecular features associated with radiographic response, PFS and overall survival (OS).

Method

Patient enrolment

This study included 22 histologically confirmed GC patients aged 18 years or older, who were diagnosed as HER2-positive through IHC or FISH, and received first-line treatment with trastuzumab in combination with chemotherapy between December 2021 and November 2024. Trastuzumab was administered as an intravenous loading dose of 8 mg/kg on day 1 of cycle 1, followed by 6 mg/kg intravenously every 21 days. The main chemotherapy regimens consisted of the SOX regimen (S-1 plus oxaliplatin) in 14 patients and the XELOX regimen (capecitabine plus oxaliplatin) in eight patients. In the SOX regimen, S-1 was given orally twice daily. The total dose was 80 mg/day for body surface area (BSA) <1.25 m2, 100 mg/day for BSA ≥1.25 to <1.5 m2, and 120 mg/day for BSA ≥1.5 m2. Oxaliplatin was administered at a dose of 130 mg/m2 as an intravenous infusion over 2 h on day 1 of each cycle. In the XELOX regimen, capecitabine was given orally at 1000 mg/m2 twice daily on days 1–14 of a three-week cycle. Oxaliplatin was administered in the same way as the SOX regimen.

Several important clinicopathological information of recruited patients, including age, sex, tumour location, differentiation, Lauren classification, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS), sites of metastasis was collected. PFS was measured from the start of treatment until PD or death, whichever occurred first. OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death. Patients who were still alive were censored at the last follow-up. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The First People’s Hospital of Jiashan (Approval No. 2025-003), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). Written informed consent to participate in the study was obtained from all participants.

Sample collection and DNA sequencing

Tumour tissue before treatment and at PD of each patient was collected and then prepared into formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks. WBCs were also collected from all patients and used as the normal control to distinguish germline variations. All samples were sent to a centralized testing centre of Nanjing Geneseeq Technology Inc. (Nanjing, China) for targeted NGS. Sample processing, library construction and targeted-sequencing were conducted as previously described [30]. Briefly, genomic DNA from the WBCs was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Genomic DNA of tissues was extracted using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and sheared into fragments approximately 300–350 bp using a Covaris M220 instrument. Then, the DNA underwent end repair, A-tailing and adaptor ligation, followed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and purified. Customized xGen lockdown probes (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA) were employed for hybridization enrichment targeting 425 cancer-relevant genes (Geneseeq, Nanjing, China). The capture reaction was performed using Dynabeads M-270 (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) and the xGen Lockdown Hybridization and Wash kit (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA). Captured libraries were PCR-amplified with KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA) and the purified library was quantified using the KAPA Library Quantification kit (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA). Finally, the enriched libraries were sequenced on HiSeq 4000 NGS platforms (Illumina, San Diego, CA) to achieve targeted mean coverage depths of at least ×1000.

Bioinformatics analysis

Bioinformatics analyses were conducted following established protocols from previous studies [30]. In summary, sequencing data were demultiplexed using bcl2fastq (v2.19) and filtered with Trimmomatic [31]. The filtered data were then aligned to the hg19 reference human genome using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (bwa-mem) [32] and further processed with the Picard toolkit and the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) [33]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions or deletions (indels) were identified using VarScan2 [34] and the HaplotypeCaller/UnifiedGenotyper in GATK, applying a mutant allele frequency (MAF) cutoff of 0.5%, followed by filtering with dbSNP and the 1000 Genomes Project. Germline mutations were excluded by comparing the results to the patient’s white blood cell controls. Gene fusions were detected with FACTERA, and copy number variations (CNVs) were analysed using ADTEx [35]. The cutoff for CN gain was set at a log2 ratio of 2.0, while a log2 ratio of ≤0.6 indicated CN loss. Allele-specific CNVs were evaluated with FACETS, employing a drift cutoff of 0.2 for unstable joint segments. The CIS was defined as the proportion of segments exhibiting CN alterations (log2 depth ratio >0.2 or < −0.2). Tumour mutational burden (TMB) was calculated as the total number of somatic base substitutions [36]. CIS or TMB values at or above the median were classified as high CIS (CIS-H) or high TMB (TMB-H), while those below the median were classified as low CIS (CIS-L) or low TMB (TMB-L). Intratumour heterogeneity was determined as the proportion of subclonal mutations relative to all mutations, as previously detailed [37]. Cancer-related pathways were selected based on another prior study [38].

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as median (range) or number of patients (percentage). Comparisons between groups were analysed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Fisher’s exact test. Survival analysis was conducted with Kaplan–Meier’s curves, and the p value was determined with the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated by the Cox proportional hazards model. Univariable or multivariate Cox regression was employed to explore the associations between various variables and PFS or OS, with results presented as HRs and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided p value of <0.05 was considered significant for all tests. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics® version 25 (Armonk, NY).

Results

Patient characteristics and treatment efficacy

A total of 22 advanced GC or gastroesophageal junction cancer (GEJC) patients with HER2-positive who received first-line trastuzumab plus chemotherapy were included in the study. Their baseline clinicopathological characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 63 years (range: 22–76), with eight individuals (36.4%) aged 65 or older. 77.3% (17/22) were male and 81.8% (18/22) of the tumours were located in the stomach body, while the rest were at the gastroesophageal junction. Of the tumours, 36.4% (8/22) were poorly differentiated and 64.6% (14/22) were moderately or moderately poorly differentiated. Among them, 81.8% (18) had the intestinal type and 18.2% (4/22) presented with the diffuse type, according to the Lauren classification. Most of the patients (81.8%) had a PS of 0–1, while four had a PS of 2. The most common metastatic organs were the lymph nodes (63.6%, 14/22) and liver (63.6%, 14/22), while eight (36.4%) involved the peritoneum. Of all patients, 13 (59.1%) exhibited IHC3+, while nine (40.9%) displayed IHC2+ and were confirmed as FISH positive.

Correlations between pre-treatment genomic features and treatment response and outcomes

All of the tumour tissues before treatment and at PD were successfully underwent NGS test. The mutational landscapes of pre-treatment are shown in Figure 1(C), and TP53 gene exhibited the highest frequency of somatic mutations, altering in 17 (77.3%) cases. Other somatic mutations identified in at least three patients included the PKHD1 and PREX2 genes. For CNV, ERBB2 amplification is the most common, observed in 86.4% (19/22) of cases. In addition, amplification of CCNE1, MYC and TOP2A genes was noted in ≥3 cases each. Comparative genomic profiling of paired pre-treatment and PD samples identified recurrent genomic alterations, encompassing both lost and acquired variants (Figure 1(D,E)). Genomic analysis revealed recurrent mutational losses (observed in ≥2 cases), with PKHD1 (13.6%), PREX2 (13.6%) and TP53 (13.6%) emerging as the most frequently lost genes. CNV profiling identified amplification of ERBB2 (27.3%), CCNE1 (18.2%) and MYC (18.2%) as the predominant genes exhibiting loss events. Acquired mutations predominantly involved TP53 (18.2%). ERBB2 amplification represented the most frequent acquired copy-number alteration, observed in three patients (27.3%). Notably, these three patients had wild-type (WT) ERBB2 in pre-treatment samples, whose CNs were below the defined amplification threshold. Then, the correlations between response, PFS or OS and the pre-treatment somatic mutations or CNV observed in at least three cases were analysed. For somatic mutations, no genes showed significant correlations in overall response, PFS or OS (data not shown). Regarding CNV, cases with MYC amplification had prolonged PFS (mPFS: 14.3 vs. 6.0 months, p = 0.018), and suggested a potential benefit for OS (mOS: 38.3 vs. 20.3 months, p = 0.105) (Figure 2(A,B) and Table S1). Similarly, patients with TOP2A amplification also indicated better PFS (mPFS: 14.3 vs. 6.0 months, p = 0.036) compared to those with TOP2A WT, and showed a trend towards improved OS (mPFS: not reached (NR) vs. 20.3 months, p = 0.094) (Figure 2(C,D) and Table S1). However, amplification of MYC or TOP2A was not significantly correlated with overall response (Figure S1A,B). Due to the lack of a significant correlation between ERBB2 gene amplification, as determined by NGS, and PFS or OS when the CN threshold was set at 4 (Table S1), we further investigated this relationship by applying alternative CN thresholds. The analysis demonstrated that setting the threshold at a CN of ≥10, compared to <10, resulted in the lowest HRs for both PFS and OS, with the most statistically significant p values observed (Table S2). Subsequently, we compared the CN of ERBB2 between the responders and non-responders and found that, although numerically higher in the responders, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.198, Figure 3(A)). Similarly, the relationship between alterations of cancer-related pathway and responses, PFS, or OS in altered or WT pathways identified in at least three individuals. No pathway was associated with response (data not shown), PFS or OS (Table S3). Patients with alterations in the MYC pathway seemed had better PFS (mPFS: 12.5 vs. 6.5 months, p = 0.054), while those with alterations in the WNT pathway tended to have shorter OS (mOS: 9.7 vs. 38.3 months, p = 0.098; Figure S2 and Table S3).

Furthermore, the relationships between pre-treatment TMB, CIS or ITH and treatment efficacy were assessed. TMB and ITH did not showed associations with the response (Figure S1C–F). In contrast, CIS was significantly higher in responders compared to non-responders (Figure 3(B,C)). However, cases with CIS-H only demonstrated a trend towards improved PFS (mPFS: 10.6 vs. 5.2 months, p = 0.115), and no significant difference in OS was found between individuals with CIS-H and CIS-L (p = 0.324) (Figure 3(D,E) and Table S1). Moreover, patients with TMB-L experienced longer PFS (mPFS: 10.6 vs. 6.2 months, p = 0.033), and showed a trend towards OS benefit (mOS: 38.3 vs. 20.3 months, p = 0.184) (Figure 3(F,G) and Table S1). However, no difference in PFS or OS was observed between patients with ITH-L and ITH-H (Table S1).

Associations between baseline clinicopathological features and treatment efficacy

The correlation between clinicopathological features and treatment outcomes was analysed, including age, sex, tumour location, PS, tumour differentiation and Lauren classification, as well as lymph node metastasis, liver metastasis, peritoneum metastasis or distant metastasis events. No significant differences in these characteristics were observed between responders and non-responders (data not shown). Patients with a PS of two exhibited worse PFS (1.4 vs. 8.6 months, p = 5.18E − 04) and OS (4.9 vs. 38.3 months, p = 2.54E − 05) compared to those with a PS of 0–1 (Figure 4(A,B) and Table S1). Poor tumour differentiation was significantly associated with shorter PFS (mPFS: 2.1 vs. 9.4 months, p = 0.020) but not OS (p = 0.545) when compared to moderate or moderate-poor ones (Figure 4(C,D) and Table S1). Other factors had no significant correlation with PFS or OS (Table S1). Additionally, in the bivariate analysis, both tumour differentiation and PS were identified as significant independent factors influencing PFS (Table S4).

Acquired genomic alterations and changes in molecular characteristics post-treatment

At PD, the most frequently mutated gene remained TP53, found in 18 cases (81.8%). Other somatic mutations observed in at least three patients included AMER1, APC, ERBB2, FAT1, GNAS and LRP1B genes. For CNV, only two genes were identified in three or more patients: ERBB2 and CCNE1 (Figure S3). Compared to the pre-treatment mutation landscape, 20 patients had acquired mutations and the number of acquired mutations for each patient is shown in Figure 5(A). The average number of acquired mutations was 3.9 (range: 0–11). The gene with the highest frequency among the acquired mutations was TP53, while other genes were observed in two or fewer patients (Figure 5(B)). Considering the significant heterogeneity of acquired mutations, we examined all the genes harboured acquired mutation in relation to cancer-related pathways to determine their associated signalling pathways. The RTK–RAS pathway includes the most patients (31.8%), followed by the PI3K pathway (22.7%). Other notable pathways with relatively high frequencies are TP53 (18.2%) and WNT (18.2%) (Figure 5(C)). Furthermore, we investigated the associations between recurrent acquired mutations and signalling pathways (both defined as occurring in ≥3 cases), as well as the number of acquired mutations (≥median vs. <median), and PFS or OS, but no significant correlations were observed (Table S5).

Furthermore, our previous univariate analysis indicated that the clinical factors of PS and tumour differentiation were significantly associated with PFS or OS. To further investigate the relationship between acquired genomic features, clinical characteristics and prognosis, we conducted bivariate analyses of PS or tumour differentiation with acquired TP53 mutations, number of acquired alterations, and acquired pathway alterations for both PFS and OS. As shown in Table S6, PS and tumour differentiation remained significantly associated with PFS. In contrast, most acquired genomic features were not significantly correlated with PFS, except for the number of acquired alterations, which demonstrated borderline significance (p = 0.050). For OS, neither clinical variables nor genomic features showed significant associations.

Additionally, we analysed the changes in the CN of ERBB2 gene, CIS, TMB and ITH before treatment and at PD. ERBB2 CN did not differ significantly between pre-treatment and progression samples in either responders (p = 0.153) or non-responders (p = 0.638) (Figure 5(D)). Most responders exhibited a decrease at progression; however, one outlier showed a marked increase (13.2–65.6), which masked the overall reduction. Exclusion of this outlier revealed a significant post-treatment decrease in responders (p = 0.024, data not shown). Among non-responders, four patients showed an increase and seven a decrease in CN at progression, though the reductions were less pronounced than those in responders (Figure 5(D)). Notably, TMB was significantly increased after treatment in non-responders (p = 0.037; Figure 5(E)), while no obvious changes were noted in responders (p = 0.575; Figure 5(E)). However, CIS significantly decreased in responders (p = 0.010; Figure 5(F)) but not in non-responders responders (p = 0.534; Figure 5(F)). Regarding IHT, no significant changes were noted in either responders or non-responders (p > 0.05; Figure 5(G)).

Discussion

In this study, we used a customized panel of 437 cancer-related genes to perform high-throughput sequencing on paired tissue biopsy samples before treatment and at the time of progression from patients with HER2-positive advanced GC who were treated with trastuzumab plus chemotherapy as first-line therapy. At baseline, MYC amplification, TOP2A amplification and TMB-L were associated with significantly longer mPFS and showed a trend towards improved mOS. Additionally, CIS was significantly higher in responders than in non-responders and appeared to correlate with prolonged PFS. Upon disease progression, non-responders experienced a significant increase in TMB, whereas no significant change was noted in responders, compared to baseline. Conversely, CIS significantly decreased in responders, with non-responders showing no significant change in CIS levels. Although ERBB2 gene did not show statistically significant changes in CN from baseline to progression in either group, most responders demonstrated a greater reduction in CN compared to non-responders.

Among the baseline genomic biomarkers associated with treatment response and survival outcomes, MYC gene amplification remained a significant association with PFS after multivariate analysis. Patients with MYC amplification experienced a PFS more than double that of patients with MYC WT. MYC, located on chromosome 8, is a pleiotropic transcription factor and a crucial regulator of cell growth, proliferation, metabolism, differentiation and apoptosis [39,40]. Amplification of MYC gene is a relatively common genetic event in GC, reported in over 15% of cases [41,42]. In this study, MYC amplification was observed in 22.7% (5/22) of patients. In breast cancer, MYC amplification has been reported to be potential predictors of response to HER2-targeted therapies [43,44]. However, there is no evidence to date that MYC amplification is associated with the efficacy of trastuzumab treatment in HER2-positive patients with GC. We hypothesize that the potential mechanisms by which MYC amplification enhances PFS in first-line treatment of advanced GC with trastuzumab combined with chemotherapy may include the following: (1) signalling pathway addiction: HER2 activation primarily promotes proliferation/survival via PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK/ERK pathways. c-MYC is a key downstream effector. Tumours with high c-MYC expression may exhibit stronger dependence (‘addiction’) on these pathways, rendering them more sensitive to HER2 blockade by trastuzumab, leading to enhanced response [45,46]; (2) enhanced chemosensitivity: the c-Myc protein promotes G1/S transition, accelerating cell cycle progression. Many commonly used chemotherapeutics are most effective against highly proliferative cells [47,48]. MYC-amplified tumours exhibit heightened cell cycle activity, potentially increasing sensitivity to chemotherapy; this effect may be further augmented when combined with trastuzumab. Of course, considering that protein overexpression may be independent of gene amplification, further researches are needed to explore the relationship between c-MYC protein overexpression and the efficacy of trastuzumab treatment in HER2-positive GC patients. This will help clarify the specific role of the MYC gene in such context.

TMB-H was associated with shorter PFS, whereas higher CIS suggested a trend for better PFS, though it did not reach statistical significance. TMB-H tended to be shorter OS, while there was almost no correlation between CIS and OS. Additionally, ITH was not correlated with either PFS or OS. In terms of response, TMB and ITH did not significantly impact patients’ response to therapy. However, CIS was strongly associated with response, with responders exhibiting significantly higher CIS than non-responders, which was consistent with previous reports from WES analysis [8]. The mechanism underlying the association between higher CIS and improved response remains unclear. Thompson et al. [49] proposed that CIN signatures may function as predictive biomarkers for sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapies and taxanes, potentially through encoding accumulated DNA damage from impaired DNA repair pathways. While TMB is recognized as a positive predictor for immunotherapy efficacy or prognosis, its role in advanced GC treated with chemotherapy ± trastuzumab remains unclear. Duan et al. [50] reported that among 40 GC patients receiving first-line chemotherapy, those with TMB-H had shorter mOS and PFS compared to TMB-L patients, aligning with our results. They proposed that DNA damage response and repair systems, informed by experiences in lung cancer treatment, might be key to this effect. Similarly, Wang et al. [51] found that in resectable GC, patients with TMB-L receiving postoperative chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy had better OS and disease-free survival (DFS), mirroring our observations in advanced patients. They suggested that poor response to adjuvant therapy in TMB-H patients might relate to TMB-associated NK cell infiltration and a hypoxic microenvironment. Conversely, in a smaller study, Kim et al. [52] observed significantly superior median PFS in TMB-H patients. Despite conflicting results across studies, multivariate analysis in our cohort indicated that TMB was not an independent factor associated with PFS, although a statistical trend existed. Indeed, the impact of immunotherapy-related genomic subtypes like TMB and deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) on chemotherapy ± trastuzumab in GC remains ambiguous, with a study suggesting dMMR may shorten PFS in first-line chemotherapy for advanced GC [53]. Therefore, further investigations are warranted to elucidate how TMB or dMMR influences PFS in HER2-positive advanced GC patients treated with first-line trastuzumab-based combination chemotherapy. ITH has been shown to be associated with the effectiveness of immunotherapy in various cancers [19,20, 54,55]. Generally, higher ITH correlates with worse efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors, and vice versa. However, no reports have noted the impact of ITH on the effectiveness of trastuzumab in HER2-positive GC or breast cancers, which is consistent with our findings that no significant correlation between ITH and treatment response, PFS or OS was observed.

In addition to pre-treatment clinicopathological and molecular characteristics, we explored acquired genetic abnormalities following trastuzumab treatment and the changes in genomic features before and after treatment. The genetic variants in pre-treatment samples showed significant heterogeneity, and this trend continued with acquired variants at PD. Acquired TP53 mutations were present in four patients, while mutations in other genes were found in two or fewer patients. From the perspective of cancer-related pathways, acquired mutations primarily occurred in RTK–RAS and PI3K pathways, with the genes in these pathways located downstream or in bypass of ERBB2. Compared to pre-treatment samples, the number of mutations in non-responders significantly increased at PD, whereas changes in responders were less evident. Most non-responders (72.7%) saw an increase in TMB, although a few (18.2%) experienced a decrease. Although some responders (45.5%) also showed an increase in TMB, more than half of the patients (54.5%) still had a decrease or no changes in TMB. This finding suggested that mutations in tumour cells were more likely to increase in patients who did not respond to trastuzumab, but were more likely to decrease or remain unchanged in responders. Potential drivers of increased post-treatment TMB may include: (1) unrepaired chemotherapy-induced DNA damage generating new somatic mutations [56,57]; (2) selection pressure promoting the survival and expansion of resistant clones with higher mutation burdens [58,59]; (3) secondary HER2 pathway alterations (mutations/amplifications) under treatment pressure, enhancing trastuzumab resistance, stimulating proliferation/DNA replication stress (increasing mutation load) and potentially compromising DNA repair to exacerbate genomic instability [60]. Ultimately, failure to respond allows mutation accumulation reflected in elevated TMB, whereas therapeutic success reduces tumour burden, potentially masking TMB changes in post-treatment samples. Conversely, the CIS of responders significantly decreased at PD, whereas this was not observed in non-responders. We speculate that the underlying mechanisms may include: (1) clonal purification under therapeutic selection pressure, where chemotherapy and trastuzumab preferentially eliminate tumour cells with a high CIN phenotype [58,61]. These cells, which exhibit marked chromosomal numerical or structural abnormalities, are more vulnerable to replication stress induced by treatment. As a result, high-CIN subclones are selectively cleared from residual tumour tissue, leading to an overall reduction in the CIS. (2) Restoration of cell cycle regulation, as the inhibition of the HER2 pathway by trastuzumab may normalize the expression of cell cycle proteins, thus decreasing chromosomal aberrations associated with excessive proliferation [62]. (3) Modulation of the immune microenvironment, whereby in responsive patients, the immune system may selectively clear ‘immunogenic’ tumour cells with CIS-H, further reducing the survival of genomically unstable subclones and suppressing CIS progression [63,64]. Furthermore, in this study, responders exhibited substantially higher baseline CIS compared to non-responders, and the lack of significant CIS difference between groups at PD, resulting in a larger relative CIS decrease in responders.

The study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the small sample size of HER2-positive GC patients may impact the generalizability and reliability of the findings. Second, as a single-centre study, the potential heterogeneity of the population may limit the applicability of our results to other research contexts. Third, this study only conducted genomic analysis at the DNA level and did not explore other levels. Future researches should incorporate more comprehensive multi-omics analyses to investigate the mechanisms related to the efficacy.

In summary, this study identified key molecular and clinicopathological features associated with treatment response and survival in HER2-positive advanced GC patients receiving first-line trastuzumab plus chemotherapy. Specifically, pre-treatment MYC amplification and PS were identified as independent predictors of clinical outcomes. Furthermore, the divergent dynamic changes in TMB and CIS following treatment provide valuable insights into distinct evolutionary trajectories related to therapeutic response and acquired resistance. These findings enhance our understanding of the genomic mechanisms underlying trastuzumab efficacy and may inform future strategies for patient stratification and combination therapy in HER2-positive GC.
