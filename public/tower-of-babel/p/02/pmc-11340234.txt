Perinatal intimate partner violence among teen mothers and children’s attachment disorders in Rwanda: potential mediating factors
Authors: Martin Nshimyumukiza, Japhet Niyonsenga, Clementine Kanazayire, Simeon Gitimbwa Sebatukura, Jean Mutabaruka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: The alarming prevalence of teen mothers’ exposure to perinatal intimate partner violence (IPV, 8.3-67%) and attachment disorders (ADs) among their children is a global concern, especially in sub-Saharan Africa with high teenage pregnancy rates. This study, therefore, aimed to examine the link between teen IPV and AD in their offspring. We sought also to explore the mediating roles of postpartum depression symptoms, maternal sensitivity, parenting stress, and perceived social support in the relationship between perinatal IPV and children’s ADs.Method: This cross-sectional study selected a random sample of 309 teen mothers from Nyanza district. This sample size was determined using Yamane's formula, with random sampling. Various instruments were used for data collection, including questionnaires on intimate partner violence, social support, maternal sensitivity, postpartum depression symptoms and parenting stress and early trauma-related disorders. The data was analysed using SPSS, with mediation analyses performed using the PROCESS macro (version 4.1).Results: IPV was found to be significantly associated with attachment disorders. Simple mediation models showed that parenting stress completely mediated these relationships, while postpartum depression, perceived social support, and maternal sensitivity partially mediated the relationship between IPV and children’s ADs. In parallel mediation model, the combined roles of all mediators fully mediated the associations between IPV and ADs.Conclusion: These findings offer valuable insights in designing or strengthening the appropriate interventions to prevent and mitigate the perinatal intimate partner violence and its detrimental impact on children’s attachment disorders. Combating intimate partner violence in post-conflict situations is challenging in teen mothers, however, our results suggest that efforts to address maternal mental health and parenting practices may protect children from attachment disorders.KEYWORDS: Intimate partner violence, postpartum depression, maternal responsiveness, adolescent mothers, attachment disorders

Background

Exposure to intimate partner violence during pregnancy and the postpartum period poses significant challenges for adolescent mothers. IPV during these critical periods is linked to adverse maternal mental health outcomes, including postpartum depression symptoms (Adamu & Adinew, 2018; Ankerstjerne et al., 2022; Desta et al., 2021; Gebrekristos et al., 2023; Malta et al., 2012; Velonis et al., 2017), low maternal sensitivity (Coe et al., 2021; Gustafsson et al., 2015), and increased parenting stress (Malta et al., 2012; Neece et al., 2012a; Renner, 2009). These maternal mental health issues can severely impact infant-mother attachment, leading to dysfunctional parent-infant interactions and subsequent socio-emotional challenges in children (Davies et al., 2023; Gustafsson et al., 2015; Gustafsson & Cox, 2012a; Howell et al., 2014). Additionally, IPV has been associated with increased risk of developmental delays and behavioural problems in children (Bornstein & Putnick, 2012; Dahmen et al., 2013; Goossens et al., 2015; Hou, 2020; McPeak et al., 2015; Scherer et al., 2019), as well as long-term mental health issues such as anxiety and depression (Carlson et al., 2019; Kitzmann et al., 2003; Slomian et al., 2019; Uwitonze et al., 2020).

Less sensitive maternal behaviour due to IPV exposure leads to dysfunctional parent-infant interactions and increased risk of attachment disorders in children. Maternal sensitivity is crucial for developing a secure attachment bond, which in turn is linked to positive social-emotional and cognitive development in children (Bornstein & Putnick, 2012; Scherer et al., 2019). Exposure to IPV undermines maternal sensitivity, often resulting in harsh and intrusive parenting practices that negatively impact children’s socio-emotional challenges (Gustafsson et al., 2015; Gustafsson & Cox, 2012a). Research has shown that maternal sensitivity mediates the relationship between IPV and child outcomes, highlighting the importance of supportive interventions (Davies et al., 2023; Levendosky et al., 2012).

Higher perceived social support can buffer the negative effects of IPV on maternal mental health and parenting practices, thereby reducing the risk of attachment disorders. Social support provides emotional, informational, and practical assistance, which can alleviate stress and promote positive parenting behaviours (Cohen & Wills, 1985; Feeney & Collins, 2015; Ozbay et al., 2007; Thoits, 2011). In the context of IPV, perceived social support helps mothers cope with stress, improves their mental health, and enhances their ability to engage in sensitive and responsive parenting (Levendosky et al., 2004; Stanton-Salazar, 2011). Studies have found that social support mediates the effects of IPV on maternal and child health outcomes, emphasizing its protective role (Carlson et al., 2002; Owen et al., 2008; Schultz et al., 2021).

IPV exposure increases the risk of postpartum depression, which in turn negatively affects mother–child bonding and increases the likelihood of attachment disorders. Postpartum depression disrupts the mother’s emotional availability and responsiveness, which are critical for healthy attachment (Field, 2010; Letourneau et al., 2012; Santona et al., 2015). The association between IPV and postpartum depression is well-documented (Ankerstjerne et al., 2022; Desta et al., 2021; Malta et al., 2012; Velonis et al., 2017), and depression serves as a significant pathway through which IPV affects child development outcomes (Howard et al., 2013; Howell et al., 2017). Recent meta-analyses have suggested that postpartum depression mediates the impact of IPV on adverse child outcomes, reinforcing the need for mental health support for affected mothers (Alvarez-Segura et al., 2014; Norhayati et al., 2015).

High levels of parenting stress resulting from IPV exacerbate the difficulties in maintaining a healthy parent–child relationship, leading to attachment disorders. Parenting stress can lead to negative parenting behaviours such as irritability, hostility, and inconsistency, which negatively affect child development (Crnic & Low, 2002; Deater-Deckard, 1998). IPV significantly contributes to parenting stress, as the ongoing threat and psychological burden of violence disrupt the mother’s capacity to provide stable and nurturing care (Gustafsson & Cox, 2012b; Rodriguez & Green, 1997). Research indicates that parenting stress mediates the relationship between IPV and child behavioural problems, underscoring the importance of addressing stress in interventions (Neece et al., 2012b; Owen et al., 2006).

While several studies have reported elevated prevalence of IPV among teen mothers, research associating this violence with children’s attachment disorders (ADs) is scarce. Furthermore, no studies have explored the mediating factors of these associations. This study addresses this gap by examining the relationship between exposure to IPV and ADs, and the potential mediating factors in this relationship among Rwandan teen mothers. We hypothesize that maternal sensitivity, perceived social support, postpartum depression, and parenting stress mediate the relationship between exposure to IPV and children’s ADs. Specifically, we proposed that reduced maternal sensitivity due to IPV leads to dysfunctional parent-infant interactions and increased attachment disorders, while higher perceived social support can mitigate the negative effects of IPV on maternal mental health and parenting practices, reducing attachment disorder risk. Furthermore, we hypothesize that IPV exposure heightens the risk of postpartum depression, adversely affecting mother–child bonding and increasing attachment disorders, and that elevated parenting stress resulting from IPV complicates maintaining healthy parent–child relationships, further contributing to attachment disorders.

The alarming prevalence of teen mothers’ exposure to perinatal IPV (8.3–78%), (Adjimi Nyemgah et al., 2024; Stiller et al., 2022), and associated ADs among their children is a global concern. According to the WHO (2024), adolescents aged 15 to 19 years in low- and middle-income countries experience approximately 21 million pregnancies each year, resulting in nearly 12 million births. Sub-Saharan African countries, including Rwanda, exhibit high rates of early childbearing (Human Right Watch, 2018; Kassa et al., 2018; Ruzibiza, 2021; Yakubu & Salisu, 2018). In Rwanda, unexpected pregnancies increased from 17,100 in 2016–19,200 in 2018, exacerbating challenges such as education discontinuation (Niyonsenga & Mutabaruka, 2021). Given the severe consequences of IPV for mothers and children, this study is crucial for understanding the mechanisms linking IPV to attachment disorders. This research innovatively examines multiple mediating factors, including maternal sensitivity, social support, postpartum depression, and parenting stress, which have not been collectively studied in this context before. By identifying these mechanisms, the study contributes to developing targeted interventions to mitigate the impacts of IPV, informing both clinical practice and policymaking to support adolescent mothers and their children.

Methodology

Study design

This cross-sectional study design aimed to examine the mediating factors of the association between teen mothers’ exposure to IPV and their children’s attachment disorders.

Population and sample

The study population comprised beneficiaries of Association Francois Xavier Bagnoud (FXB) Rwanda’s ‘DREAMS’ (Determined, Resilient, Empowered, AIDS-free, Mentored and Safe), targeting teen mothers in Nyanza district (N = 11,314). where n = sample size; N = total population, N = 962; e = marginal error, e = 0.05. This formula yielded a sample size of 282 teen mothers. Assuming a non-response rate of 10%, the final sample size was adjusted upward to 309. Simple random sampling was applied, using an Excel-generated list of eligible teen mothers from FXB Rwanda. Inclusion criteria were: teen mothers aged 14–19 years, having a child aged from 9 months to 5 years for attachment disorders concerns, living with a child in the same household at Nyanza for possible interactions, unmarried at the time of study, and the ability to communicate effectively.

Data collection tools

Sociodemographic questionnaire

Participants completed a questionnaire covering caregiver characteristics (parental status, age during pregnancy, age during study, having a job, and educational level), and child characteristics (age and sex).

The WHO violence against women scale (VAW)

The 13-item WHO Violence Against Women Scale evaluate various types of violence women face, including physical, emotional, and sexual violence, as well as controlling behaviours and intimidation (WHO, 2005). Respondents rate the frequency or intensity of their encounters on a Likert scale from 0 (never) to 4 (very often). Higher scores indicate greater frequency or severity of violence. In this research sample, the Cronbach's alpha was 0.85.

Maternal infant responsiveness instrument (MIRI)

The MIRI is a 22-item self-report tool assessing maternal sensitivity to infant cues and perceptions of the infant responses (Drake et al., 2007). Items are rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with high scores indicating greater responsiveness. The MIRI does not have a specific cut-off point. The Cronbach's alpha for this study was 0.876.

The edinburgh postnatal depression scale (EPDS)

The EPDS, used to assess maternal depression at a year after childbirth. It contained a total of 10 items on 4 – point Likert scales (0–3), with total scores ranging from 0 to 30 points (Cox et al., 1987). A cut-off score of 13 or higher indicates probable depression. The Cronbach’s alpha for this study was 0.88.

Multidimensional perceived social support (MSPSS)

The MSPSS is a 12-item scale measuring support from family, friends, and significant other, rated rated on 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), (Zimet et al., 1988). Higher scores indicate greater perceived social support. While the MSPSS does not have a universally established cut-off score, a midpoint score (4 out of 7) is sometimes used to distinguish between low and high perceived social support. In this study, we did not use a specific cutoff point. The Cronbach's alpha for this study was 0.94.

Parenting stress index-short form (PSI-SF)

The PSI-SF contains 36 items divided into three subscales: Parental Distress (PD), Parent–Child Dysfunctional Interaction (PCDI) and Difficult Child (DC),(Abidin, 1990). Each subscale consists of 12 items, and higher scores indicate higher levels of parenting stress. Although there is no single cutoff score for the PSI-SF, scores above the 85th percentile are typically considered indicative of clinically significant levels of stress. In this study, we did not use a specific cutoff point. The Cronbach's alpha for the overall scale in this study was 0.93.

The early trauma-related disorders questionnaire (ETRADQ)

The 42-item ETRADQ, a caregiver report, assesses attachment problems caused by early trauma (Lehmann et al., 2020). It includes scales for Reactive Attachment Disorder (RAD) and Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder (DSED), based on DSM-5 criteria (APA, 2013). Items are scored on a 4-point Likert-type scale: 0 = not at all true, 1 = a little bit true, 2 = pretty much true and 3 = very much true. While the ETRADQ was initially validated for children aged 5–12 years (Monette et al., 2022), its design is theoretically applicable to younger children from 9 months of age. The ETRADQ items cover all RAD and DSED symptoms as defined in DSM-5, relevant for children beginning at 9 months old. According to DSM-5 criteria, the subscales include RAD: Low selective attachment (Criteria A1, A2), RAD: Low social and emotional responsiveness (Criteria B1, B2), RAD: Emotional unpredictability (Criterion B3), DSED: Interaction with unfamiliar adults (Criteria A1, A3, A4), and DSED: Social disinhibition (Criterion A2). Additionally, pilot testing was conducted to assess the tool's reliability and validity in children aged 9 months to 5 years. In this study, Cronbach's alpha was 0.89.

Procedure

The study was approved by the local Research Ethics Committee, Institutional Review Board of the College Medicine and Health Sciences at the University of Rwanda (Ref: CMHS/IRB/164/2023), and conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. Consents and assent forms were obtained from each participant. All questionnaires were adapted to the Rwandan context and translated and back-translated with two different teams of psychologists. Participants were asked if they still agreed to data usage after completing all questionnaires. Only teen mothers who consented to participate at the beginning of the study and agreed to data usage at the end of the survey were included in the analyses.

A one-week workshop, including a two-day piloting phase, was conducted with four clinical psychologists serving as data collectors. During this phase, 10% of the sample size was selected for piloting to test the reliability and validity of the Early Trauma-Related Disorders Questionnaire (ETRADQ) among teen mothers of children aged 9 months to 5 years. This piloting phase helped refine the data collection process and ensured the appropriateness of the instruments used in the study. Additionally, emotional support systems were provided by the clinical psychologists to offer psychological support to participants as needed during the data collection process. These systems were crucial in addressing any distress that may have arisen from discussing sensitive topics related to violence and trauma.

Data analyses

All data was double entered into a database and checked for accuracy. Analyses were performed using the SPSS (v. 29, Chicago, IL, USA) statistical packages. Descriptive statistics and distribution properties of the variables of interest were examined. The Pearson’s r coefficient was used to assess the zero-order correlation between variables. The PROCESS macro within SPSS version 29 (Hayes, 2018) was used for mediation analyses, specifically using model number 4, which tests for simple and parallel mediation analyses. Four simple mediation models and one parallel mediation model were conducted. To test for multicollinearity among variables of interest, preliminary correlational analyses were carried out in accordance with Baron and Kenny's (1986) statistical recommendations for determining mediation. This step ensures that multicollinearity does not bias the mediation results. Potential confounders, such as child age and maternal age, were controlled for in the analyses to isolate the effects of the primary variables of interest. The suggested mediational effects are regarded as exploratory because our data are cross-sectional, and these analyses do not show a causal link between variables.

Mediating factors of the relationship between intimate partner violence (IPV) and reactive attachment disorder (RAD)

Parenting stress, postpartum depression, perceived social support and maternal responsiveness were tested as potential mediators in the simple mediation model because correlational analyses had showed that scores on them were most consistently associated with measures of IPV and RAD (Figure 1a-d).

Simple mediating effects of maternal responsiveness in the relationship between IPV and RAD

Analysis was done on the mediating role that maternal responsiveness had in the association between IPV and RAD (Figure 1a). The results of the study demonstrated that IPV strongly predicted maternal responsiveness (path an estimate = −0.23, p < .0001, LL = −.36, UL = −.100) and maternal responsiveness predicted RAD (path b estimate = −.40, p < .001, LL = −.54, UL = −.247). By analysing the bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals, the indirect relationship of IPV with RAD through maternal responsiveness (path a × b estimate = 0.09) was different from zero (lower limit: 0.028; upper limit: 0.177). There was a statistically significant direct relationship between IPV and RAD (path c estimate = 0.470, p = .000) in presence of mediator. It is possible to conclude that maternal responsiveness acted as a partial mediating factor in the association between IPV and RAD since both the direct and indirect effects were statistically significant (Figure 1a).

Simple mediating effects of parenting stress in the relationship between IPV and RAD

Analysis was done on the mediating role that parenting stress played in the association between IPV and RAD (Figure 1b). According to this analysis, IPV significantly predicted parenting stress (path an estimate = 1.256, p < .0001, LL = .96, UL = 1.54) and parenting stress strongly predicted reactive attachment disorders (path b estimate = .335, p < .001, LL = .28, UL = .39). The indirect relationship of IPV with RAD through parental stress (path an x b estimate = 0.424, p < .0001) was shown to be different from zero (lower limit: 0.303; upper limit: 0.567) by examining the bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals. The direct relationship of IPV with RAD was not statistically significant (path c estimate = 0.136, p = .105). It may be inferred that parental stress fully mediated the association between IPV and RAD because the direct impact was not significant, but the indirect effect was (Figure 1b).

Simple mediating effects of post-partum depression in the relationship between IPV and RAD

An analysis was conducted on the mediating role of post-partum depression in the association between IPV and RAD (Figure 1ac). The results indicated that IPV strongly predicted post-partum depression (path a estimate = .38, p < .0001, LL = .305, UL = .45) and post-partum considerably predicted RAD (path b estimate = .55, p < .001, LL = .29, UL = .803). The indirect relationship of IPV with RAD through post-partum depression symptoms (path a × b estimate = 0.21) was shown to be different from zero (lower limit: 0.104; upper limit: 0.32) by examining the bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals. RAD were directly related to IPV in a statistically significant way (path c estimate = 0.354, p = .0005). Therefore, we may infer that post-partum depression partially mediated the association between IPV and RAD since both indirect and direct effects were statistically significant (Figure 1c).

Simple mediating effects of perceived social support in the relationship between IPV and RAD

An analysis was conducted to examine the mediating role of perceived social support (PSS) in the association between IPV and RAD (Figure 1d). Our findings showed that IPV significantly predicted perceived social support (path a estimate = −.83, p < .0001, LL = −104, UL = −.62) and PPS predicted RAD (path b estimate = −.27, p < .001, LL = −.36, UL = .−.186). The indirect association of IPV with RAD through PSS (path a × b estimate = 0.223) was found to be different from zero (lower limit: 0.13; upper limit: 0.33), according to an examination of the bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals. In presence of mediator, the direct relationship between IPV and RAD were statistically significant (path c estimate = 0.333, p = .0005). Because the indirect effect was significant while the direct effect was still significant, it can be concluded that the relationship between IP and RAD was partially mediated by PSS (Figure 1d).

Parallel mediating roles of parenting stress, post-partum depression, maternal sensitivity and perceived social support in the relationship between IPV and children's RAD

Inspired by single mediation models, we explored a parallel mediation model with all mediators in the relationship between IPV and children's RAD (Figure 2). The results indicated several important findings. First, the total effect of IPV on RAD is statistically significant (b = 0.5606, bootstrapped 95% CI [0.3842, 0.7371], β = 0.34, SE b = 0.0897, p < .001), suggesting that IPV has a direct positive relationship with RAD among children of adolescent mothers. However, when parenting stress, postpartum depression, maternal sensitivity, and perceived social support were added as mediators to the model, the direct path between IPV and RAD was not significant (b = 0.1700, bootstrapped 95% CI [−0.0022, 0.3423], SE b = 0.0875, β = 0.1019, p = .053).

Parallel mediating roles of parenting stress, post-partum depression, maternal sensitivity, perceived social support in the relationship between IPV and children's RAD.

An indirect path through parenting stress was significant (b = 0.426, bootstrapped 95% CI [0.27, 0.5964], SE b = 0.0823, β = 0.26, p < .001). However, an indirect relationship via postpartum depression was not significant (b = −0.12, bootstrapped 95% CI [−0.25, 0.0030], SE b = 0.0644, β = −0.0734, p = .08). Similarly, an indirect path via maternal sensitivity was not significant (b = 0.0127, bootstrapped 95% CI [−0.028, 0.067], SE b = 0.0239, β = 0.0076, p = .44). Additionally, an indirect path via perceived social support did not reach significance levels (b = 0.0739, bootstrapped 95% CI [−0.0146, 0.1689], SE b = 0.0467, β = 0.044, p = .067).

The total indirect effect was significant (b = 0.3906, bootstrapped 95% CI [0.2631, 0.5324], SE b = 0.0684, β = 0.2341, p < .001). These findings indicate that IPV significantly increases parenting stress and postpartum depression, decreases maternal sensitivity and perceived social support, with parenting stress being the most significant mediator in the relationship between IPV and RAD. The direct relationship of IPV on RAD is not significant, highlighting the importance of mediators in this relationship. This implies that IPV exerts an indirect influence on children's attachment disorders primarily through the mediation of parenting stress, while postpartum depression, maternal sensitivity, and perceived social support do not serve as significant mediators.

Simple mediating effects of maternal responsiveness in the relationship between intimate partner violence (IPV) and disinhibited social engagement disorders (DSED)

The mediating role of maternal responsiveness in the relationship between IPV and DSED was analysed (Figure 3). This analysis revealed that IPV significantly predicted maternal responsiveness (path a estimate = −0.23, p < .0001, LL = −.36, UL = −.100) and maternal responsiveness significantly predicted DSED (path b estimate = .18, p < .001, LL = .076, UL = −.29). By inspecting the bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals, the indirect relationship of IPV with DSED through maternal responsiveness (path a × b estimate = −.0414) was different from zero (lower limit: −.077; upper limit: −.011). The direct relationship of IPV and DSED was statistically significant (path c estimate = −.20, p = .000). Because the direct effect was significant and the indirect effect reached significance, it can be concluded that the relationship between IPV and DSED was partially mediated by maternal responsiveness (Figure 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to examine whether children’s attachment disorders (ADs) would be associated with intimate partner violence (IPV), postpartum depression symptoms, maternal responsiveness, parenting stress and social support among teen mothers in Rwanda. Our findings align with existing literature, showing that these variables significantly explain the variance in ADs among children (Adar et al., 2022; Dubber et al., 2015; Gilden et al., 2020; Huth-Bocks et al., 2004; Muzik et al., 2017; O’Dea et al., 2023; Pang & Thomas, 2020; Schröder et al., 2022). They also provide new insights into the complex interplay between these variables. We specifically explored both simple and parallel mediation models to understand the pathways through which IPV affects children's attachment outcomes.

Attachment theory, developed by John Bowlby, posits that secure and nurturing attachments between infants and caregivers are fundamental for healthy social and emotional development (Bowlby, 1984). Secure attachment provides a child with a safe base from which to explore the world and develop confidence in their interactions. Conversely, exposure to adverse conditions such as IPV can disrupt this secure base, leading to attachment disorders (Ainsworth & Bell, 1972; Bell, 1970). Our study corroborates this theory, demonstrating that IPV is significantly associated with reactive attachment disorders (RAD) and disinhibited social engagement disorders (DSED).

The direct association between IPV and attachment disorders found in our study is consistent with existing literature. IPV creates a chaotic and insecure environment that impairs the emotional availability of the caregiver, leading to attachment disruptions (Levendosky et al., 2012; Pang & Thomas, 2020). Children exposed to IPV may develop RAD, characterized by a lack of attachment to caregivers, or DSED, characterized by overly familiar behaviour with strangers (Zeanah & Gleason, 2015). These behaviours reflect a child's adaptation to an unpredictable and unsafe environment.

In our simple mediation analyses, maternal responsiveness emerged as a significant mediator in the relationship between IPV and both RAD and DSED. Sensitive and responsive parenting is critical for fostering secure attachments (Bowlby, 1979). Our findings suggest that IPV reduces maternal responsiveness, which in turn leads to attachment disorders. This aligns with previous studies indicating that maternal sensitivity can buffer the negative effects of environmental stressors on child development (Leerkes & Zhou, 2018; Manning, 2019). The significant indirect effects observed indicate that maternal responsiveness partially mediates the relationship between IPV and child attachment outcomes.

Similarly, parenting stress was found to be a significant mediator. High levels of parenting stress can lead to inconsistent caregiving and emotional unavailability, which are detrimental to secure attachment formation (Crnic & Low, 2002; Deater-Deckard, 1998; Huth-Bocks et al., 2004; Moss et al., 2011). IPV significantly predicted increased parenting stress, which in turn was associated with higher levels of RAD (Neece et al., 2012a; Owen et al., 2006). These results underscore the critical role of parenting stress in the transmission of the effects of IPV to child attachment disorders, underscoring the importance of addressing stress in interventions.

Postpartum depression also emerged as a significant mediator in our simple mediation models. Postpartum depression affects a mother's ability to be emotionally available and responsive, exacerbating attachment issues (Dubber et al., 2015; O’Higgins et al., 2013). IPV significantly predicted postpartum depression, which in turn was associated with increased RAD. This finding highlights the need for mental health support for mothers to mitigate the impact of IPV on child attachment.

Perceived social support was positively associated with better attachment outcomes and served as a significant mediator. Social support provides mothers with emotional and practical resources, reducing stress and enhancing their capacity to engage in sensitive and responsive parenting (Adar et al., 2022). This finding is consistent with the buffering hypothesis, which posits that social support mitigates the adverse effects of stress on health and well-being (Cohen & Wills, 1985).

Our parallel mediation model simultaneously included maternal responsiveness, parenting stress, postpartum depression, and perceived social support as mediators to understand the pathways through which IPV affects children's attachment outcomes. The total effect of IPV on RAD was statistically significant, indicating a direct positive relationship between IPV and RAD among children of adolescent mothers. However, when the mediators were added, the direct path between IPV and RAD was no longer significant, suggesting that the relationship is fully mediated by the combined effects of the mediators. Parenting stress emerged as the most significant mediator, indicating that IPV increases parenting stress, which in turn leads to higher RAD levels. Postpartum depression and maternal responsiveness also contributed to the mediation, though their effects were less pronounced. Perceived social support, while positively associated with better attachment outcomes, did not reach significance levels, suggesting a weaker mediating effect in the presence of other factors.

Implications for research and practice

The results of this study highlight the need for multifaceted support programmes for adolescent mothers. Interventions should focus on reducing IPV, alleviating parenting stress, and addressing postpartum depression. Providing resources to enhance maternal sensitivity and social support can mitigate the impact of IPV on children's attachment and development (Howell et al., 2014; Renner & Slack, 2006). In perinatal practice, it is crucial to screen for IPV, parenting stress, and postpartum depression. Health professionals should provide targeted interventions to support maternal mental health and parenting practices, promoting a stable and nurturing environment for children(Milgrom et al., 2011; O’Higgins et al., 2013). Policymakers should prioritize accessible mental health services and robust social support systems for adolescent mothers. Community-based support programmes can play a vital role in ensuring safe environments and reducing the prevalence of attachment disorders (Kang et al., 2019; WHO, 2021).

Strengths and limitation

This study provides valuable insights into the parallel mediation effects of post-partum depression, maternal sensitivity, and IPV on children's reactive attachment disorders among adolescent mothers. The use of multiple mediators offers a comprehensive understanding of the pathways through which IPV impacts child attachment. However, this study has some limitations. First, its cross-sectional design, which provides a snapshot of data at a single point in time, making it difficult to establish causal relationships. Future research could benefit from longitudinal designs to assess the dynamic relationships over time. Second, the assessment of attachment disorders relied on adolescent mothers’ perceptions, which might be influenced by underreporting or hesitation, especially in the context of IPV. Incorporating a multi-method approach, including self-report measures from children, clinical assessments, and observations by trained professionals, would provide a more comprehensive understanding. Lastly, the focus on adolescent mothers limits the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Future research should include more diverse samples to enhance generalizability.

Conclusion

Our findings underscore the significant mediating roles of parenting stress, post-partum depression, maternal sensitivity, and perceived social support in the relationship between maternal exposure to IPV and children’s attachment disorders. These results imply that adolescent mothers who experience higher levels of IPV are at increased risk of raising children with attachment disorders. Maternal insensitivity, parenting stress, perceived social support and post-partum depression appear to be critical pathways through which IPV influences children's attachment disorders. Interventions targeting maternal mental health and parenting practices are essential to mitigate the impact of IPV on children's development. This study emphasizes the need for comprehensive support programmes for adolescent mothers to break the cycle of IPV's adverse effects on child attachment and development. Future research should consider longitudinal designs and diverse samples to further explore these relationships.
