Evaluating the Readability and Understandability of Online Patient Materials Regarding Balloon Sinuplasty
Authors: Shreya R Bhalla, Lauren E Bancalari, Ariana L Shaari, Justin P McCormick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAn increasing number of patients are electing to undergo balloon sinuplasty for treatment of chronic rhinosinusitis due to its minimally invasive nature. This study aimed to evaluate the readability and understandability of online patient education materials (PEMs) related to balloon sinuplasty.Study DesignCross sectional.SettingDigital environment.MethodsA Google search was performed using 6 different search terms related to balloon sinuplasty. Websites related to PEMs for balloon sinuplasty were included. Four different measures of readability were utilized to assess the readability of each website: Flesch‐Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), Flesch‐Kincaid Reading Ease (FKRE), Gunning‐Fog Index, and Simple Measure of Gobbledygook Index. Understandability was determined using the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (PEMAT).ResultsFifty‐three online PEMs related to balloon sinuplasty were included. The average FKGL was 9.07 ± 2.1, which is higher than the recommended grade reading levels. The average FKRE was 45.11 ± 9.3, indicating they were difficult for the general public to read. The mean PEMAT was 49.7 ± 16.3%, which is lower than the accepted standard of 80%, which indicates materials are not readily understandable. Most PEMs were obtained from private medical clinics (79.2%, N = 42).ConclusionOnline PEMs related to balloon sinuplasty are written above the recommended 6th grade reading level. Poor readability was seen across all readability measures and all material categories. Patients and clinicians alike should be aware of the shortcomings of online materials and consider the effects on patient understanding and decision making.Keywords: chronic rhinosinusitis, comprehension, decision making, patient education, sinusitis

Balloon sinuplasty (BSP) utilization has increased substantially since its approval by the Food and Drug Administration in 2005 as a minimally invasive treatment for chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS). 1 BSP relieves obstruction of sinus drainage pathways allowing for improved mucociliary clearance, which can provide symptomatic relief to patients with CRS. 1 CRS is most frequently treated with medical therapy; however it is estimated that 300,000 patients elect to undergo sinus surgery annually. This includes BSP, functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS), or a combination of both treatment modalities. 1 Prior studies have reported high satisfaction rates of BSP for CRS, with some studies demonstrating long‐term success in 88.5% of patients. 2 Despite the benefits, BSP is not an entirely benign procedure. Bleeding, damage to the orbit, and damage to the skull base have been reported as complications. 1 Thus, patient education is crucial to ensure patients understand the benefits, risks, and outcomes associated with BSP.

While physicians are the ideal source of medical information, patients commonly utilize the internet to supplement their understanding of medical procedures. Previous studies indicate a rise in patient use of the internet prior to otolaryngology appointments, from a fifth of patients in 2004 to over a third of patients in 2018. 3 , 4 Previous analysis of online search behavior demonstrates a high volume of searches related to BSP, with thousands of monthly searches focused on preoperative concerns, treatment options, and procedural details. The majority of these online queries are directed to nonacademic sources, such as medical practice websites, rather than academic or society‐based resources. 5 Recent studies have demonstrated that, online materials related to common otolaryngologic procedures, such as septoplasty and parotidectomy, are consistently written above the American Medical Association (AMA) recommended 6th grade reading level and demonstrate inadequate understandability. 6 , 7 , 8 Therefore, even if online materials are accurate, the understanding of information can be limited by the use of medical jargon and technical language, which can act as a barrier for patients. 9 , 10 The American Academy of Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery, in its 2025 guideline on surgical management of chronic rhinosinusitis, explicitly recommends high‐quality, patient‐oriented education as part of the preoperative process. The guideline notes that such education improves recall of surgical risks, enhances patient involvement in decision‐making, and is associated with increased satisfaction and improved outcomes. 11

Online patient education materials (PEMs) serve as an important resource for patients to obtain medical information, so this information must be readable and understandable to patients with varying literacy levels. To our knowledge there have been no published studies analyzing the readability and understandability of online PEMs related to BSP. It is important that physicians evaluate the information patients are accessing online to not only improve understanding of the procedure but to potentially improve understanding and satisfaction levels. Therefore, the objective of this study was to assess the readability and understandability of the online PEMs available regarding BSP.

Materials and Methods

Search

The online search was conducted using Google (www.google.com) in New Brunswick, New Jersey on October 17, 2024. Google was chosen as it is the most commonly used search engine in the United States of America. 12 Prior to initiating the search, all search history, cache, and cookies were cleared. Location services were disabled for all sites. In order to minimize the influence of previous search history and location, the search was completed using Google Chrome guest mode. Six search terms were used: “balloon sinuplasty patient information,” “balloon sinuplasty,” “balloon ostial dilation,” “balloon ostial dilation patient information,” “sinus dilation surgery,” and “sinus dilation surgery patient information.” The first 30 websites generated from each search term were reviewed according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria described below. Only the first 30 links generated by Google were included because previous studies demonstrated that nearly 30% of users click on the first link, and only about 10% click on the third link, with even fewer proceeding to subsequent pages. 13 Institutional Review Board approval by Rutgers University was not required for this study, as all data is publicly accessible.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

All search results that were PEMs regarding BSP were included in the analysis. Website exclusion criteria were: websites not written in English, websites that were not accessible, audiovisual materials, blogs, scientific articles geared towards medical professionals (ie, PubMed), and websites without information regarding BSP. Duplicates were removed. Because the study aimed to evaluate traditional patient education resources, AI‐generated content (eg, Google's AI Overview) was excluded.

Categorization of Sources

The included websites were categorized into 6 categories: academic institutions, private medical clinics, professional organizations, government websites, medical information websites and other sources. Two authors initially reviewed and categorized websites independently. Disagreements were resolved by a third independent reviewer when necessary.

Websites associated with accredited universities, medical schools, or hospital systems were classified as academic institutions, as these sources typically provided evidence‐based educational material authored or reviewed by academic faculty. Websites run by private otolaryngology or sinus surgery practices were categorized as private medical clinics, characterized by promotional content aimed at informing and attracting patients to their services. Professional organizations included national or regional societies, such as the American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery, which provided standardized, peer‐reviewed educational content developed for patients. Government websites were defined as those maintained by public health agencies or institutions, typically identified by a “.gov” domain, and focused on disseminating unbiased health information to the general public. Medical information websites encompassed commercial platforms like WebMD, which aimed to offer broad, accessible health content to the general public without direct affiliation to specific practices or academic centers. Finally, websites that did not clearly fit into any of the above categories, including crowd‐sourced platforms like Wikipedia, general news sites, and commercial content without formal institutional review, were categorized as other sources.

Outcome Measures

Readability Evaluation

Plain text from each included website was used to determine the readability of information provided. Readability refers to the measurement of reading skills that an individual should have to comprehend written material. The recommended readability for PEMs is at or below a 6th grade reading level. 7 To assess readability, the Flesch Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), Flesch Kincaid Reading Ease (FKRE), Gunning Fog Index (GFI), and Simple Measure of Gobbledygook Index (SMOG) were calculated using a validated online readability calculator tool “Readability Test Tool” (WebFx). 14

FKGL is the most commonly used tool to assess readability. The calculation includes the number of words, sentences, complex words, and syllables in a given text. The accepted standard for readability is a FKGL between 6 to 8. The FKRE incorporates the total number of words, total number of sentences, and syllables per word. FKRE scores range from 0 to 100 with a score of 100 being the ideal readability and a score of 0 being unreadable. The accepted standard for FKRE is a score >60. The GFI is an additional readability tool that includes the average number of words per sentence and the percentage of words with more than 2 syllables. An acceptable GFI is 7 to 8. Finally, SMOG takes into account the number of complex words and number of sentences in a given text to estimate the number of years of education required for a person to comprehend the material. A summary of the formulas used to calculate each readability measure is included in Table 1.

Understandability Evaluation

The Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (PEMAT) was utilized to determine understandability. Written information is deemed understandable when patients of diverse health literacy can process them. 15 PEMAT weighs factors such as the use of medical terminology, numbers, visual aids, and formatting to calculate an understandability score. The accepted standards for PEM understandability is a PEMAT score is >80%. 16

Statistical Analysis

The FKRE, FKGL, GFI, SMOG, and PEMAT scores were individually calculated for each online PEM. Means and standard deviations were calculated for each readability and understandability measures using Microsoft Excel 16.90.02 (Microsoft Corp.). A Kruskall–Wallis test was used to analyze average results across readability and understandability measures based on website category using R 4.4.1 (R Studio Inc.). The ɑ threshold was set at 0.05.

Results

One hundred and eighty websites were yielded from the searches performed. Duplicates were removed and inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. There were 53 PEMs that met inclusion criteria and were evaluated for readability and understandability. Individual readability and understandability for each included PEM can be found in Supplemental File S1, available online.

Of the included PEMs 79.2% of websites were from private medical clinics (n = 42), 1.9% of PEMs were categorized as other (ie, Wikipedia) (n = 1), 15.1% were from academic institutions (n = 8), 1.9% were from medical information websites (n = 1), and 1.9% were from professional organizations (n = 1). There were no PEMs from government organizations. The categorization of online materials are presented in Figure 1.

Readability

Readability was measured using FKRE, FKGL, GFI, and SMOG calculations across all PEMs and by website category. The mean scores for all sources across readability measures is presented in Table 2. The mean FKRE for PEMs regarding BSP across all website categories was 45.11 (std: 9.29). The accepted FKRE score is >60 indicating that overall the websites included were difficult to read for the general public. Across PEM categories, websites categorized as private medical clinics had the highest mean FKRE of 46.51 (std: 9.28), while PEMs from academic institutions had the lowest mean FKRE of 36.7 (std: 6.22) meaning that they were extremely difficult to read for the general public. A Kruskal‐Wallis test revealed no significant differences in mean FKRE scores across categories (H(4) = 4.0, P = .41).

Understandability

Understandability was calculated using the PEMAT for printable materials. A source is considered to have good understandability with a PEMAT score of >80%. The average understandability across all sources was 49.7% (std: 16.26). Websites categorized as medical information websites had the highest absolute understandability of 62.5% (std: 0) while websites categorized as academic institutions had the lowest understandability of 40.1% (std: 26.83). None of the website categories were able to reach the accepted understandability score. A Kruskall‐Wallis test revealed no significant differences between understandability scores across categories. Discussion

Patients can access a wide variety of PEMs regarding BSP by searching their web browsers. This abundance of resources reflects the significant increase in BSP procedures in the past 2 decades since its FDA approval. 17 While PEMs regarding BSP are widely available, the readability and understandability of these sources remains inadequate. Our findings suggest PEMs related to BSP do not meet accepted standards of readability and understandability. Patient interaction with these PEMs can significantly affect satisfaction after undergoing head and neck surgery. 18 PEMs may help patients understand the risks and recovery of their surgery, ensuring patients feel educated about their procedure. Prior studies have shown that patients who were satisfied with their preoperative education were more likely to report that their recovery met their expectations and patients also reported decreased uncertainty about their procedure. 18 While patients receive verbal counseling from their physician, written materials can help patients retain this information better, and providing written materials has been shown to increase the recall rate of complications in patients undergoing head and neck surgery. 19 The findings of this study however, suggest that many patients may not be able to read and understand these materials, which could limit the benefits of PEMs on BSP.

Our findings demonstrate that across all sources, the mean reading level was found to be higher than the AMA recommended level of 6th grade. Previous studies analyzing the readability of PEMs regarding sinus and other head and neck surgeries have shown similar findings, with all studies reporting an average reading level above 10th grade. 6 , 8 , 20 , 21 , 22 This issue is not limited to otolaryngology, as PEMs in several surgical subspecialties are found to have poor readability. 23 The lack of readable materials poses a significant issue for patients, as the National Literacy Institute reports that over half of adults in the United States read below a 6th grade level. 24 Despite this discrepancy, little has been implemented to improve the readability of PEMs, potentially due to lack of time or funding to train authors in this area. Authors can consult resources such as the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) Health Literacy Universal precautions toolkit and the CDC website for health literacy as initial guides to creating readable and understandable PEMs. 25 , 26 These resources contain suggestions for improving readability that include breaking up larger sentences into shorter phrases or bullets, using active voice and choosing words with fewer syllables. An example would be to change “following balloon sinuplasty, you can expect mild discomfort in the nose and nasal area, which generally can be treated with over‐the‐counter medication” to “after this surgery, you may feel some nose pain” followed by “you can take pain medicines like ibuprofen to help.” 27 This breaks up 1 lengthy 22‐word sentence into two 9‐word sentences and replaces 3‐syllable words like “discomfort” with 1‐syllable words like “pain,” all of which improve the readability significantly. Authors can use online calculators to calculate validated readability scores like FKRE, FKGL and SMOG to assess their PEMs throughout drafting. While these modifications are important to make, they also require significant time. Fortunately, researchers are beginning to explore the use of artificial intelligence to solve this issue. A recent study found that prompting ChatGPT to change the reading level of existing otolaryngology PEMs to fifth grade effectively improved readability scores to the acceptable level. 28 It is therefore possible that authors can utilize artificial intelligence as a fast and free means of improving the readability of the PEMs they create.

Understandability refers to a material's ability to be comprehended by readers with varying degrees of health literacy. 16 Our results demonstrated that BSP PEMs are also below the acceptable threshold for understandability, so it is expected that many patients would not be able to understand these materials. Patients with low health literacy make up a significant proportion of patients undergoing surgery, with one systematic review finding this proportion to be at least one third across multiple studies. 29 Not only would this impair a patient's ability to ascertain information like risks and recovery from the PEM, but it could also have deleterious effects if misunderstood by patients. This is because PEMs can affect patients' decision making, with one study finding that 84% of parents with children undergoing otolaryngology procedures responded that the information they read on the internet affected medical decision making for their children. 30 Other studies using PEMAT to evaluate otolaryngology PEMs similarly find that understandability on average is below the acceptable 80% threshold, indicating that this issue is likely present across the specialty. 9 Authors should make their PEMs more understandable by using the PEMAT as a guide, ensuring they incorporate visual aids, refrain from using medical jargon and plainly explain any numbers used. 15

Health literacy is a critical determinant of patient health outcomes, with some studies citing health literacy as the single greatest predictor of an individual's health. 7 , 31 , 32 Poor health literacy is associated with worse clinical outcomes, patient‐satisfaction, and medication adherence. 7 , 31 It has been shown that poor patient understanding can disproportionately affect patients with low health literacy; however, there is limited research regarding the impact of low health literacy on surgical outcomes in otolaryngology. Of the current literature, one study found a significant correlation between health literacy and post‐operative treatment adherence in patients undergoing kidney transplantation such that limited health literacy was significantly correlated to decreased medication adherence. 33 Additionally, a 2016 meta analysis by Miller demonstrated that lower health literacy was significantly associated with decreased treatment adherence. 34

It is important that patients undergoing surgical management of CRS understand that post‐operative management typically includes the continuation of topical medical therapies. 1 Poor health literacy combined with poorly understandable PEMs could impair patient adherence to these instructions. 34 This raises the concern that a lack of understandable PEMs regarding BSP may contribute to disparities in postoperative outcomes for patients with lower health literacy. Future studies should address the association between health literacy and post surgical outcomes across surgical fields.

Our study also compared PEMs from varying organizations such as private medical groups, government websites, and academic medical centers to determine if a single category was more readable or understandable than another. Few existing studies within otolaryngology compare these categories, but one notable study regarding septoplasty found that academic PEMs were written at a lower grade level than private clinic PEMs. 8 In contrast, our study found that there were no significant differences in the readability or understandability among any of these groups. Ultimately, there is no single category that physicians can preferentially refer patients to for finding comprehensive PEMs about this procedure. This is an important consideration for BSP, because when compared to FESS, patients are more likely to find answers to common questions regarding BSP on medical practice websites rather than academic websites. 5 These findings highlight the need for improved readability and understandability of PEMs, especially in the medical clinic category, to ensure patients who seek answers from these sources can realistically obtain them.

There are several limitations to this study. First, websites that were not written in English were excluded, so these results may not be generalizable to materials written in other languages. Additionally, our findings are limited to a relatively small sample size of websites that represent only a portion of the vast array of information available on the internet. This sample was obtained from a single search engine using 6 search terms, raising the possibility that a different search procedure could yield slight variations in these results. Despite efforts made to remove bias in the search for PEMs by decreasing personalization, there still exists a potential for regional bias from IP routing or language preferences. Additionally, the continual updates in the algorithm of this search engine (Google) raise the possibility that future research replicating our procedure could also produce variations in results if they affect which PEMs are displayed.

Overall, BSP PEMs share the readability and understandability issues that are prevalent among PEMs in otolaryngology and surgery as a whole. As BSP has grown in popularity in recent years so has the availability of relevant PEMs. By quantifying the readability and understandability of these materials, this study has clarified areas of opportunity for improving written materials for the benefit of patients considering or undergoing BSP. In addition, it alerts physicians about the shortcomings of online information in this area, which is an important consideration for them to take when communicating with patients regarding the procedure.

Conclusion

PEMs describing BSP are generally written above the recommended reading level and have inadequate understandability. Given the growing use of this procedure for treatment of rhinologic issues, physicians should be aware of the effect these shortcomings can have on patient education and authors should focus on these areas to improve future materials.
