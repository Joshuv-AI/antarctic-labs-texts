Genomic Correlates of Disease Recovery in Natural Populations of the Sierra Nevada Yellow‐Legged Frog ( Rana sierrae )
Authors: Allison Q Byrne, Andrew P Rothstein, Roland A Knapp, Erica Bree Rosenblum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn our rapidly changing world, evolution is likely to play an important role in facilitating the resilience of wildlife populations. The Sierra Nevada yellow‐legged frog ( Rana sierrae ) provides a rare example of recovery following severe declines caused by the amphibian chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis). However, the role of evolution in facilitating this recovery remains circumstantial. In this study, we sought to gain insights into the potential role of evolution by comparing genomes of frogs from naive and recovering populations located in close proximity. Using multiple methods to scan frog genomes for signatures of selection, our study reveals several genomic variants associated with frog recovery. Specifically, we identify outlier gene variants across genes related to skin integrity and intracellular regulation, an interferon‐related gene and a recovery‐associated variant in RIN3—a gene that may play a critical role in disease defence and wound healing. Finally, we report no differences in genetic diversity between naive and recovering populations. Our study provides a rare example from natural populations that suggests that evolution can produce individuals that harbour adaptive alleles and allow population recovery in the presence of novel stressors. These findings complement recent research on rapid amphibian evolution in response to disease and provide mechanistic hypotheses for how individuals can prove resilient to disease outbreaks.

Introduction

Species across the tree of life are increasingly threatened with extinction. For example, an estimated 48% of animal species are currently declining (Finn et al. 2023) and models predict an accelerated rate of extinction in the next century as the effects of climate and other global change stressors intensify (Pereira et al. 2024). To persist, affected taxa will often need to adapt to their altered environment or face further decline and possible extinction. The process whereby rapid evolutionary change increases the frequency of adaptive alleles and restores positive population growth is known as ‘evolutionary rescue’ (Carlson et al. 2014; Gomulkiewicz and Holt 1995, Searle and Christie 2021). During this process, individuals with favourable traits within a declining population can persist and reproduce in the face of ongoing disturbance, passing on favourable characteristics to their offspring and thereby avert population extinction. In addition to preventing the extinction of declining populations, individuals from rescued populations could also disperse or be reintroduced to empty habitats and promote larger‐scale recovery. Although this overall scenario is potentially of critical importance for the conservation of an ever‐growing number of imperilled taxa, descriptions of its occurrence and effectiveness in the wild are lacking.

One particularly important threat to wildlife is the increasing incidence of emerging infectious diseases (Fisher et al. 2012; Jones et al. 2008), which have gravely impacted a variety of taxa (e.g., Hewson et al. 2014; Wake and Vredenburg 2008). Amphibians in particular have suffered massive declines from disease, mostly due to outbreaks of the amphibian chytrid fungus, Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd) (Longcore et al. 1999; Luedtke et al. 2023). Bd is a generalist fungal pathogen, infecting a wide variety of host species across all continents where amphibians are found (Olson et al. 2013). Bd can persist in aquatic environments for extended periods of time (Walker et al. 2007) and can be spread between water bodies by tolerant hosts (Burns et al. 2021), making it a particularly challenging threat to manage. Although the impacts of Bd are of great conservation concern in amphibian systems, amphibian recoveries in the face of disease have provided critical insights into what role rapid evolutionary change in populations can play in disease resilience.

Laboratory studies have investigated the mechanisms underlying host survival in the face of Bd infection, revealing that multiple pathways may exist for amphibian species to persist in the presence of Bd. For example, elevated immune responses are linked to lower Bd susceptibility in the Cascades frog ( Rana cascadae ) (Gervasi et al. 2014) and early activation of the immune system is linked to higher survival in a population of alpine tree frogs ( Litoria verreauxii alpina) with a long history of Bd exposure (Grogan, Cashins, et al. 2018). Additionally, certain major histocompatibility complex (MHC) alleles are associated with survival in frogs challenged with Bd (Bataille et al. 2015; Savage and Zamudio 2011; Cortazar‐Chinarro et al. 2022; Fu et al. 2023). However, Bd is known to actively suppress amphibian immune systems (Fites et al. 2013; Rosenblum et al. 2012) and some amphibians that mount robust immune responses are nonetheless highly susceptible (e.g., Atelopus zeteki , Ellison, Savage, et al. 2014; Ellison, Tunstall, et al. 2014, Rana yavapaiensis ; Savage et al. 2020). These findings indicate that there may be a specific timing, type or intensity of immune response that is most effective against Bd. In addition to immune mechanisms, there are many other factors that may contribute to host survival in the presence of Bd, including behavioural avoidance (McMahon et al. 2014), Bd genotype (McDonald et al. 2023) and the presence of certain reservoir species (Wilber et al. 2020). Therefore, although there is mounting evidence that the timing, intensity and type of immune response is critical to Bd‐related disease outcomes, other factors make understanding the mechanisms of resilience in natural systems challenging.

A quintessential example of the impacts of Bd can be seen in the mountain yellow‐legged (MYL) frogs, composed of the sister species Rana muscosa and Rana sierrae (Vredenburg et al. 2007). These frogs were once the most common amphibians in alpine regions of California's Sierra Nevada mountains (Grinnell and Storer 1924), but are now extirpated from more than 90% of their historical range due in part to widespread Bd outbreaks in the region (Vredenburg et al. 2007). The arrival of Bd in the Sierra Nevada in the mid‐1900s (Vredenburg et al. 2019) and its subsequent spread caused large‐scale extirpations (Rachowicz et al. 2006; Vredenburg et al. 2010), resulting in the listing of both R. muscosa and R. sierrae as federally endangered. Because Bd can persist in aquatic habitats without amphibian hosts (Walker et al. 2007), it represents a long‐term threat that MYL frogs will need to overcome to persist in this system.

Most Bd‐naive populations of MYL frogs are extirpated following Bd arrival. However, some populations persist after outbreaks (Briggs et al. 2010) and are now recovering (Knapp et al. 2016). Recovery after Bd outbreaks has also occurred in other amphibian species around the world (e.g., Scheele et al. 2017; Voyles et al. 2018). Field and lab studies have both shown that MYL frogs in recovering populations have lower susceptibility to Bd (characterised by reduced infection intensities) (Briggs et al. 2010; Joseph and Knapp 2018; Knapp et al. 2011, 2016), consistent with host resistance against Bd infection. The fact that Bd loads on MYL frogs change little when frogs are translocated between sites (Knapp et al. 2024) indicates that the putative MYL frog resistance is a host‐specific characteristic and is not strictly due to environmental conditions. The observed resistance of MYL frogs could be the result of several mechanisms, including natural selection (Grogan, Cashins, et al. 2018; Savage et al. 2016), acquired immunity (Grogan, Robert, et al. 2018) and/or between‐population differences pre‐dating Bd exposure. The possible evolution of MYL frog resistance and population recovery is consistent with that expected under an ‘evolutionary rescue’ scenario, opening the door to conservation interventions such as reintroducing resistant frogs to vacant habitats (Joseph and Knapp 2018; Mendelson III et al. 2019).

Critically, a recent study of R. sierrae reported that frogs collected from naturally recovering populations and translocated into vacant habitats can reestablish populations despite the ongoing presence of Bd (Knapp et al. 2024). Furthermore, survival of reintroduced frogs differed between source populations, suggesting that some populations may have traits (e.g., disease resistance) that confer higher fitness. Based on these results, Knapp et al. (2024) argued that the resistance to Bd is likely essential for R. sierrae survival, population establishment and long‐term viability (Knapp et al. 2024). In the current study, we aim to determine whether R. sierrae in recovering populations show evidence of selection and whether these genomic changes are associated with disease resistance.

We compared whole exome sequences from Bd‐naive and Bd‐exposed recovering populations of R. sierrae to characterise the genetic diversity of each population and search for gene variants associated with recovering populations. We hypothesised that evidence of selection exists in the exomes of putatively resistant R. sierrae and that some of the gene regions under selection are associated with immune functions. We also compared the genes identified in our study to those identified in the growing body of research on evolutionary responses to disease to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how rapid evolutionary change can shape amphibian population resilience in disease‐impacted systems and inform management strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

To determine whether R. sierrae populations show genomic patterns consistent with an evolutionary response to Bd, we compared frog exomes (i.e., coding region of a genome) between populations with contrasting histories of Bd exposure. Specifically, we compared frog exomes sampled in three populations that have not yet experienced a Bd‐caused epizootic (‘naive’) (Zhou et al. 2015) versus in four populations that experienced a Bd epizootic during the past several decades and have since recovered to varying degrees (‘recovering’)(Knapp et al. 2016; Vredenburg et al. 2010). Bd‐exposure histories of the seven study populations are based on 10–20 years of visual encounter surveys and Bd surveillance using skin swabbing (e.g., Knapp et al. 2016; Wilber et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2015). Naive populations are characterised by large numbers of adults (i.e., typically 1000s), Bd prevalence that is generally 0% except during occasional Bd failed invasions (during which Bd loads remain very low, Wilber et al. 2022) and no history of Bd epizootics since we first surveyed these populations in the late 1990s and early 2000s (Zhou et al. 2015). In contrast, recovering populations exist in an enzootic state (Briggs et al. 2010), characterised by smaller numbers of adults (generally < 500), high Bd prevalence (often > 80%, Knapp et al. 2011) and, in adults, moderate Bd loads that are typically well below the level expected to cause mortality (< 10,000 zoospore equivalents, Vredenburg et al. 2010).

Sample Collection and Sequencing

We collected DNA samples via buccal swabbing (Goldberg et al. 2003) from 41 R. sierrae individuals: 18 from three naive populations and 23 from four recovering populations. We chose to use buccal swabbing to collect DNA samples instead of toe clips to minimise harm to animals and because studies have shown buccal swabs to yield similar results to toe clip samples (Rainey et al. 2024). The R. sierrae populations sampled for this study are located in the southern Sierra Nevada, from northern Yosemite National Park to southern Kings Canyon National Park (Figure 1A). Samples were collected from five or six frogs per population. To minimise potential confounding effects caused by variation in frog genotypes across latitude (Byrne et al. 2023), we selected sampling sites such that both population types were represented across similar latitudinal ranges and were often in close proximity (naive populations: latitude range = 36.763–37.776, recovering populations: latitude range = 36.882–37.969; Figure 1A).

DNA was extracted using the Qiagen DNEasy Blood and Tissue Kit following the manufacturer's protocols. We sequenced the samples using an exome capture assay as described in detail in (Byrne et al. 2023). Briefly, genomic libraries were prepared using a Kapa Hyper Prep kit (Roche) and captured using a set of custom Nimblegen capture probes (Roche) which included 14,508 targets for a total target size of 31.4 Mb. Capture baits were designed based on the coding regions of the R. muscosa transcriptome (GenBank accession GKCT00000000.1). Fifty nanograms input DNA was used for each sample and all samples were included in one capture pool with a total DNA input of 2800 ng. Captured libraries were then amplified with nine cycles of enrichment PCR and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 150PE Flow Cell S1 at the Vincent J. Coates Genomics Sequencing Lab at UC Berkeley.

Data Pre‐Processing and Cleaning

Raw reads were filtered for adapters and contaminants using fastp (Chen et al. 2018) and aligned to the R. muscosa genome (NCBI SRA: SRS6533475 (Hon et al. 2020)), with repetitive elements masked using bwa (‘mem’ mode) (Li 2013). Exact PCR duplicates were marked using Picard (Picard Toolkit 2019). Variants were then called following GATK best practices (v.4.2.0.0, McKenna et al. 2010). Briefly, raw variants were called for each sample using HaplotypeCaller and combined using CombineGVCFs. Next, genotypes were jointly called using GenotypeGVCFs. Variants were then hard filtered using gatk VariantFiltration using the following parameters to remove low‐quality sites: QD < 2.0, FS > 40.0, SOR > 3.0, MQ < 50.0, MQRankSum > 3.0, MQRankSum < −3.0, ReadPosRankSum > 3.0, ReadPosRankSum < −3.0. This initial filter resulted in 1,595,206 variant sites across 41 individuals.

We then further filtered our dataset at the individual and variant level. First, we trimmed variants to only include those with minor allele frequency > 0.02, a maximum depth of 250 and minimum depth of 5 and a maximum missing proportion of 0.5. This filter resulted in 384,089 sites, of which 318,109 were SNPs and 65,980 were INDELS. Finally, we trimmed samples with an average depth across filtered sites < 7x (n = 1). Our final dataset included 40 samples, 18 from naive and 22 from recovering populations, with an average depth of coverage of 16.7x (range = 7.4x – 26.1x, Table S1).

Population Genetic Analyses

First, to visualise the population genetic relationships between sampling sites we conducted a PCA using the glPCA function in the adegenet R package (Jombart 2008) using the set of 318,109 SNPs described above. Next, to characterise general patterns of genetic diversity between naive and recovering populations, we conducted three analyses. First, we calculated heterozygosity for each sampled frog using VCFtools (Danecek et al. 2011). Second, to characterise genome‐wide patterns of diversity, we used VCFtools to calculate nucleotide diversity (π) in 100 kb sliding windows along the genome for each population.

Identifying Gene Variants Associated With Recovering Populations

Due to the scarcity of naive populations, we could not conduct replicated analyses among pairs of geographically proximate persistent and naive populations. Instead, to obtain adequate power, we pooled all naive individuals and all recovering individuals across populations and conducted an analysis where we looked for genes with a strong and consistent signature of selection across population types using the methods described below.

To detect regions of the genome that differed between naive and recovering populations, that is, putative regions under selection, we used two approaches: (1) a multivariate linear mixed model to evaluate individual variants (SNPs and INDELs) and (2) a splined window analysis to evaluate larger genomic regions (Beissinger et al. 2015). For the variant analysis, we first used a stringent data filter to include only variants with < 5% missing data (missing for no more than two individuals) and then calculated the likelihood ratio statistic for the resulting set of 132,486 high quality variants across 127 contigs using GEMMA (Zhou and Stephens 2014). GEMMA calculates and incorporates a relatedness matrix for input samples, allowing us to account for relatedness and population structure when calculating likelihood ratio statistics. We identified variants showing different allele frequencies between naive versus recovering populations (‘outliers’) using a Bonferroni‐corrected significance level of 0.01. We visualised the results using a Manhattan plot and qqplot. We developed a more liberal set of outlier variants using a Bonferroni‐corrected significance level of 0.05 and used this set solely for the gene ontology (GO) analysis (see below; Table S2).

In the splined window analysis, we identified outlier regions using F ST and differences in nucleotide diversity (πdiff) between naive and recovering populations. First, we calculated per‐site F ST between the naive and recovering individuals for all bi‐allelic SNPs in the 30 largest contigs (98% of all SNPs) using VCFtools (Danecek et al. 2011). Next, we calculated per‐site nucleotide diversity π separately for all naive (N = 18) and recovering (N = 22) individuals using VCFtools, then calculated πdiff for each population (πdiff=πnaive−πrecovering). We concatenated the values for F ST and πdiff in order of size‐sorted chromosome number and adjusted the SNP position based on the relative position in the genome (for more efficient data processing and to better contextualise the strength of the outlier signals in different regions of the genome). We then used the GenWin R package (Beissinger et al. 2015) to conduct a splined discrete window analysis for F ST and πdiff. This method calculates where non‐overlapping window boundaries should occur by identifying inflection points in the spline fitted to F ST and πdiff values along the genome, therefore balancing false positive and false negative results that occur using other window‐based methods (Beissinger et al. 2015). This method also calculates a W‐statistic allowing for outlier identification. We identified outliers as those with a W‐statistic greater than four standard deviations above the mean for F ST or above/below the mean for πdiff. These standard deviations represent strict criteria to select only the top ~0.3% of windows. Shared outliers were then identified as those that were outliers in both analyses, meaning that they showed (i) high differentiation between naive and recovering populations and (ii) differential patterns of nucleotide diversity in the same region. Finally, we extracted gene transcripts mapped within each region and retrieved annotation for that region using BLAST (Table S2).

GO Analysis and Predicted Effect of Outlier Variants

Using the liberal set of outlier variants (identified using a Bonferroni‐corrected p‐value of 0.05, which included 64 outliers: 59 SNPs and 5 INDELS from 49 distinct genes across 20 contigs), we determined if any GO biological functions, molecular functions or cellular processes were overrepresented. To do this, we retrieved the BLAST hits and mapped GO terms for each gene in our targeted transcriptome. We then conducted a statistical overrepresentation test (Fisher's exact test) using Blast2GO (Götz et al. 2008) to compare the 49 unique outlier genes to the complete set of genes in our target transcriptome. We repeated this process for the set of 10 genes located in the five shared regions of the F ST and πdiff splined windows.

In the analysis of individual variants, for each outlier variant we determined whether the variant was synonymous (protein sequence the same for each variant) or non‐synonymous (protein sequence differs between variants) and where in the gene it was located. To do this, we first extracted the reference genome sequence surrounding the variant using the bedtools ‘getfasta’ function (Quinlan and Hall 2010). Next, we re‐annotated each sequence using BLAST to get the predicted gene location based on the closest annotated reference (Altschul et al. 1997). We then translated each variant to amino acids and aligned this translation to that of the gene annotation to ensure proper frame of reference using Geneious (Kearse et al. 2012). After ensuring proper translation, we characterised variants as within or outside the coding sequence of the gene and as either synonymous or non‐synonymous.

Results

Population Genetic Structure of Naive and Recovering Frogs

Individual frogs clustered into three separate groups in the first two principal components (Figure 1b) and clusters reflected known population divisions and population genetic signatures of isolation‐by‐distance that are characteristic of MYL frogs (Byrne et al. 2023; Poorten et al. 2017; Rothstein et al. 2020). Importantly, the naive and recovering groups are distributed among multiple clusters, allowing us to distinguish allelic associations of individuals sampled in the two population types versus allelic associations resulting from population structure and genetic drift.

Candidate Genes Under Selection Identified via Comparing Naive and Recovering Exome Sequences

Results from the individual variant and splined window analyses show that recovering populations have signatures of selection on multiple regions of the genome. The analysis of individual variants identified 17 ‘outlier’ SNPs (i.e., showing significantly different allele frequencies between naive versus recovering populations, adjusted p < 0.01, Table 1, Table S2) from 14 distinct genes across 8 contigs (Figure 1C,D). For the set of outlier SNPs (p < 0.01), frequency differences between the naive and recovering populations ranged from 0.61 to 0.97 (Table 1). Most of the variants in the liberal set of outliers (adjusted p < 0.05) showed frequency differences in only a subset of the sampled populations, however, the variants with the lowest p‐values (and largest change in allele frequencies) showed consistent differences in frequencies across all populations (e.g., RIN3, FHIP, KPNA4; Figure 2). This result is suggestive of parallel selection at these loci across multiple populations.

No Differences in Overall Genetic Diversity Between Population Types

Although the joint processes of Bd‐caused population declines and selection in response to Bd exposure could affect genetic diversity of recovering populations, we found no consistent differences in individual‐level heterozygosity or population‐level π between naive and recovering populations (Figure 4). Thus, despite localised selection in particular regions of the genome, we did not find evidence for reduced genetic diversity across the genome in recovering populations.

Discussion

Our genomic results indicate that frogs from naive and recovering populations show significant differences at several loci, including loci within genes related to immune functions, metabolic stress and cellular integrity. We identified candidate genes under selection in recovering populations using two methods (1) likelihood ratio tests to identify specific variants associated with recovering individuals and (2) splined window analyses to identify regions of the genome that show both high differentiation between recovering and naive individuals (F ST) and large shifts in nucleotide diversity (πdiff) in the same gene region. Because we did not identify any significantly enriched GO terms in either set of genes, our results suggest that Bd recovery is not driven by a single pathway but instead reflects a polygenic phenotype. The regions we identified as under selection fit into categories that align with biological expectations of how resistant amphibians survive Bd infections (i.e., by managing infection and skin damage rather than eliminating the pathogen outright, Eskew et al. 2018). Specifically, we identified genes involved in endosomal and nuclear trafficking (RIN3, FHIP, ASAP3, KPNA4), adhesion and cytoskeletal organisation (NECTIN4, CASS4, SYNE2, NES), metabolism and stress‐response (HK1, HKDC1, SUPV3L1, ERO1A, GPX1‐like) and one particularly interesting immunologically relevant gene (GVINP1) that has been directly linked to disease resistance in other taxa (see below).

The strongest outlier loci identified in our study are consistent with selection on skin integrity and tissue tolerance rather than canonical antifungal immunity. Several top candidates (RIN3, FHIP, ASAP3 and KPNA4) are involved in intracellular trafficking, membrane dynamics and nuclear signalling, processes that influence how epithelial cells respond to and resolve stress and inflammatory cues. Additional outliers affecting adhesion and cytoskeletal organisation (NECTIN4, CASS4, SYNE2 and NESTIN) further implicate the maintenance of epidermal cohesion and mechanical resilience, which are critical given that Bd pathology arises from disruption of amphibian skin structure and physiological homeostasis. Together with genes involved in metabolic and redox balance (HK1, HKDC1 and GPX1‐like), these results support skin integrity and tissue tolerance as central components of Bd resistance in R. sierrae .

Similar results can be found in another study that used exome‐sequencing methods to identify gene variants associated with naturally persisting frogs (Panamanian harlequin frogs, Atelopus varius and A. zeteki ) following Bd‐induced declines (Byrne et al. 2020). The list of genes identified as putatively under selection in Byrne et al. (2020) (i.e., genes with variants that were significantly associated with persisting populations vs. pre‐Bd populations) showed no significantly enriched GO terms, however, their candidate gene list did include immune response genes (e.g., toll‐like receptors, interferon‐induced proteins), metabolism genes (e.g., cytochrome p450) and cell trafficking genes (e.g., lysosomal trafficking regulator, exocyst complex component 8). While there were no exact gene matches between Byrne et al. (2020) and this study, there is a significant overlap in the biological processes represented by these genes, including intracellular trafficking, interferon‐related immune response and metabolism. Therefore, in both systems we see evidence of the same biological processes being under selection in each system (rather than identical genes being present in both datasets), reflecting the complex, polygenic nature of amphibian responses to Bd at the gene level and convergent responses at the broader, functional level.

Maintaining skin integrity and regulating stress responses appear to be key components of amphibian resistance to Bd. Multiple gene expression studies have shown that amplified immune responses to Bd are associated with susceptibility rather than resistance (Ellison, Savage, et al. 2014; Ellison, Tunstall, et al. 2014; Poorten and Rosenblum 2016; Eskew et al. 2018). For example, Bd‐resistant amphibian species such as Agalychnis callidryas exhibited minimal transcriptional changes following Bd exposure, maintained low pathogen loads and showed differential expression primarily in genes associated with epithelial integrity rather than immune activation (Ellison, Tunstall, et al. 2014). Similarly, resistant bullfrogs ( Rana catesbeiana ) displayed a low overall transcriptional response, with upregulation of metabolic and biosynthetic processes, including keratinization (Eskew et al. 2018). Poorten and Rosenblum (2016) likewise reported that more Bd‐resistant toad species ( Rhinella marina ) exhibited reduced transcriptional responses relative to a more susceptible species ( Anaxyrus boreas ) and Savage et al. (2020) found that susceptible R. yavapaiensis had lower MHC expression than their resistant counterparts. However, it should be noted that amphibian gene expression is known to vary with the tissue sampled and the Bd genotype used for inoculation (Chondrelli et al. 2024, Zamudio et al. 2020). Together, insights from gene expression studies suggest that Bd resistance is often characterised by limited immune activation coupled with maintenance of skin structure and physiological function, consistent with transcriptional evidence from MYL frogs showing disruption of skin integrity pathways during infection (Rosenblum et al. 2012).

The region with the strongest indication of directional selection (reduced πdiff in recovering populations) contains the interferon‐related gene GVINP1. Several previous studies of amphibians have found this gene to be differentially expressed during Bd infection (e.g., Ellison, Savage, et al. 2014; Ellison, Tunstall, et al. 2014; Price et al. 2015) and in populations differing in Bd susceptibility (Grogan, Cashins, et al. 2018). This gene is also strongly linked to amoebic gill disease in salmon, explaining a notable 20% of the resistance phenotype (i.e., gill damage and amoebic load as heritable host resistance traits; Robledo et al. 2018, 2020). Therefore, our study contributes to a growing body of evidence that interferon‐related genes play a critical role in amphibian responses to Bd. Interestingly, lower expression of GVINP1 was linked to resistant populations of alpine tree frogs, fitting the narrative that suppressed immune responses are likely more adaptive for amphibians challenged with Bd (Grogan, Cashins, et al. 2018). While this immune‐related gene is a strong candidate gene for Bd‐associated responses, the directionality of response (i.e., whether a gene is up‐ or down‐regulated) is an important variable and the overall pattern of selection in our study suggests the key role of regulation and tissue tolerance rather than pathogen clearance via immune response.

Based on the analysis of individual outlier variants, the RIN3 gene showed divergent allele frequencies across naive and recovering frog populations sampled in this study (Figure 2c), indicating consistent patterns of selection across the sampled geographic area. This gene is associated with immune response and in Xenopus is highly expressed during appendage regeneration (Fukazawa et al. 2009). Therefore, in agreement with its role in intracellular trafficking and immune regulation, RIN3 could play a critical role in skin repair. Collectively, the genes associated with these genomic differences may confer at least some degree of resistance against Bd infection, an attribute that may be critically important to population resilience and recovery in the presence of Bd.

It is important to note that our genomics work cannot provide a direct assessment of the strength or timing of natural selection at particular loci due to sampling constraints. As with many studies of highly endangered species, we do not have time‐series data or a paired sampling design. Comparing populations with different infection histories allowed larger sample sizes and replication across the landscape. The alternative approach of comparing samples from the same populations before and after Bd exposure is not feasible in this system because Bd arrived in most MYL frog populations decades ago and population persistence/recovery is rare and unpredictable. As a result, samples from recovering populations collected before and after Bd exposure are not available and are unlikely to be available in the future. However, comparing exome‐wide signatures of selection between population types allow us to identify specific regions of the genome that merit further study for their potential contribution to survivorship in recovering populations. Further studies that combine Bd‐testing with an ecological transcriptomics approach (e.g., Czypionka et al. 2015) to non‐lethally characterise gene expression of specific gene targets in naturally infected populations could serve to test the hypothesis that the genes identified in our study play an important role in Bd susceptibility or resistance.

In light of a recent complementary study documenting strong associations between source populations and the probability of successful reestablishment following MYL frog translocations despite the presence of Bd (Knapp et al. 2024), our findings provide key insights into the mechanisms that may be at work in this conservation success story. In addition to addressing fundamental questions regarding evolutionary responses to disease, our study has real‐world conservation applications. For example, the ability to identify genomic correlates of Bd resistance in MYL frogs could enhance our ability to screen populations for predicted reintroduction success. However, future studies that use genomics to track translocation success and use larger sample sizes, along with more studies to validate candidate genes, will be necessary to establish robust links between genetic mechanisms and natural resilience to Bd. Additionally, selecting for certain gene variants can have negative consequences if it results in reducing effective population sizes or increases the chances of inbreeding depression; therefore, extreme caution must be taken when using genomic data to inform translocation or reintroduction decisions. Overall, our study provides an example of how we can use genomics in wildlife conservation to better understand eco‐evolutionary dynamics that have real‐world applications.
