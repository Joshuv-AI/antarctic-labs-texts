The Five‐Day Trajectory of Nutritional Indicators and Influencing Factors in Adult Patients After VA‐ECMO: A Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Wenyu Li, Chuhuan Zhao, Zhiyang Xie, Jing Zheng, Lianlian Sun, Yani Yan, Xiaoqun Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to characterize the 5‐day trajectory of nutritional and metabolic profiles and identify influencing factors in adults receiving VA‐ECMO support.MethodsA total of 46 patients with cardiogenic shock receiving VA‐ECMO (2022–2024; median age 50 [IQR: 41.0–63.3]) were prospectively enrolled. Demographic, clinical, nutritional, and metabolic biomarkers were serially collected over 5 days. Multivariate analysis was employed to evaluate longitudinal trends and determinants of change.ResultsSignificant dynamic changes were observed in nutritional and metabolic parameters, including serum albumin, neutrophil count, prognostic nutrition index (PNI), triceps skinfold thickness, and mid‐upper arm circumference. A marked decline in albumin occurred within the first 48 h (mean differences: –5.663, p = 0.035; –5.728, p = 0.003). Age and hyperlipidemia were independent factors affecting albumin levels (p < 0.001 and p = 0.032, respectively). Progressive reductions in skinfold thickness and arm circumference were noted from day 2 to day 5 (p < 0.001).ConclusionsECMO support is associated with early changes in nutritional status. Rapid albumin decline within 48 h and later anthropometric reductions suggest a need for prompt nutritional monitoring and individualized intervention, though further validation is warranted.Implications for Clinical PracticeThese findings highlight the critical importance of integrated nutritional and metabolic management initiated early during VA‐ECMO support to improve patient outcomes.

Background

Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) is a life‐support technology for respiratory and/or cardiac failure, widely used in patients with severe cardiopulmonary failure [1]. Over the past decade, its global utilization has increased at an annual rate of approximately 17% [2], and its application in awake, non‐intubated patients has become increasingly common [3]. Nevertheless, ECMO itself is associated with significant challenges. Contact between blood and the non‐endothelialized circuit and oxygenator can induce extracorporeal circulation‐related injury, ischemia/reperfusion injury, coagulation abnormalities, and inflammatory pathway activation, ultimately leading to a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) [4]. This state triggers a hypermetabolic response characterized by hypersecretion of catabolic hormones (e.g., glucagon, catecholamines, and corticosteroids), resulting in increased energy consumption, enhanced lipolysis, and accelerated protein breakdown. Consequently, patients are at high risk of nutritional and metabolic derangements, muscle wasting, infectious complications, prolonged hospitalization, and elevated mortality [5, 6, 7, 8, 9].

Optimal nutritional and metabolic support is essential for this vulnerable population. The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) guidelines recommend initiating enteral nutrition early (within 48 h) in critically ill patients, including those on ECMO [10]. However, a substantial gap persists between guideline recommendations and clinical implementation. The balance between the potential benefits of early feeding and the theoretical risks of delayed feeding remains a contentious issue in clinical decision‐making.

This disagreement stems from two primary factors. First, evidence supporting early nutrition is largely derived from retrospective studies, with a notable lack of prospective, high‐quality data in hemodynamically unstable patients—particularly those on VA‐ECMO, in whom hemodynamic instability is more severe and the risk of gastrointestinal complications is higher. Second, some clinicians argue that in specific subpopulations (e.g., patients with refractory cardiac arrest or profound hemodynamic instability), delaying enteral nutrition may be a safer strategy to avoid diverting blood flow away from critical organs such as the brain to the splanchnic circulation [11, 12].

There remains an urgent clinical need for accurate, objective, and dynamic biomarkers to evaluate integrated changes in intestinal function, nutrition, and metabolism in patients on ECMO support, thereby enabling more precise timing for individualized interventions [13]. Therefore, a detailed characterization of the early dynamics of key nutritional and metabolic parameters in patients undergoing VA‐ECMO may help resolve ongoing controversies, validate the benefits of early nutritional support, and contribute high‐quality evidence to inform clinical decision‐making. This study aims to prospectively describe the 5‐day trajectory of nutritional and metabolic profiles and influencing factors in adults receiving VA‐ECMO, providing a foundation for science‐guided and personalized support strategies.

Measurement of Nutritional, Metabolic, and Inflammatory Parameters

Based on previous literature [14, 15, 16, 17, 18], a set of parameters was selected to systematically evaluate nutritional, metabolic, and inflammatory status. All data were extracted from the hospital′s electronic medical record system. Measurements were obtained at admission (baseline) and then once daily for five consecutive days. The parameters assessed included: Laboratory biomarkers: serum albumin, C‐reactive protein (CRP), serum creatinine (SCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total bilirubin, hemoglobin, absolute lymphocyte count, absolute neutrophil count, and platelet count. Anthropometric measurements: triceps skinfold thickness and mid‐upper arm circumference. Composite indices: the prognostic nutritional index (PNI) was calculated as a composite marker reflecting nutritional and immune status to evaluate its clinical utility.

Anthropometric Measurement Protocol

All anthropometric measurements were conducted by trained personnel following standardized protocols outlined in established references [19, 20]. The following equipment was used: electronic digital calipers (accuracy: 0.1 mm; Shengtaixin Electronics Co. Ltd., Zhejiang, China) for skinfold thickness and non‐stretch tape measures for circumferences. Triceps Skinfold Thickness: Measured at the midpoint on the posterior aspect of the arm between the acromion and olecranon processes. A skinfold parallel to the long axis of the arm was grasped with the elbow flexed at 90°. Mid‐Upper Arm Circumference: Measured horizontally at the midpoint of the arm between the acromion and olecranon processes with the arm relaxed and hanging naturally.

To ensure data accuracy and reliability, all measurements were performed in triplicate, and the mean value was calculated for analysis. All data were recorded immediately using standardized forms to ensure completeness and traceability.

Ethical Approval

The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of XX Medical University (Approval No.: 2022(018)). All participants were thoroughly explained the purpose and significance of the study, and written informed consent was obtained. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time without any negative impact on their treatment. Furthermore, all data were used exclusively for research purposes and maintained strictly confidential. The study adhered to all relevant guidelines and regulations and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical Methods

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Categorical variables were reported as n (%) and compared using χ² or Fisher's exact tests. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD and compared using t‐tests or ANOVA; non‐normal variables as median (IQR) with Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests. Longitudinal changes were assessed with repeated‐measures ANOVA. A two‐sided p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Baseline and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 46 patients with cardiogenic shock requiring VA‐ECMO support were prospectively enrolled. Among these, 29 (63.0%) were diagnosed with severe myocarditis and 17 (37.0%) with cardiogenic shock secondary to acute myocardial infarction. All patients received femoral VA‐ECMO cannulation with arterial cannulae (15–17 Fr) and venous cannulae (20–21 Fr). ECMO support was initiated using MAQUET ROTAFLOW or CARDIOHELP systems (Getinge, Sweden), with an initial blood flow set at 60 mL/kg/min and pump speed titrated to maintain a 1:1 flow‐to‐rpm ratio. Anticoagulation was monitored via activated clotting time (ACT, target 160–180 s) or activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT, target 60–80 s).

The cohort consisted of 30 (65.22%) males and 16 (34.78%) females, with a median age of 50.0 [41.0, 63.3] years and a median preoperative BMI of 22.7 [20.4, 24.7]. Preoperative nutritional risk screening (NRS 2002) scores were low for most patients (86.96% scored 0), and the median preoperative SOFA score was 5 [3, 7]. 34 (73.91%) patients received vasoactive drugs, 26 (56.52%) received inotropic agents, and 18 (39.13%) received glucocorticoids. In contrast, five key parameters met the criteria for both homogeneity of variance and normality of residuals: serum albumin, absolute neutrophil count, PNI, triceps skinfold thickness, and mid‐upper arm circumference. These were therefore selected for further parametric longitudinal analysis.

As detailed in Table 4, all five parameters exhibited statistically significant changes across the measurement time points (p < 0.001 for all), indicating a marked decline in these nutritional, metabolic, and body composition indices during the first 5 days of VA‐ECMO support.

Factors Influencing Longitudinal Changes in Measured Parameters

Multivariate analysis identified specific patient factors independently associated with longitudinal changes in the measured parameters (detailed in Table 5). Age and hyperlipidemia were identified as significant independent predictors of serum albumin dynamics. For absolute neutrophil count, age, smoking history, alcohol use, and COPD status were significant independent determinants. Similarly, age and hyperlipidemia were significantly associated with variations in PNI. In contrast, gender was the only significant factor associated with changes in both triceps skinfold thickness and mid‐upper arm circumference. Other examined variables, including hypertension, diabetes, and previous myocardial infarction, were not significantly associated with any of the outcome measures.

Discussion

Inflammation, Metabolic Stress, and Nutritional Implications

The observed increase in absolute neutrophil count following ECMO initiation aligns with existing literature [8, 21]. This phenomenon is likely triggered by blood contact with the non‐biologic surface of the circuit, which activates complement and immune pathways, initiating a systemic inflammatory response akin to SIRS. Furthermore, the procedural stress associated with ECMO initiation (including surgery and cannulation) may exacerbate this response, promoting neutrophil activation, endothelial infiltration, and potential end‐organ injury.

Our multivariate analysis further reveals that this inflammatory response is modulated by specific patient factors. Age, smoking history, alcohol use, and COPD status all demonstrated a significant influence on neutrophil activity (all p < 0.05), suggesting that complex immunomodulatory pathways interact to shape the ultimate neutrophil response.

These findings highlight that the ECMO‐triggered inflammatory state directly contributes to the hypermetabolic response and nutritional depletion observed in these patients. Therefore, our results underscore the need for proactive, personalized nutritional support—particularly in elderly patients, smokers, alcohol users, and those with COPD—to modulate immune activation, enhance ECMO tolerance, and potentially mitigate associated complications.

Dynamics and Determinants of Nutritional‐Metabolic Changes

Serum albumin levels declined sharply during the initial 48 h of ECMO support (mean differences: −5.663, p = 0.035; −5.728, p = 0.003). This early reduction likely reflects the combined effects of surgical stress, underlying critical illness, and ECMO‐induced inflammatory responses. Although nutritional support was associated with a moderated rate of decline after day 2, levels continued to decrease, indicating persistent nutritional‐metabolic stress.

Multivariate analysis revealed that age (p < 0.001) and hyperlipidemia (p = 0.032) were significant independent predictors of albumin dynamics. Patients with hyperlipidemia exhibited consistently lower albumin levels from day 2 onward (Table 4), highlighting this subgroup′s particular vulnerability.

Similar negative trends were observed in somatic composition. Triceps skinfold thickness exhibited a significant decrease throughout the observation period (p < 0.001), indicating rapid mobilization of subcutaneous fat reserves. Mid‐upper arm circumference showed a substantial decline from day 2 onward (p < 0.001), suggesting progressive muscle catabolism.

Subgroup analysis identified gender as a significant factor influencing both measures, with male patients exhibiting a more rapid rate of loss of both fat and lean mass across all time points compared to females (Table 5). This disparity may be attributed to inherent physiological differences in body composition between sexes as well as the distinct clinical profiles within our cohort. Specifically, most female patients were diagnosed with severe myocarditis, whereas a larger proportion of male patients presented with cardiogenic shock following myocardial infarction, which may reflect a greater overall disease burden in males.

The PNI, a composite indicator, exhibited a significant early decline during the initial 48 h of ECMO support (day 1: −56.635, p = 0.035; day 2: −57.289, p = 0.003), indicating substantial impairment in global nutritional‐metabolic status. Although the rate of decline attenuated after day 3, the persistent downward trajectory (p = 0.064) further underscores the critical window for early nutritional intervention to mitigate ongoing catabolism.

Clinical Implications and Directions for Nutritional Management

Our findings have important implications for the clinical management of patients receiving ECMO support. The rapid development of systemic inflammatory response, accompanied by a hypermetabolic state and early declines in nutritional biomarkers and body composition parameters, supports the need for targeted nutritional intervention. Multivariate analysis revealed that this pathophysiological cascade is modulated by specific patient factors. The significant influence of age, smoking history, alcohol use, and COPD on neutrophil activation identifies a high‐risk phenotype predisposed to exaggerated inflammation. Similarly, age, hyperlipidemia, and male sex were associated with accelerated protein catabolism and muscle loss. These findings strongly support the implementation of personalized, risk‐stratified nutritional strategies.

The initial 48–72 h window following ECMO initiation represents a critical period for intervention. Our data support the evolving paradigm that nutrition in this context should be regarded not merely as supportive care but as adjunctive therapy to modulate immune responses [11]. Incorporating specific nutritional formulations (e.g., immunonutrition) into the regimen of high‐risk individuals—particularly elderly patients, smokers, and those with COPD—may help mitigate the deleterious inflammatory cascade and improve ECMO tolerance. Furthermore, the accelerated loss of lean body mass, especially among male patients and those with hyperlipidemia, necessitates aggressive high‐protein supplementation combined with feasible early physical therapy and mobilization to counteract anabolic resistance and muscle catabolism [22].

We therefore recommend implementing a standardized nutritional protocol that includes: (1) Pre‐ECMO nutritional risk assessment incorporating identified modifying factors (age, smoking, alcohol use, COPD, hyperlipidemia); (2) Dynamic monitoring of nutritional and metabolic indicators (e.g., albumin, PNI, triceps skinfold thickness, mid‐upper arm circumference) throughout the ECMO course; and (3) Early initiation of personalized nutrition that tailors caloric and protein targets, as well as immunomodulatory components, to the individual′s risk profile.

The successful implementation of such a comprehensive strategy requires a multidisciplinary collaborative approach involving intensivists, nutritionists, pharmacists, and physical therapists to optimize delivery, monitor efficacy, and integrate nutritional support within the overall goal of improving survival and functional recovery [23]. Future prospective studies, particularly randomized controlled trials, are warranted to validate the efficacy of this personalized nutritional management protocol in improving outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size and single‐center design may limit the statistical power and generalizability of our findings to broader patient populations and clinical settings. Furthermore, the inclusion of patients with different underlying etiologies (e.g., myocarditis and myocardial infarction) may introduce heterogeneity into the cohort; although the overall metabolic trends were consistent, the limited sample size precluded meaningful stratified analysis by etiology. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate and extend our observations, particularly through subgroup analyses based on specific clinical conditions.
