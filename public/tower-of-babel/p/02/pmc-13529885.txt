Prologue: language abilities in persons with dyslexia
Authors: Tiffany P Hogan, Norma Hancock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe purpose of this special issue is to showcase current research on language abilities in persons with dyslexia. Although dyslexia is most often defined as a word-level reading and spelling disorder rooted in phonology, a large and growing body of evidence shows that the language difficulties of dyslexia extend well beyond phonology and are too often overlooked in research and practice. The articles in this issue (a) examine how broader oral language has and has not been accounted for in dyslexia research and policy, (b) characterize the language profiles of children with dyslexia with and without co-occurring developmental language disorder (DLD), (c) trace early language markers and subtypes that precede and predict reading difficulty, and (d) consider practical tools for detecting language vulnerability and for tailoring intervention to it within populations with reading and language difficulty. Read together, these contributions converge on a single message: dyslexia is best understood as a language learning disability, and assessment and instruction that ignore broader oral language risk leaving the needs of many children unmet. Universal screening, evaluation, and intervention for children with or at risk for dyslexia should routinely include measures of and stimulation for all components of oral language.

Prologue: language abilities in persons with dyslexia

The same cognitive-linguistic system that supports talking and understanding spoken language also supports learning to read and write. This special issue gathers a set of studies that, from different vantage points, return again and again to the fact that reading is language (Snowling & Hulme, 2025), and that the oral language abilities of children with dyslexia deserve more attention (Adlof & Hogan, 2019). Throughout this prologue, we use the term oral language to refer to the comprehension and production of spoken language across multiple linguistic domains—phonology, morphology, syntax, vocabulary, and discourse (Bloom & Lahey, 1978). Phonology is, by this definition, a component of the language system rather than a skill that sits outside of it. When we contrast phonological processing with broader oral language, we are distinguishing it from the non-phonological domains, notably vocabulary, grammar, and discourse (see Adlof & Hogan, 2018, for review).

The characterization of dyslexia as a language-learning difficulty is not new. It can be traced back a century ago to Samuel Orton (Orton, 1925). However, two influential framings of dyslexia have likely contributed to less focus on language in persons with dyslexia. First, dyslexia was framed as unexpected word reading failure relative to general intelligence, that included broader language skills, which then often inadvertently excluded children with weak broader language skills (see Vellutino et al., 2004 for review). Second, the phonological-deficit hypothesis located the core impairment in phonological processing (Stanovich, 1988), frequently in a subset of children with dyslexia who had otherwise good broader language skills. Notably, although phonological processing is, by definition, part of the language system, this hypothesis often treated it as a cognitive process rather than as a component of oral language (Wagner & Torgesen, 1987). Within these views, dyslexia became a disorder of written language, conceptually separated from broader language difficulties (Snowling et al., 2020). The articles in this special issue provide updated research on the language abilities of persons with dyslexia. Across longitudinal cohorts, clinical samples, classroom-based assessments, and analyses of policy, they document how the boundaries of written and spoken language blur, and how much is lost when broader oral language is left unattended in children with dyslexia.

Accounting for oral language in dyslexia: research and policy

This special issue opens by asking, when researchers and policymakers study and legislate about dyslexia, do they account for broader oral language? Cabbage and colleagues (2026) address the research side directly. In a review of the literature from 2000 to 2022 spanning medicine, psychology, education, and speech-language pathology, they coded the criteria used to classify children as dyslexic, including whether broader oral language skills were considered diagnostically or descriptively. Across disciplines, phonological awareness was by far the most commonly assessed language skill, and studies from speech-language pathology were more likely than others to include measures of broader oral language. Their analysis exposes an inherent confound when investigators fail to characterize the language abilities of their samples, the conclusions drawn about “dyslexia” may rest on hidden heterogeneity. The authors call for more comprehensive oral language assessment in dyslexia research, which is a fitting charge to begin the special issue.

Where Cabbage and colleagues look to research, Georgan (2026) turned to public policy. Building on the wave of advocacy that has produced dyslexia legislation in 49 of 50 U.S. states, they ask whether existing statutes already make room for a focus on broader oral language. Using deductive and inductive coding, they performed a document analysis of 156 legislative documents to determine how often “language disorder” or comparably broad terms appear, and how language-related keywords are distributed across screening, intervention, preservice preparation, and professional-development requirements. They found that two states explicitly name language difficulties within the scope of dyslexia legislation, an additional eight use vague terms that could encompass it, and 29 of the 49 states include at least one language-related keyword, such as language comprehension and vocabulary. The authors argue that this existing verbiage offers a foothold on which advocates, clinicians, and parents can build from current statutes to press for universal broad oral language screening alongside dyslexia (i.e., word reading) screening, so that children with language difficulties are identified early.

Profiles of dyslexia with and without developmental language disorder (DLD)

If oral language is too often unmeasured, what do we see when we measure it carefully? The next group of articles characterizes the language and literacy profiles of children with dyslexia, with particular attention to the roughly half (Adlof & Hogan, 2018) who also meet criteria for Developmental language disorder (DLD), a spoken language learning disability (McGregor et al., 2020). Snowling, Hayiou-Thomas, and Hulme (2026) draw on a longitudinal study of children at high risk of dyslexia, followed from age 3;6, to compare children classified at age 8 as having dyslexia alone versus dyslexia plus DLD. One year after classification, the dyslexia-only group, although not reaching criteria for DLD, nonetheless showed subclinical broad language weaknesses (e.g., in vocabulary and listening comprehension), weak phonological skills (e.g., phoneme awareness, rapid naming, and nonword repetition), and poor arithmetic. The comorbid group showed far more severe deficits, more than two standard deviations below typical readers across language and reading and, unlike the dyslexia-only group, significant reading comprehension difficulty. Notably, phonological and broader language skills were strongly correlated at the one-year follow-up assessment (age 9), leading the authors to argue against treating these as independent dimensions and toward a view of dyslexia as a language-learning impairment in which two separable but additive risks, one affecting broader oral language, one affecting phonology, combine in cases of comorbidity.

Petersen and colleagues (2026) extend this profiling work from norm-referenced diagnosis to curriculum-based expectations. Reasoning that standardized cut-points may underestimate the breadth of language difficulty in dyslexia, they collected narrative-retell language samples from 183 students with dyslexia in first through sixth grade and evaluated discourse, sentence, and vocabulary complexity against functional benchmarks. The results are striking because the majority of students fell below grade-level expectations at every level of analysis, with 94% to 100% lacking complex sentence structures and 82% to100% failing to incorporate complex vocabulary as expected. Critically, students with dyslexia who did not have DLD performed nearly as poorly as those who did, and by fourth and sixth grade essentially all students were below expected normative standards. Because these data are cross-sectional, however, the pattern across grades should be interpreted with caution. The authors conclude that complex academic language weaknesses in dyslexia are common among students with dyslexia across this age range, though their developmental course and severity likely vary, and that systematic broad oral language screening and targeted language intervention are essential to meeting these students’ educational needs.

Early markers and subtypes that precede and predict reading difficulty

Two articles examine the language markers and subtypes that emerge before, or alongside, the onset of formal reading. Gott and colleagues (2026) investigate whether the distinction between accuracy-disabled and rate-disabled dyslexia can be detected before children learn to read. Hebrew is a particularly informative testing ground for this question: unlike English, Hebrew word formation is built from consonantal roots and vowel patterns that carry core meaning and grammatical information, so that morphological awareness plays an outsized role in early word recognition, alongside phonological awareness. In a longitudinal study of Hebrew-speaking children assessed in preschool on phonological awareness, morphological awareness, and rapid automatized naming (RAN), and followed into first grade, they identified two preschool subgroups: one with selective deficits in phonological and morphological awareness but intact RAN, and one with impaired RAN but preserved awareness. As predicted, the awareness-impaired group showed lower first-grade reading accuracy; the RAN-impaired group showed slow but accurate reading. Their work reinforces the contribution of both phonological and morphological awareness to early word reading and points to a highly specific RAN-rate subtype with implications for how dyslexia is defined and identified. This pattern, in which morphological awareness predicts word reading alongside phonological awareness, dovetails with the revised 2025 International Dyslexia Association definition of dyslexia, which for the first time names morphological processing, alongside phonological processing, as a common underlying source of difficulty in dyslexia (Catts et al., 2026), formally recognizing that word-level reading problems can arise not only from sound-based processing weaknesses but also from difficulty manipulating the meaningful parts of words.

Palser and colleagues (2026) studied children with dyslexia aged 8 to 12 who had (35%) and had not (65%) shown delayed two-word-phrase production as toddlers, 24 months of age. Although both groups showed comparable word- and passage-level reading in middle childhood, children with a history of early spoken-language delay had poorer sentence repetition and memory for words. These findings suggest that there may be distinct language contributions to dyslexia depending on a child’s early language history, and they underscore the value of attending to early spoken-language milestones, long before reading instruction begins, when thinking about dyslexia risk and later reading outcomes.

From detecting language vulnerability to tailoring intervention

The issue closes by moving from characterization to clinical action: how might educators and clinicians efficiently flag the children with dyslexia who carry broader language vulnerability? A pilot study of sentence repetition takes up this question in a sample of 22 fourth-grade students at a specialized school for language-based learning disabilities, all carrying a dyslexia diagnosis. Using a standardized measure of sentence repetition, the authors examined whether performance distinguished students who currently or recently qualified for speech-language services, a proxy for broader oral language difficulty, from those who did not. Students receiving services scored substantially lower than their peers who were not. The authors are appropriately cautious, framing the work as preliminary and associative, but the implication is practical and consequential: a brief, language measure may help detect, within an already dyslexia-identified population, the children whose persistent difficulties reflect unaddressed broader oral language weakness and who may therefore need more comprehensive, language-focused support.

Capin and colleagues (2026) carry this logic one step further, from detecting language vulnerability to acting on it, asking not only whether a language-based intervention works but for whom. Drawing on a sample of 357 elementary students in grades 1 through 4 with language and literacy difficulties, they used latent profile analysis to classify students by their pre-intervention word reading and listening comprehension, then examined how each profile responded to SKILL, an evidence-based narrative language program, at immediate post-test and at a five-month follow-up. Students across all profiles made statistically significant gains in narrative language, the program’s primary target. More revealing were the differences that emerged over time, at follow-up, the students with the greatest listening comprehension and word reading difficulties showed the most notable gains, and response also varied with initial narrative writing performance. The authors are careful to note that their sample was defined by reading and language difficulty rather than by clinical diagnosis, but the practical message dovetails with the rest of the issue. Profiling children’s language and reading at the outset can help educators anticipate who will respond and tailor instruction accordingly, so that the children with the most pervasive language-learning needs are not overlooked.

Conclusion

Taken as a whole, this special issue makes a case that is at once theoretical and practical. Theoretically, the studies collected here support a view of dyslexia not as an isolated disorder of print, walled off from oral language, but as part of a broader, interdependent language system in which phonological and non-phonological skills are tightly linked. Practically, they converge on a clear set of recommendations: account for broader oral language in our research samples; write it into our policies; assess it not only against clinical thresholds but against the curriculum demands children actually face; attend to early language history and subtype; and equip educators and clinicians with efficient tools to detect language vulnerability in the children in front of them.

It is our hope that readers will come away from this special issue persuaded that screening, evaluating, and treating reading difficulties without considering broader oral language is no longer defensible. Whether through assessment, intervention, professional preparation, policy, or advocacy, those who serve children with dyslexia are positioned to ensure that language is brought back to the center of the conversation, where the evidence has always suggested it belongs.
