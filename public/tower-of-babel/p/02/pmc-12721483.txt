Cyp2e1 deletion in mice increases sensitivity to ethanol‐induced motor impairment and escalation of drinking behavior in the chronic intermittent ethanol model
Authors: Hyland C. Gonzalez, Kelly R. Misare, Kate E. Glorioso, Joe R. Delaney, Howard C. Becker, Marcelo F. Lopez, Patrick J. Mulholland, Jessica H. Hartman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic ethanol use drives numerous neurological dysfunctions. Ethanol in the brain is mainly metabolized into acetaldehyde by catalase and cytochrome P450 2E1 (Cyp2e1), and acetaldehyde does not freely cross the blood–brain barrier. While Cyp2e1 is a well‐known enzyme in liver toxicology, how central nervous system (CNS)‐expressed Cyp2e1 contributes to ethanol toxicity is unknown.MethodsWe investigated the effects of Cyp2e1‐driven ethanol metabolism in the brain of male and female global Cyp2e1 knockout (KO) and wild‐type (WT) 129S1/SvImJ mice. RNA‐seq was completed on the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), the dorsal and ventral hippocampus, and cerebellum to see whether there are baseline differences in gene expression between WT and KO mice. To investigate acute functional tolerance (AFT), we evaluated blood ethanol concentrations (BECs) of mice on a fixed‐speed rotarod after two consecutive ethanol doses. Next, we used the chronic intermittent ethanol (CIE) exposure model to study dependence‐induced escalation of drinking. After the study, catalase protein expression was analyzed in the mPFC, hippocampus, and cerebellum.ResultsTranscriptomic pathway analysis in ethanol‐naïve mice revealed differences in ethanol‐important pathways in both male and female Cyp2e1‐KO mice. Glyoxylase‐1 (Glo1) was downregulated in KO animals. Both WT and Cyp2e1‐KO mice had a similar AFT. However, after both injections, Cyp2e1‐KO mice had to reach a lower BEC to balance on the rotarod (p < 0.001), indicating increased sensitivity to ethanol intoxication. In the drinking study, Cyp2e1‐KO mice drank more than WT controls during baseline drinking sessions (p < 0.01, n = 8–9). After CIE exposure, only Cyp2e1‐KO mice significantly escalated their drinking (p < 0.001, n = 16–17). Catalase levels were not significantly higher in KO mice in the brain regions studied regardless of condition.ConclusionsThis study reveals an important role for Cyp2e1 in ethanol‐related behaviors and highlights a need to better understand the effects of ethanol and its metabolites in mediating ethanol drinking and sensitivity.

INTRODUCTION

Ethanol use can alter molecular processes in all tissues, in part because of its rapid and extensive distribution upon exposure (Egervari et al., 2021). When individuals drink large amounts of ethanol regularly, adaptive changes in the brain increase the risk for developing alcohol use disorder (AUD), characterized by an inability to stop drinking. Chronic excessive alcohol (ethanol) consumption has been linked to neurological dysfunction (de la Monte & Kril, 2014), and it can contribute to neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's (Anton et al., 2025), dementia (Langballe et al., 2015; Schwarzinger et al., 2018), and Parkinson's (Eriksson et al., 2013) disease.

Although many of these neurological effects are attributed to ethanol itself, metabolites of ethanol may also contribute to its behavioral, neurobiological, and toxicological effects. Ethanol is metabolized through three different enzymatic pathways in the body: alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) in the cytosol of cells, catalase (CAT) in the peroxisomes, and cytochrome P450 2E1 (CYP2E1) (Zakhari, 2006) in the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria. All three of these pathways convert ethanol to acetaldehyde, with ADH being the most efficient due to a lower K m for ethanol. Acetaldehyde is thought to be the primary driver of ethanol‐related toxicity (Ceni et al., 2014), and can be detoxified through aldehyde dehydrogenase enzymes (ALDH), mainly ALDH2 localized in the mitochondria. Minor metabolic pathways for ethanol include phase II conjugation to ethyl glucuronide and ethyl sulfate. The liver is the major site of ethanol metabolism but reports as early as the 1950s indicated that ethanol metabolism also occurs extrahepatically (Larsen, 1959). It is estimated that the liver contributes ~90% to ethanol metabolism, while the remaining ~10% is distributed among other tissues, including the intestine, lung, brain, and other tissues (Boleda et al., 1989).

CYP2E1 is thought to be a minor contributor to the overall clearance of ethanol (Lieber & Decarli, 1970). However, surprisingly, acetaldehyde levels are significantly reduced in the blood and liver of Cyp2e1‐null mice compared with wild‐type mice (Vasiliou et al., 2006). Furthermore, extrahepatic production of acetaldehyde in tissues that do not express ethanol‐metabolizing isoforms of ADH may provide an expanded role for CYP2E1 in the brain (Zimatkin et al., 2006). Additionally, high expression of aldehyde dehydrogenase at the blood–brain barrier limits acetaldehyde in circulation from entering the brain, further supporting the role of CYP2E1 and catalase in local acetaldehyde production in the brain (Deitrich et al., 2006).

CYP2E1 is a unique P450 isoform with a small binding pocket, limiting its substrates structurally to low molecular weight hydrophobic compounds, including endogenous molecules, drugs, and pollutants (Miller, 2008). CYP2E1 is well‐known to be induced by ethanol (Tsutsumi et al., 1993), particularly in the liver. It is important to note that CYP2E1 mRNA expression is not a reliable predictor of protein and activity levels due to post‐transcriptional regulation (Novak & Woodcroft, 2000). In the brain, induction of CYP2E1 may be region‐specific, with several studies demonstrating higher (typically ~twofold increased) protein and activity levels in the hippocampus, olfactory bulb, frontal cortex, cerebellum, and brainstem that varied with experimental design and timing of endpoints (Anandatheerthavarada et al., 1993; Howard et al., 2003; Yadav et al., 2006; Zhong et al., 2012). These data suggest that the brain, like the liver, is subject to ethanol‐induced elevations in the ethanol‐metabolizing enzyme CYP2E1.

Given the role of CYP2E1 in acetaldehyde production and its elevation in the brain by ethanol administration, previous studies have employed the global CYP2E1 knockout (KO) mouse developed by Dr. Frank Gonzalez (Lee et al., 1996). Compared with CYP2E1‐expressing wild‐type animals, male and female CYP2E1 KO mice were demonstrated to have compensatory upregulation of catalase in the telencephalon and cerebellum (Correa et al., 2009), and they displayed no differences in ethanol elimination (Vasiliou et al., 2006). These mice also produced less acetaldehyde in the brain, were more sensitive to ethanol‐induced sleep times (Vasiliou et al., 2006), and showed reduced ethanol preference in a voluntary consumption model at intermediate concentrations (Correa et al., 2009). These results demonstrate that CYP2E1 has a role in contributing to biochemical and behavioral ethanol responses.

In this study, we performed mRNA‐sequencing analysis on brain tissue (cerebellum, medial prefrontal cortex [mPFC], and hippocampus) in ethanol‐naïve wildtype and CYP2E1 KO mice to better understand the impact of CYP2E1 deletion in the brain. Based on the results of the RNA‐seq analysis, we further investigated the impact of CYP2E1 deletion on acute functional tolerance (AFT) using the rotarod test and baseline voluntary drinking and drinking escalation in the chronic intermittent ethanol (CIE) model. We selected the CIE exposure model as the field standard to compare effects on moderate stable levels of intake with escalated drinking displayed following CIE exposure (Becker & Lopez, 2004; Lopez & Becker, 2005). Together, the results of this study provide new insight into the role of CYP2E1 in behavioral response to ethanol (ataxia) and drinking behavior.

METHODS

Mouse husbandry

Male and female 129S1/SvImJ (Jax #002448) and 129/Sv‐Cyp2e1 tm1Gonz /J KO (Jax #002910) mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratories and group‐housed with littermates (n = 2–5 mice/cage) until used for experiments. Mice had unrestricted access to rodent food (Diet #2918; Harlan Teklad, Madison, WI, USA) and water throughout the experiments. The Medical University of South Carolina Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved all procedures in accordance with NIH guidelines for the humane care and use of laboratory animals.

RNA sequencing

Three 16‐ to 17‐week‐old mice from each genotype and sex were sacrificed via carbon dioxide gas and cervical dislocation. Brains were extracted and the mPFC (including portions of the anterior cingulate, prelimbic cortex, and infralimbic cortices), the dorsal and ventral hippocampus, and the anterior vermis of the cerebellum were isolated and flash frozen for RNA isolation. RNA isolation was completed using the RNeasy mini kit instructions (no.: 74106; Qiagen), including the optional on‐column DNase I treatment and the optional re‐elution step. RNA quantity was assessed, and 11 μL of each sample was aliquoted and shipped to NovoGene for analysis. RNA quality check (yield, purity, and integrity), cDNA library construction, and Illumina sequencing were performed by NovoGene (Sacramento, CA, USA). Raw data of fastq format were processed by Novogene to remove adapter reads and low‐quality reads. The clean paired‐end reads were then mapped to the reference genome (mm39) using Hisat2 v2.0.5. Mapped reads were assembled by StringTie (v1.3.3b) and featureCounts v1.5.0‐p3 was used to count the reads numbers mapped to each gene. Finally, the FPKM of each gene was calculated based on gene length and reads counts. Differential expression within male and female samples was performed using the DESeq2 R package (1.20.0). Genes were considered as differentially expressed if they had an absolute log2fold change of >0.5 and q (adjusted p‐value) <0.05. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was performed using the easy Visualization and Inference Toolbox for Transcriptome Analysis (eVITTA) and uses up‐to‐date and species‐specific databases for gene ontology. Pathways were considered to be enriched if the q (adjusted p‐value) <0.05. Raw RNA‐seq data have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information's GEO and are accessible through GSE307546.

Acute functional tolerance test

Six 13‐week‐old mice from each sex and genotype were weighed and then subjected to the fixed‐speed rotarod challenge to assess AFT. Initially, mice were trained to walk on a 3 cm‐diameter rotarod at 10 rpm for 60 s. All mice achieved success within one to two training trials. After successfully walking for 60 s without falling off, mice were given an i.p. injection of 2.5 g/kg ethanol (v/v) in a sterile saline solution (10 mL/kg) and placed on the rotating rod every 5 min until they were able to walk for 60 s. Time spent on the rod and time it took to complete the task were both recorded. Upon completion of the task, blood ethanol concentration (BEC) was measured by collecting blood (retro‐orbital sinus), centrifuging it at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to separate out the plasma, and measuring the ethanol concentration of the serum with the Analox system (Analox Instruments, Lunenburg, MA, USA). After the blood collection, mice were immediately given a second injection of 1.25 g/kg ethanol in sterile saline and made to repeat the task until completion. After successfully walking for 60 s, a second BEC was measured. The measurement of AFT was obtained by subtracting the first BEC from the second BEC (BEC2‐BEC1). The protocol is summarized in Figure 6A.

Modeling ethanol dependence with chronic intermittent exposure (CIE)

Male and female 10‐week‐old wildtype and CYP2E1 KO mice were single housed (n = 16 per sex, genotype except for male KO mice where n = 17). Mice were introduced to ethanol through sucrose fading over 2 weeks in a two‐bottle choice format for 2 h/day, 5 days/week, starting 3 h after lights off. To start, one bottle contained water and one contained 15% (v/v) ethanol, 5% (w/v) sucrose solution. The sucrose was gradually reduced to a final concentration of 15% ethanol, 2% sucrose. Water and ethanol bottle orientation were alternated to avoid a side preference. Reducing sucrose to 0% resulted in negligible drinking, particularly in the male mice, so 2% sucrose was maintained throughout the rest of the study. Bottles were weighed before and after the drinking session, and two additional “spill” bottles were placed in empty cages for the same amount of time and used as a control for the drinking bottles. After completion of sucrose fading, mice underwent a 3‐week period of baseline drinking with 15% ethanol/2% sucrose and water as the alternative fluid (2 h/day, 5 days/week). Bottles were weighed before and after each session along with “spill” bottles. Mice were then randomized into air‐control or ethanol vapor groups for chronic intermittent exposure (CIE) based on their baseline drinking. This resulted in eight mice per sex/genotype/treatment, except for the male Cyp2e1‐KO CIE treatment group, which had nine mice. On the Monday following the last baseline drinking session, mice underwent a weekly cycle of inhalation exposure to either air or ethanol vapor in chambers for 16 h/day followed by 8 h of withdrawal for 4 days a week. On the following Monday after each week of air or ethanol vapor exposure, mice were given access to two‐bottle choice drinking for 5 days/week. Exposure to ethanol vapor (or air) followed by a week of test drinking sessions was repeated for a total of three cycles (or 6 total weeks). Upon removal from the chambers, BEC was measured using an Analox Analyzer (Analox Instruments) to ensure the mice were reaching a BEC of at least 175 mg/mL. At the end of the third week of post‐CIE drinking, mice were sacrificed 24 h after their final drinking session via rapid decapitation. Brains were extracted and the mPFC (containing anterior cingulate, infralimbic cortex, and prelimbic cortex), dorsal and ventral hippocampus, and cerebellum were isolated and flash frozen for protein quantification. The method is summarized in Figure 7A.

Homogenizing brain tissues

Brain regions isolated from the drinking study were weighed and homogenized in 10 times volume RIPA lysis buffer (catalog no.: 89900; Thermo Scientific) with added phosphatase and protease inhibitors (catalog no.: 78442; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The mPFC and hippocampal sections were processed in 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes with appropriately sized pestles (no.: 1415‐5390; USA Scientific), and cerebellar samples were processed in 5 mL glass Dounce homogenizers (no.: 357538; Duran Wheaton Kimble) and transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes. Homogenates were spun at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, aliquots were taken to measure total protein concentration with a BCA assay (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, no.: 23227; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and both the pellet and the supernatant were frozen for later use.

Measuring catalase levels in brain section homogenates

Samples were thawed on ice and diluted to 0.2 mg/mL in 0.1× sample buffer (made from 10× Sample Buffer 2 from the 12–230 kDA Separation Module [no.: SM‐W004; ProteinSimple, Minneapolis, MN, USA] and one‐fifth of the total volume of 5× master mix [made from EZ Standard Pack 1, no.: PS‐ST01EZ‐8; ProteinSimple]). Tubes were vortexed and denatured at 95°C for 5 min. Tubes were again vortexed and briefly centrifuged. Samples were loaded into a 25‐well Jess Simple Western plate along with 10 μg/mL catalase primary antibody (no.: AF3398; R & D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and ready‐to‐use goat secondary antibody (no.: 043–522‐2; ProteinSimple). Total protein was also detected using the Total Protein Detection Module (no.: DM‐TP01; ProteinSimple) and used to normalize catalase levels.

Statistical analysis of the BECs, recovery time, and AFT from the rotarod experiments, as well as catalase expression was completed via two‐way ANOVA with multiple comparisons using GraphPad Prism with an α value of 0.05.

RESULTS

Deletion of CYP2E1 in mice differentially impacted ethanol‐relevant pathways in ethanol‐naïve mice

Based on previous literature demonstrating brain regions that are important for ethanol drinking behavior and are known to express ethanol‐inducible CYP2E1, we performed RNA‐sequencing analysis on mPFC, dorsal hippocampus (dHC), ventral hippocampus (vHC), and cerebellum (CB). This germline CYP2E1‐KO was created by replacing exon 2 of the gene with the neomycin resistance cassette, and therefore with an intact transcriptional start site is still able to produce mRNA, but not protein (Lee et al., 1996). Interestingly, in all regions, we saw higher counts of the Cyp2e1 mRNA in the KO compared with the WT, but closer inspection of the reads confirmed that none mapped to exon 2 (Figure S1). To our knowledge, this is the first RNA‐sequencing data published using this model, so previous efforts (Northern blot, qPCR) may have been designed to target exon 2 and thus not observed increased mRNA levels (as we report in Figure S2). Increased mRNA expression in KO brain may suggest that there is pressure to produce Cyp2e1 mRNA. Full output of the differentially expressed genes and significantly enriched pathways for all brain regions are given in Tables S3 (DEG), S4 and S5 (Pathways). Starting with the mPFC (Figure 1A), male mice had upregulated mRNA processing and extracellular matrix genes (Figure 1B,D), and downregulated ATP metabolism, locomotion, dopamine transport and metabolism, and other metabolic pathways (Figure 1E). By contrast, female mice had upregulated pathways in learning and memory, cognition, locomotion, dopamine signaling, and neuron, glia, and astrocyte projections (Figure 1C,F). Female mice had downregulated mPFC axon development, synaptic transmission, neurotransmitter transport, cognition, and Wnt signaling (Figure 1G).

We examined dorsal and ventral aspects of the hippocampus separately based on previous reports for diverging roles controlling ethanol‐related behavior (Griffin et al., 2023; Rezayof et al., 2008). In the dHC (Figure 2A), like in the mPFC, male mice had upregulated mRNA processing and extracellular matrix genes (Figure 2B,D). Upregulated pathways in females included neurotransmitter transport, locomotory behavior, lipid transport, and axonogenesis (Figure 2C,F). Both male and female mice had downregulated cognition, learning, and memory pathways, as well as downregulated neurotransmitter activity (Figure 2E,G). The female mice specifically had downregulation of GABAA pathways (Figure 2G).

In the vHC (Figure 3A), male mice had upregulation of pathways related to mRNA processing and splicing and neurotransmitter transport (Figure 3B,D), and few downregulated pathways related to extracellular matrix and ribosome (Figure 3E). Females had upregulation of pathways involved in ion transport, axon, learning and memory, cognition, and synaptic activity (Figure 3C,F), and downregulation of pathways involved in postsynaptic activity, GABAA signaling, learning and memory, dopamine/catecholamine, extracellular matrix, and respiration (Figure 3G).

Finally, in the CB (Figure 4A), males had upregulation in RNA splicing, chromatin modification, DNA repair, circadian rhythm, and sex chromosome pathways (Figure 4B,D), and downregulation of axon development, signaling pathways, ethanol response, cocaine response, Wnt signaling, cognition, learning and memory, locomotion, and dopamine pathways (Figure 4E). In females, CYP2E1‐KO animals had upregulated cerebellar RNA splicing, mRNA processing and extracellular matrix pathways (Figure 4C,F), and downregulated axon ensheathment, gliogenesis, learning and memory, locomotion, cognition, Wnt signaling, and cell–cell junction pathways (Figure 4G).

Overall, we observed several common pathways that were altered by Cyp2e1 deletion among the different brain regions, including upregulation of mRNA processing and splicing (especially in male mice) and downregulation of locomotion, learning and memory, dopamine pathways, and neurotransmitter signaling pathways.

Top differentially expressed genes in all regions by CYP2E1‐KO revealed a common gene set

We also noticed when looking at the differentially expressed genes that some common genes were highly differentially regulated in all brain regions between WT and KO mice, and upon further examination we found that they share a similar pathway (Figure 5). Glo1, or glyoxylase‐1, is an enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of methylglyoxal to S‐lactoylglutathione and was dramatically repressed in CYP2E1‐KO mice of both sexes in all brain regions (Figure 5B,G,L,Q). CYP2E1 is known to produce methylglyoxal from the ketone body acetone. A decrease in methylglyoxal formation due to KO‐mediated reduction of functional CYP2E1 would be consistent with the observed decreased transcription of Glo1 to process the decreased methylglyoxal. Gm10503 is a long noncoding RNA that has been previously grouped with Glo1 as distinguishing genes between M1 and M2 macrophages (Coates et al., 2008), and it is completely lost in CYP2E1‐KO in all brain regions (Figure 5C,H,M,R). Klhl8, or Kelch‐like protein 8, is not well described but is predicted to be involved in ubiquitin‐mediated protein degradation and has been previously identified with Glo1 as downregulated by anti‐CD3 (Costello et al., 2010). We found Klhl8 to be upregulated in all brain regions (Figure 5D,I,N,S). Glo1 was found to interact directly with Nudt9 in a yeast two‐hybrid experiment (Zhang et al., 2003), and Nudt9 was upregulated in CYP2E1‐KO in all brain regions (Figure 5E,J,O,T). We found this gene set to be of particular interest, as Glo1 inhibition has been reported to attenuate drinking in mice and rats, and overexpression of Glo1 in mice increased ethanol withdrawal seizure susceptibility (Barkley‐Levenson et al., 2021). Based on these findings, we wondered if depletion of Cyp2e1 in mice would influence their acute tolerance or drinking behavior.

CYP2E1 KO mice were more sensitive to ethanol exposure

A two‐dose AFT test was used to determine the impact of CYP2E1 deletion on tolerance, adapted from previous studies (Blednov et al., 2021). We found that BECs were significantly lower in both male and female KO mice compared with WT mice at the time of successfully walking on the rotarod following the first injection of ethanol (2.5 g/kg) (Figure 6B, Table S1, p < 0.05). The BECs were also significantly lower in males than females after the first injection task completion (Figure 6B, Table S1, p < 0.05). After the second injection of ethanol (1.25 g/kg), BECs were significantly lower again in KO versus WT mice within each sex (Figure 6C, Table S1, p < 0.001); males and females did not differ at this time point. Thus, after both injections, KO mice had to consistently reach a lower BEC to complete the task as compared to WT animals, indicating an increased sensitivity. There were no significant differences across genotype in recovery time (Figure 6D, Table S1) nor in AFT (Figure 6E, Table S1). BECs and recovery times for individual mice are shown in Figure S4. Latency‐to‐fall times for individual mice during the first injection are shown in Figure S5 and the second injection in Figure S6. Together, these results suggest that mice lacking CYP2E1 had to reach a lower blood ethanol level to successfully balance on the rotarod but still gained AFT comparable to the wild‐type mice. Given the altered sensitivity to acute ethanol injections, we next wanted to measure drinking behavior in the CYP2E1 KO mice.

CYP2E1 deletion increased drinking behavior

Data over the 3 weeks of baseline drinking were analyzed via ANOVA with genotype (WT, KO) and sex as main factors. The results from the ANOVA indicated that CYP2E1 KO mice consumed more ethanol than wildtype mice (main effect of Genotype: F 1,61 = 10.701, p < 0.01; Figure 7B). Consistent with higher ethanol drinking in other strains of female mice (Meliska et al., 1995), female mice consumed significantly more ethanol than males during baseline (main effect of Sex: F 1,61 = 44.326, p < 0.0001; Figure 7B). The ANOVA showed no interaction between sex and genotype during baseline. Analysis of drinking data after CIE exposure was analyzed via ANOVA with genotype (WT, KO), sex, and group (CTL, CIE) as main factors. The ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of sex where female mice consumed more ethanol than males, regardless of genotype (F 1,57 = 48.230, p < 0.0001), and a significant effect of group (F 1,57 = 19.441, p < 0.0001) due to overall higher intake in CIE mice compared with CTL. Notably, the ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between treatment group and genotype (F 1,57 = 5.116, p < 0.05; Figure 7C). Post hoc analyses revealed that ethanol dependence did not alter drinking in WT mice (CTL vs. CIE; p = 0.134). In contrast, CIE exposure significantly increased drinking in KO mice compared with dependent WT mice (p < 0.05) and nondependent KO controls (p < 0.001). Baseline and post‐CIE ethanol consumption averages for individual mice are shown in Figure S7. These results suggest that ethanol drinking is influenced by CYP2E1 during baseline, and CYP2E1 KO facilitated drinking in a model of ethanol dependence.

Catalase expression is not increased in CYP2E1‐KO brain tissues

In order to determine how catalase may be contributing to the observed changes in drinking behavior, we measured catalase levels in different brain regions by western blot (Figure 8A–D). When comparing genotype and treatment within each sex and brain region, we did not observe any compensatory upregulation of catalase. Catalase levels were only slightly but significantly different within the mPFC for males. In the male mPFC, catalase expression was significantly lower in CYP2E1 KO mice compared with wildtype animals within the air‐exposed (control) group (Figure 8E, Table S2, p < 0.05), and catalase expression was also significantly lower in the wildtype mice that were chronically exposed to ethanol (CIE group) as compared to the controls (Figure 8E, p < 0.01). There was no significant difference in catalase expression in the male hippocampus (Figure 8F, Table S2). There were no significant differences in catalase expression in the male cerebellum (Figure 8G, Table S2). Within the female PFC, there was a main effect of genotype (p < 0.05, Table S2) but not a significant interaction or main effect of treatment (Figure 8H, Table S2). There were no significant differences in catalase expression in the female hippocampus (Figure 8I, Table S2) and cerebellum (Figure 8J, Table S2). Together, these results indicate that catalase is present at similar or lower levels in KO compared with WT animals in the brain regions tested.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrated that global CYP2E1 deletion results in transcriptional changes across multiple ethanol‐relevant brain regions, including the mPFC, dorsal and ventral hippocampus, and cerebellum. Behaviorally, we observed increased sensitivity to ethanol‐induced ataxia in the context of acute ethanol exposure with no corresponding increase in AFT. We then used the chronic intermittent ethanol (CIE) exposure model and observed increased ethanol consumption at baseline and increased escalation of drinking after CIE in the KO mice compared with the WT mice. This altered drinking behavior occurred without catalase upregulation in the prefrontal cortex, the hippocampus, and the cerebellum. Together, these results provide new insight into the role of CYP2E1 in ethanol use.

Under acute ethanol exposure, global CYP2E1 KO mice had to reach a lower BEC to recover balance in a fixed‐speed rotarod task, indicating a greater ethanol sensitivity. This aligns with previous findings that found CYP2E1 KO mice had increased sleep times in a loss of righting reflex test compared with WT mice, while having no differences in ethanol clearance (Vasiliou et al., 2006). We have considered possible explanations for this increased sensitivity to ethanol‐induced loss of motor coordination. An important consideration in these experiments is that alcohol was administered via i.p. injection, which bypasses first‐pass metabolism in the liver and may potentially make the role of brain Cyp2e1 more important. The increased sensitivity could be due to reduced clearance of ethanol in the brain, leading to locally higher levels that exert a stronger inhibitory effect on coordination and locomotion. Previous studies have found no differences in ethanol clearance rates from the blood (Vasiliou et al., 2006), but in our study, the KO mice reached a lower BEC in roughly the same amount of time, raising the possibility that there are differences in clearance. Nonetheless, if the KO mice had higher clearance and a similar sensitivity to ethanol‐induced motor impairment, they would have recovered significantly faster and completed the task sooner at a similar BEC to the WT mice. An alternative explanation is that the higher sensitivity could be due to an overall decrease in acetaldehyde in the brain due to the absence of CYP2E1 and no compensatory increase in catalase levels. Previous studies have found a role for acetaldehyde in the dose‐dependent stimulatory and inhibitory locomotory effects of ethanol. Intraperitoneal injection of acetaldehyde to C57BL/6 mice produced reduced locomotion and loss of righting reflex at higher doses (Quertemont et al., 2004). Direct injection of acetaldehyde into the brain was shown to induce locomotor stimulant effects in rats (Arizzi‐LaFrance et al., 2006; Correa et al., 2003), but caused inhibition of locomotion in mice (Tambour et al., 2006). Pharmacological inhibition of ALDH to drive accumulation of acetaldehyde prevented the locomotory stimulatory effects of ethanol (Escarabajal & Aragon, 2002; Tambour et al., 2007), although the interpretation of these experiments is tricky due to a lack of specificity of the inhibitors used. Cyanamide is an inhibitor of both ALDH2 and catalase (Tambour et al., 2007), as well as an inhibitor of CYP2E1 (Yao & Abdel‐Rahman, 2020). Fomepizole, or 4‐methylpyrazole, is a potent inducer of CYP2E1 (Wu & Cederbaum, 1993) and a potent competitive inhibitor of both ADH (Li & Theorell, 1969) and CYP2E1 (Hartman et al., 2014). Nonetheless, it seems that acetaldehyde levels play some role in the dose‐dependent locomotory effects of ethanol and could explain the differential sensitivity in the KO mice.

Additionally, we acknowledge that mouse size and locomotor activity can potentially impact rotarod performance. For the male mice, Cyp2e1‐KO animals did weigh significantly less than their WT counterparts (Figure S3, p < 0.0001), but there were no significant differences between the genotypes for female mice. As recovery times are similar across genotypes, differences in weight and locomotor activity do not appear to contribute to the differences in BECs in the AFT assay.

It should also be noted that in the absence of ethanol, CYP2E1 KO mice have been previously shown to have significantly reduced locomotion at baseline in an open field test (Correa et al., 2009). We also have observed decreased locomotion in the CYP2E1‐KO mice (unpublished observations), and this finding is supported by the RNA‐sequencing data presented here reporting downregulation in the transcriptional pathways related to locomotion in the dorsal hippocampus and cerebellum in the KO mice. In that study, they used a lower dose of ethanol (1.5 g/kg) to stimulate locomotion, and saw a similar increase in locomotion in both KO and WT animals (Correa et al., 2009). Therefore, the loss of CYP2E1 seems to impact the sedative‐depressant effects of ethanol at higher doses more than the stimulatory effects at lower doses. Despite reductions in locomotor activity, KO mice consumed more ethanol during baseline and showed an escalation of drinking following induction of dependence.

In the CIE exposure model of dependence, the CYP2E1 KO mice escalated their drinking after CIE, unlike their WT counterparts. Female mice overall drank more than males, which aligns with drinking data from C57BL/6 mice (Rivera‐Irizarry et al., 2023). Because the CYP2E1‐KO mice are on a 129S1/SvImJ background, the mice were less inclined to voluntarily drink ethanol (Yoneyama et al., 2008), and we had to include 2% sucrose in the ethanol in order to promote drinking. We cannot rule out the impact of sucrose in the drinking escalation behavior; however, CYP2E1 does not metabolize sugars. Furthermore, CYP2E1‐KO mice are not known to have a greater seeking behavior for sugar or fat, with no difference in food intake of a high‐fat diet or fast‐food diet (Western diet) fed ad libitum as compared to WT controls (Abdelmegeed et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2022). Therefore, we believe that the escalation is due to a lack of ethanol metabolism in CYP2E1‐KO mice. Given that many of the negative effects of ethanol are due to the toxic metabolite acetaldehyde produced by CYP2E1 and other pathways, the lack of CYP2E1 may prevent negative reinforcement of ethanol drinking through toxicity. Brain acetaldehyde may be especially impacted by global CYP2E1 deletion because peripherally produced acetaldehyde should be at least partially blocked by high ALDH2 expression at the blood–brain barrier. In agreement with this notion, it was recently demonstrated that Adh1 deletion in mice (inhibiting liver clearance of ethanol) increases levels of ethanol in the blood, liver, and cerebellum with a concomitant decrease in acetaldehyde in the blood and liver, but not the cerebellum. In that study, they observed decreased drinking behavior in the ADH1‐KO mice despite similar brain acetaldehyde levels, which they suggest may be due to a dramatic decrease in blood, liver, and cerebellar acetate levels in the ADH1‐KO compared with WT (Mackowiak et al., 2025). This could suggest a complex interplay between signals promoting and inhibiting drinking behavior mediated by downstream ethanol metabolites.

When examining catalase levels in the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum, we saw very minor differences in catalase expression comparing WT and KO animals. In all cases where there were statistically significant differences, catalase expression was lower in KO animals. Lower catalase levels in the KO mice compared with the WT mice contradict previous findings that catalase had compensatory increased expression in both the brains and the livers of CYP2E1 global KO mice (Correa et al., 2009). Those measurements were taken from the telencephalon and the whole cerebellum, while our measurements were taken from more specific brain regions, including a subdivision of the cerebellum. These differences could indicate that the upregulation seen previously comes from a region of the brain we did not measure. Additionally, we did not measure catalase expression in the liver in this study.

It is interesting that the canonical Wnt pathway was downregulated in multiple brain regions in CYP2E1‐KO mice. The Wnt/β‐catenin pathway has been connected to CYP2E1 in the liver in mice in vivo (Yang et al., 2014), in primary human hepatocytes in vitro (Gerbal‐Chaloin et al., 2014; Groll et al., 2016), and in the context of alcoholic liver injury (Xu et al., 2019), metabolic dysfunction‐associated liver disease (Chen et al., 2021) and hepatocellular carcinoma (Zhu et al., 2022). The role of CYP2E1/Wnt/β‐catenin in regulating drinking behavior and/or ethanol effects remains to be elucidated, but a previous study found that chronic drinking could alter the Wnt signaling pathway in the PFC (Liu et al., 2022). Furthermore, the Wnt signaling pathway is well known to be dysregulated in neurodegenerative diseases (Serafino et al., 2020).

We were also interested in the finding that the dicarboxylic acid pathway was significantly downregulated in the CYP2E1‐KO male PFC. In addition to metabolizing acetone, CYP2E1 can also metabolize fatty acids at the omega and omega‐1 positions (Adas et al., 1998, 1999). The fate of these ω(−1) hydroxy fatty acids is to become dicarboxylic acids, which act as a nonstorable energy source for tissues and may also act as signaling molecules. When administered to mice with a high‐fat diet, dicarboxylic acids reversed some of the deleterious hepatic and organismal effects of the high‐fat diet (Angelini et al., 2024; Goetzman et al., 2024). Ethanol use inhibits the mitochondrial β‐oxidation of fatty acids and increases ω‐oxidation (Ma et al., 1993), which results in increased dicarboxylic acid levels in urine in human studies (Ma et al., 1999). The shift to ω‐oxidation was more pronounced in males in both the human and rat studies, and we only saw this pathway significantly impacted in the male mPFC, suggesting a potential sex difference for this pathway.

We found that one of the most consistently differentially regulated genes between alcohol‐naïve WT and KO mice regardless of region and sex was Glo1, which codes for the enzyme glyoxalase 1, the initial enzyme in the methylglyoxal detoxification pathway. Methylglyoxal can be formed nonenzymatically as a byproduct of glycolysis, but CYP2E1 can also form methylglyoxal through a two‐step process from the ketone body acetone. Glo1 has previously been investigated in the context of ethanol. Overexpression of Glo1 in mice resulted in increased voluntary ethanol consumption in a drinking in the dark (DID) model, and both deletion and inhibition result in decreased voluntary drinking (McMurray et al., 2017). Inhibition in rats also decreased ethanol consumption in an operant self‐administration model (de Guglielmo et al., 2018) However, this effect does not seem to result in downstream changes in locomotor activity (Barkley‐Levenson et al., 2018; McMurray et al., 2017). There is also some evidence that Glo1 mediates susceptibility to seizures from ethanol withdrawal (Barkley‐Levenson et al., 2021). The impacts of Glo1 modulation on ethanol drinking and seizure susceptibility have been attributed to its role in controlling methylglyoxal levels. High methylglyoxal resulting from the loss of Glo1 is thought to agonize the GABAA receptor, resulting in inhibition of drinking. We observed a dramatic downregulation of Glo1 in the CYP2E1‐KO animals, which could be predictive of a decrease in drinking behavior based on the previously published findings. However, if CYP2E1 production of methylglyoxal represents a major source of methylglyoxal in the brain, perhaps the deletion of CYP2E1 had a larger effect of depleting brain methylglyoxal. This concept is supported by the downregulation of GABAA pathways we observed in the dorsal and ventral hippocampus in female ethanol‐naïve CYP2E1‐KO mice. Further studies are needed to characterize the molecular connections between CYP2E1, Glo1, methylglyoxal, and acetaldehyde in the brain, but our study provides the first evidence for this possible mechanism.

Our study had important assumptions and limitations. First, we were not able to distinguish between brain CYP2E1 and peripheral CYP2E1 due to the use of a global deletion mouse model. We assume that due to high acetaldehyde dehydrogenase activity at the blood–brain barrier and in astrocytes, the majority of acetaldehyde in the brain would be produced locally by CYP2E1 and catalase. However, it is known that chronic ethanol use induces leakiness of the blood–brain barrier (Wei et al., 2021), and we and others have not been able to distinguish acetaldehyde produced locally in the brain from peripheral acetaldehyde. We also did not measure ethanol clearance/PK in our study, as we did not expect any differences between the genotypes based on previous studies. In future studies, new models are needed to distinguish the effects of brain CYP2E1 from liver/intestinal CYP2E1 in mediating behavioral and biochemical effects of ethanol.

Overall, this study provides evidence for a role for CYP2E1 in drinking behavior and ethanol sensitivity, an enzyme that has been studied in far greater detail for its role in ethanol‐induced liver injury. We reported brain region‐specific transcriptional effects of global CYP2E1 deletion and behavioral impacts of the loss of CYP2E1 in the context of acute ethanol sensitivity and drinking escalation in the CIE model. Future studies are needed to further define the connection between CYP2E1 and Wnt/β‐catenin, Glo1, and fatty acid metabolism in the brain.
