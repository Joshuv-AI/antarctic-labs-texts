Research on the application of debriefing in simulation-based education of surgical nursing
Authors: Xi Cao, Min Wang, Ye Huang, Qiu-Min Hu, Rong Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the application of debriefing in simulation-based education of surgical nursing.MethodA self-controlled before-and-after study was conducted among third-year nursing students (8 classes) in a 12-hour surgical nursing simulation course. The first 6-hour session in the initial semester served as the control condition (conventional teaching), while the subsequent 6-hour session in the following semester served as the experimental condition (debriefing teaching). Outcomes including theoretical scores, critical thinking disposition, general self-efficacy, and student evaluations of the debriefing were compared between the two phases.ResultsIn the experimental phase, theoretical exam scores, critical thinking disposition scores, and general self-efficacy scores were (73.92 ± 7.40), (298.11 ± 52.68), and (3.20 ± 0.60), respectively, compared to (65.81 ± 9.42), (228.41 ± 20.60), and (2.82 ± 0.55) in the control phase. The differences between the two phases were statistically significant (P < 0.001). Student evaluations suggested that the majority of students in the experimental phase had a positive attitude towards this teaching method.ConclusionThis self-controlled study indicated that the integration of debriefing into simulation-based education in surgical nursing was associated with higher theoretical knowledge, critical thinking disposition, self-efficacy, and learning satisfaction.

Introduction

Simulation-based education has become a cornerstone of modern nursing education, providing a safe environment for students to develop clinical skills and decision-making abilities. This teaching method typically encompasses three phases: pre-briefing, scenario simulation, and debriefing [1, 2]. Among these, debriefing is widely recognized as a critical component of the learning process, functioning as a reflective activity through which students analyze their performance and consolidate knowledge [3–5].

The efficacy of debriefing is well-established in the international literature. Evidence demonstrates its positive impact across various medical disciplines, including emergency medicine, surgery, and anesthesiology. For instance, debriefing after cardiac arrest resuscitation has been associated with greater compliance with guidelines and better patient outcomes, leading to its endorsement by the American Heart Association [6]. Consequently, omitting debriefing after simulation or experiential learning is considered a substandard practice [7, 8]. The focus of debriefing research has thus evolved from establishing its utility to optimizing its delivery, with significant attention to faculty development [9–11] and the integration of technological innovations [12, 13].

Despite this global momentum, the application of debriefing in nursing education in China is still in its nascent stages. A comprehensive review of the literature reveals a scarcity of empirical studies focusing on the implementation and evaluation of standardized debriefing frameworks within mainland China’s nursing education system. This gap underscores an urgent need for localized, empirical studies to explore and validate effective debriefing practices within China’s unique educational context.

Therefore, this study aims to address the research gap in the Chinese context by implementing a structured debriefing intervention within a surgical nursing simulation course for undergraduates. Grounded in Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle (ELC), this study will employ the PEARLS (Promoting Excellence and Reflective Learning in Simulation) framework [14] to operationalize the debriefing process. This study is designed to evaluate the effectiveness of this theory-driven debriefing intervention and seeks to answer the following questions:

How do we apply the structured and standardized debriefing in simulation-based education of surgical nursing?Does the implementation of this structured, theory-driven debriefing show differences in students’ critical thinking disposition scores and general self-efficacy scores compared to conventional teaching summaries?

Subjects and methods

Research subjects

A self-controlled before-and-after study was conducted with third-year, full-time undergraduate nursing students from eight classes in a college in Sichuan Province, China. All participants were enrolled in a standard four-year program. The study was implemented within a 12-session surgical nursing simulation course. The first 6-hour session in the initial semester served as the control group (conventional teaching), while the subsequent 6-hour session in the following semester implemented debriefing as the experimental group (debriefing teaching). The control phase assessments were conducted at the end of the initial semester, and the experimental phase assessments were completed at the end of the subsequent semester, resulting in an interval of approximately one academic semester (about 4–5 months) between measurements for the same cohort.

Research methods

Teaching process

Teaching content

The primary textbook used was Surgical Nursing (7th ed., 2021) by Li and Lu, published by People’s Medical Publishing House. Supplementary materials included Clinical Nursing Scenario Simulation Teaching Application Guide and Typical Case Collection (Liang & Guo) and Clinical Common Nursing Skills Scenario Simulation (Yang & Li), both also published by People’s Medical Publishing House. The instructional content integrated these textbook resources with real clinical cases, focusing on four specific conditions: burn shock, breast cancer, abdominal disease, and hepatobiliary-pancreatic diseases. All cases underwent two rounds of expert review, analysis, and revision before being finalized for teaching use.

Division and training

Three teachers participated in both the debriefing and traditional teaching sessions throughout this study. They collaboratively developed the teaching plans and underwent standardized training prior to implementation to ensure instructional consistency. Following this preparation, all teachers demonstrated clear understanding of their respective roles and responsibilities.

Teaching grouping

The eight classes were first organized into four teaching groups (Classes 1–2, 3–4, 5–6, and 7–8). Each teaching group was then divided into three experimental groups, with each group facilitated by one teacher in the surgical nursing laboratory. These experimental groups were further subdivided into five teams, each comprising 4–6 students. The simulation-based training consisted of four case-based scenarios, with one dedicated case covered in each scenario. Each case scenario spanned three sessions, resulting in a total of 12 sessions.

Control group (traditional teaching)

Based on clinical cases, simulated scenarios were developed. Students were divided into small groups and assigned to various roles, including the patient, family members, primary nurses, head nurses, nursing specialists, and bystanders. They then participated in role-playing activities according to their respective roles. Upon completion of the simulation, the teacher delivered immediate oral feedback and concluding remarks. This feedback was unstructured and delivered in an ad hoc manner, typically consisting of the teacher’s general observations about student performance, positive comments on what was done well, and a summary of key learning points. No structured debriefing framework (such as PEARLS or any other formal approach) was employed during this phase, and teachers did not receive specific training regarding feedback delivery for the control condition. This approach reflects the conventional debriefing practice routinely used in our simulation teaching prior to this study.

Experimental group (debriefing teaching)

Time and format: Debriefing was conducted 30 minutes after the simulation-based education. Students remained in their simulation groups and were guided by a teacher who had participated in the entire simulation exercise.

Theoretical framework and debriefing model: The debriefing was structured and delivered using the PEARLS framework. This framework was selected because its phases align seamlessly with Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle, ensuring that the debriefing was not merely a discussion, but a theory-driven intervention.

Facilitator training and fidelity

To support rigorous and standardized implementation across the process, the participating teachers underwent a training program. This program included: (1) theoretical instruction on Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle and the PEARLS framework; (2) a review of a detailed debriefing guide with scripted prompts for each debriefing step; (3) regular meetings were held after the intervention to maintain consistency and continuous improvement in subsequent implementations.

To promote adherence to the protocol during the study, the following fidelity-supporting measures were implemented: all facilitators used the same scripted guide during debriefing sessions; the teaching team held brief alignment meetings before and after each simulation day to discuss the case objectives and review facilitation approaches; and the lead researcher conducted periodic, informal observations to provide formative feedback based on the PEARLS structure. These observations confirmed that facilitators generally adhered to the structured phases of the PEARLS framework and followed the scripted prompts, suggesting that the debriefing protocol was implemented as intended. While these steps aimed to maximize consistency, a formal, validated tool for assessing debriefing quality (e.g., the Debriefing Assessment for Simulation in Healthcare [DASH]) was not used to generate objective fidelity scores across sessions. This constitutes a methodological limitation, which is addressed in the Limitation.

Debriefing process: The debriefing process consisted of five consecutive steps, as shown in Fig. 1. This process was integrated within the PEARLS framework and was based on the Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle as its theoretical foundation.

Step 1 raising questions and step 2 self-reflection

In these two steps, their core design was based on the “reflective observation” stage of Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle. Specifically, they were achieved through the two phases of “setting the scene” (P) and “encouraging emotions and reactions” (E) in the PEARLS framework. Operationally, the facilitator first employed open-ended questions to establish a psychologically safe environment, guiding learners to mentally replay the simulation and articulate their initial emotions and perceptions. This process of raising questions was designed to surface uncertainties and cognitive conflicts, effectively bridging the learners’ concrete experience with reflective observation. Building directly upon this, the self-reflection step prompted learners to turn inward, conducting an introspective examination of their own clinical reasoning, decisions, and actions to form initial personal hypotheses about their performance, thereby deepening the reflective process.

Step 3 analyzing causes and step 4 Open sharing

These steps collectively formed the core and culmination of the “analysis of the case” (A) phase within the PEARLS framework. Together, they drove a deeper progression into the “abstract conceptualization” stage of Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle. In Step 3: analyzing causes, the facilitator guided the group in a collaborative investigation to determine the root causes of both effective and ineffective performance. This shifted the discourse from individual introspection to a shared, evidence-based analysis. Building directly on this foundation, Step 4: open sharing was conducted, which served to solidify abstract conceptualization by exposing learners to a diversity of peer perspectives. Through this dialogue, learners tested and refined their mental models against those of others. The process that corrected misconceptions and enabled the co-construction of a more robust and nuanced clinical understanding. This step not only solidified the abstract conceptualization, but also served as a pivotal transition, concluding the analytical phase and preparing the group for the forthcoming synthesis and application of lessons.

Step 5 summarizing

This final step encompassed the review and summarize key lessons (R); link to clinical practice and research (L); synthesize and apply (S) phases of the PEARLS framework. It was strategically designed to bridge the newly constructed understanding into active experimentation within Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle. The facilitator guided learners to systematically distill and articulate the key lessons from the analysis. Then, the learners connected these insights to broader clinical contexts and supporting research. Finally, and most critically, the facilitator supported them in formulating a concrete, actionable plan for applying this synthesized knowledge in future clinical situations.

Evaluation criteria

Theoretical exam scores

At the end of each semester, all students took the exam using the unified questions, with a duration of 120 min.

Critical thinking disposition

The study used the Chinese version of critical thinking disposition inventory (CTDI-CV) to evaluate students’ critical thinking disposition, which was translated by Mei-ci Peng from the Hong Kong Polytechnic University [15]. A total of 70 items were scored using the likert 6 scale, including strongly agree, agree, basically agree, mostly disagree, disagree, and strongly disagree. Negative critical thinking disposition was defined as ≤ 210 points, moderate critical thinking disposition was defined as 211–279 points, and positive critical thinking disposition was defined as ≥ 280 points. The content validity of this scale was 0.89, the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.90, and the homogeneity reliability coefficients Q for each dimension were 0.54–0.77, indicating that the scale had good validity and reliability.

General self-efficacy

The study used the Chinese version of the general self-efficacy scale (GSES) to evaluate students’ general self-efficacy, which was revised by Zhang [16]. This scale consisted of 10 items, using a 4-point Likert scale for scoring. For each item, participants respond based on their own actual situation to completely incorrect (1 point), somewhat correct (2 points), mostly correct (3 points), and completely correct (4 points). The scale was a unidimensional measure without subscales. Following the scoring approach validated for the Chinese context, a mean item score was calculated by dividing the sum of the 10 item scores by 10, yielding a score range of 1 to 4 which is equivalent to a summative score range of 10 to 40. Higher scores indicate greater general self-efficacy. The critical threshold for the mean item score was 2.5 points, equivalent to a summative score of 25; scores below this threshold indicate low general self-efficacy. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.87, the test-retest reliability was 0.83, and the split-half reliability was 0.90, indicating that the scale had good reliability and validity.

Effectiveness evaluation of debriefing

Reviewing relevant literature, the study used the Questionnaire of Effectiveness Evaluation of Debriefing, which was developed by Chun-feng Liu [17]. The scale consisted of 15 items. It mainly investigated the opinions and evaluations of students on debriefing based on experiential learning theory.

Statistical analysis

After the questionnaires were collected, they were checked and screened by two researchers, and those questionnaires with missing items or obvious logical errors were eliminated. All data were verified for accuracy and recorded by two researchers. A database was established using Excel. Descriptive statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Before the analysis, based on the results of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (K-S test) and the Q-Q plot, the theoretical exam scores, critical thinking disposition, and general self-efficacy values of the control group and the experimental group were basically in accordance with the normal distribution. Moreover, no missing data was found in the dataset.

Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Count data were described using rates. The scores of the theoretical examination, critical thinking disposition, and general self-efficacy of students in control and experimental phase were tested by a paired sample t-test. This study not only reports the t-statistics and p-values, but also presents confidence intervals (95% CI), and percentage change for all pre/post measures. Cohen’s ds (the standardized mean change for paired data) as the primary effect size, calculated as the mean difference divided by the standard deviation of the difference scores. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Chengdu Medical College, with the review opinion number: Chengdu Medical Ethics Review 2022NO.24. All participants obtained informed consent prior to inclusion. Our study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Students’ effectiveness evaluation of debriefing in experimental phase

A questionnaire survey was conducted to evaluate students’ perceptions of debriefing in surgical nursing education. The results showed that in terms of knowledge acquisition, more than 90% of students perceived the debriefing as useful for understanding, mastering, and applying knowledge. In terms of emotional attitude, over 86% of students reported that the debriefing was perceived as supportive of their confidence in solving clinical problems, humanistic literacy, and interpersonal communication skills. In terms of nursing skills, more than 87% of students believed that the debriefing contributed to the development of their nursing skills. In the evaluation feedback, 88.23% of students liked the relaxed and lively classroom atmosphere and felt the professional value of nursing. Most students (78.53%) thought that the debriefing provided a platform for self-expression and catharsis, which was perceived as helpful for alleviating tension, anxiety, and stress in clinical scenarios. The majority (87.65%) of students hoped to continue applying debriefing in situational simulation classes in the future. These findings suggest that the teaching method was well-received by the students, as shown in Table 5.

Discussion

Influence of debriefing on the mastery of theoretical knowledge

Following the debriefing intervention, final theoretical examination scores were significantly higher compared to baseline scores from the control phase (p < 0.001). The magnitude of this difference (Cohen’s ds = 1.09) is relatively large, suggesting that the debriefing may be associated with the internalization and application of theoretical knowledge. This large difference, together with the moderate magnitude observed for self-efficacy, suggests that the intervention was associated with meaningful differences across multiple learning domains. This finding aligns with the “abstract conceptualization” stage in Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle. During the analysis phase of debriefing, students connect their concrete simulation experiences with nursing knowledge through guided discussion. They systematically organize, synthesize, and generalize their reflective observations. This process may enable them to distill generalizable principles and patterns, thereby facilitating the transformation of specific experiential learning into an abstract and universally applicable knowledge system. The finding is consistent with previous studies [18, 19], which reported associations between debriefing and learning outcomes following simulation.

However, caution is warranted when interpreting these findings. Theoretical examinations primarily assess the recall and application of specific knowledge points at a single point in time. Despite the large magnitude of the observed difference, it cannot be determined whether the knowledge can be retained over a longer period. This result also does not indicate whether students acquired the ability to flexibly apply the knowledge in complex and variable clinical environments. Therefore, while the difference in theoretical examination scores could be considered an important and positive indicator of the potential value of debriefing, it should be combined with other measures such as critical thinking, self-efficacy, and direct behavioral observation to form a more comprehensive evaluation system.

Influence of debriefing on the critical thinking disposition

Students’ critical thinking disposition was significantly higher in all seven dimensions and the total score following the debriefing intervention (all P < 0.001). The magnitudes of these differences (Cohen’s ds = 0.51–1.69) show a consistent pattern across dimensions, with percentage differences ranging from 16.43% to 41.67%, suggesting that different thinking traits may have shown varying degrees of change. This finding is consistent with previous research [20], which reported that debriefing is associated with higher critical thinking disposition. Particularly, the Self-confidence (Cohen’s ds = 1.69, 41.67%) and Inquisitiveness (Cohen’s ds = 1.56, 41.25%) dimensions were substantially higher. This pattern can be considered in light of Bandura’s social cognitive theory [21]: debriefing may provide students with mastery experiences (successful coping with simulated challenges) and vicarious experiences (observing and learning from peers’ diverse strategies). When students are guided to review their own successes and analyze areas for improvement in a psychologically safe environment, their willingness to explore the unknown (Inquisitiveness) and trust in their own judgment (Self-confidence) may be higher. Consistent with previous research [22], these findings suggest that debriefing is associated with higher student confidence.

Simultaneously, the large differences observed in the Analyticity and Systematicity dimensions (Cohen’s ds = 0.95–1.72, percentage change 22.77%–33.72%) suggest a potential association between debriefing and deeper cognitive processing. During the Analysis phase of the PEARLS framework, debriefing guides students to deconstruct their clinical reasoning process. Through Analyzing causes, students are prompted to scrutinize evidence and trace the logical chain of their decisions. The teacher, through a series of guiding questions, prompts students to analyze clinical scenarios, distinguish relevant information, and examine the logical thinking behind their own decisions. Through this process, students may practice analytical and systematic information organization skills. The result is consistent with previous work [23], suggesting that students’ judgments may be more prudent when facing complex simulated scenarios following debriefing. This process may be associated with students’ integration of their specific experiences with theoretical knowledge, but it should be recognized that the degree to which this connection is established may vary depending on the students’ individual foundational knowledge.

However, the magnitudes of the differences for the Truth-seeking (Cohen’s ds = 0.51), Maturity (Cohen’s ds = 0.68) and Open-mindedness (Cohen’s ds = 0.74) dimensions were moderate, lower than those observed in other dimensions. Correspondingly, their percentage differences were more modest (16.43%, 22.77%, and 30.42%, respectively). This finding may reflect the influence of cultural background and educational traditions on students’ thinking dispositions. In Confucian Heritage Culture (CHC) educational contexts, knowledge is often perceived as transmitted from authoritative sources (teachers, texts) rather than constructed through individual inquiry and critique [24, 25]. Students’ reluctance to challenge teachers or question established procedures is not merely a behavioral habit but reflects a deeply embedded epistemological belief about the nature and source of knowledge. As Nguyen et al. [24] note, in CHC classrooms, the teacher is regarded as the authority figure and knowledge holder, and questioning or challenging the teacher may be perceived as disrespectful or disruptive to classroom harmony. This cultural orientation may explain why the Truth-seeking dimension, which reflects the disposition to question given information, probe for evidence, and revise one’s views in light of new evidence, showed the smallest difference among all CTDI-CV subscales.

This interpretation is consistent with research by Tiwari et al. [26] with Hong Kong nursing students, who found that while problem-based learning was associated with higher critical thinking disposition, changes in the Truth-seeking dimension were slower to emerge compared to other dimensions such as Analyticity or Self-confidence. The authors attributed this to the persistence of culturally embedded learning habits that privilege the reproduction of established knowledge over its critical examination. This cultural epistemological orientation suggests that even when pedagogical conditions are optimized, as in the structured debriefing environment designed to promote inquiry and reflection, students may still experience internal resistance to questioning established knowledge, as doing so conflicts with deeply ingrained cultural norms regarding respect for authority and the preservation of classroom harmony. Consequently, the cultivation of dispositions such as Truth-seeking and Maturity may require not only structured pedagogical interventions but also sustained, explicit attention to the cultural beliefs that shape students’ epistemological assumptions. This deeper cultural analysis underscores the importance of culturally responsive pedagogies that acknowledge and address the epistemological tensions inherent in introducing Western-derived educational approaches into CHC contexts.

Therefore, even in a psychologically safe environment during debriefing, students may still be reluctant to question teachers or established procedures. This culturally embedded disposition may be difficult to substantially transform within a single semester. Nevertheless, the observed moderate differences in these specific dispositions are practically meaningful. In nursing education, nurturing even incremental growth in the willingness to question (Truth-seeking) or consider diverse perspectives (Open-mindedness) represents a critical step toward developing reflective practitioners. These differences, while not as pronounced as those in confidence or analytic skills, signify important foundational differences upon which further development can be built with continued, targeted instructional support. This result suggests that future teaching may benefit from consciously and strategically supporting the development of these critical thinking dispositions.

These results suggest a significant positive difference in students’ self-reported critical thinking disposition, as measured by their inclinations to engage in truth-seeking, open-mindedness, and analytical thinking. While this dispositional growth is a vital prerequisite, it should be distinguished from directly observed clinical reasoning performance in authentic, complex situations. Our findings suggest that structured debriefing may be associated with the development of attitudinal and cognitive habits that form a foundation for clinical judgment.

Although the above outcomes are encouraging, the results of this study must be interpreted with caution. First, there is the limitation of the measurement tool. The Chinese Version of Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CTDI-CV) assesses students’ self- perception and disposition rather than their practical abilities. Scores on this measure may not directly correspond to actual behaviors exhibited in real clinical scenarios. Second, this study was a short-term intervention conducted in controlled simulation scenarios. Whether these observed differences can be maintained over the long term and effectively transferred to high-pressure, busy clinical environments require longitudinal follow-up studies. Additionally, the observed associations may be influenced by students’ initial cognitive styles and prior educational experiences.

Influence of debriefing on the general self-efficacy

Following the debriefing intervention, General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) scores were significantly higher compared to baseline scores from the control phase (p < 0.001). The magnitude of this difference was moderate (Cohen’s ds = 0.47) according to conventional benchmarks. While this magnitude is smaller than that observed for critical thinking disposition, this pattern aligns with theoretical expectations: self-efficacy is a relatively stable personal belief that typically develops gradually through cumulative mastery experiences over extended periods [27]. In this context, observing a moderate difference over a single semester may represent a practically significant educational outcome. That is, while this magnitude is conventionally considered moderate, for a relatively stable trait such as self-efficacy, observing such a difference over a limited intervention period may reflect a practically meaningful difference in students’ belief in their capacity to handle clinical challenges. This difference could be associated with greater engagement in clinical learning, increased resilience when facing setbacks, and a stronger foundation for the transition from student to professional nurse. Therefore, even a moderate difference in self-efficacy represents a valuable outcome, as it is associated with the development of a crucial professional attribute that underpins lifelong learning and clinical competence. This result is consistent with previous research [28], which reported that debriefing is associated with higher self-efficacy.

This finding may be explained by Bandura’s theory of self-efficacy [27]. Bandura identified four primary sources of self-efficacy: (1) enactive mastery experience—confidence built through successful performance; (2) vicarious experience—learning from observing others succeed; (3) verbal persuasion—encouragement and support from others; and (4) physiological and affective states—how stress and emotions shape efficacy beliefs. Within this framework, the debriefing process may integrate the first three sources of self-efficacy. Importantly, Kolb’s experiential learning cycle is not merely a linear sequence but a recursive loop in which learners continuously move between concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation. The PEARLS-facilitated debriefing in the study was designed not simply to follow this sequence but to activate the cyclical learning process. During the Analysis phase, teachers used heuristic questioning to prevent students from remaining at the level of surface reflection (e.g., simply describing what happened). Instead, questions such as “Why do you think that happened?” and “What principles can we draw from this experience?” were intended to push students toward abstract conceptualization, distilling generalizable knowledge from the specific simulation experience.

The observed differences in students’ critical thinking disposition, particularly in the Systematicity and Analyticity dimensions, align with the theoretical expectation that structured debriefing may be associated with deeper cognitive processing. These dimensions reflect a tendency to engage in structured, in-depth analysis of experiences, which aligns with the abstract conceptualization phase of Kolb’s cycle. Furthermore, the higher theoretical knowledge scores observed following the debriefing may indirectly suggest that students were able to apply conceptual learning from debriefing to new situations, potentially indicating engagement with the active experimentation phase. However, without direct observational data or systematic analysis of students’ reflective processes, the precise mechanisms by which debriefing activates the recursive learning cycle remain theoretical. While the findings are consistent with Kolb’s framework, future research should employ qualitative methods, such as analysis of reflective journals or think-aloud protocols during simulations, to empirically examine how students internalize and recursively apply debriefing insights across multiple simulation experiences. This would provide richer evidence for understanding how debriefing facilitates movement through the complete experiential learning cycle.

In addition, several cautions are warranted when interpreting these findings. First, the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) measures students’ self-reported beliefs rather than their objective behavioral competence. In simulation-based education, higher self-reported confidence may not always correspond perfectly with the development of actual clinical abilities. Second, the differences observed in this study represent short-term effects. Whether the higher confidence observed following the intervention can be maintained in challenging clinical environments and effectively translate into sustained problem-solving ability requires verification through longitudinal studies.

Influence of debriefing on students’ satisfaction

Following the debriefing intervention, students provided highly positive feedback on their learning experience. This finding is consistent with previous work [29], which reported that debriefing was associated with higher students’ satisfaction with learning. The results showed that over 90% of students perceived that debriefing as useful for the understanding, mastering, and applying knowledge. The outcome is similar to prior research [22], where students generally perceived debriefing as a “bridge” connecting theory and practice, potentially supporting the activation and deepening of abstract knowledge in specific contexts. Additionally, in terms of nursing competencies such as “Effect on emotional attitude” and “Effect on nursing skills”, students also reported positive perceptions (with approval rates all > 86%). This suggests that debriefing is not only regarded as a skill practice tool but was also perceived as an educational strategy that may support the development of comprehensive qualities.

Furthermore, students’ evaluation of the debriefing revealed its perceived core elements: psychological safety and a positive learning atmosphere. The research showed 88.23% (n = 300) of students agreed that debriefing was perceived as creating a more relaxed and lively classroom atmosphere, while 78.53% (n = 267) perceived it as a platform for self-expression and catharsis. This finding is particularly significant as it reflects the foundation of debriefing. This is in agreement with previous research [8, 30]. Establishing and maintaining a psychologically safe environment is considered a priority for teachers, and a prerequisite for students to candidly face their mistakes and engage in debriefing. The data suggest that the debriefing implemented in this study was perceived as creating such a supportive environment.

However, a critical analysis of these highly positive responses is equally necessary. Notably, while the majority of students (87.65%, n = 298) expressed their desire to continue using debriefing in the future, the agreement rate for “Don’t increase the usual study burden” was relatively lower (60.00%, n = 204), with 31.47% (n = 107) remaining uncertain. This may reflect the demands that debriefing places on students: it requires more active cognitive investment and emotional engagement, which may be subjectively perceived as a “burden”. Simultaneously, not all students can immediately adapt to and fully utilize this open-ended, discussion-based learning method. This is consistent with the findings of Franco et al. [31]; Kolbe, et al. [30], suggesting that students’ cognitive, affective, and social engagement may be associated with their perceptions of debriefing. All participants in this study had completed the foundational courses, which may be a necessary condition for benefiting from reflective analysis. For students with a weaker foundation, the outcomes may be more limited.

Students’ high satisfaction provides preliminary support for the acceptability and perceived value of debriefing in the context of Chinese nursing education. However, it is important to recognize that satisfaction surveys measure subjective perceptions, and their results may be influenced by novelty effects or social desirability bias. Therefore, while students’ preferences are important, they cannot replace objective assessment of long-term learning outcomes and clinical behavioral changes. Future curriculum design should consider how to optimize the debriefing process, ensuring depth of reflection while minimizing cognitive burden where possible. Educators may need to provide additional support to help students, particularly those who are introverted or less accustomed to discussion-based learning, to benefit more fully from debriefing experiences.

Implications for nursing education

Firstly, nursing education institutions should commit to developing standardized debriefing operational guidelines and implementing systematic teacher training to support the quality and consistency of debriefing. This training should go beyond the theoretical level and incorporate teaching exercises based on the PEARLS framework. The focus should be on developing teachers’ practical skills in three core areas: creating a safe psychological environment, applying heuristic questioning techniques, and connecting theory with practice in meaningful ways.

Secondly, debriefing should be deeply integrated into the simulation curriculum to establish a systematic framework for developing higher-order thinking. It is suggested that debriefing be considered an essential component of simulation-based education, with sufficient time allocated for its implementation. This approach may help address the common phenomenon of “conducting simulation without debriefing”. In practice, teachers may use guiding questions to help students externalize their clinical reasoning processes. Furthermore, it is crucial to foster a classroom environment that supports questioning and inquiry, encouraging students to listen to diverse perspectives and engage in pluralistic dialogue. Interdisciplinary learning activities may be promoted to facilitate students’ application of critical thinking across different disciplines. To support the transfer of reflective competencies developed in simulation to real-world practice, educators should explicitly address this connection. A key strategy is to integrate structured debriefing principles into clinical post-conference discussions and ward-based reflections. Facilitators can adapt the core techniques, such as creating a safe learning climate and using facilitative questioning, to guide students in analyzing their actual clinical experiences, thereby creating a consistent “reflective thread” throughout their education. Encouraging students to maintain reflective journals that connect insights from simulation with observations from clinical placements may further reinforce this transfer. This approach may elevate debriefing from an optional activity to a core teaching procedure, while also providing ongoing support for students’ higher-order thinking development.

Finally, a multi-method approach may be considered for evaluating debriefing. Future curriculum assessments may go beyond self-report scales and consider introducing Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCE) to directly observe students’ performance in simulated scenarios. Teachers may use standardized tools to assess students’ ability to identify and respond to clinical errors. They may collect students’ debriefing logs and analyze the depth of their reflection and critical thinking characteristics. Furthermore, observing and tracking students’ performance in real clinical environments may help validate whether the changes observed following debriefing are associated with clinical practice abilities, which may provide more robust evidence for the value of debriefing in nursing education.

Conclusion and outlook

In summary, this intervention was associated with statistically significant differences across multiple learning domains. Importantly, the magnitudes of these differences, ranging from moderate to large, suggest that these changes are not merely statistically significant but possess practical relevance for nursing education. Notably, while the magnitude for self-efficacy (Cohen’s ds = 0.47) is conventionally considered moderate, observing such a difference over a single semester, given the stable nature of self-efficacy beliefs, may represent a practically meaningful difference with implications for students’ professional development. Differences in critical thinking dispositions and self-efficacy, even of a moderate degree, may be relevant to the essential skill set and professional mindset required for safe and effective nursing practice.

This self-controlled study implemented a debriefing approach, guided by Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle and PEARLS framework, within simulation-based education of surgical nursing. Theoretical exam scores, critical thinking disposition, and general self-efficacy were all higher following the intervention. Furthermore, students provided positive evaluations of debriefing, suggesting its perceived value in facilitating knowledge acquisition, skill enhancement, and the development of humanistic qualities, as well as in fostering a psychologically safe learning environment. Although this study has methodological limitations and the self-reported results warrant cautious interpretation, taken together, the findings provide preliminary evidence for the potential value and acceptability of debriefing within simulation-based nursing education in China. However, given the non-experimental design, these findings should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal.

Limitations and future research

This study initially confirmed the potential value and application prospects of debriefing in in simulation-based education of surgical nursing. These results provide some empirical evidence for promoting this teaching method in the local nursing education context in China. However, when interpreting these encouraging findings and inferring their implications, several limitations of the study must be considered.

The primary limitation lies in the self-controlled before-and-after design. While pragmatic for educational research, this design is inherently susceptible to threats to internal validity, including: (a) maturation effects reflecting the cognitive and academic development of students over two semesters; (b) history effects from exposure to other concurrent courses or clinical experiences—although the formal intensive clinical practicum was scheduled outside the study timeframe, students may have been engaged in other clinical observations or parallel theory courses that could independently contribute to the observed outcomes; and (c) testing effects from familiarity with the assessment instruments upon repeated administration. Thus, while this design accounts for baseline differences within the same cohort, it cannot fully isolate the specific contribution of the debriefing intervention from the cumulative professional development that occurs throughout the nursing curriculum. Furthermore, although the simulation content and assessment difficulty were designed to be equivalent between semesters and aligned with consistent learning objectives, inherent variation in clinical cases may introduce unmeasured confounding.

Second, this was a single-center study conducted with a convenience sample of nursing students from one college, which may limit the generalizability (external validity) of our findings to other student populations or educational contexts. Third, the absence of blinding for both participants and teachers, coupled with students’ awareness of participating in a novel teaching method, raises the possibility of performance bias (e.g., the Hawthorne effect) and detection bias in self-reported outcomes.

Fourth, limitations related to measurement tools should be considered. The validity of our conclusions is also bounded by the characteristics of the instruments used. The Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CTDI-CV) assesses the propensity or inclination to think critically rather than the actual application of critical thinking skills in clinical reasoning. Thus, while our data show higher scores in learners’ self-reported dispositions, they do not directly measure greater critical thinking skills in practice. The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) is a global, trait-like measure of perceived competence. Self-efficacy is known to be context-sensitive, and a simulation- or task-specific self-efficacy instrument might have been more sensitive to changes specifically tied to the simulation-debriefing context and could have provided a different magnitude or pattern of results. Our choice of these global measures, while providing valuable insights into broad shifts, underscores the need for future research to incorporate domain-specific and behavioral performance assessments.

Fifth, while structured training and a common guide were used to promote consistency, and periodic informal observations confirmed that facilitators generally adhered to the PEARLS framework, we did not employ a standardized, validated instrument (e.g., the Debriefing Assessment for Simulation in Healthcare [DASH]) to objectively measure debriefing quality across all sessions. This constitutes a limitation in intervention fidelity, as we cannot provide objective data on facilitation skill or consistency. Without such objective ratings, we cannot definitively rule out the possibility that variations in facilitation style or depth among instructors, rather than the structured debriefing model per se, contributed to the observed outcomes. Consequently, our ability to attribute the outcomes solely to the intended intervention is somewhat constrained. Future studies should incorporate tools like the DASH to support and document high-quality, consistent debriefing, which may provide more robust evidence for the relationship between the intervention and its outcomes.

Finally, the reliance on theoretical exams and self-report scales, rather than direct observation of clinical performance (e.g., via Objective Structured Clinical Examinations), means we can only assess perceived and theoretical learning gains. The cross-sectional nature of our assessment also precludes any conclusions regarding the long-term retention of these benefits.

Future research should address these limitations by employing more robust designs, such as cluster-randomized controlled trials conducted within a single semester to control for temporal confounders. Longitudinal studies are needed to track the persistence of critical thinking disposition and general self-efficacy differences into clinical practice. Incorporating objective performance measures like Objective Structured Clinical Examinations is crucial to better understand the relationship between simulation-based learning and practical skills. Furthermore, qualitative research methods, such as conducting focus group interviews immediately after debriefing sessions or collecting in-depth reflective journals, should be integrated into the study design. This approach would capture rich, real-time data on students’ subjective learning experiences, perceptions of cognitive engagement, and the contextual factors influencing their reception of debriefing, which may provide deeper insights into the underlying processes through which debriefing may influence learning.
