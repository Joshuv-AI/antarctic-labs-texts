Polypharmacy and deprescribing attitudes among stroke survivors: Opportunities for enhanced patient centered care
Authors: Alyssa K. Chao, James F. Burke, Ran Bi, Chun Chieh Lin, Lesli E. Skolarus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to 1) describe the burden of polypharmacy among stroke survivors compared to older adults without a history of stroke and 2) assess their attitudes towards deprescribing.Methods:This is a cross-sectional study of US Medicare Beneficiaries aged ≥65 who participated in the National Health and Aging Trends Study. Polypharmacy was defined by the number of regular medications reported (no polypharmacy, ≤5; moderate, 6–10; severe, >10). Deprescribing attitudes were elicited from the Medication Attitudes Module. Ordinal and logistic regression assessed the associations between polypharmacy, history of stroke, and deprescribing attitudes, adjusting for falls and comorbidities.Results:Of the 2073 respondents, 253 reported a prior stroke. Compared to older adults without a history of stroke, stroke survivors were more likely to report polypharmacy (moderate: 39.4 % vs 29.8 %; severe: 22.6 % vs 9.8 %; p < 0.01). Most stroke survivors (87.1 %) and older adults without stroke (89.0 %) would be willing to stop one or more medications. Polypharmacy had the strongest association with favorable attitudes toward deprescribing.Conclusion:Stroke survivors report greater polypharmacy than older adults without a history of stroke. Both groups overwhelmingly favor deprescribing. Stroke survivors represent a key population that would welcome and benefit from efforts to deprescribe.

Polypharmacy is a common and increasing issue in the United States.1,2 Although necessary at times, it can pose numerous problems for older adults. Polypharmacy is associated with drug-drug interactions, increased fall risk, accelerated physical and cognitive decline, and increased hospitalizations.3 Given the nature of these complications, polypharmacy may pose an even larger risk of harm to stroke survivors, who are multi-morbid and experience a higher burden of falls, disability, and cognitive impairment.

The most common solution to polypharmacy is deprescribing.4 Although deprescribing has been shown to be safe, cost-effective, and positively impactful on quality of life, it remains a nuanced shared decision that requires investment from both provider and patient.5 As such, patient attitudes towards deprescribing are fundamental to the success of these efforts. However, little is known about the extent of polypharmacy among strong survivors specifically, nor their attitudes towards deprescribing. The purpose of this study was to 1) describe the burden of polypharmacy among stroke survivors compared to older adults without a history of stroke in the US and 2) assess attitudes towards deprescribing.

Methods

This is a cross-sectional study from the 2016 National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS), a nationally representative survey of Medicare beneficiaries 65 years and older. We included those who answered the Medication Attitudes Module (n = 2301, Supplementary Material Figure 1), adapted from the validated Patients’ Attitudes Towards Deprescribing questionnaire.6 We excluded cases of those who resided in nursing homes (n = 177) and missing polypharmacy data (n = 51). Older adults self-reported age, race, sex, history of falls, and comorbidities, including stroke. Fall risk was calculated from the survival analysis based on a physical performance battery, whose methods are described in Kerber et al., 2022.7 Dementia status (probable, possible, none) was determined from a composite measure of cognitive testing, proxy reporting, and self-report of medical provider diagnosis.8 Self-care and household activity limitations and whether they are accompanied to provider visits were also included.9 We categorized polypharmacy as moderate for those taking 6–10 different regular medications and severe for those taking >10 medications.1 In a sensitivity analysis, we explored the influence of the polypharmacy definition by including all 7 NHATS number of medication categories (0, 1–2, 3–5, 6–10, 11–15, 16–20, >20). Deprescribing attitudes were assessed with the agreement to the statement, “If a doctor said it was possible, you would be willing to stop one or more of medicines” (willingness to stop). We also assessed the desire to reduce medications with the statement, “You would like to reduce the number of medicines you are taking” (wanting to reduce). Responses were collapsed into binary variables (strongly disagree/disagree/don’t know (disagree) vs. strongly agree/-agree (agree)).

Statistical analysis

We described the cohort using descriptive statistics. Chi-square analysis was performed to explore whether older adults with polypharmacy were more willing to stop or wanted to reduce their medications among older adults without a history of stroke and among stroke survivors. We then conducted an ordinal logistic regression to explore the association between polypharmacy and stroke adjusted for a history of falls, comorbidities, and dementia status. The association between deprescribing attitudes and stroke was examined using logistic regression, adjusted for polypharmacy and history of falls. We then repeated all models adjusting by adding age, sex, race/ethnicity, activity limitations, and presence of a medical companion and added comorbidities and dementia status to the deprescribing attitudes models. In sensitivity analyses, we repeated the models by replacing the history of falls with fall risk and repeated the polypharmacy analysis with all 7 categories of the number of regular medications. All analyses were sampling weighted and performed using Stata (version 17; Stata Inc., College Station, TX). The data used in this analysis are publicly available and have no unique identifiers. This study was deemed exempt from review by the Ohio State University Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained from participants when they were recruited to NHATS.

Results

Polypharmacy

Of the total 2073 respondents, 253 (weighted percentage 9.9 %) reported a prior stroke (Table 1). Stroke survivors were more likely than older adults without a history of stroke to have moderate and severe polypharmacy (39.4 % vs 29.8 % took 6–10 medications, 22.6 % vs 9.8 % took >10 medications, p < 0.01) (Table 1). After adjusting for other comorbidities, dementia, and a history of falls, a history of stroke was associated with polypharmacy (OR = 1.5, 95 %CI: 1.0–2.2, p = 0.04, Table 2). After adding age, race, ethnicity, sex, activity limitations, and medical companion, history of stroke was no longer associated with polypharmacy in the 3- category polypharmacy outcome model (OR = 1.3, 95 %CI 0.9–2.0, p =0.13) but remained significantly associated with polypharmacy in the 7-category polypharmacy outcome model (OR = 1.4, 95 %CI 1.0–2.0, p = 0.04, supplemental Table 1).

Deprescribing Attitudes

A large majority of stroke survivors (87.1 %) and older adults without a history of stroke (89 %) reported a willingness to stop medications (Table 3). This endorsement was nearly universal among stroke survivors with severe polypharmacy (97.0 %). Additionally, 66.1 % of stroke survivors reported wanting to reduce their number of medications, which also was most pronounced among those with severe polypharmacy (78.6 %). Similar findings were observed for older adults without stroke.

After adjusting for polypharmacy, and falls, a history of stroke was not associated with a willingness to stop medications or wanting to reduce their number of medications (Table 2). Moderate polypharmacy (OR = 2.9, 95 %CI 1.75–4.80; p < 0.001) and severe polypharmacy (OR = 3.1, 95 %CI 1.68–5.77; p = 0.001) were associated with willingness to stop medications. A similar pattern was observed for those who wanted to reduce their number of medicines taken. The results were similar in the fully adjusted models (supplemental Table 2). History of falls was not associated with deprescribing attitudes. Older adults with a greater fall risk had lower odds of desire to reduce their medications (OR 0.7, 95 %CI 0.5–0.98; p = 0.04).

Discussion

In a nationally representative sample of older adults, we found that stroke survivors exhibit 1.3 times more moderate polypharmacy and 2.3 times more severe polypharmacy compared to their non-stroke survivor counterparts. Stroke survivors, similar to the general population of older adults, overwhelmingly endorse willingness to stop medications if their doctor said it was possible and wanting to reduce their number of medications. These attitudes are especially pronounced among stroke survivors with severe polypharmacy. After adjusting for polypharmacy, falls, comorbidities, and dementia, a history of stroke was not associated with favorability towards deprescribing, whereas polypharmacy was strongly associated with deprescribing attitudes. This finding suggests that the high medication burden, rather than the stroke history itself, may contribute to deprescribing attitudes among people who have had a stroke.

Given stroke survivors’ prevalence of polypharmacy, stroke survivors represent a key population who would welcome efforts to deprescribe. Similarly, stroke survivors may have greater benefit from deprescribing, given their higher frequency of falls and cognitive and physical impairment.10 History of falls approached significant association with polypharmacy, while unexpectedly fall risk was associated with a lower desire to reduce the number of medications. Future studies should explore this hypothesis-generating finding further.

The study’s limitations include deprescribing attitudes as hypothetical, a lack of consideration of side effects, and reasons older adults might want to reduce their medications. This study relied on self-reported stroke, which has a high specificity and positive predictive value in older adults.11 Our results are mostly limited to a community-dwelling population of older adults. Future studies should include individuals residing in nursing homes, as they may experience a high burden of polypharmacy.

In conclusion, polypharmacy is common among stroke survivors. Most stroke survivors, particularly those with severe polypharmacy, would favor deprescribing. A better understanding of the determinants of deprescribing in stroke survivors of deprescribing, as well as the perspectives of clinical providers, may lead to strategies that help both providers and stroke survivors appropriately deprescribe.
