Effect of PCSK9 Inhibition With Alirocumab in Patients With Probable Familial Hypercholesterolemia or Type III Hyperlipoproteinemia: Results From the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES Trial
Authors: Gregory P. Geba, Kusha A. Mohammadi, Amy Damask, Charles Paulding, Luca A. Lotta, George Hindy, Robert Pordy, Garen Manvelian, Michael D. Shapiro, Vera A. Bittner, Deepak L. Bhatt, Michael Szarek, Gregory G. Schwartz, Ph. Gabriel Steg, Sergio Fazio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn this post hoc analysis of the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES (Evaluation of Cardiovascular Outcomes After an Acute Coronary Syndrome During Treatment With Alirocumab) trial, we evaluated the efficacy of alirocumab in patients with probable heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HeFH) or type III hyperlipoproteinemia (T3HLP).MethodsPatients had clinical HeFH if baseline low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol was ≥250 mg/dL and the qualifying acute coronary syndrome event occurred before age 55 (men) or 60 years (women), or if baseline low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol was ≥330 mg/dL. Patients had T3HLP if the ratio, validated using the UK Biobank database, of non–high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol/apolipoprotein B was >2.6. Genetic variation affecting low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels (HeFH) and APOE genotypes (T3HLP) was assessed in a pharmacogenomics subgroup.ResultsIn total, 5.8% of patients from ODYSSEY OUTCOMES and 5.9% from the pharmacogenomics subgroup qualified as having clinical HeFH. In the pharmacogenomics subgroup, 295 patients (2.5%) had genetically confirmed HeFH; 45.8% also qualified as having clinical HeFH. Lipid responses and clinical benefits of alirocumab were similar in all patients with or without HeFH. In the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES cohort, 30 of 18 924 patients (0.16%) were identified as having T3HLP. The pharmacogenomics subgroup included 13 of these 30 patients, of whom 7 (53.8%) were APOE2/2 homozygotes. Lipid responses to alirocumab in patients with T3HLP were comparable with those in the general population. In those with genetic dyslipidemia, adverse event incidence was similar to the overall group.ConclusionsIn ODYSSEY OUTCOMES, individuals with HeFH were common while those with T3HLP were rare. In their lipid responses, carriers of both conditions benefited from alirocumab.RegistrationURL: https://clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT01663402.

PCSK9 (Proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9) inhibiting monoclonal antibodies, which increase expression of the low‐density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), effectively reduce low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) levels even in patients with heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HeFH), whose LDLR function is diminished. 1 , 2 However, while outcome trials that confirmed the clinical benefits of the 2 available antibodies did not specifically target patients with HeFH, it is likely that many participants had this condition given the study entry requirement (LDL‐C >70 mg/dL on maximally tolerated, high‐potency lipid‐lowering therapy [LLT] and preexisting coronary artery disease). 3 , 4 Several useful diagnostic criteria have been developed for the clinical diagnosis of familial hypercholesterolemia (FH). 5 , 6 , 7 One of the main tools was developed by the Dutch Lipid Clinic Network (DLCN), which uses a scoring system (3–5, possible FH; 6–8, probable FH; and >8, definite FH) to predict the likelihood of FH in the absence of genetic information. 5 Because large trials do not usually collect much of the information needed for the DLCN score (ie, xanthomas, arcus, detailed family history, or genetic data), retrospective attribution of FH status must rely on the presence of LDL‐C levels (≥250–330 mg/dL [adds 5 points]; ≥330 mg/dL [adds 8 points]), 7 and the presence of premature coronary artery disease (adds 2 points). While it is difficult to extract off‐treatment data in those who have been receiving long‐term statin therapy, methods to back‐calculate off‐treatment LDL‐C levels based on type and dose of statin have been used and validated. 8

Type III hyperlipoproteinemia (T3HLP) is another genetic dyslipidemia often misdiagnosed as common hypercholesterolemia or combined hyperlipidemia. T3HLP develops due to a variant in the gene for apolipoprotein E (Apo E) (APOE), resulting in impaired processing and clearance of lipoprotein remnants, thus causing their accumulation in plasma. 9 The most common genetic basis is homozygosity for the variant of APOE called APOE2, 9 although many sporadic cases have been reported as secondary to rare APOE variants in the heterozygous state. 10 , 11 , 12

Although several statins are approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of T3HLP, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 PCSK9 inhibitors have not yet produced evidence of benefits for this condition. Just like HeFH, T3HLP is more rigorously and accurately diagnosed with a genetic test but, unlike HeFH, its genotype only rarely produces dyslipidemia (because of low penetrance), and its phenotype is not easily identified solely on the basis of the standard lipid panel (often diagnosed as common combined dyslipidemia). Several biomarker levels and ratios have been adopted for the identification of T3HLP without the need for genetic testing, 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 although most are neither specific nor sensitive enough for a diagnosis without knowledge of the APOE genotype status.

The ODYSSEY OUTCOMES trial was a phase 3, randomized, placebo‐controlled, multicenter study designed to compare the efficacy of alirocumab versus placebo on cardiovascular outcomes among patients with recent acute coronary syndrome (ACS) on intensive or maximally tolerated statin therapy. 4 A total of 18 924 patients were randomized, and the study met the primary end point of a 15% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in the treatment group versus placebo (hazard ratio [HR], 0.85 [95% CI, 0.73–0.98]).

In this post hoc analysis, we used established and novel biomarkers to identify patients from the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES trial who may have been affected by HeFH or T3HLP, and to determine the efficacy of alirocumab on their lipid parameters and, for HeFH, cardiovascular outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The primary efficacy and safety results for the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES trial have been published. 4 In brief, the trial consisted of patients aged ≥40 years who had been hospitalized with ACS 1 to 12 months before randomization and had LDL‐C levels ≥70 mg/dL (1.81 mmol/L), non–high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) levels ≥100 mg/dL (2.59 mmol/L), or apolipoprotein B (Apo B) levels ≥80 mg/dL after at least 2 weeks of stable LLTs. Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to blinded treatment with subcutaneous alirocumab 75 mg or matching placebo every 2 weeks. The dose of alirocumab was adjusted under blinded conditions to target an LDL‐C level of 25–50 mg/dL (0.6–1.3 mmol/L).

HeFH Study Population

To identify patients with HeFH in the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES trial, the DLCN criteria were used. 5 As most patients were already on statins, the untreated LDL‐C concentration was calculated using the adjustment factor provided in the supporting documentation for the DLCN tool. 5 , 22 Untreated LDL‐C was calculated using LLT‐specific adjustment factors with a formula that was previously validated. 8 , 23 Back‐calculated, untreated LDL‐C and the presence of early‐onset coronary artery disease (age <55 years in men and <60 years in women) were the 2 criteria used to assess the DLCN score for each participant. We defined an individual as having HeFH if the DLCN score categorized them as having at least probable HeFH (DLCN score ≥6).

Ethics Statement

This study was approved by the institutional review board/independent ethics committee, and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences International Ethical Guidelines, and the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All patients provided written informed consent before participation.

Pharmacogenetic Analysis and Development of LDL‐C Polygenic Risk Score

A pharmacogenetic analysis was conducted in a subset of the patients from the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES study. A total of 11 880 patients provided consent to participate in this post hoc analysis and had a DNA sample that passed quality‐control measures. Patient samples were genotyped with the Illumina Global Screening Array, version 1.0 (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA) and underwent whole exome sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc.) with the xGen version 3.0 capture kit (IDT, Coralville, IA). Population structure was assessed using principal component analysis with plink version 1.9 (Cog.genomics.org). 24 , 25 Eigenvalues (axes of variation) from the top 10 principal components from this analysis were used as covariates in any subsequent genetic analyses. Genotype imputation was conducted with Minimac4 26 , 27 with the TOPMed imputation panel. 28

Source data for the polygenic risk score (PRS) was a meta‐analysis of genome‐wide association studies of lipid levels, comprising 188 577 individuals. 29 The effect size estimates from this analysis were trained in an independent population (UK Biobank, 439 997 individuals with LDL‐C measures) to identify the optimal algorithm (maximum r 2 between the LDL‐C PRS and measured LDL‐C levels). The LDPred algorithm 30 with a ρ value of 0.01 was selected. The weights derived from the LDPred algorithm, comprising up to 2 019 264 variants, were used to calculate the LDL‐C PRS in the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES population.

Genetic variants from the whole exome sequencing platform were evaluated to identify variants linked with HeFH. 31 Identified variants met 1 of the following criteria: (1) ClinVar 32 (July 2023 download) variant with an associated disease/condition of hypercholesterolemia and a review status of ≥1 star (variants with a 1‐star rating due to conflicting reports of pathogenicity were removed if ≥50% of the submissions did not report the variant as pathogenic or likely pathogenic); (2) predicted loss of function in LDLR (ie, a nonsense or splicing variant in LDLR); and (3) a copy number variant (ie, deletion) in LDLR. All variants had a minor allele frequency of <0.01. Predicted loss‐of‐function variants are expected to alter protein function (eg, frameshift, splice acceptor, splice donor, start lost, stop gained).

T3HLP Study Population

Patients with T3HLP were identified through a screening tool metric proposed by Murase et al. 33 Using the baseline measurements of non–HDL‐C and Apo B levels, T3HLP was defined as a non–HDL‐C/Apo B ratio of ≥2.6. The initial validation was done using the large cohort of the UK Biobank (n=487 553), which has both phenotypic and genetic data (APOE genotypes). The UK Biobank is a population‐based prospective study that recruited individuals aged 40 to 69 years between 2006 and 2010. 34 The generation of the UK Biobank genotyping array and quality control was previously described. 35 The TOPMed reference panel was used for genome‐wide imputation. 26 , 28

Assessments

Baseline characteristics for patients with HeFH and T3HLP were assessed. Changes in LDL‐C, HDL‐C, triglycerides, total cholesterol, and Apo B after treatment with alirocumab were evaluated from baseline to month 48. Changes in lipoprotein (a) after treatment with alirocumab were evaluated from baseline to month 12. MACEs (defined as the composite of death from coronary heart disease, nonfatal myocardial infarction, fatal or nonfatal ischemic stroke, and unstable angina requiring hospitalization) after treatment with alirocumab were assessed in patients with HeFH and T3HLP.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using mean±SD or median (interquartile range). For categorical or ordinal variables, frequencies and percentages were provided. Baseline characteristics were summarized by HeFH and T3HLP status. Additionally, changes in LDL‐C, HDL‐C, triglycerides, total cholesterol, Apo B, and lipoprotein (a) were summarized using descriptive statistics. Efficacy data were summarized by treatment arms for MACE outcomes in subgroups of HeFH and T3HLP. The proportion difference and 95% CIs were reported for descriptive purposes to make within‐group comparisons in patients with and without HeFH. Treatment HRs and 95% CIs were estimated using a Cox proportional hazard model, stratified by geographic region. A P value for interaction was calculated to assess the heterogeneity of treatment differences in subgroups by HeFH status. The proportional hazard assumptions were checked via the Schoenfeld test, and the influential observations were examined through the “difference in the estimates of β” values. The cumulative incidence of MACEs over time was estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods by treatment group. Due to the low sample size of patients with T3HLP, we provided only the incidence for descriptive comparisons by treatment arm and T3HLP status. In the genetic analyses, a linear regression model adjusting for age, sex, and 10 principal components were used to evaluate the relationship between the LDL‐C PRS and LLT‐adjusted (using the DLCN documentation noted above) baseline LDL‐C measures. All analyses were done in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), plink 1.9/2.0, and R 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

HeFH Patient Population and Genetic Confirmation

Table 1 describes the demographics and baseline characteristics of all patients in the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES trial. Overall, the mean±SD age of all patients was 58.6±9.3 years, and the majority were men (75%) and White individuals (79%). The mean age of patients with HeFH was significantly lower than that of patients without HeFH (53.3±8.2 and 58.9±9.3 years, respectively; P<0.0001). Of the 18 924 patients in the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES study, 4.5% had probable HeFH and 1.3% had definite HeFH at baseline (Table 1). Within the pharmacogenomics subgroup, 63% of the original trial cohort (11 880/18924) had genetic data available for analysis; of these, 4.4% had probable HeFH and 1.5% had definite HeFH at baseline (Figure S1).

Genetic studies in the pharmacogenomics subgroup of patients revealed overall low genotype–phenotype concordance, as previously reported. 36 , 37 , 38 A total of 295 patients were identified as having a hypercholesterolemia‐associated variant, and 45.8% of these patients met the criteria for probable (26.8%) or definite (19.0%) HeFH by clinical assessment alone (ie, LLT‐adjusted baseline LDL‐C or age at qualifying ACS event). The majority (n=281) of the identified genetic variants was annotated in ClinVar, including 46 of the 51 predicted loss‐of‐function (pLoF) variants in LDLR. Five patients had an LDLR pLoF not previously submitted to ClinVar (all 5 pLoFs were frameshift variants meeting FH variant curation expert panel consensus guidelines, ie, ClinGen of “very strong pathogenic” criteria), 31 and LDLR copy number deletions were identified in 10 patients, summing to a total of 296 variants among the 295 carriers. Patients with loss‐of‐function variants (ie, pLoFs and copy number deletions) tended to have higher baseline LDL‐C levels (Table S1). Overall, variants in LDLR comprised the majority of FH‐associated variants; however, 25% of individuals with genetic FH carried variants in APOB. Among patients allocated to the alirocumab treatment arm, individuals with FH‐associated pLoF mutations (eg, nonsense, frameshift, splicing variants, or copy number deletions) showed a mean percentage decrease from baseline to month 4 in LDL‐C of 58.9%, comparable with the decreases in carriers of FH‐associated missense mutations (54.4%) and noncarriers (59.4%).

There was an enrichment in genetic HeFH carriers among patients with clinically defined probable (n=528) and definite (n=173) HeFH; the percentage of patients with genetic HeFH increased to 15.0% (n=79/528) and 32.4% (n=56/173), respectively. We applied a PRS for LDL‐C to explore genotype–phenotype concordance. Individuals in the top 10% of LDL‐C PRS were ≈28% more likely to meet the clinical criteria for probable or definite HeFH (7.6% of individuals in the top 10% by PRS qualified as having probable HeFH versus 5.9% in the overall pharmacogenomics cohort). Conversely, carriers of genetic HeFH were 7.8 times more likely to meet clinical HeFH criteria (probable/definite HeFH was observed in 45.8% of genetic HeFH carriers versus 5.9% overall). While individuals with higher PRS scores have greater risk of recurrent cardiovascular events, the risk is of far lesser magnitude in comparison with carriers of FH variants.

Effect of Alirocumab on Lipid Levels in the HeFH Population

Patients with HeFH treated with alirocumab experienced marked reductions in LDL‐C, non–HDL‐C, and Apo B levels from baseline to month 48 (Figure 1). Although substantial reductions in lipid levels were observed for patients with both HeFH and non‐FH, lipid levels for patients with HeFH remained consistently higher for the study duration (Figure 1). A reduction in lipoprotein (a) levels also was observed for patients with both HeFH and non‐FH treated with alirocumab (Figure S2).

Effect of Alirocumab on MACEs in the HeFH Population

During a median 2.8 years of follow‐up, the incidence of MACEs was higher in placebo‐treated patients with HeFH than in those without HeFH. The MACE relative risk reduction with alirocumab was consistent among those with (HR, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.61–1.19]) and without HeFH (HR, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.77–0.93]; P for interaction=0.8328; Table 2; model assumptions were verified, see Figure S3). Of note, the absolute risk reduction was numerically larger in patients with HeFH compared with those without. The cumulative incidence of MACEs in patients with HeFH is shown in Figure S4. The incidence of other key secondary end points according to treatment group and HeFH status is summarized in Table S2 and indicates that the relative benefit of alirocumab did not depend on HeFH status.

T3HLP Patient Population and Genetic Confirmation

Patients with T3HLP were identified using a previously published but not yet validated T3HLP biomarker (ratio of non–HDL‐C/Apo B >2.6). Applying the T3HLP biomarker to the UK Biobank database validated its specificity (99%) and sensitivity (89%) to diagnose T3HLP among carriers of the diagnostic APOE2/2 genotype. Discussion

We investigated the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES database to determine the lipid and clinical responses to alirocumab treatment in 2 groups of patients likely to carry genetic dyslipidemias (HeFH or T3HLP) and increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) events due to lifelong exposure to dyslipidemia. The importance of lifetime exposure to high levels of LDL‐C is corroborated by our results, which show similar LDL‐C levels but higher CVD rates among patients with HeFH treated with alirocumab compared with patients without HeFH receiving placebo. Using clinical biomarkers, we determined that ≈6% of the total trial population could be classified as having probable HeFH. This is consistent with the entry criteria for this trial (elevated LDL‐C on maximally tolerated oral therapy and history of coronary artery disease). In contrast, only 30 patients with T3HLP were identified. This is also consistent with expectations because this condition is rare, recessive, and with low penetrance. Treatment with alirocumab decreased LDL‐C, non–HDL‐C, and Apo B levels in patients with HeFH or T3HLP to a similar extent compared with the overall study population. This is the first time that effective lipid lowering in patients with T3HLP has been shown with alirocumab or any PCSK9 inhibitor. In addition, patients with HeFH showed similar treatment HR for MACEs compared with the overall study population, although the absolute reduction in risk of MACE was numerically greater in the former group.

Our results provide additional support for the concept that individuals with ACS and hyperlipidemia may have unrecognized genetic dyslipidemias, conferring a higher risk of CVD events, and that these individuals are responsive to effective LLTs. The younger age of individuals with versus without HeFH in this study, together with the increased rate of MACEs in those with HeFH, highlights the need for early diagnosis of this condition. Our data show an overall modest concordance between genetic and clinical diagnosis of HeFH, with a number needed to test to find a genetically confirmed patient with HeFH of ≈40. These results suggest that genetic testing in patients with post‐ACS with elevated LDL‐C may be cost effective. Furthermore, given the relatively low cost of genetic testing in the United States, the benefit of cascade screening in HeFH is potentially very large. Patients with HeFH have been extensively investigated and shown to respond to PCSK9 inhibitor monoclonal antibodies, 2 , 39 but the clinical outcome studies have been designed without the intent to enrich for, or even clearly identify, individuals with HeFH. A similar effect on LDL‐C and CVD rates for patients with or without FH was also suggested in an analysis of the SPIRE (Studies of PCSK9 Inhibition and the Reduction of Vascular Events) program of clinical trials of bococizumab, a PCSK9 inhibitor monoclonal antibody whose development was halted. 40 However, real‐world evidence suggests that these medications provide clinical benefits to patients with HeFH, and the indications for use already mention this condition. 41 , 42

The pharmacogenomics analysis highlights a previously well‐documented genotype–phenotype discordance. 43 When evaluating variants in LDLR and other genes associated with HeFH, 2.5% of patients in the overall pharmacogenomics subgroup were carriers. Although rare, genetically confirmed HeFH status is a much stronger predictor of elevated LDL‐C and earlier age of CVD onset than LDL‐C PRS. Nevertheless, within the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES cohort, additional genetic drivers beyond the 4 known genes affecting LDLR function 44 played a role in the development of the clinical HeFH phenotype.

T3HLP has been known for many decades to be caused by variants in APOE, most commonly homozygosity for the common APOE2 variant and less commonly from rare heterozygous variants in APOE. Our results confirmed the very low frequency of the T3HLP lipid abnormality, with only 30 individuals identified using the validated biomarker. Seventeen of these 30 individuals were also in the genetic subgroup, with the APOE2/2 genotype strongly represented among carriers (23% APOE2/2 carriers versus only 0.05% APOE2/2 noncarriers were T3HLP positive), supporting the notion that T3HLP was the correct diagnosis for most of these patients. Genetic testing is seldom used to diagnose T3HLP, due to insurance coverage issues and the APOE–Alzheimer connection that makes physicians wary of the consequences of knowing the APOE genotype.

This study includes limitations inherent to post hoc analyses. Other limitations include back‐calculated LDL‐C levels to derive HeFH diagnosis and a smaller pharmacogenomics subgroup, making it difficult to draw inferences about possible CVD risk reduction in patients with T3HLP. In addition, the phenotypic definition of T3HLP did not account for statin use. Moreover, the DLCN scoring process did not include family history data, as these did not include information on age at the time of event. Additionally, at entry into the trial, case report forms did not collect information regarding a prior clinical or genetic diagnosis of HeFH. Therefore, we do not know the proportion of patients with genetically proven or clinically presumed HeFH. The lack of interaction with/inability to inform participants and their relatives during this post hoc analysis was a missed opportunity and a lesson for future trials. Finally, it was assumed the T3HLP biomarker ratio is constant when untreated patients are taking high‐intensity statins.

In conclusion, our study shows that patients with HeFH, identified using established criteria, appear to benefit from PCSK9 inhibition similarly to patients with common hypercholesterolemia in terms of lipid responses and clinical outcomes. In addition, patients with T3HLP, identified using a biomarker ratio validated using the UK Biobank database, also benefit from PCSK9 inhibition similarly to patients with common dyslipidemia in terms of lipid responses.
