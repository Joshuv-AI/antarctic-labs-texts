Inhibitory effects and structural analysis of drug candidate PF-07817883 against coronaviral main proteases (Mpro) and SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants
Authors: Zhenyu Zhao, Xuelan Zhou, Haihai Jiang, Weiwei Wang, Bing Fan, Jian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMain proteases (Mpros) are a class of conserved cysteine proteases found among coronaviruses, playing a key role in their replication. These properties make Mpros ideal targets for the screening and design of pan-coronavirus drugs. Recently, PF-07817883 (ibuzatrelvir) has been developed against SARS-CoV Mpro by optimizing PF-07321332 (nirmatrelvir), showing broad-spectrum inhibitory efficacy against coronaviral Mpros. In the present study, we revealed the crystal structures of Mpros from SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV, HCoV-NL63, and HCoV-229E, and several SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants bound to PF-07817883. Detailed structural analyses demonstrates key molecular determinants necessary for interaction and elucidates the binding patterns of PF-07817883 to different coronaviral Mpros. The potencies of PF-07817883 against these investigated Mpros were further evaluated through molecular dynamic simulation and Enzymatic Activity Inhibition Assay. It shows that both E166N and E166R mutants exhibiting a certain degree of resistance to PF-07817883, the E166N mutant shows a more substantial impact on enzyme activity. These data provide molecular insights into broad-spectrum inhibition against selected coronaviral Mpros by PF-07817883 and the similarities and differences of PF-07817883 when bound to various Mpros, which will push the development of novel antivirals with pan-Mpro inhibitory efficacy.Keywords: Main protease, Coronavirus, Inhibitor, PF-07817883, Crystal structure

Introduction

To date, a sum of seven coronaviruses could cause infections in human beings. Among these, HCoV-NL63 and HCoV-229E are alphacoronaviruses, while the other five CoVs, namely HCoV-HKU1, HCoV-OC43, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV), Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) are betacoronaviruses (Kesheh et al., 2022; Fung and Liu, 2021; Tang et al., 2022). HCoV-229E, HCoV-OC43, HCoV-NL63, and HCoV-HKU1 mainly bring about mild and self-limiting viral respiratory illness, while the other three coronaviruses can be extremely pathogenic and deadly (Kesheh et al., 2022; Fung and Liu, 2021; Tang et al., 2022). Compared with MERS-CoV and SARS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2 has faster ‘human-to-human’ transmission rates (Tang et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2020). Since its first appearance in Wuhan in December 2019, SARS-CoV-2 infection has lead to more than 700 million cases of COVID-19 patients and over 7 million cases of death all over the world (https://covid19.who.int/). Some kinds of vaccines have been developed and approved to fight against SARS-CoV-2 (Lamb, 2021; Li et al., 2022a). But the constantly emerging SARS-CoV-2 variants, including the recently evolved JN.1 (BA.2.86.1.1) and KP.2 (JN.1.11.1.2), could easily weaken the immunoprotective effects of available vaccines (Shrestha et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024). In consideration of health threat caused by HCoVs and constantly arising SARS-CoV-2 variants, developing broad-spectrum antivirals is strongly necessary.

Some microbiological (or molecular) components among different human coronaviruses are important and conserved, such as the 3C-like main protease (Mpro), and could serve as targets for the development of broad-spectrum antivirals. As a matter of fact, Mpro could realizes the generation of most non-structural proteins related with viral replication and transcription and, therefore, is a key protease in the life cycle of human coronaviruses (Anand et al., 2003; Jin et al., 2020; Hegyi and Ziebuhr, 2002; Mengist et al., 2021). More importantly, Mpro shares no homologous proteins in humans (Zhang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Ren et al., 2013). These characteristics make Mpro an ideal target for developing antiviral drugs to fight against human coronaviruses. SARS-CoV-2 Mpro, which is indispensable for viral replication via cleaving viral polyproteins into functional units, is highly conserved across known coronaviruses and structurally distinct from human proteases, thereby rendering it a primary target for antiviral drug development (Zhang et al., 2020; Ullrich and Nitsche, 2020). The escalating development of Mpro inhibitors has been accompanied by growing concerns over the emergence of drug-resistant mutations driven by increasing selective pressure, with projections indicating potential evolution of novel resistance variants beyond current characterization (Hu et al., 2023; Padhi and Tripathi, 2022). Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms by which these mutants confer drug resistance is critical for the rational design and optimization of antiviral therapeutics. Naturally occurring Mpro resistance mutations such as S144A/F/Y, M165T, E166Q, and H172Q have been experimentally validated (Padhi and Tripathi, 2022). Further studies identified S144, M165, E166, H172, and Q192 as hotspot regions for nirmatrelvir resistance; multiple mutants at these positions (e.g., S144Y, E166V/G/A, etc.) retain catalytic activity comparable to the wild-type while exhibiting significantly reduced inhibitor binding (Ki increase >10-fold) (Padhi and Tripathi, 2022). Residue E166 has been identified as one of potential hotspot for drug resistance (Jochmans et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2022). Although not directly involved in the catalytic process, this residue plays a pivotal role in modulating enzyme activity and drug sensitivity (Huang et al., 2023). Therefore, the study of E166 mutation provides valuable information for solving drug resistance mutations, and promotes the development and optimization of new drugs.

After the outbreak of COVID-19, a lot of inhibitors against SARS-CoV-2 Mpro with potent efficacy have been reported (Ma et al., 2020; Kitamura et al., 2022; Drayman et al., 2021; Owen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022; Boras et al., 2021). Spectacularly, nirmatrelvir (PF-07321332) that was developed by Pfizer is the first Mpro inhibitor approved for the treatment of COVID-19 patients (Owen et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022b). The development of nirmatrelvir was a major breakthrough since it validated inhibition of Mpro as a treatment for COVID-19.

Nirmatrelvir treatment complements vaccination campaigns and provides a safe means to cure SARS-CoV-2 infections in vulnerable groups. Successfully, it has contributed to reducing the death rate associated with SARS-CoV-2 infections and helped enable a return to pre-COVID-19 lifestyles. Nevertheless, an anti-HIV agent ritonavir must be taken along with nirmatrelvir to enhance its efficacy (Owen et al., 2021). The use of the ritonavir ingredient of paxlovid limits the clinical scope of nirmatrelvir, because ritonavir has side effects and its use may affect the efficacy of other medications that are commonly prescribed in vulnerable groups (Akhvlediani et al., 2023; Xu and Desai, 2009). To overcome this limitation, PF-07817883 (ibuzatrelvir) was developed by Pfizer as a second-generation SARS-CoV-2 Mpro inhibitor (Allerton et al., 2024). It has improved metabolic stability versus nirmatrelvir and no longer requires ritonavir to prolong blood concentration. Encouragingly, PF-07817883 exhibits extensive inhibitory activity against Mpro from different human coronaviruses in vitro, including SARS-CoV-2 delta and Omicron variants of concern, and demonstrates efficacy in a mouse-adapted SARS-CoV-2 model following oral administration (Allerton et al., 2024). Additionally, in vitro assessments indicate that PF-07817883 has low potential for drug-drug interactions (Allerton et al., 2024). Thus, PF-07317883 could serve as a clinical candidate for the treatment of COVID-19 as well as the diseases caused by other human coronavirus in the future.

Although PF-07817883 inhibits multiple coronaviral Mpros and its SARS-CoV-2 Mpro complex structure is known (PDB: 8V4U), no crystal structures with most other Mpros or any resistant mutants are available, leaving its interaction mechanism unclear (Allerton et al., 2024). To fill this gap, in this study, we solved the crystal structures of PF-07817883 in complex with Mpros from SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV, HCoV-NL63, HCoV-229E, and several SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants carrying previously reported single amino acid substitution, including G15S, K90R, E166N, and E166R (Ullrich et al., 2022; Ou et al., 2023). We further perform, to our knowledge, the first molecular dynamics simulations and binding free-energy calculations to evaluate the potencies of PF-07817883 against these Mpros, revealing that both E166N and E166R mutants exhibit significant differences in sensitivity to PF-07817883, displaying distinct enzymatic inhibitory profiles.

Detailed structural analysis provided molecular basis for the interactions between PF-07817883 and various coronaviral Mpros, and Mpro inhibitions by PF-07817883. Together, these data demonstrate that PF-07817883 is potent against β-coronaviruses (e.g., SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV) but only weakly active against α-coronaviruses (e.g., HCoV-229E, HCoV-NL63); therefore, it can be described as a “broad-spectrum inhibitor against selected α/β coronaviral Mpros”. This provides an experimental and theoretical foundation for the continued development of potent, broad-spectrum Mpro-targeted antivirals against coronaviruses.

Materials and methods

Expression and purification of Mpro from different human CoVs and coronavirus mutant

The codon-optimized cDNAs for Mpro of SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV, HCoV-229E, HCoV-NL63 and SARS-CoV-2 mutants (K90R, G15S, E166N, and E166R) were commercially synthesized from Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd., the target genes were amplified by the polymerase chain reaction and subcloned into the pet-28a vector with a 6 × His tag. The constructed plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells, which were acquired from Tiangen Biochemical Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd., for protein expression. The recombinant E. coli was cultured in 1 L of LB broth at 37 °C to express SARS – CoV - 2 Mpro mutants. When OD600 was 0.6–0.8, IPTG was added to a final concentration of 500 μmol/L, and the culture was induced overnight at 18 °C. After induction, the bacteria were harvested, crushed under high - pressure, and centrifuged at 4 °C (11,000 rpm, 30 min) to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was initially purified with a nickel column and further purified with the Superdex 200 PG column. The expression and purification of these proteins are performed according to the standard methods of our laboratory. TEV protease was added to remove the N-terminal His tag.

Inhibition assay for enzymatic activity

The inhibitory activity of PF-07817883 against Mpro was evaluated using commercially synthesized fluorescent donor-quencher substrate pairs which peptide with the sequence Dabcyl-KTSAVLQSGFRKME-Edans (GL Biochem,730,985-85-1). Based fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) enzymatic assays were conducted in 384-well microtiter plates (PerkinElmer, Cat No: 8213–21,381), with PF-07321332 serving as the positive control to evaluate the activity of PF-07817883 pairs against Mpro. By adding a FRET substrate to a reaction buffer system (1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.3), and 150 mM NaCl), the reaction was initiated. Meanwhile, blank control and solvent control groups were established: the former only had reaction buffer, while the latter had the inhibitor substituted with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, TCI, Cat No: D5293). All experimental conditions were independently executed in triplicate. Stock solutions of PF-07817883 and PF-07321332 (10 mM) were prepared in DMSO, 3-fold serial dilution, and mixed with coronaviral Mpro proteins and SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants. The mixture was incubated at ambient temperature for 30 min to facilitate protein-ligand binding. Fluorescence signals were continuously monitored throughout the reaction using a microplate reader, and relative fluorescence units were recorded for subsequent analysis. Inhibition percentages of PF-07817883 against the target Mpros were finally calculated using GraphPad Prism software, by fitting the dose-response curves with a standard four-parameter logistic model (Inhibitor vs. response—Variable slope). To account for background fluorescence and solvent effects inherent in the assay system, the inhibition rate was calculated using normalized luminescence data according to the formula: Inhibition (%) = 100 × [1 – (RLU_compound - RLU_control) / (RLU_DMSO - RLU_control)].

Crystallization and data collection

PF-07817883 was mixed with Mpros at a molar ratio of 5:1 and incubated on ice for 30 min.The mixtures were crystallized using the sitting-drop vapor-diffusion method by mixing 0.2 μL protein and 0.2 μL reservoir solution at 18 °C. After about five days, the complex crystal of Mpros with PF-07817883 was obtained. Complex crystal of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro with PF-07817883 were grown in the reservoir solution comprising 0.1 M HEPES pH 7.0, 22% w/v PEG 10000. Complex crystals of SARS-CoV Mpro with PF-07817883 were grown in the reservoir solution comprising 0.1 M HEPES pH 6.5, 10% PEG 8000, 8% Ethylene glycol. Complex crystals of HCoV-229E Mpro with PF-07817883 were grown in the reservoir solution comprising 10% Isopropanol, 0.1 M HEPES pH 8.0, 18% PEG3350. Complex crystals of HCoV-NL63 Mpro with PF-07817883 were grown in the reservoir solution comprising 0.1 M Sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate pH 5.5, 20% w/v PEG 20000. Complex crystals of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro K90R with PF-07817883 were grown in the reservoir solution comprising 0.1 M NaSO4, 20% PEG3350. Complex crystals of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro G15S with PF-07817883 were grown in the reservoir solution comprising 0.2 M Na2SO4, 20% PEG3350. Complex crystals of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro E166N with PF-07817883 were grown in the reservoir solution comprising 0.15 M NaSO4, 18% PEG3350. Complex crystals of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro E166R with PF-07817883 were grown in the reservoir solution comprising 0.15 M NaSO4, 20% PEG3350. All data sets were collected at 100 K on macromolecular crystallography beamline10U2 (BL10U2) at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF, Shanghai, China).

Molecular dynamics simulations

The initial structure of each system was derived from the crystalline experiments. PF-07817883 forms a covalent thiomidate bond with the catalytic residue C145 of Mpro. Because standard force fields do not contain parameters for this covalent linkage, the covalently linked ligand and C145 were treated together as a non-standard residue. The molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS v2022.03 (Van Der Spoel et al., 2005; Abraham et al., 2015) on the protein–small molecule complexes obtained from molecular docking. The detailed procedures and parameters are as follows. The PDB file was converted to a .gro coordinate file using the `pdb2gmx` tool, and the corresponding topology (.top) and force field parameter files were generated using the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field (Lindorff-Larsen et al., 2010). For the small molecule, pretreatment was performed using AmberTools22 to assign the GAFF force field (Özpinar et al., 2010), and Gaussian 16 W was used for hydrogen addition and RESP potential calculation, with the resulting potential data added to the topology file of the MD system. Each protein–small molecule complex was solvated using the TIP3P water model (Nayar et al., 2011), with a minimum distance of at least 1.2 nm between the protein atoms and the edge of the water box, and an appropriate number of Na+ ions was added to neutralize the system charge. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm (Donnelly et al., 2021). For short-range electrostatic and van der Waals interactions, a cutoff distance of 1.0 nm was applied; for long-range electrostatic interactions, the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method was used (Essmann et al., 1995). All bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm (Hess et al., 1997). During MD simulations, pressure was maintained at 1 bar using the Berendsen barostat (Berendsen et al., 1984), and temperature was regulated using the V-rescale coupling method (Bou-Rabee, 2014). Subsequently, the protein structure was first subjected to a 100 ps pre-equilibration to optimize its initial conformation in the solvent. Equilibration was then performed for 100 ps in the NVT ensemble to heat the protein–solvent system to the target temperature, followed by 100 ps of equilibration in the NPT ensemble for pressure balancing. After completing energy minimization and system equilibration, a 100 ns MD simulation was performed for each protein–small molecule complex under periodic boundary conditions in the NPT ensemble, with the system temperature maintained at 300 K and pressure at 1 bar. The integration time step was 2 fs, and trajectories (including coordinates, velocities, and energies) were recorded every 10 ps for subsequent analysis. To comprehensively evaluate the stability and conformational changes of the protein–ligand complexes during the MD simulation, the simulation trajectories were extracted and the following analyses were performed: root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), solvent accessible surface area (SASA), and hydrogen bonds (H-bonds).

MM/PBSA calculations

To further evaluate the binding stability of the protein–ligand complexes, the last 20 ns of stable trajectories were analyzed using the gmx_MMPBSA script (dependent on MMPBSA.py v16.0) (Valdés-Tresanco et al., 2021). The binding free energy of each protein–ligand complex was calculated using the molecular mechanics–Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) method (Genheden and Ryde, 2015), providing an in-depth quantitative assessment of the binding stability and affinity of the system. Thus, binding free energy is of great value in computational biology and is widely used in drug design, protein–protein interaction analysis, structural optimization, and related fields. The MM/PBSA method decomposes the binding free energy (ΔGbind) into the molecular mechanics energy (ΔGgas) and the solvation free energy (ΔGsolv). ΔGgas includes the van der Waals interaction energy (ΔEvdW) and the electrostatic interaction energy (ΔEelec). ΔGsolv consists of the polar solvation energy (ΔGpolar, i.e., ΔEGB) and the nonpolar solvation energy (ΔGnonpolar, i.e., ΔEsurf). The calculation formula is as follows:

Results

Inhibitory activities of PF-07817883 against different Mpros and SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants

Following established protocols (Zhang et al., 2022), we expressed and purified Mpro from SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV, HCoV-229E, and HCoV-NL63, along with four SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants (G15S, K90R, E166N, E166R) associated with circulating variants. The inhibitory activities of PF-07817883 against different coronavirus Mpro and SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants were systematically evaluated by FRET assay, and PF-07321332 was used as a positive control. It demonstrated differential inhibitory activity of PF-07817883 against the tested targets. The compound exhibited exceptional potency against wild-type SARS-CoV-2 Mpro (IC50 = 0.009 μM, Fig. 1A), maintaining moderate activity against SARS-CoV (6.99 μM, Fig. 1B). In contrast, it showed nearly no significant inhibitory activity against α-coronaviruses HCoV-229E (At the maximum test concentration (100 μM), the inhibition rate was below 50%, hence IC₅₀ > 100 μM), and HCoV-NL63 (At the maximum test concentration (100 μM), the inhibition rate was below 50%, 98.44 μM) (Figs. 1C-D). Notably, this molecule still exhibited substantial inhibitory activity against four of the currently relevant SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants, the IC50 value is 10.08 μM (G15S), 11.51 μM (K90R), 50.09 μM (E166N), and 5.25 μM (E166R), respectively (Figs. 1E-1H). Even though this IC50 values were elevated compared to those of the wild-type variant, suggesting structural adaptability against emerging variants. These data collectively establish PF-07817883 as a dual-action inhibitor capable of neutralizing both conserved coronavirus Mpro domains and mutation-induced structural variations. The inhibitory profile across divergent coronaviruses and variant-associated mutants highlights its potential as a template for developing pandemic-resilient antiviral agents.

Determination of crystal structures of PF-07817883 in complex with different coronaviral Mpros and SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants

PF-07817883 was developed to achieve broad spectrum Mpro inhibition like nirmatrelvir (Owen et al., 2021), but without the requirement for pharmacokinetic boosting effects of ritonavir. With an aim to reveal the inhibitory mechanism of PF-07817883, we crystallized PF-07817883 in complex with Mpros from two representatives of alphacoronavirus (HCoV-NL63 and HCoV-229E) and two representatives of betacoronavirus (SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2). These crystal structures were solved to 1.91 Å (SARS-CoV-2 Mpro-PF-07817883), 2.04 Å (SARS-CoV Mpro-PF-07817883), 1.92 Å (HCoV-NL63 Mpro-PF-07817883), and 1.78 Å (HCoV-229E Mpro-PF-07817883) (Table 1). SARS-CoV-2 Mpro-PF-07817883 and SARS-CoV Mpro-PF-07817883 structures are in space group C121 and P1, respectively, while HCoV-NL63 Mpro-PF-07817883 and HCoV-229E Mpro -PF-07817883 structures belong to space group P1211.

In view of potential drug resistance, structural characterization of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants in complex with the inhibitor is needed to support Mpro targeted therapy. Four previously reported SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants (G15S, K90R, E166N, and E166R) are included for co-crystalization with PF-07817883 in this study. Among them, G15S and K90R mutations were previously dominant in Lambda and Gamma variants (Ullrich et al., 2022), respectively. E166 is known to be a hot spot that form direct interactions with inhibitors and substrates and may develop drug resistance (Ou et al., 2023). E166R and E166N were investigated since they represent the other two states of charge, positively charged and uncharged states, respectively. These two substitutions have been detected in the population, with the occurrence rates being 0.182% and 0.00166%, respectively. To clarify the interaction details between PF-07817883 and these four Mpro mutants, their complex structures were solved to 1.76 Å (G15S), 1.49 Å (K90R), 2.07 Å (E166N), and 1.92 Å (E166R) resolution, respectively (Table 2). The structures of Mpro G15S and K90R mutants bound to PF-07817883 belong to space group P1211, while the structures of the other two mutants bound to PF-07817883 belong to space group C121.

Crystal structure of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro in complex with PF-07817883

To comprehend the inhibitory mechanism of PF-07817883 on SARS-CoV-2 Mpro, we analyzed the crystal structure of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro-PF-07817883. In SARS-CoV-2 Mpro-PF-07817883 structure, there is only one protomer in an asymmetric unit, which can be divided into three domains (I, II, and III) (Fig. 2A). The molecule PF-07817883 lies in the substrate-binding pocket, featuring a non-canonical dyad (C145 and H41) and locating in the cleft between domains I and II of Mpro. To elucidate the binding mode of PF-07817883 with SARS-CoV-2 Mpro, structural analyses reveal that PF-07817883 occupies the S1’, S1, S2, and S4 subsites of Mpro (Fig. 2B). Specifically, a nitrile group is positioned at the P1’ site of PF-07817883, and the electron density map unambiguously demonstrates the formation of a covalent bond between the nitrile carbon of PF-07817883 and the sulfur atom of C145, yielding a thioimidate adduct ((Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the imine nitrogen of this thioimidate moiety resides in the oxyanion hole, forming direct or water-mediated hydrogen bonds with N142, G143, and C14 5(Fig. 2D and E). Show as Fig. 2D and E, at the P1 site, PF-07817883 harbors a γ-lactam ring that inserts into the S1 subsite of Mpro. The oxygen and nitrogen atoms of this lactam ring form hydrogen bonds with the side chains of H163 and E166, respectively, while the amide nitrogen at the P1 moiety establishes a hydrogen bond with the main-chain carbonyl oxygen of H164. Additionally, a hydrogen bond is observed between F140 and PF-07817883 in the PF-07817883–SARS-CoV-2 Mpro complex. The P2 position of PF-07817883 contains a (4R)-4-trifluoromethylproline group. The fluorine atoms within this trifluoromethylproline group form three hydrogen bonds with R188 and Q189, and the carbonyl oxygen forms a hydrogen bond with the main-chain NH of E166. At the P3 site, PF-07817883 bears a tert-leucine residue, which exhibits limited interactions with the S3 subsite of Mpro (Fig. 2D and E). Finally, a methylcarbamate moiety is present at the P4 position of PF-07817883, where the amide nitrogen of this moiety forms a hydrogen bond with the main-chain carbonyl oxygen of E166. Thus, PF-07317883 occupies the active site of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro via forming a S—C covalent bond with the catalytic C145 and forming multiple noncovalent interactions with the residues surrounding the active site, including F140, N142, G143, C145, H163, H164, E166, R188, and Q189.

The binding energy of PF-07817883 with different Mpros

Binding free-energy calculation was performed to evaluate the potency of PF-07817883 against investigated Mpros and Mpro mutants. By starting from these experimental structures, molecular dynamic simulation was carried out and the trajectory after 100 ns of simulation was used to calculate the binding free-energy of PF-07817883 to different Mpros. To evaluate the conformational stability and binding mechanism of PF-07817883 with different coronaviral Mpros and SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants, we performed 100-ns molecular dynamics simulations and analyzed protein backbone RMSD, ligand RMSD, solvent accessible surface area (SASA), residue RMSF, total hydrogen bond count, pocket residue RMSF, B-factor, and hydrogen bond occupancy (Fig. 8, Fig. S2). As shown in Fig. 8A, most systems reached a plateau after 10–20 ns of simulation. SARS-CoV Mpro and HCoV-NL63 Mpro exhibited the lowest protein RMSD (≈0.17–0.23 nm), indicating the most stable complex conformations; SARS-CoV-2 Mpro wild-type showed the largest fluctuations (RMSD up to 0.5–0.6 nm), suggesting pronounced global conformational rearrangements. The G15S mutant displayed moderately elevated RMSD (≈0.30–0.40 nm), whereas E166N, E166R, and K90R showed intermediate fluctuations, indicating that these mutants retain stability but with increased conformational adaptability. As shown in Fig. 8B, ligand RMSD remained within 0.05–0.18 nm for all systems with no persistent drift, confirming that PF-07817883 did not dissociate from the binding pocket. Notably, although SARS-CoV-2 Mpro wild-type exhibited high protein RMSD, its ligand RMSD did not increase correspondingly, implying that its instability primarily originated from global/local backbone rearrangements rather than ligand displacement. SASA analysis revealed that most systems showed a slight decrease over time, trending toward more compact conformations (Fig. 8C). The G15S, E166R, K90R, and wild-type exhibited a notable late-stage reduction in SASA (final values 133–140 nm2), indicating surface contraction. In contrast, E166N maintained a higher SASA (148–156 nm2), suggesting that this mutant, while able to bind the ligand, adopts a more “open” conformation. RMSF analysis showed that high fluctuations were mainly located at the N/C-termini and loop regions (Fig. 8D). SARS-CoV Mpro had the lowest RMSF (most residues 0.08–0.15 nm), indicating minimal local flexibility. SARS-CoV-2 Mpro wild-type displayed prominent peaks at residues 140–150 and 230–280 (0.30–0.40 nm or higher), reflecting enhanced conformational flexibility in these functional regions. E166N also showed local spikes but weaker than wild-type. Hydrogen bond analysis showed that HCoV-NL63 Mpro formed the most abundant and persistent hydrogen bonds (typically 4–7, occasionally 8–9), followed by SARS-CoV Mpro, whereas most SARS-CoV-2 Mpro-related systems formed only 1–4 hydrogen bonds (Fig. 8E). Occupancy analysis further revealed that all systems shared a core anchoring motif: a double hydrogen bond between the inhibitor and the backbone of residues 165/166, with occupancies ranging from 87.10% to 98.99% (Fig. S2). Specifically, HCoV-229E Mpro (GLU165) showed occupancies of 87.10% and 93.15%; SARS-CoV-2 Mpro wild-type, 72.64% and 97.11%; and mutants E166N, G15S, and K90R achieved 91.65–97.92%, 97.90–98.73%, and 97.79–98.99%, respectively. In addition, HCoV-NL63 Mpro and SARS-CoV Mpro formed high-occupancy auxiliary hydrogen bond networks (e.g., HCoV-NL63 GLN164@O···Lig@N19 at 97.31%; SARS-CoV Mpro HIS163@O···Lig@N19 at 89.35%), further enhancing binding stability, whereas SARS-CoV-2 Mpro wild-type exhibited weaker peripheral interactions (HIS164@O···Lig@N19 at only 38.70%; GLY143@N···Lig@N22 at 34.91%). Pocket residue RMSF and B-factor analyses indicated that major fluctuations were concentrated at residues 45–53 (pocket 1) and 150–154 (pocket 2) (Fig. 8F, Fig. 8G). SARS-CoV-2 Mpro wild-type showed the most pronounced pocket fluctuations (peak ≈0.38 nm), indicating poor local stability. SARS-CoV Mpro and E166N exhibited lower pocket RMSF (most residues 0.05–0.20 nm), suggesting a more stable binding pocket. B-factor analysis confirmed that the core binding regions remained rigid, with differences mainly in peripheral loops and distal termini. HCoV-NL63 and HCoV-229E displayed the lowest overall flexibility. The mutants E166N, E166R, G15S, and K90R reshaped the flexibility distribution, with K90R showing the most extensive increase in medium-to-high flexibility. In summary, SARS-CoV and HCoV-NL63 formed the most stable complexes with PF-07817883 (low RMSD, low RMSF, high hydrogen bond counts, high-occupancy auxiliary networks, and stable binding pocket). SARS-CoV-2 wild-type exhibited the largest global conformational shifts, greater local flexibility, and weaker peripheral hydrogen bonds, indicating relatively lower stability. The E166N mutant maintained ligand binding but adopted an open conformation, whereas G15S, E166R, and K90R tended toward more compact structures without fully eliminating local fluctuations. These differences provide a molecular basis for understanding the broad-spectrum activity and resistance mechanisms of PF-07817883.

To further characterize the binding stability of the complex, the final 20 ns stable RMSD trajectory calculated using the MM/PBSA method was employed to determine the complex's binding free energy. Because the covalent thiomidate linkage between PF07817883 and Cys145 was not explicitly included in the MD topology, the present simulations should be interpreted as describing the non-covalent/pre-reactive binding state. Accordingly, the MM/PBSA-derived ΔG values were used only to compare the non-covalent energetic stabilization of the binding interface and should not be regarded as quantitative covalent binding free energies. The MM/PBSA results showed that all eight complexes possess spontaneous binding ability, with total binding free energies (ΔGtotal) ranging from–36.28 to–49.79 kcal/mol (Fig. 9A). The SARS-CoV-2 Mpro K90R (−49.79 kcal/mol), SARS-CoV-2 Mpro E166R (−49.60 kcal/mol), and SARS-CoV Mpro (−49.51 kcal/mol) exhibited the strongest binding affinity, followed by HCoV-229E Mpro (−48.27 kcal/mol). SARS-CoV-2 Mpro WT (−45.52 kcal/mol) demonstrated moderate binding affinity, while HCoV-NL63 Mpro (−36.28 kcal/mol) showed the weakest binding. The differences of binding free energies in different systems can be further revealed by the differences in individual energy terms (Fig. 9B). The primary driving forces for binding originate from van der Waals interactions and electrostatic interactions, with ΔVDWAALS ranging from-43.87 to-57.48 kcal/mol and ΔEelec ranging from-21.22 to-39.43 kcal/mol. In contrast, the polar solvation energy ΔEGB (38.23–51.41 kcal/mol) significantly counteracts the binding effect. These results indicate that the K90R and E166R mutations significantly enhance the binding stability of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro with the ligand, whereas HCoV-NL63 Mpro exhibits the weakest thermodynamic binding advantage. Overall, these data suggest that PF-07817883 shows comparable potency against various Mpros and support that mutant is somewhat affected by PF-07817883.

Discussion

In 21st century, three fatal coronaviruses, including SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV, and SARS-CoV-2, have emerged (Kesheh et al., 2022; Fung and Liu, 2021; Tang et al., 2022). Up to now, the SARS-CoV-2 continues to spread and mutate globally, posing a threat to the public health. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop effective drugs to treat SARS-CoV-2 infection. Mpro of SARS-CoV-2 is a promising drug target due to its essential role in viral replication (Anand et al., 2003; Jin et al., 2020; Hegyi and Ziebuhr, 2002; Mengist et al., 2021). A great number of promising compounds have been identified and a few drug candidates have been developed since the emergency of SARS-CoV-2 (Ma et al., 2020; Kitamura et al., 2022; Drayman et al., 2021; Owen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022; Boras et al., 2021). Among these, PF-07321332 is a first-in-class oral small-molecule inhibitor against SARS-CoV-2 Mpro, which is co-administrated with ritonavir under the brand-name paxlovid to treat COVID-19 (Owen et al., 2021). Paxlovid treatment has contributed to reducing the death rate associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection and helps to enable a return to pre-COVID-19 lifestyles. However, the co-administration of the ritonavir ingredient of paxlovid limits the clinical use of nirmatrelvir.

Recently, researchers from Pfizer have reported on the development of PF-07817883 as a nirmatrelvir-derived second-generation orally active Mpro inhibitor and clinical candidate to treat SARS-CoV-2 infections (Allerton et al., 2024).

A phase I clinical trial (NCT05580003) was released to evaluate the safety and blood levels of PF-07817883 in healthy people. Currently, a phase II clinical trial (NCT05799495) to explore the effect and safety of PF-07817883 in adults has also been conducted. Previous study has shown that PF-07817883 displays potent efficacy against Mpros from all seven human coronaviruses and a wide range of SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern (Allerton et al., 2024). These data suggest that PF-07817883 could be considered as a pan-human coronavirus Mpro inhibitor.

This study centers on the coronavirus Mpro and SARS-CoV-2 Mpro variants, investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying their interactions with PF-07817883-specifically how these genetic variants may impact inhibitor binding and efficacy. The binding affinity of PF-07817883 to wild-type SARS-CoV-2 Mpro and four SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants (G15S, K90R, E166N and E166R) are evaluated via enzyme inhibition assays, providing quantitative insights into ligand-protein interactions. This experiment reveals that PF07817883 exhibits high efficacy against β-CoV Mpros (including Mpros from SARS-CoV-2and its mutants, SARS-CoV) but weak activity against α-CoV Mpro (HCoV-NL63 and HCoV-229E). Therefore, it should be termed having broad-spectrum activity against selected β-CoV Mpro. Thereby demonstrating its broad-spectrum inhibitory effect of PF-07817883 against genetically divergent selected viral variants is proven again by using molecular dynamic simulation and binding free energy calculation.

Complementary to biochemical analyses, structural characterization is achieved through the hanging-drop vapor diffusion technique, enabling the resolution of high-resolution crystal structures for multiple Mpro-inhibitor complexes. Specifically, the study reports the atomic-level structures of different coronaviral Mpros (SARS-CoV-2 Mpro, SARS-CoV Mpro, HCoV-NL63 Mpro, and HCoV-229E Mpro), and several SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants (G15S, K90R, E166N, and E166R), which represents key mutant forms of the protease. These crystallographic datasets—resolved to near-atomic precision—afford a systematic framework for dissecting the inhibitory profile of PF-07817883 against structurally divergent Mpro mutants. The crystal structures of PF-07817883 in complex with these Mpros were solved. Structural analysis not only illustrated the shared molecular basis for PF-07817883-Mpro interactions, but also presented some differences of PF-07817883 when bound to different Mpros. The conventional binding pattern of PF-07817883 includes covalent binding with C145 and several key hydrogen bonds with F140, C145, H163, and Glu166. Such a binding mode supports the potent inhibitory activity of PF-07817883 and update the previous report about the discovery and potency evaluation of PF-07817883 (Allerton et al., 2024). Also, the information provided in this study are helpful to comprehensively understand the inhibitory mechanism of PF-07817883 and promote PF-07817883-based drug discovery.

Multiple residues in the drug-binding site (including S144, M165, E166, H172, Q192, etc.) have been identified as nirmatrelvir resistance hotspots (Hu et al., 2023; Padhi and Tripathi, 2022). Among these, the E166 position is one of the most clinically prominent and extensively studied resistance mutations; therefore, we selected E166N and E166R as representative mutants for mechanistic investigation (Jochmans et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2022). Previous studies have also shown that different mutations at E166 have different effects on drug resistance, such as the E166R mutation markedly increases resistance to nirmatrelvir, whereas the E166N mutation diminishes enzymatic activity (Ou et al., 2023; Shaqra et al., 2022). This demonstrates the profound effect of the E166 substitution on the binding affinity and functional efficacy of the inhibitor, a result that was also confirmed in this study. Specifically, assays revealed that the E166N mutation which replaces glutamic acid (E) with asparagine (N), markedly reduces inhibitor binding affinity, leading to a substantial decrease in inhibitory efficacy. Specifically, PF-07817883 exhibited an IC₅₀ value exceeding 50 μM against the E166N mutant—markedly higher than the wild-type Mpro's IC₅₀ of 0.009 μM—indicating a > 5000-fold reduction in inhibitory potency (Fig. 1A,G). When the E166R mutation, which replaces glutamic acid with arginine (R), introduces a larger side chain, and the positively charged side chain of the mutant residue forms a more stable electrostatic interaction with the inhibito, with the IC50 value increasing >500-fold compared to the wild-type Mpro (Fig. 1A, F). By comparison, it was found that the inhibitory activity of the E166N mutant is approximately ten-fold lower than that of the E166R, underscoring the pivotal role of glutamic acid at position 166 (E166) in modulating inhibitor binding efficacy (Fig. 1G, F). These findings underscore the critical role of E166 in mediating inhibitor interactions, despite its non-catalytic structural involvement in the protease active site. The substitution at position E166 disrupts disrupts the existing interaction. In the Mpro E166R-PF-07817883 complex structure, the nitrogen atom of the amide ring at the P1 position of PF-07817883 maintains a hydrogen bond with the residue 166, analogous to the wild-type interaction (Fig. 2D, E, 7D,H). However, in the Mpro E166N-PF-07817883 structure, this amide nitrogen forms hydrogen bonds with His172, His163, and a water molecule-an interaction network absent in both the wild-type and E166R complexes (Fig. 2D, E,7C,D,G,H). Consequently, despite both E166N and E166R mutants exhibiting significant differences in sensitivity to PF-07817883, the E166N mutant shows a more substantial impact on enzyme activity. Notably, although the binding free energies of E166N and E166R mutants were comparable (−46.39 vs. –49.60 kcal/mol), the IC50 of E166N was 10-fold higher than that of E166R (i.e., weaker inhibitory activity). To further understand the dynamic basis of these differences, we performed molecular dynamics simulations. The RMSD values of the E166N and E166R complexes were generally at a moderate fluctuation level, indicating overall conformational stability but enhanced adaptability compared to wild-type. In terms of ligand binding stability, E166N showed lower and more compact ligand RMSD, suggesting stable ligand positioning, whereas E166R displayed a mild increase in ligand RMSD in the later stage (up to 0.20–0.23 nm), indicating some ligand reorientation. RMSF analysis revealed local spikes for E166N, implying enhanced conformational flexibility in certain regions. Notably, the two mutants diverged in protein compactness: E166N maintained a higher SASA (148–156 nm2), adopting a more “open”conformation; in contrast, E166R showed a pronounced late-stage reduction in SASA (to 133–140 nm2), indicating surface contraction and a tighter folding state. Overall, E166N tends to maintain stable ligand positioning but remains open and locally flexible, while E166R adopts a more compact pocket with minor ligand reorientation. These dynamic differences provide a structural basis for understanding their distinct inhibitory activities: E166R achieves stronger binding through compaction and electrostatic attraction, whereas E166N, despite stable ligand localization, may suffer from an open conformation and local flexibility that compromise its inhibitory efficacy. This interpretation is consistent with the binding free energies (−46.39 kcal/mol for E166N vs. -49.60 kcal/mol for E166R) and explains why E166N is approximately ten-fold less potent. These findings provide a structural and mechanistic basis for elucidating how mutations at the E166 residue modulate the functional properties of the protease.

The MD simulations in this study revealed that the binding free energy of PF-07817883 with the HCoV-NL63 Mpro was much lower than that with SARS-CoV-2 Mpro,which is consistent with the enzymatic inhibition assays showing that PF-07817883 exhibits almost no inhibitory activity against HCoV-NL63 Interestingly, despite forming dynamically stable complexes (low RMSD and low RMSF) with both SARS-CoV and HCoV-NL63, the binding free energies of the two systems diverged dramatically: SARS-CoV bound strongly (−49.51 kcal/mol), whereas NL63 bound most weakly (−36.28 kcal/mol). Energy decomposition revealed that the weak binding of NL63 primarily arose from its poor van der Waals contribution (ΔEVDW = −43.87 kcal/mol, the lowest among all systems), even though its rigid pocket locked the ligand in a fixed conformation. In contrast, SARS-CoV achieved strong affinity through both favorable van der Waals (−54.73 kcal/mol) and electrostatic (−39.43 kcal/mol) terms. The wild-type SARS-CoV-2 Mpro, despite poor dynamic stability, exhibited the strongest van der Waals interactions (−57.48 kcal/mol), explaining its exceptionally potent inhibition (IC50 = 9 nM) through hydrophobic-driven binding. It should be noted that HCoV-229E represents an exception: although its calculated binding free energy is strong (−48.27 kcal/mol), its enzymatic inhibition is very weak (IC₅₀ > 100 μM). The interaction forces between HCoV-229E at the S1’ site and SARS-CoV-2 Mpro show significant differences, indicating that the variation in S1’ site forces has a notable impact on enzymatic activity. This inconsistency may be related to significant differences in the interaction forces at the S1’ site between HCoV-229E and SARS-CoV-2 Mpro, and/or to an overestimation of polar solvation energy by the MM/PBSA method, a higher substrate affinity (lower Km) that hinders competition, or a stalled covalent bond formation step. It indicating that variations in S1’ site forces have an important impact on enzymatic activity and these possibilities warrant further investigation.

It should be objectively stated that the MD simulations in this study have certain methodological limitations: only a single complex simulation was performed, without ligand-independent simulations or multi-trajectory replication analyses. However, it is important to emphasize that the original simulation duration was set to 100 ns, and verification via the RMSD index showed that the system reached a stable state after the first stage of the simulation (10–20 ns). No unstable phenomena such as ligand dissociation or protein misfolding were observed, indicating that this single trajectory can effectively reflect the core binding characteristics of the complex. Furthermore, the core objective of the MD simulations was to verify the stability of the core binding mode between PF-07817883 and Mpro (e.g., maintenance of covalent bonds, integrity of key hydrogen bond networks), rather than systematically covering all possible conformations. Thus, the limitation of a single trajectory had a minimal impact on the core conclusions. Indirect support can be provided by combining the ligand structural characteristics and simulation results: the core scaffold of PF-07817883 (the P1 γ-lactam ring and P2 trifluoromethylproline group) has a rigid cyclic structure, and the spatial orientation of its key functional groups (e.g., P1’ cyano group, P2 trifluoromethyl group) is constrained by the cyclic system, preventing large conformational changes. Meanwhile, complex simulations showed that the ligand forms multiple synergistic interactions with the active site of the protein, which can further stabilize the ligand conformation and inhibit post-binding conformational rearrangement. Although ligand-independent simulations were not performed, based on the rigid structural characteristics of the ligand, conformational stability in complex simulations, and research backgrounds of homologous inhibitors, it can be reasonably inferred that there is no significant change in the core conformation during binding. Therefore, this methodological limitation did not have a substantial impact on the core conclusions of the study. It should be noted that PF07817883 forms a covalent thiomidate bond with Cys145 in the experimental structure. However, in the present MD simulations, this covalent linkage was not explicitly incorporated into the ligand–protein topology. Therefore, the simulations mainly describe the non-covalent stabilization and conformational behavior of the pre-reactive binding complex rather than the fully covalent inhibitor-protein adduct. In this context, the MM/PBSA-derived ΔG values should be interpreted with caution, as they primarily reflect non-covalent energetic contributions and do not account for the free energy associated with covalent bond formation. It also should be noted that this study did not perform a systematic quantitative assessment of residue-wise interaction persistence, as such analysis typically requires a longer simulation timescale (≥500 ns) or multiple replica trajectories to obtain statistically reliable conclusions. Under the current simulation conditions (a single 100 ns trajectory), only indirect assessment through hydrogen bond occupancy analysis and key distance monitoring was conducted to avoid overinterpretation. Future studies can further optimize the analysis by supplementing replicate simulations, ligand-independent simulations, and enhanced sampling techniques. In addition, from an experimental perspective, this study lacks cellular validation. This study did not validate the antiviral activity of PF-07817883 at the cellular or live-virus level, primarily due to the lack of BSL-3 facilities at our institution. The literature has reported cellular antiviral activity of this compound against β-coronaviruses (SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV, MERS), but that study did not provide the specific EC50 value for the α-coronavirus HCoV-229E, nor did it report cellular activity for HCoV-NL63. Comparison of the antiviral activity and enzymatic inhibition profiles indicates that PF-07817883 is markedly more potent against β-coronaviruses than against the α-coronavirus HCoV-229E, and its enzymatic inhibition (IC50) against HCoV-229E and HCoV-NL63 is weaker than that against β-coronaviruses. Notably, the IC50 value for HCoV-229E Mpro determined in our study (>100 μM) differs considerably from the value reported by Allerton et al. (≈100 nM). We attribute this discrepancy to subtle differences in experimental conditions, such as protein construct, buffer composition, or assay temperature. Since the primary goal of our study was to compare the relative sensitivity of different Mpros to PF-07817883 rather than to determine absolute IC50 values, our conclusion (that both 229E and NL63 are very weakly inhibited) remains consistent across our experimental replicates. Based on these data, it is reasonable to predict that the compound exhibits antiviral activity against β-coronaviruses at the cellular level, while its antiviral activity against α-coronaviruses is very low. Therefore, despite the lack of independent live-virus validation, existing evidence supports that the biochemical inhibitory effect of PF-07817883 can be translated into antiviral efficacy in a cellular environment. By integrating structural and functional analyses, the study delineates how amino acid substitutions in the protease active site modulate inhibitor engagement, thereby offering mechanistic clarity on the structural basis of drug resistance and binding specificity in coronaviral Mpro variants.

Conclusion

In this study, we clarified the binding modes of PF-07817883 with the Mpro of four coronaviruses, namely SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV, HCoV-NL63, and HCoV-229E, as well as SARS-CoV-2 Mpro mutants carrying reported single amino acid substitutions (including G15S, K90R, E166N, and E166R), through X-ray crystallographic analysis. We further systematically evaluated its inhibitory efficacy by integrating molecular dynamics simulations, binding free energy calculations, and enzymatic inhibition assays. The results demonstrated that PF-07817883 exhibits inhibitory activity against multiple coronaviral Mpros, with significant differences in sensitivity observed between the E166N and E166R mutants, thereby revealing the regulatory role of mutation sites in drug binding. These comprehensive structural and functional data not only provide a direct molecular basis for elucidating the interaction mechanisms and inhibitory modes of PF-07817883 with diverse coronaviral Mpros, confirming its potential as a broad-spectrum inhibitor of selected coronaviral Mpro, but also offer critical experimental evidence and theoretical support for the subsequent optimization and development of efficient, broad-spectrum Mpro-targeted inhibitors against coronaviruses. Additionally, this study provides valuable insights for addressing the challenge of reduced drug sensitivity caused by coronavirus mutations.
