Modeling the invasive potential of the opuntia cactus in arid ecosystems by assessing current and future distribution trends
Authors: Dhafer A Al-Bakre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvasive plant species threaten biodiversity, ecosystem stability, and agricultural productivity by outcompeting native flora and altering ecological processes. Opuntia ficus-indica is a highly invasive species that has established itself across arid and semi-arid regions due to its resilience to extreme environmental conditions and rapid vegetative propagation. This study employs ecological niche modeling (ENM) with MaxEnt to assess the current and future distribution of O. ficus-indica in Saudi Arabia. Occurrence records were compiled from field surveys, literature, and biodiversity databases. Occurrence records were compiled from field surveys, literature, and global biodiversity databases. Nineteen bioclimatic, ten soil, and three topographic variables were initially selected, and six key predictors were retained after multicollinearity reduction and Jackknife testing. Model performance was validated using the area under the curve (AUC) and partial ROC to ensure predictive accuracy. The most influential environmental predictors shaping its distribution were maximum temperature of the warmest month (bio5), NDVI, precipitation of the wettest month (bio14), mean temperature of the warmest quarter (bio10), soil organic carbon (SOC), and soil pH. The results indicate that the current distribution of O. ficus-indica is concentrated in southwestern Saudi Arabia, particularly in regions with moderate precipitation and temperature. Future climate projections (SSP245 and SSP585) suggest a northward and inland expansion, with increasing habitat suitability in Najran, Al Qassim, and central Riyadh, while certain highly suitable areas in the southwest may decline due to extreme warming. These findings highlight the potential ecological risks posed by climate-driven invasion and underscore the need for proactive management strategies. Integrated control measures, including mechanical removal, biological control, habitat restoration, and policy interventions, are recommended to mitigate its spread. Introduction

Biological invasions have emerged as one of the most significant drivers of biodiversity loss and ecosystem degradation1,2. Invasive species typically grow rapidly and tolerate diverse environments, allowing them to spread easily in new habitats3. Their spread alters ecosystems, impacting soil, water cycles, nutrients, productivity, and species composition4–6. Human activities like trade, transport, and habitat changes create pathways and disturbances that aid invasive plant establishment7,8. Many invasive taxa exhibit strong competitive advantages through allelopathy and phenotypic plasticity, further exacerbating their dominance over native vegetation9,10. The ecological and economic ramifications of plant invasions necessitate robust predictive frameworks to identify at-risk regions and inform management interventions11.

Climate change is expected to exacerbate biological invasions by altering environmental conditions and expanding the ecological niches of invasive species12. Climate changes boost invasive species’ fitness by affecting physiology, growth, and reproduction13. Warmer winters, longer growing seasons, and increased disturbances like wildfires enable invasive species to expand into new areas14. Climate-driven habitat changes may reduce competition from native species, especially in ecosystems where water availability and temperature limit vegetation growth15,16. Predictive models indicate invasive species will expand their ranges and alter phenology and life-history strategies, complicating control efforts17,18. Understanding these interactions is critical for developing proactive management strategies that integrate both invasion biology and climate adaptation measures19,20.

Ecological Niche Modeling (ENM) has become an essential tool in biogeography and conservation biology, offering quantitative frameworks to predict species distributions based on environmental constraints21. These models integrate occurrence data with abiotic and biotic factors to delineate a species’ fundamental and realized niches, providing insights into habitat suitability under current and projected climate scenarios22. The MaxEnt algorithm, widely regarded as one of the most robust methods for presence-only modeling, applies machine-learning techniques to estimate species-environment relationships and generate probabilistic habitat suitability maps23,24. By leveraging geospatial datasets and climate projections, ENMs have been instrumental in identifying priority areas for conservation, assessing habitat fragmentation, and predicting shifts in biodiversity under global change25. Despite their utility, model accuracy remains contingent on the quality of occurrence records, the selection of predictor variables, and the resolution of environmental datasets26. The incorporation of ensemble modeling approaches and mechanistic simulations has improved the reliability of ENM predictions, particularly for species with complex ecological interactions27.

The application of ENMs in invasion biology has advanced our understanding of the spatial dynamics of invasive species and their potential response to climate change28. These models provide critical insights into habitat invasibility, risk assessment, and the effectiveness of management interventions by identifying high-risk regions before invasions occur29. Studies on invasive plants have demonstrated that ENMs can accurately predict range expansion patterns and inform strategic mitigation efforts, particularly in regions experiencing rapid climatic shifts30. Projections for the invasive plants in arid and semi-arid ecosystems suggest that future climatic conditions may significantly enhance their potential distribution31. By integrating ENMs with field validation and advanced statistical analyses, researchers can refine spatial predictions and develop proactive management strategies to mitigate the ecological and economic consequences of plant invasions32,33. This study utilizes ENM techniques to assess both the current and projected future distribution of O. ficus-indica in Saudi Arabia, offering a predictive framework to guide invasion management under multiple climate change scenarios. Specifically, this research aims to (i) quantify the influence of key bioclimatic and edaphic variables on the habitat suitability of O. ficus-indica using MaxEnt modeling and (ii) evaluate the potential expansion of the species under different climate change scenarios to identify high-risk regions requiring early intervention and control measures. These objectives will contribute to a deeper understanding of the invasion dynamics of O. ficus-indica and inform evidence-based strategies for mitigating its spread in vulnerable ecosystems34,35.

Results

Model validation and performance of O. ficus-indica distribution

The ecological niche model for O. ficus-indica, developed using Maxent, demonstrated exceptional predictive accuracy with an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.954, indicating high sensitivity and specificity in identifying suitable habitats across Saudi Arabia (Fig. 1). The ROC curve analysis revealed an efficient balance in model sensitivity and specificity, substantiating its capability to delineate true species distribution with minimal error. Further validation through omission rates and predicted area assessments confirmed the model’s robustness, with observed omission closely matching predicted rates, ensuring the reliability of the model’s conservative projections as thresholds increased (Fig. 1).

Contribution of environmental variables

The Maxent model’s efficacy in predicting the distribution of O. ficus-indica across Saudi Arabia was significantly influenced by several key environmental variables according to the updated analysis. The variable with the highest contribution was the Maximum Temperature of the Warmest Month (bio5), accounting for 33.8% of the model’s explanatory power and showing a permutation importance of 43.7%, indicating its critical role in the model’s predictive accuracy. Following bio5, the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) contributed 24.4% to the model performance with a permutation importance of 16.3%, suggesting its relevance in distinguishing suitable habitats. The Precipitation of the Wettest Month (bio14) and the Temperature Annual Range (bio10) also played notable roles, contributing 14.3% and 12.6% respectively, with corresponding permutation importances of 19.6% and 7.2%. Soil Organic Carbon (SOC) accounted for 12.1% of the model’s performance, reflecting a permutation importance of 11.3%, which underscores its relevance in habitat suitability modeling. Soil pH had the least influence, with a 2.8% contribution and a permutation importance of 1.9% (Table 1).

Current distribution of O. ficus-indica in Saudi Arabia

The ecological niche modeling results for O. ficus-indica in Saudi Arabia highlight its current distribution and potential habitat suitability across different regions. The model reveals that the highest suitability for O. ficus-indica is concentrated along the western and southwestern parts of the country, particularly in regions with moderate to high elevation, where climatic and soil conditions favor its establishment and spread. The areas with the highest habitat suitability for O. ficus-indica are predominantly found in the Asir, Al Baha, Jizan, and Makkah regions, particularly along the Sarawat Mountain range, which extends parallel to the Red Sea coast (Fig. 4). The rugged topography, moderate precipitation levels, and suitable temperature range in these regions create favorable conditions for the species. The model predicts that Jizan and the highlands of Asir are among the most vulnerable areas, with O. ficus-indica thriving in both natural and disturbed landscapes, such as rangelands and abandoned agricultural fields. Similarly, the Al Baha region, characterized by its moderate climatic conditions and presence of wadis, offers an ideal habitat for the species’ proliferation. Moderate suitability is observed in parts of Al Madinah, Tabuk, and Makkah, where climatic conditions support potential expansion but may impose some limitations due to lower precipitation and higher temperature fluctuations. The model predicts that while these areas can sustain O. ficus-indica growth, the likelihood of dense infestations is lower than in the highly suitable regions. Low suitability areas extend further inland, covering parts of Ha’il, Al Jawf, and Najran, where environmental conditions become less favorable due to increasing aridity and temperature extremes.

Discussion

The invasive potential of O. ficus-indica is well-documented across various ecosystems globally, where it has established itself as a dominant species, often at the expense of native biodiversity and ecosystem stability34. Its success as an invader is largely attributed to its remarkable physiological adaptability, including drought resistance, vegetative propagation, and rapid colonization of disturbed habitats35. In regions such as South Africa, Australia, and the Mediterranean Basin, O. ficus-indica has been classified as an ecological threat due to its ability to form dense monocultures36–38. In agricultural landscapes, O. ficus-indica poses severe economic burdens, reducing grazing land availability and causing injuries to livestock due to its spiny cladodes, as reported in South American rangelands39. Similar concerns have been raised in Pakistan, where its rapid expansion has displaced native grasslands, reduced forage availability for livestock, and disrupted traditional pastoral systems40. Understanding the ecological mechanisms underlying its invasion success is crucial for developing effective control measures and mitigating its long-term environmental consequences41.

The distribution of O. ficus-indica is strongly influenced by climatic and vegetation-related factors, with temperature and precipitation playing key roles in shaping its ecological niche. The findings of this study highlight that thermal extreme, particularly the warmest month’s temperature, are among the most significant predictors of its habitat suitability. Similarly, NDVI further contribute to its spread by indicating areas were sufficient moisture and plant cover support establishment. These results are consistent with studies conducted in other arid and semi-arid ecosystems, where invasive cacti have been found to thrive under high temperatures and moderate precipitation, often exploiting disturbed landscapes with reduced competition from native species34,42. In the Mediterranean Basin and South America, O. ficus-indica has been observed to establish most successfully in regions with seasonal rainfall patterns, aligning with its ability to endure prolonged droughts while capitalizing on periodic water availability43,44. Soil-related variables, such as organic carbon and pH, while relevant, appear to have a lower influence on the species’ distribution, suggesting that O. ficus-indica is more dependent on macroclimatic drivers than edaphic conditions. Similar trends have been reported in Ethiopia and Brazil, where the species favors semi-arid conditions with moderate vegetation cover but shows limited expansion in areas with excessive moisture or extreme aridity45,46. The consistency of these patterns across different biogeographic regions underscores the adaptability of O. ficus-indica to variable climatic conditions, reinforcing its status as a formidable invasive species47.

The model identifies key habitat suitability areas along mountainous and rangeland ecosystems, where the species thrives in both natural and disturbed environments. Similar trends have been reported in other regions, including Ethiopia, India, and the Mediterranean Basin, where O. ficus-indica establishes dense populations in areas with suitable climatic conditions48–50. In Ethiopia, for instance, niche modeling studies have shown that the species prefers semi-arid regions with seasonal rainfall, favoring highland and degraded rangelands where native vegetation struggles to compete51. Likewise, in India, ecological modeling has highlighted the species’ ability to colonize anthropogenically disturbed landscapes, altering native plant communities and reducing biodiversity52. These findings reinforce the high adaptability of O. ficus-indica to variable environmental conditions and its potential to expand further under favorable climatic scenarios. The strong agreement between this study and previous modeling efforts underscores the reliability of ecological niche models in predicting invasive species distributions53. However, regional variations in habitat suitability indicate that local-scale environmental factors, such as soil characteristics and land-use patterns, may further modulate the species’ spread, necessitating region-specific management interventions to mitigate its ecological impact54.

Climate change is projected to significantly alter the distribution of O. ficus-indica in Saudi Arabia, with future scenarios indicating both expansion and contraction across different regions. Under moderate climate change projections (SSP245), the species is expected to persist in its current core habitats in the southwestern regions. However, highly suitable areas in Jizan and the southernmost parts of Asir may shrink due to rising temperatures. Simultaneously, low and moderately suitable regions are predicted to expand into Tabuk, Al Madinah, and Najran, suggesting a gradual northward and inland spread. By the late century (2081–2100), under high-emission scenarios (SSP585), the species is projected to undergo further range shifts, with suitability increasing in Al Qassim, southern Ar Riyad, and Najran, while contraction may occur in some southwestern highland areas due to extreme warming. These findings are consistent with ecological niche modeling studies in other arid and semi-arid regions8,31,55. In Ethiopia, O. ficus-indica is projected to expand under future climate scenarios, particularly in areas that are currently marginal but expected to become more suitable due to warming and altered precipitation patterns56. Similar trends have been observed in India, where climate-driven range shifts are predicted to facilitate the invasion of O. ficus-indica into new habitats, potentially increasing its competitive dominance over native species57–60. The expansion into previously unsuitable environments is largely attributed to the species’ high drought tolerance, ability to thrive in degraded landscapes, and reliance on vegetative reproduction, which enhances its persistence even under fluctuating climatic conditions61–63. The projected spread of O. ficus-indica in Saudi Arabia highlights significant ecological concerns. Increased habitat suitability in central and northern regions, including Al Jawf and Ha’il, suggests that the species may exploit climatic shifts to colonize new semi-arid and desert margins. Conversely, the persistence of highly suitable areas in Asir and Makkah underscores the species’ resilience in certain microclimatic zones. The predicted trends emphasize the urgent need for monitoring and early intervention strategies, particularly in regions where climate change is creating new invasion hotspots64,65. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for mitigating the ecological and socio-economic impacts of O. ficus-indica in Saudi Arabia and other vulnerable arid ecosystems.

Implications for management and recommendations

The findings of this study underscore the urgent need for targeted management interventions to control the spread of O. ficus-indica in Saudi Arabia. Given its strong presence in semi-arid and arid regions, where it threatens native biodiversity and alters ecosystem functioning, an integrated management approach is essential. Mechanical removal methods, such as uprooting and controlled burning, should be prioritized in highly suitable regions to prevent further expansion. However, these methods must be complemented with habitat restoration efforts, such as replanting native vegetation to reduce the species’ competitive advantage. The study also highlights the importance of early detection and rapid response strategies, particularly in newly suitable areas projected under climate change scenarios. Monitoring programs should be established in Najran, Al Qassim, and central Riyadh, where future climate conditions may facilitate its expansion. Land-use policies should incorporate invasion risk assessments to regulate activities that promote the spread of O. ficus-indica, particularly in agricultural and rangeland systems. Furthermore, community engagement and public awareness campaigns are critical in ensuring local participation in control efforts, especially in rural areas where the species is rapidly proliferating. Future research should focus on refining predictive models by integrating land-use changes and human-mediated dispersal factors to develop more precise management strategies. By adopting a proactive and evidence-based approach, stakeholders can mitigate the ecological and economic impacts of O. ficus-indica and prevent its further establishment in vulnerable ecosystems.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the current and future distribution of O. ficus-indica in Saudi Arabia using ecological niche modeling. The findings highlight the species’ strong invasive potential, with its current distribution concentrated in southwestern regions where moderate precipitation and temperature conditions favor its establishment. Key environmental drivers, including maximum temperature of the warmest month, NDVI, and precipitation of the wettest month, were identified as the most influential predictors of habitat suitability. Future climate projections indicate a significant northward and inland expansion, with increasing suitability in Najran, Al Qassim, and central Riyadh, while some southwestern areas may become less suitable due to extreme warming. The projected expansion of O. ficus-indica under climate change scenarios raises serious ecological concerns, emphasizing the need for proactive monitoring, early intervention, and integrated management strategies. Mechanical removal, biological control, and habitat restoration efforts should be prioritized in highly suitable and at-risk regions. Additionally, policy frameworks should incorporate invasion risk assessments to regulate activities contributing to its spread. The findings of this study provide critical insights for policymakers and conservationists, offering a scientific foundation for sustainable invasion management to mitigate the ecological and socio-economic impacts of O. ficus-indica in arid and semi-arid ecosystems.

Methodology

Ecological impact of O. ficus-indica in KSA

O. ficus-indica, commonly known as the prickly pear cactus, is an invasive species with a remarkable ability to establish itself in arid and semi-arid environments due to its high tolerance to drought and poor soil conditions64. Originally native to Mexico, the species has been widely introduced for agricultural and ornamental purposes, yet it has become a significant ecological concern in various parts of the world, including Saudi Arabia65,66. The current distribution of O. ficus-indica in the Kingdom, as indicated by field observations, shows a strong presence along the southwestern and western regions, particularly in Asir, Jizan, Makkah, and Al Bahah provinces (Fig. 6). These regions, characterized by moderate precipitation and suitable temperature regimes, provide optimal conditions for the species’ proliferation67,68. The distribution data reveal that O. ficus-indica is particularly concentrated in mountainous and rangeland ecosystems, where it outcompetes native vegetation and disrupts local biodiversity69. This expansion is particularly concerning as it can alter ecosystem processes, impact native flora and fauna, and pose management challenges for local agricultural and conservation efforts70.

Species occurrence data collection and validation

Species occurrence data for O. ficus-indica in Saudi Arabia were compiled from multiple sources to ensure a comprehensive representation of its distribution. A total of 60 presence points were utilized in this study, comprising 22 records obtained from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) (https://www.gbif.org/), 18 records extracted from relevant literature, and 20 field survey records collected between 2023 and 2025 from various regions across the country (Additional file 1). Field surveys were conducted in key ecological zones where O. ficus-indica is known to be established, focusing on areas with high population density to avoid the inclusion of transient or sparse occurrences. Only locations where the species exhibited significant abundance and stable populations were considered to enhance the reliability of ecological niche modeling. The plant material was identified by Dr. Dhafer A. Al-Bakre (Department of Biology, Faculty of Science, Tabuk University), and a voucher specimen has been deposited in the herbarium of the same department under the deposition number O. ficus-indica 01.

Environmental data collection and processing

A comprehensive set of environmental variables was compiled to model the distribution of O. ficus-indica in Saudi Arabia, ensuring an ecologically meaningful representation of habitat suitability. Nineteen bioclimatic variables 30 s resolution were obtained from WorldClim Version 2.1 (https://www.worldclim.org/data/worldclim21.html), providing high-resolution climatic data essential for assessing species-environment relationships. To incorporate soil characteristics, ten soil variables were extracted from SoilGrids at a depth of 0–30 cm (https://soilgrids.org/), capturing key edaphic factors influencing plant establishment and growth (Additional file 2). The aridity index, derived from Zomer et al.71. It represents long-term water availability and drought susceptibility in the study region. Terrain-related attributes, including slope, aspect, and elevation, were derived from Google Earth Engine, enabling an assessment of topographic constraints on species distribution. Vegetation cover was represented using the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), calculated as the mean value of the growing season (April–August) from 2001 to 2020, offering insights into habitat productivity. Anthropogenic influences were incorporated through the Human Influence Index (HII), downloaded from NASA’s SEDAC database (https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/catalog/sedac-ciesin-sedac-lwp2-hii-geog-2.00), capturing the impact of human activities on habitat suitability. Future climate projections under six different climate change scenarios were obtained from WorldClim based on the BCC-CSM2-MR global climate model, specifically using Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP245 and SSP585) to evaluate potential shifts in O. ficus-indica distribution under moderate and extreme climate change conditions. These environmental datasets were standardized, resampled to a common resolution, and preprocessed to minimize multicollinearity before being integrated into the ecological niche modeling framework.

Selection of environmental variables for modeling

To ensure robust ecological niche modeling, a rigorous variable selection process was conducted using a combination of statistical and GIS-based approaches. Initially, the values of all environmental variables were extracted at each occurrence location using ArcGIS. To address multicollinearity and improve model reliability, a Pearson correlation test was applied, excluding highly correlated variables (r ≥ 0.8) to prevent redundancy and overfitting72 (Additional file 3). Further refinement was performed using the Jackknife test in MaxEnt, which evaluates the contribution of each variable to model performance73. Based on these analyses, six key predictor variables were retained for final modeling: maximum temperature of the warmest month (bio5), mean temperature of the warmest quarter (bio10), precipitation of the driest month (bio14), Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), soil organic carbon (SOC), and soil pH (pH). These variables were selected based on their ecological relevance to O. ficus-indica distribution and their significant influence on habitat suitability.

Model configuration and validation

To develop an optimal ecological niche model for O. ficus-indica in Saudi Arabia, a robust modeling framework was established using the MaxEnt (version 3.4.4) algorithm within the R statistical environment74–76. Model parameter tuning was conducted with the ENMeval package, evaluating multiple feature class combinations, including Linear (L), Quadratic (Q), Threshold (T), Product (P), Hinge (H), and their combinations (LQ, LQH, QH, LQHP and LQHPT), alongside regularization multiplier (RM) values ranging from 1 to 577. A total of 48 models were assessed using the 10-fold cross-validation approach, ensuring an unbiased evaluation of predictive performance. To address spatial sampling bias, a bias file was generated using ENMeval, ensuring that background sampling accounted for spatial heterogeneity in the presence data. Environmental predictors were standardized and stacked as raster layers and kernel density estimation (KDE) was applied to presence locations to improve spatial representation. The final model predictions were generated using the clog-log output format, a transformation that provides habitat suitability values between 0 and 1, with 15,000 background points, 10 replicate runs, and 500 iterations, optimizing model convergence. Predictor variable response curves were developed, and Jackknife tests were conducted to assess the relative importance of each environmental variable in explaining species distribution78.

Model performance evaluation and habitat classification

The model predictive accuracy was assessed using multiple evaluation metrics, including Area Under the Curve (AUC) of the Receiver Operator Characteristic (ROC) curve and partial-AUC (pAUC), AUC ratio, ensuring a comprehensive validation of model performance79. High AUC values (> 0.9) indicate excellent predictive ability, while an AUC ratio > 1.8 is regarded as a well-calibrated model. Statistical analyses were conducted using the SDMTune and pROC packages in R80,81. The final habitat suitability maps were classified into four suitability categories: unsuitable (p < 0.2), low suitability (0.2–0.4), moderate suitability (0.4–0.6), and highly suitable habitat (p > 0.6). Future habitat suitability projections were analyzed across six Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios (SSP245 and SSP585) for the periods 2041–2060, 2061–2080, and 2081–2100 to evaluate potential shifts in O. ficus-indica distribution. All maps were generated using ArcGIS 10.8 (ESRI, Redlands, CA, USA) based on the output layers produced by MaxEnt modeling.
