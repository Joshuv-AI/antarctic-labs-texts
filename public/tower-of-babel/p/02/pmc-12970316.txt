Late-stage microsurgical intrasaccular thrombectomy for visual decline after flow diversion of a large ophthalmic internal carotid artery aneurysm: a hybrid management strategy. Illustrative case
Authors: Cheng Qian, Tommi Kotkavaara, Ville Saarela, Juha-Matti Isokangas, Tommi K. Korhonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDLarge internal carotid artery (ICA) ophthalmic segment aneurysms are increasingly being managed endovascularly. However, symptomatic mass effect may persist or worsen despite angiographic occlusion.OBSERVATIONSA 43-year-old woman presented with a progressive right visual field defect and sensitivity loss. MRI and digital subtraction angiography revealed a large, unruptured, right ICA ophthalmic segment aneurysm. Endovascular management with a flow diversion device was performed, resulting in complete angiographic occlusion at 6 months. Despite angiographic success, her visual symptoms progressed to involve the postchiasmatic optic tract—a left visual field defect developed alongside the further progressing right visual field defect and acuity loss. Microsurgical thrombectomy via a right pterional craniotomy assisted with temporary endovascular balloon occlusion of the right ICA was performed 8 months after the flow diversion procedure. The patient’s visual symptoms slowly improved postoperatively, and her visual fields had recovered nearly completely at 17 months after the surgery.LESSONSDelayed microsurgical thrombectomy can yield near-total visual recovery even after initial endovascular treatment. A hybrid approach combining flow diversion for aneurysm occlusion followed by targeted surgical decompression may be considered in select cases with persistent mass effect–related symptoms. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25847

Symptomatic giant and very large aneurysms pose a distinct clinical challenge. While coiling can reduce aneurysm volume and relieve symptoms, recanalization or recurrence is common—reported in up to 75% of cases, particularly when coiling is used alone.1,2 Flow diversion has become the primary method of choice in the treatment of internal carotid artery (ICA) ophthalmic segment aneurysms especially for giant and partially thrombosed aneurysms. Complete occlusion rates at 6–12 months range from 65% to 78%, rising to more than 90% in longer-term follow-up, with generally favorable morbidity profiles and stable or improved visual outcomes in most cases.3–5 However, especially in large, thrombosed aneurysms, flow diversion may not adequately address the persistent mass effect, and progressive symptoms can occur despite angiographically successful aneurysm occlusion and eliminated aneurysm rupture risk.6,7

Illustrative Case

A 43-year-old woman with no relevant prior medical history presented with gradual right-sided visual decline over several months. The patient had no previous ophthalmological disorders apart from exophoria. Ophthalmological examination at presentation confirmed a visual field defect of the right eye. Visual acuity was 20/25 LogMAR in her right eye and 20/20 in her left eye (0.8 and 1.0 Snellen equivalents, respectively). The kinetic visual field (Fig. 1A) showed central concentric depression and loss of sensitivity. A nonthrombosed 18-mm aneurysm of the right ICA–ophthalmic segment was demonstrated in the initial MRI study, which a subsequent digital subtraction angiography (DSA) study confirmed; the aneurysm neck size was 6 mm (Fig. 2). Primary Endovascular Procedure

Given the aneurysm’s morphology and absence of subarachnoid hemorrhage, endovascular flow diversion was performed using a Pipeline embolization device under dual antiplatelet medication. The procedure was uneventful and well tolerated by the patient and resulted in contrast stagnation in the aneurysm sac (Fig. 2). At the 6-month follow-up DSA study, the aneurysm was fully excluded from the circulation with no residual filling (Fig. 2). However, the visual symptoms had not improved. Instead, 5 months after the endovascular procedure, the right visual field loss had progressed, and a new minor visual field disturbance related to postchiasmatic compression was now also found in the left eye (Fig. 1B). The subjective visual disturbance was still present in the right eye, and visual acuity had declined to 20/32 LogMAR (0.63 Snellen equivalent). Furthermore, a relative afferent pupillary defect suggesting ongoing optic nerve damage had developed. Indeed, compression-induced atrophy in the optic nerve head was noted in fundus photographs and thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer in an optical coherence tomography examination (Fig. 3). As the ophthalmological findings progressed bilaterally despite adequate endovascular treatment and corticosteroid medication, microsurgical thrombectomy to decompress the neural structures became indicated. Secondary Hybrid Intervention

The operation was conducted 8 months after the initial endovascular flow diversion procedure. Preoperative DSA confirmed excellent collateral flow from the left ICA to the right anterior and middle cerebral artery territories via the anterior communicating artery (Fig. 4A and B). A balloon catheter was placed to the right ICA in preparation for temporary vessel occlusion in case of intraoperative aneurysm rupture. A right-sided pterional craniotomy was then performed in a standard fashion. The optic and carotid cisterns were opened sharply, and the aneurysm was identified with its sac adherent to a thinned and displaced optic nerve (Fig. 4C). A 25-mm upward-curved Sugita aneurysm clip was applied to collapse and retract the remaining sac to reduce optic nerve compression. As the aneurysm had already thrombosed after the flow diversion procedure and had adhered tightly to the optic nerve, surgical separation of the aneurysm from the nerve was deemed unsafe. The aneurysm was therefore opened and partially debulked by removing the thrombosed mass, ensuring preservation and decompression of the optic apparatus. The thrombosed aneurysm contained firm, siderotic material rather than soft, fresh thrombus. A large slightly upward-curved 20-mm Sugita clip was placed with the closure line parallel to the optic nerve, pulling the thrombosed sac away from the optic apparatus (Fig. 4D). Outcome and Follow-Up

Postoperative CT and MRI showed no ischemic complications (Fig. 4). The patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged home on the 4th postoperative day with no new neurological symptoms. At 1 month after the surgery, the symptoms, clinical findings, visual acuity, and visual field defects remained unchanged. However, 17 months after the surgery, normal visual acuity of 20/16 (1.25 Snellen equivalent) was recorded in both eyes and the kinetic Goldmann perimetry showed improvement, with only a small bilateral depression in the central upper-left visual field recorded as a residual finding from the prior postchiasmatic compression. The patient was mostly free of subjective visual symptoms. A dramatic improvement was recorded in the central visual field with standard automated perimetry (Fig. 5). Discussion

Surgical thrombectomy with clip reconstruction has been historically performed in select patients with thrombosed aneurysms, particularly when presenting with acute compressive symptoms when the thrombus is soft and amenable to removal. Postoperative improvement in visual symptoms following early microsurgical decompression has also been demonstrated.8–10 However, the risks of optic nerve injury and perforant vessel compromise often limit surgical dissection, especially in aneurysms with organized thrombus, as in the present case. High-flow bypass surgery is another option for the treatment of these lesions, but owing to the development of sophisticated endovascular techniques, the capability to perform complex bypass surgery has diminished in many neurosurgical centers.

Nevertheless, as our case demonstrated, the indication and requirement for microsurgical decompression continue to exist in select cases. Our patient’s visual symptoms progressed despite complete angiographic occlusion following flow diversion, highlighting the limitations of fully endovascular therapy in addressing the mass effect caused by the thrombosed aneurysm. Intraoperatively, the aneurysm sac was tightly adherent to the thinned and displaced optic nerve. The sac contained firm siderotic material, precluding safe complete removal. Nonetheless, partial thrombectomy and clip-assisted collapse of the sac successfully reduced compression, and the patient experienced progressive and ultimately near-complete visual recovery.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case in which microsurgical late-stage partial thrombectomy after successful flow diversion led to full resolution of chiasmatic compression symptoms. Previous literature has focused on early intervention and endovascular treatment strategies, but very few studies have examined delayed microsurgical decompression after endovascular exclusion of the aneurysm.

Sujijantarat et al. reported an overall rate of symptom improvement of 71% in a meta-analysis and institutional series of patients with cranial nerve palsies caused by aneurysms subsequently treated with flow diversion.11 Interestingly, they noted a statistically significantly reduced rate of symptomatic improvement (53%) if the optic nerve was affected compared to other cranial nerves. On a related note, Binyamin et al. reported 2 cases in which they successfully treated oculomotor nerve palsies caused by large and giant posterior communicating artery aneurysms using flow diversion.12 As the size of the aneurysm sac remained similar despite improving symptoms and reduced MRI signal abnormality, they suggest that the beneficial effect was mediated by decreasing arterial pulsations againts the nerve or the midbrain. Taken together, these findings raise the possibility that the optic nerve may be more vulnerable either to compressive pathologies or to the potentially persisting mass effect caused by the occluded, but not debulked, aneurysm. Indeed, only the decompression of the optic nerve resulted in symptomatic improvement in our case. In their review, Hoz et al. also found the lowest complete symptom resolution rate of 43% when the optic nerve was affected by the aneurysm.13 They further noted that the symptoms of most patients (89%) had resolved within the first 6 months after treatment, which was not the case for our patient. This, together with the symptoms progressing contralaterally, led to the decision to operate as a last resort to save the patient’s worsening vision.

Our case supports a hybrid treatment paradigm, where endovascular treatment achieves aneurysm occlusion, and targeted microsurgical decompression is used to alleviate mass effect if visual symptoms persist. Our experience suggests that even in the current endovascular era, microsurgical expertise remains critical in the management of complex, symptomatic aneurysms. Select patients with persistent or progressive neurological deficits due to thrombosed aneurysms may benefit from this combined strategy, with an acceptable risk profile when performed by experienced neurovascular teams.

Observations

In patients with progressive visual symptoms due to mass effect from thrombosed aneurysms following successful flow diversion, delayed partial thrombectomy can lead to significant clinical recovery. A tailored, hybrid approach may offer the best outcomes in complex cases.

Lessons

This case highlights the continued relevance of open microsurgical techniques in the current era dominated by endovascular treatment of cerebral artery aneurysms.
