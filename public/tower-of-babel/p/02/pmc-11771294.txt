India’s Role in the Global OH Scenario: Past, Present, and Future
Authors: Shyam Pingle
Published: 2024

India is one of the fastest-growing major economies and is projected to become the third-largest economy in the world by 2030 from its current fifth position. Given its young demography, most working age population growth in the next decade will be from India. During the British era, Indian industry developed to cater to the need of British industry and economy. However, after the independence and fueled by economic liberalization and initiatives such as “Make in India”, the Indian industry has grown to be diverse. Due to high unemployment and easy availability of manpower, there was not much focus on occupational safety and health (OSH). However, as India has succeeded in lowering its fertility rate, the population growth will slow down in future. Furthermore, the industry has now realized that healthy employees with longer workability are necessary for productivity and profitability. This brings to focus the occupational health (OH) and wellbeing of employees.

New Global OSH Data

New statistics on occupational diseases and work-related injuries are now available. Jukka Takala et al.[1] published the estimated global statistics for 2019, which reveals 2,900,000 deaths each year from work-related diseases and injuries. These comprise work-related circulatory diseases, 31%; work-related cancer, 29%; and work-related injuries, 11%. This paper states that 320,000 people die each year from occupational accidents with 18,000,000 DALY (disability-adjusted life years) attributable to work-related diseases per year. It is estimated that 5.8% of global GDP is the economic loss due to work-related diseases and injuries.

The burden of work-related diseases and injuries increased from 2.3 million annual deaths in 2014 to 2.9 million in 2019 (26% increase). The DALY attributable to work have also substantially increased from 123 million in 2014 to 180 million in 2019 (47% increase).

The first WHO/ILO Joint Estimates of the Work-related Burden of Disease and Injury[2] published in 2021 estimated the burden attributable to 19 selected occupational risk factors in 183 countries. The total number of work-related deaths was estimated to be 1,879,890 deaths, and work-related DALYs were estimated to be 72 million DALY (183 countries, year 2016).

Globally in 2016, exposure to the 19 selected occupational risk factors was attributable for an estimated 1,879,890 deaths and 89.72 million DALY due to their respective health outcomes. The highest number of deaths was chronic obstructive pulmonary disease attributable to occupational exposure to particulate matter, gases, and fumes (450,381 deaths). The highest number of DALY was stroke attributable to exposure to long working hours (12.60 million DALY).

Inequalities are seen in both deaths and DALY by geographic regions, age, and sex. The absolute differences in death rates by WHO region, compared with the global rate, ranged from 10.7 in South-East Asia to 12.0 in Americas (per 100,000 working-age population). DALY ranged from 463.3 DALY in South-East Asia to 564.1 DALY in the Americas (per 100,000 working-age population).

The death rate per 100,000 working-age males is 51.4, 17.1 per 100,000 higher and 1.5 times the rate for both sexes. The death rate of 17.2 per 100,000 working-age females is lower compared with the rate for both sexes; this is 17.1 per 100,000 lower and 0.5 times this rate. Older age groups carried disproportionally greater disease burden, with the age group 85–89 years having the highest rate difference (higher than the global rate by 212.6 deaths per 100,000 working-age population) and the highest risk ratio (7.2). Conversely, the rate for the age group 15–19 years was 4.3 deaths per 100 000 working-age population, yielding a rate difference of 30.0 and a rate ratio of 0.1 compared to the global rate. Similarly for DALY, the older age groups were more burdened.

OH scenario in India

The Indian population according to the 2011 census was 1.21 billion, and the current estimate is around 1.44 billion. Thus, India accounts for about 17.8% of the global population. Considering the demographics, about 63.6% of the Indian population belongs to the working age group from 15 years to 65 years. The median age in India is 28.4 years. Thus, India already has about 24% of the global working population. Close to 92% Indians work in the informal economy, agriculture, and services. Only about 8% work in organized sectors including industry, mining, and services. As per NSSO (National Sample Survey Office) survey in 2011, India had 474 million workers, 377 million rural and 137 million urban. However, with increasing urbanization, this composition is undergoing significant changes. The female participation in the Indian workforce is increasing rapidly; in 2021–2022, 35.6% of women aged 15–59 were in the labor force, up from 25.3% in 2017–2018. (Directorate General of Employment report 2023).[3]

The major industries in the Indian economy are iron and steel, textiles, jute, sugar, cement, paper, chemical/petrochemical, automobile, information technology (IT), and banking and insurance. In 2022, 42.86% workforce was working in agriculture, 31.02% in services, and 26.12% in industry sector.[4] Agriculture and allied sectors contribute to 17% of GDP, manufacturing sector contributes 26% of GDP, and Services sector contributes the maximum, 57%, to the GDP. Now, India is the world’s second-largest coal, second-largest agrochemical, second-largest cement, second-largest steel, and third-largest electricity producer in the world.

Globally, as also in India, the labor market is divided into three major sectors, manufacturing, services, and agriculture. In developed countries, most people are engaged in the service sector and the least in the agriculture sector. The employment in manufacturing is decreasing in developed countries, but the same in the service sector is rising.

While the agriculture sector still engages the major part of Indian labor market, the number is fast reducing. In contrast to the global trends, India is jumping from agriculture to services skipping the manufacturing sector in terms of employment. However, the postpandemic global changes have given a boost to manufacturing in India not only for local consumption but also for exports as global manufacturers are turning to India for safeguarding their supply chains.

Occupational health issues in the manufacturing sector are believed to be injuries, musculo-skeletal disorders (MSDs), noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL), poisoning, and stress, while those in the service sector are believed to be stress, MSDs, and others. Agriculture sector would report more of occupational injuries, climate exposure (heat stress), MSDs, stress, pesticide poisoning, and others.

Thus, while occupational injuries will continue to be most visible, the prevalence of work-related stress is increasing exponentially. Indian employees are changing their preference from life time employment to growth and comfort at workplace. Given the large size of Indian labor market, the absolute number of traditional occupational diseases will continue to be high.

OH Evolution and Milestones in India

In the early years after independence, the industry focus was on the welfare and curative health. As the industrialization gathered pace, the focus shifted to industrial relations stressing on liaison and referral. However, the catastrophe in 1984, the infamous Bhopal gas disaster, changed the scenario. The government woke up; major statutory and regulatory changes were introduced focusing on safety and health. There was an overhaul of the Indian Factories Act in 1987, and the focus shifted to workplace surveillance and employee medical checkup in hazardous industries and hazardous processes. Occupational health staffing was prescribed, and a certificate course for physicians to work in hazardous industries; Associate Fellow in Industrial Health (AFIH) was introduced in 1995. So far, the course has trained around 4800 physicians across India through 17 institutions. There are no courses for occupational nurses, and there is only one course in Industrial Hygiene with an annual intake capacity of just 15 seats.

Economic liberalization introduced in 1991 turned out to be a significant milestone leading to expansion in Indian industries, and the entry of transnational companies in the manufacturing sector brought global HSE standards in India. The industry focus shifted to productivity, and the importance of healthy employees was realized as the business need. The year 2000, with availability of Internet and computer tech (the Y2K transition), accelerated the entry and expansion of the knowledge industry in India, further strengthening the workplace wellness initiatives.[5]

The recent COVID-19 pandemic impacted the global and Indian occupational health scenario in a major way. The industry realized the importance and contribution of OH physicians in the business continuity. It also led to work-from-home and remote working, especially in the knowledge sector, thus shifting the onus of workplace health from employers to the employees – which will become a major challenge. The unified OSH code replacing the Factories Act and other OSH legislations was passed by the parliament in 2019 and is likely to be implemented in coming years.

Factors affecting the future of occupational health in India

Given its large share in the global working population, adoption of technologies, and English-educated manpower, India is poised to play a major role in global manufacturing and services and consequently in global occupational health. Globally, industry is already transitioning from Industry 3.0 (automated production) to Industry 4.0 (smart factory). The next step will be Industry 5.0 – human-centric and socio-centric (sustainable and resilient).

New technologies and new forms of work will lead to new hazards and risks, both physical and psychosocial, characterized by increased physical inactivity, obesity, noncommunicable diseases, cancer, psychological disorders, MSDs, psychosocial risk, and work-related stress.

The workforce is aging in both developed and developing countries. Lifespan is also increasing, leading to voluntary prolongation of active working life due to personal motivation and financial necessity. Training and reskilling will be required in middle age onward, and bias against older workers for employment, retrenchment, and voluntary retirement from service (VRS) is likely. There will be increasing female participation in the workforce. Women will face a higher risk of job displacement than men since women perform more routine cognitive tasks, most prone to automation. There will be a need to guard against gender discrimination and stereotyping.

Globalization has impacted work. It will be more flexible in developed countries and progressively informal in developing countries like India. Precariousness of work is one of the main and most serious consequences of globalization. It may lead to contractualization of temporary jobs and more informal employment as already seen in India. Developed countries will see the shift of employment from industry to services – blue collar to white collar – technology/robots/wearables, leading to less physical/chemical risk but more psychosocial hazards. Countries like India will see the shift of hazardous process, low-quality jobs, exposure to obsolete technologies, and work processes.

Migration is an important adverse factor. India is both the world’s largest source of migrant workers and also a recipient nation, with regional migration from neighboring countries. 3-D jobs, dirty, dangerous, and demanding/difficult, are evolving to 4-D jobs, the fourth D–discrimination. Migrants are often excluded from policy and benefits and have higher deaths and injuries, climate hazards, high risk, and low protection. Climate change impacts work, workers, and workplaces and causes displacement and migration, leading to unemployment/productivity loss. Outdoor workers are affected more, but indoor workers are also at risk; it causes/exacerbates chronic diseases. Climate change impact is seen more in workers affected by socioeconomic disparities.[6]

Managing the future

Legal measures comprise hard law and soft law. Hard law refers to legally binding obligations, national statutes, and laws with enforcement and implementation mechanisms such as the Factories Act and ratified ILO Conventions. Since India has ratified only 4 ILO OSH conventions, it needs to ratify other important conventions related to health, safety, and wellbeing on priority. Measures in Factories Act/OSH Code need to be extended to smaller industries and informal sector. Soft law involves voluntary participation and reliance on consensus-based decision-making for action. Voluntary measures and certifications such as ISO 45001 and latest ISO 45003 (2021) will go a long way. Ethical issues arising out of new technologies like artificial intelligence need to be considered.

There is need to develop policies/practices that support decent work throughout the entire life course. The OSH community needs to support promotion of wellbeing and broader efforts to improve wages, job security, and worker voice. OSH is not a one-time but over the entire working lives of workers, from the first job to the retirement and beyond.

A life course perspective helps to understand cumulative effects of work, underemployment, unemployment, burnout, and blended work. A holistic, public health-oriented model of worker health addresses future issues, promotes systems thinking, strategic foresight, health equity, and transdisciplinary concepts in the OSH field. OH psychology and other social sciences (e.g., economics, sociology, anthropology, human relations) will be increasingly needed in these efforts. The field of OSH must evolve to meet the needs of workforce in the future. The factors that influence worker health and wellbeing go beyond the traditional OSH concerns.[6]

All stakeholders have an important role to play in ensuring decent work. While professional associations like International Commission on Occupational Health can play the role of facilitator at the global level, Indian Association of Occupational Health (IAOH) can take the lead in India. IAOH may engage with the Government and Enterprises through advocacy and awareness creation, publish fact sheets, promote preventive health and safety culture, facilitate soft laws and guiding documents, promote research to reach the “unreached”, and play the role of the conscience keeper.

There is a need for reskilling OH physicians to meet the competencies expected. Given the large number of employees in services sector, wellbeing management needs to be added to their competencies. In view of the short duration and limited scope of current AFIH Course, full-fledged diploma/degree courses in OH are needed. Residencies in OH and accreditation/board certification would be the next logical step. Capacity building of Occupational Health Nurses and Occupational Hygienists is essential.

OSH community may focus on research, practice, advocacy, governance, and education to achieve decent work.[6] OH physicians and other stakeholders should get future ready, learn (and unlearn) new skills, work for the realization of workers’ right to healthy and safe work environment in their enterprises/field of influence, provide research and professional capacities, and advise and support the organizations, employers and workers, and communities and workplaces appropriately and ethically.
