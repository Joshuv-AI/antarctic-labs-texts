Analysis of immune cell infiltration landscape and identification of diagnostic biomarkers in ankylosing spondylitis
Authors: Meng Chen, Shanbang Zhu, Yue Gu, Xinzhe Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ankylosing spondylitis (AS), characterized by inflammation of sacroiliac joints and spinal attachments, has an unclear pathogenesis. This study aims to screen and authenticate immune cell-associated biomarkers in AS. Two Gene Expression Omnibus datasets (GSE25101 and GSE41038) were combined as the discovery dataset, with candidate biomarkers screened via differential expression analysis, immune cell infiltration analysis, and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Immune cell-related biomarkers were further identified and validated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis using the confirmatory dataset GSE73754, and potential diagnostic biomarkers were finally confirmed by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), immunofluorescence staining, and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis (GSE194315). Thirty-two differentially expressed genes between the AS and control samples were identified. The ratio of M2 macrophages was significantly different between the AS and control samples. Seven candidate biomarkers associated with immune cells in AS were identified by WGCNA and Venn diagram. Then, three genes (SBK1, HNRPR, and CX3CR1) were authenticated as immune cell-associated biomarkers in AS by ROC curves, indicating a possible diagnostic value in clinical settings. The results of RT-qPCR, immunofluorescence staining, and scRNA-seq analysis all confirmed that CX3CR1 was down-regulated in AS, which was in line with bioinformatics study findings. Dysregulation of the CX3CR1 and M2-type macrophage ratio are key factors in AS, which lay the groundwork for exploring illness pathophysiology and yielding fresh perspectives on AS diagnosis and therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a kind of chronic autoimmune inflammatory arthritis of the axial skeleton, which occurs in the sacroiliac, spine, and peripheral joints.1 Joint immobility and stiffness are common symptoms of AS, leading to significant impairment in patients' daily lives and their ability to work.2 The pathogenesis of AS is still unclear. Regarding the treatment of AS, the application of anti-inflammatory drugs and biological agents targeting tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)/interleukin-17 (IL-17) are currently efficient and rapid in reducing inflammatory indicators and symptoms, but it remains controversial whether these methods are sufficient to prevent the bone remodeling that determines the patient's final prognosis.3 Additionally, a predictive AS disease risk model was constructed based on immune-related genes.4 However, it is not entirely clear how the potential genes associated with immune cells affect AS. Currently, the specificity and reliability of biomarkers for AS are insufficient. Therefore, exploring new biomarkers for AS will help us understand the pathogenesis of AS and offer new avenues for medical diagnosis and therapy.

In this study, we analyzed the immune infiltration landscape in AS by CIBERSORT. Specifically, modules that were shown to be highly linked to varying immune cells were dissected by weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). After validation of potential diagnostic markers by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis using the confirmatory dataset, the immunological functions of diagnostic genes and the signaling pathways involved were further explored through single-gene gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Promising diagnostic biomarkers in AS were ultimately confirmed by multiple methods, including reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), immunofluorescence staining, and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis. This study will contribute to better understanding the potential impacts of immune cell-related genes on AS and offer new AS diagnostic biomarkers. The flow chart of the study is shown in Fig. S1.

RESULTS

The DEGs between AS and control samples

First, we merged dataset GSE25101 with dataset GSE41038 by removing the batch effect with the “sva” package. The batch impact was effectively eliminated by cross-platform normalization, as shown by the expression box plot and principal component analyses (PCA) results of pre- and post-standardized data displayed in Fig. 1(a). In the merged dataset, a grand total of 103 DEGs (AS vs control), including 67 up-regulated genes and 36 down-regulated genes, were identified according to p value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.5 [Figs. 1(b) and 1(c), Table S6]. Next, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between AS samples and control samples in the GSE25101 dataset and the GSE41038 dataset, respectively. As shown in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b) and Tables S7 and S8, 170 DEGs (125 up-regulated genes and 45 down-regulated genes) in the GSE25101 dataset and 1702 DEGs (893 up-regulated genes and 809 down-regulated genes) in the GSE41038 dataset were authenticated. We integrated the DEGs of the GSE25101 dataset and the GSE41038 dataset using the RobustRankAggreg (RRA) package and gained 97 integrated DEGs (Table S9). Therefore, we crossed 97 integrated DEGs of the two datasets with 103 DEGs of the merged dataset to obtain 32 intersecting genes, defined as DEGs between the AS and control samples [Fig. 2(c), Table S10]. Functional enrichment analysis was carried out in order to better investigate the role of these 32 genes. As depicted in Figs. 2(d) and 2(e), 7 Gene Ontology (GO) items [1 biological process (BP) items, 5 cellular component (CC) items, and 1 molecular function (MF) item] and 13 Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were derived. This group of genes was shown to be significantly associated with “cellular defense response,” “ribosome,” “oxidative phosphorylation,” “retrograde endocannabinoid signaling,” and “chemokine signaling pathway.”

Identification of genes associated with M2 macrophages of AS by WGCNA

The co-expression network was then constructed using the R package WGCNA after filtering the outlier samples. The results showed that there was no need to eliminate outlier samples by sample clustering [Fig. 4(a)]. To guarantee that gene interactions optimally adhere to the scale-free distribution, seven was selected as the ideal soft threshold (R2 = 0.85) [Fig. 4(b)]. Following this, a gene clustering tree and a dynamic tree-cutting technique were used to create a total of 25 modules (minimum of 100 genes required for a gene module) [Figs. 4(c) and 4(d)]. The final 8 modules were achieved by merging [Figs. 4(d) and 4(e)]. We calculated the correlation of each module with M2 macrophages and found that M2 macrophages were most strongly correlated with the MEdarkred module (correlation coefficient = −0.67, p value = 4 × 10−06) [Fig. 4(e)]. Hence, we defined 2587 genes in the MEdarkred module as key module genes, namely, the genes associated with M2 macrophages of AS (Table S11).

Recognition of the biomarkers for AS

We took the intersection of key module genes and 32 DEGs between the AS and control samples to derive 7 overlapping genes, namely, CX3CR1, GPR56, HNRPR, LY96, S100A12, SBK1, and SPOCK2 [Fig. 5(a)]. In the merged dataset, LY96 and S100A12 were up-regulated in AS, and CX3CR1, GPR56, HNRPR, SBK1, and SPOCK2 were down-regulated in AS, compared to healthy controls [Fig. 5(b)]. By plotting ROC curves, we detected that the area under the curve (AUC) values of SBK1, HNRPR, and CX3CR1 were greater than 0.8. Therefore, these three genes were selected as diagnostic genes [Fig. 5(c)]. In the external validation set GSE73754, consistent with the merged dataset, CX3CR1, GPR56, HNRPR, S100A12, and SBK1 exhibited statistically significant expression differences between AS and control samples. For LY96 and SPOCK2, though their expression differences in AS samples did not reach statistical significance, their expression trends remained consistent with those in the merged dataset [Fig. 5(d)]. Further validation in the GSE73754 dataset showed that the AUC values of ROC curves for HNRPR, CX3CR1, and SBK1 all exceeded 0.6, indicating their ability to distinguish AS samples from controls to a certain extent and their potential clinical diagnostic value [Fig. 5(e)]. Based on these results, we identified HNRPR, CX3CR1, and SBK1 as biomarkers for AS. In addition, we calculated the correlation of three biomarkers with immune cells. Figure 5(f) demonstrated a strong association between CD8 T cells, T cells gamma delta, M2 macrophages, and neutrophils; intense correlations were found between CX3CR1 and active CD4 memory T cells, quiescent NK cells, and M2 macrophages; HNRPR was significantly associated with T cells gamma delta, CD8 T cells, neutrophils, and M2 macrophages; and there was a substantial correlation between SBK1 and CD8 T cells, resting NK cells, M0, and M2 macrophages.

Detection of the expression of the three biomarkers

To confirm the above-mentioned results, we examined the expression of HNRPR, SBK1, and CX3CR1 in AS and control peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) by RT-qPCR. The results showed CX3CR1 was lowly expressed in AS (P < 0.001), and the remaining two did not differ [Fig. 8(a)]. Immunofluorescence results of the articular synovium showed low expression of CX3CR1 and high expression of CD206 (marker gene for M2) in AS, which was compatible with the findings of the bioinformatics analysis shown in Fig. 5 [Figs. 8(b) and 8(c)].

Results of scRNA-seq analysis

To further validate diagnostic genes in AS at the single-cell level, scRNA-seq analysis was performed using the dataset GSE194315. First, through the quality control process, 42 449 cells and 22 211 genes were successfully retained [Fig. 9(a)]. Then, the top 2000 highly variable genes were selected for principal component analysis (PCA), and at the same time, the top 10 genes with the highest degree of variation were identified [Fig. 9(b)]. After that, based on the permutation test algorithm of the JackStraw plot and combined with the inflection point of the Elbow plot, the first seven principal components with a p value less than 0.05 were determined for subsequent single-cell sequencing analysis [Fig. 9(c)]. Later, we annotated five types of immune cells, namely, T cells, B cells, plasma cells, natural killer T (NKT) cells, and macrophages [Fig. 9(d)]. Finally, the results of the cell-type percentage differences showed that there were differences in the cell proportions of all cells between the sample groups [Fig. 9(e)]. Moreover, the violin plot showed that CX3CR1 was highly expressed in NKT cells and macrophages, and the expression trend in the AS group was lower than that in the control group [Fig. 9(f)]. This phenomenon indicates that CX3CR1 has the potential and characteristics to be a peripheral blood biomarker.

DISCUSSION

AS, an autoimmune inflammatory disorder, invades the sacroiliac joints early and eventually leads to bony ankylosis of the axial joint, which impacts the patient's quality of life significantly.5 The pathogenesis of AS is complex, and clear biomarkers are lacking so far. HLA-B27 is considered to be highly correlated with the pathogenesis of AS, but it also has a positive rate of 5%–10% in the normal population.6,7 As a kind of rheumatic disease, a variety of immune cells are involved in the pathogenesis of AS.8 Hence, the discovery of new immune-related molecular markers has major implications for figuring out how to treat AS.

First, the datasets GSE25101 and GSE41038 were integrated. Both the limma package and RRA package of the genes that distinguish those with AS from healthy controls were analyzed using the programming language R. The results of these two analyses were intersected, and finally 32 DEGs (19 up- and 13 down-regulated genes) were obtained. According to functional annotation analyses of these genes, we discovered that some pathways were found to be involved in the disease process of AS. Lari et al.9 reported that substantially greater rates of dementia are seen in AS patients compared to healthy persons, and crucially, the oxidative phosphorylation pathway is likely to be a common contributor to the etiology of both AS and dementia. The chemokine signaling pathway and associated chemokines were recognized to be strongly associated with the development of autoimmune arthropathies.10 Previous studies by our team have also demonstrated that inhibition of the chemokine receptor CXCR4 could restrain the proliferation and osteogenesis of fibroblasts in AS patients via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.11 Additionally, some DEGs have been reported to be implicated in the progression of AS. For example, the protein known as programmed cell death 10 (PDCD10) plays a crucial role in regulating signal transduction and has a wide range of potential applications.12 PDCD10 expression is augmented in AS and negatively associated with miR-495, which can down-regulate two active pathways—β-catenin pathway and TGF-β1 pathway in AS.13 Thus, potential future research directions include investigating the role of PDCD10 in AS and the miRNAs with which it interacts. The expression of gene SPOCK2 was shown to be down-regulated in another investigation that used a whole-genome microarray technique to find potential genes linked with AS.14 Sparc/osteonectin, commonly known as SPOCK2, was identified as a discriminator between SpA and healthy controls, and matrix turnover in cartilage was primarily regulated, produced, assembled, or maintained by these genes.15,16 LY96 was found to be one of the common factors underlying rheumatic diseases, and its single nucleotide polymorphism (rs11465996) was associated with the risk of AS.17,18

We first identified one type of characteristic immune cell—M2 macrophages using the LASSO. We also used the rank sum test (wilcox.test) to examine the variations in immune cell populations between samples of normal and AS and found that only the proportion of M2 macrophages was significantly different (p < 0.05). Clinical studies have confirmed the enhanced activation of M2 macrophages circulating in the peripheral blood of AS patients, and a statistically meaningful link between the M2/M1 ratio and the AS spinal ossification score (mSASSS).19 In vitro studies also demonstrated that induction of M2 polarization of macrophages could promote secretion of various pro-repair factors that play an important role in bone formation.20,21 According to the findings given in the preceding articles, it should be confirmed that the ratio of M2 macrophages is up-regulated in AS and closely associated with the ossification process, which accords with the results of our bioinformatics investigation.

On the basis of the merged dataset, we incorporated the GSE73754 dataset and used the Wilcoxon rank sum test to confirm that AS and normal samples express differently for hub genes. CX3CR1, GPR56, HNRPR, SBK1, and SPOCK2 were found to be differently expressed in the merged dataset and were down-regulated in samples from AS patients, and LY96 and S100A12 were down-regulated in control samples, as shown in Fig. 5. For the analysis of the aforementioned seven genes for diagnostic purposes, we plotted ROC curves and selected genes with AUC values greater than 0.8 in the merged dataset (SBK1, HNRPR, and CX3CR1) for validation in the GSE73754 dataset, and they all had relatively high diagnostic value. The three diagnostic genes and ten other genes that are very abundant in the immune system were all substantially correlated with M2 macrophages, as shown by a Pearson correlation study (p < 0.01). By reviewing the relevant literature, we learned that SBK1 is a serine–threonine protein kinase whose function involves signal transduction pathways. In the blood of postmenopausal women with osteoporosis, SBK1 expression was positively correlated with osteoclast activity and bone loss.22 Combined with our analytical data, we speculated that the down-regulation of SBK1 expression in AS was likely to be associated with new bone formation. A previous study showed that CX3CR1 expression was significantly reduced in AS patients,4 which was consistent with the results of this study. The differential expression of CX3CR1 can serve as an important diagnostic basis for distinguishing AS patients from healthy individuals, aiding in achieving accurate diagnosis. In addition, as a key factor in the polarization of M2 macrophages, IL-4 could rapidly down-regulate the expression of CX3CR1, accompanied by a decrease in the cell surface expression of this receptor.23 Animal experiments confirmed that M1 macrophage markers were reduced in CX3CR1−/− mice, while the expression of M2 macrophage markers, including arginase-1, was increased,24 indicating that CX3CR1 deficiency could affect the polarization of macrophages. Not only that, some papers reported that CX3CL1 directly regulated osteoclast differentiation, and antagonism of CX3CR1 ligands could inhibit bone resorption in neonatal mice by reducing the number of mature osteoclasts in bone resorption.25,26 Single-gene GSEA results also showed that CX3CR1 was involved in the activation of immune cells and osteogenic differentiation pathways. Taken together, the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 axis may be involved in the AS osteogenesis process by regulating both macrophage polarization and osteoclast differentiation. The development of drugs targeting CX3CR1 can open up new avenues for AS treatment through the regulation of the immune microenvironment of AS patients and the inhibition of abnormal bone formation.27 HNRPR is an RNA-binding protein belonging to the heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein family, which has a known role in the processing of precursor mRNA.28 Some studies have shown that HNRPR plays a role in the regulation of nervous system function.29,30 In addition, HNRPR was identified as an important positive regulator of classical and nonclassical MHC class I proteins,31 which are essential for the immune system. Thus, HNRPR, as a biomarker, may be used to dynamically monitor the immune dysfunction in AS patients, exploring new directions for the diagnosis and treatment of AS.

Finally, based on the predicted relationships, a lncRNA–miRNA–miRNA ceRNA network containing 12 lncRNAs, 2 biomarker mRNAs, and 14 miRNAs was obtained based on the three diagnostic markers. The ceRNA network has not been reported and needs to be further validated. Nevertheless, the construction of the ceRNA networks may provide the basis and direction for further studies on the upstream regulatory mechanisms of these marker genes in the future.

To verify the results of the above analysis of immune cell infiltration and bio-diagnostic markers, we used RT-qPCR to detect the expression of HNRPR, SBK1, and CX3CR1, and found that only CX3CR1 was differentially expressed in AS (p < 0.001). Based on the immunofluorescence results, the up-regulation of M2 quantity, but accompanied by low expression of CX3CR1, may contribute to the osteogenic differentiation of AS. More importantly, in line with the results of wet experiments, scRNA-seq analysis clearly confirmed that CX3CR1 was primarily expressed in NKT cells and macrophages, and its expression was significantly decreased in AS patients, manifesting the potential of CX3CR1 serving as a promising AS diagnostic biomarker.

To explore the crucial role of immune cells and related genes in the pathogenesis of AS, we have, for the first time, combined immune cell infiltration analysis with WGCNA to uncover gene modules highly associated with key immune cells such as CD8 T cells and M2 macrophages, providing new insights for the study of the mechanisms linking immune cells and related genes. Through ROC analysis and single-gene GSEA, we were able to identify immune cell-associated biomarkers in AS and investigate their potential biological processes and regulatory networks, which have not been reported previously, and offer a new direction for exploring the upstream regulatory mechanisms of biomarkers. Moreover, we also performed experimental validation of differential immune cell subpopulation numbers and gene expression levels. Dysregulation of CX3CR1 expression and M2-type macrophage ratio are key characteristics in the course of AS, providing valuable clues for developing targeted drugs and treatment strategies for AS. This study not only confirms the previous observations but also provides new targets for the subsequent diagnosis and treatment of AS.

The findings of this study have a positive impact on clinical practice and future research directions. In clinical practice, CX3CR1, SBK1, and HNRPR have been identified as potential AS diagnostic biomarkers, particularly CX3CR1, which can serve as a novel noninvasive indicator to enhance the diagnostic efficiency of AS. CX3CR1 participates in bone remodeling by regulating the polarization of M2 macrophages and the differentiation of osteoclasts, providing valuable directions for targeted therapy. Furthermore, the ratio of M2 macrophages can serve as indicators for monitoring treatment response. Apart from CX3CR1, the mechanisms by which the other two biomarkers, HNRPR and SBK1, play roles in the progression of AS are worth studying, which can be future research directions.

There are also some limitations. First, in the results of gene expression analysis, only glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as a reference gene, although it is one of the most commonly used reference genes.32 Other reference genes, such as β-actin, should also be employed in this study to enhance the reliability of the results. Second, the molecular mechanism of key genes needs further experimental confirmation. Finally, the small sample size may lead us to miss some key pathogenic molecules. Therefore, the clinical diagnostic value of biomarkers needs to be supported by data from more clinical samples. We will further focus on the function of key genes in immune regulation, as well as expand the sample size to validate their relationship with clinical parameters.

CONCLUSION

In this study, we screened biomarkers related to immune cells in AS through comprehensive bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation. Specifically, 32 DEGs were identified between AS samples and control samples by merging the analysis of the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets (GSE25101, GSE41038, and GSE73754). These genes were functionally enriched in immune response and inflammation-related pathways (e.g., chemokine signaling pathway, oxidative phosphorylation, etc.). Immune cell infiltration analysis showed that the abundance of M2 macrophages differed significantly between AS samples and control samples, suggesting that they were involved in the pathogenesis of AS. With the help of WGCNA and ROC analysis, we suggested that SBK1, HNRPR, and CX3CR1 are potential diagnostic biomarkers. Among them, CX3CR1 was consistently down-regulated in AS, as verified by RT-qPCR, immunofluorescence staining, and scRNA-seq. It is worth noting that CX3CR1 is mainly expressed in NKT cells and macrophages, and its expression is reduced in AS, which is consistent with its role in M2 macrophage polarization and osteogenesis. In summary, the results of the present study indicate that the aberrant expression of CX3CR1 and the imbalance of the ratio of M2 macrophages are the key features of AS, which offer a new perspective for analyzing the pathophysiological mechanism of AS. At the same time, CX3CR1, as a promising diagnostic biomarker, provides a potential target for the diagnosis of AS and the therapeutic intervention of AS.

METHODS

Dataset collection and information

Three AS-related datasets were mined from the NCBI-GEO database, namely, GSE25101, GSE41038, and GSE73754. The GSE2510114 dataset includes whole blood microarray sequencing data from 16 individuals with AS and 16 individuals serving as controls. The GSE4103833 dataset incorporates microarray sequencing data of knee synovial biopsy tissue samples from 2 AS patients and 4 healthy controls. The GSE7375434 dataset comprises microarray sequencing data of whole blood samples from 52 AS patients and 20 healthy controls. The clinical information of patients is listed in Tables S1–S3.

Data pre-processing

GSE25101 and GSE41038 datasets were merged by eliminating the batch effect through the combat function in R package “sva” (version 1.0.12).35,36 We chose to combine the blood datasets GSE25101 and GSE41038 because the two datasets had some degree of data complementarity. Moreover, both datasets contained information related to the inflammatory status of AS and thus shared certain biological correlations. Blood was a source of samples that were reflective of the systemic immune response. The data in GSE41038 contained certain unique information, although the sample size was relatively small. We hoped that by combining these two datasets, we could more comprehensively mine biomarkers associated with AS.

Disparate expression analysis

The R language limma package (version 3.46.0)37,38 was used to analyze gene expression differences between the AS and HC groups of the dataset GSE25101, GSE41038, and the combined dataset, respectively. The screening criteria were |log2FoldChange(FC)| > 0.5 and p value < 0.05. The intersection of DEGs and RRA results was analyzed to obtain a more robust subset of differentially expressed genes with higher cross-dataset repeatability. In addition, GO and KEGG-based enrichment analysis of the intersected differentially expressed genes was performed using the R clusterProfiler package (version 4.2.1),39 and the results were visualized using GOplot (version 1.0.2), enrichplot package (version 1.10.2), as well as baguazhang diagrams and bubble diagrams.

Identification of characteristic immune cells

To investigate immune infiltration differences between AS patients and normal samples, we used the CIBERSORT method with the LM22 immune gene set to determine the percentage of 22 immune cells types in each AS and control sample.40 After excluding the immune cells with zero proportion in >50% of samples, we performed cluster analysis using R “Pheatmap” package (version 1.0.12) and computed Pearson correlations among infiltrating immune cells. The characteristic immune cells were certificated by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression analysis41 using R package “glmnet” (version 4.0-2). After excluding samples with p > 0.05, immune cell differences between AS and control samples were compared using the Wilcoxon test.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

We used WGCNA to select immunity-related genes using R package “WGCNA” (version 1.7-3),42 which automatically generates a co-expression network. Characteristic immune cells were used as phenotypic data in WGCNA to obtain their related genes. Sample clustering via the “goodSamplesGenes” function was performed to identify and remove outliers. A soft-thresholding power was generated using the pickSoftThreshold function to ensure the co-expression network fit the scale-free distribution. Different modules were created with the dynamic tree-cutting method (minimum 100 genes per module), and parallel components were eventually merged. Further analysis explored associations between final modules and characteristic immune cells, isolating the module with the highest correlation (highest |correlation coefficient| and p < 0.05). Finally, hub genes were obtained as the intersection of intersecting differentially expressed genes and genes in the WGCNA key module.

Validation of biomarker expression and ROC analysis

In the combined dataset and GSE73754 dataset, we verified the differences and expression of the diagnostic genes between the disease samples of ankylosing spondylitis patients and normal samples by using the wilcox.test. Also, we verified the expression levels of the diagnostic genes through visualizing the scatter plots drawn with the R language ggpubr package (version 0.4.0). In addition, to investigate the predictive capacity of the identified diagnostic markers across the pooled dataset and GSE73754, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses and conducted marker validation using the pROC package (version 1.18.0)43 in R. Finally, we performed Pearson correlation analyses between diagnostic biomarkers with area under the curve (AUC) values greater than 0.8 and differential immune cells.

Functional annotation analysis

We applied the R package “clusterProfiler” (version 4.2.1)44 for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis. GO was segmented into cellular component (CC), molecular function (MF), and biological process (BP). Single-gene gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was also implemented by R package “clusterProfiler” (version 4.0.5) by setting the threshold values as |NES| > 1, p value < 0.05, and q value < 0.2 based on GO, KEGG gene sets.

Development of lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory network

The mirtarBase database (https://mirtarbase.cuhk.edu.cn/) and miRWalk database (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/) were deployed to predict the target miRNAs of biomarkers. Then the starbase database (screening criteria: CLIP-DATA ≥ 3) and miRNet database (https://www.mirnet.ca/) were used to anticipate lncRNAs aiming to regulate the miRNAs. Cytoscape was used for the ultimate lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA network construction (version 3.8.2).45

The scRNA-seq analysis

To deeply explore prognostic genes at the single-cell level, the GSE194315 dataset was further studied. This dataset contained two normal control samples (PBMC—01-3 and PBMC—01-4) and two disease samples (PBMC—07-1 and PBMC—07-2).

First, according to the quality control criteria, cells with mitochondrial counts exceeding 10%, gene expression numbers not in the range of 200–4000, and cell unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts greater than 20 000 were excluded.

Second, in the single-cell dataset, in order to obtain genes with a large coefficient of variation between cells, data standardization was carried out first: using the NormalizeData function, the gene expression value of each cell was divided by the total gene expression value of this cell, then multiplied by 10 000, and then a logarithmic transformation was performed. Then, the vst method of the FindVariableFeatures function was used to extract genes with a large coefficient of variation between cells, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were presented for subsequent analysis.

For further dimension reduction, data were scaled using the “ScaleData” function. Based on the top 2000 highly variable genes, principal components for clustering were calculated using the RunPCA function. The JackStrawPlot function determined statistically significant principal components, and the Elbowplot function visualized the PCA dimension reduction results. Combining the permutation test from the JackStraw plot with the inflection point of the Elbow plot, the first seven principal components (p < 0.05) were selected for subsequent single-cell sequencing analysis. Subsequently, data were re-normalized using the LogNormalize method, and 2000 of the most variable genes were identified via the FindVariableFeatures function. Linear dimension reduction techniques were used to determine the optimal linear dimension for cell clustering: a scree plot was generated from PCA results, and the number of principal components at the curve's plateau was selected for UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) clustering (resolution = 0.5). Marker genes were obtained according to the literature for cell annotation.46

In addition, in order to determine the number of cell clusters, unsupervised clustering analysis was carried out using the “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions (resolution = 0.1, dimension = 7), and then the UMAP generated by the RunUMAP function was used to visualize the cell clusters. Finally, in the single-cell dataset, based on the obtained cell types, a bar chart was drawn to show the proportion of different types of cells in AS and HC samples, and the Chi-square test was used to analyze the percentage differences of each cell type in AS and normal control samples. Cells with a p-value less than 0.05 were regarded as differential cells.

Patients

Five male patients (age 51.6 ± 6.0) had been diagnosed with AS according to the 1984 modified New York criteria.47 They had previously taken non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, but no drug treatment had been administered within the past year. Five male non-AS knee trauma patients (age 50.2 ± 4.8, no drug treatment within the past year) served as control subjects. The patients' informed permission was documented in writing by all of them. This research was approved by the Shanghai Changhai Hospital Ethics Committee (CHEC2024-295). Detailed information on AS patients and control subjects is provided in Table S4.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

PBMCs obtained from 5 AS patients and 5 control subjects were isolated through the Ficoll method,48 and then processed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) to extract total RNA. After PBMCs were lysed by TRIzol, chloroform was added and mixed thoroughly. The upper aqueous layer was moved to a new tube following the centrifugation at 12 000g for 15 min at 4 °C. The RNA was then precipitated by the addition of isopropanol, followed by centrifugation at 12 000g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed with 75% ethanol by centrifugation at 12 000g for 10 min at 4 °C and was dissolved in DEPC-treated water. A NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to measure the concentration of RNA. cDNA was prepared using 1 μg RNA with the TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). Reverse transcription reaction was performed with the following programs: 25 °C for 10 min, 37 °C for 120 min, 85 °C for 5 min, and storage at 4 °C. The resulting cDNA was subjected to qPCR using the SYBR Premix TaqTM II kit (Takara, Dalian, China). The reaction system of qPCR included 5 μl cDNA (100 ng), 2.5 μl forward primer, 2.5 μl reverse primer, and 10 μl 2× SYBR Green. The cycling conditions were 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. The specific primers were designed and verified by DNAMAN software. Primer sequences are listed in Table S5. The gene expression levels were analyzed by the ΔΔCT method. The results were reported as relative mRNA fold changes compared with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH).

Immunofluorescence staining

3 AS patients were diagnosed with tibial plateau fracture based on imaging studies (x-ray and CT diagnosis) and fulfilled the clinical indications for total knee arthroplasty. Knee synovial tissues were collected during the surgical procedure. Control knee synovial tissues were obtained from 3 non-AS knee trauma patients who underwent knee surgery. Synovial tissues were embedded in paraffin after fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde. Serial 5 μm paraffin sections were prepared. Next, the paraffin sections were dewaxed in xylene twice for 2 min each and then rehydrated in 100% ethanol twice for 2 min each, 95% ethanol twice for 2 min each, and PBS twice for 2 min each. Antigen retrieval was performed with 0.1% trypsin at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation with blocking buffer (3% BSA + 20% goat serum), sections were treated with primary antibodies against CD206 (1:300, GB115273, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and CX3CR1 (1:5000, GB11861, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at 4 °C overnight. Appropriate fluorescein-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:400, G1226, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) were employed, and nuclei were counterstained with 4ʹ,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride hydrate (DAPI; 1:1000, G1012, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Images were captured using the Nikon Eclipse C1 fluorescence microscope and Nikon DS-U3 (Nikon Corporation, Japan) digital imaging system, and 10 images from different parts of each tissue section were obtained for quantitative analysis. For each image, the Cell Counter plugin from ImageJ software was used to count the number of total cells, CD206-positive cells, and CD206 and CX3CR1-positive cells, respectively. The percentages of CD206-positive cells or CD206 and CX3CR1-positive cells were equal to the mean values of the quantitative results of 10 images from each sample.

Statistical analysis

The DEGs of the GSE25101 and GSE41038 datasets were integrated using the R package RobustRankAggreg (RRA) (version 1.1). The “pROC” package was used to produce ROC curves. The correlation of genes and immune cells was calculated with the Pearson method. In order to compare the outcomes of several cohorts, the Wilcoxon test was used. Results between the two groups were analyzed using two-tailed, unpaired Student's t-tests. Data were represented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). If not otherwise stated, a p value smaller than 0.05 represented the significance.
