Consistent functional properties of MSC-exosomes from different batches revealed by comparative multi-omics, bioinformatics and functional tests
Authors: Byong Seung Cho, Jun Ho Lee, Kyong-Oh Shin, Kyungho Park, Kyojin Lee, Ella Shin, Hyunju Lee, Healim Lee, Kyong-Su Park, Dae Hyun Ha, Jan Lötvall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMesenchymal stromal cell-derived exosomes (MSC-EXOs), also called Extracellular Vesicles (EVs), exhibit anti-inflammatory effects in various diseases and are being developed for clinical use. For instance, MSC-EXO promotes skin healing post-laser therapy and improves outcomes in severe COVID-19. Selecting an optimal cellular source is critical for the therapeutic development of MSC-EXO. It has been suggested that GMP-produced MSC-exosomes have slightly different molecular content. This study therefore aimed to compare adipose tissue-derived MSC-EXO (ASC-EXO) from three healthy donors, isolated and characterized under GMP conditions.MethodsExosomes were analyzed by nano-tracking analysis (NTA), cryo-electron microscopy, tetraspanin profiling, and multi-omics (proteomics, lipidomics, small RNA sequencing), as well as bioinformatics. Functional assays quantified anti-inflammatory effects in RAW264.7 macrophages and collagen production by human dermal fibroblasts (HDF). In vivo efficacy of ASC-EXOs was evaluated in a house dust mite antigen-induced atopic dermatitis mouse model through histopathological evaluation of ear thickness and differential cell counting.ResultsNo significant batch differences were observed in ASC-EXO yield or characteristics. Omics analyses revealed minor variations in protein, lipid, and small RNA cargo among the three batches, but GO-term bioinformatics indicated highly similar functional profiles. IL-6 suppression in RAW264.7 cells and cell proliferation and collagen production by HDF were similar among the ASC-EXO batches. CD73 enzymatic activity was consistent among batches. In vivo, the ASC-EXOs reduced skin thickness and eosinophilia in an atopic dermatitis model, with similar effects among the batches.ConclusionsIn summary, ASC-EXOs from all batches demonstrated comparable anti-inflammatory and collagen-modulating effects in vitro, and similar inhibition of atopic dermatitis signs in vivo. We suggest that biological efficacy combined with bioinformatics analysis should guide MSC-EXO therapeutic quality control assays. These findings support the development of ASC-EXOs for clinical applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05104-3.

Background

Extracellular Vesicles (EV), often also called exosomes, can shuttle multiple molecules between cells, and are being developed as functional nanomedicines for human use. In this publication, we have specifically chosen to use the term “exosomes” for any type of EV. Exosomes derived specifically from mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) have proven to be effective in systemic inflammation induced by SARS-CoV-2 infection in vivo and clinical double-blind and controlled studies in humans [1–3], reducing the impact of the infection on lung injury. Further, MSC-Exosomes (MSC-EXO) have been tested in a double-blind controlled study for skin wound healing, specifically in patients undergoing laser therapy for facial acne scars [4]. Additional studies of MSC-EXO have been registered in clinicaltrials.gov, which suggests that many more clinical trials in the field will be published in the coming years.

When developing a new type of treatment for patients, there are multiple hurdles for clinical development, in this case for establishing good manufacturing practice (GMP) of the exosome-based therapeutics. Further, quality control attributes need to be very well defined, including the presence of specific proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids [5]. In this study, we aim to compare the molecular cargo and function of adipose tissue-derived mesenchymal stromal cell-derived exosomes (ASC-EXO) from three different donors, to determine any differences or similarities. To do that, we performed multi-omics analyses of MSC-EXO from three healthy human donors. The isolated MSCs from each donor were expanded, and exosomes were isolated from the cultured cell supernatant. Specifically, the multi-omics analysis included LC/MS–MS proteomics, small RNA-transcriptomics, and LC/MS lipidomics analyses. Furthermore, the efficacy of the ASC-EXO from each donor was evaluated in vitro in inflammation, cell proliferation, and collagen synthesis models, as well as in vivo in a model of house-dust mite antigen-induced atopic dermatitis.

Results

Characterization of ASCs

ASCs were obtained from adipose tissue from and isolated under GMP facility conditions. The ASCs were selected based on plastic adherence to ensure the removal of any potentially contaminating hematopoietic cells. The characteristics of ASCs were described by analyzing multiple subculture passages. Specifically, cells were characterized by morphology, cell proliferation rate, expression of MSC-associated surface markers, and the ability to differentiate into adipogenic cells, osteogenic cells, and chondrogenic cells according to the criteria described by the International Society of Cellular Therapy (ISCT) [6]. Cell storage was performed under continuous GMP conditions in liquid nitrogen freezers. The three cultured ASC showed typical spindle-like morphology and were adhesive to the surface plastic culture. No morphological differences were observed between the cells from the three donors up to passage nine (Fig. 1A). The ASCs from the three different donors had similar population doublings (PDs) over nine passages (Fig. 1B). Further, cell viability was higher than 95% up to passage nine, and no difference in cell size was observed over time (Fig. 1C). All three ASCs maintained the MSC surface markers CD29, CD73, CD90, and CD105 in at least 95% of cells up to passage nine (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Fig. 1). No more than three per cent of the cells expressed the endothelial marker CD31 or hematopoietic lineage markers CD34, CD45, or HLA-DR, as observed by flow cytometry (FCM) analysis up to passage nine (Fig. 1F). The three ASCs were confirmed to differentiate into adipocytes, osteocytes, or chondrocytes, each with distinct phenotypes, once transferred to the ExoCoBio Inc. Characterization of ASC-EXOs

After the tangential flow filtration (TFF) system using the ExoSCRT™ technology to isolate exosomes from large culture medium volumes, quality control testing and the functionality of the isolated populations were required. The ExoSCRT™ approach takes approximately four hours and reduces the volume of the cell culture medium [7, 8]. In cryo-electron microscopy, in which exosomes are visualized without any dyes, the ASC-EXOs are seen as spheres, and the lipid bilayer can be visualized at the surface. No differences were observed in morphology between the ASC-EXOs from the different donors (Fig. 2A). NTA data for particle numbers, their measured size distribution, and the zeta potential of the isolated ASC-EXO are shown in Fig. 2B–D. We observed approximately 3.3 × 1010 particles/mL, with reported diameters ranging from 30 to 200 nm (mean reported size: 152 ± 5.4 nm; mode size 109 ± 1.2 nm), and a zeta potential − 40.9 ± 1.5 mV. The particle contents of ASC1-EXO, ASC2-EXO, and ASC3-EXO were 3.6 × 1010, 2.8 × 1010, and 3.3 × 1010 respectively, and the particle sizes were 146.6, 157.5, and 152.2 nm respectively, and the zeta potential showed − 41.3, − 39.3, and − 42.2 mV for respective batches. There was no significant difference in the content, size, and zeta potential of exosomes derived from the three cell banks (Fig. 2B–D). The presence of the three tetraspanin proteins CD9, CD63, and CD81 on the ASC-EXO was determined by flow cytometric analysis using dynabeads magnetic beads, and 99% of the beads were positive for the markers, with comparable levels across the ASC1-EXO, ASC2-EXO, and ASC3-EXO batches (Fig. 2E). The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) marker calnexin or the mitochondrial marker cytochrome C were present at concentrations lower than 0.3 ng/mL in any of the ASC1-EXO, ASC2-EXO, and ASC3-EXO batches (Fig. 2F). Exosome productivity was expressed as the number of isolated particles from a unit volume of conditioned media (CM) of ASC, but it may also be measured as the concentration of total exosomal proteins from a unit volume of CM. Productivity of ASC-EXO from the three different batches spanned from 4.2 × 1011 to 5.2 × 1011 particles per liter of CM and from 1670 to 2957 µg protein per liter of CM. Multi-OMICS-analysis of ASC-EXOs

LC/MS–MS of the three different exosome production batches identified 1881, 1580, and 1421 proteins in the three batches of ASC-EXO, and 777 of the proteins were identified in all three batches (Fig. 3A). To understand the overall biological function of ASC-EXO, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis using the DAVID database, which reveals the Cellular components, Biological processes, and Molecular functions. The “Cellular component” category primarily identified “extracellular exosome”, followed by “extracellular region” and “focal adhesion. The “Biological process” was primarily “Cytoplasmic translation”, followed by “Translation”. The most dominant “Molecular function” identified was “RNA binding”, followed by “Extracellular Matrix Structural Constituents” and “Protein binding” (Fig. 3B). Importantly, the GO term profiles were similar among the ASC1-EXO, ASC2-EXO, and ASC3-EXO batches (Fig. 3C). We screened for a total of 2,656 miRNAs and identified 347 miRNAs in each ASC-EXO batch (ASC1-EXO: 260, ASC2-EXO: 216, ASC3-EXO: 201), and 132 of the miRNAs were identified in all three batches (Fig. 4A). The miRNAs identified in all batches were visualized using a heat map (Fig. 4B). Twenty highly expressed miRNAs were compared between the three ASC-EXO batches. The top twenty miRNAs accounted for about 78% of the total, and the top five miRNAs were found to be let-7a-5p, let-7b-5p, miR-4301, miR-23a-3p, and miR-423-5p together contributing to approximately 46% of the counts (Supplementary Fig. 2). Among these, five representative miRNAs (let-7a-5p, let-7b-5p, miR-23a-3p, miR-24-3p, and miR-29a-3p) were confirmed by RT-qPCR analysis (Fig. 4C). Functionality of ASC-EXOs

Anti-inflammation / CD73 concentration & activitySeveral assays were performed to evaluate the functionality of ASC-EXO. First, macrophage (RAW 264.7 cells) uptake analysis using fluorescence microscopy of PKH67-labeled ASC-EXO was performed, and internalization of the labeled exosomes was associated with the macrophages (Fig. 6A). As CD73 has been implicated in mediating some of the anti-inflammatory effects of ASC-EXO, we quantified the expression of this molecule in the exosomes isolated from the three different ASCs, detecting no difference among the ASC-EXO (4.0 × 1010 particles/mL). No significant differences in CD73-associated enzymatic activity in the ASC-EXO were observed among the batches (Figs. 6B and C). Further, we observed a relationship between the CD73 concentration and the activity of CD73 in 16 different batches of ASC-EXO (Fig. 6D). ASC-EXO from all ASCs significantly decreased interleukin-6 (IL-6) release from LPS-exposed RAW 264.7 cells, amounting to approximately 50%, with no or little difference between the different batches (Fig. 6E). In addition, we observed a statistically significant relationship between the concentration of CD73 and the anti-inflammatory effect (%) in 11 batches of ASC-EXO (Fig. 6F).

Effects on HDF proliferation

The uptake of PKH67-labeled ASC-EXO to HDF was observed using fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 7A). Further, the ASC-EXO (5.0 × 109 particles/mL) increased HDF cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner, with the ASC1-EXO, ASC2-EXO, and ASC3-EXO showing an average of 138%, 142%, and 144% increase in total proliferation compared to the control, respectively. This indicates no difference in this functionality between the batches (Fig. 7B).

Enhanced collagen synthesis in HDF

Quantifying collagen type I produced by HDFs indicates that the ASC-EXO dose-dependently increased collagen production (Fig. 7C). Further, increased synthesis of the extracellular matrix Procollagen type I protein (PIP) was observed after treatment with ASC-EXO, with no differences between batches. Thus, the lower dose of ASC-EXO (3.0 × 109 particles/mL) induced increases of 8.1, 9.9, and 12.4-folds of PIP with ASC1-EXO, ASC2-EXO, and ASC3-EXO compared to conditioned media. As a result of treatment with a concentration of 1.5 × 1010 particles/mL of ASC-EXOs, significant increases of 14.4, 18.3, and 20.1-folds in ASC1-EXO, ASC2-EXO, and ASC3-EXO were confirmed (Fig. 7D).

Effect of ASC-EXO on Atopic dermatitis-like lesions in NC/Nga mice

To determine whether ASC-exosomes improve AD signs in vivo, we determine the effects of ASC-EXOs in NC/Nga mice. Biostir®-AD cream was used to induce AD-like lesions. This cream contains house dust mite antigens. The ASC-EXO were given subcutaneously (SC) eight or twelve times over 4 weeks. Prednisolone (10 mg/kg) was orally administered daily as a positive control (Fig. 8A). The ASC-EXO treatment significantly decreased AD signs, with ear thickness consistently reduced (Fig. 8B and C). Also, ASC-EXO treatment reduced the number of eosinophils significantly (Fig. 8D).

Discussion

In this study, we have carefully characterized exosomes produced by different cell culture batches from three different donors of adipose-tissue-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (ASC-EXO) to determine inter-individual and inter-batch variability of the different cell banks. Firstly, there were no differences in the characteristics of cell growth or cell surface markers. The exosomes released from the three different batches had similar morphology, similar size distribution measured by nano tracking analysis, similar zeta potential, and similar expression of tetraspanins. Further, the different batches produced similar quantities of exosomes. Proteomics analysis showed that 777 proteins were detected in all three ASC-EXO production batches, and bioinformatics analysis suggests that the top biological process is “cytoplasmic translation”, the top cellular component is “extracellular exosome”, and the top molecular functions are “RNA-binding” and “Extracellular Matrix constituent”. Short RNA sequencing identified approximately 300 miRNAs in each ASC-EXO batch, with Let-7a-5p, Let-7b-5p, miR-23-3p, miR-24-3p, and miR-29a-3p being some of the most common, and with small variability among batches. Lipidomics analysis identified phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine as the most common lipids, with minor variability among batches. Similar anti-inflammatory effects were observed with the ASC-EXO in a model of LPS-induced IL-6 release from macrophages. Further, similar quantification of CD73 and CD73 functions were observed in the exosomes from the different batches. The exosomes from the three batches were taken up similarly by macrophages. Overall, this study suggests that donor- and batch consistency is relatively high for ASC-EXO.

MSCs can be acquired from many different tissues beyond adipose tissue, including bone marrow, umbilical cord blood, umbilical cord Wharton’s jelly, and placenta. MSCs and their released exosomes, regardless of MSC tissue source, have documented anti-inflammatory and tissue-regenerating characteristics, with relatively small differences among the sources [9–12]. Another parameter that can influence biological function is the donor's age. Thus, MSC-Exosome from adipose tissue of young adults, as used in the current study, has been suggested to be more effective in different disease models [13, 14]. A third parameter to consider when developing MSC-Exosome therapeutics is the number of cell passages that the MSC has undergone before exosome isolation [15], which is why we were careful to maintain similar passage numbers among the tested batches [9–11].

The yield of ASC-EXO per cell in culture is approximately 5000 ~ 8000 particles/cell, with similarities between the different batches. This amount should be considered reasonable from a manufacturing perspective and is similar to what has been reported by others [16–18]. The isolation method is another important variable for the yield of exosomes in any setting. In this case, we have utilized tangential flow filtration, which in previous studies has moderate loss of material [19–21]. All scientific publications describing the biological effects of exosomes should include careful characterization of the vesicles, as proposed by the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV), which includes the identification of typical exosomal proteins, such as tetraspanins, and ideally, electron microscopy. In this study, we performed these analyses for all batches without identifying any apparent differences, allowing us to pursue further and more detailed multi-omics analytical comparisons among the batches [22].

The proteomics analysis of ASC-EXO from the three different batches provided surprisingly similar data. As the goal of this project was to compare the ASC-EXO from ASC derived from other donors, a single proteomics run was performed per batch, which resulted in comparable information emerging from the three different cellular sources (Fig. 3), which is similar to the results from previous projects comparing batches from single MSC cellular source, in this case originating from bone marrow [23]. Further, GO-term functional analysis showed very similar results among the batches, despite some small differences in molecular cargo, suggesting they have the same biological effects. This observation suggests that ASC-EXOs contain multiple functional proteins, such that if certain molecules are present at lower levels in one batch, others can compensate to maintain activity, indicating functional redundancy. Overall, these data suggest that the stability of the exosome proteome and functionality is similar among different batches of cells.

To further emphasize the robustness of our production process, we compared the molecular consistency of our ASC-EXO batches with both proteomic and transcriptomic approaches of MSC-derived exosomes. For instance, recent study reported that adipose-derived MSC-Exosomes produced under standardized conditions showed high similarity in miRNA and protein profiles across three independent batches, supporting the feasibility of consistent EV manufacturing [24]. While some variations have been reported in other systems [25], our results demonstrate that the core proteomic signature and the resulting anti-inflammatory efficacy of ASC-EXOs remain remarkably stable. This consistency is crucial for addressing the regulatory requirements for biologic medicines and highlights the potential of this GMP-compliant platform to provide a reliable 'off-the-shelf' therapeutic agent to treat inflammatory skin diseases.

The small RNA sequencing results revealed the presence of multiple microRNA species in the ASC-EXO, again with similarities among the different cellular sources (Fig. 4). Several of the detected microRNAs have been implicated in regulating inflammatory processes; for example, recent studies have associated miR-23a and let-7b with resolution of inflammation [26, 27]. Further, let-7b has been proposed to regulate anti-inflammatory responses in macrophages. Although the relative contribution of microRNAs in the anti-inflammatory effects of ASC-EXOs remains unclear, it is reassuring that the identified miRNAs have been implicated in relavant biological functions. Importantly, ASC-EXOs may carry other RNA species beyond miRNA, including mRNA, tRNA, and lncRNAs, which we did not analyze in the current study. However, the functionality of exosomes has often been attributed to either the protein cargo or miRNA cargo, which is why we focused on these components in this study. Further, any single molecule in the ASC-EXO is unlikely to be important for the observed anti-inflammatory function, as multiple molecules will likely contribute to the overall effects. Importantly, mirroring the proteomics data, GO-term analysis of target genes for identified small RNAs showed overlapping functional profiles across the three donors, despite minor batch-specific differences in RNA composition, again suggesting similar functionality among the batches [26, 27].

There is a limited volume of data available regarding the lipid content of MSC-derived exosomes, including those present in exosomes from ASC [28–32]. Although the global lipidomics analysis identified small differences in lipid content between the batches, the overall lipid profiles did not differ significantly among the different donors, e.g., glycerophospholipid (PC, PE, PI) and sphingolipid are the most common lipid species in ASC-EXO. It is well known that treatment with ASC-EXO in vivo effectively attenuates inflammatory responses [33]. We and others have suggested that certain lipid species, such as sphingolipids and phospholipids, may be key mediators of the anti-inflammatory responses. Thus, sphingolipids and sphingosine-1-phosphate can regulate a series of cellular signaling cascades associated with inflammation via receptor(s)-dependent or independent pathways in both endothelial and epithelial cells [34–36]. Although additional studies are required to fully understand the role of lipids in exosome functionality, we suggest that certain lipids, including sphingolipids, present in the ASC-EXO could contribute to anti-inflammatory functions [37].

We further compared the in vitro biological functionality of the ASC-EXO from the three ASC cell banks, specifically on anti-inflammatory effects on macrophages (RAW 264.7 cell line). As has been shown in many studies of MSC-EXO to date, the ASC-EXO also conveyed potent anti-inflammatory effects, as primarily studied in the macrophage cell line RAW264.7. Thus, the LPS-induced release of IL-6 was similarly reduced by equivalent doses of ASC-EXO from the three different sources. The exact molecular mechanisms by which MSC-EXOs mediate anti-inflammatory effects remain obscure and may be conveyed by multiple exosomal molecules, including proteins, lipids, and RNA-cargo. However, one molecule specifically suggested to be involved in the membrane-associated enzyme CD73 was similarly expressed in the different batches in the current study. Further, we could observe a relationship between the CD73 concentration in the ASC-EXO isolates and the anti-inflammatory effects, which indirectly implicates this molecule as a partial contributor to the observed biological effects. Further, the enzymatic activity of CD73 was similar among the batches. Several previous studies have also implicated CD73 as an essential molecule to mediate the anti-inflammatory effects of MSC-derived EXOs [38–40]. The observation of the three different batches of ASC-EXO having similar in vitro anti-inflammatory effects was confirmed in vivo in a mouse model of atopic dermatitis. Thus, allergen-induced skin thickness and blood eosinophilia were reduced when skin lesions were treated with either one of the three different batches of ASC-EXO. These results are in line with multiple previous studies showing that MSC-EXO can attenuate the skin inflammatory response in models of atopic dermatitis, as well as in clinical cases of dupilumab-associated redness of facial skin. Thus, the current results in combination with previous studies strongly support efficacy of ASC-EXO in atopic dermatitis and associated diseases [41–45].

Emerging data show that MSC-derived EXOs can have profound anti-inflammatory effects, also in clinical settings. Several double-blind, placebo-controlled studies have shown that MSC-EXO can attenuate lung injury induced by COVID-19 [1–3]. We have previously, in translational studies using in vitro techniques and in vivo models of SARS-CoV-2 infection and H1N1 influenza, observed that the effects of the ASC-EXO may be both directly anti-viral as well as anti-inflammatory, collectively resulting in improved disease control. Further, ASC-EXO has previously been shown in a double-blind controlled clinical trial to facilitate wound healing after facial laser therapy [4]. These data suggest that MSC-EXOs can also have therapeutic potential in different clinical diseases.

In summary, this study describes that three different batches of adipose tissue MSC from different donors have similar growth expansion characteristics and produce exosomes with similar protein, RNA, and lipid cargo, conveying the same biological functions. This suggests that either of these sources may be used to develop MSC-based therapeutics candidates to treat inflammation. It is important, however, that every batch of exosomes produced for human use undergo detailed quality control to provide the optimal drugs for patient use.

Materials and methods

Kits and reagents

The kits and reagents used in this study were purchased from the following sources: Minimum Essential Medium (MEM)-α, gentamycin, Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), TrypLE™ (recombinant trypsin) from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Grand Island, NY, USA); fetal bovine serum (FBS), and Exosome-Human CD9, CD63, CD81 Flow Detection Reagent (Dynabeads magnetic bead) from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Carlsbad, CA, USA); recombinant human fibroblast growth factor-basic (bFGF) from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA); Pierce™ BCA Protein assay kit, StemPro Differentiation kit for adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic lineage from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA); normal immunoglobulin G (IgG), Phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated antibodies for CD9, CD29, CD31, CD45, CD63, CD81, CD90, CD105, and HLA-DR from BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA, USA); enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for calnexin from LSBio (Seattle, WA, USA); ELISA for CD73, and cytochrome C from Abcam (Cambridge, UK) and R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA); 500-kDa molecular weight cut-off filter membrane cartridge from GE Healthcare (Chicago, IL, USA).

Characterization of ASCs

Human adipose tissue from a healthy donor was obtained by ExoCoBio Inc. (Seoul, Korea) with approval of the Institutional Review Board of CHA University Medical Center (Gyeonggi-do, Korea; IRB No. CHAMC 2019–05-040–018; Title of approval project: Establishment and Characterization of Adipose Tissue Derived Stromal Cell Bank for Exosome Production; Date of approval: 2021–09-23) and evaluated according to the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS, Korea) guidelines. A human ASC cell bank (cell codes of ASC1, ASC2, and ASC3 are ECAM-P004, ECAM-P006, and 332 ECAM-P008) of passage three was prepared as described previously [1, 7], and stored in liquid nitrogen. Frozen cells were thawed at 37 °C, plated at a density of 3 × 103 cells/cm2, and cultured with MEM-α containing 10% FBS, 10 ng/mL bFGF, and 1% gentamycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cultured ASCs were split before reaching confluence. At the end of each passage, cell counts, cell viability, and size were performed by an automated cell counter (LUNA-II™ Automated Cell Counter, Logos Biosystems, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) with trypan blue staining. The characterizations of ASCs were performed with previously described methods [7]. ASCs were characterized for cell surface marker expression by flow cytometry and trilineage differentiation potentials according to the criteria defined by the International Society of Cellular Therapy (ISCT) [6].

Isolation of ASC-EXO

ASCs isolated from the adipose tissue were sub-cultured at a density of 3 × 103 cells/cm2 in MEM-α containing 10% FBS. The cell bank of passage three was stored in liquid nitrogen (2.0 × 106 cells/mL/vial). The quality control (QC) of the ASCs was assessed by testing for cell viability, sterility, and the presence of mycoplasma, and endotoxin levels. The cell surface markers for ASCs were determined by the flow cytometry of positive markers (CD29, CD73, CD90, and CD105), and negative markers (CD31, CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR). The trilineage differentiation (adipogenic, chondrogenic, and osteogenic differentiation) abilities of ASCs were induced with differentiation kit (StemPro differentiation kits, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA) and detected by staining with oil red O (Sigma-Aldrich), alizarin red S (Sigma-Aldrich), and alcian blue (Sigma-Aldrich) solution according to the manufacturer's instructions. To generate the ASC-conditioned media (CM), a cell bank was thawed and cultured until passage 8. ASCs at passage 8 were plated at a density of 6 × 103 cells/cm2 in a 10-layer Cell Factory flask (6,320 cm2, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA) and cultured with MEM-α containing 10% FBS, 10 ng/mL bFGF, and 1% gentamycin in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 in air at 37 °C for 3 days up to 90% confluency (5 × 107 cells / 6,320 cm2/flask). For the collection of exosomes, cells were washed with DPBS and maintained in serum-free and phenol-red-free MEM-α. The cells were further cultured for 24 h before the ASC-CM was collected for further exosome isolation [1, 8].

ASC-EXO were isolated from the ASC-CM by tangential flow filtration (TFF) as previously described. Firstly, the CM was filtered through a 0.45 μm polyethersulfone (PES) membrane filter (Sartorius, Goettingen, Germany) to remove larger fragments from the CM, such as cells, cell debris, and apoptotic bodies for clarification process. The CM was then concentrated by TFF with a 500 kDa molecular weight cut-off filter membrane cartridge (Repligen, Chicago, IL, USA), and buffer exchange was performed by diafiltration with an excipient. Finally, ASC-EXO were sterile-filtered through a 0.22 μm polyethersulfone (PES) membrane filter (Sartorius, Goettingen, Germany) and stored at temperatures below − 60 °C. Characterization of the ASC-EXO was performed according to the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles 2018 (MISEV2018) recommended by the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) [5].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The cryo-transmission microscope (Cryo-TEM) analysis was performed according to the method of the previous study [1]. Cryo-TEM images were obtained using a Cryo-Focused Ion Beam-Scanning Electron Microscope installed at the Korea Institute of Science and Technology (KIST, Seoul, Korea). To prepare samples for cryo-TEM study Lacey carbon grids were glow-discharged (15 s, 25 mA) in Cressington 108 (Cressington Scientific Instruments, Watford, UK) and subsequently blotted using Vitrobot Mark IV system (FP5350/60, FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, USA) with the following settings: 100% humidity, 4 °C, blot time of 5 s, blot force of 5, blot total of 1, wait time of 5 s, and drain time of 0 s. For maintenance of vitrified sample grids at a temperature of around − 178 °C within the TEM, a side-entry Gatan 626 cryo-holder (Gatan, Pleasanton, CA, USA) was used. The grids were then examined using a Cryo-TEM (Tecnai™ G2 F20, FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA) equipped at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. A 4 K × 4 K, Ultrascan 4000 CCD camera (Gatan, Pleasanton, CA, USA) was used for image recording. A low-dose method (exposures at 1000 electrons per nm2/s) was used for cryo-TEM.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and zeta-potential measurements

The size distribution, zeta potential, and concentrations of ASC-EXO were analyzed by NTA using the ZetaView PMX220 (Particle Metrix, Meerbusch, Germany) instrument equipped with a 488 nm laser. ASC-EXOs were diluted to obtain approximately 100–300 particles per frame and analyzed by the ZetaView version 8.05.14 software (Particle Metrix, Meerbusch, Germany). Following the manufacturer’s manual, polystyrene 100 nm-sized standard beads (Particle Metrix, Meerbusch, Germany) were used for the daily calibration and instrument performance check.

miRNA profiling

Total RNA was isolated from ASC-EXO using the miRNeasy kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and purified RNA was quantified using spectrophotometry. Total RNA was stored at − 80 °C immediately after extraction. The miRNA sequencing analysis of the isolated samples was performed by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Korea). Briefly, cDNA libraries were generated using the Small RNA-Seq Kit for Illumina (Takara, Shiga, Japan). cDNA fragments are sequenced by the read length using the sequence-by-synthesis method on the Illumina platform. After sequencing, the raw sequence reads are filtered based on quality. The adapter sequences are also trimmed off the raw sequence reads. Then, the processed reads are gathered forming a unique cluster. Unique clustered reads are aligned to miRBase v22.1 across the human genome to classify known miRNAs. Total RNAs were extracted from ASC-EVs using QIAzol lysis reagent (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit (QIAGEN). To normalize for technical variation, 0.16 fmol of synthetic cel-miR-39-3p (QIAGEN) was spiked into the ASC-EVs sample after the addition of a denaturing solution. The quality and quantity of the miRNAs were assessed using a spectrophotometer (DS-11/ DeNovix, Wilmington, DE, USA). Five miRNAs were measured by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2.0 μL of template RNAs were reverse-transcribed using miRCURY LNA miRNA PCR Assay (QIAGEN), and then qPCR was performed using miRCURY LNA SYBR Green PCR Kit (QIAGEN). The miRNA primers (Let-7a-5p, Let-7b-5p, miR-23a-3p, miR-24-3p, miR-29a-3p) from miRCURY LNA miRNA PCR Assays (QIAGEN) were used. Analysis was performed using QuantStudio 3 Real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) to quantify the amount of miRNA. Relative expression levels were normalized to spike in, cel-miR-39-3p, and calculated using the standard curve methods. The target genes of miRNA were predicted using the Funrich software (version 3.1.3), and gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed with this tool.

Lipidomics analysis

Total lipids were extracted from ASC-EXO and donor cells by the method of Bligh and Dyer method, with modification [31]. The lipidomic analyses were carried out on an Exion UPLC (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) system coupled with a QTRAP 5500 system (AB Sciex, Foster City, CA, USA) under an electrospray ionization mode. Before lipid extraction, ASC-EXOs were measured for protein concentration using Micro BCA™ Protein Assay Kit. After dissolving the exosomes, total protein measurement was performed by BCA Assay, and the sample was divided into aliquots with 300 μg/mL protein, which were subsequently freeze-dried. All the quantification experiments were conducted using internal standard calibration. The various lipid types measured included phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylinositol (PI), sphingomyelin (SM), non-esterified fatty acid (NEFA), phosphatidylserine (PS), ceramide (CER), triacylglycerol (TG), diacylglycerol (DG), cholesterol ester (Chol ester), Cholesterol (Chol), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), dihydrosphingomyelin (dhSM), cardiolipin (CL), dihydroceramide (dhCER), hexosylceramides (Hex-Cer), phosphatidic acid (PA), monoradylglycer (MG). The LC–MS/MS analysis was performed according to the method of the previous report with minor modifications [33]. The ASC-EXO lipid levels were expressed in pmol per mg protein.

Proteomics analysis

The LC–MS/MS analysis was performed according to the method of the previous report with minor modifications [46]. All MS and MS/MS spectra obtained by the Ultimate 3000 UPLC system (Dionex, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) connected to a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were acquired in data-dependent top 12 mode. The MS/MS data were analyzed with MASCOT v2.7 (Matrix Science, Inc., Boston, MA, USA), using the UniProt human database for protein identification, and with a parameter corresponding to a false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff of 1%. The exponentially modified protein abundance index (emPAI) was generated using MASCOT, and mol% was calculated according to emPAI values [47]. GO terms corresponding to cellular component, biological process, and molecular function were analyzed using the Funrich analysis tool.

Anti-inflammation assay (Quantitation of IL-6 by ELISA)

The murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7 cells (ATCC, TIB-71, Manassas, VA, USA) are maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and incubated 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Cells were plated in 24-well plates. After 24 h, pre-treated with dexamethasone (200 μM) or ASC-EXO for 6 h. Cells were stimulated by a serum-free medium containing LPS (1 μg/mL). Conditioned media were collected after 24 h of treatment. The quantity of IL-6 was determined by Mouse IL-6 DuoSet ELISA kits (DY406, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) with the obtained supernatant and analyzed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration of IL-6 was quantified by measuring the absorbance at a wavelength of 450 nm with a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Quantification of CD73

The quantification of CD73 from ASC-EXO was analyzed using the Human CD73 SimpleStep ELISA kit (Abcam, ab254513, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The lysed ASC-EXO sample was prepared by mixing 200 µL of extraction buffer PTR with 20 µL of extraction enhancer solution. The mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. A standard curve ranging from 0 to 20,000 pg/mL was prepared using the human CD73 recombinant protein provided in the kit. After incubation, 50 µL of the ASC-EXO or CD73 standard solution was added to a 96-well microplate pre-coated with 50 µL of CD73 capture antibody. The plate was incubated at room temperature, 400 rpm for 60 min on a microplate mixer. Following incubation, the wells were washed three times with 200 µL of 1 × wash buffer. After the final wash, 100 µL of TMB substrate was added to each well to initiate the CD73 quantification assay. The plate was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. To stop the reaction, 100 µL of stop solution was added to each well, and the plate was gently shaken to mix the contents. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Measurement CD73 activity

The phosphate activity by CD73-mediated hydrolysis of AMP from ASC-EXO was analyzed using a phosphate Assay Kit-PiColorLock™ (Abcam, ab270004, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The ASC-EXO sample was washed 3 times using 250 µL of 20 mM Tris buffer per well each time. After removal of the last wash, CD73 enzymatic assay was initiated with 60 µL of ASC-EXO sample or the phosphate standard solutions by adding 40 µL of 2 mM adenosine 5’-monophosphate monohydrate (AMP; A2252, Sigma-Aldrich) into each well. The samples were incubated at room temperature, 300 rpm for 30 min on a microplate mixer. A phosphate standard series of 0–50 µM was prepared using a stock phosphate solution provided in the kit. 40 µL from the ASC-EXO sample or the phosphate standard solutions were transferred to a new transparent 96-well plate that was pre-loaded with 6 µL of the stabilizer in each well. The plate was gently shaken to mix the contents in each well and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. The absorbance signal at 635 nm was measured in a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Cellular uptake assay

The cellular uptake of ASC-EXO was measured in two different cell lines. (1) The murine macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 cells (ATCC, TIB-71, Manassas, VA, USA) is maintained in DMEM with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and incubated at 5% CO2, 37 °C. Cells were plated in 24-well plates. After 24 h, pre-treated with ASC-EXOs for 6 h. Cells were stimulated by a serum-free medium containing LPS (1 μg/mL). (2) HDF were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and incubated at 5% CO2 and 37 °C. The ASC-EXO were labeled with PKH67 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and labeled ASC-EXO were purified by MW3000 Exosome Spin Column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Labeled exosomes were added to HDF and incubated for 48 h at 5% CO2, 37 °C. The cells were fixed with 10% formalin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and counter-stained with CellMask™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and Hoechst 33,258 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The uptake of exosomes was visualized using a CELENA S Imaging System (Logos Biosystems, Gyeonggi-do, Korea).

Cell proliferation assay in HDF

The HDF proliferation assay was performed according to the method of the previous study [48]. Human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). HDFs were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. HDF proliferation was evaluated using a CCK-8 assay kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). HDFs were seeded into 48-well plates at a density of 20,000 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. The cells were then treated with ASC-EXOs in serum-deficient medium (1% FBS) or positive control (complete growth medium with 10% FBS) for 24 h, and cell proliferation was measured using CCK-8 assays. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Collagen synthesis in HDF

The effect of ASC-EXO on HDF procollagen type 1 C-peptide (PIP) synthesis was evaluated with a PIP enzyme-linked immune sorbent assay (ELISA) kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Takara, Shiga, Japan). HDFs were seeded in a 24-well plate and treated with ASC-EXOs in serum-free medium or positive control (HDF growth medium) for 24 h. The PIP contents were measured using a microplate reader at 450 nm (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) [8].

Immunofluorescence staining for collagen type I in HDF

HDFs were plated at a density of 50,000 cells per structure in a 24-well plate. The culture medium was changed every three days. At seven days, Ti structures were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) in PBS for 3 min at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were incubated with polyclonal rabbit to collagen type I (1:100, Boster Biological Technology Ltd., Wuhan, China) diluted in PBS containing 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Roche, Shanghai, China) for 1 h, followed by incubation with FITC-conjugated-goat-anti-rabbit (1:200, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) diluted in PBS for 1 h. Finally, cells were stained with DAPI. After each step, the cells were washed three times with PBS. Images were taken from each surface on an Olympus DP71 fluorescence microscope (Olympus Co., Tokyo, Japan).

Induction of atopic dermatitis in mice

The atopic dermatitis mouse study was performed as described previously [49]. All animal experiments were reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. The animal study was approved as the “In vivo efficacy testing of ASC-exosomes using the Nc/Nga mouse atopic dermatitis model” by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee Knotus, Republic of Korea (approval no. KNOTUS IACUC 22-KE-0639) on “2022-10-18” and performed in accordance with the Animal Experimentation Policy. AD-like skin lesions were induced in 5-week-old male (25.0 ~ 27.0 g) NC/Nga mice (LSC Inc., Japan) by applying Biostir®-AD Cream (Biostir, Inc., Osaka, Japan), an ointment containing allergens of the house dust mite antigen (HDM; D. farinae), as described previously. Briefly, after hair removal, 150 μL of 4% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate was applied to the pinna area and dried using cold air. Biostir®-AD Cream was applied twice a week topically for 3 weeks. At the start of the experiment, mice were randomly divided into the following groups: normal (negative control), vehicle-treated, ASC-EXO (2.0 × 109 particles/head)-treated via subcutaneous (SC), and prednisolone-treated (positive control). Vehicle and ASC-EXO were administered SC twice or thrice a week for four weeks, and prednisolone (10 mg/kg) was administered orally daily. A total of ten mice per group were randomly assigned to each treatment arm. During the experiment, mice of each group received isoflurane inhalant anesthesia, which alleviates pain. Subsequently, serum and skin samples were collected from the animals for further analysis. Blinding was not feasible during treatment; however, results were analyzed in a blinded manner whenever possible. For the vehicle control group, normal saline was administered using the same route and volume as the treatment groups. All animal experiments were performed in a Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) environment to prevent opportunistic infections. To minimize suffering and ensure animal welfare, the mice were monitored daily for signs of discomfort, including skin integrity, physical activity, and weight loss. No adverse events or signs of infection were recorded in the vehicle-treated mice, confirming that the experimental procedures did not induce extraneous distress. Animals were housed five per cage in rodent cages during the acclimatisation and experimental phases.

Tissue collection and histological analysis

The tissues of the experimental mice were removed, fixed in 10% phosphate-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) solution. Thickness of skin tissue section was measured by Zen 2.3 Blue Edition (Carl Zeiss, Goettingen, Germany) at three different sites in randomly captured images of H&E staining.

Eosinophil cell counting was performed on peripheral blood mononucleated cells (PBMCs), which were isolated by Histopaque-1077 (Sigma-Aldrich) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Isolated PBMCs were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in RPMI 1640 media (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The number of PBMCs was determined by TC-20 automated cell counter (Bio-Rad). One hundred μL of cells (total 1 × 105 cells) were loaded onto Cytospin slide and centrifuged by Shadon Cytospin 4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). PBMCs were dried, fixed with ethanol, and stained with Diff-Quik (Sigma-Aldrich), and differentially counted in 5 different fields under 400 × magnification.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 5.0 software (GraphPad, Bethesda, MD, USA). One-way ANOVA was used when comparing more than two groups of samples with one condition, while two-way ANOVA was used for comparisons involving more than two conditions. Post hoc Bonferroni’s correction for multiple comparisons was applied after ANOVA analysis. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with levels of significance denoted as follows: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, and **** p < 0.0001. Each experiment was performed in at least three independent replicates. Data from at least three independent experiments are presented as the mean ± SD.
