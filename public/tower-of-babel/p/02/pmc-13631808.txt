Nocturnal Hypoxemia Burden Correlates With Vestibular Dysfunction in Obstructive Sleep Apnea
Authors: Dachan Kim, Seong Hoon Bae, Hyung‐Ju Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a potential contributor to vestibular dysfunction, though specific correlating parameters remain unclear. This study aimed to determine whether hypoxemia severity indices correlate quantitatively with caloric slow‐phase eye velocity (SPV), a marker of vestibular function.MethodsIn a retrospective cohort of 82 adults undergoing both full‐night PSG and bithermal caloric testing, the primary outcome was total caloric SPV (°/s; sum of four irrigations). Fifteen prespecified predictors were evaluated, including hypoxemia burden (apnea–hypopnea index [AHI], oxygen desaturation index [ODI], cumulative time with SpO2 < 90% [CT90], nadir SpO2), sleep fragmentation (total arousal index), sleep architecture (N1, N2, N3, rapid eye movement [REM] proportions and sleep efficiency) and ancillary measures (snoring index, pulse rate, age, sex and body mass index [BMI]). Unadjusted associations were calculated using Pearson's correlations. For adjusted inference, ordinary least squares with backward Akaike information criterion (AIC) reduction were employed. Feature importance was quantified without prespecifying functional form by training a random‐forest regressor and computing TreeSHAP values.ResultsSPV was inversely correlated with AHI (r = −0.295, p = 0.007), ODI (r = −0.261, p = 0.018) and CT90 (r = −0.254, p = 0.021). Sleep efficiency, snoring index, pulse rate, nadir SpO2, and sleep stage proportions showed no significant associations. In the adjusted linear model, AHI and sex were retained; each 10‐event/h AHI increase corresponded to a 3.16°/s lower SPV (95% CI: −6.05 to −0.28; p = 0.032). The random forest model achieved a pooled fivefold R 2 of 0.065, indicating low explained variance. Regarding mean absolute SHAP values, hypoxemia‐domain metrics ranked highest (ODI, AHI, CT90), followed by BMI, while other features had minimal impact.ConclusionNocturnal hypoxemia burden—reflecting the frequency and cumulative duration of oxygen desaturation—emerged as the principal predictor of reduced caloric SPV in patients with OSA. While the low explained variance of our models (R 2 = 0.065) suggests these associations should be viewed as hypothesis‐generating, the findings support prioritising desaturation assessment in clinical settings. Further longitudinal research is essential to verify whether reducing hypoxemia leads to improved vestibular outcomes.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common sleep‐related breathing disorder characterised by recurrent upper airway collapse during sleep, leading to intermittent airflow reduction, oxygen desaturation and sleep fragmentation [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. The vestibular system—with its high metabolic demand and delicate microvascular supply—may be particularly vulnerable to these stresses [6]. Research suggests that OSA can negatively affect oxygenation in the brain and inner ear, and it has been associated with dizziness and balance issues [7, 8, 9]. Recent studies have also linked OSA to specific vestibular characteristics, such as an increased prevalence of vestibular migraine in clinical groups (~10%–11% in severe OSA) [6], abnormalities in the otolith pathway observed in cervical/ocular VEMPs that are related to the severity of OSA [10, 11, 12], and a higher occurrence of REM‐dominant respiratory events in vertigo patients with OSA (41.3% vs. 15.9%) [13]. Additionally, national epidemiological data show a greater incidence of benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, Ménière's disease and vestibular neuritis in people with OSA compared to matched controls [14]. However, the precise quantitative link between OSA and objective vestibular function is still uncertain, and a recent meta‐analysis pointed out the inconsistent effects of OSA treatment on vestibular outcomes [7].

Multiple, non‐exclusive pathways may connect OSA to vestibular dysfunction. Intermittent hypoxemia followed by reoxygenation can elevate reactive oxygen species, driving oxidative stress, systemic and vascular inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction, while also increasing sympathetic activity and blood pressure lability [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. The inner ear—especially the otolithic organs and horizontal semicircular canal—relies on terminal arterial branches with limited collateralization, rendering it susceptible to ischemia–reperfusion injury [9, 10]. In parallel, sleep fragmentation and poor sleep quality may impair central sensory integration and attention, potentially degrading vestibulo‐ocular performance even in the absence of overt symptoms [14, 15]. Metabolic comorbidities, such as obesity and insulin resistance, along with microvascular dysregulation, may further exacerbate these effects [4, 9, 15].

Amongst vestibular assessments, the caloric test is the clinical standard for evaluating low‐frequency horizontal semicircular canal function [21, 22, 23]. Its principal index, slow‐phase eye velocity (SPV)—which reflects the peak intensity of the vestibulo‐ocular reflex generated by thermal stimulation—provides a sensitive, continuous measure that can detect subtle bilateral reductions [10]. A meta‐analysis reported approximately 4.8‐fold higher odds of abnormal caloric results in patients with OSA [7]. However, definitions of “abnormal” caloric results vary substantially across studies (e.g., SPV cutoffs, definitions of canal paresis or unilateral weakness, irrigation protocols) [9, 10, 24]. This heterogeneity limits between‐study comparability and inference. Critically, few investigations have performed direct, quantitative, head‐to‐head comparisons of multiple polysomnography (PSG) parameters against SPV as a continuous outcome, leaving a key gap in understanding which OSA physiological domain most closely influences vestibular responses.

PSG quantifies distinct domains of OSA physiology, including event frequency (e.g., apnea–hypopnea index [AHI] and oxygen desaturation index [ODI]), sleep architecture and efficiency (stage proportions and sleep efficiency), and ancillary measures (e.g., snoring index and pulse rate) [13, 16, 25]. In this study, we examined the relationships between these PSG parameters and SPV in a single cohort undergoing standardised caloric testing. Using covariate‐adjusted regression and penalised, explainable modelling, we aimed to quantify the relative contributions of PSG parameters to vestibular responses without presupposing the most influential parameter.

Materials and Methods

Participants and Study Design

We retrospectively reviewed records of adult patients who underwent both full‐night attended PSG and bithermal caloric testing at our tertiary sleep–balance centre between 1 January 2017 and 6 August 2025. Both tests were performed within a 5‐year window to ensure comparable physiological states. Exclusion criteria included incomplete caloric testing (n = 4), spontaneous nystagmus during irrigation (n = 2), or air‐based rather than water‐based caloric testing (n = 7). Eighty‐two patients met all the inclusion criteria. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Severance Hospital (IRB No. 4‐2025‐1159), and informed consent was waived because only routinely acquired diagnostic data were analysed retrospectively.

PSG Acquisition and Scoring

PSG was performed overnight in a controlled laboratory (Natus SleepWorks, Natus Medical, USA) using electroencephalography (EEG; frontal, central, occipital), electrooculography (EOG), chin electromyography (EMG), electrocardiography (ECG), airflow monitoring via nasal pressure cannula and thermistor, thoraco‐abdominal belts, body position sensors and finger oximetry. Sleep stages and events were scored according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidelines (v2.6). Extracted variables included sleep efficiency, proportions of sleep stages (N1, N2, N3 and rapid eye movement [REM]), arousal index (events/h), AHI (events/h), ODI (≥ 3% drops/h), nadir oxygen saturation (SpO2, %), cumulative time with SpO2 < 90% (CT90, % of total sleep time, a measure of hypoxemia duration), snoring index and pulse rate.

Caloric Testing Procedure

Bithermal caloric testing (VisualEyes VNG, Micromedical Technologies, Chatham, Illinois, USA) was performed with patients in the supine position and the head elevated 30°. Each ear was irrigated for 30 s at 30°C and 44°C in the order: warm‐right, warm‐left, cool‐right, cool‐left, with 5‐min intervals between irrigations to mitigate carry‐over effects. Eye movements were recorded by video‐oculography, and SPV was quantified automatically using the system software. The sum of all four irrigation‐specific SPVs served as the primary vestibular outcome.

Statistical Analysis

The full predictor set comprised 15 variables (AHI, ODI, CT90, total arousal index, N1/N2/N3/REM stage proportions, sleep efficiency, snoring index, pulse rate, nadir O2 saturation, body mass index [BMI], age and sex). To characterise unadjusted relationships between each variable and the caloric outcome, we computed Pearson's correlation coefficients with two‐sided p‐values. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the substantial multicollinearity amongst physiological predictors, which renders strict corrections like Bonferroni overly conservative, we did not apply multiple testing adjustments to univariate correlations. Instead, to differentiate robust signals from noise, we prioritised variable selection using penalized linear modelling (Akaike information criterion, AIC) and non‐linear ensemble methods (specifically, random forest). For adjusted inference, we fitted an ordinary least‐squares model with SPV (°/s) as a continuous outcome. To obtain a parsimonious model relative to sample size, we used backward stepwise selection with the AIC and iteratively removed predictors that lowered AIC until no further reduction was possible.

To complement the linear analyses and allow for nonlinear relationships and interactions amongst predictors, we built a random forest regression model to predict SPV. Model complexity was tuned by a randomised search across reasonable ranges of depth, number of trees, split and leaf sizes, feature subsampling, and bootstrap sampling using fivefold cross‐validation, with the explained variance (R 2) on held‐out folds as the objective. After selecting the configuration with the highest validation performance, we refitted the forest on the full dataset and quantified feature contributions using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, a method to interpret the impact of each predictor on the model output, derived for the tree ensembles.

Results

OSA severity was classified as normal (< 5 events/h), mild (5–15), moderate (15–30) and severe (≥ 30). The proportion of women declined from 88.9% in the normal group to 34.4% in the severe OSA group (p = 0.012), while BMI increased from 23.1 ± 2.3 to 27.4 ± 4.8 kg/m2 (p = 0.005, Table 1). Respiratory parameters worsened with increasing severity: the ODI increased from 2.9 ± 2.8 to 55.4 ± 22.8 events/h (p < 0.001), CT90 from 0.1% ± 0.1% to 8.1% ± 11.2% (p = 0.009), and the O2 saturation nadir decreased from 89.9% ± 2.3% to 82.7% ± 5.9% (p < 0.001). Sleep architecture shifted towards lighter, more fragmented patterns, with a higher N1 proportion (26.2% ± 15.0% vs. 48.6% ± 17.2%, p < 0.001), lower N2 (57.9% ± 17.4% vs. 38.8% ± 15.3%, p < 0.001) and a higher total arousal index (25.9 ± 17.1 vs. 53.0 ± 16.2 events/h, p < 0.001). Stage N3 was uncommon across all groups and the REM proportion remained largely stable.

Caloric SPV demonstrated a clear inverse relationship with OSA severity markers (Figure 1). AHI showed the most robust correlation (r = −0.295, p = 0.007), followed by ODI (r = −0.261, p = 0.018) and CT90 (r = −0.254, p = 0.021). Although moderate in magnitude, these correlations were consistent across different hypoxemia metrics, suggesting a genuine biological relationship rather than a spurious association. In contrast, sleep efficiency was not associated with SPV (r = 0.004, p = 0.969). Similarly, snoring index (r = −0.086, p = 0.443), pulse rate (r = −0.083, p = 0.461) and O2 saturation nadir (r = 0.156, p = 0.163) failed to predict vestibular responses. Sleep‐stage proportions (N1, N2, N3 and REM) showed no evidence of association with SPV (all p > 0.10). In unadjusted comparisons, women exhibited higher SPV than men (p = 0.027). However, men were disproportionately represented in the severe OSA category, making disease severity a plausible confounder of this association.

The correlation matrix (Figure 2) revealed substantial collinearity amongst PSG parameters, providing a crucial context for interpreting the regression results. Hypoxemia‐related measures were highly intercorrelated: AHI with ODI (r = 0.92), AHI with total arousal index (r = 0.76) and CT90 with oxygen saturation nadir (r = −0.77). This clustering suggests that these parameters reflect overlapping aspects of OSA pathophysiology, complicating efforts to isolate their independent effects. Sleep architecture parameters exhibited distinct correlation clusters: Stage N1 percentage was strongly positively correlated with AHI (r = 0.65) and negatively with sleep efficiency (r = −0.21), whereas N2 showed the opposite pattern (r = −0.59, AHI, r = 0.24 with sleep efficiency). These relationships confirm that increasing OSA severity systematically disrupts normal sleep architecture, shifting the balance toward lighter and more fragmented sleep.

Given the strong intercorrelations amongst hypoxemia metrics and the observed sex–severity imbalance, SPV was modelled using ordinary least squares with a prespecified variable set. The backward stepwise selection process systematically eliminated 13 of the 15 initial predictors, reducing the model AIC from 811.5 to 791.2 (Table 2). The final parsimonious model retained only the AHI and sex. Each 10‐event/h increase in AHI predicted a 3.16°/s reduction in SPV (95% CI: −6.05 to −0.28°/s, p = 0.032). The sex coefficient was retained by the AIC criterion due to its contribution to model parsimony, yet it did not reach statistical significance (β = −11.27°/s for males, 95% CI: −24.95 to 2.41, p = 0.105). Consequently, the inclusion of sex in the final model should be interpreted as an exploratory finding rather than definitive evidence of a sex‐specific effect. The overall model explained 11.7% of the SPV variance (adjusted R 2 = 0.095, F‐test, p = 0.007), indicating that although OSA severity significantly predicted vestibular function, most variance remained unexplained, likely reflecting the multifactorial nature of vestibular physiology.

In an exploratory analysis to characterise the feature importance across the 15‐variable set, SHAP contributions were examined in a conservatively tuned random forest model (R 2 = 0.065). In the SHAP summary plot (Figure 3), mean absolute SHAP ranked the hypoxemia‐domain metrics highest: ODI (2.18), AHI (2.11), and CT90 (2.10), followed by BMI (1.35). At the individual level, higher AHI and ODI were generally associated with negative SHAP values, indicating contributions toward a lower predicted SPV. Features reflecting sleep fragmentation and architecture, as well as the snoring index, pulse rate, and age, contributed minimally. Accordingly, interpretation focused on the hypoxemia domain level, rather than attributing primacy to any single measure.

Discussion

This study identified nocturnal hypoxemia as the primary polysomnographic correlate of lower caloric SPV in OSA. Results converged across methods: covariate‐adjusted linear modelling and an explainable ensemble approach both pointed to hypoxemia‐domain metrics—particularly ODI, AHI and CT90 while indices of sleep fragmentation and architecture contributed minimally. In the random forest analysis, higher AHI and ODI values were predominantly associated with negative SHAP values, consistent with linear estimates and supporting a domain‐level interpretation rather than emphasising any single metric.

These findings extend prior reports linking OSA with a higher prevalence of abnormal caloric responses by shifting from a binary perspective to a quantitative, continuous relationship. Whereas earlier studies primarily contrasted “normal versus abnormal” test outcomes [9, 10, 24], our results show that increasing hypoxemia burden corresponds with progressively lower caloric SPV, even within ranges that may not cross traditional abnormality thresholds. While statistically significant, the modest magnitude of these correlations (R = −0.295) and the low model R 2 suggest that OSA‐related hypoxemia is a contributing factor rather than the sole determinant of vestibular function. Other unmeasured variables (e.g., genetic susceptibility, vascular reserve) likely play significant roles. Considering that bilateral vestibular hypofunction is often defined as an SPV < 20°/s [26] the present data imply that OSA may shift vestibular performance downward in otherwise asymptomatic adults, potentially narrowing the physiological reserve and making patients more vulnerable to dizziness or imbalance when additional stressors are present.

Clinically, these findings generate the hypothesis that prioritizing desaturation control might help preserve or improve vestibular function in patients with OSA. We hypothesise that optimising positive airway pressure (PAP) settings to minimise both the ODI and CT90 could be a viable strategy, though this requires validation through longitudinal interventional studies. As patients with similar AHI can differ markedly in their hypoxemia profiles, identifying hypoxemia‐heavy phenotypes might help target those with greater vestibular risk. Although supplemental oxygen is not a first‐line therapy for OSA, our data suggest that residual desaturation despite PAP could be a relevant marker for tracking and treating OSA, ideally within prospective protocols that evaluate vestibular outcomes. From a screening standpoint, our findings do not mandate universal vestibular testing in OSA. However, they justify a low threshold for vestibular evaluation in patients with imbalance, falls, or oscillopsia, particularly when hypoxemia metrics are elevated.

Several observations merit consideration. First, sleep fragmentation measures (arousal, sleep efficiency and snoring index) and stage composition contributed less than hypoxemia. This does not exclude the role of cortical arousal or autonomic instability, but suggests that oxygenation is the dominant pathway for the caloric outcome studied here. Second, the sex variable contributed minimally in the nonlinear model, echoing weak signals in the linear analyses; any sex‐specific differences should be considered hypothesis‐generating until validated in larger cohorts. Third, Stage N3 had a negligible impact, potentially reflecting limited interindividual variance, age‐related reductions in deep sleep, or simply that the N3 proportion is not a proximal driver of peripheral vestibular responsiveness.

This study represents an initial step in quantifying the hypoxemia‐vestibular link, but several limitations must be considered. First, the absence of a non‐OSA control group restricts our ability to draw causal inferences and determine whether the observed correlations represent a deviation from a strictly healthy baseline. Second, selection bias is inherent to the retrospective design, as the cohort included only patients clinically referred for both PSG and vestibular testing. This implies the sample may be enriched for individuals with more severe symptoms or comorbidities compared to the general OSA population. Third, the model's explained variance was limited (cross‐validated R 2 = 0.065), indicating that sleep‐related factors account for only a small fraction of the total variance in vestibular responsiveness. This low explained variance was expected given the small sample size (n = 82) relative to the predictor set and underscores that OSA is a contributing factor within a complex, multifactorial physiological system rather than the sole determinant. Fourth, variability in PSG and caloric measurements, as well as variable intervals between the two tests, could attenuate or dilute the observed relationships. Finally, multicollinearity amongst hypoxemia metrics complicates the attribution of unique effects and the random forest was conservatively tuned for stability in a small dataset, potentially underestimating nonlinear interactions. These caveats support a domain‐level interpretation, with differences in individual metrics being considered exploratory.

Future studies should include larger cohorts and adopt longitudinal treatment‐responsive designs to test whether reductions in hypoxemia precede improvements in SPV. Phenotyping using REM‐predominant hypoxemia, O2 saturation nadir thresholds, or vascular comorbidities may clarify effect modifiers. Incorporating perfusion‐sensitive inner ear imaging, otoacoustic measures, or multimodal vestibular testing (e.g., video head impulse test and vestibular‐evoked myogenic potentials) could further localise lesion sites and mechanisms.

In conclusion, hypoxemia burden is the critical sleep‐related correlate of lower caloric SPV in patients with OSA. Clinicians should pay close attention to desaturation metrics when assessing vestibular risk and planning therapy, in terms of both frequency and cumulative duration.
