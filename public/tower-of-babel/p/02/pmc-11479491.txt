Tuberculosis in infertility and in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer
Authors: Xiaoyan Gai, Hongbin Chi, Rong Li, Yongchang Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB) is a prominent infectious disease globally that imposes a substantial health burden. Genital TB (GTB), an extrapulmonary manifestation, leads to complications such as tubal adhesions, blockage, and diminished ovarian function, culminating in infertility, and is recognized as a prevalent cause of infertility in nations with high-burden TB. In regions with low TB rates, infertility and active TB during pregnancy have been reported to be most common among female immigrants from countries with high-burden TB. In the context of TB, pregnant women often exhibit exacerbated symptoms after in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer (IVF-ET), heightening the risk of dissemination. Miliary pulmonary TB and tuberculous meningitis pose a serious threat to maternal and fetal health. This article integrates recent epidemiological data and clinical research findings, delineating the impact of TB on infertility and assisted reproduction and particularly focusing on the diagnosis and treatment of GTB, underscored by the imperative of TB screening before IVF-ET. Our objective is to increase awareness among respiratory and reproductive health professionals, promoting multidisciplinary management to enhance clinical vigilance. This approach seeks to provide patients with judicious reproductive plans and scientifically rigorous pregnancy management, thereby mitigating adverse pregnancy outcomes related to TB activity.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M.tb), is a significant cause of infertility, particularly in areas with high TB prevalence.[123] This issue drives a considerable amount of medical care with assisted reproductive technologies like in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer (IVF-ET). Therefore, TB screening and treatment before IVF-ET are crucial. Reactivation of TB during pregnancy post-IVF-ET poses serious risks to maternal and fetal health. This review delves into genital TB (GTB) management, the impact of GTB and latent TB infection (LTBI) on pregnancy outcomes, and the importance of rigorous TB screening before IVF-ET. By highlighting the necessity of preventing TB reactivation, this article underscores the need for systematic TB screening in IVF-ET preparation, aiming to enhance pregnancy outcomes and ensure safer fertility process in TB-prevalent regions.

Epidemiology

In 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that TB occurred in 10.6 million people globally.[1] TB can be transmitted in humans through various pathways, with the most common mode of transmission being the inhalation of aerosolized droplets containing M.tb, which are released when patients with active pulmonary TB cough or sneeze. Other modes of transmission include the consumption of unpasteurized dairy products infected with M.tb, congenital transmission, sexual transmission, accidental inoculation, and vaccine administration. While pulmonary TB is considered the most prevalent form, extrapulmonary TB accounts for 10–45%[2] and usually affects the lymph nodes, urogenital organs, and renal and skeletal systems,[3] as shown in Figure 1.

Female GTB, a common cause of infertility, is more prevalently among individuals aged 20–40 years[3456789] and is detected in approximately 5–10% of infertile women worldwide.[10] Among the causes of infertility, GTB accounts for approximately 20%,[11] with some patients with GTB presenting infertility as their sole clinical manifestation.[12131415] GTB has been reported to be less prevalent in developed countries[4] and is more frequently observed in developing countries. For instance, the incidence of TB among infertile women has been estimated to be approximately 28.4% in China,[12] as high as 50% in India,[5] and approximately 1% in the United States.[11,16] Nonetheless, with the increase in immigration, the incidence of GTB has recently increased in developed countries.[17]

Pathogenesis

GTB affects every part of the female genital tract, including the fallopian tubes (90–100%), uterus (50–80%), ovaries (20–30%), and vagina/external genitalia (1%).[18] The primary cause of GTB-induced infertility lies in structural damage to the fallopian tubes (fibrosis, adhesions, luminal obstruction), endometrium (intrauterine adhesions), and ovaries (tubo-ovarian cysts), although non-structural damage can also contribute to infertility.[13,15,18,19] The increased release of inflammatory cytokines in GTB may result in diminished endometrial receptivity and impaired implantation. Additionally, GTB involvement in the ovaries may reduce the ovarian reserve[7] and lead to ovulatory dysfunction or endocrine disruption in some individuals.[20,21]

Diagnosis of GTB

The diagnosis of female GTB relies on the comprehensive application of various examinations, including biopsy exam, pathology, microbiology, and molecular biology. Hysterosalpingography (HSG), an imaging method, aids in the identification of GTB by revealing abnormalities such as uterine cavity deformation and fallopian tube blockage. It is usually the first investigation for tubal patency, as it is easily available even at small centres and remote places, very economical, and can be performed without anaesthesia. Approximately 40–50% of GTB patients exhibit findings like tubal blockage and intrauterine adhesions.[21,22] A study involving 87 women with GTB confirmed the efficacy of HSG by revealing abnormalities such as fallopian tube blockages and uterine cavity deformations in 38 (43.68%) patients. Importantly, no disease flare-ups were reported post-HSG, suggesting its safety and utility in diagnosing GTB-related infertility.[22] Chest radiography may also indicate possible GTB if signs of TB such as fibrotic nodules and calcifications are present. The most reliable diagnosis is achieved through laparoscopy combined with hysteroscopy,[9] which can show definite (e.g., tubercles, caseous nodules, beaded tubes) or probable (hydrosalpinx, peritubal and/or periovarian adhesions, etc.) findings of GTB lesions. Histopathological examination of biopsy samples can uncover characteristic granulomatous lesions, with sensitivity estimates ranging from 9% to 65%.[23] The combination of acid-fast staining, culture, and molecular biology methods, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and GeneXpert (Cepheid, CA, USA), is utilized for better detection.[24] While acid-fast bacilli (AFB) microscopy offers rapid and cost-effective TB diagnosis, its low sensitivity (1–22%) due to the need for a high bacterial load limits its effectiveness in paucibacillary conditions like GTB. Bacterial culture, the gold standard for diagnosis, commonly takes up to 8 weeks to yield results, albeit with slightly better sensitivity (7–42%) and nearly 100% specificity.[23] The Xpert assay leverages PCR technology for a quicker, more sensitive, and specific diagnosis of GTB, demonstrating variable sensitivity between 7% and 50% on biopsy specimens. Notably, it achieves a near-100% sensitivity for certain applications, and concurrently facilitates the detection of rifampicin resistance [Figure 2].[23,25]

The majority of patients with GTB exhibit subtle symptoms and lack specific clinical signs, making the clinical diagnosis of GTB challenging. Furthermore, traditional diagnostic methods, including microbiological culture, histopathology, diagnostic curettage, and laparoscopic examination, are prone to misdiagnosis, with a clinical diagnostic rate of approximately 7–19%.[5] Additionally, because of the paucibacillary characteristic of GTB specimens, low detection sensitivity, and associated risks of laparoscopy and hysteroscopy, the confirmed diagnosis rate remains relatively low. Hence, research on more precise biomarkers of M.tb infection is essential to improve the diagnosis of GTB.[10]

Treatment and Fertility Management of GTB

In cases where GTB is confirmed, the WHO advocates for a regimen of combined anti-TB medication over a period of 6 to 12 months.[6,26] The standard treatment for drug-sensitive active pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB involves a 2-month phase of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol (HRZE), followed by a continuation phase of isoniazid and rifampicin (HR) for 4 months. It is critical to monitor adverse events, such as hepatitis, skin reactions, gastrointestinal discomfort, and hematological responses, and adjust the treatment protocol as necessary.

Assisted reproductive technology may be helpful for patients who are facing fertility challenges after anti-TB treatment, whereas less invasive methods such as ovulation induction and timed intercourse may be applied as options to those without structural damage. For infertility due to tubal blockage, IVF-ET is the preferred approach.[27] However, even after IVF-ET, the impact of endometrial TB on fertility remains a concern, resulting in less favorable outcomes.[3,28] A Chinese study reported the pregnancy outcomes after IVF/intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI)-ET in 155 patients with GTB who had received successful anti-TB treatment, and the results showed that the pregnancy outcomes in patients with tubal TB showed no difference as compared to non-TB controls, while the patients with endometrium TB had reduced fertilization rates (61.5% vs. 68.8%, P <0.05), implantation rates (29.8% vs. 48.6%, P <0.05) and cumulative pregnancy rates (56.3% vs. 80.5%, P <0.05) as compared to non-TB patients.[28,29] Li et al[3] compared the pregnancy outcomes after IVF-ET between 113 patients with endometrial TB after successful anti-TB therapy and 452 patients of non-TB causes, and they found similar cumulative clinical pregnancy rates between the groups (64.6% vs. 65.1%, P = 0.89), but the cumulative live birth rates (40.7% vs. 52.7%, P <0.001) were significantly lower and the spontaneous abortion rates (37.0% vs. 13.2%, P <0.05) significantly higher in the TB group. Recent studies showed that the post-treatment pregnancy rate remained lower in TB patients with structural damage compared to non-TB patients.[27] The overall success rate of these methods in achieving pregnancy has been reported to be 60.6%.[27,28] Nonetheless, the impact of anti-TB therapy on pregnancy outcomes in patients without structural damage remains unclear.[27] It is critical to rule out GTB before proceeding with IVF for infertility, in order to prevent TB transmission during pregnancy, thereby ensuring better outcomes.[18]

Screening for TB before IVF-ET

IVF-ET is a commonly used procedure for infertility. For patients from areas with a high TB burden and for HIV-positive or immunodeficient patients, screening for TB before IVF-ET is critical, because performing IVF-ET without TB screening may lead to TB issues during pregnancy, which can have a negative impact on outcomes.[16] Even in low-risk countries, attention to immigrants from TB-endemic regions is necessary. Signs of TB, such as infertility due to tubal and uterine factors, declining ovarian reserve, a history of previous pulmonary TB, and chest imaging findings suggestive of TB lesions, should prompt the consideration of TB as a possible cause of infertility.[293031] It should be noted that TB screening is not just for symptomatic cases but also for all patients with these risk factors.[32,33]

For active TB cases, early anti-TB therapy is essential before IVF-ET. Screening for GTB involves biopsy, TB culture, PCR of biopsy tissue, and additionally, tuberculin skin test (TST) and/or interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA). The screening process focuses on detecting active pulmonary TB and GTB [Figure 3].[30] Diagnosing pulmonary TB relies on chest imaging, and tests such as microscopy, culture, and PCR are conducted if sputum is available.[1] Diagnosing GTB poses challenges because of the limited diagnostic precision. Reproductive TB, particularly when the fallopian tubes are unobstructed, is elusive and may be misidentified as unexplained infertility.[7,34]

Preventive anti-TB Treatment for LTBI before IVF-ET

LTBI is a condition in which the body exhibits a sustained immune response to M.tb, but active TB is not evident.[35,36] Patients with LTBI exhibit increased non-specific T-cell activation, which is considered a biomarker of progression from LTBI to active TB.[37] It is of clinical significance to identify high-risk populations with LTBI, including individuals with HIV, contacts of adult and pediatric pulmonary TB cases, patients starting tumor necrosis factor therapy, as well as other high-risk groups.

To date, there has been no definitive gold standard for diagnosing LTBI;[38,39] however, the WHO has endorsed the use of the TST and IGRA to screen LTBI. While the TST is cost-effective, it may generate false positives in individuals vaccinated with Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG). Therefore, the IGRA is recommended for LTBI screening in populations with a high BCG vaccination rate.[40] Positivity in both the IGRA and TST aids in predicting the progression from TB exposure to active TB.[41] A meta-analysis showed that the IGRA outperformed the TST in predicting TB activation, particularly in countries with low TB incidence (<100/100,000 population).[42] While the TST and IGRA may not fully capture the latent status of TB, current guidelines advocate the use of either the TST or IGRA, or a combination of both, for LTBI screening.[4344] In addition, some scholars also propose the performance of concurrent chest radiography for high-risk patients.[454647] A recent meta-analysis pointed out that the annual rate of conversion to microbiologically positive active TB was 1.0% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.3–1.8%) in patients with non-active TB lesions on chest radiography and without anti-TB treatment, highlighting the need to consider the risk of radiological-related conversion.[43]

Preventive anti-TB therapy can reduce the risk of LTBI progression to active TB in high-risk populations.[48,49] The WHO has proposed the “End TB Strategy,” emphasizing recognition and treatment of LTBI.[50] The WHO also released guidelines for screening and treating LTBI in 2015[51] and updated them in 2018.[36] The guidelines recommend a 3-month course of preventive anti-TB treatment with isoniazid and rifapentine (3HP regimen) or isoniazid and rifampicin (3RH regimen) for patients with LTBI in countries with high TB burden, especially those in the aforementioned high-risk groups.[48,51] A previous study showed that a 3-month course of 12-dose once-weekly isoniazid (H, 900 mg) plus rifapentine (P, 900 mg) (3HP) was safe for pregnant patients with LTBI who had a history of TB exposure.[52] Nevertheless, there is still debate regarding whether preventive anti-TB therapy is necessary for patients with LTBI before IVF-ET.[53,54] Some scholars advise that female infertile individuals with LTBI from TB-endemic regions or with high-risk factors, such as HIV positivity and immunodeficiency, should undergo preventive anti-TB therapy before IVF-ET.[55] However, this is currently controversial, as preventive anti-TB treatment may delay the progress of assisted reproduction. Moreover, considering the potential adverse effects of anti-TB drugs, particularly on the fetus, the need for preventive anti-TB treatment demands additional evidence obtained using a rigorous evidence-based approach. Additionally, both LTBI guidelines and infertility diagnosis and treatment guidelines do not explicitly state the necessity of preventive anti-TB therapy for LTBI before IVF-ET.[51]

By using a positive blood IGRA result as an indicator of LTBI, a Chinese retrospective study explored the impact of LTBI on pregnancy outcomes in infertile women. This study, which included 3066 cycles of intrauterine insemination, revealed that patients with LTBI had lower rates of biochemical pregnancy (12.9% vs. 17.7%, P = 0.068), clinical pregnancy (10.8% vs. 15.1%, P = 0.082), and live birth (8.1% vs. 12.1%, P = 0.076) than those without LTBI, although the differences were not statistically significant.[56] Another retrospective analysis of 628 infertile women who underwent IVF-ET, including 200 IGRA-positive and 406 IGRA-negative participants, indicated a significantly lower clinical pregnancy rate in the LTBI group (40.54% vs. 49.51%, P = 0.031), with no significant difference in live birth or miscarriage rates between the two groups.[57] A larger Chinese retrospective investigation involving 6283 female infertile patients who underwent IVF/ICSI-ET found no statistically significant differences in pregnancy and neonatal outcomes between the TST-positive group (n = 1704) and the TST-negative group (n = 4243) (P >0.05).[58]

There is limited prospective research on the relationship between LTBI and pregnancy outcomes following IVF-ET. In a small-scale prospective cohort study in the United States, LTBI was identified in 25 (7.7%) out of 323 infertile women using the IGRA. This study revealed that the rates of recurrent spontaneous abortion (28% vs. 7%, P = 0.003) and Asherman’s syndrome (8.0% vs. 0.3%, P <0.001) were significantly higher in women with LTBI than in those without LTBI.[33] A prospective study in India compared 431 infertile women with latent GTB against 453 infertile women without, and found that the former had notably lower anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels (2.0 ng/mL vs. 2.8 ng/mL, P = 0.01) and antral follicle counts (AFC) (7 vs. 8, P <0.001). After undergoing anti-TB treatment (ATT), patients with latent GTB exhibited a higher pregnancy rate compared to those without latent GTB (51.6% vs. 40.5%, P = 0.01). This study supports the notion that anti-TB treatment can improve ovarian reserve in infertile patients with latent GTB.[7] A previous retrospective cohort study at our center enrolled 14,254 infertile patients for IVF-ET. Among them, 1487 (10.4%) showed evidence of inactive TB lesions on chest radiography, with 81.8% (1239/1487) having never received anti-TB therapy. A follow-up on pregnancy outcomes revealed that the rates of pregnancy (31.7% vs. 38.1%, P <0.001) and live birth (23.8% vs. 30.6%, P <0.05) were significantly lower in the group with untreated inactive pulmonary TB than in the non-TB group. Multivariable analysis showed that untreated inactive pulmonary TB on chest radiography was a significant factor contributing to an increased miscarriage rate (odds ratio [OR], 4.19; 95% CI, 1.69–10.39; P = 0.002) and decreased live birth rate (OR, 0.45; 95% CI, 0.24–0.83; P = 0.011) in the unexplained infertility subgroup.[30] Building upon these findings, we initiated a prospective cohort study which enrolled infertile women with inactive pulmonary TB, as confirmed by chest radiography, who had not received anti-TB treatment.[59] The LTBI cohort was stratified based on IGRA results, revealing a positivity rate of 32.9% (unpublished data), which is markedly higher than the previously reported average rate of 13–20% in rural China.[60] Follow-ups for the assessment of pregnancy outcomes in groups with and without LTBI undergoing IVF-ET are ongoing.[59] This research aims to provide further evidence regarding the necessity of preventive anti-TB treatment for this specific population.

TB Reactivation during Pregnancy

Potential reactivation of TB must be considered when patients experience persistent cough (>2 weeks), fever, night sweats, and weight loss during pregnancy.[61,62] Active TB during pregnancy poses significant risks and leads to adverse outcomes such as miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth, low birth weight, fetal distress, neonatal TB, and perinatal death.[17,63,64] The prevalence of TB during pregnancy is notably higher in low- to middle-income countries.[58656667] Recent surveys in high-income countries such as the United Kingdom and Sweden revealed that active TB cases during pregnancy mostly occurred in immigrant women from countries with high TB burden.[25,68]

During pregnancy, changes in the immune system lead to a two- to three-fold increase in the risk of TB infection. In pregnant women, TB often presents a challenge due to the absence of specific symptoms. For instance, weight loss due to TB may be masked by normal abdominal weight gain, and fatigue may be perceived as a common pregnancy-related occurrence. Worries of radiation exposure with chest imaging commonly lead to the rejection or postponement of radiological examinations. TB is usually confirmed postpartum, often after a latency period of up to six months.[61,62,69]

With the widespread use of IVF-ET for infertility, reported active TB cases during pregnancy, mainly as miliary pulmonary TB and tuberculous meningitis, has increased over recent years [Figure 4].[55,70] At the reproductive center of Peking Universtiy Third Hospital, seven active TB cases during pregnancy were identified among 88,700 IVF-ET cycles performed for infertility from 2012 to 2017. These cases were characterized by severe conditions, mainly acute hematogenous disseminated pulmonary TB (7/7) and tuberculous meningitis (2/7). Chest radiography prior to IVF-ET revealed old TB lesions in six patients, all of whom did not receive previous anti-TB treatment. Salpingography showed unilateral or bilateral tubal obstruction in all seven patients. Following the recurrence of TB during pregnancy, all seven patients underwent anti-TB treatment, leading to the clinical recovery of the mothers. Among them, four experienced spontaneous miscarriage, whereas three opted for induced abortion to terminate their pregnancies.[70] This research indicates that individuals with “old” untreated TB lesions in the chest and those with concurrent tubal obstruction are highly susceptible to TB-induced infertility. These individuals are categorized to be at a high risk for TB reactivation during pregnancy after IVF-ET.[70] A recent summary of 23 miliary pulmonary TB cases during pregnancy, including 12 infertile patients who developed miliary TB after IVF-ET,[55] has emphasized the importance of TB screening for couples seeking infertility treatment with IVF-ET in TB-endemic regions.[71]

Treatment of TB during Pregnancy

When active TB co-occurs with pregnancy, timely anti-TB therapy should be initiated for improved recovery, reduced mortality, and better well-being of both the mother and the baby. Safety should be prioritized when choosing medications, which are classified by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) into five categories. Category B includes anti-TB drugs such as ethambutol and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid. Category C includes various anti-TB medications such as isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ofloxacin/levofloxacin/moxifloxacin, para-aminosalicylic acid, prothionamide, cycloserine, clofazimine, clarithromycin, and linezolid. Category D includes streptomycin, kanamycin, and amikacin. Currently, no drugs for TB fall under Category X. Over the past few decades, no significant evidence has indicated any teratogenic effects of Category B and C anti-TB drugs in pregnant women and fetuses.[72,73] For better guidance, the FDA has updated the regulations since 2015.[74]

The standard short-course regimen 2HRZE/4HR, which means isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide daily for two months, followed by four months of isoniazid and rifampicin, is recommended for drug-sensitive TB. The intensive phase involves daily administration of isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol for two months, followed by the continuation phase with isoniazid and rifampin for four months.[63,74] Combining isoniazid with rifapentine during pregnancy is safe for the fetus.[75] For drug-resistant TB during pregnancy, multi-drug regimens are necessary, and no adverse effects on the fetus have been reported for fluoroquinolones and bedaquiline.[76]

Conclusion

In summary, TB raises a prevalent and considerable concern among individuals dealing with infertility, with GTB being a particularly common culprit. Moreover, evidence regarding the necessity of preventive anti-TB treatment for LTBI remains limited. Rigorous TB screening before IVF-ET is critical, especially for individuals from TB-endemic regions or those at a high risk for TB. Offering standardized anti-TB treatment to patients with active TB is essential not only to optimize fertility but also to prevent TB recurrence during pregnancy, ensuring the health and well-being of both the mother and the child. Future research on TB biomarkers is highly desirable in order to expedite and improve the diagnostic processes.
