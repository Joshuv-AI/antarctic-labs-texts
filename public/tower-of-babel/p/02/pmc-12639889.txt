Invasive fungal sinusitis by Scedosporium boydii in an immunocompromised patient. A case report
Authors: Carla Rey-Suarez, Paula Caramazana-Lopez, Iván Fernández-Castro, Helena Esteban-Cartelle, Daniel Navarro-de la Cruz, Elena Losada-Arias
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvasive fungal sinusitis by Scedosporium spp. is a rare but severe infection in immunocompromised patients. We report an 80-year-old woman with poorly controlled diabetes and chronic corticosteroid use who presented with headache, hearing loss, and facial paralysis. She was ultimately diagnosed with sphenoid sinusitis due to Scedosporium boydii. Management required surgical debridement and prolonged antifungal therapy complicated by drug-induced liver injury. Early diagnosis and multidisciplinary treatment are crucial to improve prognosis in these complex infections.Keywords: Scedosporium spp, Immunocompromised host, Invasive fungal sinusitis, Treatment

Introduction

Fungal sinusitis, particularly in its invasive forms, is a rare but life-threatening condition that primarily affects immunocompromised individuals. Diagnosis and treatment are challenging due to nonspecific symptoms and risk of severe complications like intracranial invasion.

Scedosporium spp. is a widely distributed fungal genus with significant pathogenic potential. Its primary route of entry is usually through traumatic skin wounds. Although this infection is more common in immunosuppressed patients, it can also affect individuals without underlying immunosuppression. Furthermore, its resistance to multiple antifungal agents makes management even more challenging. This case report describes an 80-year-old immunocompromised woman with invasive fungal sinusitis caused by Scedosporium spp., highlighting the complexity of diagnosis, therapeutic decision-making, and the management of antifungal-related toxicity.

Case presentation

The authors describe the case of an 80-year-old-woman with history of dyslipidemia, poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus (HbA1c 10.3 %), hypertension, hyperuricemia, and rheumatoid arthritis, was on chronic treatment with prednisone (2mg/day), allopurinol, omeprazole, simvastatin/ezetimibe, dapagliflozin/metformin and insulin.

Approximately three months prior to admission, she was admitted for evaluation of left hemicranial pain. Suspecting giant cell arteritis (GCA) due to the patient's age, clinical signs and elevated inflammatory markers, and consequently, high-dose corticosteroids were started. Subsequently, a temporal artery biopsy and a positron emission tomography (PET) scan were performed; however, they were inconclusive. She improved slightly and was discharged on a tapering regimen of corticosteroids and analgesics, without patient follow-up.

By day 0, the patient's symptoms had worsened, with persistent hemicrania and new-onset hearing loss. Physical examination showed peripheral facial paralysis. Otoscopy revealed retrotympanic yellow material in right ear and otorrhea with edema in the left one. Laboratory analysis revealed elevated transaminases and increased C-reactive protein (CRP) levels. Given the patient's clinical history, including long-term corticosteroid treatment, with higher doses in recent months and poorly controlled diabetes, a fungal etiology was suspected. Consequently, it was decided to complete the evaluation with a determination of β-D-glucan levels and the performance of a new cranial computed tomography (CT) scan.

On days 2 and 3, the β-D-glucan test showed positive results, with values of 8.49 and 9.12 pg/mL, respectively, both exceeding the reference threshold of 7 pg/m. In addition, the CT scan revealed soft tissue density within the sinus (Image 1). A surgical intervention was performed with both diagnostic and therapeutic intent (day 3). It revealed yellowish lumpy material suggestive of sinus mycetoma/aspergilloma, which was removed. Microbiological analysis suggested filamentous fungi, leading to a differential diagnosis between Aspergillus spp. and other less common filamentous fungi (Image 2).

Empirical antifungal treatment was initiated with voriconazole, including a loading dose of 6 mg/kg/12 hours, followed by a maintenance dose of 4 mg/kg/12 hours, and amphotericin B at 5 mg/kg/day. The patient's chronic statin therapy was discontinued due to the high risk of hepatotoxicity, in context of concomitant antifungal treatment that could elevate liver toxicity.

On the 8th day, the fungal isolate was fully characterized as Scedosporium boydii, establishing the final diagnosis. Growth had been observed on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar supplemented with chloramphenicol after incubation at 30 °C under aerobic conditions. Preliminary identification was made through its macroscopic and microscopic features, and definitive identification was achieved through Sanger sequencing of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of the ribosomal DNA, amplified using the universal fungal primers ITS1 and ITS4 [1]. PCR products were purified and sequenced using the GenomeLab GeXP Genetic Analysis System (Beckman Coulter Inc., Fullerton, CA, USA).

Antifungal susceptibility testing reported a MIC of 8 μg/mL for amphotericin B and 0.25 μg/mL for voriconazole. Although terbinafine MIC values were not available, its addition to the regimen was supported by literature describing potential synergistic effect, prompting the initiation of oral terbinafine (250 mg every 12 hours) alongside the existing antifungal therapy. Nevertheless, it must be emphasized that despite the MIC values obtained for the isolated strain, no validated thresholds currently exist to define susceptibility or resistance for the antifungals tested. Concomitantly, considering the patient's clinical evolution, a diagnosis of giant cell arteritis (GCA) was ruled out, and corticosteroid tapering was subsequently initiated, allowing for coordinated management of both the fungal infection and the underlying inflammatory condition.

On day 13, after the initiation of antifungal therapy, laboratory tests revealed a progressive elevation of liver enzymes, with a cholestatic pattern and increased inflammatory markers, including procalcitonin and C-reactive protein (CRP). Although the patient's clinical condition had improved, the temporal pattern of enzyme elevation, together with the concomitant use of several antifungal agents with recognized hepatotoxic potential, strongly suggested the onset of drug-induced liver injury (DILI). In this context, the application of the CIOMS/RUCAM scale yielded 9 points, indicating a highly probable association with drug-induced hepatotoxicity and supporting the likelihood of drug-induced liver failure.

Consequently, a thorough review of all medications administered to the patient was conducted, leading to the discontinuation of potentially hepatotoxic drugs that were not deemed essential. Regarding antifungal therapy, based on antifungal susceptibility testing and a review of the literature, amphotericin B and terbinafine were discontinued, due to more limited evidence supporting their efficacy. Voriconazole dosing was adjusted according to plasma levels with a temporary interruption between days 16 and 20 due to levels exceeding the therapeutic range.

During this period, the patient remained clinically stable, without signs of deterioration, and liver enzyme levels gradually declined (Fig. 1). Otorhinolaryngology evaluations during hospitalization showed progressive clinical improvement.

On day 38, the patient was discharged with close monitoring of voriconazole levels and hepatic function. At this moment, the oral dose of voriconazole was 50 mg every 12 hours, with subsequent adjustments based on blood drug levels and liver enzyme activity. This was followed up in outpatient clinics biweekly during the first month, and subsequently monthly. The patient's overall condition improved during the following months.

By February 2025, there was notable improvement in peripheral facial paralysis, as well as in hearing function. On day 202, after sustained clinical, radiological, and laboratory improvement (Image 3 and Fig. 1), voriconazole was stopped following seven months of treatment.

Discussion

Invasive fungal sinusitis is an uncommon but potentially life-threatening clinical entity. This case illustrates an atypical presentation of a Scedosporium boydii infection in an elderly patient with multiple comorbidities, requiring several therapeutic adjustments.

In a population where the number of immunocompromised individuals is steadily increasing, it is vital to maintain awareness of fungal infections. Although often overlooked in routine clinical practice, fungal pathogens must be considered when evaluating elderly or immunocompromised patients, particularly in cases with atypical clinical presentations.

While fungi such as Candida sp. and Aspergillus sp. are the most commonly associated with fungal diseases, hyalohyphomycoses constitute a heterogeneous group of opportunistic molds, including Fusarium, Scedosporium, Acremonium, Scopulariopsis, Purpureocillium, and Paecilomyces, whose incidence is increasingly recognized. These ubiquitous environmental organisms become critically important in immunocompromised patients due to their ability to cause invasive and disseminated infections [2].

The diagnosis of these infections requires specific techniques that are typically reserved for this type of patients. Clinical suspicion should arise in the presence of persistent fever unresponsive to antibiotics, localized pain, neurological deficits, or signs of tissue necrosis. Radiological findings suggestive of deep tissue involvement, and the detection of fungal biomarkers such as β-D-glucan or galactomannan, may aid in early diagnosis. Prompt recognition is essential given the high morbidity and mortality associated with delayed treatment [3].

Regarding serological markers, aside from broad-spectrum (pan-fungal) markers like β-D-glucan or galactomannan, there are no pathogen-specific markers available; however, ongoing research involving specific antigens and monoclonal antibodies holds promise. Diagnostic evaluation includes imaging studies, laboratory testing, and tissue biopsy with the application of various staining methods. Although in vitro growth of Scedosporium spp. species may aid in guiding the diagnostic process, culture alone is insufficient for definitive identification, necessitating the use of molecular biology techniques [4].

Therapeutic management of these infections remains challenging due to limited evidence and variable in vitro antifungal susceptibility. Although terbinafine is generally considered an alternative line of treatment, several studies have reported the efficacy of its concomitant use with other antifungals. Consequently, the combination of voriconazole, amphotericin B, and terbinafine currently represents the most supported regimen [2,[5], [6], [7], [8]], its use requires continuous adjustment based on susceptibility testing and the patient's clinical response to balance therapeutic efficacy with toxicity risk.

Given the high risk of DILI, careful monitoring of liver function and minimization of hepatotoxic co-medications are essential, potentially requiring the discontinuation of all non-essential drugs in cases of significant hepatic impairment. In addition to pharmacological therapy, surgical debridement is a critical component of management, as it facilitates fungal burden reduction and improves therapeutic outcomes. These infections often require prolonged courses of antifungal treatment, frequently extending for weeks to months, depending on clinical severity, immune status and clinical evolution. Considering these difficulties, new antifungal agents, like olorofim or fosmanogepix, are being developed and have shown promising in vitro activity against atypical molds responsible for aggressive and invasive infections [2].

Invasive fungal sinusitis caused by Scedosporium spp. is a rare but potentially fatal disease if not promptly and adequately treated. This case illustrates the importance of early recognition, prompt microbiological identification, and tailored pharmacological and surgical management through a coordinated multidisciplinary approach.
