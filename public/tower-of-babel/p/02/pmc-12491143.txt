Deep learning-based survival prediction model for adult diffuse low-grade glioma: a multi-cohort validation study
Authors: Pengfei Xu, Wenxin Liu, Haibo Su, Tang Ye, Guangyuan Wu, Tao Wu, Baodong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate prognostication in adult diffuse low-grade glioma (DLGG) patients remains challenging due to the complex interplay of clinical and molecular factors. This study aimed to develop and validate a deep learning-based model for predicting survival in DLGG patients.Methods We analyzed 1,079 DLGG patients across three cohorts: training (n = 836), internal validation (n = 210), and external validation (n = 33). A deep learning model (DeepSurv) was developed incorporating seven clinicopathological variables. Model performance was assessed using C-index and integrated Brier scores (IBS). Feature importance was evaluated through permutation importance analysis and SHAP values.ResultsThe cohorts demonstrated comparable baseline characteristics except for resection extent (P < 0.001). The model achieved robust performance with C-indices of 0.81, 0.76, and 0.87 in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively. Low IBS values (0.03–0.04) confirmed strong predictive accuracy across all cohorts. Age emerged as the strongest prognostic factor, showing non-linear effects particularly pronounced in IDH-wildtype tumors after age 50. IDH mutation status was the second most influential factor, while radiation therapy alone and tumor size showed limited prognostic value.ConclusionOur deep learning model demonstrates reliable prognostic capabilities for DLGG patients, with age and IDH status as key determinants of survival. The model has been implemented as a web-based platform (https://seerlggs-f4nze5jr7iuu9k9uuaemjf.streamlit.app) for clinical use, offering personalized survival predictions. These findings contribute to more precise prognostication and may aid in treatment strategy optimization for DLGG patients.Keywords: DLGG, Deep learning, Prognostic model, IDH mutation

Introduction

Low-grade gliomas (LGGs) constitute a distinct subset of primary central nervous system neoplasms, encompassing World Health Organization (WHO) grades I and II [1]. Among adult diffuse low-grade gliomas (DLGGs), WHO grade II lesions predominate, representing approximately 5–10% of all intracranial neoplasms [2]. These entities present unique therapeutic challenges owing to their heterogeneous biological behavior and variable clinical course trajectories. While maximal safe surgical resection remains the fundamental initial therapeutic intervention, significant controversy persists regarding the optimal timing and implementation of adjuvant treatment modalities, including both radiation therapy and chemotherapy [3]. This therapeutic uncertainty underscores the critical need for sophisticated, patient-specific prognostic models to guide clinical decision-making and optimize treatment strategies.

Current clinical guidelines have identified several factors associated with poor prognosis in adult DLGGs, including advanced age (≥ 40 years), subtotal tumor resection, extensive tumor volume, presence of preoperative neurological deficits, and IDH wild-type status [4, 5, 6, 7]. However, these prognostic indicators were primarily derived from studies designed to evaluate patients’ suitability for postoperative intervention, rather than to definitively identify those requiring such treatment [8]. Notably, recent studies have failed to validate the established age threshold of 40 years, and the optimal age cutoff for determining high-risk LGG patients remains unclear [9, 10]. Furthermore, the current practice of using individual factors to guide therapeutic decisions represents an oversimplified approach, highlighting the limitations of existing prognostic frameworks in adult DLGGs management.

Conventional statistical methodologies, such as Cox proportional hazards models incorporating least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) penalization, have been utilized to optimize treatment selection across various oncological contexts [11, 12]. Nevertheless, these approaches frequently encounter difficulties in elucidating complex interactions among multiple prognostic variables without extensive empirical investigation or a priori biological insights. This constraint has catalyzed interest in more sophisticated analytical paradigms.

Machine learning, particularly focusing on deep learning algorithms, has emerged as a promising approach to tackle these challenges [13, 14]. Deep learning methodologies exhibit the ability to autonomously discern and integrate intricate feature patterns, accounting for complex interactions without the need for explicit prior specifications. Recent applications of deep learning in predicting cancer survival have showcased remarkable effectiveness in capturing non-linear relationships among variables [15], thereby propelling the field of prognostic modeling in oncology forward.

In light of the significant potential of deep learning in medical prognostication, the present study aims to develop and validate a novel deep learning predictive model for survival outcomes in adult DLGG patients. Through the integration of readily accessible clinical parameters and molecular pathological data, our proposed model seeks to provide more accurate and individualized postoperative treatment recommendations. This approach not only addresses the limitations of current prognostic methodologies but also aligns with the paradigm shift towards precision medicine in oncology.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This investigation constitutes a comprehensive secondary analysis, integrating data from two distinct sources. The main dataset was derived from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, covering the interval from 2000 year to 2019 year (Incidence—SEER Research Plus Data, 17 Registries, Nov 2021 Sub (2000–2019)—Linked To County Attributes—Time Dependent (1990–2019) Income/Rurality, 1969–2020 Counties, National Cancer Institute, DCCPS, Surveillance Research Program, released April 2022, based on the November 2021 submission). Supplementary clinical data were acquired through systematic retrospective analysis of electronic medical records from a single tertiary healthcare center, Peking University Shenzhen Hospital (Shenzhen, China). The study protocol strictly followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, and ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Boards of the participating centers. This investigation focused on adult patients with histologically confirmed diffuse low-grade gliomas (LGGs), employing a comprehensive multi-cohort validation strategy. The study population was stratified into three distinct cohorts: a primary training cohort and two validation cohorts (internal and external). The training and internal validation cohorts were derived from the SEER database, comprising 1,046 adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) with histologically confirmed LGGs. These patients were randomly allocated in an 8:2 ratio, resulting in 836 patients in the training cohort and 210 in the internal validation cohort. Subject inclusion was predicated on stringent criteria, mandating comprehensive molecular subtype documentation aligned with specific histological classifications. Furthermore, the analysis was restricted to cases with documented surgical intervention to ensure homogeneity in treatment modality. For external validation, an independent cohort of 33 patients was recruited from Peking University Shenzhen Hospital. These patients met identical inclusion parameters and had undergone surgical management, thereby facilitating robust cross-validation of the study findings. Written informed consent was provided by all patients.

Development of the prediction model

The identification and selection of potential prognostic determinants were predicated upon a systematic literature review and established clinical evidence synthesis [11, 12, 16, 17]. The incorporated variables encompassed demographic characteristics (age, sex), tumor-specific parameters (anatomical location, dimensional metrics, molecular classification), and therapeutic interventions (extent of surgical resection, radiotherapy administration, chemotherapy protocols). The prognostic model was constructed utilizing DeepSurv architecture, an advanced deep learning implementation of the Cox proportional hazards framework [14]. The model optimization methodology employed a comprehensive hyperparameter exploration strategy, systematically investigating the multidimensional parameter space. The architectural exploration encompassed networks of varying complexity, ranging from monolithic to quaternary hidden layer configurations, with nodal densities spanning from 8 to 128 units per stratum. A diverse spectrum of activation functions was evaluated to optimize the capture of complex non-linear data relationships. The hyperparameter optimization protocol implemented a rigorous 5-fold cross-validation framework, incorporating a maximum of 500 iterative cycles with convergence monitoring via mean concordance index. The optimization domain encompassed critical parameters including learning rate modulation, epoch quantification, dropout probability distributions, and L2 regularization coefficients. Furthermore, the investigation included systematic evaluation of batch normalization’s impact on model convergence dynamics and generalization capabilities. Following exhaustive computational optimization, the optimal architectural configuration was determined to be a neural network comprising a single hidden layer with 16 neuronal units, implementing the ReLU activation function. The model training protocol utilized a precisely calibrated learning rate of 0.0001, executed over 1,000 epochs. To enhance model robustness and mitigate overfitting phenomena, a dropout mechanism was implemented with a probability coefficient of 0.2, complemented by L2 regularization (coefficient: 0.0001). The integration of batch normalization techniques was employed to optimize training stability and convergence characteristics.

Validation of the prediction model

The predictive model underwent rigorous validation through a comprehensive two-pronged approach, examining both discriminative capability and calibration metrics utilizing an independent validation cohort. Discriminative performance was assessed via Harrell’s concordance index (C-index), a well-established statistical measure that quantifies the model’s ability to correctly rank patients according to their survival outcomes. The C-index, bounded between 0 and 1, serves as a standardized metric of predictive accuracy, with values approaching 1 indicating optimal discriminative performance. Calibration assessment was conducted through systematic evaluation of the Brier score, which quantifies prediction accuracy by measuring the mean squared deviation between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. To evaluate the temporal stability of probabilistic predictions, time-dependent Brier scores were calculated at predetermined intervals throughout the follow-up period. Lower Brier scores signified superior calibration, indicating stronger concordance between predicted and observed outcomes. The Integrated Brier Score (IBS) was employed to provide a comprehensive assessment of model performance across all time points, offering a global measure of predictive accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in Python version 3.8.16. For comparative analyses between cohorts, continuous variables were evaluated using Student’s t-test, while categorical variables were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To address missing data, a systematic imputation protocol was implemented: categorical variables were imputed utilizing mode substitution, while continuous variables underwent median imputation. Feature importance analysis was performed using multiple complementary approaches. We implemented DeepSurv model coupled with permutation importance analysis and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) methodology. Permutation importance was assessed by randomly shuffling individual feature values and quantifying the resultant impact on model performance. SHAP analysis was conducted using the SHAP package with Explainer to compute feature contributions to individual predictions. We specifically examined feature interactions through SHAP interaction values, generating dependency plots to visualize the relationships between key clinical and molecular variables.

Results

Clinical characteristics

In this study, a total of 1,079 patients were included, comprising 836 patients in the training cohort, 210 in the internal validation cohort, and 33 in the external validation cohort (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics comparison among the three cohorts demonstrated no significant differences in clinical features except for the extent of resection (Table 1, P < 0.001, all other P > 0.05). In the training cohort, the median age was 40.5 years (range: 19–84 years), with 495 males (59.2%) and 341 females (40.8%). The mean tumor size was 46.1 ± 36.9 mm. Regarding molecular pathological classification, IDH-mutant astrocytoma accounted for 38.8% (324 cases), IDH-wildtype astrocytoma for 11.7% (98 cases), and IDH-mutant oligodendroglioma for 49.5% (414 cases). With respect to treatment characteristics, the extent of surgical resection was distributed as follows: gross total resection (GTR) in 362 patients (43.3%), subtotal resection (STR) in 307 patients (36.7%), partial resection (PR) in 19 patients (2.3%), and biopsy in 148 patients (17.7%). Regarding adjuvant therapy, 350 patients (41.9%) received no adjuvant treatment, while 411 patients (49.2%) underwent concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT). Single-modality chemotherapy and radiotherapy were administered to 36 (4.3%) and 39 (4.7%) patients, respectively. The demographic characteristics, tumor features, and treatment patterns in both internal and external validation cohorts were comparable to those in the training cohort. Notably, the external validation cohort had no biopsy cases, which might reflect variations in surgical strategies across different centers.

Development and validation of prediction model

We developed a deep learning-based survival prediction model (DeepSurv) incorporating seven clinicopathological variables: age, sex, tumor size, molecular subtype, extent of resection, and adjuvant treatment modalities. The model was trained using the training cohort with survival time and event status as outcome measures. The model demonstrated excellent discriminative ability in predicting survival outcomes. In the training cohort, the C-index were 0.81. Similar performance was observed in the internal validation cohort, with corresponding C-index of 0.76. The external validation further confirmed the model’s generalizability, achieving C-index of 0.87. The integrated brier score was 0.04 for the training cohort, demonstrating strong predictive accuracy. Similar performance was observed in the internal validation cohort (IBS = 0.04) and external validation cohort (IBS = 0.03), confirming the model’s robust generalizability across different patient populations. Time-dependent brier score analysis revealed dynamic prediction accuracy across the 40-month follow-up period. All cohorts demonstrated consistently low Brier scores throughout the observation period, indicating good model performance. The training cohort (blue line) showed a gradual increase from baseline, with scores remaining below 0.10 for most of the follow-up period. The internal validation cohort (red line) demonstrated comparable temporal performance patterns to the training cohort, with slightly lower scores in the later period. The external validation cohort (green line), despite showing minimal changes in the first 20 months, maintained satisfactory prediction accuracy and showed notable increases between months 20–25 and months 30–35, before stabilizing. All three cohorts maintained Brier scores well below the reference value of 0.25, confirming the model’s robust predictive performance across different populations (Fig. 2A).

Feature importance

To quantify the relative contribution of each predictor variable to survival outcomes, we conducted a comprehensive feature importance analysis using multiple approaches. The DeepSurv model’s feature importance was evaluated through permutation importance analysis and SHAP values. Permutation importance analysis revealed that age was the most influential predictor, followed by IDH-mutant oligodendroglioma and IDH-wildtype astrocytoma (Fig. 3A). Surgical approach, biopsy, and adjuvant chemotherapy demonstrated moderate importance, while tumor size and sex showed relatively lower contributions to the model’s predictions. Notably, radiation therapy alone and partial resection showed the lowest feature importance values, suggesting their relatively limited independent prognostic value in the model. SHAP analysis further confirmed these findings and provided additional insights into the direction and magnitude of each feature’s effect. Higher SHAP values were consistently observed for age, with values ranging from − 5 to over 20, demonstrating its dominant role in survival predictions (Fig. 3B). Among molecular subtypes, IDH-wildtype astrocytoma showed substantial impact with predominantly positive SHAP values, while IDH-mutant oligodendroglioma demonstrated both positive and negative effects. The extent of resection showed a clear gradient effect, with biopsy associated with notably positive SHAP values, indicating poorer survival predictions, while STR (subtotal resection) showed moderate positive and negative values. Notably, adjuvant treatment strategies, particularly the absence of treatment and combined chemoradiotherapy (CRT), demonstrated intermediate SHAP values, indicating their moderate but consistent influence on survival predictions (Fig. 3C). Interaction analysis demonstrated synergistic effects between molecular subtypes (IDH-wild) and age, suggesting that the prognostic impact of age varies significantly across different molecular subtypes. While age showed a non-linear effect pattern, with minimal impact before age 50 but dramatically increased negative prognostic value in older patients, particularly in IDH-wild type tumors (Fig. 4A). This age-dependent effect was more pronounced in IDH-wild type subgroup, where elderly patients (> 60 years) showed markedly higher SHAP values, indicating substantially worse predicted outcomes (Fig. 4B).

Model deployment and clinical implementation

To translate our prognostic model into clinical practice, we developed an interactive web-based platform using Streamlit framework (https://seerlggs-f4nze5jr7iuu9k9uuaemjf.streamlit.app). The platform provides an intuitive interface where clinicians can input essential patient information, including age, sex, tumor size, molecular subtypes, extent of resection, and adjuvant treatment strategies. Upon data entry, the system generates a comprehensive survival visualization, presenting a dynamic curve that spans five years post-diagnosis (Fig. 5A). The interface displays survival probabilities with particular emphasis on one-, three-, and five-year outcomes, each clearly marked on the curve with precise percentage values.

Discussion

Our findings provided significant insights into glioma prognosis prediction, contributing to the evolving landscape of neuro-oncology prognostication. The study cohort of 1,079 patients not only demonstrated representative demographic characteristics but also reflected contemporary molecular and clinical patterns in glioma management [18, 19]. The median age of 40.5 years aligned with established epidemiological data, though it was worth noting that age distribution patterns in gliomas had shown subtle shifts in recent decades, possibly reflecting improved diagnostic capabilities and changing environmental factors [20]. The predominance of IDH-mutant astrocytomas and oligodendrogliomas (88.3%) in our cohort mirrored the molecular landscape typically observed in low-grade gliomas, yet this distribution warranted careful interpretation in the context of evolving molecular classification systems [18].

The treatment patterns observed in our cohort, with 45.6% achieving gross total resection and 49.1% receiving concurrent chemoradiotherapy, reflected current standard practices while highlighting the ongoing challenges in optimal treatment selection. The observed treatment distribution underscored the multifaceted nature of clinical decision-making in contemporary neuro-oncology practice. Multiple determinants, including anatomical considerations, preservation of neurological function, and patient-specific preferences, substantially influenced therapeutic strategy selection. Notably, the considerable proportion of patients receiving concurrent chemoradiotherapy indicated a paradigm shift toward more aggressive multimodal interventions, even in the context of lower-grade gliomas. This therapeutic trend reflected the ongoing evolution of treatment protocols and suggested a recalibration of risk-benefit assessments in neuro-oncological management strategies, particularly regarding the timing and intensity of adjuvant therapy implementation [21].

Our deep learning model’s predictive capabilities, demonstrated by C-indices of 0.81, 0.76, and 0.87 across different cohorts, represented a significant advancement in prognostic modeling. These results were particularly noteworthy when compared to traditional statistical models, which typically achieved C-indices between 0.65 and 0.75 in glioma prognostication [22]. The gradual improvement in Brier scores over the follow-up period suggested that our model’s predictive accuracy may be particularly valuable for long-term prognostication, a crucial consideration in lower-grade glioma management where survival trajectories could span decades.

The identification of age as the primary prognostic determinant, followed by IDH mutation status, added nuanced understanding to known prognostic factors [23]. The non-linear relationship between age and prognosis, particularly the inflection point at 50 years, provided clinically relevant insights for patient stratification. This age-related prognostic pattern, especially pronounced in IDH-wildtype tumors, suggested potential biological mechanisms underlying age-dependent tumor behavior that merited further investigation. The moderate prognostic value of surgical approach and adjuvant chemotherapy, coupled with the limited independent significance of radiotherapy alone and tumor size, challenged some traditional assumptions about treatment impact and suggested the need for more personalized therapeutic approaches [4].

Limitation

Despite these promising findings, several methodological limitations warrant careful consideration. The external validation cohort’s limited size represented a significant constraint, particularly given the heterogeneous nature of gliomas. This limitation was compounded by the absence of biopsy specimens, which could provide valuable molecular and histological validation. The observed surgical heterogeneity across institutions, while reflecting real-world practice, introduced variables that could influence outcome assessment and model performance. A particularly crucial consideration lay in the classification of IDH-wildtype astrocytomas in light of the 2021 WHO Classification guidelines [1]. The potential presence of cases requiring reclassification as glioblastoma based on molecular characteristics (EGFR amplification, chromosome 7 gain/10 loss, or TERT promoter mutation) represented a significant limitation [24, 25]. The inability to identify and exclude these cases due to SEER database limitations might have introduced systematic bias, particularly affecting analyses of the IDH-wildtype subgroup. Furthermore, the evolving understanding of molecular markers beyond IDH mutation status, including CDKN2A/B codeletion [26], TERT promoter mutations [24], and chromosomal copy number variations [27], suggested that our model’s predictive capabilities could be enhanced through incorporation of comprehensive molecular profiling. The absence of such data in our study, while reflecting common clinical database limitations, highlighted opportunities for future model refinement.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while our model demonstrated robust prognostic capabilities, its limitations and the evolving landscape of glioma classification underscored the need for continued refinement and validation. Future studies incorporating comprehensive molecular profiling, larger external validation cohorts, and advanced imaging features would be crucial in developing more precise and clinically applicable prognostic tools.
