Integrating proteomics and bioinformatics for the identification of breast cancer biomarkers interacting with telomeric G-quadruplex
Authors: Ilaria Iacobucci, Irene Cipollone, Flora Cozzolino, Rosa Gaglione, Maria Rosaria Mentino, Chiara Platella, Domenica Musumeci, Angela Arciello, Daniela Montesarchio, Maria Monti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe identification of reliable biomarkers is essential for improving breast cancer (BC) detection, prognosis, and treatment. This study explores a human telomeric G-quadruplex (G4) model, tel46, functionalized on Controlled Pore Glass (CPG) support, as a novel biomarker discovery tool. The oligonucleotide tel46 mimics multimeric G4 structures in telomeric overhangs. Using affinity purification-mass spectrometry, 93 proteins interacting with tel46 were identified starting from nuclear extract of MCF7 cells, linking them to pathways in DNA replication, repair, and genome stability, which are frequently altered in cancer. Integrating AP-MS data with quantitative proteomics comparing MCF7 to non-tumorigenic MCF10A cells, 27 tel46 interactors were identified among upregulated proteins. Functional analyses revealed enrichment in genome maintenance and repair pathways, while downregulated proteins were associated with fundamental cellular functions. Further bioinformatics analysis using public cancer proteomics database 19 were validated. Bioinformatic analysis based on transcriptomics and clinical data revealed MSH6, MSH2, ESRP1, and WDHD1 as the most promising potential biomarkers for breast cancer. Indeed, these proteins are highly expressed in BC and generally correlated to poor prognosis: in addition to their role as potential biomarkers for early diagnosis, these proteins might be used as targets for specific treatment, enhancing radiation sensitivity or decreasing tumour cell proliferation. As a proof-of-concept, this study proposes tel46-functionalized CPG as a potential tool for isolating cancer-related proteins and underscores the potential of G4-interacting proteins as biomarkers for BC diagnosis and therapy. Background

The pursuit of reliable biomarkers is essential for improving early detection, accurate prognosis, and effective treatment of breast cancer (BC), which remains one of the most prevalent cancer forms globally. BC accounted for approximately 11.7% of all new cancer cases worldwide, making it a leading health concern [1]. Biomarkers are pivotal in modern oncology, facilitating early diagnosis, monitoring treatment response, and personalizing therapeutic strategies [2]. Their role is critical for advancing precision medicine and improving survival rates by enabling timely intervention and tailored treatment plans. Among the many molecular targets being explored, human telomeric DNA, consisting of tandem repeats of the sequence TTAGGG and thus able to fold into G-quadruplex (G4) structures, emerged as key player in cancer biology [3]. G4s influence processes like transcription, replication, and genomic stability, which are often altered in cancer. Notably, G4s are enriched in regulatory regions of oncogenes such as MYC and TP53 and are associated with increased transcriptional activity in cancer cells [4]. Furthermore, the telomere 3’-overhang rich in guanines can be the substrate of telomerase, a DNA polymerase, overexpressed in cancer cells, that elongates telomeric DNA thus counteracting telomere shortening and leading to cellular immortality and finally cancer development and progression [5]. This makes telomeres attractive targets for cancer biomarker discovery and therapeutic intervention. In cancer cells, telomeric G4s interact with various nuclear proteins, including those involved in DNA replication, repair, and genome stability. These interactions offer unique opportunities to uncover cancer-specific protein partners and potential biomarkers. Recent advancements in proteomics and bioinformatics have enabled the systematic identification of G4-interacting proteins, revealing their roles in DNA metabolism, splicing regulation, and cell cycle progression [6] These studies have highlighted the potential of G4s not only as therapeutic targets in targeted anticancer strategies but also as platforms for discovering disease biomarkers [7]. Identifying novel biomarkers is fundamental to enable precise patient stratification and deliver personalized treatment based on individual tumour biology. Current BC diagnostics rely predominantly on immunohistochemical markers such as ER, PR, and HER2, complemented by molecular assays like FISH and multigene panels (e.g., Oncotype DX) [8, 9]. Conventional diagnostic tools provide valuable information but often fall short in revealing functional protein-level changes. Affinity Purification–Mass Spectrometry (AP‑MS) is increasingly recognized as a powerful method for biomarker discovery, capable of mapping protein complexes and specific interactions of G4 structures [10]. Moreover, MS-based proteomics workflows, transitioning from discovery to targeted assays (e.g., MRM), are well-suited for developing clinically applicable tests [11], including detection of G4-bound protein biomarkers in enriched samples.

The study of the proteins interacting with telomeric G4s can be considered a crucial aspect to elucidate the biological mechanisms in which these DNA structures could be involved, especially in cancer cells. Several proteins, besides telomerase, have been reported to interact with telomeric overhang DNA and exert various biological functions. A chemoproteomic-driven approach was previously applied to the identification of the nuclear proteins from the HCT116 cancer cells interacting with the 26-mer truncation of the human telomeric DNA sequence d[(TTAGGG)4TT] (tel26) structured in a monomeric G-quadruplex [12]. In this study three novel proteins (i.e., HMGB1, KHSRP, and LMNB1) that specifically bind to the monomeric G4 model of the human telomeric DNA were discovered, starting from a nuclear extract of human colon carcinoma cell line. The identification of these proteins suggests that G4 structures are more than just inhibitory elements for telomerase activity. In contrast, they may act as critical regulatory hubs at the telomeres, providing a scaffold for proteins that influence telomere maintenance, stability, and function [13]. These results emphasize the biological significance of G4 structures, highlighting their role in maintaining genome integrity and their potential as therapeutic targets. Particularly in cancer, where telomere dysfunction is a hallmark, understanding how G4s interact with specific proteins could lead to innovative strategies for targeting telomeric pathways.

Similarly, one of the most exciting applications of G4s is their integration into biosensors for specific pathology diagnosis and prognosis. G4-based-aptasensors, as they are often called, utilize the binding capabilities of G4 forming aptamers (i.e., short natural or modified oligonucleotide strands that can fold into stable G4 structures) towards specific targets (proteins, cell-receptors, metabolites, etc.). For instance, in cancer diagnostics, G4-based aptasensors have been used to detect crucial biomarkers such as the circulating proteins thrombin and VEGF, or the membrane proteins HER2 (epidermal growth factor receptor 2) and MUC1 (mucin 1) [14, 15]. These biosensors are often paired with advanced detection techniques like fluorescence, electrochemistry, and chemiluminescence [16], significantly enhancing their sensitivity and making them capable of detecting targets at extremely low concentrations [17]. G4s represents a game-changer compared to traditional diagnostic tools. For example, unlike antibodies, which are very sensitive to changes in temperature or pH, G4 structures remain robust across various conditions, ensuring consistent performance. They are also cost-effective; being chemically synthesized, G4-based aptamers are far less expensive to produce than antibodies. Another advantage is their reusability. Indeed, unlike traditional diagnostic tools, G4-based aptamers can often be regenerated and used multiple times, adding an element of sustainability to their application.

Since the 3′-end of the human telomeric DNA is 100–200 bases long and can form higher-order structures by clustering several consecutive G4 units (multimers), here, we undertook a proteomic study choosing a model of the telomeric overhang able to form two consecutive G4 structures, linked to a Controlled Pore Glass (CPG) support ad hoc synthesized for fishing applications [18–20]. Indeed, we investigated the nuclear interactome of the telomeric dimeric G4 model of sequence d[AGGG(TTAGGG)7] (tel46). Using AP-MS, we identified proteins selectively binding to the dimeric G4 structures in MCF7 breast cancer cells. The latter, which are estrogen receptor-positive and representative of the luminal A BC subtype, were chosen due to their well-characterized and clinically relevant molecular profile. The AP-MS approach revealed 93 tel46-binding proteins, many of which are involved in DNA replication, repair, and genome stability, processes frequently disrupted in cancer. By integrating AP-MS with quantitative proteomics comparing MCF7 to non-tumorigenic MCF10A cells, we identified a subset of 27 interactors that were significantly upregulated in tumour cells. As a non-tumorigenic counterpart, we included MCF10A cells, an immortalized epithelial line, to serve as a baseline reference for physiological nuclear protein composition either in the differential proteomics and interactomics approaches. Further bioinformatic validation across public cancer proteomics and transcriptomics databases supported 19 candidates, among which MSH2, MSH6, ESRP1, and WDHD1 emerged as the most promising potential biomarkers. To explore whether the proteins captured in MCF7 reflect a broader BC signature, we extended our analyses using publicly available proteomic and transcriptomic databases across multiple BC subtypes. This approach enabled us to assess the diagnostic and prognostic potential of the tel46-binding protein panel beyond the luminal context, laying the groundwork for subtype-spanning biomarker discovery.

This preliminary work paves the way to tel46 or other cancer-related sequences as tools for biomarker discovery and highlights its application in predicting molecular signatures associated with breast cancer subtypes. Finally, we propose MSH2, MSH6, ESRP1, WDHD1 proteins as candidate biomarkers recognized by tel46 which could be possibly used in potential diagnostic and prognostic applications for BC.

Methods

Preparation of oligonucleotide-functionalized CPG supports

The oligonucleotide-functionalized Controlled Pore Glass (CPG) supports were synthesized as previously described [21]. Particularly, using standard phosphoramidite chemistry on an automated DNA synthesizer, the oligonucleotides of sequences d[(AGGG(TTAGGG)7] (tel46) and d(CGCGAATTCGCGTTTCGCGAATTCGCG) (ds27) were assembled on an ad hoc synthesized CPG support. The final oligonucleotide functionalization on the CPG supports was 2 and 4 nmol/mg for tel46-CPG and ds27-CPG, respectively, corresponding to 29 and 33 µg/mg of each oligonucleotide. Both CPG supports were subjected to an annealing procedure (75 °C for 5 min and slow cooling down to room temperature in 3 h, followed by storage at 4 °C until usage) in a buffer mimicking the intracellular conditions (10 mM potassium phosphate, 100 mM KCl, pH 7.4) favouring the thermodynamically preferred conformations of the oligonucleotides covalently attached to the solid support. Fishing of the oligonucleotide protein partners was then performed from the proper nuclear cellular lysate. ds27 (double stranded DNA) was employed during the pre-cleaning step to reduce the protein background, while tel46 was employed as bait for interactome purification.

Cell culture

Human breast adenocarcinoma cells MCF7 were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO2. Human non-tumorigenic epithelial cells from mammary gland ATCC-CRL-10,317 MCF10A were cultured in complete Mammary Epithelial Growth Medium (MEGM) containing 100 ng/mL of cholera toxin at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO2. To obtain a suitable amount of proteins for subsequent analyses, both cell lines were seeded into 100 mm petri plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells/plate. At the end of cell amplification, ~ 40 × 106 of MCF7 and ~ 60 × 106 of MCF10A cells were obtained; they were centrifuged and then washed two times in PBS.

Cell Lysis

MCF7 cells were lysed according to a fractionated lysis protocol to separately obtain cytosolic and nuclear extracts. Briefly, buffer A (10 mM HEPES pH = 7.9, 10 mM KCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM EGTA, 0.5 mM PMSF, 0.5% Nonidet P40, and protease inhibitor tablet) was added to the cell pellet in a v/v ratio 1:40 (pellet: buffer) and incubated for 15 min on ice. The solubilized pellet was gently shaken at 4 °C for 5 min. Following centrifugation (2,000 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min), the supernatant, consisting of cytosolic protein extract, was discarded, while the nuclear pellet was washed twice in buffer A without Nonidet P40, and then lysed in buffer B (20 mM HEPES pH = 7.9, 150 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 0.5 mM PMSF, 0.05% Tween-20), v/v ratio 1:4 (pellet: buffer), left on ice for 15 min and then vortexed for 15 min. The nuclear extract was recovered after 30 min centrifugation at 13,000 rpm at 4 °C. The proteins were quantified by Bradford assay.

Affinity purification – mass spectrometry (AP-MS) analysis of CPG-tel46 interactome

CPG-ds27 support (42 µg of oligonucleotide) was suspended in MCF7 and MCF10A nuclear extracts (840 µg, each) in buffer B for 2 h at 4 °C, and the collected supernatant was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the CPG-tel46 support (36 µg of oligonucleotide) according to the AP-MS protocol [22]. Following the overnight incubation under gentle agitation on a stirring wheel, the CPG-tel46 flowthrough was discarded and both CPG-ds27 and CPG-tel46 were washed three times with buffer B supplemented with 100 mM KCl to remove potential contaminants. The specific oligonucleotides interactors were eluted with 5% SDS in ammonium bicarbonate 50 mM, pH 8, by 10 min incubation at 99 °C. CPG-ds27 retained proteins were stored and used as control. The pulldown eluates were subjected to a proteomics shotgun procedure, consisting in a direct trypsinization of eluted protein samples on S-Trap cartridges (Protifi, Huntington, NY) according to the vendor protocol. The peptide mixtures were resuspended in 0.2% formic acid and analyzed by nanoLC-MS/MS using the Easy-nLC II chromatographic system coupled with a LTQ Orbitrap XL mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Chromatographic fractionations were carried out onto a C18 capillary column (5 μm biosphere, 75 μm internal diameter, 200 mm length) using a non-linear gradient, from 5 to 50% of eluent B (95% acetonitrile, 0.2% formic acid) in A (5% acetonitrile, 0.2% formic acid in water), over 260 min [23]. Mass analyses were performed in Data Dependent Acquisition (DDA) mode by fragmenting the 10 most intense ions in CID modality. The acquisition range spanned from 300 to 1800 m/z units, and the Orbitrap resolution was set to 60,000.

Protein identification was carried out by MaxQuant software (v.1.5.2.8), using UniProt Homo Sapiens as database for Andromeda search. Parameters used for protein identification were the following: (i) a minimum of 3 peptides, including at least 1 unique; (ii) variable allowed modifications were methionine oxidation and pyroglutamate formation on N-terminal glutamine; (iii) accuracy for the first search was set to 10 ppm, then lowered to 5 ppm in the main search; (iv) 0.01 FDR was used with a reverse database for decoy. The CPG-tel46 -binding identified proteins here identified, depleted from those shared with the CPG-ds27 dataset, represented the specific interactors of tel46. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD059593. To get rid of possible contaminants, the list was subjected to analysis with Crapome database [24] by using the available option “Workflow 1” and selecting “H. Sapiens – All” as database. The interactors identified in both cell lines were filtered to remove those present in more than 40% of the experiments already reported with the selected database. The functional enrichment analyses were performed with Panther database (version 18.0 Released 2023-08-01) platform consulting cellular component, molecular functions, and biological processes gene ontology databases (Released 26-02-2024). The analyses were performed by applying a Fisher exact test and using 5% FDR as cutoff (Benjamini-Hochberg corrected). The output terms were filtered by fold enrichment ≥ 5. The plot was obtained by SRPlot tool [25].

Label free differential proteomics experiments

Three independent pellets from MCF7 and MCF10A, respectively, were lysed by adding a 5% SDS, 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution according to Cozzolino et al. [26]. Total proteins were digested with trypsin by using S-Trap cartridges (Protifi, Huntington, NY). The LC-MS/MS analysis of the peptide mixtures were carried out with an Orbitrap Exploris 240 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) mass spectrometer coupled with a VanquishNeo (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) UHPLC system. The LC gradient was set by mixing solvent A (2% acetonitrile, 0.2% formic acid in water LC-MS grade) and B (2% water LC-MS grade, 0.2% formic acid in acetonitrile) starting from 2 to 30% of B in 60 min and then from 30 to 50% of B in 5 min at 250 µl/min flow rate. The mass spectrometry analysis was carried out in DDA mode by fragmenting the top 20 intense ions [27]. The acquisition range spanned from 300 to 1800 m/z units, and the Orbitrap resolution was set to 120,000. The LC-MS/MS data were run in triplicates. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD059597.

Protein identification and quantification based on LC-MS/MS data were performed using MaxQuant (v 2.4.10), setting the search parameters as reported above. Statistical analyses were carried out by using Perseus software (v 1.6.15.0), to filter out the protein contaminants (i.e., reverse and potential contaminants) and those entries with more than 70% of not valid values in at least one group in the LFQ intensity column. The remaining zero values were imputed according to the Gaussian method [28]. A Student’s t-test was performed with 1% Benjamini-Hochberg corrected p-value cutoff. The fold changes (FC) were calculated as the mean difference of the protein log2 LFQ intensities in MCF7 versus MCF10A. The differentially expressed proteins were considered those with log2FC<−0.5 and log2FC > 0.5 as down- and up-regulated, respectively. The functional enrichment was performed for the up- and down- regulated proteins separately according to Panther database, as mentioned in paragraph “Affinity purification – mass spectrometry (AP-MS) analysis of CPG-tel46 interactome”. The ontologies with fold enrichment > 5, FDR < 0.01 and protein counts > 3 were reported in the analysis. The plots were obtained by SRPlot tool [25].

UALCAN

UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) is based on publicly available cancer OMICS datasets [29]. We interrogated the UALCAN database to find the protein expression levels of interactomics-derived proteins according to Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) dataset. The proteins were considered differentially expressed between normal and BC condition when p-value was less than 1E-04.

Gene expression profiling interactive analysis (GEPIA) dataset

The GEPIA interactive web server (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/detail.php) [30] was used to evaluate the transcriptional levels of the 19 proteomics-derived candidates comparing normal and breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) tissues according to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype Tissue Expression (GTEx) datasets. The analysis was performed by using a LIMMA test, selecting a q-value cutoff 0.01 and |Log2FC|>0.5.

Breast cancer gene-expression miner v5.0 (bc-GenExMiner v5.0) online tool

Bc-GenExMiner (http://bcgenex.ico.unicancer.fr/BC-GEM/GEM-Accueil.php?js=1) online tool hosts curated breast cancer transcriptomic data from published sources, including DNA microarrays (n = 11,552) and RNA-seq (n = 5,023). Users can conduct various analyses, named as prognostic, correlation, and expression studies. In our investigation, we used the expression correlation module [31] within “targeted” analysis to assess the linear expression correlation among the 19 candidate genes. Specifically, we focused on the DNA microarray dataset and evaluated combinations of estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) status. Additionally, we utilized the “expression” module [32] to examine the expression levels of the 19 candidates relative to clinicopathological and molecular characteristics of the population documented in the database.

Kaplan-Meier plotter analysis

Kaplan-Meier plotter (KM plotter, https://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=background) [33] correlates the expression of a gene and the patients survival in more than 350,000 samples from 21 tumour types. It applies statistical tools including Cox proportional hazards regression and the computation of the False Discovery Rate. The analysis was carried out by using the mRNA expression data in BC. In particular, the correlation between the target gene mRNA expression levels and relapse-free survival (RFS), the overall survival (OS) rate, distance metastasis free survival (DMFS) in BC groups were calculated by the Kaplan–Meier curve and log-rank test. The software calculated the hazard ratio (HR) value and the 95% confidence. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant according to the Log-rank test.

Recombinant proteins pulldowns using CPG-tel46 resin

To test the interaction between G4-tel46 and the proteins candidate as potential biomarkers, a pulldown assay was set up. For pre-cleaning, naked CPG support (1 mg) suspended in 100 µL of annealing buffer (10 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 150 mM NaCl, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 0.05% Tween-20) was used. The suspended support (20 µL) was respectively incubated with 4 µg of each recombinant proteins, and kept 1 h at 4 °C on a stirring wheel. Subsequently, the unbound fraction was incubated with the same amount of CPG-tel46 suspension agitated at 4 °C overnight. The proteins were then eluted from the supports (CPG and CPG-tel46) with 5%SDS, 50 mM AMBIC at 99 °C under stirring for 10 min. The elution of naked CPG (pre-cleaning) and CPG-G4 as well as 2 µg of protein loading control were fractionated by 8% SDS-PAGE. The gel was stained with GelCode™ Blue Safe Protein Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and destained with Milli-Q water.

Multiple reaction monitoring

Triplicates of peptide mixtures from MCF7 and MCF10A cells were obtained as already reported in “Label free differential proteomics experiments” paragraph. For each protein, transitions were predicted by using Skyline v 25.1.0.142 (MacCoss Lab Software, Dept of Genome Sciences, UW), as reported in Supplementary Table 4. At least three proteotypic peptides and three transitions for each deriving parent ion were selected for the monitoring and quantification. Xevo-TQS triple-quad mass spectrometer, coupled to a nanoAcquity UHPLC (Waters, Milford, MA, United States) equipped with iKey CHIP interface (BEH C18 130 Å, 1.7 μm 150 μm x 50 mm iKey) was used. The LC gradient was set by mixing solvent A (5% acetonitrile, 0.2% formic acid in water LC-MS grade) and B (5% water LC-MS grade, 0.2% formic acid in acetonitrile LC-MS grade) starting from 3 to 35% of B in 7 min and then from 35 to 50% of B in 5 min at 3 µl/min flow rate. Each run was analyzed in duplicate, and the total area of each peptide transition was used for the relative quantification of the specific protein, using glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) protein as normalizer.

Results

Fishing and identification of CPG-tel46 interactome

An affinity purification – mass spectrometry (AP-MS) experiment was set up to extract and identify the proteins specifically interacting with a model of the human telomeric DNA, that is the dimeric G4 structure tel46, covalently bound to our ad hoc synthesized CPG support, from the nuclear lysate of MCF7 cells, selected as model of cancer cells, and specifically of human breast adenocarcinoma. By discarding the proteins identified in the pulldown experiment with the CPG support functionalized with the control duplex oligonucleotide ds27, we identified 112 potential tel46 interactors. The list was further filtered by Crapome database by removing proteins that were found up to 40% of the time in other similar experimental settings reported in the database, and, therefore, considered very frequent contaminants. The final list of potential tel46 binders was reduced to 93 proteins (Supplementary Table 1). The latter ones were functionally analyzed by the Panther platform, interrogating Gene Ontologies classes, such as cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes. In the dot plot in Fig. 1, the significant enriched terms for each ontology are represented for Biological Processes (BP), Cellular Components (CC) and Molecular Functions (MF).

Dot-plot of the over-representation analysis performed with PantherDB of tel46 interactors. The Biological Processes (BP), Cellular Components (CC), Molecular Functions (MF) are reported in three different boxes. For each enriched term the -log10 FDR is displayed in a colour-scale, the enrichment values are reported onto the x-axis, and the number of genes associated to the term corresponds to the dot size

Among the enriched Biological Processes, we found molecular functions related to G4 structures, such as DNA replication [34] and genome integrity maintenance (i.e., mismatch repair, double-strand break repair via homologous recombination). In particular, the AP experiment selected proteins located at the nuclear replication forks (RF) (fold enrichment close to 50 in Cellular Component analysis) and showing helicase activity (fold enrichment around 13 in Molecular Function analysis), that, together with the new DNA synthesis, represents the principal activity at the RF [35]. The presence of replisome complex in the CC enriched terms also suggests the involvement of DNA replication processes, consistent with the telomeric role of the G4 structure. Actually, these structures cause a physical roadblock to DNA replication, generating a stalled replication fork [36], which allows the local recruitment of all replication factors required for the process initiation. Moreover, the “mismatch repair” (MMR), the DNA repair mechanism required for proper maintenance of the genome stability by protecting against base pair mismatches and insertion/deletion loops arising from DNA polymerase errors or during homologous recombination was also well represented in the list of Biological Processes. In particular, three proteins were identified as players in DNA mismatch repair (MMR) mechanism: MSH2, MSH6, and RPA3. MSH2 and MSH6 form a heterodimer within the MutSα complex, already described to recognize G4-DNA structures [37].

Even though unexpected, we also found processes related to translation and mRNA binding, as suggested by cytoplasmic translation initiation in BP, polysome in CC, and translation initiation factor activity in MF terms. In fact, the presence of many subunits of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 3 (eIF3) and proteins reported to bind to mRNA (e.g., LARP4B, ESRP1, CNBP and many others) enriched these classes. Within this group, many proteins showed also nuclear localization. This could be probably explained considering that RNA is also able to fold in G4 structures generally reported to be involved in different processes such as translation regulation, splicing, mRNA localization [38], as found in the Biological Processes enriched terms.

Analysis of the dysregulated proteins in MCF7 cells

To comprehensively profile the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in breast cancer in comparison to a physiological system, the total proteomes from MCF7 and MCF10A cells were compared by using a label free shotgun approach. The peptide mixtures that were analyzed in triplicate by nanoLC-MS/MS and 3748 proteins were identified and quantified by MaxQuant (Supplementary Table 2). Figure 2 illustrates the results of the differential proteomics analysis comparing the proteomes of the breast cancer cell line (MCF7) and of the non-tumorigenic epithelial cell line (MCF10A). The similarity of the samples was assessed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) (Fig. 2A), and Euclidean distances were calculated and displayed in a heatmap (Fig. 2B). The results demonstrated the robustness of the generated mass spectrometry datasets. Indeed, the PCA plot revealed a very high degree of similarity among samples within the same group (MCF7 or MCF10A). Furthermore, as expected, the analysis clearly showed the separation between the proteomes of MCF7 and MCF10A. The two cell types form distinct clusters, with PC1 accounting for most of the variance (57.5%) and PC2 contributing for an additional 8.1%. This strong divergence underscores substantial differences in the overall protein expression profiles, reflecting the different biological processes that play roles in the tumorigenic (MCF7) versus non-tumorigenic (MCF10A) cells. After the Perseus analysis, 2057 statistically significant proteins were retrieved, subsequently refined according to the fold change (FC) constrains. Figure 2B shows a heatmap of statistically significant differentially expressed proteins, where samples are clustered based on Euclidean distances, and proteins are visualized in terms of their relative expression. Analogously to the PCA analysis, the heatmap colour gradient, ranging from blue (down-regulation) to red (up-regulation), reveals clear stratification between the two cell types. The clustering indicates that MCF7 and MCF10A samples exhibit distinct proteomic signatures, with groups of proteins showing markedly different expression levels, further highlighting the divergence between the cancerous and non-tumorigenic states. The analysis yielded the identification and quantification of up- and down-regulated proteins, showing |log₂FC| >0.5 (0.7 and 1.4 on a linear scale for the down- and up-regulated proteins, respectively) graphically represented in a volcano plot (Fig. 2C).

The proteins upregulated in MCF7 may be involved in cancer-associated processes such as enhanced proliferation, invasion, and metabolic rewiring [39], whereas those downregulated may correspond to pathways more active in normal epithelial cells, like differentiation and homeostatic regulation [40]. The results provide valuable insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying breast cancer and may help to identify potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets.

Most of the mechanisms over-represented in the up-regulated proteins dataset (Fig. 3A) are related to DNA repair and replication. For instance, terms such as DNA strand elongation involved in DNA replication, double strand break repair via break-induced replication, spliceosomal snRNP assembly underscore the activation of genome stability mechanisms, likely reflect the cells response to stress or damage. The cellular component output analysis revealed a massive presence of splicing-related complexes among the overexpressed proteins (U1 snRNP, U2 snRNP, U5 snRNP, U4/U6 x U5 tri-snRNP complex), as well as proteins resident in the endoplasmic reticulum to Golgi transport (ER-Golgi intermediate compartment, ER to Golgi transport vesicle membrane). The dysregulation of the splicing machinery is associated with the progression of BC, as reviewed by Read and Natrajan [41], and aberrant splicing events correspond to the onset and aggression of BC [42]. Similarly, the regulation of ER-to-Golgi trafficking in the context of BC has been addressed in several studies, suggesting an up-regulation of this pathway, which is often associated with cancer progression [43] and metastasis [44].

On the other hand, Panel B reveals a contrasting profile for the downregulated proteins. These proteins are predominantly associated with fundamental cellular processes, such as cytoplasmic translation, ribosomal subunit biogenesis, and amino acid biosynthesis, pointing to a suppression of protein synthesis machinery. This is further supported by the enrichment of ribosomal components and activities such as rRNA binding and structural molecule activity, suggesting a downregulation of translational capacity. Deregulation of ribosomal protein expression and translation was found to promote BC metastasis, highlighting the role of specific ribosomal proteins in enhancing the metastatic potential of breast cancer cells and suggesting that their down-regulation could be part of the complex response to cancer progression [45]. Additionally, processes related to actin filament bundle assembly and cell-substrate junctions indicated reduced cytoskeletal organization and adhesion, reported in BC by several studies [46].

Overall, the high overlapping degree of the processes enriched in the interactomics data of MCF7 nuclear extract (Fig. 1) with those found in the up-regulated MCF7 protein dataset (Fig. 3A) would suggest tel46 as a useful tool for biomarker recognition in BC.

Selection of potential breast cancer biomarkers recognized by tel46

To find possible biomarkers recognizing tel46 in BC, we integrated the proteomics analysis, combining interactomics and differential proteomics results, to identify potential biomarkers associated with tel46 interactors and proteins upregulated in MCF7 cells. The approach aimed to narrow down a set of proteins with potential clinical relevance by focusing on those that are both directly associated with tel46 and show significant upregulation in cancer-related pathways. In Fig. 4A, a Venn diagram visualizes the overlap between two datasets: the tel46 interactors identified through affinity purification-mass spectrometry (AP-MS) and the proteins significantly up-regulated in MCF7 cells, as determined by differential proteomics. From the 851 proteins over-expressed in MCF7 cells and the 93 tel46 interactors, 27 proteins were found to overlap. This overlay represents a critical subset of proteins that may play significant roles in MCF7 biology, as they are not only upregulated but also likely directly interact with tel46, making them strong candidates for further analyses.

The latter data were compared with the protein expression levels in BC reported in the UALCAN database [29], which provides statistical evidence of their relevance in BC biology. UALCAN is a user-friendly online platform that provides access to cancer omics data, offering tools for the analysis and visualization of gene expression, mutations, and survival data across various cancer types. It integrates data from large-scale projects like The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and allows users to explore gene expression profiles, clinical data, and tumour-specific variations. We considered as possible biomarkers spotted by tel46 only those proteins that were reported to accumulate in BC with p-value < 1E-04 in UALCAN, obtaining the table displayed in Fig. 4B. The analysis retrieved a refined set of 19 biomarker candidates. The table lists key information for each protein, including the UniProt code for identification, the gene name, the fold change (indicating the degree of up-regulation in MCF7 cells), and the p-value from the UALCAN analysis (highlighting statistical significance). This curated list represents a focused group of proteins with both strong biological relevance and potential functional significance, making them promising targets for further validation as biomarkers or therapeutic targets.

The list of 19 proteins which mainly comprises molecules involved in DNA metabolic processes was analyzed to find their co-expression patterns in breast cancer. The results are shown in the correlation matrix in Fig. 4C, obtained by using the RNA-seq data available on Breast Cancer Gene-Expression Miner v5.0 (bc-GenExMiner v5.0) [31] platform, an online tool designed to facilitate the analysis of gene expression data in BC. It allows users to explore the expression levels of genes across different BC subtypes, clinical parameters, and patient survival data. The platform integrates data from public BC datasets, such as TCGA and METABRIC, enabling users to perform in-depth analyses of gene expression, identify potential biomarkers, and explore their clinical significance. The analysis was performed by considering the sample estrogen (ER) and progesterone (PR) receptors status, calculating Pearson’s correlation indices for each ER and PR combination. Since MCF7 is a cell model known to be positive for ER and PR status [47], the correlation matrix displayed in Fig. 4C specifically pertains to this context. The diagonal elements represent individual genes, while the lower half of the matrix shows the Pearson correlation coefficients for each pairwise comparison, color-coded from blue (negative correlation) to red (positive correlation). The upper half provides the scatterplots that visualize the relationships between the expression levels of each protein pair.

Notably, almost all comparisons show a positive correlation between expressions, albeit to varying degrees. Many proteins show strong positive correlations, suggesting that they may be co-regulated or involved in similar biological pathways. Conversely, weaker or negative correlations may indicate functional divergence or opposing roles within cellular processes. This result suggests a consistent expression trend among these genes in BC, further supported by proteomic data indicating that all 19 proteins are up regulated in MCF7 cells.

Taken together, these results allowed the integration of interactomics and proteomics obtaining a list of 27 potential BC biomarkers candidates, further refined to a set of 19 through the UALCAN database. The expression levels of these 19 candidates were found to be positively correlated in BC through the analysis performed with bc-GenExMiner platform.

By examining the expression profiles of specific transcripts, the analysis identifies genes that are significantly overexpressed in BRCA samples compared to normal tissues, shedding light on potential molecular drivers of BC. According to DNA microarrays experiments, for each protein we looked for the age of the tumour onset, nodal status, ER+/-, PR+/-, HER2+/-, mutations in p53 and BRCA1/2 genes, TNBC status, Scarff-Bloom-Richardson grade, and Nottingham prognostic index.

To validate the biological meaningfulness of the selected candidates, we performed an AP-MS experiment starting from non-tumorigenic MCF10A cell line (Supplementary Table 3), using the same protocol previously described for MCF7. Remarkably, none of the previously identified candidate proteins (ESRP1, WDHD1, MSH2, MSH6) were detected in the MCF10A dataset as displayed in the graph in Fig. 8A. The finding strengthens the hypothesis that their binding to telomeric G4 structures occurs exclusively in the tumorigenic context. Discussion

In the ever-evolving landscape of biomedical research, G4s have emerged as fascinating and highly promising structures with significant potential in biomedicine and molecular diagnostics. These unique nucleic acid secondary structures are not only stable under physiological conditions but also highly versatile, making them ideal candidates for developing innovative diagnostic tools. Their role in detecting critical biomarkers, particularly in diseases such as cancer, showcases their growing importance in modern biomedicine. Unlike conventional DNA or RNA sequences, G4s are endowed with a high polymorphism and are able to adopt different conformations that allow them to selectively bind a wide range of molecular targets, from proteins [52] to small molecules [53] and even ions [54]. Their peculiar 3D structures make them incredibly useful in diagnostics, where precision and sensitivity are of paramount importance. The role of G4s in cancer diagnostics deserves special mention. Their ability to specifically bind to biomarkers like carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and CA125 has opened new avenues for early detection and monitoring of cancer progression [55].

In this proof-of-concept study, we demonstrated for the first time that the potential of a telomeric G4 structure (tel46), covalently anchored to a solid CPG support, to isolate cancer-related proteins from nuclear extracts of MCF7 BC cells. Through interactomics and quantitative proteomics approach, we identified 27 tel46-interacting proteins that are significantly up-regulated in BC cells compared to normal epithelial cells. Enrichment analyses revealed that these proteins are predominantly involved in DNA replication, genome integrity, and cellular proliferation, processes that are frequently dysregulated in cancer. Notably, the overlap between G4-interacting proteins and those upregulated in BC cells highlights the relevance of G4 structures in cancer pathophysiology. Refinement through cancer proteomics (UALCAN) and transcriptomics (GEPIA, bcGenExMiner) led to the selection of 19 candidate biomarkers. The integration of both proteomics approaches and the subsequent bioinformatics analyses further validated the clinical relevance of the identified candidates, linking their expression profiles to BC subtypes and patient outcomes. We found a panel of interesting proteins from a clinical perspective such as MSH2, MSH6, and WDHD1, key players in DNA mismatch repair and replication fork stability [56], emphasizing their dual role as contributors to cancer progression and potential biomarkers. These findings are particularly relevant given the critical role of DNA damage repair pathways in BC and their implications for treatment resistance and disease outcomes [57].

The overall bioinformatics evidence suggested that MSH6, MSH2, WDHD1, and ESRP1 proteins are highly promising biomarkers for BC. These candidates, recognized by the tel46 dimeric G4, are crucial for their roles in DNA replication, repair, and RNA splicing processes central to tumour progression and malignancy [58]. MSH6 and MSH2, as part of the MutSα complex, are pivotal in DNA mismatch repair (MMR), mainly expressed in replicating cells, which is a pathway frequently dysregulated in cancer. Their genes are generally coamplified together, possibly due to their localization near each other on chromosome 2 and their overexpression is associated with genomic instability, a hallmark of aggressive tumours [59]. Studies have linked MSH6 to poor prognosis, particularly in hormone receptor-negative breast cancers, underscoring its potential as a prognostic marker [60]. Exploiting the expression levels of these proteins, new cancer approaches can be explored, beyond their use as biomarkers: the quantification of MSH2 and MSH6 levels in tumour biopsy samples may provide insights into the underlying genetic landscape of the tumour. For example, a higher expression of MSH2 can be indicative of an intact MMR system and can be associated with sensitivity to compounds that interact with it [61]. A decreased expression or mutations in MSH2 and MSH6 can be associated with a defective MMR system, which may contribute to the resistance to specific drug therapies [62]. Targeting MutSα complex with antagonists (e.g. via siRNA or antisense technology for each protein), in combination with cytotoxic agents, can be at the basis of a new specific and targeted cancer therapy. Similarly, WDHD1 plays a crucial role in DNA replication, cell cycle regulation and maintaining genome integrity: it is involved in replication fork stability and chromosomal integrity, with recent data suggesting that its overexpression correlates with higher malignancy grades and metastasis in BC [63]. Specifically, the aberrant expression of WDHD1 has been correlated with increased cell proliferation, chemoresistance, and stemness properties of tumour cells, highlighting its role in tumour aggressiveness and patient survival. In addition, both WDHD1 silencing by siRNA and its depletion have been shown to delay proliferation in human cells cycle cells from late S phase to G2 [64]. ESRP1, an RNA splicing regulator, is gaining attention for its role in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a critical step in cancer metastasis [65]. Elevated ESRP1 levels have been linked to worse outcomes in luminal and HER2-positive BC subtypes, making it a valuable addition to the biomarker repertoire [66]. Specifically, ESRP1 has been identified as a crucial factor in determining the splicing patterns of CD44, where its expression levels are inversely correlated with the prevalence of the variant isoforms (CD44v), often associated with aggressive carcinomas and stem-like properties in BC [67]. This suggests that ESRP1 could serve as a potential biomarker for categorizing breast tumour aggressiveness and therapeutic responses [68]. These findings suggest that therapeutic strategies addressed to the modulation of ESRP1 activity can be tailored to reverse the splicing program that favours EMT and metastasis.

Future work will focus on the experimental validation of these findings across diverse patient cohorts and the development of diagnostic tools leveraging their specificity and clinical relevance.

In summary, this work intends to offer significant insights into the use of telomeric G-quadruplexes for biomarker discovery in breast cancer through proteomics and bioinformatics approaches. By functionally validating the results both in vitro and in patients, the findings could strengthen the use of G4s and the candidate biomarkers in BC diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy. In fact, in perspective applications, the potential of G4s is boundless. By combining these structures with cutting-edge technologies such as nanotechnology and microfluidics, a new generation of more sensitive and specific diagnostic devices could be developed. These advancements are particularly exciting for personalized medicine, where real-time monitoring of disease progression and therapy effectiveness is crucial.

Conclusions

In this study, we present a novel approach for the identification of BC biomarkers by leveraging a telomeric G-quadruplex multimeric structure (tel46) immobilized on a solid support for affinity purification-mass spectrometry. By integrating interactomics, quantitative proteomics, and multi-layered bioinformatics analyses, we identified a set of proteins specifically recognized by tel46 and significantly upregulated in BC cells. Among these, MSH6, MSH2, WDHD1, and ESRP1 emerged as the most promising candidate biomarkers, showing consistent overexpression in cancer cells and a strong association with unfavourable clinical outcomes.

These findings not only suggest the utility of telomeric G-quadruplexes as molecular tools for biomarker fishing but also highlight the mechanistic and diagnostic relevance of G4-interacting proteins in BC. The study provides a conceptual and methodological framework that can be extended to other cancer models. Ultimately, our data support the development of G4-based platforms for biomarker discovery and pave the way for the future implementation of these candidates in diagnostic or prognostic applications.
