Sickle cell visualization in vivo in humans: microvascular occlusion formation and hemorheological indices∗
Authors: Marisa M. Morakis, Luojie Huang, Gregory N. McKay, Sophie Lanzkron, Lydia H. Pecker, Nicholas J. Durr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Noninvasive in vivo blood imaging in humans reveals sickle cells initiate vaso-occlusion via endothelial interactions.•In vivo hemorheological indices capture changes in blood flow related to SCD and transfusion response.

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a monogenic autosomal recessive disorder caused by a mutation in the β-globin subunit of the hemoglobin protein. Sickle hemoglobin (HbS) polymerizes when deoxygenated, causing red blood cells (RBCs) to sickle, becoming misshapen, stiff, brittle, and sticky.1,2 Vaso-occlusion, a hallmark SCD pathology, results from a combination of (1) the physical obstruction of capillaries by sickled RBCs, reticulocytes, white blood cells (WBCs), and platelets and (2) chronic vasculopathy and inflammation.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 The mechanisms underlying vaso-occlusion are incompletely understood, including their initiation, duration, and dissipation. Besides the lack of comprehensive mechanistic insight, a challenge in developing therapies for SCD is that end points for clinical trials often rely on downstream clinical complications, including pain crisis frequency, which is subjective, or end-organ damage, which requires long-term monitoring. Biomarkers based on hematologic indices, such as hemoglobin or percent HbS, are commonly used as clinical end points8,9 but may not accurately indicate in vivo blood cell function.10, 11, 12 Identifying biomarkers that can consistently predict disease severity or specific complications for both clinical care and therapy trials has been challenging in SCD due to its highly heterogeneous phenotypes.

Most research on the pathobiology and hemorheology of SCD uses in vitro microfluidic models,3,13, 14, 15, 16, 17 ex vivo animal microvasculature,18 or transgenic mouse models that express human HbS.5,19 These methodologies allow visualization of flowing blood cells for rheological measurements such as viscosity, adhesion, and deformability of RBCs, which represent functional assessments of blood and are potential evaluation metrics of treatment efficacy. Rheological biomarkers reflect pathological differences between SCD genotypes,20,21 responsiveness to therapy,22, 23, 24 and risk of pain crises.25 Additionally, they help illuminate potential therapeutic targets.16,26 Visualization of flowing blood cells has also helped define our current understanding of vaso-occlusion mechanisms.3,5 However, knowledge of in vivo sickle cell behavior in humans has been limited by a lack of high-resolution, high-speed, label-free, and noninvasive imaging technologies. Although widefield absorption-based imaging techniques have been applied to human SCD microcirculation, these approaches lack the contrast to distinguish individual RBCs from each other or discern characteristic sickle cell morphologies.27, 28, 29

We present, to our knowledge, the first study to directly observe individual blood cell dynamics and SCD vaso-occlusions in vivo in human microvasculature using oblique back-illumination microscopy (OBM). OBM is a phase-contrast imaging technique for thick tissues30 that allows the visualization of transparent features, including high-resolution membrane contours and morphology of fast-flowing blood cells,31,32 as well as empty blood vessels. The oral mucosa is an optimal imaging site for noninvasive OBM imaging because it presents superficial capillaries without an absorbing melanin layer. We hypothesize that OBM in the ventral tongue will allow visualization of cellular events preceding vaso-occlusion, revealing the mechanism of occlusion formation. We also hypothesize that hemorheological analysis based on OBM imaging will capture hemodynamic changes in SCD compared to controls, as well as in patients with SCD after red cell transfusion therapy.

Methods

Participants and study design

We recruited adults with hemoglobin SS (n = 9) or hemoglobin Sβ0 thalassemia (n = 1) receiving chronic simple transfusions from the Johns Hopkins Sickle Cell Center for Adults. The protocol at the center is to perform blood removal for chronically transfused individuals presenting with a hemoglobin >8 g/dL and then transfuse 2 units of packed RBCs, targeting a hemoglobin of 10 g/dL and ideally maintaining HbS fraction <30%. We recruited sex-matched adults with no known hematologic abnormalities as controls. Hemoglobin electrophoresis confirmed their hemoglobin AA genotypes.

For each patient with SCD, we collected ten 90-second OBM videos across 2 imaging sessions: 5 from the session immediately before transfusion and 5 from the session immediately after transfusion. For each control participant, we collected ten 90-second OBM videos in a single imaging session. Within 2 days before transfusion, participants underwent a complete blood count, a hemoglobinopathy panel, and a reticulocyte count. We collected this same set of data immediately after imaging for posttransfusion sessions and control participants. We compared participant ages and hematologic indices with Student t tests. Pretransfusion to posttransfusion comparisons used paired tests, whereas SCD to control comparisons used unpaired tests. All participants gave written informed consent, and data were collected under a Johns Hopkins University institutional review board–approved protocol (IRB00264150) and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

OBM imaging system and acquisition

We designed the OBM imaging system to acquire synchronized green and red channels. Green LED light (530-nm peak) provides high contrast of RBCs due to hemoglobin absorption. Red LED light (660-nm peak) has a longer scattering mean free path length in biological tissues than green light, which provides enhanced phase contrast. Each capillary video was acquired at 100 frames per second and 3.1 megapixels. A 20×, 0.75 numerical aperture multi-immersion objective lens (Nikon; CFI Plan Fluor 20XC MI) provided a 440-μm field of view and a theoretical resolution of 431 nm. To correct for the background intensity gradient across the field of view, we divided each image by a Gaussian-filtered version of itself (σ = 50 pixels).30,31 To combine color channels, each frame was background corrected and registered, and the red channel was subtracted from the green channel to preserve phase information from red and absorption information from green. We performed contrast adjustments to enhance visibility. We estimated velocity of individual cells in select cases by measuring the trajectory and the number of frames the cell took to travel this distance. Details of the optical system and hardware setup are published31 and reported in the supplemental Methods and supplemental Figure 1.

We modified a portable ophthalmic slit lamp microscope cart to accommodate the OBM microscope, retaining the existing forehead and chin rests for head stabilization, as well as the low-friction table for positioning the imaging system (Figure 1). We 3-dimensionally printed a custom objective lens cap from stainless steel (Protolabs). The cap was surrounded by ports attached to a small vacuum pump to provide gentle suction to the ventral tongue for further stabilization. During imaging, participants placed their tongues on the objective cap. Using a piezo objective scanner (Thorlabs; PFM450E) for depth and the low-friction table for lateral translation, we positioned the microscope to focus on capillaries 0 to 200 μm deep into the ventral tongue surface. Once a shallow capillary bed was visible, participants held still, and the vacuum was switched on when the video recording started.Figure 1.OBM. Occlusion formation and dissipation characterization

We characterized microvascular occlusion formations observed in patients with SCD (Figure 2A; supplemental Videos 1 and 2). We defined an occlusion formation as a vessel transitioning from active RBC flow to a complete blockage by a visible buildup of cells. The initiation time point was the frame in which the first cell adhered at the occlusion site. The completion time point was the first frame in which the flow was completely stopped by cell buildup. The dissipation time point was the first frame in which RBCs began moving past the occlusion site. We further investigated the cells initiating observed occlusions. We defined occlusion-initiating cells as the first 3 cells to adhere in occlusion formation (if distinguishable). We evaluated occlusion-initiating cell morphology in comparison to control cells, defined as unaffected cells in no flow vessels of control participants. Under OBM, we qualitatively identified “suspected sickled cells” based on elongated shape,33 higher phase contrast from increased hemoglobin fiber density, and lack of cell deformation between frames (eg, blue-shaded cells in Figure 2). We also measured long-axis length and aspect ratio, defined as the ratio of short to long-axis length, of each occlusion-initiating cell to quantify size and shape. We performed 2-dimensional Gaussian regression to cluster the cells based on these 2 measurements. We further compared these measurements to those of control cells using a Student t test.

Hemorheological indices

Two graduate students with 3 years of experience in OBM and capillaroscopy (M.M.M. and L.H.) performed rheological analysis manually. Deidentification and random shuffling of videos before analysis ensured graders were blinded to participant group and condition. We categorized each vessel as either no flow, slow flow, or fast flow (Figure 3; supplemental Videos 5 and 6). We defined no flow as no visible movement of blood cells in the vessel throughout the video, which included both cell buildups and empty vessels; slow flow as partially occluded, intermittent, sluggish, or sparse blood flow; and fast flow as continuous, smooth flow with abundant blood cells. We presented flow categorization results per session as the percentage of vessels in each category of all observed vessels, with each session consisting of 5 to 10 90-second videos. For robust classification, only vessels visible for ≥50 frames (0.5 seconds) were included, and participants lacking usable vessels in the majority of their videos were excluded. Additionally, we quantified RBC adherence to the endothelium, defining adhered RBCs as RBCs that remained immobile within the vessel for ≥20 frames (0.2 seconds) while other cells flowed past (Figure 2B-C; supplemental Videos 3 and 4). To assess interrater reliability (IRR), we compared the evaluations of both graders on the same set of 191 vessels from a randomly selected participant, which included both pretransfusion and posttransfusion sessions. We calculated the IRR with a weighted Cohen κ for ordinal variables with quadratic weighting, in which a κ value of at least 0.6 represents substantial agreement and at least 0.8 represents near-perfect agreement.34 For appropriate statistical tests of compositional data, we applied a centered log-ratio transform to flow category percentages, x: centered log-ratio(xi) = log(xi/g(X)); g(X) = ∛(x1 × x2 × x3).35 We used nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to compare paired pretransfusion and posttransfusion sessions and Mann-Whitney rank-sum tests to compare patients with SCD and control participants. We defined statistical significance as α of .05 for all tests.

To assess relationships between hemorheological indices and standard SCD laboratory biomarkers, percent HbS, absolute reticulocyte count (ARC), and hemoglobin, we used simple linear regression. We calculated goodness of fit with the coefficient of determination (r2), as well as 95% confidence intervals for the slope and intercept of each regression line.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of 15 consented patients with SCD, 12 completed both imaging sessions with paired complete blood count, and 10 yielded acceptable image quality. Recruitment of patients with SCD was not sex selective, but the final 10 participants were female. All 10 consented sex-matched controls completed the study and yielded acceptable images. There was no significant difference between groups in participant age (SCD vs control, 34.50 ± 14.59 years vs 26.70 ± 4.06 years; P = .40). Indications for chronic transfusion therapy for each participant are in supplemental Table 1. One participant was taking hydroxyurea (1500 mg/d), having recently resumed therapy after chronic transfusions during pregnancy. Eight of 10 participants had at least 1 of the following: chronic kidney disease (n = 4), pulmonary hypertension (n = 3), cerebrovascular disease (n = 3), severe preeclampsia (n = 2), and leg ulcer (n = 1). No participants experienced pain crises or other adverse events during or shortly after participation in the study, and no participants requested to stop the procedure early due to any discomfort. Imaging sessions were efficient; the median time to capture data in all participants was 32.9 minutes (interquartile range, 31.2-38.1). Occlusion formation and dissipation

We observed 48 total occlusion formations (n = 37 before transfusion; n = 11 after transfusion). Although all 10 patients with SCD exhibited occlusions based on the existence of no flow vessels, new occlusions were observed forming during the observation period in 8 of 10 patients. Capillary diameters of vessels in which these occlusion formations occurred were 11.7 ± 1.93 μm.

All 48 observed occlusion events were initiated by adhesion of RBCs to the endothelium. Example occlusion formations are shown in supplemental Video 1 (with representative frames in Figure 2A) and in supplemental Video 2. Subsequent RBCs adhered to the initial adherent cell and adjacent endothelial cells. Occlusion-initiating cells had more elongated shapes than control cells (aspect ratio, 0.31 ± 0.14 vs 0.61 ± 0.20; P = 1.3 × 10−17). Distributions of occlusion-initiating cell aspect ratios and long-axis lengths were largely distinct from those of control cells (Figure 4B). Suspected sickle cells based on qualitative classification were the longest and had the lowest aspect ratios, showing very little overlap with control cells: suspected sickle cell aspect ratios were mostly <0.3, whereas control cells were mostly above this threshold. Following the occlusion-initiating cells, more RBCs and occasional WBCs accumulated until the capillary became occluded. The observed mechanism is depicted in Figure 4A. The time course of the formation and dissipation processes varied widely. For 20 cases in which full occlusion formation time was measurable, occlusions took between 0.81 and 52.32 seconds to form. Hemorheological indices

For a comprehensive study of hemorheological parameters related to the vaso-occlusion mechanisms we observed, we counted adhered RBCs and quantified vessels in each of 3 flow speed categories: no flow vessels represented overall occlusion frequency, slow flow vessels potentially indicated partial or developing occlusions, and fast flow vessels represented unobstructed vessels. Figure 5 shows flow speed percentage distributions in each experimental group, with data points corresponding to the participant taking hydroxyurea indicated. A mean of 91.0 ± 42.3 vessels was observed per session. Compared to controls, pretransfusion patients with SCD had lower percentages of fast flow (48.7% ± 17.39% vs 77.8% ± 11.54%; P = 5.8 × 10−4) and higher percentages of no flow vessels (16.1% ± 10.58% vs 2.4% ± 2.01%; P = .0010). From before to after transfusion in patients with SCD, percentages of fast flow vessels increased (48.7% ± 17.39% to 65.8% ± 17.39%; P = .0098), and percentages of no flow vessels decreased (16.1% ± 10.58% to 6.0% ± 5.40%; P = .0039). There were no differences in flow speed distributions between posttransfusion patients with SCD and controls. Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to visualize individual blood cell behavior, including vaso-occlusion formation, in the microvasculature of humans with SCD. Direct observation with OBM phase contrast shows that RBCs, which were often suspected sickle cells with elongated shapes, solely initiate occlusion through endothelial adhesion, with subsequent integration of additional RBCs and WBCs. Additionally, with proposed in vivo hemorheological indices, we observed significant differences in blood flow behavior and adhered RBC counts in patients with SCD vs controls and after transfusion. These data offer insight into SCD pathobiology and suggest that OBM may be a useful tool in measuring response to existing and novel therapeutic strategies.

This study offers a new perspective on SCD vaso-occlusion formation. Although RBC involvement is uncontested, the precise order of events leading up to vaso-occlusion remains unclear.36, 37, 38 In vitro1,39 and ex vivo18 experiments observed that adhesion of deformable RBCs, followed by trapping of dense sickle RBCs, drives occlusion.40 In contrast, murine SCD intravital microscopy observed WBC-endothelial adhesion as the trigger in 20- to 25-μm wide vessels. Such conflicting evidence may indicate that occlusions arise through context-dependent mechanisms: occlusions in smaller vessels may be RBC initiated, whereas those in larger vessels or those triggered by inflammation may be WBC initiated.37,41 We observed RBC-initiated occlusions in capillaries in vivo, including some transient occlusions42 that form or dissipate in seconds to 1 minute. The association of these occlusions with pain or ischemic damage remains unknown, especially because no patients were experiencing pain crises. Our findings may align with this context-dependent framework and do not exclude the possibility of WBC-initiated occlusions in other vascular or inflammatory settings. Knowledge of the cellular processes driving occlusions related to vaso-occlusive events, such as pain or acute chest syndrome, may inform the molecular targets of disease-modifying therapies and guide personalized strategies.

OBM-derived hemorheological indices show potential as new biomarkers of SCD severity. Hematologic indices, including protein biomarkers, have limited ability to clearly and reliably indicate SCD severity.12,43 Although the relationships of flow and adhered RBCs with HbS, ARC, and hemoglobin were relatively weak, these hemorheological indices clearly reflected transfusion-induced improvements and differences between controls and SCD. In vivo hemorheological indices likely capture complexity in microvascular function and cellular interaction that traditional hematologic indices do not. For example, plasma factors promoting downstream neutrophil activation may affect flow states.44 Although the relationship of OBM-based indices with clinical manifestations needs further research, similar measurements of adhesion and occlusion in vitro showed promising correlations with clinical outcomes such as pain crisis frequency.25 Occlusion frequency in a microfluidic capillary network was elevated in SCD blood compared to controls and correlated with percent HbS, consistent with our “no flow” observations.23,45 Other in vitro assays found elevated RBC adherence in SCD and associations with ARC, as we did.21,46 These parallels suggest that OBM may be well suited to extend applications of in vitro assays, such as evaluating treatment response and investigating genetic and phenotypic variability, to the in vivo setting.16,20, 21, 22,24,26,46,47 As an example, we observed low RBC adherence in the patient taking hydroxyurea. A larger study may reveal reduced adhesion receptor expression in response to hydroxyurea treatment.48

Phase contrast of OBM and the design of quantification metrics improve sensitivity in flow evaluation compared to previous studies. Although a study of SCD conjunctival microvasculature reported lower blood flow velocity than in controls,27 similar to what we observed, other sublingual microvasculature studies showed unaltered flow speeds in SCD.28,29 Although differing study populations and small samples may explain some variation, these studies also used average flow velocity or microvascular flow index, a metric based on subjective per-quadrant labels,49, 50, 51 to quantify flow speed. Averaging velocity may conceal underlying redistributions of flow speeds and differences in less frequent no flow vessels seen here. In another study, exchange transfusions increased vessel density and reduced average blood flow velocity,52 which contrasts with our observations of simple transfusions. This prior result may be influenced by both velocity averaging and exclusion of empty vessels, which are invisible under absorption-based microscopy but detectable with OBM (Figure 3D). Thus, observed increased vessel density could instead indicate renewed perfusion of empty vessels. OBM phase contrast also reveals individual cell boundaries, facilitating more direct measurement of flow speed.

Despite the advantages of in vivo hemorheological indices, patterns in the “slow” flow category remained difficult to interpret, as it showed little change across groups. Viscosity increases after transfusion could confound flow speed improvements from improved oxygenation of healthy RBCs.53 Some slow flow likely reflects normal microcirculatory regulation mechanisms, such as precapillary sphincters, pericytes, plasma skimming at bifurcations, and network geometry.54, 55, 56, 57, 58 Distinguishing slow speed, intermittent flow, and partial occlusion may help disentangle these compound impacts.

There are several limitations of this study. The study population was small and all female. This gender disparity was likely influenced by greater clinic attendance59 among females rather than a deliberate selection bias. Paired pretransfusion and posttransfusion HbS measurements were not available for all patients. Though ventral tongue imaging maximized OBM contrast in superficial capillaries, motion prevented continuous observation of occlusion dynamics over long periods. Manual analysis of videos is time-consuming and subjective, creating a barrier to widespread adoption of this technique. Only superficial capillaries are observed, even though previous research on vaso-occlusion has found that postcapillary venules are the site of clinically relevant blockages.5,18 The imaging procedure, particularly tongue protrusion onto a metal objective cap and applying gentle suction, may impact the microvascular behavior. Exploratory tests suggest the effect of the vacuum and body-temperature cap surface is minimal; toggling the vacuum and heating the cap with a warm water bath yielded no measured flow changes in a control participant. More comprehensive tests are necessary to definitively rule out the instrument’s impact. Finally, the correlation between microvasculature of the superficial tongue and other organs often injured in SCD, such as the brain, spleen, lungs, or kidneys, has yet to be established.

Future work will expand the study to verify the mechanisms and measurements described here in a broader and more diverse population, including males, untreated SCD groups, and patients with SCD on various treatments to assess their impacts on hemorheology. Pain crisis and organ damage outcomes will be collected in this cohort to help clinically validate OBM-derived hemorheological indices as biomarkers for disease complications. We will also collect oral health and smoking history information to better contextualize observed flow changes. Imaging postcapillary venules may be possible by increasing the illumination wavelength and by imaging different anatomical sites with more superficial venules. Imaging end organs commonly damaged in SCD may reveal more relevant disease pathology. Sites such as the inner lip32 will be explored for collecting longer and more stable videos. Stable videos will enable deep learning-based analysis60,61 to automate cell counting, flow quantification, and measurement of cellular and vascular morphology, eliminating the operator dependency of analysis. With datasets sampling large areas of each capillary bed, these methods can be extended to quantify vessel density.

This study demonstrates the potential of OBM as a noninvasive and label-free tool in monitoring SCD. We show that sickled RBCs initiate vaso-occlusions in previously unseen SCD dynamics. We measure in vivo hemorheological properties in SCD microvasculature, demonstrating an improved hemorheological state with faster flow, fewer occlusions, and fewer adhered RBCs induced by simple transfusion therapy. This approach offers a novel in vivo view of blood cell behavior and an opportunity to elucidate poorly understood aspects of SCD pathobiology. With a better understanding of the clinical outcomes associated with the indices and processes observed here, OBM could potentially guide future drug development, personalized therapy management, and evaluation of treatment efficacy.
