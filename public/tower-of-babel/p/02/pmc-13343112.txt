Psychological safety and shared decision-making for patient engagement in care quality
Authors: Tatsuya Fukami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient engagement is increasingly recognised as a core component of healthcare quality and safety. However, many patients remain reluctant to voice concerns, ask questions or participate meaningfully in clinical decisions. While patient safety initiatives have traditionally focused on preventing errors through system-level controls, less attention has been paid to the relational conditions that enable patients to contribute actively to safety and quality in everyday care.ObjectiveThis narrative review aims to conceptualise psychological safety and shared decision-making (SDM) as interdependent mechanisms that enable patient engagement and shape patient experience and to examine how their absence contributes to defensive medical practices.MethodsWe conducted a narrative review of interdisciplinary literature spanning patient safety science, quality improvement, patient experience research and organisational psychology. Conceptual frameworks and illustrative clinical examples were synthesised to clarify how psychological safety and SDM function together within routine clinical encounters.ResultsPsychological safety enables patients to express concerns, uncertainty and preferences without fear of dismissal or negative judgement, while SDM provides a structured process through which patient values and clinical evidence can jointly inform decisions. When these relational mechanisms are weak or absent, defensive medical practices—often framed as actions taken ‘just to be safe’—may proliferate. Although intended to reduce risk, such practices can undermine patient engagement by limiting dialogue, increasing anxiety and reinforcing paternalistic decision-making. In contrast, integrating psychological safety and SDM reframes patient engagement as an active safety mechanism that supports learning, transparency and trust.ConclusionsReframing patient engagement as a core safety and quality mechanism highlights the importance of psychological safety and SDM in everyday clinical practice. Rather than treating patient experience as a downstream outcome, healthcare organisations should recognise it as an integral component of safe, high-quality care. Embedding psychologically safe communication and SDM into routine decision-making may reduce reliance on defensive medicine while promoting safer, more participatory and more respectful healthcare.

Introduction

Patient experience has become a defining dimension of healthcare quality, shaping how care is evaluated, delivered and improved across settings.1 Beyond clinical outcomes or efficiency, patient experience reflects how individuals perceive and emotionally respond to their interactions with healthcare systems, encompassing respect, communication and partnership. International frameworks increasingly position patient experience alongside safety and effectiveness as a core pillar of high-quality care. Despite this recognition, a gap persists between the ideals of patient-centred care and patients’ lived experiences in everyday clinical encounters. Many patients remain hesitant to ask questions, express uncertainty or voice concerns, particularly in contexts characterised by time pressure, hierarchical relationships and information asymmetry.2 As a result, patient engagement is often promoted in principle but constrained in practice, with participation reduced to formal consent rather than meaningful dialogue. Patient safety initiatives have traditionally focused on preventing errors and system failures through reporting systems, protocols and checklists.38 While these approaches have improved standardisation and accountability, they largely conceptualise safety as a technical or organisational property of care systems. Far less attention has been paid to how safety is experienced by patients themselves—specifically, whether patients feel safe to speak up, disagree or participate actively in decisions affecting their care. From the patient perspective, safety is closely tied to perceptions of trust, respect and openness. Psychological safety, originating in organisational psychology, provides a useful lens for addressing this gap.9 Defined as the belief that one can take interpersonal risks without fear of negative consequences, psychological safety has been widely studied among healthcare professionals as a prerequisite for learning and error reporting. However, its relevance to patients has received less attention. For patients, psychological safety refers to feeling able to ask questions, disclose concerns and admit uncertainty without fear of dismissal or judgement. Shared decision-making (SDM) represents another cornerstone of patient-centred care, emphasising collaboration between patients and clinicians in integrating evidence with patient values.10 Although SDM is associated with improved satisfaction and decisional quality, it cannot function effectively in the absence of psychological safety. When patients do not feel safe to speak openly, SDM risks becoming procedural rather than participatory, reinforcing existing power asymmetries.11 The consequences of this disconnect are evident in defensive medicine.12 Practices justified as being ‘just to be safe’, such as unnecessary testing or overly cautious interventions, may be intended to reduce risk but can undermine patient experience by increasing anxiety, limiting dialogue and reinforcing paternalism. This review conceptualises psychological safety and SDM as interdependent foundations of patient engagement. By reframing patient engagement as a core safety mechanism, this article explores how psychologically safe communication and SDM can shift care from fear-driven decision-making towards dialogical, trust-based partnerships, contributing to the co-creation of patient experience, safety and quality. To clarify these relationships, we propose an integrated conceptual framework (figure 1) that illustrates how psychological safety enables patient expression, how SDM translates participation into action and how their interaction shapes patient engagement, experience and safety, as well as how their absence may contribute to defensive medical practices.

Integrated framework of psychological safety, shared decision-making and patient engagement. Psychological safety enables patients to express concerns, questions and preferences, while shared decision-making (SDM) translates this expression into collaborative decisions. Their interaction facilitates patient engagement, which functions as a mechanism supporting patient experience and safety. Conversely, low psychological safety and weak SDM give rise to a negative pathway characterised by defensive medical practices that limit dialogue and undermine engagement. Organisational context (culture, leadership, time constraints) influences all components of this framework.

Methods

This study employed a narrative review approach to synthesise interdisciplinary literature on patient safety, psychological safety, SDM and patient engagement. A targeted literature search was conducted using databases including PubMed and Scopus, supplemented by manual searches of reference lists from key articles.

Search terms included combinations of ‘patient safety’, ‘psychological safety’, ‘shared decision-making’, ‘patient engagement’, ‘patient experience’ and ‘defensive medicine’. The search focused primarily on literature published between 2000 and 2025, while including seminal earlier works where conceptually relevant.

Articles were selected based on their relevance to the conceptual relationships among psychological safety, SDM and patient engagement in clinical contexts. Given the conceptual nature of this review, priority was given to influential theoretical frameworks, empirical studies illustrating relational dynamics in healthcare and key policy documents. Studies were not excluded based on methodological design but were evaluated for their contribution to conceptual clarity.

The selected literature was iteratively reviewed and synthesised to develop an integrated conceptual framework. Particular attention was paid to identifying how psychological safety enables patient expression, how SDM translates participation into decision-making and how their interaction shapes patient experience and contributes to or mitigates defensive medical practices.

This review did not aim to provide a systematic or exhaustive synthesis but to develop a conceptual framework.

Conceptual foundations

Patient experience as a core dimension of healthcare quality

Patient experience has evolved from a focus on satisfaction and service quality to a central dimension of healthcare quality frameworks. Contemporary definitions emphasise how patients perceive care across the continuum, including communication, respect, emotional support and involvement in decision-making.13 14 Importantly, patient experience reflects not only what care is delivered but how it is experienced, capturing relational and contextual aspects that are often overlooked by clinical outcome measures. Although international frameworks position patient experience alongside safety and effectiveness, it is frequently operationalised through surveys and metrics that oversimplify interpersonal dynamics. Such measures may assess satisfaction or information provision, but often fail to capture whether patients feel safe to speak up, ask questions or challenge decisions. From the patient perspective, safety and experience are inseparable: care may be technically error-free yet experienced as unsafe if communication is dismissive or rushed. Conversely, encounters characterised by openness and dialogue can foster trust and engagement even in the presence of uncertainty. Conceptualising patient experience as an experiential and relational phenomenon therefore requires moving beyond procedural indicators towards constructs that capture how safety is perceived and enacted in everyday clinical interactions.

Psychological safety in the patient context

Psychological safety, originally conceptualised within organisational psychology, refers to a shared belief that individuals can take interpersonal risks without fear of negative consequences.9 In healthcare, it has been widely applied to teams to support speaking up about errors, near misses and uncertainties.15 However, its relevance extends beyond professional teams to include patients as active participants in care.

In the patient context, psychological safety can be understood as the extent to which patients feel comfortable expressing concerns, admitting uncertainty and engaging in dialogue with clinicians. This includes feeling able to ask ‘naïve’ questions, disclose symptoms that may seem trivial or embarrassing and voice preferences that differ from clinical recommendations. When psychological safety is present, patients are more likely to share critical information and to engage meaningfully in decisions, thereby enriching both safety and experience. The absence of psychological safety, by contrast, may silence patients. Hierarchical dynamics, time constraints and implicit norms of deference can discourage patients from speaking up, even when they sense that something is wrong. From the patient’s perspective, such environments may feel unsafe not because of overt harm, but because of the perceived risk of being dismissed, judged or labelled as ‘difficult’. These dynamics directly shape patient experience and may have downstream consequences for diagnostic accuracy, adherence and trust. Conceptualising psychological safety as a patient-centred construct shifts the focus from individual confidence to relational conditions created by clinicians and organisations.16 Communication style, responsiveness to questions and openness to uncertainty all signal whether speaking up is welcomed or discouraged. As such, psychological safety functions as an experiential prerequisite for patient engagement.

Importantly, psychological safety in clinical encounters is not solely shaped by clinicians but is co-constructed through interactions between patients and healthcare professionals. While clinicians play a central role in creating conditions that invite participation, patients also contribute to the relational environment through trust, openness and honesty in communication. When patients feel able to share concerns, ask questions and express preferences authentically, they help sustain a dialogical space that supports mutual understanding. Recognising this bidirectional nature does not diminish the responsibility of clinicians; rather, it highlights psychological safety as an emergent property of relationships, requiring engagement from both sides to enable meaningful participation and SDM.

SDM as an experiential process

SDM is widely regarded as a cornerstone of patient-centred care, defined by collaborative processes in which clinicians and patients jointly consider clinical evidence, risks and benefits, and patient values.17 While SDM is often framed as a methodological or ethical ideal, it is also an experiential process that shapes how patients perceive their role within care encounters. Effective SDM depends on patients’ ability to articulate preferences, ask clarifying questions and express uncertainty. These behaviours, however, are contingent on psychological safety. Without a sense of interpersonal safety, SDM risks becoming a formalised ritual—information is presented, consent is obtained, but genuine dialogue remains limited. In such cases, the appearance of SDM may mask persistent power asymmetries and constrained patient participation. When psychological safety and SDM are aligned, clinical encounters can shift towards dialogical partnerships. Patients experience care as collaborative rather than directive, and decisions as negotiated rather than imposed. This alignment enhances patient experience by fostering transparency, respect and mutual understanding. Conversely, misalignment—such as attempts at SDM in psychologically unsafe environments—may heighten patient anxiety and erode trust. Viewing SDM through the lens of patient experience highlights its relational nature.18 It is not solely a technique to be implemented, but a process that unfolds within communicative spaces shaped by safety, trust and openness. As such, SDM serves as a critical mechanism through which psychological safety is translated into meaningful patient engagement.

Integrating psychological safety and SDM

Taken together, psychological safety and SDM form an interdependent conceptual foundation for patient experience. Psychological safety enables patients to participate; SDM provides the structure through which participation is realised. Neither is sufficient on its own. Psychological safety without SDM may allow expression without influence, while SDM without psychological safety may remain superficial. This integrated perspective offers a lens for understanding why patient engagement initiatives sometimes fall short despite formal commitments to patient-centred care. Engagement is not merely a matter of providing information or inviting questions, but of creating relational conditions that make participation possible and meaningful. By situating psychological safety and SDM within patient experience, this framework reframes engagement as a core safety mechanism embedded in everyday interactions.

Safety mechanisms and experience: beyond traditional patient safety

Patient safety has traditionally been conceptualised as the prevention of errors, adverse events and system failures.19 Over recent decades, healthcare organisations have invested heavily in reporting systems, protocols and checklists to reduce harm, reinforcing the view of safety as a technical and organisational property of care systems. While these approaches remain essential, they provide an incomplete account of how safety is experienced by patients in everyday clinical encounters. From the patient perspective, safety is defined not only by the absence of error, but also by the presence of trust, openness and opportunities to participate meaningfully in care. A technically reliable process may still feel unsafe if communication is rushed, opaque or dismissive, whereas transparent and attentive interactions can foster a sense of safety even amid clinical uncertainty. Traditional safety mechanisms tend to prioritise what can be measured—such as incident rates or protocol compliance—while overlooking relational dynamics that shape patient experience. This focus implicitly positions patients as passive recipients of safety rather than active contributors.20 Psychological safety and SDM offer an alternative perspective by framing safety as an experiential and relational phenomenon. Psychological safety enables patients to voice concerns, uncertainty and symptoms that may otherwise remain unspoken, while SDM provides a structured process through which these contributions can influence clinical reasoning and decisions. Through these interactions, safety emerges not only through control, but through dialogue. Experiential safety is dynamic and context-dependent, shaped by subtle communicative cues such as tone, responsiveness and willingness to acknowledge uncertainty. When such cues discourage participation, patients may withhold information or comply without understanding, increasing the risk of misalignment between care delivered and patient needs. Expanding the concept of safety to include patient experience therefore complements, rather than replaces, system-level protections. When supported by psychological safety and SDM, patient engagement itself functions as a safety mechanism, providing critical information and perspective that can prevent errors and clarify uncertainty.21 22 This reframing also provides important context for understanding defensive medicine, where fear-driven actions taken ‘just to be safe’ may paradoxically undermine both patient experience and engagement.

Defensive medicine and patient experience

Defensive medicine is commonly defined as clinical practices undertaken primarily to avoid litigation or criticism rather than to advance patient benefit.23 These practices often include ordering additional diagnostic tests, performing interventions of marginal clinical value, or adhering rigidly to protocols in the absence of individualised discussion. Defensive actions are frequently justified as being ‘just to be safe’, reflecting clinicians’ desire to minimise risk in uncertain or high-stakes situations. From a systems perspective, defensive medicine is often discussed in terms of cost, overuse and inefficiency. However, its implications for patient experience have received far less attention. For patients, defensive practices are not experienced as abstract risk management strategies, but as concrete encounters that shape trust, understanding and emotional response. Additional tests or interventions may be interpreted as reassuring, but they may also generate confusion, anxiety or a sense of loss of control when their purpose is not clearly explained or meaningfully discussed. Defensive medicine can undermine patient experience by constraining dialogue. When clinical decisions are driven primarily by fear of error or blame, opportunities for SDM may be reduced. Clinicians may default to standardised actions rather than engaging patients in conversations about uncertainty, trade-offs and preferences. In such encounters, patients may feel excluded from decision-making processes that directly affect their bodies and lives, reinforcing perceptions of paternalism even in otherwise well-intentioned care. The experiential consequences of defensive medicine are particularly evident in diagnostic contexts. For example, the ordering of imaging studies ‘to rule out rare but serious conditions’ may escalate patient anxiety, especially when incidental findings are identified.12 Without psychologically safe communication, patients may hesitate to ask about the necessity or implications of these tests, leading to prolonged uncertainty and distress. In this way, actions intended to reduce clinical risk may paradoxically increase emotional burden and erode trust. Defensive practices also interact with power asymmetries inherent in clinical relationships. Patients may perceive defensive decisions as non-negotiable, particularly when framed as the safest or only acceptable option. When psychological safety is limited, patients are less likely to voice preferences such as declining low-value tests or expressing concern about potential harms. As a result, defensive medicine may perpetuate compliance without understanding, reducing patient engagement and diminishing the quality of the care experience. Importantly, defensive medicine should not be understood solely as an individual clinician behaviour, but as a response to broader organisational and cultural conditions.24 Environments characterised by blame, fear of scrutiny and intolerance of uncertainty may implicitly reward defensive actions while discouraging open dialogue. In such contexts, both clinicians and patients may experience reduced psychological safety, narrowing the space for SDM and mutual learning. Viewing defensive medicine through the lens of patient experience reveals its limitations as a safety strategy. While defensive actions may offer short-term reassurance to clinicians, they often fail to address patients’ needs for understanding, participation and trust. In contrast, approaches grounded in psychological safety and SDM invite patients into conversations about risk and uncertainty, enabling more nuanced and context-sensitive decisions. These dialogical approaches may not eliminate uncertainty, but they can transform how uncertainty is experienced and managed. This analysis suggests that defensive medicine represents not only a technical or economic problem, but also an experiential one. By prioritising fear-driven actions over relational engagement, defensive practices risk undermining the very sense of safety they seek to achieve. While such actions may reduce clinicians’ perceived risk, they often initiate cascades of incidental findings (incidentalomas), leading to further tests and prolonged uncertainty. For patients, this process may not feel reassuring but instead increase anxiety, confusion and a diminished sense of control. When psychological safety is limited, patients may feel unable to question the necessity or implications of these investigations, resulting in passive acceptance rather than informed participation. An integrated framework: psychological safety and SDM as drivers of patient experience

Psychological safety and SDM function together as drivers of patient experience and engagement. Psychological safety creates conditions for expression, while SDM translates participation into action. Their interaction shapes whether care is experienced as collaborative or hierarchical. In the absence of these relational drivers, fear-driven practices such as defensive medicine are more likely to emerge. Organisational culture and leadership play critical roles in sustaining psychologically safe, SDM-oriented practice across care settings.

Practical implications for clinical practice

Operationalising psychologically safe, dialogical care requires attention to everyday interactions. Clinicians can foster psychological safety by inviting questions, acknowledging uncertainty and responding non-defensively. SDM should be embedded into routine workflows, extending beyond treatment choices to diagnostic and monitoring decisions. Addressing defensive medicine requires reframing ‘just-to-be-safe’ actions as opportunities for dialogue. At organisational level, training, measurement systems and leadership support are essential to sustain psychologically safe, SDM-oriented practice.

Fostering psychological safety in clinical encounters

Psychological safety for patients is fostered through consistent communication rather than isolated interventions. Clinicians can support it by inviting questions, acknowledging uncertainty and responding non-defensively to concerns. Brief practices—such as asking what matters most, normalising uncertainty and validating emotions—signal that speaking up is welcome. How clinicians respond to disagreement or hesitation is equally important: dismissive reactions may silence future engagement, whereas curiosity and openness reinforce trust. Over time, repeated experiences of being heard and respected shape patients’ willingness to participate, influencing patient experience across care episodes.

Embedding SDM into routine care

SDM is most effective when integrated into routine workflows. This includes presenting options in balanced language, discussing benefits and harms, and linking recommendations to patient values. Decision aids can help, but their usefulness depends on psychological safety that allows patients to ask questions and express preferences. SDM should extend beyond treatment choices to diagnostic and monitoring decisions, including conversations about whether additional testing is necessary to reduce defensive practices while supporting understanding and confidence.

Addressing defensive medicine through dialogue

Reducing defensive medicine requires shifting from fear-driven actions to relational engagement. Clinicians can reframe ‘just-to-be-safe’ decisions as dialogue, explaining rationale, limitations and potential consequences, and inviting patient preferences to inform choices. Organisational leaders can legitimise these approaches by fostering cultures that tolerate uncertainty and prioritise learning over blame, enabling clinicians to communicate openly without fear that transparency will be penalised.

Organisational supports and training

Sustaining psychologically safe, SDM-oriented practice requires alignment at the organisational level. Training programmes that emphasise communication skills, reflective practice and relational competencies can strengthen clinicians’ capacity to create safe spaces for patient engagement.25 26 Measurement systems should also evolve to capture experiential aspects of safety, such as patients’ perceived ability to speak up and participate in decisions. Leadership commitment is essential. When leaders model openness, support learning from uncertainty and recognise patient engagement as a safety strategy, these values are reinforced throughout the organisation. Such alignment enables the integrated framework to move from concept to routine practice.

Implications for patient engagement and system design

Patient engagement should be understood as a relational process embedded in routine care. Systems focused solely on efficiency and risk control may constrain dialogue, whereas learning-oriented systems better support psychological safety. Mechanisms such as patient reporting systems are meaningful only when patients trust that their voices will be heard. Aligning workflows and performance measures to legitimise SDM can reduce defensive practices and position patient engagement as a driver of quality improvement.

Limitations

This narrative review is a conceptual synthesis and does not present new empirical data. Its interpretations are based on existing literature and illustrative examples, which may not capture all context-specific variations in clinical practice. In addition, evidence on psychological safety has largely focused on healthcare professionals; further research is needed to develop patient-centred measures and to examine how psychological safety and SDM relate to patient experience across diverse care settings.

Conclusion

Patient experience is inseparable from how safe patients feel to engage in their care. Psychological safety and SDM are foundational elements of meaningful patient engagement. Integrating these elements shifts care towards dialogical, trust-based partnerships and reframes patient engagement as a core safety mechanism that supports learning and adaptability in the face of clinical uncertainty.
