Water, sanitation, and hygiene challenges in last-mile artisanal mining communities in Ghana and Uganda
Authors: David Owiredu, Hanna Chidwick, Betty Kwagala, Deborah Mensah, Lydia Osei, Lydia Kapiriri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAccess to water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) is critical for public health but remains inadequate in marginalized areas, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa's artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM) communities. Adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) in these settings face unique challenges that impact their health and wellbeing.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess WASH access among adolescent girls and young women (aged 10–24) in last-mile ASM communities in Ghana and Uganda, identifying disparities and factors influencing access.MethodsA cross-sectional, mixed-methods design was employed between March and May 2022 in selected ASM communities in Ghana and Uganda. The quantitative component included a sample of 1618 AGYW (808 in Ghana, 810 in Uganda) recruited through random household selection. Data were collected using interviewer-administered questionnaires adapted from validated sources, covering socio-demographics, water sources, sanitation, and hygiene practices. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and logistic regression were conducted, stratified by country, to examine associations between WASH access and sociodemographic factors. For the qualitative component, AGYW, community leaders, district officers, policymakers, and global experts were purposively selected. Data was collected through focus group discussions and in-depth/key informant interviews conducted in local languages. Thematic analysis was performed using NVivo 12, with illustrative participant quotes.ResultsQuantitative findings showed that 86.2 % reported access to improved water sources, but only 10.1 % had access to improved toilet facilities. In Ghana, 83 % lacked any toilet facility; in Uganda, 65 % used unimproved latrines. Water access was associated with religion and education in Ghana, and toilet access was linked to residence and wealth in both countries. Qualitative findings revealed concerns about water quality, reliability, distance to water points, and major sanitation challenges, especially for women and girls. Cultural norms and mining-related environmental impacts further exacerbated WASH vulnerabilities.ConclusionSignificant disparities in WASH persist in ASM communities, particularly for sanitation. Context-specific, community-engaged interventions are urgently needed to address these gaps and promote health equity for AGYW in rural mining settings.

Background

Water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) services are essential for promoting public and environmental health, as well as social and economic development [1,2]. Despite considerable progress over recent decades, an estimated 2.2 billion people globally still lack safely managed drinking water, 3.6 billion lack safely managed sanitation, and 2.3 billion cannot wash their hands with soap and water at home [3]. These deficits disproportionately affect low-income and marginalized communities, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where rapid population growth, urbanization, and environmental challenges exacerbate existing inequalities [4]. In SSA alone, approximately 400 million people lack access to basic drinking water services, and over 700 million lack basic sanitation [5]. This regional disparity places millions at heightened risk of waterborne diseases and poor hygiene-related infections.

In Ghana and Uganda, access to WASH services presents persistent challenges, shaped by geography, socioeconomics, and infrastructure development. In Ghana, about 22 % of the population lacks access to basic drinking water, and 21 % still practice open defecation, primarily in rural and peri-urban areas [6]. Urban centers have seen improvements in WASH infrastructure, yet rural communities and urban informal settlements continue to face disparities, grappling with inadequate facilities, water scarcity, and poor hygiene conditions. Uganda faces similar challenges [6]. Only 19 % of rural households have access to basic sanitation, and nearly 38 % rely on unimproved water sources [6].

Over the past two decades, both Ghana and Uganda have made progress in WASH access, supported by increased government spending and national strategies [7]. Ghana's National Community Water and Sanitation Programme and Uganda's Rural Water Supply and Sanitation Program have contributed to expanded rural water coverage and improved infrastructure [8,9]. Yet, funding gaps and maintenance challenges persist, slowing sustained improvements and leaving many communities underserved. Non-governmental organizations (NGOs) have played a crucial role in bridging these gaps, implementing community-based projects, constructing water points and latrines, and promoting hygiene education, especially in rural and hard-to-reach areas.

A major contributor to WASH challenges in many rural areas of SSA, including Ghana and Uganda, is the rapid change in land-use patterns, particularly the expansion of artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM) activities [[10], [11], [12]]. ASM employs millions across SSA [13], approximately 1 million in Ghana [14], and between 400,000 to 600,000 in Uganda [15] directly engaged in mining. Millions more depend indirectly on the sector [13,16]. These activities typically occur in remote rural areas where infrastructure is minimal. The environmental impacts of ASM, including deforestation [17], soil erosion [18], contamination of water bodies with chemicals such as mercury and cyanide [19], and alteration of watercourses [20], have led to the degradation of natural resources essential for safe water and sanitation. This degradation directly threatens community access to improved water sources and sanitation facilities, compounding existing WASH deficits.

Cultural factors also shape WASH outcomes [21]. In many communities, traditional norms assign water collection to women and girls, affecting their education and health [22]. Taboos around sharing sanitation facilities and beliefs about water treatment influence the adoption and use of improved WASH practices [23].

Moreover, ASM-driven migration to mining areas intensifies pressure on already limited WASH services [24,25]. The influx of miners and their families often overwhelms local infrastructure, leading to overcrowding, increased waste generation, and heightened risk of waterborne and sanitation-related diseases [26]. Many ASM communities lack adequate waste management and sanitation infrastructure, resulting in open defecation and contamination of water sources [27]. These conditions perpetuate cycles of disease transmission and poor health outcomes. These challenges are particularly acute for vulnerable groups such as women and children, who bear disproportionate burdens related to water collection, hygiene maintenance, and exposure to environmental hazards.

The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated WASH challenges globally [28]. The pandemic exposed the critical importance of hand hygiene and access to clean water for disease prevention. However, many rural areas, including ASM communities, faced worsened conditions due to disruptions in water supply chains, reduced funding for WASH programs, and limited access to hygiene products during lockdowns and travel restrictions [7,29]. In Ghana and Uganda, where many last-mile populations rely on unimproved water sources and inadequate sanitation, the pandemic amplified vulnerabilities and highlighted systemic inequities in WASH access [[30], [31], [32]].

The public health implications of inadequate WASH services are significant in any context. Poor water quality and sanitation contribute to high rates of diarrheal diseases, parasitic infections, and other communicable diseases [33], which remain leading causes of morbidity and mortality in SSA [34]. Children under five are especially vulnerable, with diarrheal diseases accounting for a substantial proportion of child deaths in the region [35,36]. Additionally, poor WASH conditions exacerbate malnutrition and impede educational attainment, thereby perpetuating cycles of poverty and inequality [37].

A significant proportion of the poor in Ghana and Uganda reside in rural areas, many of which overlap with ASM sites. The intersection of poverty, environmental degradation, and inadequate WASH infrastructure creates a complex public health crisis. ASM operations, while providing livelihoods, often perpetuate environmental damage [13] that undermines access to essential WASH services, disproportionately affecting already marginalized populations.

Efforts to improve WASH access in Ghana, Uganda, and across SSA align closely with Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 6, which aims to ensure availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all [38]. Progress towards SDG 6 is also intrinsically linked to SDG 3 (good health and well-being) and SDG 11 (sustainable cities and communities), emphasizing the interconnectedness of health, environment, and development. International organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO), UNICEF, and the United Nations (UN) highlight the urgency of addressing inequalities in WASH access, particularly among vulnerable and marginalized populations [1,29] such as those in ASM communities.

In line with the WHO 2018–2025 WASH Strategy [2], this study speaks directly to strategic objectives to expand equitable access to safely managed water and sanitation services, promote universal health coverage, and improve health through safe WASH management. This study focuses on last-mile populations residing in ASM communities in Ghana and Uganda, where limited access to WASH services and environmental degradation converge to create heightened public health risks. By evaluating the level of WASH access and existing disparities in these settings, the research aims to inform targeted strategies and policies that promote equitable, sustainable improvements in WASH infrastructure and practices. Such efforts are vital for reducing health disparities, enhancing quality of life, and achieving the SDGs in some of the most underserved and vulnerable populations in SSA.

Methods

Study design

This was a mixed methods study that adopted the convergent approach. The quantitative component employed a cross-sectional design, while the qualitative component followed a general qualitative descriptive approach. Quantitative and qualitative data were collected simultaneously to determine access to WASH facilities and to gather insights into the nuances and perceptions surrounding WASH challenges in the study communities as part of a broad project evaluating the economic and health impacts of the COVID-19 Pandemic on adolescent girls working in the ASM sector in Uganda and Ghana.

Study sites and study population

The study was conducted in artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM) communities in Bamboi (Savannah Region) and Chingakrom (Bono East Region) in Ghana, as well as Busia and Namayingo (Eastern Region), and Kassanda and Mubende (Central Region) in Uganda. These sites were selected for their strong representation of rural areas with active ASM engagement. Bamboi and Chingakrom are largely rural, with populations engaged in subsistence farming and mining [39]. The Savannah Region features dry grasslands, while Bono East combines forest and farmland. In Uganda, the eastern districts border Lake Victoria and rely on farming and fishing, while the central districts are rural with notable ASM and agricultural activity.

The selected communities are predominantly made up of adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) aged 10 to 24, who play key roles in household and community life. In ASM settings, they may be directly involved in mining activities such as digging, washing, or processing minerals, or providing support services like food vending, domestic work, or informal trading. Despite their contributions to household income, AGYW often face socio-economic challenges, including limited access to education, healthcare, and social services, as well as increased risks of exploitation and gender-based violence within the male-dominated ASM sector [38,[40], [41], [42]].

Eligibility criteria for inclusion

Eligible participants for the quantitative survey were AGYW aged 10 to 24, living and working in the study communities, and either directly engaged in mining or providing services to ASM workers. For the qualitative part of this study, in addition to eligible AGYW, older women, local community leaders, and relevant public officials were eligible for selection. Prospective participants who declined consent or whose parental assent could not be obtained were excluded.

Sample size estimation and sampling procedures

The sample size was calculated using Yamane's formula [43], accounting for a design effect of 2 and an anticipated 98 % non-response rate. Based on an estimated AGYW population of 40,000 to 50,000 in ASM communities across Ghana and Uganda (according to unpublished regional estimates), a target of 808 participants per country was set. Local guides and the research team collaborated with community leaders to compile a comprehensive list of households with eligible participants. Households were randomly selected, and one participant per household was chosen. In households with multiple eligible individuals, one was randomly selected.

For the qualitative component, purposive sampling was used to recruit participants who could offer detailed insights into AGYW experiences and resilience. Key informant interviews included five community leaders, five district officers, five policymakers per country, and ten global experts on adolescent health and resilience. Additionally, ten AGYW per country were selected to reflect diverse backgrounds for in-depth interviews (IDIs).

Data collection

Quantitative methods

Quantitative data were collected through interviewer-administered questionnaires with eligible participants in randomly selected households. The research team, accompanied by local guides, visited households and obtained informed consent or assent before administering the questionnaire. The questionnaire was part of a comprehensive baseline assessment designed to capture a wide range of topics relevant to adolescent girls and young women in artisanal and small-scale mining communities, including background characteristics, artisanal work, sexual and reproductive health, experiences of violence, and maternal health. For the present analysis, only items pertaining to WASH were extracted from the full instrument. The WASH subset comprised 6 items, covering main source of drinking water, type and location of toilet facilities. The questionnaire items were adapted from the validated DHS women's questionnaire and translated into local languages to ensure clarity and engagement. The internal consistency of the entire questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach's alpha, which yielded a value of 0.76, indicating acceptable reliability for this domain. The broader questionnaire included over 80 items in total, but only the WASH-related items were analyzed for this study. A pilot survey was conducted in two rural ASM communities in each country with similar demographics to the study sites.

Qualitative methods

For the qualitative component, eight FGDs were conducted in each country, with each group comprising 8–12 discussants. Three discussions were held for each group, each lasting 90 min. The sessions were conducted entirely in local languages to ensure the discussants' comfort and comprehension. All sessions were audio recorded to accurately capture responses and to facilitate data analysis. KIIs and IDIs were guided by questions and talking points collectively put together by the research team and were informed by literature.

Data management and analysis

Survey responses were checked for completeness and exported to Stata 17 for analyses. A wealth index variable was generated using principal component analysis (PCA) based on household assets and materials, then categorized into five wealth quintiles. Drinking water and toilet facilities were classified as ‘improved’ or ‘unimproved’ according to WHO/UNICEF classifications [[44], [45], [46]].

Descriptive statistics summarized sociodemographic characteristics and WASH access. Chi-square tests examined associations between variables and WASH outcomes. Separate logistic regression models were used to assess the relationship between sociodemographic factors and access to improved water and toilet facilities, adjusting for district, residence, age, religion, education, wealth, marital status, and type of work. Analyses were stratified by country, with results reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs).

Qualitative data were analyzed thematically using NVivo 12. Qualitative data were analyzed thematically using NVivo 12. Audio recordings from FGDs, KIIs, and IDIs were transcribed verbatim and translated into English where necessary. The research team familiarized themselves with the transcripts, then applied inductive coding in NVivo 12 to identify key patterns and concepts related to WASH challenges. Codes were reviewed and grouped into themes that captured participants' experiences and perspectives. Coding was conducted independently by two researchers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Themes were refined iteratively and illustrated with narrative summaries and representative participant quotes.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from institutional review boards in Ghana (University of Ghana ECH109/22–23), Uganda (TASO 2022–169, UNCST SS149ES), and Canada (McMaster MREB #6257). Informed consent was obtained from adult participants, and assent from minors with parental or guardian consent. Emancipated minors were allowed to consent independently.

Results

In this study, 1618 participants, 49.9 % from Ghana Uganda (50.1 %), Across both countries, over 40 % of the participants indicated that they resided permanently at their primary mining sites. The majority were aged 20–24 years (50.0 %), and 41.0 % were 15–19 years old. Most were Christian (77.1 %), with 45.2 % having primary education and 36.0 % having secondary or higher education. Wealth distribution was fairly even across quintiles, and 63.4 % of participants were single. In terms of employment, 48.7 % provided services to miners, while 24.9 % engaged in gold panning. More than half (55.1 %) of the participants earned less than USD 70 monthly.

The challenges related to access to improved drinking water, sanitation facilities, and hygiene practices were consistent across the study communities in Ghana and Uganda. In both countries, participants reported similar issues, including contaminated water sources, inadequate sanitation facilities, and insufficient hygiene practices. Despite the shared struggles, there were differences in the specifics of the challenges and the coping mechanisms employed by community members.

Quantitative results

Access to improved water

Quantitative data showed that over half (57 %) of the survey participants obtained drinking water from boreholes (see Fig. 1). Pipe-borne water was the next prominent source of water (16.1 %) followed by mineral water packaged in plastic bottles or sachets (5.8 %). 4.7 %, 3.5 %, and 3.3 % of the participants relied on unprotected dug wells, rivers, and lakes respectively, for drinking water. Thus, in the present survey sample, 86.2 % (95 % CI: 84.4 to 87.8) obtained their drinking water from improved sources as delineated by the WHO/UNICEF classification. Broadly, access to improved drinking water sources differed slightly between Ghana and Uganda (88.1 vs. 84.2). Access to toilet facilities

In this sample of AGYW surveyed, nearly 83 % of those in Ghana reported having no access to toilet facilities. In Uganda, 65.9 % used unimproved latrines, compared to just 5.9 % in Ghana (Fig. 4). The most used improved facility was the Ventilated Improved Pit (VIP) latrine (9.7 %), while only 0.25 % of participants had access to flush toilets. Overall, just 10.1 % (95 % CI: 8.8–11.7) of respondents across both countries had access to improved toilet facilities. Bivariate analyses (Table 1) showed significant disparities between access to improved toilet facilities and place of residence, education, and wealth in both Ghana and Uganda. In Ghana, only 2.3 % of individuals in mining settlements had access to improved toilets compared to 21.8 % in community areas (Chi2 = 75.71, p < 0.001); similar patterns were observed in Uganda, where access was significantly higher in community areas compared to mining settlements (p < 0.001).

Education level significantly influenced access in both countries. In Uganda, 10.2 % of those with secondary or higher education had improved toilets, versus none among those with no formal education. Wealth-based disparities were pronounced in Ghana, where 33.9 % of individuals in the richest quintile had access, compared to only 1.6 % in the poorest (Chi2 = 125.3, p < 0.001). However, in Uganda, access remained low across all wealth quintiles: 6.3 % (Poorest), 11.8 % (Poorer), 6.2 % (Middle), 7.2 % (Richer), and 11.6 % (Richest).

In Ghana's adjusted model (supplementary file s1), access to improved toilet facilities remained significantly associated with community residence (aOR = 3.13, 95 % CI: 1.33–7.34, p = 0.009), secondary education (aOR = 2.54, 95 % CI: 1.25–5.16, p = 0.010), and wealth (richest vs. poorest aOR = 14.54, 95 % CI: 4.46–47.39, p < 0.001). Interestingly, married individuals had lower odds of access compared to the never married (aOR = 0.44, 95 % CI: 0.19–0.99, p = 0.046).

In Uganda, work type emerged as the main determinant. Individuals involved in mining (aOR = 11.09, 95 % CI: 2.76–44.60), panning (aOR = 6.02, 95 % CI: 1.65–21.99), and services to miners (aOR = 4.78, 95 % CI: 1.21–18.89) had significantly higher odds of access compared to those involved in collecting or grinding stones. Unlike in Ghana, no significant associations were found with wealth or education in Uganda's adjusted model.

Qualitative results

Access to improved source of drinking water

In both Ghana and Uganda, access to clean drinking water was a major concern. Participants from both countries described contaminated water sources as the primary option for their communities. For instance, a young girl from Bugiri in Uganda explained, “We don't have water. There is a shallow well down there, but the users are many. When it rains, the water becomes dirty, and it is the very one we use.” (YG_BG_1) Similarly, an adolescent girl from Kintampo South in Ghana remarked, “Yeah. It's not easy to get access to clean water. Let me say the water which you go and fetch. You see, those stuff like frogs in the water. But you're supposed to fetch it because there's no other water over here.” (A_KT_1).

A boy from Namayingo described, “Even the boreholes we use for safe water are not within. They are in distant places.”(B_Na_1). In contrast, while some communities in Ghana had access to pipe-borne water, participants noted that its supply was often unreliable. An older woman from Kintampo South recounted, “It's now that we've gotten a pipe-borne water. As for the pipe too sometimes you can't get some when the machine becomes faulty.”(OW_K_1) Contaminated water sources were also commonly mentioned. A boy from Namayingo noted, “Even at the pond, when you feel you want to urinate you just do it there and if another one wants to drink, he just takes the very water.” (B_Na_1). Using such contaminated water sources was described as necessary due to the lack of alternatives.

Access to improved toilet facilities

Qualitative data indicated that sanitation infrastructure was a major issue across both countries. In Uganda, the lack of proper latrines was widespread, with many participants reporting a prevalence of open defecation. A boy from Namayingo in Uganda noted, “We don't have latrines at the site... People go anywhere they want anyhow,”(B_Na_1) which was echoed by a young girl from Bugiri, who stated, “At the mining sites, there are no proper latrines; people use bushes or polythene bags.”(YG_BG_1). Similarly, participants in Ghana reported the absence of toilets in their communities. In Bamboi (Ghana), a young woman described the situation in her community as: “There is no toilet in our vicinity. Sometimes you may defecate on yourself. There is a river behind us, and that's where we go. We are really suffering.”(YW_Ba_1). In both settings, participants emphasized the reliance on open defecation due to the lack of toilet facilities. Furthermore, participants in Uganda described the lack of privacy during defecation as a source of embarrassment, particularly for women and girls. A young woman from Busia in Uganda shared, “It is embarrassing to look for where to ease yourself when others are watching.”(YW_B_1), a sentiment similar to the experiences of participants in Ghana, where the absence of privacy for defecation was also a concern.

Other hygiene and sanitation challenges

Hygiene practices were heavily constrained by inadequate WASH infrastructure at work sites in both countries. In Uganda, limited water and harsh environments made hygiene difficult. A young woman from Busia noted, “When you want to clean after work, there is no water near the site,” (YW_Bus_2), while a boy from Namayingo added, “During the excavation I dig nonstop taking much of the oxygen at the same time the dust I inhale is poison.” (B_Na_3). Despite these challenges, there was a clear awareness of hygiene's importance. A Namayingo boy shared, “We know washing hands is good, but we cannot do it if water is not there.” (B_Na_3). Similarly, a girl from Bugiri said, “If a person is clean, it is very hard to get infections.” (YG_Bug_1). Menstrual hygiene was particularly difficult due to lack of privacy and water. A young woman from Busia explained, “When your period comes and there is no water, it becomes very hard to clean yourself.” (YW_Bus_4). Participants ALSO linked mining to water contamination. A boy from Namayingo remarked, “Anywhere there is gold there is no good/improved water. It is salty water.” (B_Na_2). In Ghana, stagnant water from gold-washing was described to be associated with malaria: “As for the sickness, it is from the work because the stagnant water from washing of the gold breeds mosquitoes a lot.” (MW_K_1).

Discussion

This study, conducted in selected ASM communities in Ghana and Uganda, aimed to characterize access to WASH services among adolescent girls and young women (aged 10–24 years). While the quantitative data showed that a significant proportion (86.2 %) of the participants accessed drinking water from “improved” sources as traditionally defined by WHO/UNICEF [1,[46], [47], [48]], the qualitative data, combined with contextual knowledge of the ASM environment, suggests a more complex and concerning reality. The results expose stark inadequacies in sanitation access, especially in Ghana, where a staggering 83 % of participants lacked access to any form of toilet facility.

The finding that 86.2 % of participants from both countries accessed “improved” water sources, primarily boreholes (57 %), initially appears encouraging. However, this quantitative result must be interpreted with caution, considering the more recent WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) [48] ladder classification for drinking water. The JMP framework acknowledges that access to safe drinking water depends not only on the type of water source, but also on factors such as accessibility, availability, and water quality management. The JMP ladder classifies drinking water access into levels from ‘safely managed’ to ‘unimproved’ considering whether water is located on premises, available when needed, and free from contamination.

Participants in both Ghana and Uganda described instances where improved water sources, such as boreholes and piped water, were either too far away or unavailable periodically. As highlighted earlier, participants in Uganda reported long distances to boreholes, while those in Ghana noted the unreliable supply of piped water due to machine malfunctions. These findings suggest that even when communities have access to ‘improved’ water sources in principle, the practical realities of distance and intermittency can significantly compromise their actual access to safe and reliable drinking water [49]. Socioeconomic status for example, has been widely documented to predict these challenges, with several studies reporting that community members with a lower socioeconomic status were more likely to spend more time walking to water points and also were less likely to have sustainable access to these water points [[50], [51], [52], [53]]. Therefore, when creating and sustaining WASH interventions, proximity and functionality must be prioritized.

In many ASM communities, the heavy use of mercury for gold extraction poses a severe threat to water quality [10,11,54]. Mercury, a potent neurotoxin, can leach into groundwater and surface water [55], contaminating drinking water sources and posing significant health risks, particularly to pregnant women and young children [56,57]. While this study did not directly measure mercury levels in the water sources, the widespread use of mercury in the study areas [58,59], coupled with the qualitative reports of contaminated water, strongly suggests that mercury contamination may be a significant concern. This is consistent with the available evidence of elevated mercury levels in water sources near ASM sites [60], emphasizing the potential for chronic exposure and adverse health effects in these communities. Furthermore, the uncontrolled excavation and deforestation associated with ASM can lead to soil erosion, sedimentation of water bodies, and disruption of natural watercourses. This can exacerbate water scarcity, especially during the dry season.

The data on access to toilet facilities paint a distressing picture, particularly in Ghana, where a staggering 83 % of participants reported lacking access to any form of toilet facility. This statistic is not merely a reflection of inadequate infrastructure; it signifies a profound sanitation crisis with far-reaching implications for public health, environmental sustainability, and human dignity. Open defecation, the inevitable consequence of lacking toilet facilities, contaminates water sources, and facilitates the spread of infectious diseases.

The contrast in sanitation access between Ghana and Uganda (where 65 % used unimproved facilities) highlights the importance of considering country-specific factors in WASH planning and implementation. The disparities may stem from differences in government policies, investment in sanitation infrastructure, community awareness, or cultural norms. The lack of access to improved toilet facilities was heavily dependent on participants' place of residence, particularly in Ghana (p < 0.001). The data suggests that ASM sites, with their transient populations and often-unregulated conditions, may lack the necessary infrastructure and services to support adequate sanitation. The lack of toilet facilities also poses challenges for women and girls, who may face additional safety risks and social stigma associated with open defecation.

These findings align with previous research highlighting the challenges of sanitation in informal settlements and resource extraction areas [61,62]. Studies in other ASM regions have documented similar patterns of inadequate sanitation, environmental contamination, and increased disease burden [12,19,63,64]. These studies underscore the need for integrated WASH interventions that address both water and sanitation needs, considering the specific environmental and socioeconomic context of ASM communities.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between the measured sociodemographic factors and WASH access. Secondly, self-reported data may be subject to recall and social desirability bias, particularly regarding sensitive topics such as sanitation practices. Thirdly, although efforts were made to ensure representative sampling, certain subgroups, such as transient or highly mobile populations, may have been underrepresented. Also, while data on the primary source of drinking water were collected, detailed information on key factors such as the location of the water source, the cost of purchasing water (if applicable), the time required for water collection, and water quality analysis (for example, testing for microbial contamination or heavy metals) were not. The lack of this comprehensive data limits our ability to fully characterize the nature and extent of WASH access challenges in the study communities. Had these data been collected, a more in-depth characterization of ‘safely managed’ versus simply “improved” water sources, according to the JMP ladder, would have been possible.

Additionally, the study sample only included adolescent girls and young women aged 10–24 living in artisanal mining communities, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other populations or settings. Furthermore, the assessment reflects a snapshot of WASH access and practices during the study period and does not track how these behaviors or conditions may change or be sustained over time, an aspect that could be better understood through follow-up or longitudinal research.

These limitations notwithstanding, the use of both quantitative and qualitative data allowed for triangulation of findings and a richer understanding of the context and lived experiences of the study population on this important subject, which is a key strength.

While the study population was limited to adolescent girls and young women in ASM communities, the findings have potential relevance for other underserved rural populations that face similar challenges in accessing reliable and safe WASH services. Structural barriers observed in this context, such as limited access to improved toilets and concerns about water quality are common in many marginalized settings across sub-Saharan Africa. However, some issues, including mining-related environmental contamination and the occupational and residential patterns unique to ASM sites, are context specific and may limit direct transferability. Applying these insights elsewhere will require careful consideration of the local environmental, cultural, and economic conditions that shape WASH access.

Recommendations for future research

Given that more than half of the study participants rely on boreholes for drinking water, it is crucial to conduct comprehensive research into groundwater quality in ASM communities. This research should include testing for mercury and other heavy metals, as well as microbial contamination, to assess the safety of borehole water and inform strategies for water treatment and protection. The long-term sustainability of boreholes as a water source in ASM areas depends on protecting groundwater resources from contamination.

Future research should also consider longitudinal designs to assess changes in WASH access and health outcomes over time, particularly as mining activities and environmental conditions evolve. Such approaches would also allow for evaluation of whether gains in WASH access are sustained, and whether knowledge about safe water, sanitation, and hygiene is retained and effectively translated into long-term behavioral change. This would provide critical insights into the durability and real-world impact of WASH interventions in ASM and similar underserved settings.

The findings highlight persistent and significant disparities in access to improved water and sanitation facilities in ASM communities, even where national averages suggest progress. The evidence highlights an urgent need for targeted, context-specific WASH interventions in rural mining areas, with a particular focus on vulnerable groups such as adolescent girls and young women.

Given the alarmingly low access to improved toilet facilities, urgent and targeted interventions are needed to address this sanitation crisis. These interventions should focus on the construction and promotion of household and community latrines, with a particular emphasis on reaching those residing at mining sites and those in the poorest wealth quintiles. Community-led sanitation programs, coupled with financial and technical support, may be effective in promoting sustainable sanitation practices.

Conclusion

This study provides insights into the challenges and disparities in WASH access among adolescent girls and young women in last mile ASM communities in Ghana and Uganda. The findings highlight interplay of factors influencing WASH access in these vulnerable populations, including environmental conditions, socioeconomic status, and community-specific characteristics. For improved water sources, the study found that 88.1 % of participants from Ghana and 84.2 % of participants from Uganda got water from improved water sources. Conversely, significant portions of the study population in either country lacked access to improved toilet facilities, The widespread lack of access to improved sanitation coupled with concerns about water quality and reliability, stresses the urgent need for targeted and integrated WASH interventions. Addressing these challenges requires an approach that considers the specific needs and vulnerabilities of adolescent girls and young women in ASM communities, promotes community engagement, and ensures the sustainability of WASH services. By prioritizing these efforts, it is possible to improve WASH outcomes and promote the health, dignity, and well-being of adolescent girls and young women in ASM communities.
