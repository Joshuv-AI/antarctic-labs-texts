S1Q3T3 Pattern in Lemierre Syndrome
Authors: Deitrich Gerlt, Viktor Vasilev, Huda Khan, Leigh Hunter, Nasser Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLemierre syndrome involves internal jugular vein thrombophlebitis and metastatic septic emboli. History of Presentation

A 27-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a 3-day history of high-grade fevers, sore throat, and worsening dyspnea. Two days prior, he had been diagnosed with influenza A at an urgent care facility based on a positive rapid antigen test and had been prescribed oseltamivir. Despite treatment, his symptoms worsened, with onset of pleuritic chest pain, vomiting, diarrhea, and profound diaphoresis.

On arrival, the patient appeared notably diaphoretic and dyspneic. Triage vital signs revealed a temperature of 99.6 °F (37.6 °C), blood pressure of 108/81 mm Hg, heart rate of 156 beats/min, respiratory rate of 18 breaths/min, and preserved oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. Physical examination revealed coarse breath sounds bilaterally, profuse diaphoresis, and tonsillar erythema. Notably, neck tenderness was not appreciated on the initial examination but developed later on hospital day 3 along the anterior border of the left sternocleidomastoid muscle.

Past Medical History

The patient was previously healthy with no history of venous thromboembolism (VTE), hypercoagulable disorders, or recent procedure/surgery. He denied tobacco, alcohol, or illicit drug use.

Differential Diagnosis

There was initial concern for viral prodrome, sepsis, viral myocarditis, pneumonia, and/or massive pulmonary embolism (PE) based on profound tachycardia with dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, and the preceding viral illness.

Investigations

Electrocardiogram (ECG) on admission demonstrated sinus tachycardia at 153 beats/min with an S1Q3T3 pattern (prominent S wave in lead I, Q wave in lead III, and T-wave inversion in lead III) (Figure 1). The Wells score was calculated at 4.5, consistent with moderate risk (16.2%-28.0%) of PE, and the revised Geneva score was 5, also consistent with moderate risk (20%-30%). Despite these intermediate scores, the specific ECG findings raised immediate concern for acute cor pulmonale in the setting of hemodynamically significant PE, driving the decision for computed tomography (CT) pulmonary angiography. It returned negative for central filling defects. Instead, findings included ill-defined peribronchial ground-glass opacities with multiple nodular densities (Figure 2).

Computed Tomography Pulmonary Angiography With Septic Opacification

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography displaying ground-glass opacities and a 1.6-cm nodule, consistent with septic pulmonary emboli and multifocal pneumonia.

High-sensitivity troponin was negative. Markers of sepsis were profoundly elevated: white blood cell count 12.0 × 103/μL with 90% neutrophilic predominance, lactate 3.4 mmol/L, and procalcitonin 50.5 ng/mL. Preliminary blood cultures returned with Gram-negative bacteremia.

The discordance of initial findings and potential right ventricular (RV) strain but absence of central PE prompted broader work-up. Given progressive neck pain, bedside point-of-care ultrasound was performed and identified left internal jugular vein (IJV) thrombosis (Figure 3). A dedicated CT of the neck subsequently confirmed left IJV thrombosis (Figure 4), and blood cultures returned with Fusobacterium necrophorum and group C Streptococcus, confirming a diagnosis of Lemierre syndrome.

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed on hospital day 7, after several days of antibiotic therapy. It showed a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 60%. The RV systolic pressure was 25 mm Hg, but mild RV dilation was noted, suggesting resolving strain (Figure 5).

Management

The patient was initially resuscitated with aggressive fluid boluses and broad-spectrum antibiotics (vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam). After identification of Fnecrophorum and group C Streptococcus, the regimen was de-escalated to targeted therapy with intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g daily) and oral metronidazole (500 mg 3 times a day), requiring placement of a peripherally inserted central catheter line.

The patient had been started on standard pharmacological deep-vein thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis (enoxaparin 40 mg daily) at the time of admission. When he developed left-sided neck pain and point-of-care ultrasound identified a thrombus in the IJV, therapeutic heparin was immediately initiated for presumed acute VTE. However, once the diagnosis of Lemierre syndrome was established, the anticoagulation strategy was re-evaluated. Because the thrombus did not extend intracranially and the onset was in the context of Lemierre syndrome (local infection), therapeutic anticoagulation was discontinued to avoid bleeding risk in the setting of active sepsis. The vascular surgery team recommended against thrombectomy and supported a strategy of antibiotics alone for the acute phase.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient's hemodynamic profile improved around day 4 after initiation of antibiotics. His diaphoresis resolved completely by day 5. Renal function recovered (creatinine returned to baseline). His dyspnea improved, and he was discharged in stable condition on hospital day 8.

A follow-up Doppler ultrasound at 4 weeks postdischarge to assess for thrombus resolution showed the continued presence of an occluded left IJV. The anticoagulation strategy was again re-evaluated. Given the persistence of the thrombus after the acute infectious phase had resolved, the patient was started on rivaroxaban (20 mg daily) as a planned 3-month course for provoked DVT.

Discussion

The S1Q3T3 pattern on ECG is specific for acute RV strain arising from abrupt increase in pulmonary vascular impedance.1,2 In the setting of PE, this pattern is associated with a large, central clot burden (saddle or lobar emboli) that mechanically obstructs >30% to 50% of the pulmonary vasculature.3,4

Our case presents a notable hemodynamic paradox with the presence of an S1Q3T3 pattern and profound tachycardia in the absence of central pulmonary artery obstruction. Given findings of multifocal infectious pneumonia, ill-defined peribronchial ground-glass opacities, and nodular pulmonary opacities suggestive of an infectious/inflammatory process, we concluded that the “showering” of septic pulmonary emboli from left IJV thrombophlebitis into the pulmonary arterioles likely caused an increase in pulmonary vascular resistance by 2 separate mechanisms: 1) Extensive ground-glass opacities on CT likely led to significant ventilation/perfusion mismatch, triggering widespread hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (Euler-Liljestrand mechanism); and 2) Fnecrophorum induced a release of inflammatory cytokines and vasoactive mediators (eg, thromboxane A2, serotonin) from activated platelets and endothelium, leading to diffuse pulmonary vasospasm.5, 6, 7

The echocardiogram performed on day 7 revealed mild RV dilation despite a normal RV systolic pressure (25 mm Hg). This temporal dissociation highlights the rapidity with which RV hemodynamics can recover once the septic source is controlled. The initial S1Q3T3 pattern on ECG likely captured the acute phase of a “septic” cor pulmonale, which had largely resolved by the time of the echocardiogram.

The identification of an acute IJV thrombus naturally triggers the clinical reflex to anticoagulate. However, the pathophysiology of thrombosis in Lemierre syndrome differs fundamentally from VTE. The thrombus is inflammatory (thrombophlebitis), originating from nearby peritonsillar inflammation rather than stasis-driven or hypercoagulable states.8, 9, 10 While anticoagulation is standard for DVT to prevent subsequent complications including PE, the emboli in Lemierre syndrome are septic, not thrombotic. Instead, septic emboli typically resolve with antibiotics. Current literature suggests that systemic anticoagulation in Lemierre syndrome does not significantly alter mortality or vessel recanalization rates and should be reserved for cases with retrograde extension into the cavernous sinus or persistent embolization despite optimal antimicrobial therapy.6,8,11

For our patient, the decision to withdraw heparin after the diagnosis was confirmed represents an important pivot from VTE management to septic thromboembolism management, avoiding unnecessary bleeding risk in an otherwise young, active patient. The subsequent decision to initiate oral anticoagulation at 4 weeks reflects a nuanced approach toward prioritizing safety during the acute septic phase, while addressing persistent occlusion in the chronic phase.

Conclusions

Lemierre syndrome has re-emerged in the antibiotic stewardship era. It poses a specific diagnostic challenge to the cardiovascular specialist, as it may mimic the hemodynamics of massive PE. This case shows that septic pulmonary emboli can significantly increase RV afterload comparable to a hemodynamically significant PE, and ECG changes may include an S1Q3T3 pattern. Ruling out central clot with CT pulmonary angiography in a patient with S1Q3T3 and labile hemodynamics or sepsis should trigger an assessment for sources of septic thromboembolism, rather than a dismissal of potential RV strain.
