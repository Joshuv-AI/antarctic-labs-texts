Review and comprehensive analysis of knowledge, tools, and implementation gaps for the control of contagious bovine pleuropneumonia
Authors: Lucía Manso-Silván, William Amanfu, Andrea Apolloni, Loïc Comtet, Martin Heller, Geoffrey M Muuka, Lamya Rafi, Karl M Rich, Flavio Sacchini, Elise Schieck, Philippe Totté
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractContagious bovine pleuropneumonia (CBPP) is a severe respiratory disease of cattle, caused by Mycoplasma mycoides subsp. mycoides, and is included in the list of notifiable diseases of the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH). CBPP was successfully eradicated from the USA, Australia, Europe, and parts of southern Africa, primarily through the application of drastic stamping-out policies. However, the disease remains endemic in sub-Saharan Africa, where it continues to spread despite considerable control efforts, causing significant losses and posing a serious risk of antimicrobial resistance emergence. Recognised as a major cattle disease, CBPP is included in the DISCONTOOLS database, which aims “to identify the gaps in knowledge to speed up the development of new disease control tools and to reduce the burden of animal diseases”. This work presents the outcome of a comprehensive analysis by a group of experts, who reviewed existing knowledge on CBPP aetiology, host–pathogen interactions, epidemiology, impact, and control. Key knowledge and technical gaps were identified, along with critical needs to support improved disease control and eventual eradication. These include a better understanding of the disease’s distribution, incidence and impact; the mechanisms of transmission and persistence at the population level; the molecular basis of pathogenicity and protective immune responses; the development of affordable and reliable diagnostic tools, including pen-side tests; and the design of safer, more effective, and thermostable vaccines, including DIVA and multi-valent options. In addition, cost-effective intervention strategies combining multiple control measures, such as vaccination and the rational, regulated use of antibiotics, must be developed and validated, alongside surveillance for antimicrobial resistance in the field. Social, political, and financial factors contributing to the persistence and spread of the disease must also be addressed. Importantly, based on this analysis, a set of cost-effective actions and practical recommendations has been proposed to mitigate the most critical gaps. With adequate financial, scientific, and political commitment, the eradication of CBPP remains an achievable goal.Keywords: Contagious bovine pleuropneumonia (CBPP), Mycoplasma mycoides subsp. mycoides (Mmm), Gap analysis

Introduction

Contagious bovine pleuropneumonia (CBPP) is a severe infectious respiratory disease that primarily affects zebu and taurine cattle. It is caused by the bacterium Mycoplasma mycoides subsp. mycoides (Mmm) and is associated with high morbidity and mortality [1]. CBPP is transmitted directly from infected to susceptible animals via the airborne route and exhibits a classical transboundary nature: it can spread through populations of live animals across international borders, causing considerable economic and societal harm. For these reasons, it is included in the list of notifiable diseases of the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH). In the European context, CBPP is additionally subject to an emergency health response plan, which requires immediate eradication measures to be implemented upon detection [2]. Although its precise distribution, incidence, and impact have not been established, CBPP is currently widespread in sub-Saharan Africa. The disease has also been reported in the Middle East and South Asia, although the situation in these regions remains unclear due to a lack of effective surveillance. In Europe, CBPP has not been reported since the last case detected in Portugal in 1999.

CBPP did not exist in Africa “from time immemorial”, as previously assumed, but is in fact a relatively recent disease. The emergence of its aetiologic agent, Mmm, was estimated by molecular dating analyses to have occurred around 300 years ago, most likely in Europe [3]. During the nineteenth century, the disease was introduced to other continents. However, because of its high impact, strict control measures were implemented and CBPP was subsequently eradicated from the USA, Australia, Europe, and South Africa, primarily through drastic stamping-out policies. Meanwhile, the disease continued to spread in sub-Saharan Africa, although it appeared to be under control by the 1970 s, owing to sustained research and surveillance efforts, coupled with regular international vaccination campaigns using combined rinderpest-CBPP vaccines [4, 5]. However, economic crises and civil conflicts in the 1980 s and 1990 s led to a dramatic resurgence. The eradication of rinderpest from these regions, followed by the discontinuation of combined vaccination campaigns, further contributed to this re-emergence. As a consequence, CBPP remains endemic in sub-Saharan African countries, where it inflicts severe losses, posing a significant threat to food security and compromising livelihoods and wellbeing [4]. Disease control has proven unattainable and CBPP continues to spread to territories where it had been absent for a long time. An example is the re-emergence of CBPP in Senegal after 30 years of freedom [6]. Moreover, the number of outbreaks is increasing in areas where the disease was already present. Prevalence remains high in countries such as Niger, despite years of national vaccination programmes [7], highlighting the limitations of current vaccination policies. Multiple factors contribute to the spread and persistence of the disease in sub-Saharan Africa, including the nomadic system of animal husbandry, which facilitates the spread of the disease,the absence of animal identification and traceability systems; weak and under resourced veterinary systems; low adoption of vaccination as a control measure; and the limited efficacy of existing vaccines [8].

Recognised as a major cattle disease, CBPP was included in the DISCONTOOLS database, which aims “to identify the gaps in knowledge to speed up the development of new disease control tools (diagnostics, vaccines and pharmaceuticals) and to reduce the burden of animal diseases” (DISCONTOOLS Disease Database www.discontools.eu/database/39-contagious-bovine-pleuro-pneumonia; [9]). In this work, we review the available information on CBPP aetiology, pathogenesis, epidemiology, impact, and control,identify the main gaps in knowledge, tools, and other requirements needed to improve the control of this devastating disease; and propose cost-effective actions and practical recommendations to address the most critical of these gaps.

The disease: clinical signs and lesions; experimental infection

Classical CBPP is a strictly respiratory disease characterised by a severe fibrinous pleuropneumonia that is predominantly unilateral. The clinical signs and pathological features of CBPP have been extensively described by [10] and reviewed by Thiaucourt [5] and [11]. Clinical disease may present in hyperacute, acute, subacute or chronic forms. Hyperacute cases are characterised by sudden death due to pleural/pericardial effusion or heart failure, often in the absence of clinical signs other than fever. Acutely affected animals present with fever and dyspnoea, characterised by painful, laboured breathing and violent coughing. Death may follow within 1 to 3 weeks. Typical acute lung lesions consist of fibrinonecrotic pneumonia, characterised by markedly widened interlobular septa and areas of red and grey hepatisation, giving the lungs a characteristic marbled appearance. This is accompanied by enlargement of the bronchial lymph nodes and severe serofibrinous pleurisy, resulting in the presence of large volumes of pleural fluid in the thoracic cavity and abundant fibrin deposits (known as “omelette”) on the pleura. Most often, a single lung is affected. If not pathognomonic, these lesions can be highly indicative of CBPP, though the classical presentation is rarely observed in practice due to insidious disease circulation, antibiotic treatments and concomitant infections. The animals that do not die may take many months to recover. In the subacute and chronic forms/stages, emaciation and infrequent coughing, mainly provoked upon exercise, may be the only clinical signs. The pleural fluid disappears, hard fibrotic pleural adhesions are prominent and lung lesions resolve, leading to the formation of characteristic encapsulated necrotic lesions, known as “sequestra”. The major histopathological features of CBPP have been reviewed by Di Teodoro et al. [11].

In calves up to six months of age the only sign of disease is usually arthritis, particularly of the carpal and tarsal joints, which may be accompanied by heart complications. Pulmonary lesions may also develop occasionally, and some reports seem to indicate this may be more common than originally described [12], particularly in epidemic situations, in which young animals are not protected by colostral antibodies and may then develop overt disease [5].

CBPP can be induced experimentally, and current in vivo infection models rely on intratracheal intubation for inoculation of either pleural fluid from infected animals or broth cultures of virulent Mmm strains into the lungs of susceptible cattle [13, 14]. Alternatively, a group of intubated animals may be used to infect in-contact animals, to better mimic natural infection [15]. Recently, efforts have been made to develop new in vivo cattle infection models based on intranasal atomisation, which have provided promising results [16].

It must be noted that Mmm can also induce severe lesions in cattle when inoculated artificially by other routes. For example, the local invasive oedematous lesion, known as Willems’ reaction was observed following subcutaneous inoculation of pleural fluid from the lungs of naturally infected cattle [5]. These local reactions may occasionally develop upon vaccination with the live vaccine strain T1/44, currently used in Africa. If not treated with antibiotics, they can evolve into systemic disease and may eventually result in the death of the animal [17].

Gap analysis

There are no small animal models available for CBPP, so in vivo infection experiments must be performed in cattle. The in-contact cattle challenge model mimicking natural infection requires large numbers of animals and the incubation time and disease outcome can be highly variable [18]. Direct challenge through endotracheal intubation has thus been used to standardise the timing of infection and to reduce the number of experimental cattle needed. However, endobronchial intubation generally leads to subacute or chronic CBPP rather than acute disease and is not very reproducible [15]. A reproducible cattle challenge model is therefore needed, both for pathogenicity studies and for the evaluation of vaccines and treatments. Recently developed cattle infection models using an aerosolised Mmm inoculum to reproduce more closely the natural infection have shown good transmission of classical CBPP in a large proportion of animals, allowing a reduction in the number of cattle needed in trials [16]. Efforts to develop and standardise direct cattle challenge models based on intranasal atomisation or nebulisation through a mask should thus be pursued.

Disease scoring is critical for the implementation of these in vivo models to allow vaccine and treatment efficacy estimations. In an attempt to quantify and integrate the various criteria related to animal infection and disease severity, a scoring system was developed by Hudson & Turner [19], which combines clinical, serological and pathological scores. Unfortunately, this often results in overestimating disease. Furthermore, the use of this kind of composite scoring makes no distinction between protection against infection, morbidity, mortality or other parameters of interest. Recently, an alternative, detailed lung lesion scoring system was proposed by Di Provvido et al. [20], though this complex, expert system may need to be adapted for use in efficacy studies. Appropriate scoring systems that support a standardised methodology for assessing the efficacy of control measures should be adopted to enable more accurate quantification of key parameters. Future studies should specify which parameters are used to evaluate efficacy, whether reduction in infection levels, morbidity, mortality or transmission.

In parallel, the identification and validation of early biomarkers of disease severity should be pursued, as they could offer significant prognostic value and help reduce animal suffering by enabling earlier endpoints or interventions during experimental infection. Several potential biomarker categories can be proposed based on the literature. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-17A were detected at high levels in lung lesions of experimentally infected cattle [21], and high plasma levels of TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-4, and IL-10 were correlated with disease progression and severity [15]. Acute phase proteins such as haptoglobin, serum amyloid A (SAA) and fibrinogen may also be explored, particularly SAA, which is a well-established marker of systemic inflammation in cattle and has demonstrated utility in monitoring infection severity and tissue damage in bovine respiratory disease [22]. Basic blood haematology and biochemistry tests are widely available and provide early and dynamic indicators of systemic inflammation, organ function, stress responses, and metabolic changes. They have also been widely used in infectious disease models, including bovine respiratory diseases, to identify potential biomarkers for infection severity and prognosis. Finally, the identification of specific transcriptomic and proteomic signatures associated with early host–pathogen interactions and immune activation in Mmm infections may offer a promising avenue for the development of high-resolution prognostic tools. Different candidates may be evaluated through longitudinal sampling in experimentally infected animals, combined with clinical and pathological scoring, to establish their predictive value and standardise their use in future trials.

Ultimately, replacement, reduction, and refinement (3Rs) alternatives to animal use call for the development of reliable in vitro models for pathogenicity assays, as well as the identification of biomarkers of protection for vaccine trials (see sections on pathogenicity and host immune response). When infectious challenge in cattle cannot be avoided, the use of fully susceptible monozygotic twins may further allow a reduction in the number of animals needed to obtain significant results. The subcutaneous inoculation, or Willems’ reaction model, may also be used as an alternative cattle challenge model, allowing the quantification of lesions (size of the subcutaneous inflammation) in live animals without the need for deep challenge and respiratory tract involvement. Furthermore, inoculated animals may be treated with antibiotics as soon as they reach a relevant threshold score, thus reducing animal suffering and costs. In all cases, a carefully designed approach should be adopted to maximise scientific outcomes while minimising animal suffering.

The pathogen

The CBPP agent, Mmm, is a bacterium belonging to the class Mollicutes, order Mycoplasmatales, family Mycoplasmataceae, and genus Mycoplasma. The Mollicutes, commonly referred to as “mycoplasmas”, are exceptional bacteria characterised by the minute size of their genomes and cells, which lack a cell wall. This makes them intrinsically resistant to many antibiotics. Although they appear phenotypically Gram-negative, mycoplasmas evolved from Gram-positive bacteria through a process of reductive evolution, involving massive losses of genes and, thus, of metabolic capabilities. Genome down-sizing has entailed the adaptation of mycoplasmas to restricted ecological niches where they can scavenge the necessary nutrients they can no longer synthesise [23]. This explains their relatively strict host and tissue tropism, which is particularly true for Mmm, as well as their fastidious growth requirements in vitro.

More precisely, Mmm belongs to the “Mycoplasma mycoides” cluster, which comprises five mycoplasma species and subspecies that are pathogenic for ruminants. They share many genetic and phenotypic traits, which has considerably complicated their classification. The members of this cluster, their former and current taxonomies, as well as their corresponding hosts and diseases, are shown in Table 1. Before the revision of the taxonomy of this cluster in 2009 [26], Mmm was known as the"Small Colony''biotype of Mmm (MmmSC). At the time, the CBPP agent was thus associated, in a single taxon, with the “Large Colony” isolates involved in the contagious agalactia syndrome of small ruminants. However, phylogenetic analyses showed that the “LC” strains were more closely related to other goat isolates also responsible for contagious agalactia [27], and they were thus reclassified into the taxon Mycoplasma mycoides subsp. capri, (Mmc), leaving Mmm as the exclusive designation for the CBPP agent.

This confusion arose from the fact that the growth inhibition test and other conventional serological methods used for species classification did not allow the discrimination of “LC” and “SC” strains. However, the CBPP agent exhibited distinct traits in culture (i.e., slow, filamentous growth, with development of “comets” and silky swirls in broth, and small colonies with the typical “fried egg” appearance on solid medium), which led to its designation as “SC biotype”, a term no longer in use. By contrast, the “LC” designation is still used to refer to the Mmc serovar that shares antigenic traits with Mmm, distinguishing it from the “capri” serovar.

Due to the absence of a cell wall and organised cytoskeleton or polar structures, Mmm cells are pleomorphic and non-motile. They are surrounded by an exo-polysaccharide (galactan) forming a pseudo-capsule (capsular polysaccharide, CPS), which is also secreted as a non-cell-bound extracellular form (extracellular polysaccharide, EPS) [28, 29]. Notably, secretion of galactan CPS, which is highly immunogenic, by the “LC” serovar of Mmc is responsible for the serological cross-reactions observed with Mmm. The lack of a cell wall also renders mycoplasmas very sensitive to physical, chemical, and biological factors and Mmm does not persist in the environment for more than three days in tropical regions and up to two weeks in temperate zones [10], [30]. However, biofilm formation by Mmm has been associated with increased survival, at least in vitro [31].

Mmm is a highly monomorphic pathogen and discriminating strains within a taxonomic group showing such low intraspecies variability has been challenging. Nevertheless, the genetic diversity of Mmm is relatively well characterised and strain genotyping has been achieved using various molecular techniques, including Southern blotting and multi-locus genotyping. These approaches showed that African and European strains belonged to separate clades [32]. Subsequently, a large-scale genomic approach was developed to enable high resolution strain identification for epidemiological investigations, phylogenetic analysis, and molecular dating [3]. This allowed the estimation of Mmm’s origin at approximately 300 years ago. This relatively recent emergence may reflect the adaptation of its Mmc ancestor (a small ruminant pathogen showing broad tissue tropism) to a new host (cattle) and a specific niche (the lung), as supported by Mmm-Mmc whole genome comparisons [33].

Mmm strains can be attenuated by serial passage in culture or in animals, forming the basis for the development of live attenuated vaccines. Strains with varying levels of pathogenicity are available: the type strain PG1T and vaccine strains T1sr and KH3J have no residual virulence, while vaccine strains T1/44 and V5 retain some pathogenicity. European strains isolated since 1980 lack part of the glycerol uptake system [34], which has been proposed as a virulence determinant in Mmm. These strains have been considered less virulent [35, 36], though the evidence supporting reduced pathogenicity is limited, especially given the considerable variability observed in animal infection trials.

Gap analysis

Mmm cells can produce biofilms in vitro, although the presence of Mmm biofilms in vivo, the mechanisms involved in biofilm formation, and their impact on pathogenicity and persistence remain to be explored. The same applies to extracellular vesicles, which have been identified in in vitro cultures [37] but whose presence in the host context still requires formal demonstration. Similarly, the mechanisms involved in vesicle release and their role in mycoplasma-host cell interactions have not yet been elucidated.

A better understanding of the diversity and distribution of Mmm strains is hampered by the extreme paucity of CBPP outbreak investigations, sampling, isolation and characterisation. The availability of commercial culture media for Mmm isolation and antimicrobial susceptibility testing is very limited, with shortages and long supply being common). Furthermore, both commercial and in-house media [1] are very expensive and require strict maintenance of the cold chain for storage. This is due to the need for complex supplements that must be preserved either frozen (liquid formulations) or refrigerated (lyophilised preparations). Simpler, more defined media are needed for isolation and research purposes and, most particularly, for the development of standardised protocols for antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Despite the relatively small genome size of Mmm (around 1.2 Mbp; [38]), only six independent complete genomes alongside partial genomic data are available in public databases to date. These sequences do not adequately represent the full temporal and spatial diversity of the species. Similarly, few whole genome sequences exist for other members of the “M. mycoides” cluster. The generation of complete, circularised genomes for Mmm and related species has been hampered by the presence of numerous insertion sequences, integrative conjugative elements, and large genomic duplications. However, the assembly of whole genome sequences may now be facilitated by the improvement and wider availability of sequencing technologies. Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) and Oxford Nanopore Technology platforms can now generate long reads (thousands of base pairs) with high accuracy. Obtaining fully assembled genomes of Mmm and related species will facilitate comprehensive comparative “omics” analysis, leading to the delineation of core and pan genomes at different levels, providing insights into genome plasticity and evolution and supporting the identification of functional determinants. To achieve this, a robust whole genome analysis pipeline must be developed, suitable for high resolution strain genotyping and integrated comparative “omics” investigations. Complete circularised genomes from a diverse set of “M. mycoides” cluster strains (covering different geographic and temporal origins, host and tissue tropisms, and pathogenicity profiles) should be generated. These data should be deposited in publicly accessible repositories to support open research. This strategy will enable the identification of (i) diagnostic targets for the development of improved detection assays, (ii) molecular determinants of virulence and attenuation, (iii) traits associated with host and tissue tropism, and (iv) genomic markers of antimicrobial resistance (AMR).

Finally, although increasing amounts of genome sequence data are becoming available, the functional analysis of mycoplasmas has lagged behind due to the scarcity of specific tools for their genetic manipulation, including tools for producing mutants and expressing gene products. This is particularly true for Mmm, for which in-yeast synthetic biology approaches could not be adapted, although they are accessible for the other members of the “M. mycoides” cluster. However, new genome editing tools using a CRISPR-Cas base editor system have recently been adapted to Mmm [39]. This presents new opportunities for the generation of targeted Mmm mutants, both for functional analyses and for the development of rationally designed vaccines. Still, in-yeast synthetic biology methods would greatly contribute to the engineering of whole mycoplasma genomes, and the successful application to Mmm of the recently developed CReasPy-Fusion method, which allows simultaneous cloning and engineering of mycoplasma genomes in yeast [40], raises very promising prospects. Therefore, efforts must be renewed to achieve the transplantation of Mmm genomes following in-yeast engineering.

Pathogenicity

Acute CBPP is characterised by an exacerbated inflammatory reaction in the lungs, with tissue damage and respiratory distress as paroxysmal manifestations; however, the mechanisms involved at the early stages of infection are largely unknown. Classical virulence factors typically associated with bacterial pathogenesis have not been identified in Mmm and there is general consensus that tissue damage primarily arises from the immune response rather than being a direct consequence of bacterial proliferation [11]. Mmm adhesion to lung epithelium is a prerequisite for infection and colonisation but it does not, in itself, induce tissue necrosis, as suggested by in vitro Mmm infection studies using lung explants, in which little or no tissue damage (without necrosis) was detected (DiTeodoro et al., 2018, [41]).

In the absence of classical virulence factors such as toxins, invasins, or other direct virulence-associated factors typical of bacteria, there are several molecules produced or induced by Mmm that have been proposed as potential initiators of inflammation (reviewed by [11]). Multiple elements or a cascade induced via the activation of immune and somatic cells, rather than a single key Mmm factor, are likely to contribute to CBPP pathogenicity, particularly by promoting the exacerbated inflammatory response.

No clear pattern has yet emerged to explain the differences in pathogenicity observed among Mmm strains in vivo. For example, the in vitro production of TNF-α, an archetypal pro-inflammatory cytokine, by alveolar macrophages infected with the non-pathogenic type strain PG1T was higher than that induced by macrophages infected with the highly pathogenic strain Afadé [42].

A major role in pathogenicity has been proposed for the enzyme L-α-glycerophosphate oxidase (GlpO), due to its capacity to generate cytotoxic hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in the presence of glycerol [43, 44]. However, a recent study demonstrating the strict intracytoplasmic localisation of GlpO, at least under the conditions tested, calls for a reassessment of its significance [45]. Nevertheless, an indirect effect of Mmm-produced H2O2, through the amplification of the neutrophil respiratory burst, has been observed [46].

The polysaccharide galactan is expressed by all Mmm strains but its role as a trigger for inflammation remains unclear. A genetic on/off switch of the ptsG gene involved in glucose metabolism is responsible for the alternate expression of capsular (CPS) and free extracellular (EPS) galactan [29, 47]. Oxidative stress promotes the EPS form of galactan and differences in resistance to host defence mechanisms have been observed between capsulated and non-capsulated Mmm variants [48]. The CPS may enable the masking of highly pro-inflammatory lipoproteins such that capsulated Mmm cells are poorly recognised by the host. The switch to soluble EPS may reduce that masking effect, but also enhance adherence. In addition, non-capsulated Mmm variants exhibit increased resistance to H2O2 and antimicrobial peptides [48].

Extracellular vesicles and proteases, including the S41 peptidase family and components of the so-called MIB-MIP system, are produced by many distantly related Mycoplasma species [49, 50], but expression levels among Mmm strains of different pathogenicity have not been investigated. The MIB-MIP system is a two-protein mechanism capable of capturing and cleaving the VH domain of immunoglobulins, thereby rendering cryptic epitopes accessible to recognition by phagocytes. This may result in oxidative burst and the release of IL-8, a potent neutrophil chemoattractant.

Chronic CBPP suggests that Mmm possesses mechanisms enabling it to evade the immune response and persist within the host, such as: i) phase variation of surface antigens [51],ii) immunoglobulin-cleaving proteins (MIB-MIP system; [52]),iii) biofilm formation [31], iv) toxin-antitoxin systems [53],v) EPS (free extracellular galactan), detected in circulating blood during acute CBPP, which may dampen immune defences through anti-inflammatory properties [54, 55], and vi) CPS (capsular galactan), shown in vivo to protect Mmm from clearance by the host [56].

Infection models are used to study pathogenesis and assess putative virulence determinants, notably through the analysis of targeted mutants. In vitro models employed to study cytotoxicity and adhesion include bovine cell lines such as nasal and lung epithelial cells, aortic endothelial cells, and skin fibroblasts [43, 57], and 3D systems including bovine respiratory explants [58] and bovine precision-cut lung slices [41]. Interactions with phagocytes have been explored using ex vivo primary cultures of monocyte-derived macrophages [59, 60], alveolar macrophages [42], and polymorphonuclear cells [46, 61].

Gap analysis

A clear understanding of the molecular basis of Mmm’s virulence and attenuation is still lacking and research is needed to understand how the host environment influences the regulation of Mmm virulence genes. As mentioned above, more comprehensive omics analyses on “M. mycoides” cluster strains of diverse host and tissue tropism, as well as varying levels of virulence, may help identify relevant virulence factors for further functional studies using recombinant proteins and targeted mutants. A combined omics approach, including metabolomics, of Mmm present in lung lesions at different stages of severity is needed to link molecular patterns with tissue destruction but also with Mmm persistence in the host (particularly in sequestra). The subcutaneous “Willems’ reaction” model of Mmm-induced invasive oedema may also be used for these analyses.

The early mechanisms underlying CBPP pathogenesis remain unclear. The complex, multifactorial nature of inflammation, coupled with the lack of small animal and in vitro models reproducing CBPP, represents a major obstacle to progress. Although lung explants infected in vitro provide an interesting alternative, they have limitations, as neither tissue necrosis nor sequestra are observed. Developing co-culture systems that combine lung epithelial and endothelial cells with phagocytes should be explored [62]. In particular, the impact of Mmm-mediated amplification of neutrophil respiratory burst on lung epithelial cell viability warrants investigation.

The contribution of candidate Mmm virulence factors listed above to CBPP pathogenesis remains to be elucidated. The precise role of galactan (whether CPS or EPS) in promoting inflammation on one side and immune-suppression or evasion on the other is unclear. It is also unknown whether Mmm strains exhibiting different degrees of pathogenicity in vivo differ in their ability to express EPS under oxidative stress. Furthermore, the pro-inflammatory properties of capsulated versus non-capsulated Mmm variants should be assessed in vivo using the “Willems’ reaction” model and in vitro using phagocytes.

The role of Mmm’s extracellular secretome, including secreted proteases and vesicles, remains poorly understood, and the functionality of the MIB-MIP system in attenuated and avirulent strains has not been investigated. Its possible role as an initiator or amplifier of inflammation in CBPP needs clarification.

How Mmm survives within sequestra despite tissue destruction, as well as other potential persistence mechanisms (such as biofilm formation) remains unknown. In addition, virulence determinants that act during the final stages of pathogenesis, contributing to exacerbated inflammation but having little impact on in vivo viability or fitness, need to be better characterised. This knowledge may support the development of improved live attenuated vaccines.

Host immune responses: immunopathology and protection

As expected, given the strong local inflammatory response characteristic of CBPP, phagocytes containing Mmm antigens, along with areas of necrosis associated with newly formed lymphoid follicles, are observed in infected lungs [11]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-17A, and TNF-α have been detected in infected pulmonary tissue in vivo, although their cellular sources remain unidentified [21]. In vitro studies show that TNF-α and IL-1β are produced by alveolar macrophages and neutrophils, respectively, following Mmm infection [42, 61]. Among these cytokines, only TNF-α has been measured in the plasma of infected animals,correlating positively with disease severity at a single time-point after infection [63]. Neutrophils are likely to play a central role in excessive inflammation leading to lung tissue damage, as reported in other models, including mycoplasmoses, where their activity is controlled by IL-17A produced by lymphocytes [64, 65].

Knowledge of innate defence mechanisms during mycoplasma infection is limited, largely due to consensus on their resistance to phagocytosis and killing by neutrophils and macrophages in the absence of specific antiserum (Marshall et al., 1995). This was recently confirmed for Mmm, even under conditions of non-specific complement opsonisation [60]. Nevertheless, the same study suggested that complement may contribute to CBPP resistance through direct bactericidal activity. Antibody responses are detectable after infection with Mmm, but titres of different immunoglobulin isotypes generally show no clear correlation with disease severity or protection, except IgA, correlates positively with reduced pathology [66]. A longitudinal study of IgG responses against 35 Mmm surface proteins failed to identify correlates of protection [67]. Antibody transfer experiments remain scarce and inconclusive. However, a monoclonal IgM antibody directed against galactan inhibits Mmm growth of in vitro in the absence of phagocytes and complement, forming the basis for the growth inhibition diagnostic test [68]. Polyclonal antisera from convalescent animals can induce macrophage-mediated killing of Mmm in vitro [60].

Immunisation against strongly immunogenic surface-exposed Mmm antigens such as LppQ can induce complications, including nephritis, rather than protection upon challenge [69]. T-cell mediated immunity likely plays a complex role in both pathogenesis and protection, as seen in Mycoplasma pulmonis infections [70]. In CBPP, in vivo CD4+ T cell depletion studies indicate no clear contribution to resistance or pathology during primary infection [14]. However, different CD4+ T cell subsets may still be involved, playing distinct roles. In Mycoplasma pulmonis, the Th17 subset of CD4+ cells drives pathology via IL-17A, whereas the Th1 subset contributes to protection mediated by IFN-γ [65, 70]. Notably, IL-17A production by peripheral blood mononuclear cells has recently been demonstrated in goats infected with Mycoplasma capricolum subsp. capripneumoniae [71], the causative agent of contagious caprine pleuropneumonia, a goat disease that exhibits strong clinical and pathological similarities to CBPP.

Cell-mediated immunity in CBPP has been mainly assessed via IFN-γ production in ex vivo antigen-stimulated mononuclear cells from peripheral blood, which is a common marker of cellular responses. Results from two studies where inconsistent [18, 72], likely due to protocol differences (e.g., frequency of IFN-γ assays,challenge intensity). Further work on draining lymph-nodes showed that fully recovered animals exhibited stronger Mmm-specific CD4+ T-cell recall responses than animals harbouring sequestra, although recall proliferation could not be demonstrated [73]. Subsequent studies confirmed that recall proliferation of B and T lymphocytes in draining lymph-nodes of these animals was under the control of CD4+ and IFN-γ-secreting memory T cells (Th1 subset) [74]. Both effector and central memory CD4+ subsets, equivalent to short- and long-term memory cells in humans and mice, were identified [75]. These responses were also induced following vaccination with the T1 attenuated vaccine, but only after three rounds of vaccination, highlighting the vaccine’s poor immunogenicity [76]. The activation of CD4-mediated adaptive immunity during infection and vaccination is expected, given Mmm’s extracellular life cycle; which promotes MHC Class II-dependent presentation of Mmm antigens by antigen-presenting cells to CD4+ T cells. In ruminants, CD4+ T cells are essential for strong; long-lasting antibody responses and can enhance phagocytosis and pathogen killing via IFN-γ secretion [77]. Furthermore, IFN-γ inhibits IL-17 production by CD4+ T cells and reduces pathogenic neutrophil accumulation in infected lungs [78].

Gap analysis

The mechanisms underlying uncontrolled inflammation leading to tissue destruction, as well as those involved in protection, remain unclear. Given their complexity and interconnection, an omics-based systemic approach is needed to obtain a precise understanding of the contributions of both innate and adaptive immunity.

The contribution of cell-mediated immunity to the pathology of CBPP will be difficult to determine conclusively in the absence of inbred mouse models for host gene knock-out and cell transfer experiments. However, it is feasible to characterise in situ the immunocompetent cell subsets (i.e., phagocytes and lymphocytes) present in lesions at different stages, and their respective cytokine profiles using “single cell RNA-seq”. Furthermore, the possibility that Mmm triggers IL-17A production by lymphocytes can be verified in vitro. Particular attention to neutrophils is warranted, given their central role in lung tissue damage in other models. Therefore, studies using multi-cellular in vitro systems are needed to further explore the interplay of chemokines, cytokines, and host cells that leads to neutrophil migration and activation, causing necrosis of lung epithelial cells upon Mmm infection. Finally, the subcutaneous “Willems’ reaction” model of Mmm-induced invasive oedema should be explored for cause-and-effect studies using neutralising antibodies against pro-inflammatory factors.

Knowledge on how different immunoglobulin isotypes contribute to immuno-pathology and protective immunity is lacking. Transfer of specific immunoglobulin subclasses from immune to naive animals should be performed to understand their role in immunity. To minimise the number of experimental animals required, these studies should employ enhanced nebulisation-based challenge methods. Emphasis should be placed on IgA, given its importance in the mucosal environment. The immunoglobulin classes and subclasses responsible for in vitro phagocytosis by macrophages need to be identified, as well as the targeted Mmm antigens.

Regarding protection, unsuccessful attempts to reinfect cattle recovered from artificial infection by Windsor and Masiga [79] showed that survivors acquire strong, long-lasting immunity (up to 30 months). Only one out of four and two out of seven animals reinfected by contact challenge and intubation respectively presented old sequestra at post-mortem examination, whereas the remaining animals exhibited either resolved lesions or no lesions at all. Mmm was not isolated from these tissues. Surprisingly, some animals infected 12, 24 or 30 months earlier showed positive CFT titres at the time of reinfection. Unfortunately, the kinetics of CFT titres throughput the experiment was not presented, making it impossible to identify potential reactivations before reinfection. In conclusion, although fully recovered animals can exhibit long-lasting immunity, it remains uncertain whether life-long, sterile protection (i.e., without Mmm persistence) can be achieved.

Little is known about the innate immune responses to a first infection that precede and shape the protective memory immunity that follows. A longitudinal transcriptomic analysis of cattle gene regulation in blood samples should be conducted until full recovery (i.e., when only resolved lesions are present in the lungs at slaughter).

The protective memory response recalled in clinically recovered animals during a secondary challenge is the most effective and long-lasting and thus should be fully characterised using omics and immunological tools. Correlates of protection [80] or at the very least; correlates of successful vaccination [81] would be most useful for the development and validation of novel vaccines for CBPP control. Current experimental infection/immunisation protocols induce different levels of protection against reinfection. Therefore, it should be possible to characterise protective immunity leading to full recovery (i.e., in the absence of sequestra) rather than chronic infection. Establishing a biosignature associated with protection would facilitate the selection of adjuvants to improve both existing and new vaccines.

Epidemiology

Host susceptibility and reservoirs

Under natural conditions, cattle are the species most susceptible to CBPP, including taurine (Bos taurus) and zebu (Bos indicus), and to a minor extent also yak (Bos grunniens) and water buffalo (Bubalus bubalis). Among wild species, a single anecdotal case was reported in American buffalo (Bison bison), but none in African buffalo (Syncerus caffer) or other wild ruminants [10]. Mmm has been isolated from sheep and goats in Africa, Portugal and India, occasionally associated with pathology, including pleuropneumonia [82]. A few experimental infections have succeeded in small ruminants, but their limited success indicates these species are poor hosts (Yaya et al., 2000) and transmission from small ruminants to cattle has never been documented. Neither small ruminants nor wild ungulates are considered epidemiologically significant. Mmm is not a zoonotic agent.

There are conflicting views regarding possible differences in susceptibility between taurine and zebu cattle, with some reports suggesting a disadvantage for the latter, although this has also been linked to breeding systems rather than inherent species susceptibility. However, differences in breed susceptibility have been reported, both with respect to natural disease and post-vaccinal reactions [10]. Furthermore, individual susceptibility to CBPP varies, and a proportion of cattle (typically 10–20%) seem to be naturally resistant to the disease. Age also affects susceptibility, with susceptibility markedly reduced in calves up to six months of age and increasing gradually, reaching full susceptibility in cattle over two years old [10].

No competent vectors have been implicated in CBPP transmission, but goat fleas have been identified as mechanical vectors for the closely related species Mmc, with reproduction of disease in goat kids [83]. In addition, ticks collected from sheep and goats have been found to harbour viable M. agalactiae strains, suggesting a potential role for ectoparasites as reservoirs or vectors of mycoplasma strains [84]. The possible involvement of ectoparasites in transmitting Mmm strains has not been investigated, but it is considered unlikely.

Gap analysis

It is generally accepted that certain breeds and individuals exhibit reduced susceptibility to CBPP [10], but the genetic determinants of resistance to infection have not yet been characterised. Presumably, identification of these genetic determinants may eventually allow the selection of resistant animals through breeding, though the value of such strategy is debatable, as it may compromise other important attributes such as trypanotolerance. Furthermore, resistance to infection may also depend on other factors influencing immune competence. For example, it is thought that external and internal parasite infestations may have immunomodulatory effects, leading to lower susceptibility, while concomitant disease may either impair or stimulate immune responses; it has also been suggested that animals in better physical condition may be more susceptible to CBPP (Thiaucourt et al., 2018) [23]. The potential role of other diseases (e.g., tuberculosis, pasteurellosis, lumpy skin disease (LSD), foot and mouth disease (FMD), East Coast fever, dermatophilosis) in predisposing to or triggering CBPP has not been investigated. However, this is considered of secondary importance, as Mmm is a primary pathogen capable of fulfilling Koch’s postulates.

In young animals, CBPP generally presents with reduced susceptibility and atypical manifestations, which has been linked both to colostral immunity and to the immaturity of the immune system. However, the precise mechanisms underlying age-related susceptibility remain poorly understood. Based on preliminary infection studies, it has been hypothesised that infected calves may remain asymptomatic for prolonged periods and later develop disease in adulthood, but this phenomenon has not been conclusively documented in peer-reviewed research. This gap could be addressed through follow-up studies of the course of the disease in herds during outbreaks, combined with participatory epidemiology, to assess the infection and disease status of young animals in endemic areas.

Incubation, morbidity and mortality, recovery

The incubation period is highly variable, ranging from two weeks to six months, with an estimated mean of between four and six weeks [30], and the shortest duration recorded in experimental infection trials. As previously described, clinical disease may present in hyperacute, acute, subacute, or chronic forms, these being defined by the severity of the symptoms and mortality rate. Animals that do not die develop chronic disease, which may take many months to resolve, with sequestra harbouring viable Mmm detected as long as 30 months after infection [79]. Experimental trials in Australia resulted in 8% of the infected cattle developing the hyperacute form, 14% acute, 30% mild, and 23% asymptomatic with seroconversion, while 25% were fully resistant,10% of the survivors developed sequestra [10].

In the initial stages of an epidemic, the disease tends to be acute, but as it progresses, chronic cases predominate. In fully susceptible herds, following a first introduction, mortality may be as high as 80% in the absence of antibiotic treatment. Conversely, in endemic areas, the disease tends to be mostly chronic and subclinical. When CBPP was reintroduced in the Western Province of Zambia in 1969, this outbreak resulted in 75% morbidity and 68% mortality [5], while previously mentioned experimental infection trials in Australia produced mortality rates ranging from 50 to 90% [10].

Gap analysis

The inability to determine the disease status of the animals with regard to CBPP (naive, infected, carrier, recovered) in the absence of clinical symptoms and serological response represents a major gap. This limitation not only complicates diagnosis and prognosis but also prevents accurate estimation of the duration of different disease stages. Identification of biomarkers is needed to discriminate naive from infected and recovered animals, and predict outcomes (death, chronic carriage, or recovery). Detecting silent carrier animals in incubation or chronic carrier states would be particularly valuable, given their potential epidemiological significance. Similar challenges have been addressed in other chronic infections such as tuberculosis, where immunological and molecular markers have been successfully developed to differentiate latent from active disease stages [85]. Building on the approaches described earlier for prognostic biomarkers of disease severity, longitudinal sampling combined with immunological and molecular profiling should be prioritised to validate candidate markers and standardise their application in both experimental and field settings.

Finally, as previously noted, animals that survive CBPP develop long-term immunity [79], but it remains uncertain whether reinfection can occur or whether life-long protection is achieved. Again, targeted follow-up studies combined with participatory epidemiology could help clarify the duration and completeness of immunity in recovered animals.

Transmission, geographical distribution and spread

Mmm is transmitted directly from infected to susceptible animals, primarily by the airborne route, via infective droplets excreted through coughing and sneezing. Other sources of transmission have been reported (saliva, urine, foetal membranes, uterine discharges) and transplacental infection can occur [30]. Transmission is most effective when animals are close together at watering points or grazing, or when transported together. Airborne transmission over a distance of up to 200 m may occur under favourable climatic conditions, and there are a few anecdotal reports of transmission through fomites [86], although environmental persistence and indirect transmission are thought to be unimportant [30]. The transmission of CBPP depends on three main factors: the rate of excretion from infected animals, the frequency and duration of contact between infected and susceptible animals, and the size and density of the susceptible cattle population [5]. Moreover, disease incidence and prevalence vary depending on cattle production systems: they are higher in extensive agropastoral systems compared with intensive cattle systems where animals are confined [87].

Mmm excretion can occur during the incubation period for up to 40 days [19], while animals show no clinical signs and no serological response can be detected. Similarly, clinically recovered cattle may develop pulmonary sequestra, which can persist and retain viable organisms for many months (up to 27 months [79],). It has been hypothesised that sequestra may reactivate, particularly under stress or immunosuppression, leading to shedding and CBPP transmission, but this has not been demonstrated experimentally. On the contrary, attempts to infect naive cattle by contact with recovered, chronically-infected animals were unsuccessful despite significant stresses imposed on the animals [79], suggesting that chronic carriers play a very limited role in CBPP transmission. Efforts to reactivate sequestra by corticosteroid treatment and splenectomy were also unsuccessful. However, the recovered animals in this study were infected by intubation, and outcomes might differ under natural conditions. As acknowledged by the authors, these findings do not rule out the possibility “that under certain circumstances the carrier could break down and spread disease as described in the literature”. This debate continues, and the potential role of silent carriers (whether during the incubation or chronic stages) in maintaining CBPP remains widely accepted [30].

The transboundary potential of CBPP is very high. Outbreaks often occur as a result of cattle movements leading to contact between infected and naive animals. Consequently, the speed and extent of spatial spread depend strongly on the frequency and intensity of movements of infected cattle.

CBPP is found within an area south of the Sahara Desert, from the Tropic of Cancer in the north to the Tropic of Capricorn in the south, and currently affects about 25 sub-Saharan African countries, according to WOAH reports [4]. Two trends have been identified in Africa: epidemic areas with sporadic outbreaks in northern Namibia and Zambia, and endemic areas with episodes of high clinical disease interspersed with periods of quiescence [87]. Prevalence rates ranging from 0.29% to 10.5% have been reported in these endemic areas [4], though important differences in the protocols used for prevalence estimations (e.g., at individual versus herd level,with or without targeting animals with a history of respiratory disease make it difficult to compare data from different studies.

The situation in Asia is uncertain, due to lack of efficient surveillance (with the exception of countries such as Japan, China, Singapore, India and Mongolia, which have been recognised as CBPP-free by the WOAH). However, the disease has been reported recently in Saudi Arabia and Malaysia [88], as well as Pakistan [89]. In Europe, CBPP has not been reported since the last case detected in Portugal in 1999. The detailed information on the occurrence of CBPP may be obtained from the WOAH database interface (WAHIS, WOAH, https://www.woah.org/en/what-we-do/animal-health-and-welfare/disease-data-collection/world-animal-health-information-system/; accessed 14 April 2025).

Gap analysis

The potential carriage of Mmm in the ear canal, either directly or via ectoparasites acting as mechanical vectors, has not been investigated. However, as previously noted, the significance of such alternative transmission mechanisms is considered minimal. Similarly, the potential role of concurrent infections in triggering CBPP is not regarded as a primary concern, as co-infections are unlikely to be critical for CBPP establishment or persistence. Therefore, rather than conducting dedicated co-infection studies, more cost-effective approaches mentioned previously, such as participatory epidemiology and longitudinal field surveillance, should be explored to clarify the influence of concurrent infections on disease dynamics.

CBPP is a classic example of transboundary animal disease requiring coordinated efforts between neighbouring countries for its effective prevention and control [4]. Data on transboundary movements in Africa are rarely collected, and are neither centralised nor harmonised. No information is available regarding illegal animal movements. Trade data collected by commercial and trade platforms, like the Comtrade database of the United Nations (https://comtradeplus.un.org/) should be explored to provide an overview of the temporal patterns of animal movements to assess the risk of introduction into naive areas. At national and regional levels, more fine-scale mobility data need to be collected to map movement corridors and their seasonal variation.

There is no general pattern for the re-occurrence of outbreaks and cases that may indicate the role of environmental or climatic conditions in transmission. Local conditions can influence husbandry practices, the seasonality of animal movements, and demographic trends. Consequently, the strategies adopted by cattle owners to cope with demographics with these changes and their impact on herd demographics should be investigated. Participatory approaches that give voice to livestock owners can help gather information on local practices and provide insight into how they have been affected by climate and other changes.

The true distribution and incidence of CBPP should be described in greater detail, as disease reporting to WOAH is extremely limited and significantly underestimates its occurrence. The current situation of CBPP in North Africa and Asia also requires clarification. In North Africa, trade routes creating new axes of movement movements to and from sub-Saharan Africa increase the risk of CBPP spread [90]. In Asia, positive CBPP serology has been reported in Malaysia, although the disease has not been officially confirmed by specific identification of the agent. Improved disease reporting will require efforts to raise awareness and build capacity in CBPP surveillance.

Impact

CBPP, as with many transboundary animal diseases, can have a range of socio-economic impacts. Mortality may reach 80%, particularly in naive cattle populations, with potentially devastating consequences for households and societies that depend on cattle for their livelihoods. CBPP can severely reduce animal productivity in endemic areas. Livelihood impacts are significant, and CBPP can negatively affect social status, given the role cattle play in rural societies. For instance, the use of cattle in social events such as marriages and dowry payments can be influenced by the prohibition of cattle movement during outbreaks. Similarly, since wealth and status in many rural societies in Africa are measured by the number of cattle owned by farmers, the loss of animals due to CBPP can translate into a loss in societal standing [91].

Although several studies have examined the socio-economic impacts of CBPP, the evidence remains fragmented and, compared with other transboundary diseases such as FMD and classical and African swine fever, does not employ the full range of economic techniques that could inform impact assessment [92]. Estimates of direct impacts at primary production stage, particularly at farm level, have not been comprehensively quantified across a broad evidence base at national or global scales. A few, largely outdated studies exist for Africa, but no prospective impact studies for non-African contexts have been identified in the literature.

At the macro level in Africa, various estimates of the impact of CBPP have been made. Masiga et al. [93] estimated the direct and indirect costs of CBPP in 27 endemic countries in Africa at USD 2 billion per year. Tambi et al. [87], in a sample of 12 African countries, estimated the direct costs of CBPP at EUR 30 million per year (mortality and morbidity), while indirect costs were estimated at EUR 14.7 million. Production losses in the Narok district of Kenya were estimated at USD 1.62 million per year [91]. Onono et al. [94] estimated the direct costs of CBPP to Kenya at USD 2.1 million per year and the indirect costs at USD 5.5 million per year, taking into account different control strategies such as vaccination, slaughter, and treatment.

Looking at more micro-level analyses, the available literature highlights the impact of CBPP outbreaks on prices. Market closures may increase local supply and depress prices. Conversely, outbreaks may also reduce the availability of meat, increasing its price and contributing to local inflation [8]. Outbreaks can also affect milk production, leading to protein deficiency. The use of oxen power in agriculture is severely impaired during outbreaks of the disease resulting in decreased ploughing of farmland and transportation of farm produce, and contributing to further reductions in food supplies. Such food insecurity may, in the long term, lead to a loss of societal cohesion.

Gap analysis

As noted, the suite of studies available on socio-economic impacts of CBPP is rather dated and does not take advantage of advances in economic impact assessment techniques applied in other disease contexts. For instance, there are no studies on CBPP that measure indirect costs more precisely so to highlight the range of impacts in ancillary sectors as well as dynamic effects over time (see [95–97] for examples in the case of other diseases). These issues are important, not only to guide resource allocation decisions, but also to enhance the deployment of epidemiological models of disease spread. Such integrated models can highlight the trade-offs that may exist between technical interventions (e.g., stamping-out vs. vaccination) and their economic impacts on trade and livelihoods at different temporal levels (e.g., short vs. long term, see [97]).

A more rigorous assessment of the impacts of CBPP on livelihoods is also an important gap in the literature. While the dimensions of impact on social cohesion and status have been enumerated, the use of participatory or anthropological tools to explore the nuances of these impacts is lacking, particularly in comparison with other transboundary diseases. Issues of gender dynamics, which play important roles in disease control settings for poultry and small ruminants, are wholly unexplored in the context of CBPP and constitute an important area for future inquiry [98]. Recent work and approaches on gender and animal disease used by Aboah et al. [99] in the context of PPR may hold promise.

Mathematical and economic modelling

Modelling CBPP transmission provides a better estimation of the basic reproductive ratio (R0, an estimator of the epidemic potential of the disease) and enables the assessment of both the impact of the disease and of control measures. At least 13 mathematical models have been published between 2004 and 2025 (only the most relevant are cited here). Twelve models focus on simulating the impact of the disease, the role of chronic carriers and interactions with other herds in maintaining the disease, as well as the impact of control measures on disease circulation; one model provides a quantitative assessment of the risk of disease introduction [100]. The transmission models include the compartments Susceptible, Exposed, Infectious, Carrier and Recovered, and in some of them, the Infectious and Carrier (chronic) states are further subdivided. The temporal evolution of the population progresses from the Susceptible to the Exposed states, after which animals can either become Infectious or Carriers before Recovering. If an animal is Infectious, it can also die from the disease. Some models include Treated and/or Vaccinated compartments [101–103]. The force of infection is estimated as a linear combination of the Infectious and Carrier states. The duration of the Carrier state varies across studies, from 120 days [104] to 1 year [105]. The role and infectivity associated with the carrier state are examined by Lesnoff et al. [106]. All models have been developed at village and local community scales. They are not age-structured, although new-borns (younger than 6–9 months) are considered resistant to CBPP. In the cases of Ezanno & Lesnoff [105] and Mariner et al. [107], interactions among herds belonging to nearby communities are considered. No seasonality is included in either the force of infection or demographic processes. Estimates of R0 vary from 1.24 in an endemic situation [105] to 4.5 in an epidemic context [103], between 3.4 and 4.7 according to stochastic simulations in the East African context [108], and up to 27 in an experimental setting with small herds [104].

Four control approaches have been modelled: vaccination, treatment, cattle movement control, and stamping-out through slaughter with or without compensation. Each of these aims to reduce R0 and limit the spread of the disease to uninfected areas.

Calibration of mathematical models using epidemiological data may provide estimates of CBPP morbidity and mortality, as well as its economic impact and cost–benefit analysis. Most studies on CBPP have been conducted at micro levels of analysis (herd or community levels), using relatively unsophisticated economic models, often based on accounting methods or partial budgets informed by primary surveys or semi-structured techniques. The economic impact study by Tambi et al. [87] provides a cost–benefit analysis of interventions against CBPP in 12 sub-Saharan countries, taking into account direct and indirect costs. The authors estimated that an investment of EUR 14.7 million to control CBPP would prevent a loss of EUR 30 million in these countries. This approach, while comprehensive in detailing a range of national-level losses informed by disease epidemiology, did not consider some of the second-round impacts on markets and trade that might arise from endemic disease, which more complex models could capture. Similar accounting approaches were taken by Masiga et al. [93] and Onono et al. [94].

Gap analysis

CBPP transmission models developed to date have not been explicitly calibrated using epidemiological data. This is a critical point, as such calibration could provide estimates of the force of infection, which in turn would inform R0. However, estimating the force of infection is complicated by the presence of carriers, which may silently maintain the disease in circulation, and by the short duration of seroconversion, which can hinder the use of simplified catalytic models. Nevertheless, livestock movements are the primary drivers of disease maintenance and geographical spread. Therefore, it would be useful to develop age-stratified models that account for demographic trends and husbandry practices, and to calibrate their results using incidence data. Field and experimental data should also be included to validate hypotheses concerning the role of chronic carriers and the existence of fully recovered animals. This would help to provide initial estimates of the force of infection.

To estimate the speed of disease diffusion, network analysis and statistical models should be developed to analyse patterns of outbreak declarations (including geographical information and time stamps) and correlate them with mobility patterns. This would provide insights into epidemic dynamics and, most importantly, help identify areas and periods at risk.

An important gap in economic modelling has been the over-reliance on relatively unsophisticated techniques for assessing the impact of CBPP, with a heavy dependence on partial budgeting t rather than more comprehensive models that integrate epidemiological dynamics. Given the societal, cultural, and economic importance of cattle raising, as well as the multi-scale impact of the disease (household, community, national levels), more sophisticated computational tools could be adapted from other diseases. From an economic perspective, Rich et al. [92] outlined a range of modelling tools that could be used more generally in the context of animal diseases, including CBPP. The choice of modelling tool depends on the level of analysis targeted (i.e., micro/herd/community, regional/national, or macro/international). More advanced approaches, such as partial equilibrium models (which focus on the livestock sector), multi-market models (which consider interactions between livestock and other agricultural markets, such as food and feed), and general equilibrium models (which assess economy-wide impacts following a shock to the livestock sector) are generally applied at national or international levels. These can be integrated with epidemiological models to calibrate specific shocks [97]. A key advantage of such approaches lies in their ability to capture market responses to disease-induced shocks in terms of price and quantity adjustments, while also quantifying welfare effects across markets. Additional gaps in economic modelling are provided in the previous section on impacts.

To our knowledge, no examples exist of integrated transmission and economic models that account for temporal dinamics and herder’s daptive behaviour. Econometric techniques and, increasingly, systems modelling (see [99] for a recent example) have been employed for other animal diseases at this level of analysis and could be easily adapted to CBPP. In particular, systems models combine disease dynamics at herd, household, and marketing levels, offering a more holistic framework for scenario analysis [109] that explicitly incorporates temporal and behavioural shifts.

Diagnostics

Because classical CBPP signs and lesions are highly indicative, if not pathognomonic, syndromic surveillance in the field and findings from post-mortem examinations are critical for diagnosis. Passive surveillance, including abattoir and slaughter slab inspection of lungs, pleura and associated lymph nodes, combined with the identification of suspect lesions, appropriate sampling and rapid diagnostic testing, is essential to enable a swift response and maintain CBPP-free status. For this reason, rapid molecular diagnostic methods are applied upon clinical/pathological suspicion in Europe and other CBPP-free regions. In addition, the complement fixation test (CFT; [110]), competitive ELISA (cELISA [111]), and immunoblotting [112, 113] are also used for animal testing prior to trade. According to the WOAH Manual [1], CFT and cELISA are recommended for screening and eradication programmes, while the highly specific immunoblotting test serves as a confirmatory tool but is unsuitable for mass screening. CFT and cELISA have similar sensitivity (≈ 60%,[114], but cELISA offers higher specificity (> 99.9%) compared with CFT (98%) [114, 115], which is valuable in CBPP-free countries.

In African countries where CBPP is endemic, diagnosis relies primarily on clinical and abattoir surveillance, with limited use of direct pathogen detection. CFT and cELISA are occasionally applied for diagnosis and prevalence studies, particularly for assessing the efficacy of vaccination campaigns [7]. For this purpose, sampling should be performed at least three months post-vaccination, when post-vaccinal antibody responses are no longer detectable [5]. Currently, these two assays are the only commercially available tests for CBPP diagnosis. Extensive investigations were undertaken to characterise the immunogenic surface proteins of Mmm and to detect antibodies against the corresponding recombinant proteins in positive bovine sera [116–118]. Based on these and other studies, alternative serological detection methods were developed [119, 120], although their commercialisation was either discontinued or not achieved.

Isolation of Mmm remains the WOAH prescribed confirmatory test and a prerequisite for antimicrobial susceptibility testing, but is rarely performed due to complex culture requirements, high costs and time demands. Identification of cultures by classical biochemical and immunological characterisation is rarely used today, as molecular methods are more specific, sensitive, rapid and easier to perform and standardise. Moreover, these methods can be applied directly to clinical samples, avoiding culture. Numerous conventional and real-time PCR assays have been published, and validated methods are listed in the CBPP manual [121–127].

Gap analysis

There have been severe shortages in the production and supply of cELISA and CFT kits and reagents for CBPP serology. A highly specific commercial ELISA is required for use in international trade, as well as for field surveillance and prevalence studies. Furthermore, the currently available serological assays (CFT and cELISA) do not allow the assessment of asymptomatic carriage, which limits their sensitivity; therefore, results must be interpreted at herd level. Research aimed at developing more sensitive kits for individual-level diagnosis has been identified as a longer-term need. The identification of biomarkers to detect silent carriers during incubation, latent and chronic stages would be particularly valuable.

No commercial molecular detection kits re currently validated for diagnostic use, as there are no data on the diagnostic performance (specificity and sensitivity) of the few products available on the market, which are intended for research purposes only. Although several molecular methods have been described in the literature, some are limited in specificity due to potential cross-reactions with members of the “M. mycoides” cluster. Furthermore, none have been validated under ISO 17025 accreditation. Direct molecular assays should be developed into commercial, affordable kits and validated by international standards and inter-laboratory assays. Affordable freeze-dried products should be prioritised to minimise cold-chain requirements, which present significant technical and economic challenges in Africa.

There are currently no pen-side tests available for rapid field diagnosis, either for serological diagnosis or for direct detection of the CBPP agent. A slide agglutination test using inactivated, stained Mmm antigen, although less sensitive and specific than CFT or cELISA, proved useful for rapid diagnosis of acute cases in the field [128]. A latex-based version was later commercialised, but production was discontinued due to its high market price. Similarly, no industrial partnership was secured for a lateral flow test developed for rapid serological screening [120], while a direct detection assay based on LAMP technology was also developed [129] but has not been adopted for field use. Affordable, robust pen-side assays must be developed and commercialised, with attention to cost-effectiveness and operational feasibility for rapid screening in Africa.

International inter-laboratory assays are needed to compare existing diagnostic assays, in order to validate available assays and guide the selection of appropriate methods for CBPP diagnosis. However, the availability of reference materials and well-characterised sample panels from experimentally and naturally infected animals remains very limited. Such resources are essential for the validation of both existing and newly developed diagnostic assays.

As noted in the section related to the pathogen, whole genome sequencing and comparative omics analyses will inform improved diagnostics and strain genotyping. Pen-side tests and DIVA assays (in conjunction with DIVA vaccines) are required but their cost-effectiveness must be addressed. Market studies are thus needed to assess demand and affordability, and the involvement of manufacturing companies is essential to translate innovations into successful commercial products. These studies should also explore strategic options for developing multiplex tests capable of detecting multiple pathogens in a single assay, thereby reducing costs and increasing diagnostic capacity for priority cattle diseases.

Therapeutics and antimicrobial resistance

The use of antibiotics to treat CBPP is currently either banned or officially discouraged in countries where the disease is endemic, a position long supported by the WOAH and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). However, this policy has been questioned by many researchers over the years [107, 130, 131] and in 2006 and 2015 experts met in Rome to discuss the possible role of antimicrobials in the control and eradication of CBPP [132, 133]. A policy change on this matter has been hindered by insufficient data on CBPP antibiotherapy.

The two main reasons for discouraging the use of antimicrobials have been (i) the notion that treating sick animals may facilitate the development of silent carriers by inducing the formation of sequestra; and (ii) the risk of AMR development, which is one of the greatest health challenges of our time [134].

Mycoplasmas are intrinsically resistant to many antibiotics (e.g., those targeting the cell wall) and rapidly develop resistance to aminoglycosides, which should therefore be avoided [5]. The main antibiotic classes that have been shown to be effective against Mmm are tetracyclines, macrolides, lincosamides and fluoroquinolones. Despite official restrictions, it is well known that cattle owners frequently resort to antimicrobials to treat CBPP, often using oxytetracycline or tylosin. Antibiotics are also used to manage post-vaccinal reactions and prevent vaccine rejection by farmers. Unlike vaccines, antimicrobials are freely available and used with little or no antibiotic stewardship [135, 136]. The quality of antimicrobials, as well as the optimal dose and duration of treatment, is not guaranteed, and withdrawal periods are often not respected. These poor practices increase the risk of AMR development, both in mycoplasmas and in other bacteria pathogenic to animals and humans. The increasing emergence of resistant Mycoplasma bovis strains and other species, particularly those pathogenic to humans, is a major concern [137].

Only a few reports on the in vitro sensitivity of Mmm to different antimicrobials are available [138–140], and few controlled studies have assessed the effect of specific antimicrobials in vivo. Published cattle trial data showed that long-acting oxytetracycline reduced both clinical disease and the number of sequestra formed, although it did not completely prevent their formation. Similarly, isolation of live Mmm from sequestra was markedly reduced, but not eliminated [141–144]. Two out of these studies reported the effect of oxytetracycline in reducing transmission to in-contact animals [141, 142]. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as one study lacked a control group [142] and the other involved very small in-contact groups (five animals per group [141]). Muuka et al. also tested the effect of two third-generation macrolides (tulathromycin and gamithromycin) with similar outcomes: clinical signs and lesions were reduced, but not eliminated, and live Mmm could still be recovered from lesions, albeit at much lower rates than in controls. No transmission was observed, but the low transmission in control groups limits interpretation.

Field studies on antimicrobial use during CBPP outbreaks are understandably rare [107, 108, 145–147]. In Ethiopia, no clear benefit of measures applied by farmers (including antibiotic treatment of sick animals) was observed. The poor outcome was attributed to limited statistical power and low quality of both the medications used and the health-care delivery systems [146]. Kenyan farmers reported that tetracycline treatment often resulted in disease relapse [145], and in Ethiopia, only tylosin prevented relapse, whereas tetracycline and penicillin–streptomycin did not [148]. Conversely, [130] evaluated the efficacy of the fluoroquinolone danofloxacin in naturally infected cattle in Namibia. While no improvement in clinical disease or lesions was observed in treated cattle, transmission to in-contact animals, lesion development, and isolation of live Mmm from those animals were reduced. Danofloxacin was subsequently used in a metaphylactic campaign targeting all animals in clinically affected or seropositive herds. This approach led to a progressive decline in cases, with only a few deaths in the next three months and no further outbreaks over two years, which had not been achieved previously with vaccination alone [131]. These findings support the view that targeted antibiotherapy, alongside regular mass vaccination, could play a valuable role in the control of CBPP.

Since antibiotherapy has not been prioritised for CBPP control, few specific developments have been pursued and only limited alternative treatments have been explored. Clinical studies evaluated Cyclosporin A, an immunosuppressant that inhibits T-cell activation, to modulate the immune response in cattle exposed to CBPP [149]. While the treatment did not prevent disease, it delayed clinical, humoral and pathological changes. Additionally, several studies assessed the efficacy of alternative therapies against mycoplasma species, including Mmm and Mmc [150–153]. Extracts from medicinal plants showed some inhibitory activity in vitro, though their toxicity, pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, and in vivo efficacy were not explored.

Gap analysis

Despite widespread antimicrobial use in the field to treat CBPP and other bovine respiratory diseases, little is known about which antibiotics are used, the extent of their use, treatment regimens applied, and their impacts on CBPP control and AMR emergence. More data from controlled and field studies on the effectiveness of therapeutics (particularly the efficacy of strategic intervention programs incorporating antibiotic treatments) are needed to inform a policy change on the role of antibiotherapy in CBPP control and eradication. Such policy could enable the development of essential guidelines, promote antibiotic stewardship, and reduce both side effects and AMR risks.

The impact of antibiotics on immunity, both after recovery and when combined with vaccination (i.e., for treatment of post-vaccinal reactions), requires further assessment. Similarly, the effects of antibiotic treatment on pathogen shedding and disease transmission from treated and in-contact animals should be explored.

Combined therapies, including anti-inflammatory drugs, and alternative antimicrobial agents remain unexplored. For example, the sensitivity of Mmm to antimicrobial peptides and bacteriophages has not been investigated.

The prevalence and clinical significance of in vivo acquired resistance to different antimicrobials by Mmm strains remain unknown. There are no standardised protocols or reference controls available for determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values of Mmm to currently used antibiotics. Epidemiological cut-off values (ECOFFs, which indicate the MIC threshold above which bacterial isolates exhibit phenotypically detectable acquired resistance) are not available for Mmm or other animal mycoplasmas. Likewise, clinical breakpoints, linking MIC results to treatment outcomes, have not been established. These cut-offs would improve clinical interpretation of MICs and assist veterinarians in the rational use of antibiotics. However, determining ECOFFs under EUCAST/VetCAST guidelines will be challenging due to scarcity of Mmm isolates worldwide.

The molecular mechanisms underlying AMR acquisition by Mmm are also poorly characterised. Only a few mutations in antimicrobial target sites have been identified; and these were associated with reduced antimicrobial susceptibility in vitro, suggesting a link between these mutations and resistance phenotypes induced under experimental conditions. No plasmids carrying resistance genes or efflux mechanisms have been detected. Known mechanisms in other mycoplasmas include active efflux, target modification and ribosome protection by the tet(M) determinant [137, 154].Some may be identified through molecular methods informed by knowledge from other mycoplasma species and should guide future policies on antibiotic use for CBPP treatment.

Vaccines

Historically, several live attenuated vaccines were developed and used for CBPP control worldwide: strains V5 in Australia, DK32 in Senegal, KH3J in Sudan, T1/44 and T1sr in various African regions, and Ben-181 in China [10, 155], [156, 157]). Attenuation of Mmm strains was achieved by repeated passages in broth (V5, KH3J), embryonated eggs (T1/44) or rabbits (Ben-181).

Over time, T1/44 and its streptomycin-resistant derivative T1sr became the recommended vaccines for CBPP control in Africa and, at present, they are the only vaccines commercially available in endemic regions. T1 vaccines have important limitations: low efficacy after primary vaccination and short-lived immunity, thus requiring revaccination every 6 (T1sr) or 12 (T1/44) months [158, 159]. Furthermore, T1/44 exhibits residual virulence, which results in occasional cases of severe inflammation at the injection site (i.e., the Willems’ reaction). As previously mentioned, if left untreated these local reactions may spread and, in some cases, lead to the death of the animal, which may discourage vaccine acceptance by farmers [160]. However, these reactions are mainly observed after the first vaccination and their occurrence is rare following revaccination [161]. No such side effects have been reported for T1sr.

T1/44 and T1sr are produced as freeze-dried live vaccines, with cold-chain requirements for storage, distribution, and delivery. To be effective, these vaccines must contain at least 107 colony-forming units per dose of live Mmm [1]. After reconstitution, the delivery time is limited to between 30 min and 2 h, depending on the manufacturer. Under field conditions and in hot climates, viability rapidly falls below the minimum recommended titre, compromising vaccine efficacy. Both cold-chain dependence and safety concerns mean that T1 vaccines must be administered by trained personnel. Consequently, farmers do not have access to CBPP vaccines outside the framework of campaigns organised by governmental veterinary services, which often operate with limited resources, compromising the overall success of vaccination [162].

The Pan-African Veterinary Vaccine Centre of the African Union (AU-PANVAC) holds the grandparental stocks of T1/44 and T1sr for distribution to vaccine manufacturers and provides independent quality control (QC) for veterinary vaccine batches used in Africa. The quality of CBPP vaccines is known to vary among producers and batches, which may result in inconsistent vaccine efficacy. A list of producers whose vaccines have been certified by AU-PANVAC between 2014 and 2022 is available at: https://aupanvac.org/brizy-4163/certified-vaccines-batchs/. The current QC of CBPP vaccines allows the assessment of identity, purity and titres (as a proxy for potency) [1]. Deep sequencing was recently used to assess the genetic drift in T1 vaccines, and signatures of cloning procedures were detected in commercial batches, indicating deviations from standard production protocols [163]. Therefore, genome variant analysis has been proposed as an additional QC tool, but it is not yet implemented.

In the twentieth century, attempts were made to develop inactivated vaccines based on either pleural fluid or Mmm cultures, tested with several adjuvanted formulations. However, this approach was largely abandoned, as live vaccines seemed both efficient and more cost-effective [156, 164]. Renewed efforts have been reported over recent decades. Mmm bacterins in oil emulsion [165] or formulated with Freund’s Complete Adjuvant [166] induced good protection and improved thermostability [165]. Regarding the development of subunit recombinant vaccines, initial attempts met with very limited success. The use of immunostimulant complexes (ISCOMs) as carriers for detergent-solubilised Mmm cell membranes induced strong primary and long-lasting secondary antibody responses, as well as a cell-mediated immune response in cattle [167], but did not confer protection [168]. Similarly, subunit vaccine candidates based on recombinant GlpO [169] or five putative variable surface proteins of Mmm [170] failed to protect cattle against CBPP challenge, and a recombinant LppQ vaccine even exacerbated disease [69]. However, a two-shot immunisation protocol using a vaccine based on Mmm-derived CPS conjugated to ovalbumin resulted in reduced pathology [171], and application of reverse vaccinology led to a prototype vaccine based on Mmm recombinant proteins that elicited protective immune responses against CBPP challenge [172]. Despite these efforts no alternative vaccines have reached the market to date and no DIVA vaccines are currently available for CBPP control.

Finally, multivalent vaccine strategies have been explored to reduce the cost of vaccination campaigns. A bivalent T1sr-rinderpest vaccine retained the activity of both components and was successfully employed during the rinderpest eradication campaign [10, 156]. Recently, a bivalent T1/44-LSDV live attenuated vaccine formulation showed safety and immunogenicity [173]. Concomitant administration of the bivalent vaccine with a live Rift Valley fever vaccine (in a separate formulation) resulted in satisfactory protection against all three diseases, as assessed by in vivo challenge 13 months post-vaccination [174]. However, the reported increase in protection from 6 to 13 months post-vaccination for the combined vaccine is unexpected and requires confirmation.

Gap analysis

Current live CBPP vaccines present several drawbacks across the production–delivery chain. Limited capacity and resources of governmental manufacturing plants, coupled with deficiencies in QC, result in low vaccine availability and inconsistent quality. In addition, the cold-chain requirements for storage, distribution and administration pose a great challenge to the implementation of vaccination-based intervention strategies. Furthermore, short-lived immunity and safety concerns associated with T1 vaccines reduce farmer acceptance.

In the short- and medium-term, research efforts should focus on the following priorities: The potency and duration of immunity of current T1 vaccines could be enhanced by incorporating additives acting as adjuvants to strengthen protective immunity, while thermostability could be improved by adding stabilisers to the freeze-dried formulations. The QC could be improved by monitoring the extent of genetic drift in T1 strains used in new vaccine batches compared to AU-PANVAC reference stocks, and WOAH guidelines should be enforced [1, 163]. Multivalent or combined vaccine strategies should also be considered to improve cost-effectiveness. Additionally, the efficacy of inactivated or subunit vaccine formulated with modern adjuvants, should also be investigated. These formulations should provide improved thermostability and safety and may create opportunities for developing DIVA vaccines and multivalent strategies. Finally, as previously noted, a more robust and reproducible in vivo challenge model, together with a standardised scoring system, must be established to evaluate new vaccines or vaccine formulations.

Looking towards future vaccine developments in the long-term, the priority is to obtain safer, thermostable vaccines that induce strong and long-lasting protection (exceeding one year) against mortality, clinical disease, and transmission. Such vaccines should be made accessible to the private sector, reducing reliance on national vaccination campaigns. The development of DIVA vaccines and their companion diagnostic tests would be particularly valuable in countries implementing eradication plans. In addition, multivalent vaccine formulations could substantially reduce vaccination costs in low- & middle-income countries. Market studies will be required to support the implementation of these strategies.

Recent advances in molecular biology and vaccinology offer new opportunities for the development of innovative vaccines based on targeted attenuation, as well as adjuvanted inactivated and subunit approaches. Progress will require a better characterisation of the molecular determinants of Mmm protection and, most importantly, of the host immune mechanisms involved. Identifying correlates of protection would enable the rational design of vaccines that elicit effective immune responses through optimised adjuvants and delivery systems.

However, the lack of biomarkers that serve as reliable correlates of protection remains a major obstacle to the evaluation of modern vaccine candidates. Efficacy assessment still depends on costly CBPP challenge experiments in cattle, which are logistically difficult to conduct in CBPP-free countries, while in endemic regions the lack of advanced research infrastructure and equipment further limits such studies.

In the event that a modern prototype vaccine is validated, the next challenge will be local production using new technologies. This will require investments in improved infrastructure and capacity building in manufacturing plants in Africa. Moreover, the current regulatory framework may complicate the registration of novel vaccines developed through bioengineering or gene-editing technologies, as these fall under GMO regulations.

Control and eradication strategies

Historical examples of successful CBPP eradications have been reviewed by Thiaucourt [5] and El Idrissi Hamzi et al. [4]. A strict stamping-out protocol consisting of the slaughter of affected and in-contact cattle was used to eradicate the disease in the USA and the UK at the end of the nineteenth century, when modern diagnostic tools were not available. In Australia, eradication was achieved by combining vaccination with stamping-out and strict control of animal movements [175]. The disease prevalence was first greatly reduced by applying successive rounds of serological testing every three months, followed by elimination of positive cases and vaccination of all other animals. Vaccination was then discontinued and complete eradication was achieved by slaughtering the remaining infected herds. More recently, following sporadic CBPP outbreaks in southern Europe, Italy and Portugal regained CBPP freedom by applying a strict test-and-slaughter strategy [176], resulting in eradication of the disease in 1999.

Stamping-out is considered the most effective control method for CBPP eradication, but slaughter policies have become extremely challenging, even in countries that can afford them [177]. In addition, as CBPP spread is driven by cattle movements, a strict ban on the importation of live cattle from countries where CBPP is present remains the most effective measure to maintain disease-free status. Most importantly, CBPP-free territories at risk must maintain strong diagnostic capability for early detection and surveillance. In the event of an outbreak in Europe and other CBPP-free regions today, stamping-out of all the animals in the affected establishments and stringent restrictions on cattle movements must be implemented, with backward and forward traceability and surveillance activities within and beyond the restricted zones [2].

Today, CBPP is largely confined to sub-Saharan Africa, where endemic and epidemic situations coexist with CBPP-free territories (i.e., Botswana, South Africa, Eswatini, and most of Namibia). There are inconsistencies across national and regional regulations in the continent, as well as significant differences among countries in terms of financial resources, effectiveness of veterinary services, cattle farming systems and cultural practices. Consequently, intervention strategies must be tailored to the disease status of each territory and the capacity of its veterinary services to detect and control the disease [8]. The main intervention strategies are: (i) test-and-slaughter combined with cattle movement control in countries or zones that are either free of the disease or have implemented eradication programmes, and (ii) mass vaccination (using T1/44 or T1sr) in endemic regions.

Successful CBPP control programmes have been implemented in Botswana, Namibia and Zambia. Botswana achieved the eradication of CBPP in the 1990 s through the mass culling of the infected cattle population, supported by a compensation policy that significantly improved the acceptance of this intervention [178]. In addition, strict animal movement control, enforced through fenced barriers and border controls, has contributed to its long- standing CBPP-free status. In Namibia, a double-fenced barrier, known as the veterinary cordon fence or Red Line, separates the northern communal areas, where CBPP is frequently reported and where movement restrictions are difficult to enforce, from the CBPP-free central and southern regions. To date, the efficiency of veterinary services, along with the continuous maintenance and patrolling of the cordon fence, has prevented the reintroduction of CBPP [5, 179]. Zambia serves as example of a WOAH endorsed official control programme [180], involving the progressive and sustainable implementation of combined strategies based on a zoning approach. This includes “infected”, “protected with vaccination”, “protected without vaccination”, “high surveillance” and “disease-free zones” [8]. Measures applied include: (i) annual mass vaccination and sero-surveillance in targeted areas, (ii) animal branding and movement restrictions at the district level, (iii) disease surveillance and stamping-out in CBPP-free zones, (iv) a compensation policy that takes into account farmers’ needs and promotes private sector involvement. This approach has resulted in a progressive reduction of CBPP-affected areas, which are now mainly confined to the borders with Namibia and Tanzania. However, illegal cross-border livestock movements remain a major challenge [8].

Despite some notable successes in CBPP eradication, CBPP remains enzootic in most of sub-Saharan Africa, where severe stamping-out measures are not feasible due to socioeconomic constraints and limited governmental resources for farmer compensation. Most importantly, stamping-out is not effective where strict livestock movement restrictions cannot be enforced to prevent its reintroduction. Porous international borders in sub-Saharan Africa make the implementation of control measures particularly difficult, especially the regulation of cattle movements. These are driven by traditional pastoral rearing systems and are increasing in some regions due to climate change and insecurity. Furthermore, the absence of platforms for animal identification and traceability renders the management of cattle movement one of the most critical challenges for CBPP control.

In CBPP-endemic regions, annual mass vaccination remains the main official intervention strategy. However, several factors reduce the effectiveness of national vaccination campaigns. As noted in the “Vaccines” section, the limitations of T1 vaccines, including short-lived immunity, cold-chain dependence, and safety concerns, require annual re-vaccination of cattle herds to maintain protection and restrict vaccine delivery to veterinary services, limiting herders’ access and consuming substantial public resources. In addition, low production capacity in national manufacturing laboratories and deficient QC compromise both vaccine availability and quality. These challenges, aggravated by limited resources and logistical disorganisation in veterinary services, result in insufficient vaccine coverage, leading to low population immunity and continued disease circulation [4]. The prevalence of CBPP remains high in countries and regions that have implemented regular vaccination campaigns for many years [7], indicating that current vaccination policies are not effective. Historical experience has also shown that control strategies based solely on vaccination have not achieved eradication, as illustrated by the case of Australia [175]. Modelling studies further support this view, predicting that mass vaccination alone, in the absence of additional countermeasures such as stamping-out, movement control or antibiotic treatment, is insufficient to eradicate the disease [108].

Among other potential interventions, antibiotherapy can reduce clinical signs and lesions and, most importantly, limit pathogen shedding and transmission [5]. Despite official restrictions, treatment of CBPP cases remains common practice in infected areas and both promising results from field trials [131] and predictions from mathematical models [107, 181] support the view that targeted antibiotherapy could play a key role in CBPP control.

Gap analysis

International cooperation is essential to address the transboundary spread of CBPP, making the harmonisation of control strategies and the development of regional (multi-country) intervention platforms a priority. Achieving this requires alignment of legal frameworks and operational procedures for disease surveillance, diagnosis, and control. CBPP continues to spread through cattle movements, both within and across countries, yet the full impact of such movements has not been quantified. Measures should be established to monitor cattle movements between zones within a country and across international borders. However, monitoring and controlling livestock movement is particularly challenging in most African countries due to the lack of animal identification and traceability systems. These difficulties are heightened in areas where nomadic and transhumant pastoralism is practised. As these traditional herding practices are closely linked to the epidemiology of CBPP, there is a need for anthropological research to explore how they might be adapted to incorporate disease control measures [8]. Furthermore, the active participation of rural communities is critical, especially in remote regions, and control programmes must therefore include awareness campaigns to explain the long-term benefits of interventions and improve herder understanding, engagement, and compliance.

Diagnosis, notification and reporting are essential for monitoring disease spread and informing targeted interventions. Unfortunately, current laboratory capacity to support CBPP diagnosis is inadequate, or entirely lacking, in many affected regions. Syndromic and abattoir surveillance are often the main sources of epidemiological data. Enhancing these systems through regular training in disease recognition, early response, and reporting, for all stakeholders involved in control efforts, including rural communities, can greatly enhance the effectiveness of surveillance. As previously noted, modern technologies such as distance learning platforms, telediagnosis, and artificial intelligence tools, when combined with mobile applications for disease reporting, have the potential to further strengthen surveillance systems, particularly in areas with limited infrastructure.

Slaughter policies, while effective in theory, are seldom feasible in CBPP-endemic countries due to limited government resources and a lack of compensation mechanisms for affected farmers. This remains the main source of resistance to government-led control measures. To overcome this, strategic compensation policies, that take farmers’ livelihoods into account and encourage private sector, particularly from the beef industry, should be explored and tailored to local contexts.

As previously noted, mass vaccination remains the primary official intervention in endemic regions, despite its limitations. To improve its impact, current implementation gaps must be addressed. Increasing overall coverage is essential to interrupt transmission cycles, with campaigns targeting all cattle from 2–3 months of age and repeated annually to sustain herd immunity. Standardising the exclusive use of T1/44, given its longer duration of protection, may require stronger stewardship, especially upon primary vaccination and in breeds more prone to post-vaccinal reactions. This could include veterinary supervision and, where necessary, appropriate treatment of adverse reactions, ensuring both animal welfare and the sustainability of the campaign.

As previously noted, the efficacy, safety, and delivery requirements of current vaccines present significant challenges to the implementation of vaccination campaigns, making the development of improved vaccines a relevant goal. Inactivated vaccines with enhanced safety and thermostability could be marketed as private goods, thus allowing a change in paradigm from the current government-driven intervention model. Moreover, inactivated vaccines would be particularly well suited to combined strategies that involve treating clinical cases and vaccinating the remaining at-risk population. DIVA vaccines would also be highly valuable in support of eradication programs.

The potential use of antimicrobials in CBPP control and eradication programmes must also be considered. However, any policy shift towards a targeted, regulated use of antibiotics requires supporting experimental data (both in vitro and in vivo), including field data from natural outbreaks. In this way, it may be possible to develop therapeutic regimens that (i) are effective, (ii) limit pathogen shedding and transmission, and (iii) reduce the risk of AMR development. Based on encouraging results from pilot studies, integrated strategies combining mass vaccination, rational antibiotic use, and a slaughter policy governing the use of treated animals for human consumption, could then be considered, validated experimentally, and eventually promoted. The main challenge will be to regulate and monitor antimicrobial use while assessing its impact on AMR development.

In the long-term, eradication remains the most cost-effective solution, both for farmers and for society, particularly through the reduced reliance on antibiotics. It is therefore urgent to shift the current paradigm and explore new strategies to combat and ultimately eradicate CBPP.

Conclusions

We have conducted a systematic review of the different aspects of CBPP and identified the main knowledge gaps and key requirements for improving its control. Furthermore, we have proposed cost-effective actions and practical recommendations to address the most relevant gaps (Table 2).

-Develop flexible compensation schemes based on farmers’ needs; encourage cost-sharing models involving the private sector (e.g., beef industry) to support control/eradication (MT)

CBPP remains endemic in sub-Saharan Africa, where it causes significant, though poorly documented, socio-economic losses. The main strategies currently recommended and partially implemented for disease control include test-and-slaughter, animal movement control, and mass vaccination. However, their effectiveness is severely limited. Testing is costly and rarely accessible; no compensation is given to farmers; and systems for animal identification and movement control are largely non-functional in most affected countries. The use of commercial live attenuated vaccines depends on the financial resources and capacity of the national veterinary services that are typically responsible for their delivery. Issues related to vaccine safety, efficacy, stability and availability further compromise the success of vaccination programmes. It is clear that mass vaccination alone is insufficient to achieve eradication. Therefore, new combinations of feasible intervention strategies are needed. These should include the rational and regulated use of antibiotic treatments, accompanied by antimicrobial susceptibility testing to monitor AMR development in the field, for which neither reliable data nor standardised protocols currently exist.

As CBPP is a transboundary disease and livestock mobility plays a key role in its rapid geographical spread at both national and international levels, coordinated efforts are needed to collect, centralise, and harmonise data on animal movements, disease prevalence, and socio-economic impact across different temporal and spatial scales. This information is essential to identify high-risk areas and guide the implementation of appropriate control strategies. Moreover, such data should be integrated into mathematical models to better assess the potential impact of new combinations of control measures.

The availability of diagnostics remains a challenge. With the introduction of new policies to support CBPP control strategies, demand for diagnostics (both serological and direct detection, laboratory-based and pen-side, including DIVA tests) is likely to increase. Market studies aimed at identifying financially sustainable diagnostic solutions, taking these developments into account, would be highly beneficial.

Knowledge of CBPP pathogenesis and protective immune responses has been limited by the absence of in vivo and in vitro reliable infection models and, until recently, by the lack of bioengineering tools functional in mycoplasmas. Research should prioritise the application of omics technologies and reverse vaccinology to better characterise host–pathogen interactions, identify correlates of protection, and support the rational design of improved vaccines. The development of robust in vivo and in vitro challenge models remains essential to accelerate vaccine development and validation. Meanwhile, the regional regulatory framework for vaccine registration must be updated to accommodate new-generation, engineered vaccines. Pending the development of new tools, the implementation of currently available vaccines and diagnostics must be optimised, including their QC. Finally, training in disease recognition and awareness remains a fundamental and cost-effective component of CBPP control and should be expanded through modern tools such as digital platforms, combined with mobile applications for disease reporting.

In conclusion, improved vaccines and diagnostics are essential to support CBPP control and eradication strategies. These must be accompanied by strategic policies promoting combined interventions, such as vaccination and rational use of antibiotics, validated through well-designed pilot studies. Advancing these tools and policies requires a deeper understanding of the pathogen and its interaction with the host. In addition, the social, political, and financial factors that contribute to the persistence and spread of the disease must be investigated. With adequate financial, scientific and political commitment, CBPP eradication is an achievable goal.
