Enhanced T cell activation and cytotoxicity against AML via targeted anti-CD99 nanoparticle treatment
Authors: Shephali Kadam, Atham Ali, Mateusz Pospiech, Sandra Onyemaechi, Yiting Meng, Kanaka Dhuri, J Andrew MacKay, Houda Alachkar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCD99 is a transmembrane protein overexpressed in Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML), presenting a potential novel therapeutic target. Our group has previously developed anti-CD99-A192 (α-CD99-A192), comprising of single chain variable fragment (scFv) and elastin-like polypeptides (ELPs), and reported promising anti-leukemic activity in AML preclinical models. Treatment with α-CD99-A192 induced apoptosis in AML cell lines and prolonged survival in AML xenograft models. Considering CD99’s expression and role in T cell activation, in the current study, we propose that α-CD99-A192 plays a dual function, i.e., targeting leukemic cells and activating T cells. This manuscript reports the effects of α-CD99-A192 on T cells in the context of AML. α-CD99-A192 treatment enhances T cell proliferation and activation and increases the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines along with increased aggregation of T cells, which culminates in heightened cytotoxicity against leukemic cells. Altogether, these findings suggest α-CD99-A192 enhances T cell activation and cytotoxic potential consistent with dual mechanisms of action for α-CD99-A192.Keywords: CD99, T cells, Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML), Antibodies, Nanoparticles, Immunotherapy

Introduction

CD99 is a highly O-glycosylated transmembrane protein encoded by MIC2 gene which is widely expressed in almost all human cell types, including lymphocytes [1]. CD99 plays a role in apoptosis and cell death [2,3], cell adhesion [4,5], lymphocyte development [1], leukocyte diapedesis [6], cell differentiation, and protein trafficking [2,7–9]. CD99 is expressed via two isoforms, namely the long 32 kDa and short 28 kDa isoform [5]. T cells and single-positive thymocytes express the long isoform, while double-positive thymocytes express both isoforms [10]. In T cells, CD99 contributes to cell proliferation, expression of activation markers [11], and differentiation [1,6]. Previous studies have reported an increase of CD99 expression in memory and activated T cells; however, the exact signal transduction pathway and CD99’s role in T cell regulation remains poorly understood [12]. Various CD99 targeting ligands have been developed in the form of recombinant proteins and monoclonal antibodies, which upregulate IL6, TNFα [11], IFNγ [13] and Th1 [14,15].

Pathologically, elevated CD99 expression has been linked to several malignancies, including Ewing sarcoma [16], myeloid malignancies [17–19], lymphoblastic lymphoma/leukemia [20] and malignant glioma [21,22], where CD99 functions as an oncogene [6]. In cases like Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) and Myelodysplastic Syndromes (MDS), high CD99 expression has been associated with leukemia-initiating cells, when compared to healthy hematopoietic cells [17], thus making it a potential biomarker. In tumors, CD99 is involved in cellular functions like metastasis, invasion, and migration [1]. We and others have found that CD99 presents a promising therapeutic target in myeloid malignancies [17,19]. This is further validated with studies reporting cytotoxic activity against myeloid cells with various anti-CD99 antibodies [17,23,19].

Our team recently developed anti-CD99-A192 (α-CD99-A192) nanoparticles [23] that are composed of α-CD99 single chain variable fragment (scFv) [24], which interacts with the extracellular domain of CD99 with an epitope limited by the amino acids 50–74 [24,25], linked to A192, which is an elastin-like polypeptide (ELP) [26]. ELPs are repetitive pentameric peptides derived from human tropoelastin that stabilize the assembly of scFvs into multi-valent nanoparticles [23, 27–29,30]. α-CD99-A192 exhibits specific binding to cells expressing CD99. Moreover, these fusion proteins also exhibited increased apoptosis in both primary AML cells and AML cell lines in vitro, along with prolonged survival in AML xenograft models [23]. Considering that CD99 is also expressed on T cells and given its role in T cell activation, we proposed that α-CD99-A192 plays a dual function: targeting leukemic cells while activating T cells. Thus, having established its anti-leukemic activity in pre-clinical models, this manuscript now characterizes the effects of antibody-based CD99 nanoparticle on T cells and how that impacts immune-mediated antileukemic activity.

Materials and methods

Sample collection from healthy donors

Healthy donors were consented in a written format authorizing the use of the collected blood samples prior to collection. Each subject was presented with a California Bill of Rights and provided with a copy of their signed consent form. The use of human materials was approved by the Institution Review Boards of the University of Southern California in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Ref#: HS-16–00274; Date of approval: 2 March 2019).

Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cell (PBMC) isolation

The collected whole human peripheral blood was diluted with Dulbecco’s Phosphate-buffered Saline (DPBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#14190–144) and methodically layered over a Ficoll-Paque PLUS density gradient (Cytiva, Cat#17144003). Blood layered over Ficoll was centrifuged for 30 minutes at zero deceleration and acceleration of 1. With the help of density gradient centrifugation, the desired PBMCs were present at the interface of the separated layers. The PBMCs were collected, subsequently washed thrice with DPBS, and resuspended in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 (1X) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#11875–093) culture medium supplemented with 20 % fetal bovine serum (FBS).

Stimulation and expansion of T cells

The desired amount of PBMCs were stimulated with cytokines, recombinant human interleukin-2 (rhIL-2) (R&D systems, Cat# BT-002) and Phytohemagglutinin-L (PHA) Solution (500x) (Invitrogen, Ref# 00–4977), at a concentration of 1:1000 for PHA-L and 2.5 ng/ml for rhIL-2, for 72–96 hours in RPMI media with 20 % FBS. At the end of the incubation period, the expanded T cells, also labeled as stimulated cells, were washed with DPBS, and collected for further treatment.

T cell isolation using negative selection

The desired amount of PBMCs were resuspended in a cell separation buffer at a concentration of 1×107 cells/100μl. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, 20μl of MagniSort Human T cell Enrichment Antibody Cocktail (eBioscience, Cat#4293008) were added per 100μl of cells. The cells were mixed well with the antibody cocktail by vortexing and incubating them for 10 minutes at room temperature. After the incubation, the cells were washed with the cell separation buffer and resuspended with the cell separation buffer to the original volume. 20μl of MagniSort Negative Selection Beads A (eBioscience, Cat#4297384) was added per 100μl of cells and mixed well. The cells were incubated with the beads at room temperature for 5 minutes. The tube was then inserted into the magnet and incubated at room temperature for 5 minutes. After the incubation, the supernatant was poured into a new tube and T cells were collected after spin down.

Treatment with A192 and α-CD99-A192 ELPs

The PBMCs, PHA and IL2 expanded T cells and T cells isolated by negative selection were washed and resuspended in 200μl DPBS. They were further divided into control and treated groups. The control A192 scFv ELP was added to the control group and α-CD99-A192 scFv ELP was added to the treated group at a concentration of 10μM of the final volume. The treated cells were then incubated on ice for 30 minutes and later washed with DPBS. The cells were resuspended in RPMI supplemented with 20 % FBS and cultured in 12-well suspension plates for further analyses.

Binding assay

The PHA and IL2 expanded T cells were treated with NHS-Rhodamine labeled A192 and NHS-Rhodamine labeled α-CD99-A192 scFv ELPs and incubated on ice for 30 minutes. After the incubation period, the ELP-bound T cells were washed and resuspended in 300μl PBS in flow tubes. The fluorescence intensity of phycoerythrin (PE) was used to identify the ELP binding. Flow cytometry was performed on the BD Fortessa X20 flow cytometer and data analysis was performed using FlowJo v10 10.7.1.

Cell counting and proliferation assays

The A192 and α-CD99-A192 treated PBMCs, PHA and IL2 expanded T cells and T cells isolated by negative selection, were seeded into a 12-well plate according to their treatment conditions, namely, untreated, A192 and α-CD99-A192. Cells were counted on day 1, day 3, day 5, day 7, day 9, and day 10 using Trypan blue solution and an Improved Neubauer Hemocytometer with a chamber depth of 100 μm. Cell concentration was obtained by taking the average of the quadrants, multiplying by the dilution factor, and further multiplying by 10,000 to obtain the cell concentration per ml of the solution. The experiment was performed in triplicates and terminated on day 10 after the treatment.

To evaluate cell proliferation, the PBMCs and the expanded T cells were initially stained using the Cell Trace Carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) Cell Proliferation Kit (Invitrogen, Ref#C34554). CFSE dye was reconstituted using 18μl dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) to make the 5 mM stock concentration, and the required amount was diluted using DPBS to obtain the final diluted concentration of 2μM CFSE. The cells were resuspended using the diluted stain and incubated away from light for 20 minutes. After incubation, 4x volume of DPBS was added and the cells were incubated for 5 minutes. The cells were then centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 3 minutes, washed once with DPBS and resuspended in cell culture medium. The stained cells were washed and treated with control A192 ScFv ELP and α-CD99-A192 ScFv ELP at a concentration of 10μM of the final volume. The cells were then seeded in a 12 well plate according to the treatment groups. An aliquot of the cells was taken every alternate day with day 0 being the day of treatment. The aliquot was washed and resuspended in 300μl DPBS. The cells were then analyzed on the LSRII BD Fortessa X20 flow cytometer by measuring the fluorescence intensity of Alexa Fluor 488 and data analysis was performed using FlowJo v10 10.7.1. The experiment ended on day 9.

T cell phenotyping

The A192 and α-CD99-A192 treated PBMCs, and PHA and IL2 expanded T cells were collected in flow tubes on day 5 after the treatment. The cells were then washed and resuspended with the prepared stain master mix. The master mix was prepared using anti-human CD3 FITC (Clone: UCHT1; Biolegend, Cat#300406), Anti-Human CD4 PE (Clone: OKT4; eBioscience Cat#4337556), Anti-Hu CD8a PE-Cyanine 7 (Clone: SK1; eBioscience Cat#2066348) and Anti-Hu CD25 APC (Clone: BC96; eBioscience Cat#1946588) antibodies. The cells were incubated with the master mix on ice away from light for 25–30 minutes. After incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 3 minutes, washed twice with 500μl DPBS and resuspended in 300μl DPBS for analysis on the flow cytometer. Flow cytometry was performed on the LSRII BD Fortessa X20 flow cytometer and data analysis was performed using FlowJo v10 10.7.1.

For expression analysis of the activation markers, CD38 and CD69, the control and treated T cells were collected in flow tubes on day 5 after the treatment. The cells were washed twice with DPBS and resuspended in 100μl of DPBS. Anti-Human CD3 PerCP-Cyanine 5.5 (Clone: OKT3; eBioscience, Cat#45–0037–42), Anti-Human CD38 FITC (Clone: HB7; eBioscience, Cat#11–0388) and Anti-Human CD69 PE (Clone: FN50; eBioscience, Cat#12–0699) was added to the cells. The cells were incubated for 25–30 minutes on ice and away from light. After incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 3 minutes, washed twice with 500μl DPBS and resuspended in 300 μl DPBS for analysis on the flow cytometer. Flow cytometry was performed on the LSRII BD Fortessa X20 flow cytometer and data analysis was performed using FlowJo v10 10.7.1.

RNA sample preparation and RNA-seq data analysis

The PBMCs obtained from healthy donor were expanded with PHA and IL2 for 72 hours. The expanded T cells were then treated with A192 and α-CD99-A192 ELPs at a concentration of 10μМ. The A192 and α-CD99-A192 treated T cells were cultured in RPMI media with 20 % FBS and collected on day 5 after treatment. The cells were washed once with DPBS and then resuspended in 300μl of RNA lysis buffer (RLT) buffer (containing 0.01 % of β-mercaptoethanol). Total RNA was isolated from cell pellet using the RNeasy mini kit with on-column DNAse digestion (Qiagen, Cat#74104) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and integrity were assessed by Thermo Scientific NanoDrop OneC and Agilent high sensitivity RNA ScreenTape (Agielnt Technologies, Cat #5067–5579). RNA samples were stored at −80°C until they were shipped to Azenta for library preparation and sequencing.

The RNA sequencing data obtained from Azenta was analyzed using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis-Molecular Signatures Database (GSEA-MSigDB) computational tool version 4.3.2 (Broad Institute, UC San Diego). After successfully uploading the expression dataset and phenotype label files, the parameters set for analyzing the dataset were h.all. v2023.3.Hs.symbols.gmt (Hallmark gene set) for gene sets database and Human_Ensembl_Gene_ID_MSigDB.v2023.2.Hs.chip for Chip platform. Using the uploaded files and set parameters, the analysis was run to obtain the gene enrichment plots.

Cytokine release profile analysis

The PBMCs obtained from healthy donor were expanded with PHA and IL2 for 72 hours. The expanded T cells were then treated with A192 and α-CD99-A192 ELPs at a concentration of 10μМ. The A192 and α-CD99-A192 ELP treated T cells were cultured in RPMI media with 20 % FBS and collected on day 5 after treatment. The supernatants of the A192 and α-CD99-A192 treated T cells were collected on day 5 and analyzed for their cytokine and chemokine release profile using the Proteome Profiler Array kit by R&D systems (Cat# ARY005B), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The samples were prepared by incubating the supernatants with the Human Cytokine Array Detection Antibody Cocktail at room temperature for one hour and later the solution was added to the membrane provided (which was incubated with array buffer earlier). The membranes were then incubated overnight at 2–8°c on a rocking platform. The membranes were then collected the next day, washed, and incubated with diluted Streptavidin-HRP. After incubation, the membranes were washed and visualized using Chemi Reagent Mix for 10–15 minutes on Image ChemiDoc. The visualized membranes were then analyzed using BioRad software. The plots were created using the mean pixel intensity after subtracting the mean background intensity. The adjusted mean pixel intensity was then normalized to the adjusted mean pixel intensity of the reference spots present on the membranes.

Co-culture apoptosis assay

The Carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) from the Cell Trace CFSE Cell Proliferation Kit (Invitrogen, Cat#2420667) was resuspended with 18μl DMSO to obtain a 5 mM stock concentration. The required amount of CFSE solution was diluted in DPBS to obtain the final diluted concentration of 2μM CFSE. The leukemic MV4–11 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (1X) culture medium supplemented with 20 % FBS. The required number of MV4–11 cells were collected and centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 3 minutes and washed twice with DPBS. The cells were resuspended in the diluted CFSE solution at a concentration of 2μM and incubated away from light for 20 minutes. After incubation, 4x volume of DPBS was added and the cells were incubated for 5 minutes. After incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 minutes. The supernatant was removed, and RPMI 1640 (1X) culture medium supplemented with 20 % FBS was added.

The PBMCs obtained from healthy donor were expanded with PHA and IL2 for 72 hours. The expanded T cells were then treated with A192 and α-CD99-A192 ELPs at a concentration of 10μМ. For the treatment of T cells, the control A192 ScFv ELP was added to the control group and α-CD99-A192 ScFv ELP was added to the treated group at a concentration of 10μM of the final volume. The cells were then incubated on ice for 30 minutes and later washed with DPBS to remove the ELPs. The A192 and α-CD99-A192 treated T cells were incubated with the CFSE-labeled leukemic MV4–11 cells at an Effector to Target (E:T) ratio of 5:1 in a 12-well suspension plate.

The cells were collected at 18 and 48 hours, centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 3 minutes and washed twice with DPBS. The cells were resuspended in 100μl DPBS. Annexin V stain was prepared by adding Annexin V APC (Invitrogen, Cat#17–8007) to previously diluted Annexin V Binding Buffer (10x) (Invitrogen, Cat#00–0055). The diluted stain was added to the cells and the cells were incubated on ice for 25–30 minutes away from light. After incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 3 minutes, washed twice with DPBS and resuspended in 300μl DPBS. Flow cytometry was performed on the LSRII BD Fortessa X20 flow cytometer and data analysis was performed using FlowJo v10 10.7.1. For analyzing the data, the live cell population was identified first followed by identifying the CFSC+ MV4–11 cells using the FITC channel. The MV4–11 cells undergoing apoptosis were then identified using the annexin V stain under the APC channel.

Luciferase assay

The A192 and α-CD99-A192 treated T cells were incubated with leukemic MV4–11, THP-1 and MOLM-13 cells transfected with lenti-virus plasmids carrying GFP and luciferase genes, at an Effector: Target (E:T) ratio of 5:1 in a 96-well U-bottom suspension culture plate. The cells were collected within 24 hours, washed, and incubated with the lysis reagent in a 96-well F-bottom white microplate (Greiner bio-one, Cat#655075). Luciferase substrate (final concentration:1 mg/ml) Reagent (20μl) was added to each well and plate was measured using Biotek Synergy 2 Plate Reader (Model: Synergy 2 SL). The luciferase intensity was analyzed using Microsoft Excel.

Statistical analysis

Experiments were conducted in triplicates unless mentioned otherwise in the figure legend; results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The normality of the data was determined using Shapiro-Wilk test and parametric and non-parametric tests were used accordingly. Statistical comparisons utilized unpaired T test for binding assay, apoptosis assay and activation marker analysis, One-Way ANOVA with Tukey correction for proliferation assay, Two-Way ANOVA with Tukey correction for expression of T cell phenotypes and unpaired T-tests with Holm-Sidak correction for multiple comparisons for comparing cell aggregate counts and cytokine expression analysis. Statistical significance was calculated in GraphPad Prism Version 8.2.1 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). P value <0.05 was considered significant.

Results

α-CD99-A192 induces cell aggregation in T cells

The binding of α-CD99-A192 to T cells was analyzed by observing the median fluorescence shift of NHS-Rhodamine labeled α-CD99-A192 bound PE positive cells compared with the NHS-Rhodamine labeled A192 treated cells assessed by flow cytometry (Fig. 1 A). The IL2 and PHA expanded T cells show effective binding to the NHS-Rhodamine labeled α-CD99-A192 compared to the A192 control. Compared to the control A192, the median fluorescence of α-CD99-A192 bound T cells is significantly increased. (Fig. 1B, A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 1.00 vs. 1.16, p = 0.029).

T cells that were expanded in the presence of IL2 and PHA for 3 days were treated with α-CD99-A192. α-CD99-A192 increases cell proliferation in T cells

To assess the effect of α-CD99-A192 on T cell viability and proliferation, the viability assay was performed using trypan blue cell counts on treated PBMCs, expanded T cells that were stimulated with IL2 and PHA for three days and T cells obtained by negative selection. A decrease in unstimulated PBMC counts was observed throughout all treatment groups. The control groups showed a slow decrease across the days as observed normally with hematopoietic cells. In contrast, the α-CD99-A192 treated PBMCs exhibit a rapid decline in cell number over 10 days in vitro (Fig. 2A, Day 10 cell count normalized to Day 1: untreated vs. A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 71.46 % vs. 58.71 % vs. 14.57 %, untreated vs. α-CD99-A192, p=0.002 and A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, p=0.005). On the other hand, expanded T cells treated with α-CD99-A192 exhibit increased cell proliferation compared with the untreated control and A192 treated T cell groups. The trypan blue cell counts of the α-CD99-A192 treated T cells are significantly higher than the controls, untreated and A192 treated T cell groups, on day 5 after the treatment (Fig. 2B, Day 5: untreated vs. A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 97.2 % vs. 90.5 % vs. 150.5 %, p=0.039 and p=0.022). The T cells isolated by negative selection cultured without cytokines did not show any significant differences between the control and α-CD99-A192 treated groups (Fig. 2C). The cell proliferation was also tracked using the cell trace assay which exhibited a shift in the peak as the days progressed. Compared with the control naïve T cells and A192 treated T cells, α-CD99-A192 treated T cells exhibit a significant decrease in the intensity of the CFSE dye (Fig. 2D–G, T cells: Day 6: untreated vs. A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 58.86 % vs. 57.25 % vs. 35.23 %, untreated vs. α-CD99-A192, p=0.009 and A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, p=0.02). The cell proliferation across unstimulated PBMCs was not significant across all groups implying no significant increase or decrease in proliferation.

α-CD99-A192 alters gene expression in T cells

We analyzed the RNA-sequencing results for α-CD99-A192 treated T cells and control T cells for understanding gene enrichment and the involved pathways. We identified 274 differentially expressed genes with 164 (59 %) upregulated genes and 110 (40 %) downregulated genes in α-CD99-A192 treated T cells compared to control A192 treated T cells. Among the upregulated genes, we found many genes that encoded inflammatory and immune responses (Fig. 4C, IL1B: A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 6.882 log fold, IER3: A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 2.555 log fold, CXCL3: A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 5.455 log fold, CXCL5: A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 8.286 log fold). Among the markers that are associated with T cell exhaustion such as PD-1, CTLA-4, TIM-3, and LAG-3 [31], we found a significant downregulation of the exhaustion marker HAVCR2 (TIM3) (A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, −0.72 log fold change, q=0.03) and CD84 (A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, −0.61 log fold change, q=0.01). We found significant enrichment in α-CD99-A192 phenotype of pathways involved in inflammatory response, immune response, T cell activation and differentiation such as TNFα signaling via NFKB (NES: 2.94, p<0.001, q<0.001), inflammatory response (NES: 2.63, p<0.001, q<0.001), IL6 JAK STAT3 signaling (NES: 2.31, p<0.001, q<0.001), hypoxia (NES: 1.88, p<0.001, q=0.003), KRAS signaling (NES: 1.70, p<0.001, q=0.008), IL2 STAT5 signaling (NES: 1.63, p<0.001, q=0.012), and TGF beta signaling (NES: 1.44, p=0.042, q=0.044) (Fig. 4A–4D).

α-CD99-A192 treated T cells exhibit enhanced cytotoxicity against leukemic cells

We co-cultured the control A192 treated T cells and α-CD99-A192 treated T cells with FITC (CFSE) stained MV4–11 or MV4–11Luc+ cells (leukemic cells) at an E:T ratio of 5:1 and measured the apoptosis by flow cytometry and luciferase assay. The co-culture assays show increased cell apoptosis in the FITC labeled MV4–11 cells at 18 hours (Fig. 6 A and 6E, A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 33.6 % vs. 41.13 %, p=0.015) and a reduction in the viable leukemic cells at 18 hours (Fig. 6B and F, A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 10.89 % vs. 8.05 %, p=0.034) and 48 hours (Fig. 6D and F, A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 9.69 % vs. 6.54 %, p=0.065) in the α-CD99-A192 compared with the control A192 treated group. Though no significant increase in cell apoptosis in the α-CD99-A192 group is observed at 48 hours (Fig. 6C and E). These results indicate that α-CD99-A192 induces enhanced T cell cytotoxicity. We also analyzed the apoptosis in T cells at both timepoints and we did not observe any significant toxicity in T cells due to α-CD99-A192 treatment (Fig. 6G–I). The apoptosis results from flow cytometry were also validated by the luciferase assay. We found a significant decrease in the percentage of live leukemic MV4–11Luc+ and THP-1Luc+ cells (luciferase signal) in the α-CD99-A192 group compared with the control (Fig. 6J–K, MV4–11: A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 100 % vs. 52.84 %, ~47 % reduction, p=0.032; THP-1: A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 100 % vs. 58.77 %, ~41 % reduction, p=0.0008). We also observed a decrease, though not significant, in the percentage of live leukemic MOLM-13Luc+ cells (luciferase signal) in the α-CD99-A192 group compared with the control cells (Fig. 6L, A192 vs. α-CD99-A192, 100 % vs. 74.97 %, ~25 % reduction, p=0.193).

Discussion

CD99 plays several functions in T cells, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and inflammation [1]. Various CD99 targeting antibodies have been developed and their effects on T cells have been studied in different contexts. Previous studies have established T cell expansion [15], apoptosis [32], activation [15], upregulation of cytokines [11,14] when T cells were treated with various CD99 targeting antibodies.

Using the previously developed antibody-based CD99 nanoparticle, α-CD99-A192 [23], we examined the effects of CD99 ligation on T cells. CD99 engagement with α-CD99-A192 significantly increased the T cell proliferation and aggregation. Increased cell aggregation with α-CD99-A192 activation agrees with the previously reported function of CD99 being involved in cell aggregation. This phenomenon also known as homotypic adhesion has been reported before with anti-CD99 antibodies in Jurkat cells [33] and thymocytes [4]. Cell aggregation also indicates the initiation of T cell activation and acts as a marker of T cell activity [34]. Activation of CD99 by α-CD99-A192 showed an increase in the proliferation of T cells stimulated with cytokines, but a decrease in the number of PBMCs. The decrease in cell numbers following α-CD99-A192 treatment was only observed after the PBMCs cells had been in culture for more than 7 days in the absence of any cytokines, which could naturally affect their health and survivability. Nonetheless, comprehensive toxicity studies are essential to fully establish the safety profile of CD99 antibody-based therapeutics in AML. Though, CD99 induced cell apoptosis in thymocytes has been reported in double positive thymocytes before undergoing cell differentiation [3]. There were no differences observed in proliferation of the T cells obtained by negative selection which were cultured without any cytokines. This signifies that α-CD99-A192 like previously developed anti-CD99 antibodies [15,35], activates T cells in the presence of suboptimal activation by either cytokines or CD3 activating antibodies, pointing towards a TCR and CD3 dependent activation.

Our study shows that there were no significant differences observed in the percentages of the CD4+, CD8+ and CD25+ T cell phenotypes in both the expanded T cells and PBMCs except for an increase in CD3+ cells in α-CD99-A192 treated PBMCs compared with A192 control. This is consistent with α-CD99-A192’s opposite effects of targeting the myeloid cells while expanding the T cells. In PBMCs, we also observed a slight increase, though not significant, in the expression of CD25+ cells which could imply enrichment of activated T cells upon treatment with α-CD99-A192. This is consistent with the increased expression of CD38 in expanded T cells, which is another T cell activation marker that is highly expressed in response to infections [36].

We also analyzed the expression of CD69, which is an early marker of lymphocyte activation [37,38]. However, the CD69 expression was highly variable among samples, despite RNA-seq analysis showing an upregulation of the CD69 gene which points towards lymphocyte activation. To capture changes in the expression of CD69 by flow cytometry, evaluation of different treatment timepoints might be needed.

Treatment of T cells with α-CD99-A192 resulted in changes in several cytokines which can be classified as pro-inflammatory, inflammatory, and immune modulatory cytokines, all involved in inflammatory and immune responses [39–43]. It should be noted that, despite all efforts of removing the bacterial endotoxins from the ELPs during the purification process, even low levels that persist may cause activation of inflammatory cytokines in the treated cells. Excluding LPS-induced inflammatory responses, several pathways were associated with α-CD99-A192 treatment in T cells such as IL-2 STAT5 signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway and the KRAS signaling pathway. Among the T cells activation markers, α-CD99-A192 induced CD38 expression, which has also been linked to increase in production of inflammatory cytokines [44].

Depending on the epitopes, different studies have reported different effects on T cells caused by anti-CD99 antibodies. Various antibodies, including α-CD99 scFv, have been developed targeting CD99 epitopes limited by amino acids 50–74 [1]. One such antibody YG32 binds to epitope 68–74, has been reported to show homotypic adhesion but it did not induce cell death [45]. Similarly other antibodies like, 12E7 (binding to amino acids 61–64) showed similar effects as YG32 [46]. We believe the effects observed on the cells also depend on the CD99 isoforms expressed by the cells. While myeloid cells express both CD99 isoforms, T cells are reported to express only the long isoform [10]. Considering the epitope domain α-CD99 scFv interacts with, it possibly interacts with both isoforms, but the difference in effects observed would be dependent on the isoforms expressed by the cells. It has been reported that ligation of both isoforms causes apoptosis but ligation of only the long isoform induces homotypic adhesion and does not cause cell apoptosis [46].

With the in vitro co-culture study, within the span of 24 hours, we observed increased cell apoptosis within the leukemic cells with the early apoptosis marker Annexin and in terms of viable leukemic cells. These results were also validated by luciferase assay. We did not see the same magnitude of effects at a later timepoint of 48 hours which is consistent with T cell mediated killing [47] that occurs shortly after the co-culture. These results highlight the cytotoxic effects of the activated T cells and indicate the α-CD99-A192 treated T cells mediated cell apoptosis in the absence of direct treatment of the leukemic cells with α-CD99-A192, thus validating the proposed dual cytotoxic mechanism of α-CD99-A192 as targeting leukemic cells as we have reported previously [23] while activating T cells as shown in this study. While one of the possible reasons for enhanced cytotoxicity could be mechanisms contributing to the increased cell activation and proliferation, another possible mechanism that may explain the early antileukemia effect could be that coating the T cells with the ELPs on their surface may enable engaging the T cells with leukemic cells overexpressing CD99. Therefore, α-CD99-A192 scFv ELP acts as a linker that brings T cells into contact with the leukemic cells for an enhanced cytotoxicity. To further explore the cytotoxic effects of α-CD99-A192 treated T cells further, in vivo studies with animal xenograft and validation in AML patient samples will be needed.

In conclusion, α-CD99-A192 scFv-ELP nanoparticle activates T cells in terms of stimulation of T cell proliferation, elevation of activation markers, gene enrichment of pathways involved in inflammatory and immune responses and increase in the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. α-CD99-A192 also culminates in heightened cytotoxicity of T cells against leukemic cells.
