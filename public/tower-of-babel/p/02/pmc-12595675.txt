Effects of hospital pharmacist–community pharmacist collaboration on solving pharmacotherapy problems in stroke patients with post-discharge transitions: non-randomized comparative study
Authors: Yuko Fukuda, Risa Ito, Misaki Kakihana, Megumi Kunita, Tsutomu Takahashi, Tetsuji Kanemoto, Toshiyuki Sahara, Masahiko Tsujikawa, Mitsuko Onda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeamlessly providing appropriate drug therapy to patients recovering from stroke is essential for preventing recurrence, regardless of changes in their medical care environment. To address this, several initiatives have been undertaken to improve communication between hospital and community pharmacists, including the use of discharge medication summaries. However, we previously revealed discrepancies between the contents of prescriptions upon discharge and outpatient prescriptions from other institutions. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to examine the effect of bidirectional information sharing between hospital pharmacists and community pharmacists, to assess the impact on prescription continuity in stroke patients.MethodsThe study included patients who received hospital treatment for stroke (cerebral infarction and transient ischemic attack) and were transferred to another hospital for outpatient treatment after discharge. The control group only received summaries, whereas in the intervention group, besides receiving summaries, patient information was shared bidirectionally between hospital pharmacists and community pharmacists through an online tool (secure messaging platform) for approximately 12 weeks post-discharge. The primary endpoint was the number of medication discrepancies resolved (the number of individual medications for which discrepancies were identified and successfully addressed, totaled across all patients), and the secondary endpoint was the percentage of prescription interventions by community pharmacists. These groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test.ResultsIn the intervention group, medication discrepancies were reduced from 16 to 4 drugs, indicating the resolution of 12 discrepancies, whereas that in the control group remained the same at 13 drugs, showing no change (p = 0.0051). The percentage of prescription interventions by community pharmacists ((number of drugs with at least one intervention/number of drugs)) was 16/16 (100%) in the IG vs. 3/13 (23.1%) in the CG. This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.0003).ConclusionThis study demonstrated that bidirectional information sharing between hospital pharmacists and community pharmacists promotes prescription interventions and may contribute to improving medication continuity and safety in patients recovering from stroke compared with conventional care. These findings support the implementation of pharmacist collaboration systems to reduce medication discontinuation during care transitions in stroke patients.Trial registrationThis trial is registered on Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT) (identifier: jRCT1050250043), June 9, 2025 (retrospectively registered).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04474-0.Keywords: Stroke, Discharge, Prescription discrepancy, Hospital pharmacist, Community pharmacist, Collaboration, Interactive information sharing, Seamless care

Background

Stroke is the second leading cause of death globally and the fourth in Japan, imposing a significant socioeconomic burden [1–4]. Given the high recurrence rate and poorer prognosis of recurrent stroke, seamless continuation of appropriate long-term pharmacotherapy is crucial for secondary prevention [5–8]. In Japan, patient care transitions from acute hospitals to other healthcare settings are supported by various multidisciplinary collaborations, including pharmacists [9, 10]. Pharmacists are key healthcare providers in ensuring medication continuity and safety during these transitions. While hospital pharmacists have begun providing “discharge medication summaries” to community pharmacists to facilitate information transfer, previous studies on these summaries primarily focused on one-way information sharing from hospitals to the community [11–14]. However, the impact of structured, bidirectional pharmacist collaboration on the resolution of drug-related problems (DRPs) and medication continuity in post-stroke patients remains unknown in Japan [15]. This study aimed to evaluate whether structured bidirectional collaboration between hospital and community pharmacists improves pharmacotherapy problem identification, resolution, and continuity of care among stroke patients after hospital discharge.

Methods

Study design

This was a non-randomized comparative study. Patients were allocated to the intervention (IG) or control group (CG) based on the period of enrollment rather than random assignment. Due to the significant time constraints in recruiting a sufficient number of cases and the logistical challenges of implementing a new collaborative protocol, we adopted a period-based allocation method. This approach was essential for the systematic development and evaluation of our novel intervention, which required a phased implementation. We used our previously conducted baseline study [15] as the control group.

To ensure the comparability of the two groups, we carefully evaluated potential temporal confounding factors. The time gap between the control and intervention periods was relatively short (approximately three months). Furthermore, we confirmed that there were no significant changes in the following key factors that could have influenced the outcomes:

Treatment Guidelines: No major revisions were made to the national stroke treatment guidelines or the hospital’s internal protocols.

Treatment Guidelines: No major revisions were made to the national stroke treatment guidelines or the hospital’s internal protocols.

Medical and Pharmacy Team: The core team of stroke specialists and hospital pharmacists remained the same throughout both periods.

Patient Characteristics: A comparative analysis of patient demographics and clinical data (e.g., age, sex, stroke subtype, comorbidities, and initial stroke severity scores) revealed no statistically significant differences between the two groups (see Table 1).

We believe that this non-randomized design, while not ideal for all study types, was the most ethical and practical approach for evaluating our complex, real-world intervention, and that the steps taken to confirm group comparability mitigate potential biases.

Stroke specialists or pharmacists knew whether the patients were enrolled in IG or CG. During each period, all eligible patients were consecutively included unless they chose not to participate.

Setting

This study was conducted at the Japan Community Healthcare Organization (JCHO) Hoshigaoka Medical Center in Hirakata city, Japan, and community pharmacies (designed pharmacy under Japan’s National Health Insurance) in Hirakata City and nearby regions.

Hospital

JCHO Hoshigaoka Medical Center is equipped with a stroke care unit that provides specialized care to patients immediately after the onset of stroke and an acute-phase ward that provides subsequent treatment and has been certified as a ‘primary stroke center’ by the Japan Stroke Society. The center accepts approximately 350 emergency patients with stroke annually. Our practice primarily houses stroke and neurology specialists who provide hyperacute and acute treatment, as well as three pharmacists, including the principal investigator, stationed in the stroke care unit and acute-phase ward, who are in charge of ward pharmaceutical duties.

The pharmacists are fully integrated into the clinical care team. The Pharmacists participate in weekly multidisciplinary conferences as part of their routine duties, allowing them to gain a detailed understanding of each patient’s condition. They are well-versed in stroke pharmacotherapy based on clinical guidelines. Prior to the communication with community pharmacists conducted in this study, the principal investigator explained the contents to two other pharmacists in advance. All communications were conducted in the presence of the principal investigator.

When a patient is transferred to another facility after discharge, patient referral documents—containing details of discharge prescriptions—prepared by an attending physician are handed to the patient. The patient and their family are requested to present the documents to their primary care physicians when visiting the outpatient department. Meanwhile, if a prescription has been changed/discontinued relative to the one before hospitalization, pharmacists prepare discharge summaries intended for the patient’s family pharmacy when the patient is discharged. These summaries are faxed to the family pharmacies of the patients by the Community Relations Office the day after the discharge date (next business day).

Pharmacies

The pharmacies that participated in this study included those that patients visited regularly and were not limited by their characteristics or regions. The primary investigator explained the details of the research to the family pharmacies of the participants on the phone for approximately 5 min and faxed/emailed a document specifying the research content and how to download the online tool, thus obtaining consent to participate in the study. The hospital pharmacist—the primary investigator—explained to the community pharmacists how to use the online tool. It takes approximately 20 min to download the online tool and register for an account.

Intervention group

Study population

The study included patients who received hospital treatment for acute stroke at the acute-phase ward of JCHO Hoshigaoka Medical Center and were transferred to outpatient treatment at another facility.

Estimation of required sample size

The number of cases of cerebral infarction and transient ischemic attack (TIA) (n = 40) from the baseline study [15] for comparison was considered as the target sample size.

Assuming that the percentage of drugs for which discrepancies were resolved was 1% in the control group and 20% in the intervention group, and assuming an alpha error of 5%, a power of 80%, and a sample size ratio of 1, the sample size was estimated to be 40 for each group.

As no prior studies were available on bidirectional pharmacist collaboration in post-stroke patients in Japan, the assumption of a 20% resolution rate in the intervention group was based on a logical projection derived from our baseline data and clinical judgment. In our baseline study [15], which served as the control group, the prescription intervention rate by community pharmacists was 23%, but the rate of physician acceptance was 0%. We hypothesized that our structured, bidirectional collaboration model would improve both the intervention rate and the physician acceptance rate by fostering a direct and trusted communication channel. Based on the outcomes of previous studies where pharmacist recommendations were frequently accepted by physicians [17], we made a conservative but clinically informed assumption that both rates could reach approximately 50%. This led to an anticipated resolution rate of 25% (50% intervention rate × 50% physician acceptance rate). We then set the target improvement at a slightly more cautious 20% to account for the exploratory nature of this study.

This value served as a practical and clinically informed basis for our sample size calculation. To account for potential dropout cases after inclusion, the target sample size for the intervention group was set at 50.

The enrollment of study participants started on July 24, 2023, and continued until the target subjects reached 50. The enrollment ended on October 19, 2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria comprised patients diagnosed with cerebral infarction or TIA, those aged ≥ 18 years at the time of consent, and those who provided written informed consent to participate in this study.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion included patients without a family pharmacy, those not prescribed drugs at discharge, and those deemed inappropriate by the principal investigator or research collaborators*.

* Patients deemed inappropriate by the principal investigator or research collaborators were excluded—for example, in cases where a poor patient-provider relationship was evident, such as patients being uncooperative during treatment. Such determinations were made based on information obtained from medical records or through consultation with healthcare professionals at the time of screening.

Intervention

In addition to providing summaries, bidirectional communication between hospital pharmacists and community pharmacists was implemented using an online tool during approximately 12 weeks after discharge, which was determined according to the following procedure. Using asynchronous secure messaging function of the online tool, the community pharmacist provided feedback on patient information following the pharmacy visit (e.g., contents of outpatient prescription, whether or not prescription intervention was implemented, any changes in patient condition) to the hospital pharmacist, and pharmacists exchanged opinions and evaluated prescriptions. To save time, prescription details were sent as attachments to documents normally used by pharmacists in their daily work (such as patient medication information sheets).

Based on the number of days of discharge prescriptions, hospital pharmacists sent notifications to community pharmacists to request information sharing around the estimated outpatient visit date. During the subsequent observation period, the hospital pharmacist notified the community pharmacists of the request for information approximately 4 and 12 weeks later, if they did not contact them.

Control group

A baseline study was conducted, including 42 patients diagnosed with acute-phase stroke who received hospital treatment at JCHO Hoshigaoka Medical Center between June 2022 and March 2023 before transitioning to post-discharge outpatient treatment at another facility. Of the 42 patients, 31 had cerebral infarction, 9 had TIA, and 2 had cerebral hemorrhage. In accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the IG, 40 patients (31 with cerebral infarction and 9 with TIA) were included in the CG, excluding 2 cases of cerebral hemorrhage. Only the summaries were provided to the patients in CG [15].

Assessment of prescription continuity

Continuity was assessed by comparing the contents of the prescription at discharge with those during the outpatient visit, extracting and investigating any discrepancies. The change in the prescription contents at discharge and during the outpatient visit was categorized into five categories: drug discontinuation, addition of drugs, dosage (same drug), usage (same drug), and drug changes by the principal investigator (hospital pharmacist). The characteristics of the drugs involved were identified based on the medicinal efficacy classification (87 drugs and related commodities of the Japan Standard Commodity Classification) [18]. The discrepancies were determined to fall into one of the four categories shown in Table 1 and were evaluated by hospital pharmacists (principal investigators or collaborators) who were in charge of the patients and stroke specialists.

Outcomes

Primary endpoint

The primary endpoint was the number of medication discrepancies resolved (the number of individual medications for which discrepancies were identified and successfully addressed, totaled across all patients).

The definition of “medication discrepancies resolved” is the physicians to take appropriate action in response to reports of discrepancies by the pharmacists. To ensure objective adjudication, two independent clinical pharmacists, who were not involved in the intervention, reviewed all patient charts and pharmacy records to confirm the resolution of each discrepancy. Any disagreements were resolved by a third senior pharmacist. This rigorous process was put in place to eliminate potential bias in the outcome determination.

Secondary endpoint

The secondary endpoint was the percentage of prescription interventions by community pharmacists.

Prescription interventions by community pharmacists were counted per prescription. In calculating the percentage, the denominator was the total number of unique medication discrepancies detected in patients within each group who participated in the study. The numerator was the total number of interventions performed by pharmacists. In cases where multiple interventions were performed for the same drug during the observation period (e.g., an initial inquiry followed by a secondary follow-up for the same issue), each was counted separately. This approach provides a more comprehensive measure of pharmacist activity. As a result, the number of interventions (numerator) could exceed the number of unique discrepancies (denominator), resulting in a percentage above 100%. We have chosen to retain this metric as it reflects the true volume of pharmacist engagement in the care process.

Statistical analyses

The results of primary and secondary endpoints in the intervention and control groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test. This method was chosen due to its appropriateness for analyzing categorical data with small sample sizes and expected cell frequencies below 5, which was a concern given our study’s design and cohort size. The relative risk (RR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were also calculated to quantify the effect size. Statistical significance was considered at 5%. All statistical tests were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a modified version of R commander designed to add statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics [19]. The patient characteristics—including age, sex, comorbidities, disease classification (Cerebral infarction or Transient ischemic attack), cognitive domain scores of the Functional Independence Measure (FIM), and Environment for recuperation after discharge — were described using descriptive statistics(frequency, median, interquartile range) to compare the two groups.

The functions of online tool

The online tool (MedicalCareStation operated by Embrace Co., Ltd.) used in the IG is commercial software and complies with Japanese Ministry of Health guidelines and international data security standards (e.g., ISO 27001) (Additional file 1).

The principal investigator and the designated pharmacy have entered into an agreement regarding password management methods and measures to prevent information leaks on the online tool, along with consent for research collaboration.

Results

Patient characteristics

CONSORT flowchart showing study participants in IG and CG

In IG, from July 24, 2023, to October 19, 2024, the number of patients who received hospital treatment for acute stroke at our hospital and were transferred to outpatient treatment at another facility was 149 patients. We explained the study to the subjects who met the eligibility criteria during this period and obtained consent from 59 individuals. Of the 59 enrolled, we excluded 1 patient whose follow-up facility was changed to our hospital after discharge and another who was readmitted after discharge before the online meeting (the patient had originally been diagnosed with TIA during hospitalization but was diagnosed as an epileptic seizure on readmission).

We conducted online meetings with hospital pharmacists and community pharmacists regarding the 57 patients. The online meeting lasted approximately 15 min as scheduled. We excluded four patients who were administered the initial outpatient prescription before the online meetings and two who visited different pharmacies than those where the online meetings were attended because the intervention effect could not be evaluated. We also excluded one patient who was readmitted for another disease before the initial outpatient visits after discharge. Ultimately, we evaluated the prescription continuities of 50 patients.

Thirty-nine pharmacies participated in this study. All community pharmacists in the intervention group actually participated in all scheduled interactions. Based on the number of days of discharge prescriptions, hospital pharmacists sent notifications to community pharmacists to request information sharing around the estimated outpatient visit date. As a result, successful bidirectional communication was achieved with all 50 study participants. There were no missing feedback prescriptions from pharmacies or incomplete feedback from pharmacists. Apart from the five excluded patients, no other patients were lost to follow-up during the observation period.

Pharmacist activities conducted during the study period

During the online meeting, the hospital pharmacists communicated the individual patient concerns from the study to the community pharmacists. The community pharmacists conducted activities related to medication adherence and risk factor control, and shared information with the hospital pharmacists.

Medication adherence

The hospital pharmacist listed 18 patients with poor adherence. This included a patient with a history of voluntarily discontinuing medication before hospitalization and another whose self-management status required third-person verification as assessed by the hospital pharmacist. The community pharmacist reported to hospital pharmacists that they had implemented the following interventions for these patients: remaining drugs during pharmacy visits (seven patients), conducting adherence follow-ups via phone or ICT tools outside pharmacy visits (two patients), conducting home visits (one patient), and implementing one-dose packaging (five patients) (Table 6).

Activities related to medication adherence the community pharmacists conducted for post-stroke patients

Through continuous post-discharge adherence management, a patient was found to have poor medication adherence over time, taking dabigatran after breakfast and dinner but frequently forgetting the evening dose. Preventive measures were discussed among pharmacists, and consequently, a proposal was made to the primary care physician to change the prescription to rivaroxaban once daily after breakfast.

Management of risk factors

The hospital pharmacist raised blood pressure management issues for 19 patients. After discharge, the community pharmacist verbally communicated with the patients (12 patients), reviewed their blood pressure logbooks (three patients), and performed adherence follow-up (two patients) to assess their blood pressure management status. These cases include Cases 6and 7 in Table 5., which contributed to resolving discrepancies in antihypertensive medication use.

In one case, the pharmacist performed adherence follow-up, detected a change in blood pressure, and reported the finding to the primary care physician. In another case, the patient was recommended to visit the primary care physician due to elevated blood pressure (systolic blood pressure ≥ 160 mmHg).

Discussion

This is the first prospective study in Japan evaluating the impact of structured bidirectional online communication between hospital and community pharmacists on prescription continuity after stroke. We found that collaborative interventions significantly improved overall prescription continuity and facilitated timely pharmacist-led medication reviews and discrepancy resolution. We observed improved prescription continuity, which may be associated with strengthened pharmacist collaboration.

The findings of this study suggest that the intervention holds promise as a tool for improving medication safety. The observed reduction in both the total number of discrepancies and, more critically, high-risk discrepancies, while based on a small sample size, is encouraging. These results imply that the intervention may not only reduce the overall number of errors but also target those with the greatest potential for patient harm.

This study, along with our previous case series study [15], confirmed that discrepancies may occur between discharge prescriptions and prescriptions issued by primary care physician at other hospitals after discharge. Medication discrepancies were common in both groups, underscoring the need for pharmacist-led interventions. Based on these findings, ensuring medication continuity is crucial. Pharmacists should carefully assess prescriptions for patients recovering from stroke transitioning to outpatient treatment after discharge.

We believe that the following mechanisms influenced changes in community pharmacists’ behavior or improved prescription continuity in this study. An online meeting held prior to the patient’s first outpatient visit enabled more effective communication. Through ongoing information exchange, collaborative prescription assessment between hospital and community pharmacists became possible, earlier detection of DRPs, pharmacist-led medication review and intervention, and improved prescription continuity.

Intervention process

In the present study, we successfully intervened in all medication discrepancies within IG.

Process A: community pharmacist–led interventions

Mechanisms Process A was implemented intervention to physician or patients by community pharmacists based on the information shared with online meetings at the time of discharge. For example, in Case 9, the community pharmacist proactively contacted the patient and physician for a patient who did not come to the pharmacy despite his drug stock running out, thus achieving a proactive intervention that went beyond prescription-based interventions that avoided prescription interruption. The online meeting performed before the initial outpatient visit is speculated to have effectively provided detailed patient information in a timely manner.

Process B: collaborative interventions

The feedback from the community pharmacist after discharge enabled the provision of evidence-based information to physicians evaluating prescriptions based on individual stroke risk factors, ultimately contributing to the optimization of prescriptions. Statins, prescribed for patients such as Case 5, are important for managing risk factors as indicated in the practice guidelines [20, 21]. Concerns exist that drugs intended for continued use may be omitted (underprescription) in polypharmacy cases [22]. According to Hohmann et al. regarding adherence to prescriptions by primary care physicians for post-discharge patients with ischemic stroke, Adherence to statins was poorer than that to antithrombotic therapy [16]. Our collaborative intervention appears to be effective in preventing the omission of medications that should be continued, even in cases where certain drugs or conditions are prone to being overlooked.

Physician approval of pharmacist recommendations

In IG, 12 out of 16 proposals made by community pharmacists to physicians were approved (75%). Our study is specific to prescription continuity associated with transitions of care on post-stroke transitions rather than general DRPs. The pre- and post-discharge prescribing discrepancies we have assessed are a subset of DRPs.

Summary of prior study

In the study by Hedegaard et al., the definition of DRPs was not clearly specified. However, they reported that the most frequent type of DRP involved cases where medications related to recurrent stroke risk—such as statins, antithrombogenic agents, and antihypertensive drugs—were not prescribed despite clear indications, which closely aligns with our definition of discrepancy type 1. Our findings also showed that discrepancy type 1 accounted for the majority of cases, suggesting that the types of DRPs may have been similar. The acceptance rate of pharmacists’ recommendations by physicians was slightly lower in our study. This may be attributed to the larger sample size and the extended six-month duration of the previous study. On the other hand, the study by Harris et al. provided a clear definition of DRPs. In the Patient Care Network of Europe Classification for Drug-Related Problems, version 8.01 they used, the factors contributing to DRPs include drug selection, drug form, dose selection, treatment duration, dispensing, drug use process, patient-related problems, and others. The DRPs resolution rate of Harris et al. [23] seems high, but it includes issues such as dispensing and drug use process, which we do not target, and the rate of suggestions to doctors among the data counted as resolution rate is unknown. Therefore, it would not be possible to make a simple comparison with our physician prescription acceptance rate.

Barriers to resolving discrepancies

Patient factors were among the obstacles to resolving discrepancies (Cases 1 and 2). In Case 2, a benzodiazepine drug, which was not prescribed during hospitalization, was resumed at the request of the patient. Benzodiazepines are likely to cause adverse events in older individuals, requiring special precautions. It is important for patients receiving healthcare, including their families and the general public, to have an awareness of polypharmacy and an understanding of high-risk drugs for older individuals [24]. AHA/ASA and the Japan Stroke Society recommend that multiple professionals actively participate to support informed decision-making based on accurate information, providing individualized education to patients and their families [9, 25]. The Japanese Stroke Society Consultation Manual [26] specifically notes that pharmacists play a role in maintaining medication adherence through medication counseling. Support is needed to help patients accept decisions regarding medication therapy (discontinuation or change of drugs) based on accurate information.

Next, other factors include primary care physicians and specialists appear to have differing recognitions regarding the use of drugs to prevent recurrence of stroke as in cases 10. If active prescription interventions by community pharmacists become more common in the future, as seen in the results of this study, improved communication with primary care physicians may also be expected. And also, primary care physicians’ recognitions them might be cleared.

Activities the community pharmacists conducted for post-stroke patients

The hospital pharmacists shared the challenges of medication adherence and risk factor management for each patient with community pharmacists through online meetings. Based on the information provided, the community pharmacists then conducted one-dose packaging, checked remaining drugs, and assessed blood pressure management status. Furthermore, some pharmacies initiated follow-up services such as phoning and social media outside of patient visits, whereas others introduced home visit drug management guidance.

Detailed communication of patient information may be useful in identifying patients who need follow-up care from community pharmacists. These activities are similar to multifaceted stroke relapse prevention programs implemented by pharmacists in other countries, including patient counseling, medication adherence management, and drug optimization [27]. Apart from the study by Hohmann et al., no other pharmacy-based research has investigated long-term management after stroke onset [27]. They conducted a study on multifaceted interventions, including patient education and DRP identification by community pharmacists. The community pharmacists had received education and training on pharmaceutical care for patients recovering from stroke. As a result, the health-related quality of life of post-discharge patients recovering from stroke remained more stable than that for patients receiving conventional care. As local community pharmacies are familiar and accessible to patients, the implementation of long-term pharmacy-based follow-up may lead to better outcomes.

Future directions

Potential scalable solutions of Time limitations include introducing standardized workflows to reduce the time burden and providing funding support to cover additional staff hours.

While the spread of digital infrastructure has facilitated bidirectional information sharing and brought many benefits, low-tech alternatives such as fax and telephone should also be considered to ensure prescription continuity. Scalable solutions could involve regional funding initiatives for IT upgrades and piloting standardized communication protocols to make such collaboration more common.

On the motivation of pharmacists, excluding community pharmacies from pharmacist collaboration may compromise the continuity of drug therapy during the transition period for stroke patients, which could otherwise be improved through their active involvement. Hospitals and community pharmacies need to recognize the benefits of this model—good patient outcomes resulting from continuity of drug therapy—and consider the future direction of collaboration. Possible solutions include training programs, establishing regional pharmacist networks, and sharing successful case examples to sustain engagement.

This study shared information based on pharmacist duties using commercial communication tools, without limiting the characteristics of pharmacies or the area. We believe that if hospitals and pharmacies are motivated to share patient information bidirectionally, the processes in this study can be easily replicated in other care models. By grouping the challenges and proposing scalable solutions, this section aims to provide a more forward-looking perspective on the implementability of this collaborative pharmacist model in stroke care.

We believe that for this model to be successful, hospital pharmacists need to communicate patient information necessary for community pharmacies and evaluate the appropriateness of post-discharge prescriptions considering the patient’s condition. To do so, hospital pharmacists are encouraged not only to be well-versed in general stroke pharmacotherapy and participate in the stroke team and understand the patient’s treatment plan. In the first place, these are skills generally required of ward pharmacists. On the other hand, while specialized stroke-related skills are not mandatory for community pharmacists, effective communication with primary care physicians could enhance the likelihood of this model’s success.

Establishing regional pharmacist networks or piloting standardized communication protocols could facilitate the broader adoption of pharmacist collaboration models in stroke care.

Limitations

The intervention group was prospectively enrolled, whereas the control group was historical. This design may introduce potential bias, such as temporal changes in clinical practice or pharmacist behavior. Because the control group was derived from historical data, unmeasured temporal changes in stroke management, pharmacist roles, or prescribing practices may have influenced outcomes. In addition, the hospital pharmacists assessing discrepancies were not blinded to group assignment. Since the principal investigator was involved in both intervention delivery and assessment, the possibility of observer bias cannot be excluded. Therefore, the intervention effect might have been overestimated, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. Blinded assessment of outcomes should be incorporated in future studies to minimize observer bias and strengthen internal validity. This study was conducted at a single medical institution, and verification at multiple facilities is necessary in the future. Because patients without an established family pharmacy or those receiving in-house prescriptions were excluded, the findings may not generalize to broader community settings where continuity challenges are likely greater. The prescription continuity of such patients remains unknown, highlighting the need for improved scrutiny in the future. This study could not evaluate the potential clinical implications (e.g., on stroke recurrence, hospitalization, costs). Future studies with clinical endpoints and economic evaluations are needed. Despite these limitations, this study is significant as the first intervention study conducted in Japan aimed at ensuring prescription continuity in patients recovering from stroke transitioning to outpatient treatment from an acute-phase hospital.

Conclusion

This is the first prospective study in Japan demonstrating that structured, bidirectional pharmacist collaboration can enhance prescription continuity for post-stroke patients transitioning to outpatient care. Future multicenter studies incorporating clinical endpoints, economic analyses, and larger patient populations are needed to validate these findings and assess the potential for integrating pharmacist-led collaborative models into standard stroke care.
