Phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase subunit beta downregulation by spi1 proto-oncogene modulates lung adenocarcinoma progression and immune microenvironment via mammalian target of rapamycin pathway
Authors: Yiting Wang, Yifan Zhou, Shangwei Chen, Jianwei Huang, Chen Zhang, Shuping Huang, Yujia Pan, Xiaoyan Huang, Junqi Qin, Shenghua Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase subunit beta (FARSB) is implicated in the progression of multiple cancers and represents a potential therapeutic target. However, its role in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) progression and the immune microenvironment remains poorly understood, warranting further investigation into its regulatory mechanisms.Methods:We conducted bioinformatics analyses to investigate the expression levels of FARSB in LUAD, identify enriched pathways, and assess its correlation with patient prognosis and CD8+ T cell infiltration. Bioinformatics analysis was also used to explore the transcriptional repression of FARSB by spi1 proto-oncogene (SPI1) and to validate the targeting relationship between SPI1 and FARSB. Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was utilized to measure the mRNA expression of FARSB and SPI1, while western blot was used to detect the expression of FARSB, SPI1, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and related signaling pathway proteins. Functional assays were performed, including CCK-8 assay for cell viability, EdU incorporation for cell proliferation, and flow cytometry for apoptosis analysis. Carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) staining was used to analyze CD8+ T cell proliferation, and flow cytometry was used to assess the expression of cytokines interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), Granzyme B (GZMB), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α).Results:FARSB expression was significantly upregulated in LUAD tissues and cells, and it inhibited CD8+ T cell infiltration. Mechanistically, FARSB activated the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway, enhancing LUAD cell viability, proliferation, and anti-apoptotic capabilities, consequently promoting CD8+ T cell exhaustion. The transcription factor SPI1 repressed FARSB expression, thus inhibiting LUAD progression and promoting CD8+ T cell anti-tumor immunity.Conclusion:SPI1 downregulated FARSB expression through transcriptional repression, thereby blocking the mTOR signaling pathway and suppressing LUAD progression and promoting CD8+ T cell anti-tumor immunity.Keywords: FARSB, Immune microenvironment, Lung adenocarcinoma, mTOR signaling pathway, SPI1

1. INTRODUCTION

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) represent the two most common subtypes of non-small cell lung cancer.1 Current LUAD treatment strategies include surgical resection, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies aimed at overcoming tumor immune suppression.2 Emerging evidence underscores the critical role of the tumor immune microenvironment in shaping LUAD patient prognosis and therapeutic response.3 During LUAD progression, cancer cells use diverse mechanisms to suppress immune cell function, promote immune escape, and modulate immune responses, thereby evading immune surveillance. Concurrently, immune cells, under the influence of cancer cells, often become functionally impaired, exhausted, or necrotic, ultimately failing to mount an effective anti-tumor response.4,5 Given these complex interactions, elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of the LUAD immune microenvironment is essential for advancing next-generation immunotherapies.

Phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase subunit beta (FARSB), a key aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (ARS), plays crucial roles in diverse physiological and pathological processes, including transcription, translation, inflammation, and tumorigenesis.6,7 ARSs are increasingly recognized for their involvement in tumor immunity, not only contributing to immune-related diseases (eg, autoimmune disorders, infections, and cancer immunology8 but also being secreted by tumor-associated immune cells to modulate cancer progression.9 Although FARSB has been implicated in gastric, colorectal, and hepatocellular carcinomas, where it drives tumor proliferation, DNA damage repair, and immune evasion,10–12 its functional mechanisms in LUAD remain poorly characterized. Importantly, the influence of FARSB on the LUAD immune microenvironment is entirely unexplored, warranting systematic investigation.

Mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), a highly conserved serine/threonine kinase, is a central regulator of cell proliferation, survival, metabolism, and immunity.13 It exerts these functions primarily by phosphorylating downstream effectors, including 70 kDa ribosomal S6 protein kinase (70S6K) and 4E binding protein 1 (4EBP1).14 Given its pivotal role in cellular metabolism, mTOR is increasingly recognized as a critical regulator of cancer immunity.15,16 Beyond its direct effects on immune cells, the mTOR pathway can also regulate programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression in tumor cells.17 As the primary ligand for programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), PD-L1 enables tumor cells to evade CD8+ T cytotoxicity, thereby contributing to immunosuppression.18 While FARSB has been reported to drive hepatocellular carcinoma progression via mTOR pathway activation,19 its potential role in modulating LUAD tumor immunity through this mechanism remains unexplored.

In this study, we elucidated the prognostic significance of FARSB in LUAD and its critical role in shaping the immune tumor microenvironment (TME). Integrating bioinformatics analyses with experimental validation, we demonstrated that the spi1 proto-oncogene (SPI1) downregulated FARSB expression, thereby regulating LUAD progression and the tumor immune microenvironment through mTOR signaling pathway activation. This research aimed to provide novel targets and avenues for immunotherapy of LUAD, contributing to the improvement of TME and the inhibition of tumor progression.

2. METHODS

2.1. Cell culture

Human normal lung epithelial cells BEAS-2B (BNCC359274) were cultured in DMEM-H complete medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The LUAD cell line A549 (BNCC337696) was maintained in F-12K complete medium with 10% FBS, while Calu-3 (BNCC359757) was cultured in EMEM complete medium with 10% FBS. The LUAD cell line PC-9 (BNCC340767) was also grown in DMEM-H complete medium with 10% FBS. All media included 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) and were provided by Pricella (Wuhan, China). All cell lines were obtained from BNCC (Beijing, China) and cultured in a sterile cell culture incubator (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) at 37°C with 5% CO2.

For the co-culture system, CD8+ T cells were isolated from human peripheral blood mononuclear cells using Dynabeads™ Untouched™ Human CD8+ T Cell Kit (Thermo Fisher). CD8+ T cells were stimulated in vitro for three days using CD3/CD28 antibody-conjugated beads (ACRO, Beijing, China). Following adhesion to tumor cells, activated CD8+ T cells were co-cultured at a 1:100 ratio with tumor cells for 24 h before stopping the co-culture. The suspended CD8+ T cells in the culture medium were collected by centrifugation and subjected to subsequent functional assays.

2.2. Cell transfection

si-FARSB, oe-FARSB, oe-SPI1, and their corresponding controls were synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). For siRNA constructs, the designed siRNA sequences were chemically synthesized and purified. For overexpression plasmid constructs, the target gene was amplified by PCR and cloned into an expression vector. Following restriction enzyme digestion and ligation, the constructed plasmids were verified by sequencing before use in subsequent experiments. Transient transfection of PC-9 cells was performed using Lipofectamine® 2000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher). Transfection efficiency was evaluated 48 hours post-transfection.

2.3. Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted from PC-9 cell lines using an RNA extraction kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a cDNA reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan). PCR amplification was performed with an ABI7500 system (Thermo Fisher). Each sample group was analyzed in triplicate, and data were processed by the 2−ΔΔCt method, normalizing mRNA levels of target genes to GAPDH. The following primers were used: FARSB: F: GCCCTGTGCTCCCAAGAAGATA, R: AGGAAGAAGGGTAGTGCGTGCC; SPI1: F: GCGACCATTACTGGGACTTCC, R: GGGTATCGAGGACGTGCAT; GAPDH: F: ACAA ACTTTGGTATCGTGGAAGG, R: GCCATCACGCCACAGTTTC.

2.4. Western blot

Proteins were extracted from cultured PC-9 cells with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and protein concentration was determined with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to a PVDF membrane. After blocking with 5% nonfat dry milk, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. The next day, the membrane was incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour. Protein visualization was performed using a chemiluminescent reagent (Millipore, Billerica, MA), and band intensity was analyzed using ImageJ. The antibodies used were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK) and included: rabbit anti-GAPDH (ab181602), rabbit anti-PD-L1 (ab213524), rabbit anti-FARSB (ab126950), rabbit anti-SPI1 (ab302623), rabbit anti-mTOR (ab134903), rabbit anti-p-mTOR (ab109268), rabbit anti-p70S6K (ab32359), rabbit anti-p-p70S6K (ab59208), rabbit anti-4EBP1 (ab32024), rabbit anti-p-4EBP1 (ab259329), and goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab6721).

2.5. CCK-8 assay

PC-9 cells treated under different conditions were seeded (2000 cells/well) in a 96-well plate. After cell incubation for 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 hours, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, China) was added to each well. Plates were incubated for an additional 4 hours at 37°C, and absorbance at 450 nm was assessed using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher).

2.6. EdU assay

PC-9 cells from different groups were cultured in six-well plates. The BeyoClick™ EdU-555 Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) was used per manufacturer’s instructions. Images were captured with a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

2.7. Flow cytometry

For apoptosis analysis, PC-9 cells were harvested, rinsed, and incubated with Annexin-V-PE and 7-AAD (Beyotime, China) for 15 minutes, analysis was performed within 1 hour using a flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), and data was processed using FlowJo software.

For measurement of CD8+ T cell-related cytokines, the suspended CD8+ T cell pellet was washed and stained with fluorescent antibodies. After incubation, cells were rinsed with PBS and resuspended in 500 μL of PBS buffer for analysis on a flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies). Antibodies included: PE Anti-Human interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) Antibody (E-AB-F1196, Elabscience, Wuhan, China), Granzyme B (GZMB) Monoclonal Antibody (GB11, APC, Thermo Fisher), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) Monoclonal Antibody (MAb11, APC, 17-7349-82, Thermo Fisher).

2.8. Carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester staining

Carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) dye (E-CK-A345, Elabscience, China) was diluted to the appropriate concentration and added to the CD8+ T cell suspension. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes, followed by adding five times the volume of cold medium to terminate the staining. Cells were incubated on ice for an additional 5 minutes, centrifuged, and washed three times with fresh medium. The cells were resuspended in fresh medium, and CFSE fluorescence intensity was assessed with a flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies).

2.9. Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The promoter region of the FARSB gene was cloned into the pGL3 luciferase reporter vector (Beyotime, China). Cells were co-transfected with either pGL3-FARSB-WT or pGL3-FARSB-MUT alongside the control Renilla luciferase plasmid pRL-TK (Beyotime, China) and either oe-SPI1 or oe-NC. Following 48-hour incubation, luciferase activity was tested with a dual-luciferase reporter assay system.

2.10. Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis of the FARSB gene promoter region was done with the Magna ChIP Kit (Millipore). The experimental procedure involved crosslinking cells with formaldehyde, followed by sonication to generate fragments of 200 to 500 base pairs. Immunoprecipitation was conducted with protein A/G magnetic beads coated with rabbit anti-SPI1 antibody (ab302623, Abcam, UK), using IgG as a negative control. DNA-protein complexes were purified using a DNA extraction kit (GeneMark, Shenzhen, China) to release the crosslinked DNA. Finally, qPCR was performed to analyze the DNA fragments recovered from the beads using the following primers: FARSB: F: TCTGGCCTTCTAGGTGGGAA, R: CCTGACAAAAAGCTGTGCCA.

2.11. Bioinformatics analysis

Different expressions of FARSB and SPI1 in LUAD and normal tissues were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Samples were categorized into high and low FARSB expression groups based on the median expression value, and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed to plot survival curves. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using GSEA 3.0 software. The TIMRE2 website was used to analyze the correlation between FARSB expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration in LUAD. Finally, transcription factors associated with the downregulation of FARSB expression were screened, and JASPAR was utilized to predict potential motif sites within the FARSB promoter for SPI1, along with Pearson correlation analysis of gene expressions.

2.12. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS v21.0 and visualized with GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA). All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± SD. Differences between two groups or among three or more groups were assessed using Student’s t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or the Kruskal-Wallis test. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and ns indicated no significant differences.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Elevated expression of FARSB in LUAD and its inhibition of CD8+ T cell infiltration

We first analyzed different expressions of FARSB in LUAD using data from TCGA, revealing that FARSB expression levels were significantly elevated in LUAD tissues (Fig. 1A). Survival analysis indicated that patients in the low FARSB expression group had a significantly higher overall survival rate compared to those with high FARSB expression, suggesting a correlation between high FARSB levels and poor prognosis in LUAD patients (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) results showed that FARSB expression was markedly higher in LUAD cell lines, particularly in PC-9 cells (Fig. 1C). Additionally, analysis using the TIMRE2 database revealed a significant negative correlation between FARSB expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration in LUAD (Fig. 1D), indicating that FARSB suppressed CD8+ T cell anti-tumor immunity. Collectively, FARSB was overexpressed in LUAD, correlated with poor prognosis, and inhibited CD8+ T cell infiltration.

3.2. FARSB promotes tumorigenesis and immune suppression in LUAD

To validate oncogenic roles of FARSB in LUAD, we transfected PC-9 cells, which had relatively high FARSB expression, with si-NC and si-FARSB to establish FARSB knockdown LUAD cell lines. Transfection efficiency was confirmed via qRT-PCR and western blot (WB) analysis (Fig. 2A, B). CCK-8 assays revealed that cell viability significantly decreased in the si-FARSB group compared to controls (Fig. 2C). EdU staining indicated a marked reduction in cell proliferation in the si-FARSB group compared to the control group (Fig. 2D). Flow cytometry results showed that FARSB inhibition led to increased apoptosis compared to the control group (Fig. 2E, F). WB analysis of PD-L1 protein expression revealed that FARSB knockdown reduced PD-L1 levels in PC-9 cells (Fig. 2G). These findings suggested a positive correlation between FARSB expression and cancer cell progression, confirming its oncogenic role in LUAD. Next, we co-cultured cancer cells from the si-NC and si-FARSB groups with CD8+ T cells to investigate the impact of FARSB on tumor immunity in LUAD. Notably, FARSB knockdown promoted CD8+ T cell proliferation (Fig. 2H), and the levels of cytokines IFN-γ, Granzyme B, and TNF-α were significantly elevated in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2I-K). Furthermore, existing literature indicates that PD-L1 suppresses T cell proliferation and reduces effector cytokine secretion through PD-1.20 This underscores the critical role of PD-L1/PD-1 interaction in tumor immunity. To investigate the impact of anti-PD-L1 antibody on T cells in this study, we co-cultured PC-9 cells with CD8+ T cells and treated the co-culture system with either IgG or anti-PD-L1. Results demonstrated that anti-PD-L1 treatment enhanced CD8+ T cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 1A, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A348), while also upregulating the expression of cytokines IFN-γ, GZMB, and TNF-α to varying degrees (Supplementary Fig. 1B-D, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A348). In summary, FARSB enhanced the viability and proliferative capacity of LUAD cells while upregulating surface PD-L1 expression and inhibiting apoptosis. Consequently, it suppressed the anti-tumor activity of CD8+ T cells in LUAD and promoted an immunosuppressive TME.

3.3. FARSB regulates the mTOR signaling pathway, affecting LUAD progression and anti-tumor immunity

To explore the mechanisms by which FARSB influences LUAD progression and anti-tumor immunity, we conducted GSEA and found that differential expression of FARSB in LUAD was significantly enriched in the mTOR pathway (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, we constructed FARSB-overexpressing PC-9 cells and conducted rescue experiments using the mTOR signaling pathway inhibitor Rapamycin (Rapa, MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ). WB analysis revealed that overexpression of FARSB resulted in increased phosphorylation levels of mTOR, p70S6K, and 4EBP1. Importantly, rapamycin effectively inhibited the positive regulatory effect of FARSB overexpression on mTOR pathway-related proteins (Fig. 3B). To investigate the effect of FARSB knockdown on the mTOR pathway, we constructed FARSB knockdown cells and performed a rescue experiment by adding the mTOR signaling pathway agonist MHY1485 (MCE). We measured the phosphorylation levels of mTOR, p70S6K, and 4EBP1 by WB, and found that the protein levels of p-mTOR, p-p70S6K, and p-4EBP1 were significantly decreased after FARSB knockdown, while MHY1485 could counteract the inhibitory effect of FARSB knockdown on mTOR signaling-related proteins (Supplementary Fig. 2A, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A349). These results demonstrated that FARSB overexpression activated the mTOR signaling pathway, while FARSB knockdown inhibited the activation of the mTOR signaling pathway. Next, we investigated whether the mTOR signaling pathway plays a role in FARSB-mediated LUAD progression. CCK-8 assays, EdU staining, along with flow cytometry analyses of cancer cell apoptosis, showed that inhibition of the mTOR pathway reduced the oncogenic effects of FARSB overexpression in LUAD. This confirmed that FARSB promoted cell proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis through mTOR activation (Fig. 3C-E). Finally, WB and flow cytometry experiments indicated that blocking the mTOR pathway reduced PD-L1 expression (Fig. 3F). Rapamycin reactivated CD8+ T cells in FARSB-overexpressing LUAD cells and reversed inhibitory effects of FARSB overexpression on IFN-γ, GZMB, and TNF-α, thereby enhancing the tumor immune microenvironment (Fig. 3G-J). Collectively, these findings suggested that FARSB promoted LUAD progression and inhibited CD8+ T cell anti-tumor immunity by activating the mTOR signaling pathway.

3.4 SPI1 downregulates FARSB expression

We further investigated the mechanisms regulating FARSB expression and identified nine transcription factors associated with the downregulation of FARSB using bioinformatics analysis (Fig. 4A). Among these, SPI1 was selected based on hTFtarget screening. Pearson correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between SPI1 and FARSB expression (Fig. 4B). Using the JASPAR online tool, we predicted potential motif sites between SPI1 and the FARSB promoter (Fig. 4C), suggesting that SPI1 may serve as an upstream transcription factor for FARSB. Dual-luciferase reporter and ChIP assays were done to assess the transcriptional regulatory effect of SPI1 on FARSB. Overexpression of SPI1 significantly reduced luciferase activity in cells expressing FARSB-WT, but not in those expressing FARSB-MUT (Fig. 4D). ChIP assays validated the interaction between SPI1 and the FARSB promoter (Fig. 4E). Additionally, an analysis of TCGA database revealed that SPI1 expression was lower in LUAD tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 4F). qRT-PCR also demonstrated that SPI1 had reduced mRNA levels in LUAD cell lines, in contrast to the trend observed for FARSB (Fig. 4G), further confirming the negative correlation between SPI1 and FARSB. In summary, SPI1 transcriptionally repressed FARSB, leading to its downregulation.

3.5. SPI1 downregulates FARSB to modulate the mTOR signaling pathway, thus influencing LUAD progression and anti-tumor immunity

To further investigate whether SPI1 regulates the mTOR signaling pathway in LUAD by downregulating FARSB expression, we overexpressed SPI1 in LUAD cells and subsequently upregulated FARSB for rescue experiments. qRT-PCR and WB analyses confirmed that SPI1 expression increased, leading to a decrease in FARSB levels. Importantly, when FARSB was overexpressed, it primarily upregulated its expression without significantly affecting SPI1 levels, corroborating the previous finding of SPI1’s transcriptional repression of FARSB (Fig. 5A, B). Next, we assessed the changes in signaling pathway-related proteins through WB analysis, revealing that SPI1 overexpression inhibited the mTOR signaling pathway, while FARSB upregulation countered the suppression of this pathway induced by oe-SPI1 (Fig. 5C, D). A series of assays to evaluate LUAD progression indicated that SPI1 overexpression inhibited cell viability, proliferation, migration, and invasion, while promoting apoptosis. Conversely, overexpression of FARSB reversed these effects (Fig. 5E-G). Finally, we assessed tumor immunity-related markers to validate the impact of SPI1-mediated FARSB downregulation on the tumor immune microenvironment. The results showed that the increased expression of SPI1 suppressed PD-L1 levels, enhanced CD8+ T cell proliferation, and elevated expression of immune-promoting factors such as IFN-γ, GZMB, and TNF-α. Notably, FARSB upregulation repressed immune-promoting effects induced by SPI1 (Fig. 5H-L). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that SPI1 downregulated FARSB expression, inhibited the mTOR signaling pathway and PD-L1 expression, and subsequently suppressed LUAD progression while promoting CD8+ T cell anti-tumor immunity.

4. DISCUSSION

LUAD incidence is associated with factors such as smoking, occupational exposures, and environmental influences.21 Although immunotherapy has significantly improved survival outcomes for LUAD patients, challenges such as low response rates and resistance mechanisms substantially limit its curative potential.22,23 Thus, there is an urgent need to identify novel driver genes to predict treatment responses, overcome resistance, and expand the population benefiting from immunotherapy. In this study, we demonstrated that FARSB contributed to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment in LUAD patients, as well as to tumorigenesis and disease progression. These findings suggest that FARSB may serve as a promising new therapeutic target for LUAD.

ARSs contribute to the pathogenesis of various malignancies and exhibit non-canonical functions beyond protein translation, including roles in apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis across multiple diseases.24 FARSB, the β subunit of phenylalanine tRNA synthetase, has been implicated in autoimmune and genetic disorders through its non-classical functions.19 However, its significance in LUAD progression had not been previously reported. By integrating bioinformatics analysis with experimental validation, we demonstrated that FARSB was upregulated in LUAD, where it drove tumorigenic processes such as enhanced cell proliferation and suppression of apoptosis, ultimately leading to poorer patient prognosis.

Given the critical role of tumor-TME interactions in cancer progression and immunotherapy response,25,26 we systematically investigated the impact of FARSB on immune TME. Our findings indicated a significant negative correlation between FARSB and CD8+ T cell infiltration in LUAD, suggesting that FARSB may induce an immunosuppressive microenvironment. CD8+ T cells are crucial immune cells targeting cancer, and their infiltration is closely linked to tumor formation, survival, and metastasis.27 Furthermore, CD8+ T cell presence can influence patient prognosis and the efficacy of immunotherapy.28,29 Previous studies have often characterized impaired anti-tumor immunity by the presence of dysfunctional CD8+ T cells within TME as significant markers of cancer.30 Additionally, the immune response of CD8+ T cells is influenced by immunosuppressive factors present in local TME, such as signals released from the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint.31 PD-L1 inhibits T cell proliferation and reduces the secretion of effector cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α), leading to CD8+ T cell exhaustion and a decline in anti-tumor immunity, allowing tumor cells to evade immune surveillance.20 In this study, we demonstrated that FARSB upregulation indirectly promotes PD-L1 expression by modulating the mTOR signaling pathway, thereby affecting CD8+ T cell infiltration in the TME and the secretion of cytokines (IFN-γ, GZMB, and TNF-α), ultimately contributing to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment and facilitating LUAD progression. Furthermore, FARSB may regulate PD-L1 expression through other intermediate mechanisms, which warrants further investigation to fully elucidate the role of FARSB in LUAD immunotherapy.

Mechanistically, we found that FARSB actively participated in the regulation of the mTOR signaling pathway. Increasing evidence highlights the role of mTOR in various tumor processes, including apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, and cancer cell proliferation.32 mTOR is abnormally activated in multiple malignancies, including lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and cervical cancer.19,33,34 mTOR not only acts as a driver in cancer development but also plays a crucial regulatory role within the TME.35,36 T cells, macrophages, and antigen-presenting cells are modulated by mTOR, which fosters their development and activation, thereby enhancing anti-tumor effects or facilitating cancer growth, progression, and metastasis.37 Targeting the mTOR signaling pathway is significant in anti-tumor therapies. This study is the first to elucidate how FARSB activates the mTOR signaling pathway in LUAD, promoting malignant progression and contributing to the immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby providing new research directions and therapeutic targets for LUAD. We hypothesize that FARSB may modulate the activity of the mTOR signaling pathway by regulating certain upstream signaling pathways (eg, the PI3K/AKT pathway).38 This represents one of our key future research directions.

Additionally, we investigated the regulatory mechanisms of FARSB expression and identified the transcription factor SPI1. SPI1 is known to participate in tumor progression through transcriptional regulation.39 For instance, lncRNA small nucleolar RNA host gene 16 (SNHG16) recruits transcription factors SPI1 to enhance PARP9 transcription, thereby promoting tumorigenesis in cervical cancer.40 SPI1 binds to the Zinc Finger E-Box Binding Homeobox 1 (ZEB1) promoter to upregulate ZEB1 transcription, maintaining stemness and metastatic potential in colorectal cancer.41 Interestingly, our study revealed that SPI1 transcriptionally repressed FARSB and downregulated its expression, consequently inhibiting LUAD progression while promoting CD8+ T cell anti-tumor immunity. This finding contrasts with the typical transcriptional activation role exhibited by SPI1 in previous studies, offering a novel perspective for LUAD intervention and potential therapeutic development.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that SPI1-mediated downregulation of FARSB suppressed PD-L1 expression through the mTOR signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting LUAD progression and enhancing CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity. Our findings provide preliminary evidence for the diagnostic/prognostic value and immunomodulatory properties of FARSB in LUAD. However, a key limitation is the lack of in vivo validation. Future studies will focus on in vivo verification using genetically engineered or pharmacologically treated mouse models to characterize how the SPI1-FARSB-mTOR axis influences tumor growth and immune cell infiltration, and clinical-level validation through patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models to better recapitulate human LUAD pathology and evaluate potential FARSB-targeted therapies. These investigations will strengthen the evidence for FARSB as a novel immunotherapeutic target and facilitate translational applications.
