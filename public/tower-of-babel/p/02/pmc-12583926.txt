Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Biofilm in Chronic Wounds. What Do Practitioners Think? Consensus Through an Electronic Delphi Survey
Authors: John D Ivory, Duygu Sezgin, Patricia M Coutts, Davood Roshan, Chloe M Hobbs, José Verdú Soriano, James P O'Gara, David Gallagher, Georgina Gethin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aimed to gain clinician consensus on which signs/symptoms reported to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds are likely to be so. An international, two‐round eDelphi process including wound care clinicians ran from December 2023 to February 2024. Participants rated 26 items on a 9‐point Likert scale. Consensus to include: ≥ 70% of respondents rate an item 7–9, ≤ 15% rate it 1–3. Consensus to exclude: ≥ 70% of respondents rate an item 1–3, ≤ 15% rate it 7–9. Eleven items (visual indicators [a shiny, slimy, persistent layer, easily removed, returns quickly without frequent intervention]; failure to respond to antimicrobials; infection > 30 days duration; poor quality granulation tissue; stalled wound despite optimal management; persistent/prolonged inflammation; wound > 6 weeks duration; soft tissue deterioration despite antimicrobials/debridement; signs of local infection; tunnelling/undermining; presence of slough) achieved consensus to include status. To our knowledge, consensus work on this topic has not previously been performed on such a wide scale. When examined alongside similar work, clinical opinion on the matter lacks coherence. We hope that these findings will help direct us toward greater cohesiveness. The work supports a need for research to quantify the predictive abilities of signs and symptoms reported to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds.Keywords: biofilms, chronic wounds, consensus, electronic Delphi, signs and symptoms

Introduction

Microorganisms can present as a free‐living, planktonic phenotype or as sessile aggregates of cells known as a biofilm [1]. The biofilm phenotype occurs when bacterial cells begin to form attachments to a surface structure that are initially reversible but become more permanent and secure, and as they do, cells aggregate to each other and fixed microcolonies surrounded by a self‐produced protective extracellular matrix start to develop [2, 3]. These colonies expand through growth and through recruitment of other bacterial cells to form mature, complex biofilms characterised by oxygen, nutrient, and other substrate gradients which create a diverse microenvironment and physiological heterogeneity among the cells dwelling within [3, 4]. The biofilm phenotype predominates, and it forms and thrives in diverse environments including water systems, sediments, and soil, as dental plaque, medical devices, and living tissue [3, 4, 5, 6, 7].

Biofilms can be pathological and have been implicated in infections of various tissues of the body, for example, lung infections in cystic fibrosis patients, osteomyelitis, otitis media, periodontitis, infective carditis, and chronic wounds, as well as infections associated with medical devices such as catheters, pacemakers, and prosthetic heart valves [8, 9]. The pathology of biofilm infections is persistent and progressive, largely arising out of the host's local inflammatory response to biofilm presence, and they tend to be chronic and difficult to treat [8]. Clinical features common to chronic biofilm infections include persistent infection of more than 7 days' duration, signs of infection (tumour, rubor, dolor, loss of function, low‐grade fever), failure of antibiotic treatment and recurrence of infection, and systemic signs and symptoms of infection that resolve with antibiotic therapy but recur on cessation of treatment [8].

In terms of chronic wounds, biofilms can be characterised as ‘aggregate microorganisms that have unique characteristics and enhanced tolerance to treatment and the host defences. Wound biofilms are associated with impaired wound healing and signs and symptoms of chronic inflammation' [10]. The ability of biofilms to tolerate adverse environmental conditions significantly impairs the effectiveness of antimicrobials to manage wound bioburden, and according to Schultz et al., they are ‘probably the most important single cause of persistent, delayed healing’ after controlling for other factors such as venous or arterial insufficiency, pressure, or underlying systemic diseases [7, 11]. Wolcott et al. wrote that in spite of having dissimilar aetiologies, chronic wounds have specific biochemical and cellular similarities; in essence, they are fixed in a persistent inflammatory state with elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, proteases, and neutrophils, and this state may be a result of interference from biofilm communities [12]. They proposed that these communities are difficult to eradicate and thus tend to perpetuate inflammation, which promotes chronicity of these wounds.

Biofilms are prevalent in chronic wounds, estimated to be present in 60%–78%, possibly over 90% of these injuries, yet they have only been found in 6% of acute wounds [13, 14, 15].

Biofilms can be difficult to diagnose in chronic wounds; standard culturing methods can underestimate the degree of bacterial colonisation and species diversity, and while advanced microscopy is effective, it is not feasible to incorporate as part of standard practice, meaning that clinicians may use clinical signs and symptoms to determine their presence in chronic wounds at the bedside [8, 16, 17, 18]. Furthermore, given the high prevalence estimates for biofilm in chronic wounds, it is likely that a significant proportion of patients are seen outside the hospital setting in the community, where microscopic tests are even less feasible. While healthcare professionals may rely on specific signs and symptoms to confirm biofilm presence, little validation work has been done to confirm their predictive abilities [11, 19, 20]. Biofilm‐targeting wound management strategies incorporating debridement, therapeutic cleansing with surfactants and antiseptics, and antimicrobial wound dressings are recommended, and there are agents available that reportedly have antibiofilm capabilities, but supporting evidence is limited [2, 10, 11, 15]. A critical concern arises then in terms of the effectiveness of antibiofilm strategies and interventions in that if we cannot easily determine biofilm presence in chronic wounds, how can we be sure that it was there in the first place, that our strategies have been effective, and that its removal actually facilitated healing?

We completed a scoping review of the literature in which we collated a list of clinical signs or symptoms that were reported to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds [21].

The purpose of this study is to refine this list by gaining consensus from an international panel of experienced wound care clinical personnel through an electronic Delphi process on which of its items most likely indicate biofilm presence in chronic wounds.

Material and Methods

The methodology for this anonymous, two‐round Delphi survey was developed by an international steering committee comprising nine experienced wound care clinicians and researchers and is based on guidance for conduct of the Delphi technique provided by The COMET handbook: version 1.0 [22]. A protocol has been published previously [23].

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was sought from and granted by the University of Galway Research Ethics Committee (REC) (application reference number: 2023.08.015). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Recruitment, Sample Size and Eligibility Criteria

Attempts to contact 18 wound care organisations located worldwide were made with a request for assistance with this Delphi study. Gatekeepers of responding organisations were asked to distribute an invitation email that included a participant information leaflet and a link to the first round of the study to organisation members. The invitation email also included a snowballing element in which recipients were asked to consider forwarding it to colleagues in their field who were not necessarily organisation members.

The study aimed to recruit at least 30 clinical personnel currently active in wound care practice, including nursing, podiatry, and medicine, to form a single participant panel. There is no power calculation for statistically determining sample sizes for Delphi work, and finalizing this number is often based on matters of practicality [23]. The sample size for this work was decided after discussion between steering committee members and centered around factors of attrition, estimate of external validity, representativeness, participant appropriateness, and practicality.

Eligible participants could be located in any geographical region worldwide and had to possess three or more years of clinical experience, 50% or greater of which must have been spent in a wound care environment. Response options for eligibility questions and the consent to participate question were a dichotomous yes or no. Choosing the no option for any eligibility question or for the consent to participate question eliminated the responder from the questionnaire at that point.

Data Collection

QuestionPro survey software [24] was the platform of choice for the creation and distribution of questionnaires.

Round One

Consenting participants were asked to provide demographic details in terms of years of clinical experience, years of wound care experience, specialty (nursing, podiatry, medicine, other), field of practice (general, wound, ostomy, continence nursing, gerontology, dermatology, other), practice setting (private, community, nursing home, hospital inpatient or ambulatory, other), and country of practice.

A list of 26 clinical signs and symptoms (items) reported in the literature to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds was derived through a comprehensive scoping review (Table 1) [21]. The list was randomised in Microsoft Excel, and participants were asked to rate each item along a 9‐point Likert scale of not likely to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds (1–3), somewhat likely to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds (4–6), and very likely to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds (7–9).

Participants were also given the option to justify their rating score for each item. Finally, they were asked to provide an email address to receive a link to the second round of the questionnaire. A reply to this request was not mandatory.

The first round was open for 21 days. One week after initiation, organisation gatekeepers were asked to distribute a single reminder to complete via email to invitation recipients.

Definition of Consensus

A consensus decision to keep or reject an item on the list was made based on the following scoring system [25]:

Consensus to include: ≥ 70% of participants rate an item 7–9 and ≤ 15% rate it 1–3.

Consensus to include: ≥ 70% of participants rate an item 7–9 and ≤ 15% rate it 1–3.

Consensus to exclude: ≥ 70% of participants rate an item 1–3 and ≤ 15% rate it 7–9.

Consensus to exclude: ≥ 70% of participants rate an item 1–3 and ≤ 15% rate it 7–9.

No consensus: Any item not meeting the criteria above.

No consensus: Any item not meeting the criteria above.

Items receiving a no consensus score in round one were carried forward to round two of the process.

Feedback Between Rounds

Item ratings were examined at the end of round one, and a feedback pdf document for each item to be brought forward to round two (i.e., items receiving a no consensus score [see section 2.3.2 Definition of consensus]) was created (Supporting Information). Each document reported the number of responding and non‐responding participants and the distribution of participant scores in terms of mean/standard deviation and median/interquartile range/1st and 3rd quartiles. In addition, a frequency distribution table, bar plot, and boxplot illustrating how scores spread across the 9‐point Likert scale and a list of score justifications provided by responding participants were included. Each document was then attached to its corresponding item in the round two questionnaire.

Round Two

A questionnaire was distributed to participants who provided an email address at the end of round one. These participants were asked to provide demographic information (see section 2.3.1 Round one) and to rate a list of all items that received a no consensus score in round one along a 9‐point Likert scale (see section 2.3.1 Round one). Items receiving a consensus to include score (see section 2.3.2 Definition of consensus) at the end of round two were retained. This round was kept open for 21 days, and a reminder to complete the email was distributed 1 week after the initiation of the round.

Statistics

Data relating to demographics and participant scoring of sign and symptom items was presented in terms of number and percent of total respondents. Mean/standard deviation, median/interquartile range, and 95% confidence interval values were calculated for participant scoring of sign and symptom items.

Results

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to gain consensus by means of an electronic Delphi study from an international panel of experienced wound care professionals on which clinical signs reported in the literature to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds are most likely to be so, and we found that from a list of 26 clinical signs and symptom items, 11 met our consensus to include criterion and 15 were left with a status of no consensus after two rounds of voting.

On comparing the items that met the consensus to include criterion to those that received a no consensus status, it is difficult to discern patterns that might point toward why our participants came to the observed conclusions. The option to justify voting decisions was provided in round 1, but uncovering potential patterns in this data would require a qualitative analysis which was not specified in the original study protocol [23]. However, in the next four paragraphs, we will present our own thoughts on why panel members might have voted the way they did.

It is interesting that the visual indicators (a persistent shiny, slimy layer on the wound that is easily removed but returns quickly in the absence of frequent intervention) item received the highest percentage of votes to award it consensus to include status of all items presented to participants in our study, notwithstanding that there is associated controversy. There are those who advocate the idea that biofilm might be confirmable in chronic wounds via visual indicators, and early‐stage supporting evidence has been presented [19, 26]. However, there are also those who are critical, saying that biofilms are difficult to see with the naked eye, that other features in the wound such as exudate, slough, and debris may be erroneously conflated with biofilm presence, and that the visualisation approach is subjective, unproven, and without basis and objective criteria [11, 27]. Biofilm infections are a complicated subject and are difficult to diagnose and treat [8, 9]. However, the simplicity principle posits that we are biased toward less complex explanations of phenomena that we encounter [28]. If this principle holds true, then it might make the idea of condensing the biofilm/wound dynamic into an easily explained, observed, and managed phenomenon such as a visible and removable layer on a wound surface that can be kept at bay with regular cleansing and debridement understandable and relatable. This bias toward simplicity may at least partially explain why the panel advocated so strongly to grant this item consensus to include status. The presence of slough or fibrin item also met the consensus to include criterion despite the cautions outlined above.

Six of the items meeting the consensus to include criterion are associated with infection, wound chronicity, and failure to heal (failure to respond as expected to antimicrobial therapies; infection lasting more than 30 days/persistent cycles of recurrence [waxing and waning]; wound failing to progress to healing despite optimal management; wound duration of more than 6 weeks; soft tissue deterioration despite therapy and signs of local infection). It is consistently reported in the literature that biofilm is associated with delayed healing, and this might explain why those items were voted to meet this criterion.

Of the 15 items with no consensus status, 6 or 40% (increased exudate; malodour; swelling/wound bed with pale oedema; temperature; wound pain/pain at site; and low‐level/increased erythema) constitute individual signs of local infection versus 3 or 27% (poor quality granulation tissue e.g., discoloured, friable, fragile; or hypergranulation; tunnelling/undermining; and prolonged/persistent inflammation) of the 11 items meeting the consensus to include criterion [10]. One of the consensus to include items, however, refers to signs of infection. It could be that signs of infection are better conceptualised as a complete entity, rather than as individual components. Indeed, a validation study of clinical signs to assess bacterial burden in chronic wounds from 2009 concluded that ‘no one clinical sign may be sufficient to help clinicians decide whether a topical or systemic antibiotic should be prescribed’ [29]. The same study found that combining any three signs led to an increase in sensitivity.

It is not surprising that some items from the original list of 26 were left with a status of no consensus. Sepsis, defined as ‘life‐threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection’ [30] is an extreme and acute complication of infection involving multiple systems in the body. It is probably more surprising that this item was not completely excluded during the consensus process. Other items (other patient history indicators [change in the patient's overall wellbeing or quality of life]; extreme tolerance to host immune defence and history of systemic antibiotics in the last 12 months/prior or current use of antimicrobials) could be seen as vague and/or all‐encompassing in terms of the patient, and thus one might not intuitively associate them with a local wound context.

Placing our findings in the context of other similar research, an online survey by Metcalf and Bowler [31, 32] asked 81 wound care nurse practitioners from the United States and Europe how they knew if a wound contains biofilm. Of the 43 clinicians who responded to that question 20 (46.5%) reported that it is sometimes visible while 8 (18.6%) said that ‘it is not possible to see biofilm sometimes’ or ‘suggested indirect indicators of wound biofilm that is otherwise microscopic’. Ninety percent of our responding participants voted visual indicators as very likely to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds versus 4.6% who voted them not likely to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds. When asked about the impact of biofilm on wound healing, 13 of 37 respondents (35%) in Metcalf and Bowler's study referred to antimicrobial tolerance whereas 86% of our responding participants voted the failure to respond as expected to antimicrobial therapies item as very likely to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds (1.7% voted this item as not likely to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds). ‘Poor responses to standard care, including antimicrobial dressings or antibiotics.’ ranked highest on a frequency chart of indirect signs that included among others, ‘delayed healing’, ‘exudate’, ‘odour’, and ‘inflammation’ in Metcalf and Bowler's work. Similarly, the wound failing to progress to healing despite optimal management item was voted very likely to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds in our study.

In a Delphi study by Haesler et al. which also queried clinical signs as indicators for biofilm [33], 14 international infection experts returned median (30%–70% interpercentile range [IPR]) scores of 3.5 (1.7–5.6) and 2.0 (1.4–2.0) for rounds one and two of the process respectively that a ‘surface substance on the wound that can be visualised is an indicator of potential biofilm in a wound’ meaning that this group was opposed to the idea of visual signs as indicators of biofilm in chronic wounds. The views of participants in our Delphi work and those in the work of Haesler et al. thus ran contrary for this item. The participant populations in each study were different in terms of size, but more importantly, they may have been different in terms of composition. Haesler et al. recruited their participants from an international group that specialises in wound infection whereas our study expressed no such requirement and included participants from nursing, medicine and podiatry. While the majority of the participants in our study were highly experienced in terms of wound care, it is not inconceivable that a small group of clinical and research personnel chosen from a group that specialises in wound infection management might possess greater familiarity with the thinking and literature in that area and thus be more critical. On the other hand, of the remaining 10 clinical indicators in our study voted very likely to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds, five (failure to respond as expected to antimicrobial therapies; prolonged/persistent inflammation; signs of local infection; poor‐quality granulation tissue e.g., discoloured, friable, fragile or hypergranulation; and wound failing to progress to healing despite optimal management) corresponded to items receiving a vote of ‘agreement’ in the list of 11 clinical indicators presented to panel members in the Haesler et al. study. Four items in that study referenced antimicrobial failure (i.e., failure, unresponsiveness, recalcitrance and recurrence of delayed healing on cessation) versus a single general reference to the same in our work. Increased exudate and low‐level erythema which received agreement status in the Haesler et al. study maintained a no consensus status after two rounds of voting in our study and were thus excluded from our final list. Five items voted very likely to be indicative of biofilm in chronic wounds in our study (Infection lasting more than 30 days or persistent cycles of recurrence [waxing and waning]; wound duration of more than 6 weeks; soft tissue deterioration despite therapy; tunnelling and/or undermining; and presence of slough or fibrin) were not presented to participants in the Haesler et al. study. In summary, regarding those clinical signs thought to point to biofilm in chronic wounds subjected to rounds of voting in both studies, there appeared to be more agreement between the potentially diverse participant groups than disagreement.

This carefully designed study brought together a large, multinational group of clinical personnel, greatly experienced in hands‐on care of chronic wounds. They provided their opinion according to a Likert rating system on which items on the list of all known signs and symptoms reported to point toward biofilm in chronic wounds are most likely to do so. To our knowledge, this is the first time a consensus study on this topic of interest has been performed on such a wide scale. We anticipate that its findings might help direct us toward a greater degree of cohesiveness on a matter that can yet be seen as inconclusive.

Strengths and Limitations of This Study

The Delphi technique is not flawless, and critique has been directed toward aspects such as methodological uniformity for example, with modifications often made to the process by researchers, uncertainty, and lack of clear guidance in terms of its conduct, and need for a reporting standard for publication [22, 23, 34, 35, 36, 37]. In addition, logistics and common sense would appear to drive the selection of expert panellists rather than universally agreed criteria, and there is no clearly defined set percent agreement [23, 38, 39]. A significant strength of this study was the steering committee that convened to develop the study protocol [23]. This committee consisted of nine individuals from three different countries with considerable experience in wound care practice and research and also experience in Delphi methodology. The committee had use of a framework in the form of a comprehensive guidance document [22] which helped ensure that the development process was thorough and carefully thought through.

One hundred and three clinicians consented to participate in this Delphi process, 56 completed round 1, and of these, 25 responded to round two, with 23 participants completing it fully. The completion rates, therefore, were 54% for round one and 92% for round two. The attrition rate was high; however, sample sizes ranging from 12 to > 300 have been reported across Delphi studies [22, 23] and the number of completers for each round of our study falls within that range. The guidance we used to direct our methodology [22] deems a response rate of 80% to be satisfactory in most situations, although they also report that there is no guidance on what an acceptable response rate actually is. Nevertheless, we aimed to recruit at least 30 participants, and at the end of round 2, we had 23, which accounts for 76.7% of our anticipated sample size.

The participant group in this study was strong. We hoped to recruit a global sample of wound care clinicians, and we were not disappointed, with representatives from 20 countries taking part. More than three specialties were represented, and nursing, a discipline very much found on the front line in terms of hands‐on wound assessment and management, formed the majority. In addition, our participant group as a whole was greatly experienced, having for the most part between 11 and 21+ years of experience in the field.

Protocol Deviations

We planned to retain items with a no consensus status at the end of round two and include them in a list of potential signs and symptoms of biofilm in chronic wounds to be subjected to testing against clinical samples. However, 15 items were left with this status, and we considered this to be too large and cumbersome a number to be practical in a clinical scenario.

We wanted to experiment with a Bland Altman analysis approach in an attempt to assess change in participants' scoring on items between rounds to examine the degree of consensus [22, 23, 40]. Ultimately, this was not possible because only items with ‘no consensus’ status passed through both rounds of voting, there was attrition between rounds, and mostly because the survey platform assigned a different response identification number to participants for each questionnaire round, making it impossible to match respondents to their responses across rounds. The platform records the IP address of each participant, but we could only match IP addresses across both rounds in the case of five respondents.

Conclusion

This electronic Delphi exercise found that in the opinion of an international panel of experienced wound care clinicians there are 11 signs and symptoms that are very likely to indicate the presence of biofilm in chronic wounds. Experienced clinical personnel are doubtless cognisant of the concept of biofilm in chronic wounds, of the risks it can pose and of the treatment strategies available, but when the findings of this study are viewed in unison with other opinion work on this topic it becomes apparent that wound care practitioners are not entirely coherent with regard to clinical indicators of biofilm. We previously observed that little validation work has been done in this area, and we advocated that efforts should be made to validate reported signs and symptoms of biofilm in chronic wounds [21]. The findings of and observations made in this Delphi study would appear to lend further support to this call.
