Transcultural Adaptation of the Spanish Version of the ABILHAND Scale for Hand Surgery in a Colombian Population of Patients with Hand Pathologies
Authors: Mónica Botero Bermúdez, Luis Alejandro García González, María Carolina Suaza Ramos, Isabel del Socorro Moreno Luna, Francisco Aguilar Sierra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeBecause manual ability is a latent and complex variable that is closely linked to an individual’s well-being and daily functioning, it is important to assess it through patient-reported outcome measures that capture the patient’s own perception of dexterity in everyday life. This study aimed to develop a Spanish version of the ABILHAND-Hs scale that is equivalent to the original but adapted to evaluate the most prevalent hand pathologies in Colombia.MethodsA group of bilingual experts translated the ABILHAND scale from English into Spanish using a forward–backward translation method. A patient focus group evaluated the suitability and comprehensibility of each item. The researchers administered the Spanish version of the scale, along with the QuickDASH (Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand), 12-item Short Form Survey (SF-12), and the numerical pain scale (NPS), to 220 adults diagnosed with carpal tunnel syndrome, trigger finger, de Quervain tenosynovitis, distal radius fracture, or hand fracture who attended hand surgery consultations or underwent physical or hand therapy between May and August 2023. The scale’s validity, internal consistency, reliability, and fit to the Rasch model were analyzed using WINSTEPS software.ResultsThe Spanish version of the ABILHAND-Hs covers a measurement range of 6.27 logits and includes 19 bimanual items and two unimanual activities on a 3-level grading scale. The instrument demonstrates validity, reliability, unidimensionality, and local independence and shows no important differential item functioning based on patient characteristics. It correlates fairly with SF-12, moderately with QuickDASH, and poorly with the NPS. It remains stable in the same clinical conditions and shows fair sensitivity to change.ConclusionsABILHAND-Hs in Spanish achieves conceptual, semantic, operational, and functional equivalence with the original instrument.Type of study/level of evidenceDiagnostic Ib.Key words: Disability evaluation, Hand surgery, Manual ability, Patient-reported outcome measures, Transcultural adaptation

Although hand pathologies are rarely life-threatening, they can cause notable disability and affect patients’ quality of life.1,2 Proper assessment and management of these conditions require a comprehensive understanding of the patient’s daily manual ability, which includes assessing not only objective variables, but also patient-reported outcome measures that reflect symptoms, adaptations in daily life, and patient’s perception of their own ability.1, 2, 3 Before the development of ABILHAND, no instrument specifically measured manual ability. Most available scales available were validated using classic test theory, which has key limitations: it cannot handle missing responses, it often assumes that all items function uniformly—an assumption frequently invalid, as items may measure different aspects or domains of a construct—and its total scores sum ordinal variables with nonconstant units, so incremental changes may not represent true differences in the underlying trait.4

To address these limitations, the Rasch model provides a modern measurement approach. It models the relationship between manual ability and the probability of success (the likelihood of rating an activity as easy) using a logistic function that converts ordinal scores into linear interval measures, expressed as “logits”.5,6 This approach links the continuous range of ability to observable responses, which are determined by the subject’s ability, the item’s difficulty, and its discriminative power. It also allows activities to be ordered by difficulty on the same scale as patients’ ability, so that higher ability corresponds to successfully performing more complex manual tasks.4,7

Specifically, ABILHAND was originally developed by Massimo Penta for patients with rheumatoid arthritis, but has since been adapted for various populations. Versions share certain “anchor” items, enabling cross-population comparisons.5,8 Specifically, ABILHAND-Hs includes 23 items assessing patients’ ease or difficulty performing daily activities during the previous week, without assistance from tools or others, regardless of adaptations. Each item is rated as “easy,” “difficult,” or “impossible,” whereas nonperformed activities can be marked missing without compromising validity.5,9

Given the absence of a comparable tool in Spanish, this study aimed to adapt ABILHAND-Hs for Colombian patients with common hand conditions such as carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), trigger finger (TF), de Quervain tenosynovitis (DQT), distal radius fracture (DRF), and hand fracture (HF). The objective was not only translation and psychometric validation, but also cultural adaptation to ensure relevance and accuracy for this population.

Materials and Methods

Recruitment

Researchers obtained approval from the authors of ABILHAND-Hs and the institutional review boards before conducting this study. The protocol followed the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all patients provided written informed consent. Adults diagnosed with CTS, DRF, TF, DQT, or a fracture of the finger or metacarpals were eligible. Because Colombia has a relatively stable population with few immigrants (5%), we did not require proof of nationality; instead, we relied on pronunciation, accent, and vocabulary to identify native Colombian Spanish speakers.10

We excluded patients with partial or complete hand amputations, cerebral palsy, active rheumatologic disease, cognitive impairment, or those with casts or sutures still in place. Crosscultural adaptation

The authors of the scale provided a repository of 83 items derived from previous ABILHAND versions. A forward–backward translation process was conducted, in which a panel of experts (a certified translator, a bilingual physician, and hand surgeon) independently translated the instrument. The authors then reached a consensus to select the translation to keep for the Spanish version. Subsequently, during backward translation, a different group of experts translated the instrument back into English, and the result was shared with the original author to verify that the meaning had been accurately preserved. Finally, a patient focus group evaluated the clarity and cultural relevance of each item.

The researchers then tested the translated instrument with 30 patients, analyzed response variability, and assessed interitem relationships using a polychoric correlation matrix, as the preliminary sample size was insufficient to perform a proper Rasch analysis.13,14 Following the established protocol, researchers removed items with high collinearity (r > 0.95), low correlations (<0.3), or those deemed irrelevant by the focus group. The remaining items, including all from the English version of the ABILHAND-Hs, were administered to the full sample in five random orders, alongside validated Spanish versions of the QuickDASH (Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand), 12-item Short Form Survey (SF-12), and the numerical pain scale (NPS).9,15,16

Researchers reassessed patients via cellphone 1–4 weeks later. Using the Colombian QuickDASH minimally detectable change (20 points) as a reference, they classified patients within ±20 points as stable for test-retest analysis and those outside this range for sensitivity to change analysis.16

The evaluated pathologies respond heterogeneously to medical interventions; for instance, steroid injections for TF can produce immediate improvement, whereas fractures require longer functional recovery. Researchers therefore selected an intermediate follow-up to capture both stability and change across conditions. Furthermore, extending follow-up beyond 1 month posed a methodological risk, as Colombia’s ongoing health insurance financial crisis has created instability in service provision, with variable agreements between insurers and study institutions. A 1-month follow-up minimized patient attrition and prevented unrecorded external interventions that could induce bias.

Fit analysis

The protocol outlined the Rasch-based steps used to determine whether further modifications to the instrument were necessary. These steps aligned with those originally applied in the validation of the ABILHAND-Hs, as well as with the procedures described in the WINSTEPS User’s Guide and standard Rasch analysis practices.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9,17,18

In first instance, researchers anchored item difficulty to the English ABILHAND-Hs and estimated patient ability with unconditional maximum likelihood method. Measurement range, standard error of measurement and the minimally detectable change (MDC), and floor/ceiling effects were calculated, along with a Wright map that was plotted to evaluate item targeting.19

To test Rasch model assumptions, unidimensionality was analyzed through residuals, which represent the difference between expected and observed measures in logits for patients’ ability levels and the item difficulty. When data fit the Rasch model, residuals are expected to be small and randomly distributed. However, because residuals are real numbers, it is necessary to calculate the mean square residuals (MNSQ) for comparison. MNSQ values weighted by variance are referred to as “infit”, whereas unweighted values are referred to as “outfit” (adequate range 0.5–1.5).20, 21, 22

Standardized fit statistics are also calculated from MNSQ to evaluate the probability that the fit statistics occurred by chance. A range between –2 and 2 is desirable to achieve 95% confidence in the results. Moreover, a correlation matrix of the standardized residuals is constructed to evaluate potential codependency (correlations above 0.5 between items).20, 21, 22 All these statistics are considered to determine whether an item should be removed.

Unidimensionality was also assessed through a residual principal component analysis (PCAR) to determine whether secondary dimensions, beyond manual ability, explained the data. Any secondary components were expected to rise by chance and account for less than 5% of the variance.14,23 Additionally, a paired Wilcoxon test compared scores from the full instrument with those derived from only the easiest or most difficult items. No significant differences were expected (P < .01), confirming unidimensionality.

Response categories analysis

We assessed response variability, flagging items with <10 observations per category or frequent missing values. This information contributed to the decision on whether to eliminate items.

Researchers verified that the response categories were appropriately discriminated by respondents through Andrich thresholds, which represent the ability level at which the probability of choosing one response option over another is 50%. Ideally, the thresholds should increase by at least 1.4 logits but less than 5.0 logits between categories, ensuring a hierarchical order.24,25

Analysis of the generalizability of the instrument

Differential item functioning (DIF) is a key aspect of validity, as item interpretation should not vary by patient characteristics. To assess this, researchers used a Rasch–Welch t test to examine whether ability logits differed by demographic variables. Differences are expected to be <0.43 logits or nonsignificant at P = .01 (Bonferroni corrected).26

Concurrent validity, reliability, and sensitivity

Researchers evaluated concurrent validity against QuickDASH, SF-12 and NPS, as there is no gold standard for measuring manual ability. Reliability was assessed with Rasch indices: person reliability (discrimination across strata), item reliability (hierarchy stability), and separation indices (>1.5 acceptable), as Cronbach’s alpha is invalid for Rasch theory because it is based on nonlinear data.17,27,28

The reliability index, ranging from 0 to 1, is the ratio of true variance to observed variance, with values above 0.8 indicating adequate consistency. Person reliability assesses the instrument’s ability to distinguish different ability levels, whereas item reliability reflects how accurately the sample orders items along the latent trait. The separation index, calculated as the ratio of true standard deviation to error, indicates how many strata the instrument can identify and its discriminative power, with values above 1.5 considered acceptable.17,28,29

After follow-up, a Rasch–Welch t test was conducted to evaluate whether reassessed patients exhibited DIF compared with those lost to follow-up, with a nonsignificant P value (P = .01) considered ideal. Stability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (0.5–0.75 = moderate).30,31 Sensitivity to change was confirmed when patients exceeded the MDC on both ABILHAND and QuickDASH. Finally, researchers dichotomized patients into “skilled” and “non-skilled” groups (median ability = 0.37 logits), and a Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare scores between these groups. A statistically significant difference was expected, supporting the scale’s sensitivity to ability level.

Results

Item selection

During the preliminary phase of analysis, 32 items were removed from the original repository because of cultural inappropriateness, ambiguity, redundancy, or collinearity. This left a 51-item questionnaire, which was further refined through iterative Rasch analyses. Items were eliminated for various reasons: 11 misfit the model based on infit and outfit statistics, 13 were codependent with better fitting items, two presented disorganized Andrich thresholds, and one showed a narrow threshold. Additionally, three items were not relevant to the Colombian population, as they were not part of everyday activities and showed over 50% missing responses (eg, “playing a racket sport”).

Metric properties

The final version of the Spanish ABILHAND-Hs contained 21 items, including 19 bimanual and two unimanual activities. Item difficulty was anchored to the English version to ensure comparability. The items are reported in Table 2 in descending order of difficulty. The instrument demonstrated a measurement range of 6.27 logits, with mean item difficulty at –0.89 logits. Patient ability scores spanned from –6.28 to 6.16 logits, with minimal ceiling (0.45%) and no floor effects. Fit analysis

Fit analysis supported the unidimensionality of the scale. Global mean square infit values were 1.02 for items and 1.03 for patients, and outfit values were similarly close to one. All items showed acceptable fit, except “taking a coin out of the pocket,” a unilateral activity. Local independence was maintained as there was no evidence of item codependence.

PCAR showed that the Rasch dimension explained 59% of variance, whereas secondary components each explained less than 5%, suggesting random noise rather than additional dimensions. This was supported by the Wilcoxon test, which showed no significant differences when comparing parameter estimates across “extreme items” (P = .02).

Thresholds between response categories “impossible,” “difficult,” and “easy” were logically ordered at –1.71 and 1.71 logits, respectively, with no evidence of disordered steps. The Figure 2 illustrates the expected response for each item according to ability level.

No item–trait interaction was detected, and DIF was absent across age, education, diagnosis, treatment, and severity of condition according to the Rasch–Welch t test. Two exceptions were observed: “taking a coin out of the pocket” showed sex-related DIF, whereas “handling scissors” showed DIF related to dominant hand impairment.

Concurrent validity, reliability, and sensitivity

Concurrent validity testing revealed fair correlations with the SF-12 physical (–0.68) and mental (0.56) domains, moderate correlation with the QuickDASH (0.3), and poor correlation with the NPS (–0.26). Reliability was strong: person reliability ranged from 0.89 to 0.9, and item reliability reached 0.99. The person separation index (2.87) confirmed the instrument’s ability to discriminate across ability levels, while the item separation index (10.25) demonstrated stable item hierarchy.

Although only 44% of patients were available for phone follow-up, the Rasch–Welch t test confirmed the absence of DIF between reassessed patients and those lost to follow-up (P < .01). Test-retest analysis confirmed reproducibility, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.8 (95% CI, 0.68–0.87, P < .01).

After a mean 33-day follow-up, 19 patients showed improvement and six worsened. Only 9 of 25 had ABILHAND scores exceeding the MDC, and just five of these aligned with their QuickDASH results. However, the instrument was able to detect significant differences across groups stratified by ability, supporting sensitivity to change (H = 158.78, d.f. = 2, P < .01).

Discussion

This study adapted the ABILHAND-Hs scale into Spanish for Colombia, focusing on the most prevalent pathologies in this context. Linguistic validity was ensured using a forward–backward translation technique, which facilitated communication with the original author and provided researchers with a deeper understanding of the translated content while maintaining control over the process.32,33 However, the objective was not to produce a literal translation of the English version. Instead, a qualitive analysis with a focus group and iterative Rasch analyses were conducted to tailor the instrument to our population. This was possible because the Rasch model allows the creation of different versions of the same instrument without compromising validity or equivalence, as long as some anchor items are maintained.4

The final Spanish instrument included 21 items, 10 of which were retained from the English version. Items were removed because of lack of contextual relevance, poor model fit, codependency, disrupted response hierarchies, or insufficient discrimination.

Validity was confirmed using multiple analyses. PCAR supported unidimensionality for 95% of the scale. All items fit well except “taking a coin out of the pocket,” which had a normal infit but high outfit, likely because of compensation with the healthy hand. The item was retained because it assesses intrinsic muscle activity essential for manual ability.

Response categories functioned appropriately since analysis reflected an appropriate number of response options and adequate coverage of the construct. Proper functioning was further confirmed, with consistency and reliability demonstrated by reliability indices approaching one.17 Likewise, the separation indices indicated that the instrument was sufficiently sensitive to distinguish between patients with low and high ability.

No DIF was observed except for two items. “Taking a coin out of the pocket” exhibited DIF based on sex, likely because this activity is more affected by HFs, which were more prevalent in men than in women (18% vs 4%). Similarly, “handling scissors” showed DIF related to impairment of the dominant hand. Hence, items were retained as invariance was not compromised.

Concurrent validity was comparable to the English version, with slightly lower correlation with NPS, likely because of assessment timing after therapy sessions or consultations.18 A stronger correlation was not expected with any instrument, as the comparison scales are “generic” and therefore less sensible to manual ability.32 Moreover, the instrument demonstrated stability, with adequate intraclass correlation coefficients despite follow-up being conducted by phone.34

ABILHAND-Hs in Spanish, unlike other available tools, tolerates missing responses, detects aberrant response patterns, and allows probabilistic predictions of patient responses, which is useful for monitoring and prognosis. Furthermore, its three response categories make it easier for individuals with low literacy to complete, providing greater reliability of measurements.35

The Spanish ABILHAND-Hs showed clinical relevance for patients with greater manual impairment, offered a broad measurement range without notable gaps, and had advantages over the English version, including applicability to more diagnoses, fewer items, validation across three centers, and a lower ceiling effect.9,18

However, this instrument is not applicable to excluded patients, particularly those presenting with conditions outside the diagnostic scope for which this scale was designed. The instrument specifically targets the most prevalent hand pathologies in Colombia, which may limit its generalizability to other clinical conditions.

Additional limitations include the short follow-up period and the loss of a proportion of the initial sample during follow-up, which may be related to the previously described instability of Colombia’s health care system. Although no differences were observed in the measured demographic variables between reassessed patients and those lost to follow-up, residual attrition, and selective nonresponse bias may have occurred, potentially reducing the validity and statistical power of the test-retest and sensitivity to change analyses.

Furthermore, reliance on the QuickDASH as a reference measure may have led to misclassification of clinical stability or change because of its multidimensional nature. In addition, although phone-based follow-up has been validated for ABILHAND, differences in administration mode—initial in-person assessment followed by telephone reassessment—may have influenced the results.

Equally important, ABILHAND-Hs analysis is methodologically complex and requires proprietary software, which may pose a challenge for researchers unfamiliar with the Rasch model. Nevertheless, the instrument does not require specialized training for administration and can be completed during routine consultations. To facilitate this, we developed a guide to assist health care professionals in estimating patient ability from raw scores efficiently and accurately, requiring minimal time for calculation and interpretation (see Appendix S1, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org).

Overall, this study demonstrates the scale’s applicability in a new population and provides a foundation for further research to evaluate its use in other languages and settings, including the assessment of DIF and broader validation efforts, while addressing the limitations identified in the present study.
