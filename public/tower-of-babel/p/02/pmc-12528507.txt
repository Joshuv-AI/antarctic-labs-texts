Impact of oral hygiene interventions on dental health in chemotherapy patients with malignant salivary gland tumors: A retrospective study
Authors: Arstanbekov Sabyrbek Rustamovich, Abdirasulova Tattybubu Abdirasulovna, Riaz Ahmad, Pakyrov Zhenishbek Karakozuevich, Mamatkulov Shakhobidin Abduvakhidovich, Arstanbekov Mamatzhan Arstanbekovich, Akhmatov Abdikhalil Tolobayevich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examined the dental status of individuals with malignant salivary gland epithelial tumors before and after oral hygiene therapy to identify the incidence of dental issues. This study examines how pre-treatment dental care affects oral health during chemotherapy in malignant salivary gland tumor patients. The research included 318 salivary gland malignant epithelial tumor patients, 166 male and 152 female. The tumor staging showed 104 stage I patients (32.7%), 122 stage III patients (38.4%), and 92 stage IV patients (28.9%). A comprehensive dental exam assessed caries, pulpitis, wedge-shaped deformities, and dental treatment needs. The examination found significant oral disease rates in patients. The typical man had 3.16 cavities to fill and 6.83 teeth to remove before dental cleaning. After cleaning, no teeth required extraction; however 4.81 were carious, with pulpitis rising. The average number of teeth filled in women was 2.71, whereas 1.19 needed extraction before cleaning. Pulpititis caused 9.15 carious teeth, however post-cleaning extraction was not needed. Our research reveals how chemotherapy impacts oral health in Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan, emphasizing the need for preventive dental care. Therapeutic and sanitation treatments enhanced oral hygiene, making early intervention crucial. Future research should focus on long-term oral health care strategies for chemotherapy patients in Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan to enhance treatment outcomes.

Introduction

One of the most challenging issues in treating neoplastic diseases in salivary glands is their malignant nature. These tumors disrupt the exocrine system by means of uncontrollably growing malignant cells (Fabrya et al. 2024). Furthermore adverse to the body are radiation and chemotherapy, widely used treatments for malignant tumours. This combined action usually results in the aggravation of pre-existing oral lesions as well as the development of other concurrent inflammatory diseases within the oral mucosa (Sun et al. 2024). Patients with malignant epithelial tumors, especially in the maxillofacial area, may have major oral health problems including xerostomia, viral stomatitis, mucositis, and infections—both fungal and bacterial even with adherence to ideal oral hygiene measures (Watters et al. 2025). These disorders may cause bleeding as well, especially in those receiving chemoradiotherapy. Unfortunately, before starting inpatient anticancer therapies, the required cleanliness and oral hygiene practices for control of chronic inflammatory foci are often neglected. This gap emphasizes the need of evaluating oral condition before starting chemoradiotherapy. These tests provide a baseline for monitoring the condition of the oral cavity and assist to reduce any issues that can aggravate during cancer therapy. Tracking the effects of chemoradiotherapy over time as well as early metastases depend on this baseline (Xiong et al. 2020).

Dentists undertake certain procedures to guarantee the best oral health in patients with malignant epithelial tumors of the salivary glands in the head and neck area ready for chemoradiotherapy. Usually removed are damaged teeth, those with sharp edges, or those displaying indicators of persistent periodontal disease. Likewise, teeth acting as focal points of persistent infection—that is, those with chronic granulomatous periodontitis—should also be taken out (Lanzetti et al. 2023). Therapeutic dental procedures—including filling of carious teeth, restoration, and reconstruction—should be scheduled prior. Old or defective restorations should be improved and orthodontic and orthopedic structures should be regularly inspected especially to avoid damage to the mouth mucosa (Levi and Lalla 2018). Before starting radiation, all metallic dental structures—including crowns, detachable partial dentures, and implants—should be taken out, especially considering preoperative or drastic dosages (Liang et al. 2024). While nystatin is sometimes used to prevent fungal infections, existing plastic prosthetic constructs must be regularly cleaned and treated with antiseptic treatments (Gøtzsche and Johansen 2014). Timing of surgical operations is essential given the possibility of late radiation effects such osteoradionecrosis. Extractions should start at least ten to twelve days before the expected neutrophil count drop. Tooth extractions should be done using the least stressful techniques and finished two to three weeks before the start of radio-chemotherapy in order to encourage best recovery. While extractions for lower jaw teeth should be done no later than 7 days in advance, those for upper jaw teeth should take place no more than 5 days before chemotherapy (Buurman et al. 2022). Any exostoses or sharp edges that develop during extraction should be polished right away to avoid further problems. It is advised against using hemostatic agents or wound healing accelerators at the extraction site as they might start an infection. Appropriate healing depends on ensuring main scar closure of the extraction socket (Guo et al. 2019; Mahardawi et al. 2023).

The aim of this study is to evaluate the dental status of patients with malignant salivary gland tumors before and after oral hygiene interventions, identifying how pre-treatment dental care impacts oral health outcomes during chemotherapy. It also evaluates both before and after oral cavity cleanliness and, the dental condition of individuals with salivary gland malignant epithelial tumors. Given chemoradiotherapy, this study is especially pertinent in helping to grasp the oral health issues experienced by such individuals. This research offers insightful analysis on enhancing patient outcomes and reducing oral problems during cancer therapy by evaluating the pre-treatment dental condition and the efficacy of oral hygiene practices. Moreover, the results underline the importance of integrated care strategies combining dental and oncological therapy to guarantee that patients get thorough treatment covering both their cancer and oral health requirements. This study also provides the foundation for the next studies meant to maximize dental treatment strategies in cancer patients, especially in areas like Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan, where such data are limited.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study of 318 patients, aged 21 to 70 years, of both sexes, diagnosed with salivary gland cancer, was conducted at two clinical sites: the Department of Orthopedic Dentistry with a course in Orthodontics at Osh State University, Kyrgyz Republic, and the Dentistry, Pediatric Dentistry, and Orthodontics Center for Professional Qualification Development in Tashkent, Uzbekistan. Data were retrospectively collected from these institutional records), including clinical exams, radiographs, and treatment logs. All records were anonymized and standardized to ensure consistency in analysis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The patient cohort was categorized based on the stage of their disease. Staging followed the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual, 8th Edition (Amin et al. 2017). 104 patients (32.7%) were diagnosed with stage I disease, 122 patients (38.4%) with stage III disease, and 92 patients (28.9%) with stage IV disease. Patients with stage II disease or unclassified/unknown staging or with simultaneous active malignancies, significant comorbidities (e.g., end-stage renal disease, decompensated cirrhosis), or factors likely to distort survival analysis were excluded due to their limited sample size and clinical heterogeneity compared to Stages I, III, and IV. Additionally, patients without baseline staging documents, treatment records, or without follow-up data were excluded.

Data collection

To assess the dental status of the patients, both clinical examinations and radiological studies were performed. The prevalence and intensity of dental caries were evaluated, and the oral hygiene status was assessed using the Fedorov-Volodkina hygiene index (FVI). The examination and assessment were conducted at two time points: prior to the initiation of oral sanitation procedures and after their completion.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association 2013). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent was waived by the relevant ethics committees. All patient information was anonymized during data collection and analysis to prevent any identification of individuals. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was started to analyze connections between salivary gland cancer stages (I, III, IV) and dental health factors, while examining the influence of oral cleaning treatments. Demographic and clinical characteristics (age, sex, cancer stage) were summarized using means ± standard deviations (for normally distributed continuous variables) or medians with interquartile ranges (for non-normal distributions). Categorical variables (e.g., caries prevalence, comorbidities) were given as frequencies and percentages. To prevent retrospective research biases, sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of results across subgroups (e.g., by clinical location). Stratified analyses verified findings were similar across Osh State University (Kyrgyz Republic) and the Tashkent Center (Uzbekistan). Analyses were done using SPSS v.27 (IBM Corp.) or R v.4.3.1, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Missing data (where existent) were handled by multiple imputation or complete-case analysis, depending on the level of missingness.

Upon being admitted to the hospital for inpatient treatment, a thorough evaluation of oral health among patients diagnosed with malignant epithelial tumors of the salivary glands indicated an overall importance of expert oral hygiene. This significant need for dental care emphasizes a general disregard of oral hygiene among this patient group, which might have consequences for general health as well as oncological therapy efficacy.

Measuring oral hygiene status using the FVI, the scores averaged between 2.4 and 2.8 (± 0.2), indicating a “dissatisfactory” to “poor” degree of oral cleanliness. Consistently unsatisfactory findings were shown. This result emphasizes the great necessity of oral hygiene development both before and after cancer treatment. During chemotherapy, an interval often compounded by mucositis, opportunistic infections, and increased inflammatory responses, all of which may be aggravated by poor dental health, the need for thorough oral care becomes more important.

Combining surgical intervention with adjuvant treatments, including chemotherapy and radiation, a multimodal therapeutic strategy is used in the conventional treatment plan for malignant epithelial salivary gland tumors. Whenever possible, dental care is included in the pre-treatment phase to reduce the risk of oral problems during these treatments. The timing and the extent of the dental sanitation process were influenced by multiple important factors, including the timing of hospital admission, the need for pre-treatment consultations with multidisciplinary specialists, and the scheduling of main oncological interventions, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Furthermore affecting the whole management of oral health was the intricacy of specific types of dental caries that call for long treatment periods. Therapeutic dental treatment was thus given to the teeth once found for cleanliness during the preliminary stage.

After dental cleansing was finished, the elements of the caries index (KPUUx) clearly changed. Whereas the component for “carious teeth” dropped from 3.16 to 1.02, the signal for “teeth required extraction” dropped to zero. Reflecting the removal of non-restorable teeth, the component “extracted teeth” changed simultaneously from 16.04 to 22.87. Rising from 2.67 to 4.81, the component known as “pulpitis” indicated the detection and control of extra dental pathology over the course of therapy. Among female patients, the elements for “carious teeth,” “pulpitis,” “extracted teeth,” and “teeth requiring extraction,” likewise showed notable improvements; “pulpitis” dropped to zero. Whereas the component for'extracted teeth'rose from 8.56 to 9.75, the component for'carious teeth'dropped from 2.71 to 0.8. For “pulpitis,” the component went from 7.24 to 9.15.

Over the course of therapy, statistical analysis revealed a notable variation in the caries index components—carious lesions, pulpitis cases, removed teeth, and teeth needing extraction. The consistently low oral health scores shown in the Fedorov–Volodkina index, which ranged from 2.4 to 2.8 (± 0.2), confirm the already mentioned need for improved oral hygiene habits. Patients were highly urged to give regular and strict oral hygiene practices top priority in order to reduce and prevent the negative symptoms connected with chemoradiation treatment, including mucosalis and stomatitis. Particularly in relation to radiation treatment, managing oral problems like mucositis and stomatitis still presents a significant therapeutic difficulty for proactive preventative actions.

Before starting thorough oncological treatment, a planned oral health intervention was carried out to maximize patients'dental hygiene and reduce the likelihood of therapy-related problems. After professional dental cleansing, this intervention consisted of patient education sessions stressing the need to keep strict oral hygiene habits. Dental plaque and calculus were efficiently removed with mechanical debridement done using curettes or ultrasonic scalers. Patients presenting with more tooth sensitivity—especially in the cervical areas resulting from hyposalivation—were given localized therapy with fluoride-containing gels to strengthen the enamel and lower pain.

Patients were encouraged to utilize mouth rinses, such as physiological saline or bicarbonate solutions, which assist in neutralize oral acidity and encourage mucosal healing, to manage and avoid oral mucosal problems typically linked with cancer therapy, like stomatitis and mucositis. Working with the oncology care team—including chemotherapists and radiologists—a multidisciplinary approach was used to customize oral care plans depending on the hematological profile of the patient. Patients with thrombocytopenia especially received particular attention as a drop in platelet counts below 20,000 units/mm1 greatly increased the risk of spontaneous gingival bleeding. Standard brushing methods were changed for such cases; patients were advised to use extra-soft toothbrushes, sterile sponges, or cotton swabs for mechanical cleaning while including antiseptic rinses such as 0.6% chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine for microbial control and infection prevention.

These focused oral hygiene practices clearly improved the results of oral health maintenance. Originally ranging from 2.4 to 2.8 (± 0.2), the Fedorov-Volkodkina hygiene index indicated a statistically significant decrease to 1.8 to 2.0 (± 0.16), so classifying “satisfactory” though still somewhat bordering on “unsatisfactory.”

All things considered, the thorough dental examination carried out among patients diagnosed with salivary gland malignant epithelial tumors indicated a high frequency and severity of main dental pathologies, including dental caries and periodontal disorders. These results highlight the urgent necessity of integrated dental care within the multidisciplinary management of oncological patients to enhance treatment outcomes and lower the probability of therapy-related problems along with consistently poor oral hygiene ratings.

Discussion

Upon hospital admission, the examination of oral health conditions in patients diagnosed with malignant epithelial tumors of the salivary glands indicated an immediate need for oral cleaning. The fact that none of the male patients had a clean oral cavity suggests a significant discrepancy in pre-admission oral hygiene. This result fits research stressing the significance of pre-treatment oral health optimization in oncology patients to lower the risk of problems during cancer treatment (Epstein et al. 2012; Yarom et al. 2019) (Epstein et al. 2012; Yarom et al. 2019).

Men revealed an increased risk of dental disease than women according to a study by Lipsky, Martin et al. (2021). Men had 3.16 average carious teeth; 16.04 teeth had previously been removed, and 6.83 teeth were found to need extraction. Men also showed an average of 2.67 filled teeth, well below the female group. Men's overall index for carious teeth, pulpitis, removed teeth, and teeth needing extraction came out to be 28.7, indicating a significant burden of oral illness. Especially among 12 male patients (20%) aged 70 years and beyond, full edentulism was seen, suggesting severe tooth disease development and maybe past lack of dental treatment.

The observed increase in pulpitis post-sanitation may be partially attributed to radiation-related dental decay, a recognized complication of chemoradiotherapy, especially in the absence of fluoride-based prevention protocols. By comparison, female patients showed somewhat higher indices of dental health. In women, the combined index for carious teeth, pulpitis, removed teeth, and teeth needing extraction was 19.7. Women arrived with an average of 2.71 carious teeth, 8.56 removed teeth (nearly half compared to males), and only 1.19 teeth needing extraction, a six-fold decrease relative to men. Furthermore, women's average number of filled teeth—7.24—was almost three times higher than men's, suggesting more past involvement with restorative dentistry.

Impact of chemotherapy on oral health

Particularly among patients receiving chemotherapy, a strong need for the restoration of oral hygiene was observed. Study by S Al-Ansari et al. (2015) shows immunosuppressive effects of chemotherapy, which compromise the body's natural defenses and increase patients'vulnerability to a range of oral health problems including mucoseis, stomatitis, and an increased risk of bacterial, viral, and fungal infections in the oral cavity, drive this need (Al-Ansari et al. 2015). These oral symptoms not only affect the comfort and quality of life of chemotherapy patients but also might delay the cancer treatment to be completed. In our study, with a Fedorov-Volodkina index score ranging from 2.4 to 2.8 ± 0.2, the evaluation of oral hygiene in these individuals indicated an initial “unsatisfactory” or “poor” level. This score indicates to a considerable deficiency in oral hygiene habits, which can aggravate chemotherapy-related problems like infection risk (Amin et al. 2024). These results highlight the vital requirement of proactive dental treatment, including frequent oral exams, instruction on appropriate oral hygiene practices, and quick interventions to prevent and control oral health problems. Such steps are crucial to lower the effect of side effects connected to treatment and improve the general health and well-being of patients under chemotherapy.

Hygiene index improvement

Initially evaluated as “unsatisfactory” to “poor” with a range of 2.4–2.8 (± 0.2), the Fedorov-Volodkina hygiene index showed a notable rise after a sequence of therapeutic and sanitation treatments. Reflecting generally improved oral hygiene, the index score dropped to a range of 1.8–2.0 (± 0.16) after the intervention. This change shows that the hygienic degree stayed on the verge of the “unsatisfactory” category even though it was reclassified as “satisfactory.” The success of the treatment interventions—which most certainly included the elimination of plaque, lowering of bacterial load, and relief of mucosal irritation—helps to explain the improvement (Li et al. 2023). Notwithstanding this encouraging change, the hygienic level still indicates that further developments in dental care and hygiene habits are required to reach ideal oral health and stop upcoming issues. Such findings highlight the requirement of continuing patient education on appropriate oral hygiene maintenance and the need for regular dental monitoring, especially in patients with reduced immunity resulting from chemotherapy or other treatments.

Challenges and future recommendation

Enhanced preventive programs and patient compliance

It is important to create comprehensive preventive oral hygiene programs for chemotherapy patients, concentrating on early intervention and education as per the study by Al Shajwani et al. (2024). However, a substantial challenges remains assuring patient adherence to these programs, since the pain associated with chemotherapy-induced oral symptoms, such as mucositis, might impede patients’ capacity or motivation to continue strict oral hygiene practices. To overcome this, personalized patient education and frequent follow-ups should be prioritized to enhance compliance (Watach et al. 2021).

Longitudinal studies and variability in chemotherapy regimens

Longitudinal studies are needed to monitor the long-term consequences of chemotherapy on oral health, therefore guiding the creation of more successful dental treatment plans. These studies may help to solve the problem caused by the variety in chemotherapy regimens as various medications and treatment strategies might result in varied degrees of oral problems. Identifying trends helps one create customized oral care plans for each patient depending on their particular chemotherapy treatment (Najafi et al. 2011).

Multidisciplinary approach and limited access to dental care

Better communication between oncologists and dental specialists depends on dental care being included in cancer treatment teams, therefore guaranteeing that oral health is given priority. This method may be restricted however, by limited access to specialist dental treatment in certain areas—particularly in Low and Middle Income Countries (LMICs). Especially in underprivileged regions, future initiatives should concentrate on improving access to dental care services for chemotherapy patients, maybe by means of mobile dental units or telemedicine consultations (Katakura 2022).

Patient-specific oral care regimens and cost of dental interventions

Given the severity of symptoms like mucoseis or stomatitis, future plans should include creating customized oral care routines fit to the patient's particular requirement (Stephens et al. 2018). The financial load of continuous dental treatments is a difficulty, as insurance or healthcare programs could not always pay for them. This problem calls for the application of affordable treatments, including adequately priced dental care products or financial assistance programs for cancer patients on chemotherapy (Heidari et al. 2020).

Use of technological advances and lack of awareness/training

Using digital technologies and artificial intelligence to monitor oral hygiene has interesting chances to enhance chemotherapy patient treatment. Still, there are difficulties as individuals and healthcare professionals lack knowledge of the need for dental health during chemotherapy. Efforts should be made to include oral health education in oncology training courses and increase patient knowledge of the vital part dental hygiene plays in controlling adverse effects from chemotherapy (Mahesh Batra and Reche 2023).

Although our study focused on patients receiving chemotherapy in 2 Central Asian countries i.e., Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan and gathered a noteworthy quantity of cases, its retrospective design poses certain limitations. Among them are possible inadequate data that can cause selection bias and inconsistent reporting of oral hygiene routines. Furthermore influencing the data's accuracy might be the dependence on self-reported hygiene behaviors. Also, the current retrospective analysis did not stratify results by histopathological subtypes of salivary gland tumors due to limitations in record completeness. Future studies should incorporate this distinction to enhance clinical relevance. Further limitation is the lack of uniform documentation regarding the time interval between oral sanitation and reassessment, which restricts our ability to propose evidence-based follow-up durations. Another limitation is participant variations in chemotherapy regimens, which was not completely under control and would have affected the degree of oral problems. Notwithstanding these limitations, we ensured consistency in the evaluation of oral hygiene by means of standardized instruments and exhaustive data review intended to ensure accuracy.

Conclusion

Particularly in patients undergoing treatment for malignant epithelial tumors of the salivary glands, this retrospective research offers an insightful perspective on the effect of chemotherapy on oral hygiene. Our results show that chemotherapy seriously compromises oral health, which increases the frequency of certain dental illnesses. Radiological tests and clinical investigations combined to evaluate dental condition showed that among these individuals there was a clear need for therapeutic and preventative actions to raise oral sanitation. Men had an average number of carious teeth of 3.16, removed teeth of 16.04, and teeth needing extraction of 6.83; the composite index of carious teeth, pulpitis, extracted teeth, and teeth requiring extraction was 28.7. For women, this score came at 19.7. After cleanliness practices, significant changes were seen: zero teeth needed to be extracted. With averages of 4.81 in males and 9.15 in women, carious teeth dropped, and the component related to pulpitis rose. The Fedorov-Volodkina hygienic index also improved, falling to an average range of 1.8–2.0 ± 0.16. These findings highlight the need for early dental maintenance in order to avoid problems with oral health during chemotherapy. Large-scale, longitudinal studies should be the main emphasis of future studies to investigate the long-term efficacy of oral care therapies and thereby improve methods of maintaining oral health in cancer patients. Such research will help us to better target dental therapy for this patient population and maximize their general therapeutic results.
