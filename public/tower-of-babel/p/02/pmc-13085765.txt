Global resistome architecture of Escherichia coli across human and animal hosts: analysis of 9,696 genome
Authors: Adel Azour, Ghassan M Matar, Melhem Bilen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEscherichia coli is a ubiquitous bacterium that acts both as a commensal and a pathogen, serving as a major reservoir of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs). The global spread of ARGs, particularly those conferring resistance to last-resort antibiotics such as carbapenems and colistin, poses a serious threat to human and animal health. In this study, we analyzed 9,696 publicly available E. coli genomes from human, animal, and unknown-origin metadata, identifying 42,813 acquired ARG occurrences across ten antibiotic classes. Multidrug resistance (ARGs from ≥ 3 classes) was observed in 44.7% of genomes, with aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, multidrug efflux pumps, and β-lactamases being the most abundant. Clinically important genes, including blaTEM−1B, blaCTX−M−15, blaNDM−5, and mcr-1.1, were widely prevalent across hosts. Using binary presence/absence profiles, co-occurrence analyses revealed structured resistance gene modules, with strong associations among aminoglycoside, sulfonamide, and trimethoprim genes consistent with integron-associated assemblages. In contrast, major β-lactamase genes and efflux determinants showed weaker and more heterogeneous co-occurrence patterns, suggesting frequent independent acquisition. Geographic and host analyses highlighted significant heterogeneity and extensive ARG sharing between humans and animals. This study provides a global-scale overview of the E. coli resistome across human and animal hosts, offering a reference framework for genomic surveillance and informing strategies to curb the spread of multidrug-resistant lineages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04791-5.Keywords: Escherichia coli, Antimicrobial resistance, Resistome, Multidrug resistance, Comparative genomics

Introduction

Escherichia coli is one of the most extensively studied bacterial species due to its dual role as a harmless commensal in the gut microbiota of humans and animals and as a versatile pathogen responsible for a wide range of intestinal and extraintestinal infections [1–3]. Beyond its clinical relevance, E. coli serves as a major reservoir and vehicle for the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), which can be horizontally transferred to other pathogens. The global spread of resistant E. coli strains, particularly those carrying extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs), carbapenemases, and plasmid-mediated colistin resistance genes, poses a critical challenge to both human and veterinary medicine [4–6].

Over the past decades, numerous studies have reported ARGs in E. coli from clinical, animal-associated settings, including livestock and companion animals, highlighting extensive circulation of resistance determinants across reservoirs linked to antimicrobial use and selective pressure in both sectors. However, most investigations focused on small datasets or specific contexts, providing fragmented insights into the resistome. Large-scale genomic approaches, leveraging the rapidly expanding availability of bacterial genomes, now enable systematic mapping of ARG diversity and distribution across thousands of genomes worldwide. Such analyses are essential for identifying dominant resistance determinants, tracking emerging threats, and establishing baseline data for global genomic surveillance [6–8].

Here, we map the global distribution and host-sharing patterns of clinically important ARGs in E. coli using 9,696 available genomes with curated human and animal metadata, providing a global-scale overview of the species resistome. By uncovering cross-host resistance networks, this study provides a foundational reference for understanding how resistance genes circulate across reservoirs and informs the development of coordinated human–animal genomic surveillance strategies.

Results

General description of the dataset

A total of 9,696 E. coli genomes were analyzed to characterize the resistome. Across these genomes, a total of 42,813 ARG occurrences were identified. The number of resistance genes occurrences per genome ranged from 1 to 39, with a median of 4 genes occurrences and a mean of 4.41 genes per genome, highlighting the heterogeneous distribution of resistance determinants among E. coli genomes (Table 1).

The genomes originated from diverse sources, broadly categorized as human, animal, and unknown. Environmental-origin genomes were excluded from downstream comparative analyses because they were extremely under-represented in the metadata; genomes with unknown origin were retained as a separate category and were not considered environmental. Approximately 35.3% of genomes (n = 3424) were derived from humans, 20.1% (n = 1948) from various domestic and wild animals, and 44.6% (n = 4324) had unknown origins. This dataset provides a large-scale basis for assessing the distribution and prevalence of antimicrobial resistance genes in E. coli across human and animal-associated genomes.

Distribution of resistance genes by antibiotic classes

A total of 42,813 resistance genes occurrences were identified and classified into major antibiotic classes, including β-lactams, aminoglycosides, macrolides/lincosamides (MLS), tetracyclines, sulfonamides, trimethoprim, colistin, fluoroquinolones, fosfomycin, and other rare categories (Table 2). Aminoglycoside resistance genes were the most abundant (9,784 occurrences, 22.9%), closely followed by multidrug resistance/efflux genes (9,671 genes, 22.6%), β-lactams (6,822 occurrences, 15.9%), sulfonamides (4,421 occurrences, 10.3%), and tetracyclines (3,819 occurrences, 8.9%). Other classes were less represented, including trimethoprim (2,860 occurrences, 6.7%), MLS (1,697 occurrences, 4.0%), phenicols (1,670 occurrences, 3.9%), fluoroquinolones (1,356 occurrences, 3.2%), colistin (456 occurrences, 1.1%), and fosfomycin (258 occurrences, 0.6%). Within these families, specific genes were highly prevalent. The multidrug efflux gene mdf(A) was the most abundant determinant, accounting for 9,671 occurrences across the analyzed genomes. Aminoglycoside resistance was largely driven by aph(6)-Id (2,243 occurrences), aph(3’’)-Ib (2,145 occurrences), aadA5 (897 occurrences), and ant(3’’)-Ia (1,128 occurrences). Among β-lactams, resistance was dominated by the blaTEM family, particularly blaTEM−1B (2,402 occurrences), as well as blaCTX-M variants, including blaCTX−M−15 (760 occurrences) and blaCTX−M−14 (299 occurrences), alongside blaOXA−1 (407 occurrences), blaNDM−5 (246 occurrences), and blaKPC−2 (63 occurrences). Sulfonamide, tetracycline, and trimethoprim resistance were mainly associated with sul2 (2,278 occurrences), tet(A) (2,391 occurrences), and dfrA17 (1,009 occurrences), respectively. Resistance to last-resort agents was less frequent but noteworthy, represented by mcr-1.1 (423 occurrences, colistin), qnrS1 (780 occurrences, fluoroquinolones), and fosA3 (165 occurrences, fosfomycin). Gene family analysis and focus on critical resistance genes

Analysis of gene families revealed that, among β-lactam resistance determinants, the most common were blaTEM, blaCTX−M, blaOXA, and blaSHV. The blaTEM family accounted for 3,583 occurrences, largely dominated by blaTEM−1B gene, while blaCTX−M genes (1,663 occurrences) included key ESBL variants such as blaCTX−M−15, blaCTX−M−14, and blaCTX−M−27. The blaOXA family (685) included blaOXA−1 and blaOXA−48, whereas the blaSHV family comprised 208 occurrences, mainly blaSHV−12. Aminoglycoside resistance was dominated by aph(6)-Id, aph(3’’)-Ib, aadA5, and ant(3’’)-Ia, while MLS resistance was mostly associated with mdf(A), mph(A), and erm(B). Although less frequent, critical resistance genes with major clinical implications were also identified. Carbapenemases such as blaNDM variants (327 occurrences, mainly blaNDM−5) and blaKPC (82 occurrences, dominated by blaKPC−2) confer resistance to last-resort carbapenems. Colistin resistance genes were primarily represented by mcr-1.1 (423 occurrences), along with rarer variants (mcr-1.11, mcr-3.1, mcr-3.5), highlighting an emerging public health concern. Extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) of the blaCTX-M type, detected across multiple host backgrounds, further indicate probable inter-host dissemination. To highlight clinically relevant resistance determinants, the ten most frequent AMR genes were identified according to their occurrence and World Health Organization (WHO) priority categories. According to the WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List, carbapenem-resistant and third-generation cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacterales are classified as critical priority pathogens [9]. Genes of critical clinical importance, including blaNDM−5 (246 occurrences), mcr-1.1 (423 occurrences), blaKPC−2 (63 occurrences), blaOXA−48 (112 occurrences), and blaCTX−M−15 (760 occurrences) as well as highly important or important genes, such as blaCTX−M−14 (299 occurrences), blaCTX−M−55 (292 occurrences), blaTEM−1B (2,402 occurrences), tet(A) (2,392 occurrences), sul2 (2,278 occurrences), and mph(A) (1,177 occurrences), were also frequently observed. Multidrug resistance and co-occurrence

To evaluate multidrug resistance (MDR), we examined the number of antibiotic classes represented in each genome. Genomes carrying genes from three or more different antibiotic classes were considered MDR. Approximately 44.7% of genomes (n = 4,336) met this criterion, highlighting the high prevalence of multidrug-resistant E. coli strains (Table 4).

A genome-centric analysis based on binary presence/absence profiles was performed to characterize resistance gene content across the 30 multidrug-resistant E. coli genomes with the highest resistance gene burden (Fig. 2). By including all resistance genes detected in at least one of these genomes, the heatmap provides a direct genome-level representation in which the number of detected genes per genome exactly reflects its total resistance gene burden. Across this subset, resistance gene burden ranged from 13 to 28 genes per genome, highlighting pronounced inter-genome heterogeneity in resistome composition. The most gene-rich profile corresponded to genome 562.95846, which harbored 28 distinct resistance genes, followed by multiple genomes carrying 21–26 genes, whereas even the least gene-rich genomes within this group retained more than a dozen resistance determinants.

Despite this marked variability, a shared core resistome was evident among highly resistant genomes. Most profiles combined multiple β-lactamase genes, including blaTEM and blaCTX−M variants, with aminoglycoside resistance determinants such as aph(3′), aph(6), and aac genes. Tetracycline resistance genes (e.g., tet(A), tet(B), tet(M)) and sulfonamide resistance genes (sul1, sul2, sul3) were also frequently detected, often co-occurring with trimethoprim resistance genes of the dfrA family. In addition to this conserved core, a broad set of low-frequency resistance genes was sporadically distributed across genomes, contributing to the overall resistance gene burden and generating a pronounced long-tail distribution of rare resistance determinants.

Jaccard similarity analysis reveals distinct resistance gene modules

Jaccard similarity analysis based on binary presence/absence profiles of the 30 most prevalent antimicrobial resistance genes across 9,696 E. coli genomes revealed non-random and structured co-occurrence patterns (Fig. 3). The Jaccard index, ranging from 0 (no co-occurrence) to 1 (perfect co-occurrence), highlighted several well-defined gene modules.

Heatmap showing pairwise Jaccard similarity indices based on binary presence/absence profiles of the 30 most prevalent antimicrobial resistance genes across 9,696 E. coli genomes. Warmer colors indicate higher Jaccard values, reflecting stronger gene co-occurrence, whereas cooler colors indicate weak or absent associations. Hierarchical clustering reveals non-random and structured co-occurrence patterns, delineating several discrete gene modules. Prominent clusters include a module dominated by aminoglycoside resistance genes, a second module comprising trimethoprim–sulfonamide resistance determinants, and additional genes showing weaker and more diffuse associations, including β-lactamase and efflux-related genes

Strong co-occurrence was observed among aminoglycoside resistance genes, particularly between aph(6)-Id and aph(3ʺ)-Ib (J = 0.94), and between sul2 and aph(6)-Id (J = 0.74). Trimethoprim resistance determinants also clustered within this module, with dfrA17 showing high similarity with aadA5 (J = 0.85) and moderate similarity with sul1 (J = 0.42).

A second module was characterized by associations between sulfonamide and macrolide resistance genes, including sul1 and mph(A) (J = 0.48). Tetracycline resistance genes (tet(A) and tet(B)) displayed moderate co-occurrence with aminoglycoside-associated genes (J = 0.2–0.3), but showed limited similarity with β-lactamase genes.

In contrast, clinically important β-lactamase genes, including blaCTX−M−15, blaOXA−1, and blaTEM−1B, exhibited generally low Jaccard similarity values (J < 0.3), indicating limited systematic co-occurrence with other resistance determinants. Efflux-associated genes such as mdf(A) also showed low similarity with most genes (J ≤ 0.23), consistent with their broad and independent distribution across genomes.

Overall, the Jaccard heatmap delineates several discrete co-occurrence modules connected by weak cross-links, reflecting structured but heterogeneous resistance gene combinations across the E. coli population.

Geographic distribution of antimicrobial resistance genes

The geographic distribution of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes showed marked heterogeneity across the global dataset (Fig. 4). After normalization by the number of genomes analyzed per country, substantial variation in ARG burden was observed. A total of 928 genomes lacked country information but collectively carried 3,399 ARG occurrences, including aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (e.g., ant(3")-Ia, aph(3")-Ib, aph(6)-Id), β-lactamases (blaTEM−1B, blaTEM−116, blaCTX−M−15), the multidrug efflux pump mdf(A), and additional determinants conferring resistance to sulfonamides, tetracyclines, quinolones, and colistin. Because several countries were represented by a limited number of genomes, country-level estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Geographic distribution of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes in E. coli genomes. The map shows the normalized abundance of major AMR genes per country. Aminoglycoside (aph(3’’)-Ib, aph(6)-Id), β-lactam (blaTEM−1B, blaCTX−M−15), and sulfonamide (sul1, sul2) genes were the most widespread, while mcr-1.1 and qnrS1 appeared sporadically, highlighting global yet uneven ARG distribution

To reduce potential bias associated with uneven sampling, ARG distributions were additionally summarized at the regional level by grouping countries into Africa, Europe, Asia, the Americas, the Middle East, and Oceania (Table 5). At the regional scale, Asia accounted for the largest number of detected ARG occurrences (15,181), driven primarily by China (9,703 occurrences), Japan (1,466 occurrences), India (681 occurrences), Thailand (662 occurrences), and Viet Nam (505 occurrences). Europe represented the second largest contribution (12,286 occurrences), with high ARG counts in the United Kingdom (4,639 occurrences), Norway (2,521 occurrences), Germany (959 occurrences), France (714 occurrences), Switzerland (578 occurrences), and Spain (332 occurrences). In the Americas, substantial ARG burdens were detected (8,349 occurrences), dominated by the United States (5,409 occurrences) and Canada (1,555 occurrences), with additional contributions from Brazil (397 occurrences), Ecuador (253 occurrences), Cuba (217 occurrences), and Mexico (196 occurrences). Africa collectively contributed 2,139 ARG occurrences, including Egypt (470 occurrences), Tanzania (289 occurrences), South Africa (274 occurrences), Nigeria (241 occurrences), and Ghana (226 occurrences). The Middle East contributed 778 ARG occurrences, led by Lebanon (369 occurrences) and Jordan (211 occurrences), while Oceania included Australia (527 occurrences) and New Zealand (153 occurrences) for a total of 680 ARG occurrences. Country-specific ARG counts and normalized values are provided in Supplementary material 1.

The distribution of multidrug-resistant (MDR) E. coli genomes also showed pronounced geographical disparities (Supplementary material 2; Supplementary material 3). Several countries, including Lebanon, Jordan, Afghanistan, Myanmar, Malawi, Senegal, Taiwan, and Armenia, exhibited normalized MDR values of 1, indicating that all analyzed genomes from these countries were MDR. In contrast, countries with larger datasets, such as China (normalized MDR = 0.72), the United States (0.38), and the United Kingdom (0.46), showed lower MDR proportions, reflecting the coexistence of MDR and non-MDR lineages.

Comparative distribution of resistance genes across human, animal genomes

The distribution of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes varied markedly across hosts. In human genomes, a total of 17,252 resistance gene occurrences were identified, with β-lactamase genes (notably blaTEM-1B and blaCTX-M-15) and aminoglycoside resistance genes being the most frequent.

Across animal-derived genomes, 9,982 antimicrobial resistance gene occurrences were detected, revealing pronounced differences in resistome composition among host species. Overall, food-producing animals such as chickens, pigs, and cattle harbored the highest ARG burdens, whereas companion animals and wildlife contributed fewer occurrences. Across animal hosts, multidrug efflux genes (mdf(A)), tetracycline resistance genes (tet), sulfonamide resistance genes (sul), and β-lactamases (blaTEM, blaCTX-M) were consistently dominant, highlighting a shared core resistome.

Discussion

This study represents one of the largest genomic analyses of E. coli resistomes, encompassing 9,696 genomes from human and animal sources. We identified 42,813 resistance gene occurrences, with a median of four genes per genome, highlighting the heterogeneous nature of E. coli resistomes. This comprehensive dataset provides unprecedented insight into global ARG distribution, surpassing previous regional or species-limited studies and establishes a baseline for understanding core and accessory resistomes to guide surveillance priorities [10–12].

Multidrug efflux and aminoglycoside resistance genes were the most abundant, reflecting sustained selective pressure in both clinical and veterinary settings. β-lactamases, including blaTEM−1B (2,402 occurrences) and blaCTX−M−15 (760 occurrences), were widely disseminated, consistent with the ongoing global spread of extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) [13]. The detection of last-resort resistance determinants such as colistin (mcr-1.1, 423 occurrences) and carbapenemases (blaNDM−5, 246 occurrences; blaKPC−2, 63 occurrences) underscores the relevance of animal-associated reservoirs and the human–animal interface as potential contributors to clinically significant resistance [14, 15].

Nearly half of all genomes (44.7%, n = 4,336) carried genes conferring resistance to three or more antibiotic classes, indicating a pervasive multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotype within the E. coli population. Co-occurrence analysis based on binary presence/absence profiles revealed structured and recurrent associations among aminoglycoside, sulfonamide, and trimethoprim resistance genes, forming well-defined modules of frequent co-presence. The strong Jaccard similarity observed between genes such as aph(6)-Id, aph(3ʺ)-Ib, sul2, and dfrA variants is consistent with their frequent joint occurrence within shared genetic contexts, as commonly reported for integron-associated resistance gene arrays [16, 17]. In contrast, clinically important β-lactamase genes, including blaCTX−M−15, blaTEM−1B, and blaOXA−1, exhibited generally weak co-occurrence with other resistance determinants (Jaccard index < 0.3), indicating heterogeneous genomic backgrounds and largely independent dissemination routes, likely reflecting repeated horizontal acquisition events rather than stable linkage within resistance gene clusters. Efflux-associated genes, particularly mdf(A), also showed low Jaccard similarity with most resistance genes despite their high prevalence, supporting a model of broad and independent distribution across genomes rather than consistent co-occurrence with specific resistance modules [18–20].

The genome-centric binary heatmap reveals that highly multidrug-resistant E. coli genomes exhibit an extreme gene-accumulation phenotype, characterized by the co-occurrence of resistance determinants targeting multiple antibiotic classes. Rather than reflecting the dominance of a single resistance mechanism, these profiles are shaped by the non-random association of resistance genes, consistent with stepwise acquisition driven by horizontal gene transfer [21, 22].

The structured co-occurrence of resistance determinants is compatible with the involvement of mobile genetic elements, including integrons, transposons, and plasmids, which are known to facilitate the accumulation, co-selection, and long-term stabilization of multiple resistance genes within bacterial genomes [17, 23–25]. Such redundancy likely provides functional resilience under antibiotic exposure, contributing to the remarkable evolutionary adaptability of E. coli across heterogeneous antimicrobial environments [26].

By relying on binary presence/absence profiles and including all resistance genes detected across highly resistant genomes, this analysis provides a conservative and assembly-robust representation of resistome architecture. This approach minimizes potential inflation associated with copy number variation or fragmented assemblies, while emphasizing that multidrug resistance in E. coli arises from both gene accumulation and structured co-occurrence of resistance determinants rather than from isolated resistance mechanisms.

In addition, our analysis revealed marked heterogeneity in the prevalence and diversity of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) across countries. China contributed the largest dataset, with 1,161 genomes and 9,703 detected ARG occurrences, exhibiting a highly diverse resistome dominated by aminoglycoside resistance genes, β-lactamases, tetracycline resistance genes, plasmid-mediated colistin resistance, and multiple efflux pumps. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing high ARG abundance and diversity in Chinese E. coli isolates, largely driven by intensive antibiotic use in human medicine and livestock production [27, 28]. Distinct regional profiles were also observed. High normalized ARG densities were detected in Africa (Botswana, Malawi, Senegal), the Middle East (Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon), Asia (Taiwan, Armenia, Myanmar, Philippines, Vietnam), and Latin America (Cuba, Ecuador), reflecting concentrated resistomes enriched in combinations of β-lactamases, aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, tetracycline and sulfonamide resistance genes, and efflux pumps [29–35]. Other countries, including Georgia, Niger, Afghanistan, Cambodia, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Morocco, Switzerland, Mongolia, Singapore, Turkey, Pakistan, Thailand, Spain, and Libya, also displayed substantial normalized ARG burdens, highlighting pronounced regional differences in resistome composition [36–38]. These patterns underscore that ARG abundance and diversity are unevenly distributed globally, shaped by local antibiotic usage, agricultural practices, and environmental exposures. These findings highlight the need for context-specific monitoring strategies to mitigate the spread of antimicrobial resistance.

These country- and region-level patterns should be interpreted with caution, as genome availability in public repositories is highly uneven and often shaped by targeted sequencing of clinically severe or multidrug-resistant infections. Consequently, resistome richness observed for some countries may partly reflect preferential sampling of MDR lineages rather than true population-wide prevalence.

Our analysis also reveals marked geographical disparities in the distribution of multidrug-resistant (MDR) E. coli. Countries such as Lebanon, Jordan, and Afghanistan exhibited normalized MDR values of 1, indicating that all analyzed genomes were multidrug-resistant. In contrast, countries with large datasets such as China, the United States, and the United Kingdom showed lower normalized MDR proportions, suggesting coexistence of MDR and non-MDR lineages. Regions with very low normalized MDR values likely represent either limited MDR prevalence or insufficient genomic surveillance. These differences may reflect variations in antibiotic usage policies, healthcare infrastructure, and surveillance capacity between regions. Strengthening genomic surveillance in underrepresented areas remains essential to better capture the true global burden of MDR E. coli and guide targeted interventions [39, 40].

Comparative analysis of antimicrobial resistance genes across human and animal-associated E. coli genomes revealed extensive overlap in resistome composition, highlighting strong genetic connectivity between these two host reservoirs. Clinically important resistance gene families, including blaTEM, blaCTX−M, aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, tetracycline resistance genes (tet variants), and sulfonamide resistance genes (sul variants), were widely shared across hosts, supporting previous evidence of bidirectional transmission at the human–animal interface [41–43].

In human-associated genomes, β-lactamase genes such as blaTEM−1B and blaCTX−M−15, together with aminoglycoside resistance genes (aac(3)-IId) predominated, reflecting clinical antimicrobial pressure [44–46]. In contrast, animal-associated genomes, particularly poultry, cattle, and pigs, tetracycline resistance genes (tet(A), tet(B)), sulfonamide resistance genes (sul1, sul2), and multidrug efflux pumps like mdf(A) were widely distributed, suggesting veterinary antimicrobial-driven selection [44, 47, 48]. Companion animals, such as dogs, showed similar patterns but at lower abundance, indicating potential cross-transmission between humans and domestic animals [49].

Consistent with these observations, Venn diagram analysis identified 111 antimicrobial resistance genes shared between human and animal genomes, including extended-spectrum β-lactamases, carbapenemases, and plasmid-mediated colistin resistance determinants. In addition, 91 ARGs were uniquely detected in human-associated genomes, while 23 ARGs were specific to animal-associated genomes, reflecting host-associated selective pressures linked to antimicrobial use in clinical and veterinary settings. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of integrated human and veterinary genomic surveillance strategies to mitigate the dissemination of multidrug-resistant E. coli [43, 50, 51].

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the analysis focused on acquired antimicrobial resistance genes and did not include chromosomal point mutations, which play an important role in resistance to certain antibiotic classes such as fluoroquinolones and colistin. Accordingly, the present work focuses on the acquired resistome and does not aim to comprehensively capture chromosomal resistance mechanisms. Integrating point mutation–based resistance, particularly for fluoroquinolones and colistin, represents an important next step that will be explored in future large-scale analyses. Second, the dataset was based on publicly available genomes, which may be affected by sampling biases toward multidrug-resistant or clinically relevant isolates. In addition, some countries were represented by a limited number of genomes, and country-level estimates for these regions should therefore be interpreted with caution. Despite these limitations, the large sample size and standardized analytical framework provide a robust global overview of the distribution and sharing of antimicrobial resistance genes in E. coli.

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive global overview of the E. coli resistome across human and animal hosts, revealing extensive multidrug resistance, conserved co-occurrence modules, and widespread sharing of clinically important ARGs. These findings highlight the need for strengthened, integrated human and veterinary genomic surveillance to inform targeted strategies aimed at mitigating the dissemination of multidrug-resistant E. coli worldwide.

Materials and methods

A total of 12,053 E. coli genomes were initially retrieved from the Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatics Resource Center (BV-BRC) database (https://www.bv-brc.org/, accessed on 5 August 2025), along with associated metadata including host origin (human, animal, or unknown), sample type, and geographic information when available. To ensure high-quality assemblies, only genomes with a total length between 4.5 and 5.5 Mb, a GC content ranging between 50% and 51.5%, and ≤ 100 contigs were retained. Duplicate entries were removed based on identical BV-BRC genome identifiers, and incomplete or low-quality assemblies were excluded. Because environmental-origin genomes were extremely under-represented in the BV-BRC metadata, environmental-origin genomes were excluded from all downstream comparative analyses. The final dataset therefore comprised 9,696 genomes from human-associated, animal-associated, and unknown-origin categories; genomes with unknown origin were retained as a separate category and were not considered environmental.

Antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) were identified using the ResFinder database (version 4.7.2) with a minimum identity threshold of 90%52 and coverage ≥ 80% [52]. ResFinder detects acquired resistance genes (typically plasmid- or mobile genetic element–associated determinants) based on sequence similarity and does not call chromosomal point mutations. Accordingly, this study provides a global overview of the acquired resistome of E. coli. Chromosomal resistance mechanisms mediated by point mutations (e.g., gyrA/parC for fluoroquinolones and pmrAB/mgrB for colistin) were intentionally excluded and will be addressed in dedicated follow-up analyses.

Genomes carrying resistance genes in three or more antibiotic classes were defined as multidrug-resistant (MDR). To explore the global distribution of MDR E. coli, the normalized MDR proportion was calculated for each country as the number of MDR genomes divided by the total number of E. coli genomes analyzed from that country. This normalization allowed direct comparison of MDR prevalence across countries, regardless of dataset size or sequencing intensity.

In parallel, the normalized ARG count was computed to estimate the relative ARG burden per country. This metric was calculated by dividing the total number of detected ARGs by the number of genomes from each country, providing an average number of ARGs per genome. This approach enabled standardized comparisons of resistance gene accumulation between countries with variable sampling depth and genomic coverage.

Co-occurrence and accumulation of ARGs were analyzed using a binary presence/absence matrix, and distributions per genome were summarized using mean, median, and range. Visualizations, including histograms and heatmaps, were generated in RStudio (RStudio 2023.12.0 + 369 “Ocean Storm”) and Python (version 3.12.3) to illustrate patterns of resistance and MDR profiles (Fig. 6).
