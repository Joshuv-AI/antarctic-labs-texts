The association between social support and depressive symptoms in rural older adults with cardiometabolic multimorbidity: a network analysis
Authors: Zhiwei Wang, Shengjia Xu, Xueqing Song, Lan Gao, Siya Meng, Xiaorong Luan, Wenzhe Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiometabolic multimorbidity and late-life depression are increasingly prevalent among rural older adults in China, imposing a compounded health burden. Although social support is recognized as a protective factor, the specific linkages between its dimensions and individual depressive symptoms in this population remain unclear. Network analysis provides a novel approach to explore these nuanced relationships. This study employed network analysis to examine the detailed associations between multidimensional social support and specific depressive symptoms in rural older adults with cardiometabolic multimorbidity.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 1,486 rural adults aged ≥ 60 years with cardiometabolic multimorbidity in Shandong Province, China. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9, and social support was measured with the Social Support Rating Scale. A regularized partial correlation network was constructed to visualize symptom-level interactions. Centrality and bridge centrality indices were calculated to identify core and bridging nodes.ResultsThe strongest connections were within depressive symptoms, notably between anhedonia and sad mood (edge weight = 0.55), and anhedonia and fatigue (0.33). Subjective and objective support were closely linked (0.45). The strongest (negative) bridge connection emerged between subjective support and sad mood (-0.05). Sad mood, fatigue, and anhedonia were the most central symptoms, while subjective support and sad mood were key bridge nodes. The network showed high stability.ConclusionInterventions targeting central depressive symptoms—sad mood, anhedonia, and fatigue—may effectively alleviate depression in this population. Given the modest strength of bridge connections within the network, interventions focusing on bridge nodes such as subjective support should be implemented in conjunction with those addressing core depressive symptoms, rather than in isolation. These findings underscore the necessity for integrated psychosocial and medical care within rural health services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26982-5.Keywords: Cardiometabolic multimorbidity, Depression, Social support, Network analysis, Rural older adult

Introduction

The 21st century has seen significant demographic and epidemiological changes. Populations are aging rapidly, leading to a shift in the disease burden from infectious diseases to chronic non-communicable conditions (NCDs) [1]. Among these, cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs)—such as hypertension, coronary heart disease, stroke, diabetes, and dyslipidemia—are the leading causes of global mortality and disability [2]. A particularly challenging aspect is the high prevalence of cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), defined as the co-occurrence of two or more of these conditions in an individual [3]. Multimorbidity is more than the sum of its parts; it leads to a synergistic decline in health. This deterioration exponentially increases the risks of functional decline, polypharmacy, hospitalization, and mortality, thereby placing an immense strain on healthcare systems worldwide [4–7].

Simultaneously, late-life depression is a critical public health issue that frequently coexists with chronic physical diseases like CMM [8]. This relationship is bidirectional and pernicious. CMM increases depression risk through pathways such as chronic inflammation, persistent pain, functional limitations, and the psychological distress of managing a complex illness [9–11]. Conversely, depression can adversely affect self-management behaviors (e.g., medication adherence), amplify somatic symptoms, and dysregulate physiological systems, thereby worsening CMD progression and outcomes [12–14]. This vicious cycle creates a syndemic that significantly diminishes quality of life and accelerates health deterioration [15].

This syndemic is particularly acute among older adults in rural areas of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), such as China [16]. China’s rapid urbanization has created a stark urban-rural health divide. Rural older adults often face a “triple jeopardy”: (1) the universal challenges of aging; (2) a heightened burden of CMM due to changing lifestyles; and (3) significant socioeconomic disadvantages and scarce healthcare resources [17, 18]. Compared to their urban counterparts, rural elders often have lower incomes, less education, limited access to specialized care, and greater social isolation due to the out-migration of younger generations [16, 19]. These factors collectively create a fertile ground for the development and persistence of depression.

In this context, social support is theorized as a crucial buffer against adverse health outcomes [20]. The Stress-Buffering Model posits that social support can protect individuals from the deleterious effects of stressful events by providing emotional comfort, tangible aid, and informational resources [20]. Social support is a multidimensional construct, generally encompassing: subjective support (an individual’s perception of being loved, cared for, and valued), objective support (the actual receipt of material aid or social connectivity), and support utilization (the tendency to actively seek out and use available support) [21]. While numerous studies have established a negative correlation between overall social support and depression, this aggregate-level approach obscures critical nuances [22, 23]. A fundamental question remains unresolved: which specific dimensions of social support are most powerfully linked to which specific depressive symptoms in this vulnerable population. Understanding these precise pathways is essential for moving beyond generic associations to develop targeted interventions.

Traditional psychological research, relying on latent variable models and composite scores, struggles to elucidate the fine-grained, symptom-level interactions between social support and depression [24]. This approach obscures whether specific support dimensions have preferential relationships with specific symptoms, thereby limiting insights for precise intervention [25, 26]. Network analysis offers a transformative alternative by conceptualizing psychopathology as systems of directly interacting symptoms, and while it has been applied to depression in various populations, significant gaps remain [27–33]. No prior study has used this method to map the relationships between multidimensional social support and specific depressive symptoms in a sample exclusively composed of rural older adults with CMM. The unique socioecological context of rural China likely shapes the network structure in ways that cannot be inferred from studies in Western or urban settings [16–19].

To address these gaps, this study employs a network analysis in a large sample of rural older adults with CMM in Shandong Province, China. Our objectives are: (i) to estimate the network structure between social support and depressive symptoms; (ii) to identify the most central depressive symptoms; (iii) to identify bridge nodes connecting support to depression; (iv) to assess node predictability; and (v) to evaluate the network’s stability and invariance across subgroups. This detailed map aims to inform targeted strategies to alleviate the dual burden of chronic physical and mental illness in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design, participants, and sample size

This cross-sectional study utilized baseline data from a large-scale survey investigating health management adherence among rural older adults with CMM in Shandong Province, China. The survey was conducted in August 2023 using a multi-stage stratified cluster sampling method. First, three prefecture-level cities (Weifang, Tai’an, and Dezhou) were selected based on socioeconomic development level, geographical distribution, and prevalence of CMM among older adults. From these cities, three county-level cities were randomly selected: Gaomi, Xintai, and Laoling. Subsequently, 11 townships/towns were randomly selected from these counties, followed by the random selection of 6 villages from each township, resulting in 66 villages total. From village clinic health records, potential participants were identified and randomly selected. Eligible participants were aged ≥ 60 years, diagnosed with at least two CMDs (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease), permanent rural residents, cognitively intact, and provided informed consent. Exclusion criteria included severe hearing or speech impairment, history of mental or neurological disorders, severe complications, or refusal to participate.

The sample size was calculated to ensure adequate statistical power for the overarching cross-sectional survey from which these data were derived (see formula). Based on an expected CMM prevalence (ρ) of 33.7% among older adults (derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) national database), a significance level (α) of 0.05, a relative error (r) of 0.1, and a design effect (deff) of 1, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 756 participants [10]. Accounting for potential non-response and invalid questionnaires, the target sample size was expanded to 1,000. A total of 1,505 individuals were initially surveyed, and 1,486 valid responses were retained after data cleaning, resulting in a response rate of 98.74%. It is important to highlight that although the sample size calculation was initially based on prevalence estimation, the final sample size of 1,486 substantially surpasses the recommended threshold for achieving stable and accurate estimates in network analysis [34]. Typically, the number of observations required should exceed the total number of parameters [34]. In this study, there were 78 parameters, comprising 12 threshold parameters and 66 association parameters (calculated as 12 × (12 − 1)/2) [34]. According to the rule of thumb, which suggests a minimum of 5 observations per parameter, a sample size of at least 390 would be necessary. Therefore, the considerably larger sample size employed in this study ensures the robustness of our primary analytical approach. Furthermore, this large sample size provides high precision for our bootstrap-based accuracy and stability assessments, as evidenced by the high correlation stability coefficient (CS-C) obtained. No missing data were present in the variables included in the network analysis, as all participants provided complete responses to questionnaire measures.

Measures

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were collected, including age, gender, education level, marital status, residential arrangements, annual income, smoking status, drinking habits, health insurance status, body mass index (BMI), and number of CMDs.

Depressive Symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a 9-item self-report measure that evaluates the frequency of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks [35]. The items assess the following domains: anhedonia, depressed or sad mood, sleep difficulties, fatigue, appetite changes, feelings of worthlessness, concentration difficulties, psychomotor agitation or retardation, and thoughts of death. Each item is scored from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), with total scores ranging from 0 to 27. In this study, the scale showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.849).

Social Support was measured using the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), a 10-item instrument that assesses three dimensions: subjective support (4 items), objective support (3 items), and support utilization (3 items) [36]. Items are scored on a Likert-type scale with varying response options, and total scores range from 12 to 66, with higher scores indicating greater social support. The SSRS has been widely used in Chinese populations and showed acceptable reliability in this sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.709) [37, 38]. For transparency, the specific items comprising each dimension are detailed in the Supplementary Material.

Data collection and ethical considerations

Trained research assistants conducted face-to-face interviews using the standardized scales described in section "Measures": the PHQ-9 and the SSRS. The research team received comprehensive training on administration procedures, ethical considerations, and quality control measures. Interviews were conducted in participants’ homes or community health centers in a private setting. All participants provided written informed consent before data collection. All data were anonymized to ensure the full protection of participants’ privacy. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health, Shandong University (Approval No: LL20230603).

Statistical analysis

Prior to network analysis, we performed descriptive and comparative analyses. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables, which were non-normally distributed, were presented as medians with the first and third quartiles (Q1, Q3). Group differences were examined using non-parametric tests: the Mann-Whitney U test for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal-Wallis H test for comparisons across more than two groups.

Network estimation

We estimated a regularized partial correlation network using the Gaussian Graphical Model. The Graphical Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (GLASSO) method was applied for regularization [34, 39]. This technique uses a penalty to reduce small, potentially spurious correlations to zero, resulting in a sparse and more interpretable network. The extent of this regularization was tuned using the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) to optimally balance model fit with network sparsity. All network estimations were conducted using the qgraph package in R.

Network visualization

The estimated network was visualized with nodes representing each variable from the PHQ-9 and SSRS scales. Edges (lines) between nodes represent the unique statistical relationships between them, quantified as partial correlation coefficients [28]. These connections are shown after controlling for the influence of all other variables in the network [34]. In the plot, thicker edges indicate stronger associations. Purple edges designate positive correlations, while red edges indicate negative correlations.

Centrality analysis

Consistent with network theory, centrality indices identify the most influential nodes, and interventions targeting these nodes may have cascading effects on the entire system [29]. We computed three indices to identify the most influential nodes in the network: strength, closeness, and betweenness [40] Strength is the sum of the absolute weights of all edges connected to a node. Closeness is the inverse of the sum of the shortest path distances from a node to all others. Betweenness counts how often a node lies on the shortest path between two other nodes. Following current recommendations in the psychological network literature, we primarily interpreted strength centrality as the most reliable indicator of a node’s influence [41].

Bridge centrality

To identify nodes that connect the social support and depressive symptoms communities, we computed bridge strength centrality using the networktools package [30, 42] Nodes with high bridge strength are considered potential targets for interventions aimed at modulating the interaction between the two constructs [30].

Predictability

We calculated the predictability for each node, which represents the proportion of its variance that is explained by its neighboring nodes in the network [43]. This was computed using the mgm package. Higher predictability suggests a node is more controllable through the network [44].

Accuracy and stability

We assessed the accuracy of edge weights using a non-parametric bootstrap approach with 1,000 samples, from which we derived 95% confidence intervals. Network stability was evaluated using a case-dropping bootstrap procedure to compute the CS-C for strength centrality. A CS-C value above 0.50 is generally considered to indicate adequate stability [34]. Additionally, we performed bootstrap-based difference testing (1000 samples) to examine the significance of disparities in edge weights and node strength across the network.

Network comparison

We conducted network comparison tests (NCT) to examine whether network characteristics differed across subgroups based on key demographic and clinical variables, using the NetworkComparisonTest package [45]. These tests evaluated differences in both the overall network structure and global strength (the sum of all absolute edge weights in the network).

Sensitivity analysis

Considering that both PHQ-9 and SSRS items represent ordered categorical data with non-normal distributions, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of our primary network model. To this end, we employed the cor_auto function from the R package qgraph to automatically compute an appropriate correlation matrix. This function detects the measurement level of each variable and, for ordered categorical items (including those with more than seven categories), estimates polychoric correlation coefficients via the lavaan package. The resulting polychoric correlation matrix was then used as input for the GLASSO algorithm to estimate an alternative regularized partial correlation network. We compared the structure, centrality indices, and bridge centrality of this sensitivity network with those obtained from our primary analysis. This approach is a well-established and recommended practice for estimating psychopathological networks from ordered psychological data, ensuring that our findings are not unduly influenced by the statistical assumptions regarding data distribution [46].

All analyses were performed using R version 4.0.2 and SPSS Statistics version 25.0. A two-sided p < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 1,486 rural older adults with CMM were included in this cross-sectional study. The cohort was predominantly female (65.3%; n = 970), with the majority of participants aged 70–79 years (53.0%; n = 788), followed by those aged 60–69 (38.6%; n = 574), and 80 years or older (8.3%; n = 124). Socioeconomic profiling revealed that 46.5% (n = 691) had no formal education, 33.0% (n = 491) attained primary education, and 20.5% (n = 304) completed junior high school or higher; 74.2% (n = 1102) were married. In terms of residential arrangements, 65.7% (n = 976) cohabited only with their spouse, 20.3% (n = 302) lived alone, and 14.0% (n = 208) resided with both their spouse and children. Economically, 59.2% (n = 880) reported an annual income below ¥5,000, while 24.8% (n = 368) and 16.0% (n = 238) reported incomes of ¥5,000-¥10,000 and above ¥10,000, respectively. Health insurance coverage was nearly universal, with 96.2% (n = 1429) enrolled in urban-rural resident basic medical insurance, 2.5% (n = 37) covered by urban employee basic medical insurance, and only 1.3% (n = 20) uninsured. Lifestyle factors included smoking status: 76.3% (n = 1134) never smoked, 11.2% (n = 166) were current smokers, and 11.4% (n = 169) were former smokers; drinking habits: 74.8% (n = 1111) never consumed alcohol, 12.4% (n = 185) were current drinkers, and 9.4% (n = 140) were former drinkers. BMI categories comprised overweight (43.1%; n = 641), normal weight (33.1%; n = 492), obesity (21.5%; n = 320), and underweight (2.2%; n = 33). Network structure estimation

The network structure depicting relationships between social support and depressive symptoms is presented in Fig. 1. The model comprised 12 nodes in total, with 9 derived from the PHQ-9 (representing depressive symptoms) and 3 from the SSRS (reflecting social support dimensions). Among the 66 possible edges, 40 were non-zero. Of these, 33 edges indicated positive partial correlations, while 7 exhibited negative associations. All negative edges were located exclusively between the social support and depressive symptom communities.

Within the depressive symptom community, the strongest connections were identified between Anhedonia and Sad Mood (edge weight = 0.55), followed by Anhedonia and Fatigue (edge weight = 0.33). The strongest cross-community interaction was a negative edge linking Subjective Support and Sad Mood (edge weight = -0.05). Centrality and bridge centrality

As illustrated in Fig. 2, strength centrality analysis identified Sad Mood, Fatigue, and Anhedonia as the most influential nodes within the network, indicating their central roles in the overall structure. Furthermore, bridge centrality analysis (Fig. 3) identified Subjective Support and Sad Mood as the nodes with the highest bridge strength indices. These bridge nodes represent the statistically identifiable pathways through which social support and depressive symptoms interact. Although their edge weights were modest, they highlight potential—though not necessarily strong—levers for clinical attention within a broader intervention framework.

Predictability of nodes

The average predictability of the nodes was 39.55%, meaning that nearly 40% of the variance in each node was explained by its neighboring nodes. Anhedonia and Sad Mood had the highest predictability (67.1% and 65.9%, respectively), while Support Utilization had the lowest (9.1%) (Table 1). The predictability of each node is visually represented by the circular outlines surrounding respective nodes in Fig. 1.

Network accuracy and stability

The case-dropping bootstrap procedure yielded a CS-C of 0.75 for strength centrality, indicating that the network remained stable even when up to 75% of the sample was omitted (Fig. 4). Bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals for edge weights were narrow, supporting the reliability of the network estimates (Supplementary Fig. 1). Difference tests for edge weights and node strengths showed no significant deviations across bootstrap samples (Supplementary Figs. 2–3).

Stability of node strength centrality in the social support and depressive symptoms network assessed through case-dropping bootstrap procedure

Subgroup network comparisons

Given that demographic and clinical variables were associated with both social support and depressive symptoms (as shown in Supplementary Table 1), we conducted NCT to examine whether they significantly influenced the network structure. Subgroup analyses were performed across categories including CMDs, age, income, and marital status. Results indicated no significant differences in network structure or global strength for most demographic and clinical comparisons (all p > 0.05), with the exception of a borderline significant difference in global strength between current drinkers and never-drinkers (S = 0.90, p = 0.045; see Supplementary Table 3). The lack of significant differences in sufficiently powered comparisons may indicate true invariance across these subgroups. However, the inability to perform tests for several comparisons due to small subgroup sizes precludes any conclusion about invariance for those specific demographics. For illustrative purposes, the network structures for the CMDs subgroups are visually presented in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Robustness check for ordered categorical data

The sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the primary network architecture. The core symptom connections (particularly the strong association between affective and motivational symptoms), the internal structure among social support dimensions, and the bridging role of subjective support remained consistent across different methodological approaches. Observed differences were confined to variations in the magnitude of specific edge weights rather than any fundamental alteration in the network’s topology. Detailed results of the sensitivity analysis are presented in the Supplementary Material.

Discussion

This study is the first to employ network analysis to elucidate the complex interrelationships between specific dimensions of social support and individual depressive symptoms in a large sample of rural older adults with CMM in China. Our analysis yielded several key findings. First, strong connections within the depressive symptom community formed the core skeleton of the network. This was particularly true for the edges between Anhedonia and Sad Mood, and between Anhedonia and Fatigue. Second, within the social support community, Subjective Support and Objective Support were closely linked. Third, the strongest negative bridge connecting the two communities was between Subjective Support and Sad Mood. Furthermore, Sad Mood, Fatigue, and Anhedonia emerged as the most central symptoms in the network. Subjective Support and Sad Mood were identified as critical bridge nodes. Finally, the network demonstrated high overall stability and invariance across various demographic and clinical subgroups. Collectively, these findings provide a fine-grained map of the mechanisms sustaining psychosocial distress in this vulnerable population. They also pinpoint precise targets for clinical intervention.

Integrated interpretation of network structure, centrality, and predictability

The network structure, centrality, and bridge centrality analyses yielded highly consistent results. They collectively identify several core mechanisms underlying depression in the context of chronic comorbidities. These findings were further supported by node-level predictability metrics. First, the strongest edge in the entire network was between Anhedonia and Sad Mood. This result aligns with many depression network studies worldwide [47, 48] It reinforces that affective disturbance (low mood) and disruption of the reward-motivation system (anhedonia) are tightly linked core features of depression [49, 50]. Within our rural sample with high comorbidity, the persistent suffering, functional impairment, and economic strain from chronic illnesses likely provoke profound sadness and helplessness [51]. Such persistent negative affect may directly undermine the capacity to experience pleasure [52]. Conversely, a pervasive loss of interest may intensify feelings of worthlessness and hopelessness [53]. This forms a self-sustaining vicious cycle. Anhedonia also exhibited a strong connection with Fatigue, forming a clinically salient “symptom triad.” Fatigue is both a common manifestation of CMDs and a core depressive symptom [54]. Our findings suggest that among rural older adults, disease-related physical exhaustion is deeply intertwined with psychological aspects of depression. Fatigue can directly impede engagement in activities, thereby worsening anhedonia [55]. In turn, motivational deficits and behavioral withdrawal from anhedonia and low mood may further reduce energy levels [54]. This cyclic interaction is particularly detrimental in older adults with comorbidities. It may compromise self-management abilities, such as medication adherence and maintaining a healthy diet, thereby exacerbating physical health conditions [56].

These three highly interconnected symptoms—Sad Mood, Anhedonia, and Fatigue—also emerged as the nodes with the highest centrality values. This indicates they are among the most statistically interconnected and influential elements within the observed network structure. Consistent with network theory, such high-centrality symptoms are hypothesized to act as key drivers of the depressive syndrome, and interventions targeting them may, in theory, produce cascading benefits across the network [29]. For example, Behavioral Activation encourages participation in rewarding or meaningful activities. From a network perspective, this could simultaneously mitigate anhedonia (direct target), potentially enhance mood (via positive reinforcement), and disrupt the passivity-fatigue cycle [55, 57, 58]. The high predictability of these three nodes (all > 58%) further supports the idea that their states are closely tied to the network. It indicates that a substantial proportion of their variance is accounted for by their neighboring symptoms. This suggests that, if the network structure reflects causal pathways, intervening on adjacent nodes could represent an effective indirect treatment strategy [59]. However, it is important to note a dual implication of high predictability: while it may indicate potential pathways for influence through the network, it also signifies that these central symptoms are deeply embedded within and highly dependent on the current system’s state. Consequently, if the surrounding network structure is stable and self-reinforcing, these very central symptoms might also exhibit resistance to change, presenting a challenge for interventions.

Second, bridge centrality analysis identified key pathways linking social support dimensions to depressive symptoms. Subjective Support was the most influential bridge node on the social support side. Its strongest (negative) connection was directed specifically toward the central symptom Sad Mood. This result carries significant theoretical and practical implications. It provides empirical support for the “stress-buffering model” of social support [20]. This underscores that the subjective perception of being supported, rather than merely objective support availability, plays a critical role in attenuating the psychological impact of stressors. However, the strength of this bridge connection must be interpreted within the context of the network topology: although the bridge between Subjective Support and Sad Mood is statistically identifiable and aligns with theoretical models, its edge weight (-0.05) is markedly weaker than the strongest within-community connections (e.g., Anhedonia–Sad Mood = 0.55). This suggests that the two communities—social support and depressive symptoms—are largely structurally independent, and the direct dynamic influence of this bridge is limited. Consequently, interventions targeting this bridge alone are unlikely to produce substantial immediate symptom reduction; instead, it may be better positioned as a strategic component within a broader intervention framework, implemented in conjunction with strategies targeting core depressive symptoms such as sad mood, anhedonia, and fatigue. In practice, this points to a dual-path intervention approach [60]. On one hand, improving perceived social support—for instance, through family therapy to enhance communication quality or peer support groups to strengthen belongingness—may help alleviate Sad Mood [61]. On the other hand, effectively treating Sad Mood (e.g., through cognitive-behavioral therapy) may reduce cognitive biases and social avoidance, thereby enabling individuals to better perceive and utilize available objective support [62]. In summary, focusing on such bridge connections may offer a nuanced avenue for addressing the cycle between inadequate social support and depression [59, 63]. Nevertheless, intervention designs must take into account the relatively modest effect strength of this pathway and integrate it within a more comprehensive treatment framework.

In contrast, Objective Support and particularly Support Utilization exhibited limited connectivity within the network. The very low predictability of Support Utilization (9.1%) suggests that an individual’s help-seeking behavior is largely determined by factors external to the immediate psychopathological system. These may include deeply ingrained personality traits, cultural norms, and pervasive mental health stigma [64, 65]. This finding carries crucial clinical implications. It indicates that merely providing available resources is insufficient if patients face significant barriers to utilizing them. Therefore, clinical interventions must move beyond a passive provision of support. They must actively address these external barriers [66]. This could involve psychoeducation to normalize help-seeking and reduce internalized stigma. It also requires systematic screening for logistical obstacles and training healthcare providers to proactively offer support in a culturally sensitive manner [67, 68]. Enhancing the utilization of existing support may prove to be a more efficient strategy than solely creating new resources.

Clinical and public health implications

The findings of this study can be translated into multi-level and targeted practical applications. At the clinical level, assessment should move beyond total depression scores to prioritize the severity and interactions of the three core depressive symptoms: sad mood, anhedonia, and fatigue. The evaluation of social support must incorporate indicators of subjective perception while also addressing the utilization of existing support systems. At the intervention level, integrated approaches should be designed, combining individual symptom management with environmental modifications. For instance, village doctors or family physicians could be trained not only to manage chronic diseases during follow-ups but also to encourage patients’ participation in social activities to alleviate anhedonia and loneliness. Additionally, guiding family members on providing more effective emotional support may enhance patients’ sense of subjective support. It must be emphasized, however, that enhancing subjective support should complement, rather than replace, direct symptom-focused therapies.

At the public health and policy level, the results advocate for establishing integrated care models within resource-constrained rural health systems. Primary care, often the only point of contact for older adults, should be strengthened to routinely screen for depression—particularly the core symptom triad. Task-shifting strategies, such as training village doctors or community health workers in basic psychosocial assessment and supportive counseling skills, could expand service coverage. Public health initiatives should develop low-cost, community-based interventions that combine gentle physical activity—to counteract fatigue and improve cardiometabolic health—with social components to enhance subjective support. Examples include structured group exercise programs or health mutual-aid clubs. Policy support is essential to formally integrate mental health into chronic disease management protocols and to incentivize collaborative care that addresses both physical and psychosocial needs. This shift toward a holistic, person-centered approach is crucial to mitigate the syndemic of multimorbidity and depression in the aging rural population.

Uniqueness, limitations, and future directions

The uniqueness of this study lies in its pioneering depiction of the social support-depressive symptom network in a highly vulnerable population. This group faces a “triple jeopardy” of aging, the burden of multimorbidity, and scarce medical and social resources in rural settings. Our network reveals two key findings. While the core structure of depression (e.g., the anhedonia-sad mood link) is consistent with the general population, its strong connection to Fatigue and its specific bridge relationship with Subjective Support likely reflect the unique psychosocial experience of this group [48]. The physical burden from chronic conditions and potential social isolation in rural areas make fatigue and the sense of subjective support critical leverage points influencing their mental health.

However, several limitations of this study chart the course for future research. First, the cross-sectional design fundamentally limits causal inference. This is a critical constraint for network theory. As highlighted in recent methodological discussions, high centrality in a cross-sectional network reflects strong conditional associations but does not necessarily equate to causal importance or being a “driver” of the system [41]. A node can be central due to being a common effect of other variables. Therefore, the proposition that intervening on central or bridge nodes (e.g., Sad Mood or Subjective Support) would cause cascading effects remains a compelling but untested hypothesis generated by our model. Our data support the identification of such nodes as potential targets, not their causal efficacy. To infer causality, longitudinal designs (e.g., using panel data or Vector Autoregression models) are essential to establish temporal precedence. Ultimately, future intervention studies are required to experimentally test whether altering these identified nodes produces the theorized network-wide benefits. Second, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. The sample was drawn from a single province in China, and its applicability to other cultural or healthcare contexts requires validation. Furthermore, while the NCT mostly showed no significant differences across subgroups—which supports the internal robustness of our model—we cannot rule out that the lack of significant findings for certain comparisons (e.g., across specific BMI groups) was due to insufficient statistical power in some under-represented subgroups. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples, including urban populations and other healthcare systems, are needed to verify the universality of the identified network structure. Third, the present network was confined to nodes from the PHQ-9 and SSRS, which may introduce omitted variable bias. The inclusion of other clinically and theoretically relevant constructs—such as loneliness, functional disability, self-efficacy, or illness perceptions—could potentially alter the network structure. For instance, loneliness might attenuate the observed bridge centrality of subjective support, while functional disability could serve as a more direct mediator between CMM and symptoms like fatigue, thereby repositioning the relative importance of these nodes. Future research should aim to expand the nomological network by incorporating these variables to construct a more comprehensive and ecologically valid model of depression in this population. Fourth, although the SSRS is a validated instrument, its operationalization may not fully capture the nuanced structure of social support. Specifically, the “Objective Support” node primarily reflects quantitative aspects (e.g., number of supporters, frequency of contact) rather than qualitative dimensions—such as the perceived adequacy of emotional or tangible aid—which are often more psychologically salient. In network analysis, a node should ideally represent a distinct and coherent construct. The heterogeneity of the items comprising the “Objective Support” node may result in a composite variable that does not function as a unitary psychological factor, potentially leading to an underestimation of its true association with depressive symptoms and contributing to its weak connectivity within the network. This represents a key methodological limitation in interpreting the role of objective support. A similar issue may apply to the “Support Utilization” node, which also showed limited integration in the network. Future studies should consider employing measures that distinguish between structural and functional dimensions of support to establish more precise network nodes or, with sufficient sample size, modeling individual support items separately to provide a more nuanced understanding of the support–depression interface. Fifth, the network in this study was estimated using a Gaussian Graphical Model with GLASSO regularization, which inherently assumes multivariate normality. Although, as noted in the main text, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the cor_auto function, which employs polychoric correlation matrices appropriate for ordered data, and the results indicated that our primary findings were not unduly influenced by distributional assumptions—partly mitigating concerns regarding non-normality—it must be acknowledged that this approach remains an approximation based on continuous latent variables. Consequently, the resulting edge weights and centrality indices should be interpreted as approximate estimates under the assumption of normally distributed latent variables. While the overall network topology—such as which nodes are highly interconnected—may be relatively robust, the precise magnitude of specific edge weights or centrality values could vary depending on the distributional properties of the data. Future studies could employ network models specifically designed for ordinal data (e.g., the Ising model) to perform additional sensitivity analyses, thereby further validating the robustness of the network structure identified here. Sixth, the analysis revealed a statistically significant yet modest-strength association between Subjective Support and Sad Mood (edge weight = -0.05). This suggests that isolated interventions targeting this specific pathway may have limited efficacy in symptom reduction. Their primary clinical value may instead lie as a strategic and nuanced component within a comprehensive treatment plan, aimed at achieving long-term synergistic effects through mitigating a key risk factor. Finally, although standardized scales were used, the reliance on self-report data is susceptible to biases like recall bias. Future studies could incorporate objective measures (e.g., actigraphy to measure activity levels reflecting fatigue) and informant reports to enrich the data sources.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study uses network analysis to provide a detailed map of the interactions between social support and depressive symptoms in rural older adults with CMM. We identified Sad Mood, Anhedonia, and Fatigue as central symptoms requiring prioritized intervention, and Subjective Support and Sad Mood as critical bridges connecting the socio-environmental context with individual suffering. These findings emphasize the necessity of integrated strategies that combine medical and psychosocial support, offering targeted interventions crucial for alleviating the dual disease burden in this rapidly growing population.
