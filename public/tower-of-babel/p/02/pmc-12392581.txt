Vulnerability analysis and sustainable rehabilitation strategies for mangrove ecosystems in Bedono coastal area, Central Java
Authors: Fuad Muhammad, Marsum Marsum, Sapto Purnomo Putro, Mochammad Hadi, Murningsih Murningsih, Jafron Wasiq Hidayat, Waqor Ahmad, Puteri Inandin Nabiha, Slamet Wardoyo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mangrove ecosystems in Bedono Village, Demak Regency, Central Java, are seriously degraded due to aggressive coastal abrasion, extreme soil salinity (45‰-80‰), and failure of previous rehabilitation programmes. This study aims to assess the level of vulnerability of mangrove habitat and formulate an integrated rehabilitation strategy that includes ecological and institutional aspects. Data were collected through tidal analysis, measurement of sediment texture and soil salinity, identification of plankton and macrobenthos diversity, and analysis of mangrove species community structure. The Coastal Vulnerability Index (CVI) was calculated based on four biophysical parameters, namely tidal inundation duration, maximum inundation height, soil salinity, and substrate type. Spatial analysis was conducted using ArcGIS 10 software to map the zones with high vulnerability. In addition, the Analytical Network Process (ANP) approach was used to prioritise ecological and institutional problems and develop solutions based on stakeholder interviews and expert validation. The results showed that the mangrove ecosystem in Bedono is highly vulnerable, with tidal inundation for 29–31 days per month, an average inundation height of 0.97 m, and a dominance of loamy sand textured soil. Avicennia marina species dominated in all growth stages, while the diversity of other species was very low. Key problems identified include coastal abrasion, low planting success rates due to non-adaptive techniques, and weak coordination between stakeholders. Priority solutions include installation of sediment traps, implementation of adaptive planting techniques, community involvement in monitoring, and establishment of village regulations to support programme sustainability. This study provides a new framework for integrating CVI-based ecological vulnerability mapping and institutional analysis to support comprehensive mangrove rehabilitation planning in heavily impacted coastal areas.

Introduction

The mangrove habitats in the coastal area of Bedono, Demak Regency, Central Java, are experiencing severe degradation caused by a combination of natural and anthropogenic factors [1]. Coastal erosion, land subsidence, and rising sea levels have significantly altered the physical environment, while human activities such as aquaculture expansion, unsustainable land use, and overharvesting of mangrove resources have exacerbated these impacts [2]. These conditions have reduced the ability of mangrove ecosystems to provide essential services, including coastal protection, biodiversity support, and carbon sequestration. The decline in mangrove cover has not only increased vulnerability to climate change impacts but also diminished the resilience of local communities who rely on these ecosystems for their livelihoods [3, 4].

Analyzing the vulnerability of mangrove habitats is critical to understanding the underlying drivers of degradation and identifying areas that require immediate intervention. A vulnerability analysis offers insights into the environmental and socio-economic stressors affecting mangrove ecosystems and assesses their resilience and adaptive capacity [5, 6]. Such analysis is crucial for prioritizing actions and designing targeted solutions that address both ecological and community needs. However, vulnerability analysis alone is insufficient to mitigate the risks faced by mangrove ecosystems [7]. To translate findings into meaningful outcomes, the analysis must be supported by strategies that optimize management and rehabilitation efforts [6].

The integration of vulnerability analysis with management optimization strategies is necessary to develop a holistic approach to mangrove conservation. Effective management strategies not only address the identified vulnerabilities but also provide actionable solutions to enhance ecosystem resilience and promote sustainable use [8]. These strategies involve engaging stakeholders, leveraging local knowledge, and incorporating innovative restoration techniques such as sediment replenishment and mangrove-friendly aquaculture [9]. Moreover, they facilitate the alignment of ecological priorities with socio-economic goals, ensuring that conservation efforts are both practical and sustainable [10].

While most previous studies have tended to separate ecosystem vulnerability analyses and management strategies, this study combines the two within a unified framework. The main focus of the research was to identify and map the vulnerability of mangrove ecosystems in the coastal area of Bedono, Demak, an area documented to experience annual abrasion of up to 5 m and seawater intrusion leading to extreme increases in soil salinity. The research also highlighted the limited capacity of local institutions to support sustainable rehabilitation. By integrating Coastal Vulnerability Index (CVI)-based biophysical analysis and spatial mapping using ArcGIS, as well as prioritisation of problems and solutions using an Analytical Network Process (ANP) approach, this research offers a novel approach to designing ecologically relevant and institutionally applicable rehabilitation strategies at the village level. The findings of this research are expected to not only enrich the literature on mangrove conservation in Indonesia, but also provide practical contributions in designing evidence-based local policies.

Methods

This study adopted an integrated approach to assess the vulnerability of mangrove habitats in the coastal area of Bedono, Demak Regency, Central Java. The methodology encompassed ecological assessments, field sampling, and spatial analysis to evaluate bio-physical and oceanographic parameters influencing mangrove ecosystems. The collection of soil and vegetation samples for this study was conducted following the necessary permissions. Prior to sampling, approval was obtained from the Environmental Agency of Central Java Province. Additionally, verbal consent was acquired from local residents at the sampling sites. The sampling locations included both public and privately used lands, and appropriate permissions were ensured before data collection. The methods employed are described as follows:

Study area and sampling framework

The research was conducted in Bedono, Demak, focusing on key mangrove habitat variables. Sampling locations were selected based on visual assessments of mangrove distribution and variability in habitat conditions. Mangrove data collection and analysis

Mangrove data were collected and analysed to evaluate the biophysical condition of the habitat and form the basis for calculating the Coastal Vulnerability Index (CVI). The four main variables assessed included tidal inundation duration, maximum inundation height, soil salinity and substrate type. All methods used were based on scientific guidelines and have been applied in previous similar studies [3, 5, 11].

Tidal analysis

The 2014 tidal data from Semarang Maritime Meteorological Station was analysed using the harmonic method (Formzahl number). The F value was calculated to classify tidal types and calculate the frequency of inundation. The tidal patterns were determined through the calculation of the Formzahl number (F) using the formula:

Here, O₁ and K₁ are diurnal tidal components caused by lunar and solar gravitational forces, and M₂ and S₂ represent semidiurnal tidal components. This analysis provided insights into tidal behavior, including the frequency and height of inundation.

Soil salinity

Salinity levels were measured using a hand refractometer, with samples taken from depths of 5–30 cm. The measurements provided information on salinity variations, which are critical for mangrove habitat health.

Substrate type

Grain size analysis was conducted in a laboratory to classify sediment types, including proportions of sand, silt, and clay. The substrate composition was a key determinant of mangrove distribution and growth patterns.

Substrate characterisation

Soil samples were analysed at the Soil Mechanics Laboratory, Diponegoro University, to determine the sand, silt and clay fractions. Determination of soil texture using the hydrometer method [12, 13] and the results were categorised according to the USDA Soil Texture Triangle. Substrate types were then classified based on their susceptibility to mangrove growth.

Scores were assigned based on a ranking system: 1 (low vulnerability), 2 (medium vulnerability), and 3 (high vulnerability). These scores were categorized following established guidelines.

Spatial analysis

Spatial data processing was conducted using ArcGIS 10 software. The analysis included geometric and radiometric corrections of satellite imagery, data cropping to refine the study area, and the creation of composite images to visualize mangrove vulnerability patterns. The processed data were used to generate vulnerability maps, highlighting areas of high ecological risk. The collected data were synthesized to provide a comprehensive understanding of mangrove habitat vulnerability. The CVI score, combined with spatial visualizations, enabled the identification of priority areas for conservation and rehabilitation efforts. This methodology integrated quantitative and spatial analyses to inform effective management strategies for mangrove ecosystems in Bedono, Demak.

Optimization of rehabilitation management strategies

In an effort to formulate a relevant and applicable mangrove rehabilitation strategy in the Bedono coastal area, this study used the Analytical Network Process (ANP) approach. This approach was chosen because it is able to integrate qualitative assessments from stakeholders with a systematic quantitative analysis framework, and take into account the interrelationships between problems and solutions in complex social-ecological systems.

Key data for the ANP analysis were obtained through focus group discussions (FGDs) and in-depth interviews conducted in July 2023. The FGD participants consisted of 12 people representing various stakeholders, including village officials, representatives of the district environment office, coastal non-governmental organisations (NGOs), mangrove farmer groups, and academics with experience in mangrove ecosystem conservation and rehabilitation. Discussions focussed on identifying key barriers to mangrove rehabilitation and developing alternative solutions that were considered realistic to implement at the local level.

The structure of the ANP model consists of two main clusters, namely the problem cluster and the solution cluster, each of which is divided into ecological and institutional aspects. The problems identified include coastal abrasion, limited adaptive planting techniques, and ecosystem degradation on the ecological side, as well as weak coordination between stakeholders, lack of monitoring systems, and the absence of specific regulations at the village level on the institutional aspect. Meanwhile, the proposed solutions include the construction of sediment traps, training on planting techniques that suit local environmental conditions, community-based monitoring systems, strengthening coordination across sectors, and formulating village regulations (Perdes) that support mangrove conservation.

In the analysis process, FGD participants were asked to provide pairwise comparison ratings of all elements in the ANP model using a numerical scale of 1–9 according to the methodology developed by Saaty [14]. The assessment data were then analysed using Super Decisions software version 3.2. To ensure consistency and validity of the assessment results, Kendall’s concordance coefficient (W) was used. A W value above 0.7 was used as a minimum threshold to indicate good agreement between respondents.

All stages of the process were designed to ensure that the resulting strategies were not only conceptual, but also contextualised, taking into account the biophysical conditions of the Bedono coastal area and the social capacity of the local community. This approach draws on best practices from the current literature on participatory and evidence-based coastal management, as proposed by Patil et al. and Shafein et al. [6, 8]. Thus, the results of the ANP analysis are expected to provide a strong basis for strategic decision-making to support the success of mangrove rehabilitation in a sustainable manner.

Result

The results of this study provide a comprehensive analysis of the mangrove habitat vulnerabilities in Bedono, Demak Regency, as assessed through tidal analysis, soil salinity measurement, substrate characterization, and the calculation of the Coastal Vulnerability Index (CVI).

Mangrove ecosystem condition

Abundance and diversity of plankton

The abundance and diversity of plankton in the coastal waters of Bedono, Demak, were analyzed across three sampling stations, representing varying proximity to mangrove habitats. These stations were selected to capture the spatial variation in plankton community structure and to assess their ecological significance within the mangrove ecosystem.

The results include a comprehensive identification of species belonging to different groups such as Chlorophyta, Chrysophyta, Pyrophyta, and Entomostraca. Diversity indices, evenness, and total abundance values were calculated to determine the ecological status of the plankton community. Station 1 exhibited the highest plankton abundance due to its proximity to nutrient inputs, while diversity indices across all stations indicated moderate diversity.

Abundance and diversity of macrobenthos

Macrobenthos diversity was analyzed to evaluate the health and functionality of the mangrove ecosystem in Bedono, Demak Regency. Sampling was conducted across three stations representing distinct zones within the mangrove forest: the inner edge (Station 1), the middle part (Station 2), and the outer edge (Station 3). These stations capture variations in ecological conditions influenced by tidal inundation, sediment composition, and organic matter availability.

The analysis identified macrobenthos specimens categorized into groups such as Gastropoda, Bivalvia, Polychaeta, and other taxa. Gastropods dominated in abundance, particularly in Station 3, which exhibited the highest macrobenthos density. The species diversity index (H’) varied across stations, with Station 2 displaying the highest diversity, indicating favorable ecological conditions in the middle part of the mangrove forest. Evenness index (e) values highlighted relatively uniform species distribution in Stations 1 and 2, whereas Station 3 showed lower evenness, reflecting the dominance of a few species.

Density, frequency, coverage, and importance value of Mangrove species

The ecological dominance of mangrove species in Bedono, Demak Regency, was assessed using relative density, relative frequency, relative coverage, and Importance Value Index (IVI). The analysis was conducted at three growth stages—tree (pohon), sapling (pancang), and seedling (anakan)—to capture variations in ecological roles across different life stages.

At the tree stage, Avicennia marina demonstrated overwhelming dominance, contributing significantly to density (88.25%), frequency (68.89%), and coverage (96.63%). These values translated into an exceptionally high IVI of 253.77, highlighting its ecological importance in stabilizing sediment and protecting coastal areas. Other species, such as Rhizophora sp. and Sonneratia sp., had much lower IVI values of 33.67 and 12.56, respectively, reflecting their limited representation in the study area.

At the sapling stage, Avicennia marina again showed dominance, with relative density (100.63%) and frequency (88.13%) contributing to an IVI of 188.76. However, Rhizophora sp. displayed an increased presence compared to its performance at the tree stage, achieving an IVI of 98.81. Sonneratia sp. remained the least dominant, with an IVI of 12.43.

For seedlings, the community structure was more diverse. Avicennia sp. maintained its ecological significance with an IVI of 163.20, followed by Rhizophora sp. (IVI of 84.16) and Sonneratia sp. (IVI of 44.66). Notably, Bruguiera sp. appeared at this stage, though it had a minimal ecological role, as indicated by its low IVI of 7.98.

CVI (Coastal vulnerability Index)

Soil salinity

The soil salinity in the study area ranged from 45‰ to 80‰, with an average of 65‰. These levels are significantly higher compared to previous measurements by Kushartono (2009), which ranged from 38‰ to 53‰ depending on depth. The high salinity levels are primarily attributed to seawater intrusion during high tides, which permeates and accumulates in impermeable soil layers. Salinity is a critical factor influencing mangrove habitat as it limits the growth of certain species to specific salinity ranges. Key contributors to the elevated salinity include land openness, tidal activity, seawater intrusion, evaporation rates, low rainfall, and limited freshwater inflow from rivers. Based on its salinity, the mangrove habitat in Bedono is classified as highly vulnerable, with a vulnerability score of 3. The spatial distribution of salinity vulnerability is shown in Fig. 2a.

Discussion

The results of this study provide valuable insights into the vulnerabilities and management challenges of the mangrove ecosystem in Bedono, Demak Regency. This discussion elaborates on three key findings: the condition of the mangrove ecosystem, the Coastal Vulnerability Index (CVI), and the optimization of rehabilitation management strategies.

Analysis of the abundance and diversity of plankton and macrobenthos in mangrove ecosystems provides insight into the ecological dynamics and health of these environments. The higher abundance of Station 1 plankton is likely due to nutrient inputs from terrestrial sources, which is consistent with findings that nutrient enrichment can significantly affect plankton communities in mangrove ecosystems [15, 16]. A moderate diversity index at Station 1 indicates stable ecological conditions, as diversity often reflects ecosystem health and resilience [17]. Meanwhile, Station 2’s high macrobenthos diversity indicates favourable ecological conditions, possibly due to optimal environmental parameters that support a wide range of species [18]. The dominance of gastropods at Station 3, with low evenness, highlights the impact of tidal inundation and sediment composition on species distribution, which is a common phenomenon in mangrove ecosystems [15]. While nutrient enrichment can increase productivity, it can also lead to ecological imbalances if not managed properly. Excessive nutrient inputs can result in eutrophication, affecting species diversity and ecosystem health. Therefore, understanding the balance between nutrient inputs and ecological stability is critical to the conservation and management of mangrove ecosystems [19].

The analysis of mangrove species dominance further highlights the critical role of Avicennia marina, which demonstrated overwhelming dominance across all growth stages [20]. Its high Importance Value Index (IVI) reflects its ecological importance in stabilizing sediment and providing coastal protection [21]. However, the limited presence of other species such as Rhizophora sp. and Sonneratia sp. indicates a need for diversification in mangrove restoration efforts to enhance ecosystem resilience and biodiversity.

The CVI assessment identified key environmental vulnerabilities affecting the mangrove habitat. High soil salinity, ranging from 45‰ to 80‰, highlights the impact of seawater intrusion, evaporation, and limited freshwater inflow. These conditions pose significant challenges for mangrove growth and survival, necessitating adaptive strategies tailored to high-salinity environments [5, 22]. The sediment analysis classified the substrate as sandy loam, with varying proportions of silt and clay, corresponding to moderate vulnerability. The prevalence of sandy loam indicates a need for erosion control measures to stabilize sediment and enhance mangrove establishment [23].

Tidal inundation duration and maximum inundation height were also identified as critical factors contributing to habitat vulnerability. The mixed tidal regime results in prolonged inundation, with an average duration of 29–31 days per month, placing additional stress on mangrove habitats. Maximum inundation heights averaging 0.97 m further highlight the susceptibility of the ecosystem to tidal dynamics [24]. These findings underscore the need for integrated management approaches that consider hydrodynamic factors in mangrove restoration.

The prioritization of ecological and institutional problems using the Analytical Network Process (ANP) provided a structured framework for addressing the challenges in mangrove rehabilitation. Abrasion emerged as the most critical ecological problem, necessitating targeted erosion control measures such as sediment traps and coastal barriers. The lack of resilient planting techniques and ecosystem degradation further emphasize the need for adaptive restoration practices and systematic conservation efforts.

Institutional barriers, including weak stakeholder coordination, inadequate monitoring, and limited budget allocations, highlight governance gaps that hinder effective rehabilitation. Strengthening stakeholder collaboration and community-based monitoring are essential for fostering ownership and ensuring long-term sustainability. The introduction of village-level regulations (Perdes) to support labor-intensive rehabilitation programs offers a practical solution to align local governance with conservation objectives.

The ANP results also ranked abrasion management and strengthening stakeholder coordination as the top solutions, reflecting the need for an integrated approach that addresses both environmental and governance challenges. These findings provide a clear roadmap for developing effective and sustainable mangrove management strategies in Bedono, Demak Regency.

Conclusion

This study shows that the mangrove ecosystem in coastal Bedono, Demak, is in a vulnerable condition. The high level of vulnerability is caused by very high soil salinity (45‰−80‰), the dominant substrate of clayey sand, as well as the duration of tidal inundation which is almost every day with an average maximum height of 0.97 m. These conditions limit the growth and diversity of mangrove species. These conditions limit the growth and diversity of mangrove species. The vegetation structure showed a strong dominance of Avicennia marina at all growth stages, with limited representation of other species such as Rhizophora sp. and Sonneratia sp. This indicates the need for species diversification in rehabilitation activities.

Through an Analytical Network Process (ANP) approach, the main challenges identified were coastal abrasion, lack of adaptive planting techniques, and weak coordination among stakeholders. Proposed solutions include installing sediment traps, training on planting techniques, community-based monitoring, and strengthening village regulations.
