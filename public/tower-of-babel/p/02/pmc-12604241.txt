Integrative analysis of the transcriptome and metabolome reveals the peel coloration mechanism of Zanthoxylum armatum
Authors: Yun Ren, Jiamin Liu, Liangping He, Jinjue Jiang, Ting Gong, Qian Zhou, Qiang Li, Jing Liu, Ning Tang, Zexiong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundZanthoxylum armatum is a globally recognized medicinal and edible plant, the fruit peel color of which is considered an important trait that influences its economic value. In this study, Z. armatum fruits were used at five different stages of development to explore the law of metabolite biosynthesis and its potential molecular mechanism.ResultsUsing transcriptomic and widely-targeted metabolomic analyses, 23 differentially accumulated metabolites, 274 differentially expressed structural genes, and 230 differentially expressed transcription factor genes associated with anthocyanin synthesis were identified. Based on co-expression network analysis, key structural genes positively associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis were identified and the corresponding hub transcription factor genes were predicted. Cyanidin-3-O-arabinoside, cyanidin-3-O-glucoside, cyanidin-3-O-(2ʹʹ-O-glucosyl)glucoside, cyanidin-3-O-(6’’-O-malonyl)glucoside, cyanidin-3-O-rutinoside, cyanidin-3-O-galactoside, and petunidin-3-O-glucoside-5-O-arabinoside were identified as key anthocyanins accumulated in the peel of mature Z. armatum fruits, the synthesis of which appeared to involve the structural genes PAL5, PAL8, PAL9, PAL11, PAL13, PAL14, C4H2, CHS17, F3’H2, DFR1, ANS2, and ANS4 as candidate catalysts. Furthermore, dual-luciferase reporter assays indicated that bHLH-MYC9 and MYB19 may trans-activate the promoters of ANS2 and ANS4, respectively.ConclusionsOur findings in this study provide new perspectives regarding the regulation of peel color development during the ripening of Z. armatum fruit.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07564-w.Keywords: Zanthoxylum armatum, Peel coloration, Anthocyanin biosynthesis, Transcriptome, Metabolome

Background

Zanthoxylum armatum is a globally recognized dual-purpose plant used for both medicinal purposes and as a food, and it is also a characteristic economic forest species that is mainly developed in China [1]. Among the different varieties of Z. armatum, red peel color is considered an important economic feature, the development of which is mainly attributable to the accumulation of different types and levels of anthocyanins [2, 3], an important class of flavonoids that play multiple roles in plant development, and are believed to have certain beneficial effects on human health [4, 5].

In many plant species, a variety of anthocyanins have been identified [6, 7]. Anthocyanin derivatives of delphinidin (Dp), petunidin (Pt) and malvidin (Mv) are generally sources of purple and dark colors, while derivatives of cyanidin (Cy) and pelargonidin (Pg) are major pigments in bright red fruits [8]. The types of anthocyanins in fruit peel of Z. armatum have not been determined. Although several genes encoding proteins involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis and regulation have been identified, such as structural genes phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamic acid 4-hydroxylase (C4H), and 4-coumarate: coenzyme A ligase (4CL) [9], chalcone synthase (CHS) [10], chalcone isomerase (CHI), flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H), flavonol synthesis (FLS), flavanone 3’-hydroxylase (F3’H), flavonoid 3’5’-hydroxylase (F3’5’H), and dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR) [11, 12], anthocyanidin synthase (ANS), flavonoid 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UFGT), et al. [13, 14], and transcription factors (TF) myeloblastosis (MYB) [15, 16], basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) [17], the MYB-bHLH -WD40/WDR (MBW) complex [18], bZip [19, 20], NAC [21, 22], et al., insights into the regulation of each anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway are still the goal of the future.

Combinations of large-scale datasets, based on high-throughput functional genomics, have been widely used [23, 24]. In recent years, advances in high-throughput functional transcriptomic and metabolomic approaches have facilitated studies on the regulation of genes associated with peel coloration [3, 25], and in a number of plant species, this has enables the construction of networks linking multiple genes and metabolites, based on joint analysis such as correlation and cluster analyses of transcript and metabolite datasets [26].

In this study, the regulatory networks associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis at different stages of fruit development in Z. armatum, at both the transcriptome and metabolome levels were examined. In particular, we focused on the differential accumulation of metabolites and the differential expression of genes, including structural biosynthetic genes and TFs, and on the basis of an integrative analysis of transcript and metabolite datasets, a connection network was constructed to identify the regulatory genes associated with the metabolites contributing to anthocyanin biosynthesis. The findings of this study provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of peel coloration during fruit development in Z. armatum.

Results

Peel coloration during Z. armatum fruit ripening and changes in chlorophyll and main anthocyanin contents

During the process of Z. armatum fruit ripening, the most obvious change is the transition of peel color from green to red (Fig. 1A). To assess the changes in pigment profiles, the contents of chlorophyll and the main anthocyanins in the peel of Z. armatum during the course of fruit development were determined. With respect to chlorophyll, an initial increase and a subsequent sharp decline with the progression of Z. armatum fruit ripening, with corresponding gradual increases in the contents of anthocyanins (Fig. 1B) were detected. The dark green fruits characterizing developmental stage S3 reflect the high chlorophyll content at this stage, and we established that the degree of green pigmentation is associated with the amounts of chlorophyll in the peel. The subsequent accumulation of anthocyanins, together with the simultaneous rapid loss of chlorophyll, coincided with the color transition from green to red.

Target metabolite assays revealed metabolic differences among the five stages of Z. armatum fruit development

To compare the composition of metabolites involved in fruit coloring at the five different stages of Z. armatum fruit development, sample quality control (QC), correlation, principal component (PCA) and cluster analyses on the obtained dataset were performed. The concordance between the findings of display analysis and correlation analysis of the total ion current of the mass spectrometric detection of different QC samples revealed the data to be highly reliable (Fig. S1). Moreover, the PCA score plot and cluster heatmap results revealed the distinct well-separated general secondary metabolite profiles corresponding to the five defined stages of fruit development (Fig. S2A, B).

To model differences between samples obtained at the five developmental stages, orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) score plots and S-plots (Fig. S3) were used. By setting fold changes of ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 and VIP values of ≥ 1.0 (variable importance in projection from the obtained multivariate analysis OPLS-DA model) as thresholds for defining significant differences, 387, 265, 275, and 86 metabolites characterized by differential abundance between stages S1 and S2, S2 and S3, S3 and S4, and S4 and S5, respectively, were selected (Fig. S2C). Among these, 138, 162, 179, and 26 metabolites were upregulated and 249, 103, 96, and 60 metabolites were downregulated in the S1 vs. S2, S2 vs. S3, S3 vs. S4, and S4 vs. S5 comparisons, respectively (Fig. S2D).

Differential accumulation of peel coloration-related metabolites among the five stages of Z. armatum fruit development

Anthocyanins are a class of natural water-soluble pigments with important physiological activity that are synthesized in the phenylpropanoid pathway via the flavonoid pathway. To date, the pathways of anthocyanin synthesis have been reported for a range of plants, including Arabidopsis, grape, and petunia. The six most commonly identified types of anthocyanins are Cy, Dp, Pg, peonidins (Pn), Pt, and Mv, which are distinguished by the hydroxyl moiety or methoxy substitution of the B ring (Fig. S4). Accordingly, these six classes of anthocyanins and their intermediates were selected for further metabolomic analysis of Z. armatum (Table S1).

Cyanidin glycosides and Pt-3-O-glucoside-5-O-arabinoside were identified in all the samples. At the mature stages S4 and S5, peel coloration was found to coincide with the marked accumulation of Cy and Pt pigments, with Cy-3-O-arabinoside, Cy-3-O-glucoside, Cy-3-O-(6ʹʹ-O-malonyl) glucoside, Cy-3-O-rutinoside, Cy-3-O-galactoside, and Pt-3-O-glucoside-5-O-arabinoside showing increases of 16.38-, 4.30-, 2.66-, 3.41-, 4.32-, 1.39-Log2FC in S4 compared with S3 samples and 2.59-, 2.15-, 2.44-, 2.25-, 2.24-, 1.79-Log2FC in S5 compared with S4 samples, respectively, and Cy-3-O-(2ʹʹ-O-glucosyl) glucoside showing an increase of 12.06-Log2FC at S4 compared with S3 (Table S1).

In addition to the aforementioned anthocyanins, increases or reductions in certain other proanthocyanidins, flavonoids, flavonols, and some related acylated groups at different stage of fruit development were detected (Table S1). The identified anthocyanins and related compounds in the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway, based on reference to the KEGG and PMN databases and previous literature, is illustrated in Fig. 2. This reveals variations in the composition and contents of 23 compounds (shown in bold in Table S1 and Table S2) in the pathway at different stages of Z. armatum fruit development. Among these, anthocyanidin derivatives, such as Cy and Pt, were found to accumulate during the latter stages of development (Fig. 2 and Table S2). Notably, pronounced differences were detected in the compositions of flavonoid glycosides during the five developmental stages (Fig. 2 and Table S1), which may provide evidence that these metabolites are potentially associated with the peel coloration of Z. armatum fruit.

Differential expression of genes among the five stages of Z. armatum fruit development

To determine the molecular processes associated with the metabolic transformations during Z. armatum peel coloring, transcriptomic sequencing of 15 libraries derived from five fruit samples (three replicates per sample) was conducted, which yielded 108.18 Gb of clean data, with each sample reaching at least 6 Gb, in which the Q30 base percentage was 92% or higher (Table S3). Difference analysis revealed that there were 23,353, 22,689, 21,695, 27,181, 21,466, 19,936, 25,253, 17,501, 22,255, and 5627 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for the S1 vs. S2, S1 vs. S3, S1 vs. S4, S1 vs. S5, S2 vs. S3, S2 vs. S4, S2 vs. S5, S3 vs. S4, S3 vs. S5, and S4 vs. S5 comparisons, respectively, among which, 11,192, 11,758, 10,936, 12,785, 11,497, 11,350, 13,082, 8857, 10,173, and 1400 genes were up-regulated, and 12,161, 10,931, 10,759, 14,396, 9969, 8586, 12,171, 8644, 12,082, and 4227 down-regulated genes in S2 compared with S1, S3 compared with S2, S4 compared with S3, and S5 compared with S4, respectively (Fig. S5A). Cluster analysis of the DEGs by the short time-series expression miner (STEM) generated 50 clusters, including downregulated genes in clusters 0–25 and upregulated genes in clusters 26–49 (Fig. S5B). The downregulated clusters 2, 3, 6, 7, 9, 14, 16, 18 23, and 24 and the upregulated clusters 35, 39, 41, 44, 45, and 46 were statistically significant (P ≤ 0.05), and the number of genes assigned to clusters 2, 3, 6, 7, 9, 14, 16, 18, 23, 24, 35, 39, 41, 44, 45, and 46 was 2604, 784, 4890, 511, 1826, 2744, 938, 784, 1064, 574, 2310, 1274, 3464, 966, 3248, and 1708, respectively (Fig. S5B).

KEGG pathway analysis of the identified DEGs (in clusters 2, 3, 6, 7, 9, 14, 16, 18, 23, 24, 35, 39, 41, 44, 45, and 46) revealed significant changes in 593 genes associated with the biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids, flavonoids, and anthocyanins at the different stages of fruit development (Table 1 and Table S4), including 274 anthocyanin synthesis structural genes (PALs: 15, C4Hs: 5, 4CLs: 27, CHSes: 20, CHIs: 6, F3Hs: 2, FLSes: 7, F3ʹHs: 5, DFR: 1, LARs: 2, ANSes: 4, ANRs:2, UFGTs: 12, trans-caffeoyl-CoA 3-O-methyltransferases (CAOMTs): 38, ferulate-5-hydroxylases (F5Hs): 4, hydroxycinnamoyltransferases (HCTs): 47, p-coumaroylshikimate/quinate 3ʹ-hydroxylases (C3ʹHs): 3, cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCRs): 31, cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CADs): 18, UDP-glycosyltransferase (UGTs): 21, and F3ʹ5ʹHs: 4 transcripts) (Fig. 2 and Table S4). In addition, 230 differentially expressed TFs were identified at the five developmental stages, including those annotated as MYB, bHLH, WRKY, WD40, MADS-box, and b-ZIP (Table 2, Table S5).

Analysis of the associations between transcripts and anthocyanin-related metabolites at the five stages of Z. armatum fruit development

To characterize the regulatory network of anthocyanin synthesis, correlation analysis was performed to determine quantitative changes in metabolites and transcripts at the five stages of Z. armatum fruit development. Specifically, the associations between 23 anthocyanin-related metabolites and 504 DEGs (274 anthocyanin synthesis structural genes and 230 TFs) were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis (Table S6), which revealed that 383 DEGs (204 anthocyanin synthesis structural genes and 179 TFs) showed strong correlations (R2 > 0.9) with the 23 metabolites (Fig. 4, Tables S7 and S8).

Connection network between regulatory genes and anthocyanin-related metabolites. The networks were visualized with the Cytoscape software (version 3.7.2). Red circles represent differentially accumulated metabolites (A and B), and green circles represent differentially expressed structural genes (A) and transcription factor genes (B)

Network analysis indicated that the metabolites and DEGs could be divided into four groups (A–D) and six clusters (I–VI), respectively (Fig. 4, Fig. S6 and Table S9). DEGs in clusters I and II were found to be correlated with metabolites in group A, including hesperetin, cinnamic acid, Cy-3-O-glucoside, Cy-3-O-galactoside, and Pt-3-O-glucoside-5-O-arabinoside, and group B, including Cy-3-O-rutinoside, kaempferol, and 5-O-caffeoylshikimic acid. DEGs in cluster III were highly correlated with the metabolites in three or all four groups, whereas the DEGs in cluster IV were highly connected to group C metabolites, including gallocatechin, caffeic acid, xanthohumol, Cy-3-O-(6ʹʹ-O-malonyl)glucoside, l-phenylalanine, chalcone-2ʹ-O-glucoside, catechin, eriodictyol, epicatechin, naringin, and p-coumaraldehyde. DEGs in cluster VI were strongly connected with group D metabolites, including Cy-3-O-(2ʹʹ-O-glucosyl)glucoside, p-coumaryl alcohol, phloretin-2ʹ-O-glucoside, and Cy-3-O-arabinoside, and DEGs in cluster V were highly correlated with the metabolites in groups C and D (Fig. 4).

During each of the transitional stages of fruit development, differential regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis-related structural DEGs was detected (Fig. 4, Table S9). Among the 44 genes identified as being associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis in clusters I, II, and III, homologs for seven PALs, one C4H, three LDOXs/ANSs, one DFR, and one UFGT were significantly up-regulated coincident with the S3 to S4 transition, whereas homologs for one F3H, two CAOMTs and one HCT were significantly down-regulated. Similarly, homologs for four 4CLs, nine CHSs, 10 CCRs, and four CADs were more significantly up- or down-regulated in the S3 to S4 transition. Of the 62 genes associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis in cluster IV, homologs for one FLS, two CHIs, two 4CLs, one F3ʹ5ʹH, 14 HCTs, one C3ʹH, and one UFGT were significantly up-regulated both in the S1 to S2 and S2 to S3 transitional periods, whereas two CHS and C4H homologs were strongly down-regulated, and homologs for five UGTs, 11 CAOMTs, 16 CCRs, three CADs, and three F5Hs were significantly up- or down-regulated. In addition, 33 homologs were identified from cluster V (8 4CLs, 10 HCTs, 3 CAOMTs, 2 C3ʹHs, 2 CHSs, 3 UGTs, 3 CADs, an LDOX/ANS, and an F3ʹ5ʹH) and 65 homologs from cluster VI (12 CAOMTs, 6 4CLs, 14 HCTs, 2 LARs, 3 CHIs, 5 PALs, 4 F3ʹHs, a C4H, 3 CADs, 4 CCRs, an F3ʹ5ʹH, an FLS, 4 UFGTs, 4 UGTs, and a CHS) that exhibited up-regulation during the S1 to S2 and S2 to S3, concomitant with the green stages, and down-regulation during the S3 to S4, concomitant with the green-to-red transition, or were down-regulated during the S1 to S2 and S2 to S3 transitions, but up-regulated during the S3 to S4 transition.

TFs in clusters I and II were found to be highly correlated with group A and group B metabolites, respectively, and were strongly expressed during peel coloration (Fig. 4, Table S9). Of these, two MYB TFs were up-regulated, and four (a CCT, a WD40, a WRKY, and a bHLH) were down-regulated, and 12 TFs (3 MADS-boxes, 7 bHLH-MYCs, and 2 B-boxes) were up- or down-regulated during the S3 to S4 transition. In cluster III, connected with metabolites in both group A to D, homologs of CCT and bHLH-MYC were significantly up-regulated during the S3 to S4 transition. Moreover, 159 TFs (63 in cluster IV, 34 in cluster V, and 62 in cluster VI) were strongly connected with group C and D metabolites that were abundantly detected during peel coloration (Fig. 4, Table S9). Among the 63 TFs in cluster IV, 10 MYBs, one MADS-box, one bHLH-MYC, and most GATAs (13/14), bHLHs (5/6), and response regulator receiver RRR (6/7) TFs were upregulated and only single GATA (1/14), bHLH (1/6), RRR (1/7), and bZIP TFs were significantly downregulated during both the S1 to S2 and S2 to S3 transitional periods, whereas 10 WRKY, nine CCT, two SWIRM, and two WD40 TFs were significantly up- or downregulated during both these transitions. TFs in cluster V (6 CCTs, 4 MYBs, 4 RRRs, 7 GATAs, 3 bZIPs, a B-box, 6 WRKYs, a bHLH, a bHLH-MYC, and a WD40) and cluster VI (9 CCTs, 3 MYBs, 2 RRRs, 15 GATAs, 5 MADS-boxes, 3 SWIRMs, 6 B-boxes, 4 WRKYs, 9 bHLHs, 5 bHLH-MYCs, and a WD40) were upregulated in the green S1 to S2 and S2 to S3 transitional stages but downregulated during the green to red S3 to S4 stage transition, or downregulated during the S1 to S2 and S2 to S3 transitions but upregulated during the S3 to S4 transition.

Association analysis of anthocyanin biosynthesis-related DEGs and anthocyanins at the five stages of Z. armatum fruit development

Our analysis of the distribution of anthocyanins during the ripening of Z. armatum fruits revealed that Cy and Pt anthocyanin derivatives were abundant during the red stages S4 and S5 (Figs. 1 and 2). Metabolites in groups A and D were found to contain relatively high levels of anthocyanin (Fig. 4, Fig. S6), which is consistent with the stages of Z. armatum fruit development. Accordingly, for the purposes of association analyses, five Cy and Pt anthocyanin derivatives were selected from groups A and D and 178 genes in Cluster I (connected with Group A) and Cluster VI (connected with Group D). The results revealed that 140 genes (82 structural genes and 58 TFs) were strongly correlated (R > 0.95 or R < −0.95) with the five selected Cy and Pt anthocyanin derivatives (Fig. 5, Tables S6 and S10).

Discussion

Anthocyanin pigments contribute to the red color of plant organs, which for Z. armatum fruit influences the ornamental, dietary and market value [27]. Over the past decade, several genes encoding proteins involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis and regulation have been identified; however, the types of anthocyanins in Z. armatum fruits and the regulation of each anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway are not well understood.

Flavonoids are the major class of compounds determining plant tissue pigmentation, among which anthocyanins comprise the largest water-soluble pigment family in the plant kingdom [28–30]. In our syudy, it was found that these anthocyanidins are produced during the transition from green to red skin peel (S3–S4 and S4–S5) in ripening fruit, whereas accumulations of the metabolites of proanthocyanidins, flavonoids, flavonols, and acylated groups were detected primarily during the green (S1–S2 and S2–S3) fruit period. Thus, we hypothesized that metabolite transformation and anthocyanin production occurs predominantly during the green-red (S3–S4) fruit period.

Cyanidin, delphinidin, pelargonidin, peonidins, petunidin and malvidin are the six most common types of anthocyanins. Among them, Dp, Pt and Mv are usually purple and dark sources, while derivatives of Cy, Pe, and Pg are the main pigments of bright red fruits [8]. In this regard, Zheng et al. [2] investigated the roles of anthocyanins in the coloring of Zanthoxylum L. fruit and found a total of 10 anthocyanins, including pelargonin-O-hexoside-O-rhamnoside-O-hexoside, pelargonidin 3,5-diglucoside, cyanidin O-syringic acid, peonidin O-hexoside, peonidin 3-O-glucoside, cyanidin 3-O-glucoside, cyanidin 3-O-galactoside, peonidin, malvidin 3-O-glucoside, and malvidin 3-O-galactoside were the main anthocyanins in the peel. Contrastingly, in the present study, Pt-3-O-glucoside-5-O-arabinoside, Cy-3-O-arabinoside, Cy-3-O-galactoside, Cy-3-O-glucoside, Cy-3-O-(6ʹʹ-O-malonyl)glucoside, Cy-3-O-(2ʹʹ-O-glucosyl)glucoside, and Cy-3-O-rutinoside as prominent anthocyanins (Fig. 2, Table S1) were identified in Z. armatum. Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed that the contents of petunidin and the cyanidin (except Cy-3-O-rutinoside) anthocyanins in groups A and D were consistent with fruit stage development in Z. armatum (Fig. 4), thereby indicating that they may be key anthocyanins in the peel of mature fruit. Zheng et al. [2] found that Pg-O-hexoside-O-rhamnoside-O-hexoside, Pg-3,5-diglucoside, Cy-O-syringic acid, Pe-3-O-glucoside, and Pe-O-hexoside were the main anthocyanins in Z. bungeanum peel. The difference in anthocyanin types may contribute to the difference in fruit color between the bright-red mature fruits of Z. bungeanum and the dark-red mature fruits of Z. armatum. It should be noted, however, that all anthocyanin and related compound values reported here are relative equivalents because certified standards for most compounds are still lacking; acquisition or synthesis of authentic standards is required to absolutely quantify these key pigments and further validate the colour–compound relationships.

In general, two types of genes are involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis in plants: one is the structural genes encoding enzymes to catalyse anthocyanin biosynthesis [31]– [32] and the orther is the regulatory genes that control the transcription of these structural genes [33–38]. Our findings in the present study revealed that the expression of structural genes PAL5, PAL8, PAL9, PAL11, PAL13, PAL14, C4H2, CHS17, F3’H2, DFR1, ANS2, and ANS4 were positively correlated with the content of key Cy and Pt anthocyanins in the peels (Fig. 5, Tables S7 and S11); Although glycosylation is essential for anthocyanin stability, no UGT/UFGT isoform reached the stringent R2 >0.9 threshold during the critical S3→S4 transition. Whether this reflects post-transcriptional regulation, rapid protein turnover, or as-yet-unidentified UGT families remains to be tested; functional validation of the weakly correlated candidates (UFGT6, UGT15, UGT23) is now in progress. TFs MYB19, MYB21, bHLH24, WD40-5, bHLH-MYC3, bHLH-MYC5, bHLH-MYC9, bHLH-MYC11, bHLH-MYC13, bHLH-MYC14, bHLH-MYC23, WRKY15, WRKY26, WRKY27, MADS1, MADS3, MADS6, MADS7, MADS10, B-box5, B-box10, GATA4, GATA15, GATA34, GATA39, CCT11, CCT22, and CCT33 were positively or negatively correlated with the key Cy and Pt anthocyanins in peels at different stages of Z. armatum fruit development (Table S6), which suggests that these structural genes and TFs are candidate regulators potentially associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis. At present the roles of GATA TFs in flavonoid biosynthesis remain poorly defined. Accordingly, although it has been reported that these TFs participate in multiple biological processes, it would be of interest to determine whether, and if so how, GATA TFs regulate fruit peel coloration in Z. armatum.

Transcription factors determine the location and level of anthocyanin accumulation by activating or inhibiting the expression level of key structural genes independently [39–41] or jointly [42–44]. Our gene co-expression network analysis of the key structural genes and TFs revealed that CHS17 was negatively correlated with CCT33 and B-box5; ANS2 and ANS4 were negatively correlated with bHLH-MYC13; ANS2 was positively correlated with bHLH-MYC9 and B-box10 and negatively correlated with bHLH-MYC11 and MADS10; and ANS4 was positively correlated with MYB19. In addition, the TFs B-box5, bHLH-MYC13, MADS7, bHLH-MYC9, bHLH-MYC11, and bHLH-MYC14 were found to be positively correlated with the TFs CCT33, WD40-5, GATA39, B-box10, MADS10, and WRKY27, respectively; MYB21, WRKY26, and bHLH-MYC23 were positively correlated with MYB19 expression; and MADS7, bHLH-MYC9, and B-box10 were negatively correlated with WD40-5, bHLH-MYC11, and bHLH-MYC14, respectively (Fig. 6, Tables S11 and S12). Further dual-luciferase reporter assays suggested that the protein products of the putative hub TF genes bHLH-MYC9 and MYB19 can activate the promoters of the structural genes ANS2 and ANS4, respectively (Fig. 7). Thus, we hypothesize that potential regulatory relationships may exist between these key TFs and structural genes, as well as among the TFs themselves(Fig. 8).

Possible regulatory pathways of anthocyanin biosynthesis in Z. armatum. The overlap of black arrows indicates that there are multiple steps of reaction, and the appropriate illustrations were given in the Supplementary Fig. S7

Consequently, on the basis of our collective observations, we propose the following characteristics associated with the regulatory pathway for peel color development in the ripening fruit of Z. armatum (Fig. 8): PAL5, PAL8, PAL9, PAL11, PAL13, PAL14, C4H2, CHS17, F3’H2, DFR1, ANS2, and ANS4 are the key structural genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis, and the transcription factors bHLH-MYC9 and MYB19 participate in anthocyanin biosynthesis by regulating the structural genes ANS2 and ANS4, respectively, potentially contributing to the accumulation of cyanidin and petunidin anthocyanins in Z. armatum peels. However, simple correlation analyses and dual-luciferase reporter assays—which only preliminarily indicate that bHLH-MYC9 and MYB19 can trans-activate the ANS2 and ANS4 promoters (Fig. 7)—are insufficient to accurately resolve the precise relationships among structural genes, transcription factors, and anthocyanins. Therefore, further validation by in vivo DNA-binding assays such as Y1H, EMSA, or ChIP, as well as transgenic or virus-induced gene-silencing experiments, will be necessary.

Conclusions

In this study, a combination of metabolomic and transcriptomic approaches were used to analyze the five stages of Z. armatum fruit color development, with the aim of characterizing the regulatory network associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis. Initially, the key anthocyanins and related metabolites in the peel of Z. armatum fruits were identified, and subsequently the correlations between metabolites and genes were analyzed to identify the key structural and regulatory genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis. Finally, a gene co-expression network linking key regulatory transcription factors and structural genes associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis was constructed. The findings of dual-luciferase reporter assays suggested that bHLH-MYC9 and MYB19 may trans-activate the promoters of the ANS2 and ANS4, respectively. Our findings offer candidate regulators and a framework for further dissecting the control of anthocyanin biosynthesis in Z. armatum.

Materials and methods

Plant material collection and permits

Zanthoxylum armatum germplasm resources were obtained from the university-owned experimental farm of Chongqing University of Arts and Sciences (Yongchuan District, Chongqing, China; 29.03° N, 105.86° E). The farm is managed by the Scientific Research and Demonstration Center of the university. Permission was obtained from the managing authority, and sampling was conducted in accordance with the university’ s internal research protocol. Based on the progression of fruit growth, the following five discrete stages of development were identified: the fruit set stage (S1) at approximately 2 months after blooming (April 2020), during which fruits initial form; the fruit expanding stage (S2) at approximately 3 months after blooming (May 2020), during which the fruits enlarge; the slow-growth stage (S3) at approximately 4 months after blooming (June 2020), during which the fruits continue to enlarge and assume the characteristic fruit shape; the early ripening stage (S4) at approximately 5 months after blooming (July 2020), during which the fruits begin to turn red; and the fully ripened stage (S5) at approximately 6 months after blooming (August 2020), during which the fruits turn completely red, and the seeds ripen. For chlorophyll and anthocyanin contents analysis, three biological duplications were conducted and each duplication samples were collected from three different plants at each of the five stages. Three replicate biological samples (fixed weight sample) were collected from three different plants and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent HPLC analysis or RNA extraction.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data analysis

RNA sequencing analysis

RNA, extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA), was used to construct cDNA libraries of the tissues of fruit collected at the different stages of development (3 replicates). Library sequencing was performed using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform to obtained 150-bp paired-end reads, which were subsequently filtered and corrected to eliminate sequencing errors and obtain high-quality reads. The clean reads thus obtained were then pooled and assembled into transcripts using Trinity version 2.2.0 with default parameters. Transcripts of less than 300 bp in length were discarded and the longest transcript was defined in each de Bruijn plot as a unigene using the de Bruijn strategy. These unigenes were used as reference sequences for assembly and coding sequence (CDS) prediction using TransDecoder (http://transdecoder.github.io/). Assembled sequences were annotated based on a BLASTX/BLASTP similarity search using the Trinotate program. The clean reads were pooled from all samples and normalized the read count to the aligned fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM), and then quantified gene expression levels and identified DEGs using Cufflinks and Cuffdiff software (version: 2.1.1). As criteria for defining differential expression, fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 was used as the cut-off values, and the false discovery rate (FDR) was cross-gene adjusted at the significance level (≤ 0.05) for all tests. To obtain knowledge about expression profiles of DEGs throughout five development stages, the STEM was used to cluster DEGs. The gene expression data and gene annotation files were uploaded to STEM. Expression profiles were analyzed using the STEM clustering algorithm. Functional annotation of all annotated unigenes was performed with reference to the Clusters of Orthologous Groups of Proteins (COG), Gene Ontology (GO), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases (E-value ≤ 1e−5).

Metabolite extraction and profiling

To facilitate sample extraction, metabolite identification, and quantification analyses, MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd (www.metware.cn; Wuhan, China) was used. Prior to analysis, specimens of the biological samples were freeze-dried using a Scientz-100 F lyophilizer. The freeze-dried samples were pulverized for 90 s at a frequency of 30 Hz in a MM 400 Retsch mixer mill, using zirconia beads. Samples (100 mg) of the freeze-dried powders were mixed with 1.2 mL of 70% methanol, stirred vigorously for 30 s at 30 min intervals (for a total of six times), and then stored overnight at 4 °C. The following day, having initially been centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 10 min, the extracts were passed through 0.22-µm pore size SCAA-104 filters (ANPEL; Shanghai, China; http://www.anpel.com.cn/) prior to ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) analysis.

For the analysis of sample extracts, a combined UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC: Shimadzu Nexera X2; MS: Applied Biosystems 4500 Q TRAP) was used. For UPLC, an Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm), with a mobile phase consisting of solvent A (pure water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid) was used. The gradient program for sample measurements commenced with initial proportions of 95% A and 5% B, followed by a 9-min linear gradient to 5% A, 95% B, at which it was maintained for 1 min. Thereafter, the gradient was reversed to 95% A and 5% B within 1.10 min, at which it was held for 2.9 min. The solvents were flowed at a speed at 0.35 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, the amount of sample injected was 4 µL, and the effluent was conveyed to an ESI-triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP) mass spectrometer.

A Q TRAP AB4500 UPLC/MS/MS System equipped with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray interface was employed for the acquisition of both linear ion trap (LIT) and triple quadrupole (QQQ) scans, which was controlled using Analyst 1.6.3 software (AB Sciex, Ontario, Canada), and operated in both positive and negative ion modes. The ESI source parameters were set to a source temperature of 550 °C and an ion spray voltage of 5500 V in the positive ion mode and − 4500 V in the negative ion mode, with gas I, gas II, and the curtain gas at 50, 60, and 25.0 psi respectively. Additionally, the collision-activated dissociation was set to high. For instrument tuning and mass calibration, 10 and 100 µmol/L polypropylene glycol solutions were used in the QQQ and LIT modes, respectively. An multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) experiment was used for QQQ scanning, the collision gas (nitrogen) was set as the medium, and declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) optimized for each MRM transition. For each time period, a designated set of MRM conversions were monitored based on the metabolites eluted during that time period.

Analyst 1.6.3 software (AB SCIEX) was used for metabolite data analysis. A range of statistical methods, including PCA and OPLS-DA, were used to detect different metabolites between within species by considering the VIP values of the PCA and OPLS-DA models. The R package MetaboAnalystR was employed to generate the VIP values, and prior to OPLS-DA, the data were log transformed and mean-centered. Subsequently, metabolites were selected based on a VIP value of 1 or more and an absolute Log2FC of 1 or higher. Additionally, a permutation test (200 permutations) was conducted to prevent overfitting. The identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG Compound database and then assigned these to the KEGG Pathway database. To assess pathways associated with significant regulation of metabolites, metabolome enrichment analysis was performed and established significance based on a hypergeometric test p-value. Based on the KEGG and plant metabolic network (PMN) databases and the literature [45], targeted selection of anthocyanins and their intermediate metabolites was performed.

For the purposes of identifying anthocyanins, initially, an anthocyanin material library was assembled, obtained secondary spectra of the standards, and established a characteristic ion pair database of these standards. In cases in which no relevant standard was available, the compound was identified according to the fragmentation rule and retention time of the standard, and added to the characteristic ion pair database. Having performed these steps, quantitative information for a given substance was collected in the MRM mode. For each sample subjected to mass spectrometry analysis, high-resolution spectrum of the mixed sample was obtained and compared with secondary spectra in the database, and MRM was used for quantification. Compound analysis was carried out by relative quantifications.

Combined metabolome and transcriptome analyses

To integrate metabolome and transcriptome data, the respective Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated. To this end, using the metabolome and transcriptome data, initially, the means of all biological replicates for each stage of fruit development were determined, and thereafter calculated the fold changes for each stage and compared these values with corresponding values obtained for the preceding stage. Finally, DPS (v15.10) was used to calculate the log2 (fold change) of each metabolite and each transcript. To visualize relationships between the metabolome and transcriptome, a threshold correlation coefficient of R2 > 0.9 was slected, and Cytoscape (version 3.7.2) was used for analysis.

Gene co-expression analysis

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to identify associations between structural genes and TF FPKM values. Initially, the mean FPKM values for each developmental stage in the transcriptome data and the mean expression of each transcript was calculated, and then the fold change for each stage in the transcriptome data was calculated and compared this with the corresponding value obtained for the preceding stage. Finally, DPS (v15.10) was used to calculate the log2 (fold change) of each transcript. To visualize the relationships between the structural genes and TFs, a threshold correlation coefficient of R > 0.95 or R < −0.95 was selected, and Cytoscape (version 3.7.2) was used for analysis.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis

To validate the expression levels of 24 randomly selected putative regulatory DEGs, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analyses were performed. RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse transcribed using a HiScript III 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, China) in accordance with the kit instructions. For qPCR amplification, a LightCycler 96 thermocycler (Jena, Germany) was used in conjunction with ChamQ SYBR Green master mix (Vazyme, China). The reaction program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. To verify the absence of non-specific products and primer dimers, melting curve analysis was performed, and used the 2–△△Ct method to determine the levels of relative expression. The ZaUBC gene was employed as an internal reference gene [46] to normalize the expression of the target gene for the three biological and technical replicates. The primers used for amplification are listed in Table S13. The FPKM values were normalized as x∗=(x − min)/(max − min). DPS (v15.10) was used to calculate the Pearson correlation coefficient between qPCR value and FPKM value, and the correlation coefficient R2 >0.9 was selected as the correlation basis.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To examine the association between anthocyanin synthesis-related structural genes and transcription factors, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed. To generate effectors, the full-length cDNA fragments of the regulatory transcription factor genes bHLH-MYC9/13, B-box10 and MYB19 were cloned and recombined in a pGreen II 62-SK vector. The 1500-bp DNA fragments upstream of the transcription start site in the promoter regions of the ANS2 and ANS4 genes were cloned separately into pGreenII0800-LUC vectors to obtain two different reporter vectors. The recombinant plasmids were then used to transform Agrobacterium GV3101, and transient expression analysis was performed using infiltrated tobacco plant leaves following a previously described method [47]. For the purposes of determining firefly luciferase (LUC) and Renilla luciferase (REN) activities, a Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (11402ES60; Yeasen, China) was performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The assessed sample groups were as follows: Background F: untransfected cells + firefly luciferase detection reagent; Background R: untransfected cells + firefly luciferase detection reagent + Renilla luciferase detection reagent; Experimental groups: transfected cells treated with experimental compounds (experimental group F and experimental group R); Control groups: transfected cells receiving no treatment (control group F and control group R). The ratio of LUC/REN served as an index of promoter activity, with the following ratios being calculated: Experimental group ratio = (experimental group F - background F)/(experimental group R - background R); Control group ratio = (control group F - background F)/(control group R - background R); and Expression fold = experimental group ratio/control group ratio. The LUC/REN ratio of the Control was taken as 1. The primers used are listed in Table S13.
