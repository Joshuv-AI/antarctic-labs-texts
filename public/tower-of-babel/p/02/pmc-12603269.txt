Using entropy weight method and quality function deployment to develop emotion-driven dashboard interfaces for NEV
Authors: Xinwei Guan, Lin Li, Jinchi Fu, Dengkai Chen, Suihuai Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
New energy vehicles (NEVs) demand dashboard interfaces that not only present critical driving information but also evoke positive user emotions to enhance satisfaction and safety. This study develops a structured framework that integrates the Entropy Weight Method (EWM) and Quality Function Deployment (QFD) to embed emotion-driven requirements into NEV dashboard design. 56 Kansei words were collected through an online survey (n = 300) and prioritized to 15 through experts interviews. An anonymous online questionnaire (n = 101, 98 valid responses) collected adult drivers’ emotional perceptions across 15 descriptive adjectives. Exploratory factor analysis distilled these descriptors into four principal dimensions: Pleasant, Technological, Stylish, and Minimalist. Entropy weight calculation quantified the relative importance of each dimension, revealing Pleasantness as the highest-priority factor. The QFD process then translated these weighted emotional dimensions into specific design attributes, including optimal color contrast ratios, iconography style guidelines, typography choices, and interface layout coherence. Prototype dashboards developed according to this schema were evaluated in a pilot study, demonstrating strong congruence between user-prioritized emotions and perceived design quality. This framework offers a replicable, data-driven method for systematically aligning emotional user needs with interface design parameters in NEVs, thereby providing actionable guidance for designers aiming to improve user experience through emotion-driven dashboard development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18149-w.

Introduction

Background

The automotive industry is undergoing a profound transformation due to the urgent need for sustainable and eco-friendly transportation solutions. New Energy Vehicles (NEVs), including Electric Vehicles (EVs) and Hybrid Electric Vehicles (HEVs), have emerged as alternatives to traditional Internal Combustion Engine (ICE) vehicles, offering advantages such as reducing greenhouse gas emissions, lowering operational costs, and improving energy efficiency1– 2. However, the transition to NEVs presents unique challenges, particularly in designing vehicle components to meet the evolving needs of users3– 4.

The dashboard interface plays a key role in NEVs by providing essential driving information directly to the driver, such as speed, battery status, navigation and alerts. The dashboard provides essential information about the vehicle’s status, such as speed, battery charge, navigation, and other important alerts5– 6. With the advancement of NEV technology, the design of the dashboard module must continuously evolve to enhance user experience, safety, and functionality7– 8. For example, integrating cutting-edge technologies such as autonomous driving features, connectivity functions, and infotainment systems into the dashboard design is a significant challenge while at the same time ensuring user-friendliness and ergonomic efficiency9–11.

Traditional dashboard designs often fail to fully support the requirements of NEV drivers. Compared with conventional vehicles, NEVs demand additional functions such as real-time energy consumption monitoring, battery health and range prediction, and charging guidance. These functions are closely tied to drivers’ concerns about efficiency, range anxiety, and eco-driving behaviors, which are less prominent in traditional vehicle contexts. In addition to technological integration, user experience and ergonomics remain significant challenges. Dashboards must therefore offer intuitive interaction, ensure accessibility of critical information, and minimize driver distraction12.

Recent studies have explored various approaches to optimizing NEV dashboard designs. For example, human-centered design principles have been employed to enhance user experience, emphasizing intuitiveness and accessibility13– 14. Kansei Engineering (KE), was originally developed by Mitsuo Nagamachi, who defined it as translating technology of a consumer’s feeling and image for a product into design elements15. It is a user-centered methodology that translates users’ emotional needs into tangible design elements, and has also received significant attention in emotional and product design16. Research using KE has demonstrated its effectiveness in aligning product design with user expectations, especially in the automotive sector17– 18.

Beyond KE, advancements in digital interface technologies have also influenced dashboard design. Studies have shown that adaptive and customizable interfaces significantly improve user satisfaction and reduce cognitive load during driving19– 20. Moreover, sustainable design practices, such as using recyclable materials and energy-efficient displays, have been highlighted as critical considerations in NEV dashboard development21– 22.

Kansei Engineering (KE) has been widely applied in product design as an effective method to translate user emotions into design elements12. Traditional KE methods primarily rely on the extraction and quantitative evaluation of Kansei words, such as the Semantic Differential Method and Multidimensional Scaling, to capture users’ emotional perceptions of products23. However, these methods often depend heavily on expert experience, making them highly subjective, costly to implement, and less scalable.

To overcome these limitations, researchers have introduced methods such as fuzzy logic and grey system theory to address uncertainty and ambiguity in Kansei evaluations. For instance, integrating grey relational analysis into group Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) has helped derive comprehensive weights, mitigating the strong subjectivity of traditional AHP. Additionally, combined models using intuitionistic fuzzy sets and TOPSIS have been applied to evaluate HMI design solutions. Despite these advancements, these methods still exhibit complexity in model construction and dependence on expert input, limiting their application in practical design scenarios. To address these issues, a fuzzy cognitive model and mapping method was proposed to translate Kansei Engineering words into multisensory design elements, enhancing the understanding of individual needs in emotional design24.

Nevertheless, existing methods remain limited in practical dashboard applications, particularly for NEVs. The novelty of this study lies in two main aspects. First, it integrates EFA, the EWM, and QFD into a coherent framework. While each of these methods is well established individually, their systematic combination provides both statistical rigor and design translation capability, thereby reducing the subjectivity traditionally associated with KE. Second, the study applies this integrated framework specifically to the context of NEV dashboards. Compared with conventional vehicles, NEVs involve distinct requirements such as energy efficiency monitoring, range prediction, and charging management, which have not been sufficiently addressed in existing Kansei Engineering research. By aligning quantitative weighting with design attribute mapping in this domain, the study contributes a structured and reproducible process for advancing human-centered interface design in NEVs.

Research gap and objectives

Despite these advancements, achieving an optimal balance between functionality and emotional resonance in dashboard design remains challenging. Traditional dashboard layouts often fail to meet NEV users’ unique preferences, particularly in integrating advanced technologies and enhancing aesthetic appeal25– 26. These requirements stem from the specific driving context of NEVs, such as range monitoring, charging management, and eco-driving goals, which differ from those of traditional vehicle users. To address these gaps, recent studies have proposed data-driven methods such as the Entropy Weight Method and Fuzzy Comprehensive Evaluation Method to quantitatively assess user preferences and refine design strategies.

This study aims to develop an NEV dashboard module design optimization strategy based on Kansei Engineering. By leveraging KE, this research seeks to create a dashboard design that not only fulfills functional requirements but also resonates emotionally with users. The specific objectives are:

Methods

The methodology used in this study integrates advanced analytical techniques to translate user perceptions and preferences into feasible design improvements for the NEV dashboard module. This approach includes Exploratory Factor Analysis, Entropy Weighting Method and Quality Function Deployment to ensure a comprehensive and systematic optimisation strategy.

Research framework

In this study, the research objectives were directly aligned with the methodological framework (Fig. 1). EFA was applied first to address Objective 1 (identify the key emotional and functional needs) by extracting core factors from the 15 Kansei words. EWM was then employed to achieve Objective 2 (develop a methodological framework) by objectively weighting these factors, thereby integrating emotional and functional needs in a systematic manner. Finally, QFD was used to fulfill Objective 3 (optimize dashboard module design) by translating the weighted user needs into specific design attributes. This sequential process established a coherent data flow: user evaluations → EFA (factor extraction) → EWM (weight determination) → QFD (design translation) → design iteration.

Sample collection and recruitment

Stage 1: Kansei-word collection (300 participants)

To build the initial pool of emotional words, an open-ended, anonymous online survey (from October 15 to November 5) was administered to 300 adult NEVs users. All participants were recruited online within China. Only individuals with prior driving experience or familiarity with NEVs were included in the study. A total of 39 dashboard samples were initially collected. These included interfaces from commercially available NEV models available in public automotive design resources. The selection aimed to cover a wide range of layout structures, functional emphases, and aesthetic styles. These 39 samples were presented to participants in the first stage of evaluation. Each participant viewed a set of dashboard designs and was invited to list all adjectives or phrases that captured their emotional impressions. A total of 56 candidate Kansei words were extracted from these responses. 6 experts (3 male, 3 female; each with ≥ 3 years of automotive HMI or design experience) independently reviewed the list and, by consensus, reduced it to 15 representative Kansei words: Techy, Future, Simple, Edgy, Novelty, Smart, Natural, Cozy, Effective, Warm, Fresh, Grand, Stylish, Aesthetic, Useful. The detailed process of factor analysis is presented in Sect. 3.3.

Stage 2: Kansei-word collection (101 participants)

From November 6 to December 10, 2024, 101 participants were invited to provide emotional feedback regarding various NEVs dashboard designs. The resulting 101 × 15 rating matrix served as the foundation for subsequent emotional needs analysis and model development.

The evaluation procedure was conducted using a five-point Likert scale, where 1 represented ‘strongly disagree’ and 5 represented ‘strongly agree’. Each participant was asked to rate the perceived relevance of each of the 39 dashboard samples with respect to the 15 representative Kansei words identified in stage 1. This ensured that the evaluation directly reflected the emotional and perceptual dimensions most relevant to NEV dashboard design. The use of a standardized Likert scale enabled consistent data collection across participants, facilitating subsequent quantitative analysis. Details of the surveys are presented in Chap. 3, while the complete questionnaire is available in the supplementary materials (Document S1, S2). This study involved only the collection of non-sensitive information through the questionnaire, with participants providing their emotional feedback on the NEV dashboard designs. All responses were anonymized, and no personal or sensitive data were collected. All subjects studied were adults and signed an informed consent form. As such, the study does not require formal ethical review or approval. Ethical guidelines regarding participant consent and data confidentiality were adhered to throughout the data collection process.

Exploratory factor analysis

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was applied exclusively to the Stage 2 rating matrix (101 respondents × 15 items), as the first step in our approach to identifying the underlying structure of users’ perceptions of the dashboard design of new energy vehicles. EFA is a dimensionality reduction technique that groups variables according to their correlation with each other in order to reveal the core factors that have a significant impact on users’ emotions and preferences. When the dimensions of the questionnaire itself are uncertain, the dimensions of the questionnaire can be explored using exploratory factor analysis.

Factor analysis is a set of methods for explaining correlations between variables in terms of more fundamental entities called factors. It arose from the observation that variables (e.g., tests of human aptitude or measures of interpersonal functioning) tend to be correlated with each other in a well-formulated domain. According to factor analysis, variables are correlated because they are determined to some extent by common, but unobservable, influences30. Based on philosophical and statistical principles31, EFA was first applied by Spearman32, and quickly became an essential tool for evaluating theories and validating measurement instruments33–35. As Edwards and Bagozzi point out, the relationship between the constructs and their indicator variables is important because this knowledge unambiguously maps theoretical constructs onto empirical phenomena36, allowing for meaningful testing of theories37]– [38.

In general, the suitability of a scale for factor analysis can be tested on the basis of the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value, which is judged as shown in Table 2. Entropy weight method

Entropy weight method (EWM) is a method used to determine the weights of each indicator in a multi-indicator evaluation system. In multi-attribute decision analysis, different indicators have different degrees of influence on the overall evaluation results, so the weights need to be set. The core idea of the entropy weight method is to measure the amount of information of each indicator based on the size of the information entropy, and use this to determine the importance of the indicator. The smaller the entropy value is, the more informative it is, and the higher the weight should be. This method can well avoid the interference of subjective factors, making the determination of weights more scientific and objective.

In practice, EWMs are commonly used in environmental assessment, economic evaluation, and engineering, where multiple variables can have an impact on decision outcomes. For example, Anand et al. detailed how entropy methods can objectively construct and quantify weights in complex decision-making environments, improving the accuracy of assessments by minimizing subjective influences. Similarly, a study evaluated sustainable electric vehicles using EWM and combined it with fuzzy logic to manage uncertainty in the criteria weights, demonstrating that EWM can be adapted to complex scenarios of MDPI involving both qualitative and quantitative data41.

The EWM procedure generally involves three key steps: (1) data normalization to eliminate scale differences, (2) calculation of entropy and entropy redundancy to reflect the amount of information each indicator conveys, and (3) determination of final weights and composite scores for ranking alternatives.

A detailed explanation of the mathematical derivation and formulae used in EWM is provided in Appendix B in the Supplementary Information. The entropy weighting method effectively solves the difficult problem of objectively assigning weights in multi-criteria decision-making and ensures a more scientific approach to prioritising decision-making criteria.

Quality function development

Quality Function Deployment (QFD) is a structured method used to translate user needs into corresponding design specifications. It was originally developed by Akao42, QFD systematically captures WHATs (user needs) and maps them to HOWs (design features) within the familiar House of Quality matrix. QFD is very effective in complex design processes. For example, Chan and Wu’s review43 highlights its application in various design domains, showing that it improves customer satisfaction by aligning design specifications with customer expectations. In addition, QFD has been widely applied in industrial, software, and automotive design contexts to translate fuzzy user demands into concrete design strategies44, and recent work has further demonstrated the effectiveness of QFD in cross-domain design optimization.

In practice, QFD evaluates the relationship between user needs and design attributes in the form of a matrix (often referred to as House of Quality), thus enabling designers to capture these subtle emotional dimensions. Research process and results

The results presented here are derived from the methodological procedures detailed in Sect. 2.

New energy vehicle dashboard interface sample collection

In order to collect design samples of NEV dashboard interfaces that meet users’ emotional needs, this study first used key terms such as new energy vehicle dashboard and smart cockpit interface design as search criteria. Samples were collected from multiple sources, including the internet, academic journals, and industry reports. The initial dataset included approximately 150 interface design images and data from internationally renowned design firms, automotive manufacturers, and industry design forums. These samples covered various styles and functional modules, ensuring representativeness of the existing dashboard interface designs.

To avoid emotional bias caused by poor image quality or outdated designs, a further screening process was conducted, narrowing the dataset to 80 interface samples. These samples were selected based on their prominent emotional appeal, high user ratings, or detailed design features. This process involved multiple rounds of discussion and evaluation with design experts to ensure the selected samples were highly representative in terms of emotional expression and user experience.

Finally, based on comprehensive evaluations of interface smoothness, visual hierarchy, and information presentation details, the most representative 30 design samples were selected from the screened dataset. These samples serve as the core dataset for this study, providing essential data for subsequent emotional factor analysis and QFD model construction.

Collections of Kansei words in new energy vehicle dashboard interfaces

In the Kansei word collection phase, this study first conducted surveys (Step 1 in Sect. 2.1) to gather emotional vocabulary related to NEVs dashboard interfaces. The questionnaire included open-ended questions and multiple-choice questions to comprehensively capture users’ emotional experiences and preferences regarding dashboards. A total of 300 valid responses were collected, and 56 initial Kansei words were extracted from the feedback. These words were evaluated for relevance and semantic clarity, and ambiguous or irrelevant words were removed, resulting in 32 candidate Kansei words.

To further enhance the representativeness and scientific validity of the vocabulary, 6 experts in the field (3 females and 3 males, each with over 3 years of design experience or relevant academic background) were invited to evaluate the candidate words. Through expert discussions and feedback, 15 of the most representative Kansei words were finalized. This systematic, well-organized selection process ensured that the final 15 Kansei terms were both representative of user feedback and vetted by domain experts, providing a solid foundation for subsequent design optimization.

Dimension reduction of Kansei words in exploratory factor analysis

Based on the analysis, the 15 most representative Kansei words were identified: Techy, Future, Simple, Edgy, Novelty, Smart, Natural, Cozy, Effective, Warm, Fresh, Grand, Stylish, Aesthetic, Useful.

To comprehensively understand the alignment between these Kansei words and NEV dashboard interface designs, a questionnaire survey was conducted using a random sampling method with 101 NEVs users (Step 2 in Sect. 2.1). The sample structure included three age groups: 18–30 years (n = 52, 51.5%), 31–49 years (n = 36, 35.6%), and 50 years and above (n = 13, 12.9%). A total of 101 questionnaires were distributed, and 98 valid responses were collected, yielding a response rate of 97%.

The survey used a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree) to rate each Kansei word. The purpose was to assess users’ agreement levels with the 15 Kansei words and their relevance to NEVs dashboard design samples. To ensure objectivity, the average score for each Kansei word across all samples was calculated through statistical analysis. Forms analysis of UI components in NEV meter dashboards

When conducting form analysis of user interface components in NEV dashboards, it is essential to consider both functional and aesthetic factors. This ensures that the components not only enhance usability but also evoke emotional resonance that aligns with user preferences.

Selection of indicators and basis for analysis

The selection of key indicators for form analysis of NEV dashboards was grounded in industry standards, prior research on human-machine interface design in the automotive sector, and principles of Kansei Engineering. Selected indicators, such as overall color, shape, speed display mode, speed icon color, position, energy display mode, energy display color, advanced driver assistance display mode, turn signal indicator color, mileage display mode, and overall layout, were chosen based on their impact on both functional usability and aesthetic appeal. These choices aim to capture aspects that influence users’ emotional and experiential responses while aligning with Kansei words such as Technological and Stylish.

By deconstructing the visual forms of dashboards from a dataset of 30 NEVs, this study analyzed the relationship between dashboard design, user emotional needs, and information transmission efficiency. Key features affecting user experience were extracted from visual elements, which were broadly categorized into four types: digital information, gauge-based information, icons, and schematic diagrams. Based on these categories, the dashboard was divided into ten design features: overall color, overall shape, speed display mode, speed icon color, speed display position, energy display mode, energy display color, advanced driver assistance display mode, turn signal indicator color, mileage display mode, and overall layout structure.

Since each design feature has varying degrees of influence on user experience and perception, a product semantics approach was employed to explore user needs. Initially, user needs were gathered through experiments. The collected data were organized and cleaned to extract design-related keywords from user semantic expressions. These keywords were then transformed and mapped into specific design objectives.

The selection of these specific indicators was informed by user-centered design principles, feedback from automotive HMI research, and guidelines in interface aesthetics and usability. Furthermore, related design theories and Kansei analysis literature provided insights into which UI elements most significantly affect users’ emotional and functional interactions with NEV dashboards.

The group’s task was to evaluate various dashboard samples to determine how each design feature influenced user perception and satisfaction. Through a series of discussions, the experts reviewed the effectiveness of the selected indicators in delivering an ideal user experience, particularly those associated with Kansei attributes.

QFD to Establish mapping models for user’s emotions

Since QFD effectively establishes a mapping model between user emotional imagery and design elements, this study applied QFD to link drivers’ emotional needs with the design features of automotive dashboards. Based on the weight values of Kansei words calculated using EWM, the study introduced the weights of pleasantness, technological appeal, fashionability, and simplicity into the user requirements side of the QFD House of Quality. Next, 10 experts in relevant fields were invited to participate in the evaluation, including five graduate students specializing in related disciplines and five automotive designers. The experts scored the alignment between different design features in QFD and user emotional needs based on predefined evaluation criteria. The scoring system assigned 3 points for weak correlations, 5 points for moderate correlations, and 7 points for strong correlations. Design practice

The results of this study show that in the design of new energy vehicle dashboards, the key to satisfying users’ emotional needs lies in highlighting the core elements of different emotional dimensions. It should be noted that the mapping between emotional dimensions and dashboard features was not determined subjectively, but derived from the weighted contribution values calculated in the QFD analysis. Each feature could contribute to multiple emotional dimensions, and the prioritization was established based on their aggregated weights rather than a one-to-one assignment. Among them, the sense of pleasantness, as the core emotional demand of dashboard design, emphasises the sense of harmony of colours, materials and overall layout; followed by the sense of technology, which is mainly reflected through innovative display technologies and simple interactive interfaces; the sense of fashion is presented through elements such as shapes and icon styles; and the sense of simplicity is embodied in the design in terms of the simplicity of the structure and functions.

In actual design practice, the QFD method helped this study to perform sentiment mapping and weighting analysis of multiple dashboard components, such as dashboard color schemes, speed display formats, background pattern, light brightness and so on. As shown in Fig. 4 in Sect. 3.6, these associations were derived from the weighted contribution values of the QFD matrix. Each feature may contribute to multiple emotional dimensions, and the final prioritization was determined by their absolute weights, ensuring transparency and reproducibility of the mapping process. The design features with the highest weights were prioritised. For example, ‘light blue’ scored the highest in the colour design to create a sense of pleasantness, while the ICON + scale + number combination mode of the speed display scored high in the technological sense dimension.

In the process of design implementation, the choice of colour and material is carried through the entire dashboard design to convey a sense of pleasantness; the speed display adopts an innovative digital display combined with icon style to present a sense of technology; the interface icon design conveys a sense of fashion through a simple and modern style; the overall layout adopts a minimalist style to ensure an intuitive and smooth user experience. EWM calculates the weight of each emotion word through the discrete degree of the data itself, so it can reduce the interference of human factors on weight allocation and improve the scientificity of data analysis. In the case of complex and diverse emotional imagery, EWM can effectively identify highly relevant emotional words and assign corresponding weights to them, so that in the subsequent design process, it can focus on the emotional needs that users are most concerned about and ensure that the hierarchical structure of the emotion mapping model is reasonable and clear.

However, EWM also has certain limitations. Because it is based on data distribution characteristics, if the frequency distribution of emotion words is more uniform, it may lead to insignificant differences in weights, which cannot fully highlight the priority of specific emotions. Therefore, in future research, the introduction of other multi-indicator weighting methods can be considered and combined with EWM to enhance the adaptability of the model.

As a systematic method of transforming user emotion into design elements, KE has unique advantages compared with other emotional design methods. For example, KE has quantified emotional vocabulary through semantic difference method and multidimensional scaling analysis, while TRIZ focuses more on solving technical problems, and it is difficult to directly map abstract emotions. Moreover, the KE method is able to capture the potential emotional needs of users, while methods such as AHP are more suitable for simple evaluation in the conceptual design stage.

However, the limitations of KE method cannot be ignored, shown in Table 11. For example, its traditional analysis process relies on expert knowledge, is more subjective, and is inefficient in processing when the data amount is large. In order to solve these problems, artificial intelligence technologies such as artificial neural networks can be introduced in the future to realize the efficient processing and accurate mapping of emotional image data.

Conclusions

In this study, the main emotional and functional needs of NEVs users were first identified through a comprehensive survey and exploratory factor analysis. It was found that drivers valued the pleasantness, technology, stylishness and simplicity of the dashboard interface. Building on these insights, this study employed the EWM in combination with the QFD model to establish a mapping framework between user emotional imagery and design elements. This framework assigns objective importance weights to each emotional dimension and maps those priorities onto specific design features, ensuring that user feelings directly shape the interface. Finally, the constructed research framework is utilized to propose specific design recommendations. For example, in this case, light blue color scheme enhances the sense of pleasure, while the icon-scale-number hybrid speed display strengthens the perception of advanced technology. Expert evaluations confirm that these choices can raise driver satisfaction and promote sustainable use.

By combining EWM and QFD, this research overcomes the subjectivity of traditional methods (e.g., AHP, TRIZ) and offers a scalable, data-driven approach for emotion-driven interface design. The framework not only fills a gap in user-centered dashboard development for NEVs but also provides actionable guidelines for other domains where emotional experience is critical.

Despite these advances, relying on questionnaire data from a single cohort may affect the generalizability of our findings. Future work will refine our emotional assessment instruments, expand the participant pool to enhance statistical validity. Further efforts will serve to explore machine-learning techniques to automate and scale the emotion-to-design mapping process. By systematically relating design choices to quantified user emotions, this work paves the way for more engaging, user-centric vehicle interfaces, and reminds us that technology truly excels when it resonates with how people feel.
