Post‐Omicron cognitive decline is largely reversible: Two‐year multicenter cohort study of domain‐specific outcomes in hospitalized patients
Authors: Min Qiu, Mengwen Wang, Shengcai Chen, Wenbo Zuo, Jiashuo Lin, Luojinyun Wang, Senwei Tan, Quanwei He, Yanan Li, Huijuan Jin, Xuan Niu, Wanli Li, Fengguang Li, Ming Huang, Yan Wan, Bo Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive impairment represents a significant concern within the spectrum of post‐acute sequelae of Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) infection, particularly impacting the aging population. This study aimed to assess the incidence of reversible and irreversible cognitive decline after Omicron infection, identify changes in specific cognitive domains, and determine the risk factors for progressive cognitive decline.MethodsIn this multicenter longitudinal cohort, 3419 patients diagnosed with Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID‐19) between December 2022 and March 2023 from nine hospitals in China were enrolled. Cognitive function was assessed at 6 months and 2 years post‐infection using the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE) and Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status‐40 (TICS‐40). Domain‐specific performance was analyzed, and logistic regression was used to identify risk factors.ResultsIn total, 2087 patients with COVID‐19 completed both the 6‐month and 2‐year follow‐up (1172 [56.2%] men; 915 [43.8%] women; median age 63.0 years [interquartile range]: 53.0–71.0) respectively. Two years after discharge from the hospital, 67.1% (1401/2087) of survivors maintained stable cognitive function, 22.0% (460/2087) of survivors experienced reversible cognitive decline, and 10.8% (226/2087) experienced irreversible cognitive decline (3.6% [76/2087] showed progressive cognitive decline). The profile of recovery or deterioration in cognitive domains involved attention/calculation, executive function, delayed recall, registration, and learning, all of which are multi‐dimensional. Age ≥60 years (OR = 4.31, 95% CI [1.47, 12.65]), reinfection (OR = 2.12, 95% CI [1.33, 3.38]), and vaccination doses (OR = 0.69, 95% CI [0.55, 0.87]) were associated with progressive decline.ConclusionPost‐Omicron cognitive decline is largely reversible, with significant recovery observed in key domains including attention/calculation, executive function, delayed recall, registration, and learning. However, a vulnerable subset with risk factors such as older adults, reinfection, and inadequate vaccination, is at risk for progressive cognitive decline. These findings highlight the necessity of targeted long‐term monitoring and early intervention for high‐risk populations.

INTRODUCTION

Since the onset of the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID‐19) outbreak, over 700 million people worldwide have developed confirmed infection with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus type 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2). 1 Although initially characterized as a respiratory illness, COVID‐19 demonstrates neurologic manifestations in 36.4% to 82% of affected patients, which may extend beyond the acute phase. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 The term “long COVID” refers to the persistence of COVID‐19‐related clinical symptoms beyond the acute phase of infection, commonly including fatigue, shortness of breath, and cognitive dysfunction. 7 , 8 , 9 The cognitive component, frequently described as “brain fog,” encompasses deficits in attention, memory, receptive language, and executive functioning, and has been observed in a substantial proportion of COVID‐19 survivors. 10 , 11 , 12

Cognitive impairment following SARS‐CoV‐2 infection has been widely reported and is recognized as a major long‐term sequela of COVID‐19. 12 , 13 , 14 The pathophysiological mechanisms of cognitive impairment following SARS‐CoV‐2 infection are multifactorial and not fully understood. Proposed contributors include direct viral neuroinvasion (e.g., via the olfactory bulb or bloodstream), sustained neuroinflammation (microglial activation, cytokine release), blood‐brain barrier disruption, cerebrovascular endothelial injury, hypercoagulability with microthrombi, and chronic hypoxia. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 These factors may act synergistically. Reversible deficits may result from the resolution of acute neuroinflammation and cytokine activity. 19 SARS‐CoV‐2 infection can alter brain structure, activate glial cells, induce neuroinflammation, and even cause regional brain atrophy, leading to irreversible cognitive decline. 18 , 20

A Post‐Hospitalization COVID‐19 study reported that 52.1% of survivors experienced subjective cognitive decline during 2–3 years of follow‐up, with the rate reaching 65% among those aged ≥65 years. 21 Another 2‐year follow‐up study reported an incidence of cognitive impairment ranging from 29.3% to 34.3%. 22 However, these studies primarily focused on individuals infected with the original virus strain or the Alpha and Delta variants, which appear to be associated with greater cognitive deficits. 23 Given the attenuated virulence and lower rates of severe disease associated with Omicron and its sublineages, 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 it remains unclear whether long‐term cognitive symptoms caused by earlier variants are generalizable to patients infected with Omicron.

The outcomes of COVID‐19‐related cognitive dysfunction exhibit significant heterogeneity. At 30 months, 8% of severe cases showed cognitive improvement, 28 and at a median follow‐up of 26 months after infection, 57% of patients with post‐COVID cognitive deficits had recovered. 29 However, cognitive deficits may also persist or progressively worse, with 19.7% of severe cases experiencing progressive decline at 30 months, 28 and persistent cognitive deficits equivalent to a 10‐year age difference remained nearly 2 years post‐infection. 30 Cognitive dysfunction can affect multiple core domains, including attention, executive function, memory, processing speed, and learning ability. 23 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 Although prior studies have examined trajectories of cognitive outcomes, the incidence of reversible and irreversible cognitive decline among individuals infected with the Omicron variant, as well as the cognitive domains most susceptible to recovery or persistent deterioration, remain to be determined.

Therefore, this study aims to conduct a 24‐month longitudinal cohort study of hospitalized patients infected during the Omicron‐predominant period, using validated cognitive assessment tools, to achieve the following objectives: (1) to characterize the longitudinal trajectories of cognitive function post Omicron variant infection; (2) to quantify the incidence of reversible and irreversible cognitive decline; (3) to identify specific cognitive domains associated with recovery or deterioration; and (4) to explore risk factors for progressive cognitive decline. We hypothesized that, compared with earlier variants, cognitive decline following Omicron infection would be reversible in the majority of patients.

METHODS

Participants

This multicenter longitudinal cohort study was conducted across nine medical institutions in China: Wuhan Union Hospital, the Wuhan Union Hospital Chegu Campus, the Wuhan Union Hospital Jinyinhu Campus, the People's Hospital of Dongxihu District, the Hubei Provincial Hospital of Integrated Chinese and Western Medicine, Hubei General Hospital, Wuhan Puren Hospital, the First People's Hospital of Jiangxia District, Wuhan, and Wuhan Red Cross Hospital. The study protocols received approval from the institutional review boards of all participating hospitals. Since this study was conducted via telephone interviews, written informed consent was waived. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to participation. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies in its design and reporting.

This study enrolled patients hospitalized with COVID‐19 between December 1, 2022, and March 31, 2023. Patients with confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection using reverse transcription‐polymerase chain reactions or serological testing were included. Criteria for inclusion comprised: (1) identification of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA in nasopharyngeal or oropharyngeal swabs through real‐time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction; (2) the timing of patients’ SARS‐CoV‐2 infection was within 1 week of hospital admission; (3) self‐reported absence of prior infection history together with no previous positive nucleic acid test record; and (4) consent to participate in the research. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) inability to comprehend questionnaire items, or communication barriers due to linguistic or auditory limitations; (2) clinically diagnosed or documented in medical records any type of cognitive impairment, including but not limited to Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, frontotemporal dementia, and dementia with Lewy bodies. (The cognitive impairment was defined as TICS‐40 ≤20, which was conducted prior to study inclusion.); (3) first‐degree familial history of dementia; (4) comorbid neurological conditions with potential cognitive impact; or (5) severe cardiovascular, hepatic, or renal dysfunction, or any malignant neoplasms. The study adhered to the 1975 Helsinki Declaration and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No. 20200052).

Clinical data collection

Trained physicians collected data on factors related to COVID‐19 infection from electronic hospital records (nursing and medical records, consulting notes) or a knowledgeable family member for each participant. Demographic information comprised age, sex, education level, body mass index (BMI). Comorbidities included: hypertension, type 2 diabetes, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), liver disease, and chronic kidney disease. COVID‐19‐related neurologic symptoms during the hospitalization were documented based on the frequently reported manifestations in previous literature, including delirium, brain fog, headache, dysgeusia, anosmia, and fatigue. Moreover, other characteristics comprised length of inpatient stay, severity of COVID‐19, and intensive care unit admission in hospital. Blood test results included neutrophil counts, lymphocyte counts, C‐reactive protein (CRP), and D‐dimer, and neuroimaging assessments encompassed brain atrophy, lacunar infarction, and white matter lesions. All participating centers adopted standardized procedures to ensure consistency of laboratory test results across the multicenter study. The severe cases were defined by the American Thoracic Society guidelines for community‐acquired pneumonia. 36 Reinfection with SARS‐CoV‐2 was characterized by a positive lab outcome occurring a minimum of 90 days following initial confirmation of infection.

Cognitive evaluations

The survey was conducted at both 6 months and 24 months post‐patient recruitment via telephone interviews to collect data on vaccination doses, reinfection, and cognitive scales assessment. The telephone cognitive assessments were conducted by trained raters (Min Qiu, Mengwen Wang, Wenbo Zuo, and Jiashuo Lin). Cognitive status was evaluated using the Chinese version of the Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status‐40 (TICS‐40), 37 a validated tool comprising 10 variables with a maximum score of 40. A score ≤20 indicated mild cognitive impairment (MCI), while a score ≤12 indicated dementia. The TICS‐40 assessed cognitive domains encompassing orientation, registration, attention and calculation, comprehension, language, and delayed recall. Cognitive changes over the first 6 months and across the 24‐month follow‐up were evaluated by the Chinese version of the short form Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE), 38 a 16‐item scale assessing changes in memory and other cognitive functions, particularly learning and executive function. Cognitive decline was defined as a mean IQCODE score ≥3.5. 39 Clinically meaningfully cognitive decline between 6 months and 24 months post‐infection were defined as a decrease of 3 or more points decrease of TICS‐40, while an increase of 3 or more points of TICS‐40 was defined as cognitive improvement. The association between IQCODE and TICS‐40 scores was analyzed to ensure the consistency of the 2 cognitive assessments verified in previous studies.

Longitudinal cognitive changes were classified into four categories. Stable cognition was defined as an IQCODE score <3.5 at 6 months and a change in TICS‐40 scores within −2 to 2 points between 6 and 24 months. Reversible cognitive decline was defined as an IQCODE score ≥3.5 at 6 months and an increase in TICS‐40 scores of ≥3 points between 6 and 24 months. Progressive cognitive decline was defined as an IQCODE score ≥3.5 at 6 months and a decrease in TICS‐40 scores of ≥3 points between 6 and 24 months, whereas non‐progressive cognitive decline was defined as an IQCODE score ≥3.5 at 6 months and a change in TICS‐40 scores within −2 to 2 points during the same period. These two categories were collectively defined as irreversible cognitive decline.

Statistical analysis

Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene's test were used to assess normality and homogeneity of continuous variables, respectively. Continuous variables with normal distributions were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using t‐tests or one‐way ANOVA. Non‐normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using Mann–Whitney U tests or Kruskal–Wallis tests. Categorical variables were reported as numbers (percentages) and compared using χ 2 tests or Fisher's exact test. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify risk factors for progressive cognitive decline. A two‐tailed p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (Version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.r-project.org/) for Windows.

RESULTS

Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants

A total of 3419 participants were screened; 2087 met the eligibility criteria for analysis, 462 were excluded, 531 were lost to follow‐up, and 339 had died. Ultimately, 2087 COVID‐19 survivors (1172 male [56.2%]; median [IQR] age, 63.0 [53.0–71.0] years) completed both the 6‐month and 2‐year follow‐ups (Figure 1). Based on IQCODE and TICS‐40 scores at these time points, participants were categorized into three groups: stable cognition, reversible cognitive decline, and irreversible cognitive decline. The latter was further subdivided into progressive and non‐progressive cognitive decline. Stable cognition was observed in 67.1% (1401/2087) of participants, while 22.0% (460/2087) exhibited reversible cognitive decline. Non‐progressive and progressive cognitive decline were present in 7.2% (150/2087) and 3.6% (76/2087) of participants, respectively (Table 1).

Median length of inpatient stay was longest in the progressive group, followed by the reversible group, with no difference between the stable and non‐progressive groups (14 vs. 10 vs. 7 vs. 7 days). Indicators of COVID‐19 severity during the acute phase of infection, including the rate of severe case, ICU admission, and delirium, were associated with poorer cognitive outcomes. The rate of severe case in the progressive group was threefold higher than in the stable group (34.2% [26/76] vs. 11.6% [163/1401]), and ICU admissions were 10‐fold more frequent (9.2% [7/76] vs. 0.6% [9/1401]). Delirium incidence increased progressively across the groups, from 1.2% (17/1401) in the stable group to 11.8% (9/76) in the progressive group. Brain fog was most frequently reported in the non‐progressive group (15.3% [23/150]) and least frequently in the stable group (4.1% [57/1401]). Headache (19.3% [89/460] vs. 9.1% [128/1401]) and fatigue (22.6% [104/460] vs. 15.6% [218/1401]) were most common in the reversible group and least common in the stable group. The highest prevalence of dysgeusia was observed in the reversible group (11.1% [51/460]) and the lowest in the progressive group (3.9% [3/76]). Similarly, anosmia was most prevalent in the reversible group (6.1% [28/460]) and least prevalent in the non‐progressive group (2.0% [3/150]). Finally, the progressive group had the lowest median number of vaccination doses (1.5 vs. 3) and a higher number of infections (median [IQR]: 1 [1–2] vs. 1 [1–1]) compared to the other groups. Compared to the reversible cognitive decline group, the irreversible group not only had higher rates of severe illness (25.2% [57/226] vs. 14.8% [68/460]) and ICU admission, but also a higher incidence of delirium, brain fog, and dysgeusia during hospitalization.

Baseline hematological indices (neutrophil and lymphocyte counts) were within normal limits and comparable across the four groups. In contrast, markers of coagulation and inflammation showed heterogeneity: median D‐dimer levels were highest in the progressive group and lowest in the non‐progressive group (0.82 vs. 0.57 mg/L FEU), and CRP levels were highest in the progressive group and lowest in the reversible group (30.20 vs. 23.15 mg/L). Neuroimaging revealed that structural brain abnormalities (lacunar infarcts, brain atrophy, white matter lesions) were significantly associated with unfavorable cognitive outcomes, with detection rates in the progressive group 1.9‐ to 3.0‐fold higher than in the stable group. Patients in the irreversible cognitive decline group had significantly higher rates of brain atrophy (56.6% [128/226] vs. 27.6% [127/460]) and white matter lesions (41.2% [93/226] vs. 18.3% [84/460]) compared to the reversible cognitive decline group.

Changes in cognitive domains assessed by TICS‐40 and IQCODE during a 24‐month follow‐up after COVID‐19 infection

At 6 months, the median IQCODE score was highest in the non‐progressive group compared with the reversible, progressive, and stable groups (3.72 vs. 3.69 vs. 3.62 vs. 3.00). By 24 months, cognitive function in the reversible group had improved (3.26 vs. 3.69), with a 0.43‐point decrease in IQCODE score, reflecting an 11.7% improvement. In contrast, the progressive group demonstrated continued decline (3.88 vs. 3.62), with a 0.26‐point increase in IQCODE score, indicating a 7.2% worsening. Similarly, at 6 months, the stable group had the highest TICS‐40 score relative to the other groups (26.46 vs. 21.00 vs. 16.00 vs. 17.00). By 24 months, cognitive trajectories had diverged: participants in the reversible group showed improvement (24.00 vs. 21.00), with a 3‐point increase in TICS‐40 score, corresponding to an 14.3% gain. In contrast, participants in the progressive group experienced a 4‐point decline (13.00 vs. 17.00), representing a 23.5% decrease from baseline.

At 6 months, the incidence of MCI was highest in the non‐progressive group compared with the reversible and progressive groups (80.0% [120/150] vs. 35.2% [162/460] vs. 64.5% [49/76]). Dementia incidence was highest in the progressive group (15.8% [12/76]), followed by the non‐progressive (13.3% [20/150]) and reversible groups (0.7% [3/460]). By 24 months, both MCI and dementia had resolved in the reversible group. In the non‐progressive group, the proportion of participants with MCI increased to 81.3% (122/150). In the progressive group, MCI prevalence decreased to 56.6% (43/76), but dementia rose to 40.8% (2.6‐fold increase, [31/76]) (Table 2).

Patients in the reversible cognitive decline group exhibited improvements at the 24‐month follow‐up in attention and calculation (50.0%), delayed recall (33.3%), registration (25.0%), orientation (16.7%), learning ability (18.3%), and executive function (11.8%). In contrast, patients in the irreversible cognitive decline group experienced declines in attention and calculation (50.0%), delayed recall (50.0%), registration (33.3%), learning ability (14.3%), and executive function (9.1%). Patients who experienced progressive cognitive decline deteriorate in attention and calculation (50.0%), delayed recall (50.0%), registration (33.3%), orientation (20.0%), executive function (12.5%), and learning ability (9.0%) (Tables 3 and 4). Both reversible and irreversible cognitive decline groups showed multiple cognitive domain impairment, but the distribution of affected domains differed between the two groups (Supporting Information S1: Tables S1 and S2).

Risk and protective factors for progressive cognitive decline in COVID‐19 patients 24 months after infection

Logistic regression models were employed to identify potential risk factors for post‐infection progressive cognitive decline in patients with COVID‐19. As shown in Table 5, age ≥60 years, per 1‐year increase in education, hypertension, coronary heart disease, COPD, chronic kidney disease, length of inpatient stays during acute COVID‐19, disease severity, ICU admission, delirium, number of vaccine doses, number of infections, brain atrophy, white matter lesions, and stroke during follow‐up were all associated with progressive cognitive decline in the univariable analysis. In the multivariable model, age ≥60 years (OR = 4.31, 95% CI [1.47, 12.65]), hypertension (OR = 2.32, 95% CI [1.15, 4.67]), longer inpatient stay during the acute phase (OR = 1.09, 95% CI [1.05, 1.13]), severe case (OR = 2.85, 95% CI [1.28, 6.38]), a greater number of infections (OR = 2.12, 95% CI [1.33, 3.38]) and white matter lesions (OR = 2.22, 95% CI [1.02, 4.84]) were independently associated with an increased risk of progressive cognitive decline. Conversely, two factors demonstrated a protective effect against progressive cognitive decline: per 1‐year increase in education (OR = 0.91, 95% CI [0.84, 0.98]) and a higher number of vaccine doses received (OR = 0.69, 95% CI [0.55, 0.87]) (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we conducted a 24‐month follow‐up of hospitalized COVID‐19 patients during the predominance of the Omicron variant to assess changes in cognitive trajectories. We found that 22.0% of survivors experienced reversible cognitive decline, 10.8% experienced irreversible cognitive decline (including 3.6% with progressive decline), and the remaining 67.1% maintained stable cognitive function. By contrast, Su et al. 40 reported a 15.2% prevalence of cognitive impairment 52 days following Omicron infection in a community‐based cohort. This lower incidence may be attributed to the shorter follow‐up duration, as well as the milder acute symptoms in the community population and the relatively weaker neuroinvasive potential of the virus. Another large‐scale online survey involving 74,075 participants in China reported that 20.05% experienced memory decline and 10.81% experienced brain fog at 12 months after Omicron infection. 41 The incidence of cognitive impairment was lower than the 32.9% observed in our study, a difference that may be attributable to the fact that our subjects were all hospitalized patients with more severe COVID‐19 and a higher burden of comorbidities. Furthermore, Wee et al.'s 42 electronic health‐record cohort study in Singapore demonstrated a 24% elevated risk of cognitive or memory disorders at 300 days after Omicron infection. And a retrospective cohort studies including 1284437 patients demonstrated that the risk of dementia among Omicron‐infected individuals increased by 48% over a follow‐up of more than 140 days. 43 Moreover, the cognitive domains most improved or deteriorated were attention and calculation, delayed recall, registration, learning ability, and executive function. It is worth noting that different types of cognitive impacts are multi‐dimensional. These findings offer a more multidimensional understanding of post‐COVID cognitive impairment.

Encouragingly, from the 6‐month to the 24‐month follow‐up, approximately two‐thirds of hospitalized patients with post‐COVID cognitive impairment demonstrated a trend toward recovery to normal cognitive status, suggesting that the adverse cognitive effects of Omicron variant infection may be relatively limited and potentially reversible. Compared with the 30‐month follow‐up study conducted by Liu et al., 28 the incidence of cognitive impairment among individuals aged 60 years or older at 24 months following Omicron variant infection in the present study was comparable to that of the control group (13.9% vs. 14.2%), yet significantly lower than that observed in patients infected with the original variant (13.9% vs. 19.1%), suggesting that Omicron infection may not confer an increased risk of long‐term cognitive deficits. Notably, long‐term follow‐up results demonstrated that the proportion of patients exhibiting cognitive improvement was substantially higher in the Omicron group than in both the ancestral strain group (21.7% vs. 2.2%), coupled with a lower rate of progressive cognitive decline (5.7% vs. 10.7%). 28 These findings may, to some extent, alleviate concerns among recovering patients and clinicians. The cognitive trajectory heterogeneity observed in this study likely reflects the unique biological properties of the Omicron variant and the complexity of host immune responses. Relative to earlier variants, Omicron demonstrates attenuated virulence. 24 , 25 , 27 , 44 Diminished spike protein cleavage efficiency and consequent reduced membrane fusion may limit direct viral neuroinvasion and neural injury. 25 Animal studies in hACE2‐transgenic mice have further shown that Omicron produces milder pulmonary infection, lower viral burdens, and less central nervous system damage compared with Delta and ancestral strains. 26 These findings provide a biological basis for the high rates of cognitive stability and recovery observed in our cohort.

Older age, lower education level, and hypertension are recognized risk factors for dementia. 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 The findings showed no significant association between neurological symptoms during the acute phase and long‐term progressive cognitive decline. It differed from earlier research on infection with the original strain, which suggested that acute‐phase symptoms such as headache, anosmia, et. al. might increase the risk of long‐term cognitive impairment. 28 , 50 The observed difference might indicate that acute neurological damage caused by the Omicron variant is more likely to be reversible. In addition, consistent with previous studies, longer hospitalization was identified as an independent risk factor for persistent cognitive impairment, 51 which typically reflects a more severe infection. Similarly, vaccination reduced the risk of progressive cognitive decline by 29%. A large community cohort study confirmed that participants received two or more vaccinations had a small advantage in cognitive advantage. 23 Vaccination could prevent SARS‐CoV‐2 induced cognitive impairment that derived by IL‐1β. 52 The impact of repeated COVID‐19 infections on the risk of long‐term cognitive decline is an area that has been less extensively studied. In our cohort, 11.5% of participants experienced multiple COVID‐19 infections. A multicenter retrospective cohort study in Iran reported an overall reinfection rate of 122.64 per 1000 person‐years among patients with a history of COVID‐19 hospitalization, which is in line with our study. 53 Study revealed that reinfection contributed onefold risks of death, twofold risks of hospitalization and 60% risks of neurological disorders at 6‐month follow‐up. 54 The present study showed that one additional infection increased 1.12‐fold risk of progressive cognitive decline. The mechanisms underlying the increased risks of cognitive decline in reinfection are not completely clear. However, viruses can directly infect and damage brain microvascular endothelial cells, resulting in BBB damage and brain dysfunction. 18 Multiple viral attacks can exacerbate this damage. Additionally, cognitive impairment may heighten reinfection risk due to patient negligence, creating a bidirectional relationship that accelerates cognitive deterioration.

Our study further found that the cognitive domains most improved in cases of reversible decline were attention and calculation, delayed recall, registration, learning ability, and executive function. In contrast, Vakani et al. 55 reported significant improvement in processing speed and executive function at 6 months in a cohort of 88 COVID‐19 patients, although attention continued to decline. Ferrucci et al. 56 reported significant improvements in verbal memory, attention, and processing speed after 1 year. These discrepant findings may be attributable to variations in study populations, follow‐up duration, and viral strains. Reversible cognitive decline may stem from functional neurological disturbances rather than structural damage. Acute systemic inflammation can transiently disrupt neurotransmitter homeostasis, synaptic function, and regional cerebral metabolism, particularly in the prefrontal‐parietal networks and hippocampus, which are critical for attention, memory, and executive function. 15 , 19 As inflammation subsides, these functional alterations may normalize, facilitating cognitive recovery. This hypothesis is supported by an 18F‐FDG PET study reporting significant improvement in frontoparietal and temporal hypometabolism 6 months after COVID‐19. 57

Furthermore, we found that irreversible or progressive cognitive decline primarily involved deterioration in attention and calculation, delayed recall, registration, executive function, and learning ability. Neuroimaging examinations of post‐COVID syndrome performed 11 months after the first symptoms of SARS‐CoV‐2 reported hypoconnectivity between left and right parahippocampal areas, potentially underlying persistent deficits in memory and learning. 58 A meta‐analysis of 27 studies reported that post‐COVID‐19 cognitive impairment predominantly affects executive function, attention, and memory. 59 One study found that 27% of patients with long COVID exhibited cognitive deficits in attention, working memory, processing speed, and verbal fluency, which were associated with abnormal changes in white matter regions. 32 In contrast, irreversible and progressive cognitive decline likely reflects more severe, sustained neuropathological injury. Persistent neuroinflammation, blood brain barrier disruption, cerebrovascular endothelial damage, and microthrombotic events may collectively contribute to neuronal injury and loss. 58 , 60 These pathological processes are particularly detrimental in individuals with pre‐existing vulnerabilities such as advanced age and hypertension, who have diminished cerebrovascular reserve and heightened susceptibility to endothelial injury, thereby increasing the risk of white matter damage and microinfarcts that perpetuate cognitive deterioration. 15 , 20 Our finding that patients with these risk factors exhibit a significantly higher likelihood of progressive decline aligns with this mechanistic framework. Furthermore, reinfection may exert cumulative effects on the brain through repeated immune activation and vascular injury, thereby accelerating cognitive decline. 54 This study did not conduct animal model experiments to validate the observed clinical phenomena and underlying mechanisms. Existing literature has demonstrated in K18‐hACE2 mice, mouse‐adapted SARS‐CoV‐2‐infected mice, and golden hamster models that SARS‐CoV‐2 infection can induce cognitive deficits through mechanisms such as neuroinflammation, kynurenine pathway metabolic abnormalities, Wnt/β‐catenin signaling inhibition, and blood‐brain barrier disruption. 61 , 62 , 63 Furthermore, persistent viral presence in the brainstem and dopaminergic neuron vulnerability have also been identified as key pathogenic factors. 62 , 64

This was a large‐sample, long‐term cohort study that included thousands of hospitalized COVID‐19 patients during the predominance of the Omicron variant and conducted a 24‐month follow‐up. Patients were evaluated repeatedly after SARS‐CoV‐2 infection to examine the dynamic changes in post‐COVID cognitive trajectories. This study has several important clinical implications. First, we found that cognitive decline following Omicron infection is largely reversible, with 67.1% of patients maintaining stable cognitive function and 22.0% showing improvement over the 2‐year follow‐up period. This reassuring finding may help alleviate undue anxiety among patients and clinicians regarding post‐COVID “brain fog.” For this population, routine cognitive screening may not be necessary, allowing healthcare resources to be prioritized for high‐risk groups. Second, we identified the core cognitive domains associated with recovery or deterioration, namely attention/calculation, delayed recall, learning ability, and executive function. These findings provide specific targets for the development of tailored cognitive rehabilitation programs. Third, we identified key risk factors for progressive cognitive decline, including age ≥60, reinfection, insufficient vaccination, severe case, prolonged hospitalization, lower educational attainment, hypertension, and white matter lesions. These factors facilitate early identification of vulnerable individuals in clinical practice who may require long‐term follow‐up and timely intervention, such as implementing more frequent cognitive monitoring for elderly patients with hypertension and a history of reinfection.

The results of our study should be interpreted with caution due to several limitations: (1) A notable limitation of our study is the absence of an uninfected control group. Consequently, this cohort study is optimized for describing longitudinal trajectories and associations rather than establishing strict causality. (2) Furthermore, due to the logistical constraints of long‐term follow‐up during a severe pandemic wave, cognitive evaluations heavily relied on telephone‐based instruments (TICS‐40 and IQCODE). While validated for screening, telephone modalities cannot fully substitute for comprehensive, in‐person neuropsychological testing, particularly for detecting subtle cognitive changes or processing speed deficits. The informant‐reliant nature of the IQCODE also introduces potential recall bias and caregiver expectation effects, which may modestly impact the precision of the documented cognitive scores. (3) Although the present study included a relatively long follow‐up period of 2 years compared with similar investigations, cognitive impairment is generally a chronic, progressive process. Research on conditions such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) often requires observation periods of 5–10 years. Therefore, longer follow‐up is still necessary to accurately determine the long‐term impact of COVID‐19 on cognitive function. (4) Cognitive trajectory classifications derived from cognitive scale assessments serve to approximate the longitudinal clinical trajectories observed within our cohort and should not be construed as definitive causal endpoints. (5) Due to the lack of animal model validation, this study cannot definitively elucidate the mechanisms underlying post‐COVID cognitive impairment. (6) Finally, while our sample is multicenter, the inclusion of primarily hospitalized patients may limit the strict generalizability of our findings to mild, community‐managed Omicron cases.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, post‐COVID‐19 cognitive decline after Omicron infection is largely reversible, with a 67.1% (460/686) recovery rate, indicating that routine cognitive screening may not be necessary, and resources could be optimized. Recovery or deterioration in core domains—attention and calculation, delayed recall, registration, learning, and executive function—determine cognitive outcomes, supporting targeted rehabilitation. Reinfection, lack of vaccination, severe infection, older adults, lower education, prolonged hospitalization, hypertension, and white matter lesions were linked to progressive decline, highlighting the need for early identification, targeted intervention, and long‐term follow‐up in vulnerable populations.
