Safety and efficacy of the feed additives cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate, cobalt(II) carbonate, cobalt(II) carbonate hydroxide (2:3) monohydrate and cobalt(II) sulphate heptahydrate for bovines with a functional rumen, equidae, lagomorphs, rodents, herbivore reptiles, zoo mammals and camelids (Regal B.V.)
Authors: EFSA Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed (FEEDAP), Roberto Edoardo Villa, Giovanna Azimonti, Eleftherios Bonos, Henrik Christensen, Mojca Durjava, Birgit Dusemund, Ronette Gehring, Boet Glandorf, Maryline Kouba, Marta López‐Alonso, Francesca Marcon, Carlo Nebbia, Alena Pechová, Miguel Prieto‐Maradona, Ilen Röhe, Katerina Theodoridou, Maria Bastos, Fernando Ramos, Georges Bories, Jürgen Gropp, Alberto Mantovani, Jaume Galobart, Anna Dioni, Orsolya Holczknecht, Fabiola Pizzo, Maria Vittoria Vettori, Alberto Navarro‐Villa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing a request from the European Commission, EFSA was asked to deliver a scientific opinion on the safety and efficacy of cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate, cobalt(II) carbonate, cobalt(II) carbonate hydroxide (2:3) monohydrate and cobalt(II) sulphate heptahydrate as nutritional feed additives for bovines with a functional rumen, equidae, lagomorphs, rodents, herbivore reptiles and zoo mammals and camelids. The additives are currently authorised in a provisional authorisation. The applicant has provided evidence that, in essence, the additives under assessment comply with the conditions of the authorisation. The Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed (FEEDAP Panel) confirms that the use of cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate, cobalt(II) carbonate, cobalt(II) carbonate hydroxide (2:3) monohydrate and cobalt(II) sulphate heptahydrate under the authorised conditions of use are safe for the target species and the environment. The same conclusions can be assumed for the use of the additives in camelids. Due to the lack of adequate deposition data to assess consumer exposure, the FEEDAP Panel is not in the position to conclude on the safety for the consumer. With regard to the safety of users, the additives under assessment should be considered eye and skin irritants and skin and respiratory sensitisers. Any exposure is considered a risk. The FEEDAP Panel concludes that cobalt compounds under assessment are efficacious sources of cobalt in feedingstuffs for bovines with a functional rumen, equidae, lagomorphs, rodents, herbivore reptiles, zoo mammals and camelids.

INTRODUCTION

Background and Terms of Reference

Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003 1 establishes the rules governing the Community authorisation of additives for use in animal nutrition. In particular, Article 4(1) of that Regulation lays down that any person seeking authorisation for a feed additive or for a new use of a feed additive shall submit an application in accordance with Article 7.

The European Commission received two requests from Regal B.V. 2 for the authorisation of the additives consisting of cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate, cobalt(II) carbonate, cobalt(II) carbonate hydroxide (2:3) monohydrate and cobalt(II) sulphate heptahydrate when used as feed additives for bovines with a functional rumen, equidae, lagomorphs, rodents, herbivore reptiles and zoo mammals 3 and camelids 4 (category: nutritional additives; functional group: compounds of trace elements).

According to Article 7(1) of Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003, the Commission forwarded the applications to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) as applications under Article 4(1) (authorisation of a feed additive or new use of a feed additive). EFSA received directly from the applicant the technical dossiers in support of these applications. The particulars and documents in support of the applications were considered valid by EFSA as of 23 March 2023 5 and 5 August 2024. 6

According to Article 8 of Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003, EFSA, after verifying the particulars and documents submitted by the applicant, shall undertake an assessment in order to determine whether the feed additive complies with the conditions laid down in Article 5. EFSA shall deliver an opinion on the safety for the target animals, consumer, user and the environment and on the efficacy of the feed additives consisting of cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate, cobalt(II) carbonate, cobalt(II) carbonate hydroxide (2:3) monohydrate and cobalt(II) sulphate heptahydrate, when used under the proposed conditions of use (see Section 3.1.6 ).

Additional information

Cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate (3b301), cobalt(II) carbonate (3b302), cobalt(II) carbonate hydroxide (2:3) monohydrate (3b303) and cobalt(II) sulphate heptahydrate (3b305) were authorised for use in feed for ruminants with a functional rumen, equidae, lagomorphs, rodents, herbivore reptiles and zoo mammals until 15 July 2023. No application for the renewal was submitted within the time limit indicated in art. 14 of regulation of 1831/2003. Consequently, cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate (3b301), cobalt(II) carbonate (3b302), cobalt(II) carbonate hydroxide (2:3) monohydrate (3b303) and cobalt(II) sulphate heptahydrate (3b305) were provisionally authorised for use in feed for ruminants with a functional rumen, equidae, lagomorphs, rodents, herbivore reptiles and zoo mammals. 7

EFSA issued several opinions on the safety of these compounds (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2009; EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012a, 2012b).

Conditions of use

The additive is intended to be used in feed via a premixture or complementary feed for ruminants with a functional rumen, equidae, lagomorphs, rodents, herbivore reptiles, zoo mammals and camelids up to the maximum total cobalt (Co) content in complete feed of 1 mg/kg complete feed. 41 This maximum cobalt content is in line with the current authorisation. 42

Safety

The safety of the additives under assessment has been assessed by the FEEDAP Panel in different opinions (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2009; EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012a, 2012b). In these assessments, the Panel concluded that the additives were safe when supplemented up to the maximum authorised level of cobalt in feed for the target animals, the consumers and the environment. The cobalt(II) compounds assessed were considered skin and eye irritants and dermal/inhalatory sensitisers. Exposure by inhalation must be avoided.

The FEEDAP Panel notes that following the above assessments, the additives were authorised for ruminants with a functional rumen, equidae, lagomorphs, rodents, herbivore reptiles and zoo mammals for 10 years, and that although the applicant did not submit an application for the renewal on time, they are still authorised with a provisional authorisation. Consequently, the Panel considers that the approach used for the renewal of the authorisation can be applied also in the case of the current assessment for the species for which an authorisation exists.

For the current assessment, the applicant provided (i) two extensive literature searches covering different aspects of the safety of the additive, 43 (ii) an extensive literature search 44 aimed at providing information on the levels of cobalt in milk and tissues from dairy cows and cattle and (iii) additional information related to the safety of the additive for the user 45 and for the environment. 46

Extensive literature searches (ELS)

Two literature searches to support the safety for the target species, consumer, user and the environment were provided. The first literature search 47 was not further considered as it presented several limitations (only one database searched, limited search terms, poor reporting). The second ELS 48 followed the requirements described in the EFSA guidance on renewal for feed additives (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2021). The databases used were Scopus, Web of Science and the meta database of Wageningen University. 49 The search covered the period of 2010–2024 with no language restriction. The search terms included the cobalt compounds under assessment and terms relative to the safety of the additive for the target species, consumer, user and the environment. The literature search protocol followed for the inclusion and exclusion criteria applied for the screening process was clearly described. The search returned a total of 47 results of which four studies were considered relevant by the applicant. The FEEDAP Panel screened all of them, and those relevant for the current evaluation are quoted below in the corresponding section.

A third ELS was conducted 50 to search for information on the deposition of cobalt in milk and tissues from dairy cows and/or cattle. Three databases were used in the search (Scopus, Web of Science and the meta database of Wageningen University 51 ) covering the time period from 2009 to 2025. The search terms included cobalt (not restricted to the compounds under assessment) and terms relative to the safety of the additive in the related species as well as cobalt deposits in dairy cows, cattle and ruminants. The search protocol followed for the inclusion and exclusion criteria applied for the screening process was described. A total of 16 papers were identified by the applicant as relevant. The Panel screened all of them and considered that only one provided information relevant for the current assessment that is described in the consumer safety section below (Section 3.2.3).

Safety for the target species

In its previous opinions (FSA FEEDAP Panel, 2009; EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012a, 2012b), the Panel concluded that the different cobalt compounds under application were safe for the target species when used at the maximum authorised total cobalt content in complete feed, which at that time was 2 mg/kg complete feed. The current maximum authorised level of cobalt is 1 mg/kg complete feed.

The ELS did not identify any papers that would indicate an adverse effect of cobalt under the authorised conditions of use for the target species.

Considering the above, and the fact that the composition of the additives has not been modified since the previous authorisation, the FEEDAP Panel considers that the additives cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate, cobalt(II) carbonate, cobalt carbonate hydroxide (2:3) monohydrate and cobalt(II) sulphate heptahydrate remain safe for the target species (bovines with a functional rumen, equidae, lagomorphs, rodents, herbivore reptiles and zoo mammals) under the current authorised conditions (maximum content of total cobalt of 1 mg/kg complete feed). In addition, the Panel considers that these conclusions could be extended to camelids.

Safety for the consumer

The toxicological profile of cobalt and the consumer exposure were evaluated in the previous assessments (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2009; EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012a, 2012b). Cobalt(II) cations are considered genotoxic under in vitro and in vivo conditions and have CMR (carcinogen, mutagen and reproduction toxicant) properties. No data were available on the potential carcinogenicity of cobalt(II) following oral exposure either in humans or in experimental animals. However, oral exposure may potentially entail a number of adverse effects in humans (cardiac effects, effects on erythropoiesis, effects on thyroid, developmental effects and allergic dermatitis). For these threshold effects, the FEEDAP Panel developed a health‐based guidance value of 0.0016 mg/kg body weight (bw) per day for oral cobalt calculated based on the lowest observed adverse effects level (LOAEL) of about 1 mg/kg bw per day (based on polycythaemia) observed in a 22‐day study on volunteers and applying an uncertainty factor (UF) of 600. Based on the exposure calculation, no safety concern for the consumer was expected for threshold effects of oral cobalt (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012a).

None of the studies identified in ELS provided information that would lead to a modification of the health‐based guidance value of 0.0016 mg/kg bw per day as established in the previous FEEDAP opinion.

Reassessment of consumer exposure

The FEEDAP Panel notes that the dataset of deposition values used in the previous opinion (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012a) does not fully comply with the current requirements according to the EFSA FEEDAP Panel Guidance on consumer safety (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017a).

In the previous assessment (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012a), some deposition data of cobalt in animal tissues and products were derived from surveys rather than animal studies. These data were used for the exposure assessment, and the FEEDAP Panel concluded that no safety concern for the consumer was expected for threshold effects, considering that the exposure to cobalt was about 4–10 times lower than the health‐based guidance value established (0.0016 mg/kg bw per day).

Considering the limitations above, during the present assessment, the applicant was requested to provide additional deposition data in line with the requirements of the above‐mentioned guidance. No cobalt deposition studies were conducted, but an extensive literature search was performed (see Section 3.2.1), 52 aimed at providing information on the levels of cobalt in milk and edible tissues from dairy cows and cattle and/or other mammals, which were considered by the FEEDAP Panel as the main source of exposure of consumers to cobalt.

One paper (Akins et al., 2013) was considered relevant for the current assessment. Forty‐five primiparous and 45 multiparous Holstein dairy cows, 60 days before their expected calving, were used in a randomised complete block design study. The animals were randomly assigned to five treatment groups: (1) no dietary cobalt supplementation (control), (2) 25 mg/day of supplemental dietary cobalt from cobalt carbonate, (3) 25 mg/day of supplemental dietary cobalt from cobalt glucoheptonate, (4) 75 mg/day of supplemental dietary cobalt from cobalt glucoheptonate or (5) control diet plus 10 mg of vitamin B12 intramuscular/week. 53 The content of cobalt in milk was not affected by treatment (0.107 mg/L, control group vs. 0.105 mg/L cobalt from cobalt carbonate, 0.107 mg/L cobalt from cobalt glucoheptonate, 0.107 mg/L cobalt from cobalt glucoheptonate and 0.106 mg/L from vitamin B12 supplementation). The FEEDAP Panel noted this study presented some shortcomings: (a) the analyses to estimate the cobalt concentration in feed were conducted by two different laboratories, and the results obtained showed large numerical variability; (b) the control diet which was supposed to contain cobalt at the NRC requirements (0.11 mg/kg feed) contained instead much higher cobalt concentration (lactating diet: 1 or 1.9 mg/kg feed, depending on the laboratory performing the analysis); (c) the lowest cobalt concentration in the control diet, 1 mg/kg feed, corresponds to the current EU maximum recommended level; all the other treatments contained cobalt levels well above this concentration.

Despite these limitations, the FEEDAP Panel conducted the consumer exposure assessment using as input value the highest concentration value in milk of 0.107 mg/L in the control group from Akins et al. (2013) corresponding to the current maximum authorised use level (Table 5).

The limited data available would indicate that the exposure of consumers exceeds the health‐based guidance values in all population categories, highlighting a concern for consumer safety (Table 5). The FEEDAP Panel notes that these calculations did not consider exposure via food products from mammals other than milk (e.g. cattle, rabbits, horses). It is therefore likely that, when the additional exposure via food products from all animal species is considered, the health‐based guidance value would be further exceeded.

Considering the limitations described above, the Panel considers that additional data on cobalt deposition in tissues (muscle, liver, kidney, fat) and milk following the requirements of the guidance on consumer safety (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017a) are needed to allow a proper assessment of consumer exposure and a conclusion on consumer safety. Therefore, due to the lack of adequate data, the FEEDAP Panel cannot conclude on the safety of cobalt for the consumer, under the approved conditions of use. The same conclusion would apply to the extension of use in camelids.

Safety for the user

In previous opinions, the cobalt (II) compounds under assessment were considered skin and eye irritants and dermal and respiratory sensitisers. The FEEDAP Panel noted that their dust is a hazard to persons handling these substances and that exposure by inhalation must be avoided (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012a, 2012b).

No new experimental studies have been provided by the applicant on the safety of the user.

Based on the dusting potential measured values for the different compounds (see Section 3.1 ), the FEEDAP Panel considers that the exposure of users through inhalation is likely.

The FEEDAP Panel further notes that the Committee for Risk Assessment (RAC) of the European Chemical Agency (ECHA) issued an opinion on the scientific evaluation of occupational exposure limits for cobalt and inorganic cobalt compounds (ECHA, 2012). OELs of 0.0005 mg Co/m3 (0.5 μg Co/m3; respirable fraction) and 0.001 mg Co/m3 (1 μg Co/m3; inhalable fraction) have been identified. In addition, a notation for skin and respiratory sensitisation has been added.

Cobalt is classified under the Classification, Labelling and Packaging (CLP) Regulation 54 as ‘Carc. 1B’, ‘Muta. 2’, ‘Repr. 1B’, ‘Resp. Sens. 1’ and ‘Skin Sens. 1’.

Based on the information present in the safety data sheets (SDS), cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate and cobalt(II) sulphate heptahydrate are eye irritants while cobalt carbonate is a skin irritant. 55 Therefore, cobalt carbonate is also considered an eye irritant.

The FEEDAP Panel notes that cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate and cobalt(II) sulphate heptahydrate contain nickel, and cobalt(II) carbonate and cobalt(II) carbonate hydroxide (2:3) monohydrate may also contain nickel (see Section 3.1 ). The Directive (EU) 2022/43137 set an occupational exposure limit (OEL) of 0.01 and 0.05 mg/m3 for both respirable and inhalable fractions, respectively, as nickel meets the criteria for classification as carcinogenic (category 1A). Due to the presence of nickel, these should be considered as skin and respiratory sensitisers.

Therefore, the FEEDAP Panel concludes that the additives under assessment should be considered eye and skin irritants and skin and respiratory sensitisers. In addition, considering the classification for cobalt and nickel, any exposure of users to these compounds is considered a risk, and therefore, should be minimised.

Safety for the environment

In previous opinions, the FEEDAP Panel concluded that the use of cobalt from any source at the authorised maximum content in feed did not pose a risk to the environment (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012a, 2012b). It is noted that, at the time of these assessments, the maximum authorised dietary level of cobalt was 2 mg/kg complete feed.

The literature search conducted by the applicant did not provide data that would lead to the revision of the previous conclusions on the safety of cobalt compounds for the environment.

To assess the environmental risk of cobalt from the additive, the Panel compared the worst‐case predicted environmental concentrations (PECs), 56 calculated at the maximum authorised level of cobalt (1 mg Co/kg complete feed), with the natural background concentration considered as the 90th percentile value from the FOREGS database. If the predicted concentrations (PECs) fall below 10% of this value, no further risk assessment is needed.

The 90th percentile concentration of cobalt in soil based on 837 topsoil samples collected throughout Europe for the FOREGS survey was 17 mg Co/kg (FOREGS, 2005). 57

As regards the use of the additive in terrestrial farm animals, cattle for fattening were considered the worst‐case scenario and the PECsoil plateau, calculated with a DT50 of 1000 days, resulted to be 76 μg/kg. This value is well below 10% of the natural background concentration of cobalt in soil (1.7 mg Co/kg soil dry weight). Therefore, it is concluded that no concerns are expected for the environment.

Considering the above, the FEEDAP Panel concludes that the use of the additives, under the current authorised conditions of use, is safe for the environment and the same conclusion would apply to their use in camelids.

Efficacy

In its previous opinions, the FEEDAP Panel concludes that cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate, cobalt(II) carbonate, cobalt(II) carbonate hydroxy (2:3) monohydrate and cobalt(II) sulphate heptahydrate (among other cobalt compounds) were efficacious sources of cobalt for ruminants, horses and rabbits (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2012a, 2012b). The products are currently authorised for bovines with a functional rumen, equidae, lagomorphs, rodents, herbivore reptiles and zoo mammals. Considering that the current application did not request a modification of the conditions of use for these species, the Panel considers that the same conclusions could be retained and extended also to their use in camelids.

Post‐market monitoring

The FEEDAP Panel considers that there is no need for specific requirements for a post‐market monitoring plan other than those established in the Feed Hygiene Regulation 58 and good manufacturing practice.

CONCLUSIONS

The FEEDAP Panel concludes that the additives cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate, cobalt(II) carbonate, cobalt(II) carbonate hydroxide (2:3) monohydrate and cobalt(II) sulphate heptahydrate are safe for bovines with a functional rumen, equidae, lagomorphs, rodents, herbivore reptiles, zoo mammals and camelids when used up to the maximum authorised/proposed total cobalt level in feed (i.e. 1 mg/kg complete feed).

In the absence of adequate deposition data, the FEEDAP Panel cannot conclude on the safety of the additives under assessment for the consumer.

Regarding user safety, any exposure to the additives under assessment is considered a risk and should be minimised.

The FEEDAP Panel concludes that the use of the additives under the authorised/proposed conditions of use is safe for the environment.

The Panel concludes that the additives under assessment are considered efficacious sources of cobalt in feed for bovines with a functional rumen, equidae, lagomorphs, rodents, herbivore reptiles, zoo mammals and camelids.

RECOMMENDATIONS

The Panel recommends that the compounds under assessment should be incorporated to feed only in the form of a premixture.
