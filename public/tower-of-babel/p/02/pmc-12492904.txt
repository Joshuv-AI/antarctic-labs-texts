Effect of serum creatinine changes on surgical timing and prognosis in progressive necrotizing Enterocolitis
Authors: JinBao Han, JianYing Cao, Gang Liu, Lian Duan, MengNan Yu, Guang Li, LiuMing Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo explore the effect of elevated serum creatinine on the timing of surgery and prognosis in patients with progressive necrotizing enterocolitis.MethodsA total of 367 children who underwent surgery for necrotizing enterocolitis(NEC) from May 2008 to April 2024 were collected, and 156 patients with incomplete data were excluded. Among the remaining 211 surgical cases, 168 patients (before August 14, 2019) were included in the statistical analysis, and 43 patients (after August 14, 2019) were used as the validation set. The basic data of the patients, including gender, gestational age, birth weight, age at onset, the length of ICU stay, cardiac ultrasound results, Bell’s stage, whether they were small for gestational age, urine volume in the 24 h before operation, preoperative blood routine (white blood cells, platelets), C-reactive protein (CRP), blood gas analysis, renal function (serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen) and weight at the time of surgery were collected for statistical analysis.ResultsA total of 98 (26.7%) patients died among the 367 patients, of which 47 (48%) had acute kidney injury. Between the 211 patients included in the statistical analysis, 47 (22.3%) died, and among the 168 patients in the data set, 39 (23.2%) died. Of the 43 patients in the validation set, 8 (18.6%) died. There were statistically significant differences in birth weight, length of ICU stay, preoperative serum creatinine, urine volume, white blood cell count, lactate, HCO3-, heart malformations, and small for gestational age status in the collected data, p < 0.05. After ROC-AUC analysis, it was found that when serum creatinine cutoff = 60.5ummol/L, the area under the curve AUC = 0.842, sensitivity = 0.795, specificity = 0.705, and after COX regression analysis, the serum creatinine HR value = 7.242, 95% CI [2.852–18.388]. Log Rank in the K-M curve of SGA = 42.958, p < 0.001. Among the 43 cases in the validation set, 35 survived, sensitivity = 0.75, specificity = 0.771.ConclusionIn addition to clinical manifestations, imaging, and infection indicators as factors for determining surgical indications in progressive NEC, more active intervention should be considered when serum creatinine begins to rise to 60.5ummol/L.Level of evidenceLevel III Retrospective Comparative Cohort Study.Supplementary Information The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06122-5.Keywords: Neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis, Surgical treatment, Acute renal failure, Serum creatinine, Mortality

Neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is one of the gastrointestinal diseases that seriously affects the survival rate of newborns, especially premature infants [1]. Although surgical techniques and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) capabilities have significantly advanced, the overall mortality rate over recent decades has remained relatively unchanged, staying within the range of 6–30% [2]. Therefore, NEC remains a disease with high mortality and morbidity in neonatal intensive care units [3, 4]. In recent years, various biomarkers have been found to predict surgical indications for surgical necrotizing enterocolitis(sNEC). Neonatal surgical centers worldwide have made significant efforts to reduce mortality rates and improve outcomes for patients with sNEC [5]. Renal injury has an incidence rate of more than one-third in neonatal intensive care units. When combined with acute kidney injury (AKI), the mortality rate among newborns increases to over four times that of unaffected infants [6, 7]. If the septic shock associated with progressive NEC is not effectively controlled, it can lead to a continuous decrease in renal perfusion. Serum creatinine (SCr) is an important marker of renal function and glomerular filtration rate. When SCr levels change, it indicates that renal perfusion has changed. An increase in SCr can, to some extent, reflect the severity of septic shock. There is limited data on the impact of acute kidney injury on NEC, and even fewer studies on the indications for sNEC with acute kidney injury. A previous study found [3] that approximately 46.7% of NEC-related deaths are associated with acute kidney injury (AKI). This suggests that septic shock and renal involvement in progressive NEC are one of the main causes of death. In premature infants, the application of bedside hemofiltration or hemo/peritoneal dialysis is extremely challenging, which makes it very difficult to manage acute renal failure once it develops. Currently, neonatal surgeons widely accept surgical indications based on clinical symptoms in conjunction with seven clinical metrics of metabolic derangement (MD7), the Duke Abdominal Assessment Scale (DAAS), as well as changes in plasma white blood cell count, platelet count, and C-reactive protein levels [8, 9]. However, when premature infants present with these conditions alongside acute renal function changes, current clinical symptoms and imaging modalities are insufficient to accurately predict the optimal timing for surgical intervention in these patients. For the above reasons, we tried to analyze the relationship between changes in serum creatinine and mortality in neonatal patients undergoing surgery for progressive NEC, to predict the timing of surgery and reduce the mortality of sNEC patients through changes in SCr.

Materials and methods

This study was a retrospective study approved by the Ethics Committee of the Seventh Medical Center of the General Hospital of the Chinese People's Liberation Army (2021-97), and attention was paid to protecting patient privacy information. Patients who underwent NEC surgery in the Department of Neonatal Surgery of the Seventh Medical Center of the General Hospital of the Chinese People's Liberation Army from May 2008 to April 2024 were collected. A total of 211 patients were collected after excluding 156 patients with incomplete clinical data. 168 patients from May 2008 to August 14, 2019 were statistically analyzed to establish a clinical model. 43 patients from August 14, 2019 to April 30, 2024 were used as the validation group for clinical model validation (Fig. 1).

Data collection and analysis flow chart

Clinical data of all patients with NEC who underwent surgical operation were collected, including gender, gestational age, weight, onset age, duration of stay in the NICU, preoperative blood routine test (white blood cells, platelets), CRP, preoperative renal function (serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen), urine volume in the 24 h before operation, preoperative blood gas analysis, cardiac ultrasound results after admission, presence of cardiac malformations, Bell’s stage, whether the patient was small for gestational age(SGA), and weight at the time of surgery. Exclusion criteria: children with idiopathic perforation and secondary intestinal necrosis or perforation caused by other diseases, congenital gastrointestinal malformations and renal malformations, and genetic metabolic diseases. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was based on the modified Kidney Disease Global Outcomes Organization (KDIGO) criteria for neonates [10]. The surgical patients were divided into a survival group and a death group for comparative analysis of the differences in preoperative basic information, blood routine test, serum creatinine, blood gas analysis, urine volume, cardiac malformations, and other results. The optimal area under the serum creatinine curve and cutoff value were found, and Cox regression survival analysis was further performed, and K-M curves were drawn to compare the survival relationship between small for gestational age infants and appropriate for gestational age infants.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS, version 29.0.1.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The mean and standard deviation (± SD) were used for measurement data that followed normal distribution, and the independent sample t-test was used for comparison between the two groups. Non-normally distributed measurement data were expressed as M (P minimum, P maximum), and the independent sample Wilcoxon rank sum test was used for component comparison. The inter-group comparison of count data was performed using the X2 test. GraphPad Prism 10.4.0. Software was used to draw ROC curves and K-M curves. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Validation results

43 patients diagnosed with NEC and underwent surgery from August 15, 2019, to April 30, 2024, were collected for validation of the model accuracy. The accuracy rate was 81.4% (35/43), the mortality rate was 18.6%, of which 8 people were misjudged, ROC-AUC = 0.798, sensitivity = 0.75, specificity = 0.771.

Discussion

Although the treatment of NEC is continually improving, NEC remains a serious gastrointestinal disease that impacts mortality in neonatal intensive care units, especially in premature infants, and can lead to severe complications [11–13]. Due to its rapid progression, NEC’s mortality rate in neonatal intensive care units remains the highest. When NEC requires surgical intervention, there is often debate about the appropriate timing for surgery. Although many studies discuss prediction models for NEC surgical indications [8, 9], NEC affects not only the intestines but also other organs, deciding on when to operate more challenging when combined with severe damage elsewhere. Most patients undergoing NEC surgery experience acute kidney injury [14, 15]. Changes in SCr during AKI significantly influence the prognosis of sNEC patients [16–18].

The researchers found that AKI is closely associated with NEC, and a consensus as well as clinical guidelines has been established regarding the prognosis of neonatal acute kidney injury [6, 10]. However, there is limited research on how changes in serum creatinine (SCr) levels affect the timing of surgery and patient outcomes in cases where NEC is combined with AKI. In progressive NEC, the inability to promptly correct septic shock leads to inadequate renal perfusion, causing a continuous rise in the SCr level. If surgery can rapidly control the progression of intra-abdominal infection, renal perfusion may improve once the shock is resolved, allowing for recovery from AKI and thereby reducing mortality associated with acute kidney injury. This study focuses on combining sNEC with SCr to predict the timing of surgery, to reduce the mortality rate of NEC and improve the survival rate. As uncorrectable infection is the underlying cause of NEC [19], deaths associated with acute kidney injury accounted for 48% of the total mortality among the NEC patients included in this study. The mortality rate among patients with acute kidney injury in our dataset was 23.2%, which is slightly lower than the reports in the literature.

After collecting and analyzing clinical data, it was found that birth weight, preoperative WBC, Lac in blood gas analysis, preoperative SCr, and urine volume on the day of surgery were statistically significant differences different, indicating that the patients in the death group generally had lower body weight and more severe infections. When infections cannot be controlled with conservative medical treatment, they lead to inadequate tissue perfusion and impaired metabolism, resulting in lactate accumulation. The kidneys also experience reduced urine output due to inadequate perfusion, which worsens renal damage and subsequently impairs the child’s ability to metabolize harmful substances. The failure of septic shock to improve results in decreased renal perfusion and aggravate AKI. A persistent increase in serum creatinine (SCr) levels indicates worsening septic shock. At this time, surgical treatment can effectively halt the progression of septic shock and significantly reduce the overall mortality rate in patients with NEC combined with AKI. This indicates that AKI in NEC patients is mainly caused by infection.

Further ROC-AUC analysis of the two groups of data with statistically significant differences revealed that the area under the curve for SCr was 0.824, with a sensitivity of 0.795, specificity of 0.705, and a cutoff value of 60.5. At the same time, after using the SCr cutoff value as the boundary for binary classification, and performing Cox regression analysis on the statistically different values, it was found that Lac, SGA, and SCr had a higher impact on death. Among these, SCr was associated with more than a 7-fold increase in the risk of death in newborns. A possible explanation is that infection-induced systemic circulatory changes may impair renal perfusion, resulting in elevated SCr levels. When the excessive SCr could not be metabolized and excreted through the kidneys, it further aggravated renal injury, leading to a vicious cycle. Therefore, infection is considered a risk factor for acute kidney injury, and abdominal infection, in particular, is also considered a risk factor for AKI in pediatric patients [20–22]. The progressive stage of NEC is often characterized by an uncontrollable, explosive infection. Because the renal development of premature infants is immature and combined with more serious infections, the changes in SCr in this process have a great impact on the death of newborns.

In Cox regression, infants who are SGA have a risk of death that is more than three times higher. This shows that when birth weight and gestational age do not match, low birth weight is also an important factor affecting death. In the K-M curve, it can be seen that the death rate of infants who are SGA is significantly faster than that of infants who are NSGA. The reason for this may be that the organs of premature infants themselves are immature, and the development of the organs of infants who are SGA is more backward than that of premature infants of the same gestational age. In addition, the underdeveloped kidneys have a poorer ability to compensate for the body’s metabolic products. When SCr increases, the condition worsens further. Therefore, we should pay more attention to infants who are SGA when SCr begins to increase. And when we redivided the patients in this group into subgroups with different birth weights and gestational ages, we found that although the Cutoffs between the groups were slightly different, most of them fluctuated slightly around 60.5ummol/L. In our later validation data analysis, we found that the sensitivity of SCr 60.5ummol/L as a predictor of surgical indications for advanced NEC was 0.75, the specificity was 0.771, and the ROC-AUC area was 0.798, which was lower than the original model prediction success rate. The possible reason for the analysis was that the sample size was small during the validation, and the sensitivity to SCr was different when the gestational age and birth weight of the infant were low.

Although the ROC-AUC area of Lac in the model was 0.78, the mortality risk in the COX regression model was lower than that of SCr and SGA. The possible reason for the analysis was that Lac was the result of multiple factors, and its sensitivity in renal function was not as obvious as SCr, so Lac was not included in the later analysis, and the simple change of SCr could well meet the reference value of the model.

Compared with septic shock caused by other abdominal infections, the potential reason why serum creatinine has an important impact on septic shock in premature infants with NEC may also be due to the difficulty of renal replacement therapy at this stage. Both plasma filtration and dialysis are limited by the gestational age and weight of the infant, and peritoneal dialysis cannot be implemented when there is an abdominal infection. The lack of excretion pathways for excessive inflammatory factors will further aggravate the SIRS reaction. Although there are many factors affecting the changes in serum creatinine, the continued increase in serum creatinine can still reflect the degree of worsening inflammation in premature infants with NEC. At present, the judgment of surgical indications for NEC and various prediction models is almost all based on imaging and infection indicators in clinical practice. There is a lack of reference to indicators related to severe sepsis that directly lead to death. This group of data attempts to evaluate the progression of sepsis in NEC based on changes in serum creatinine, which can add a new reference direction for surgical indications.

This study also has certain limitations. First of all, this study is a single-center retrospective research model with a relatively small sample size. Although the model was later validated, it still requires a large sample size and multi-center participation in the study. Secondly, although the cutoff value of SCr was proposed, there is a lack of more detailed subgroup analysis of gestational age and weight. Especially for patients with younger gestational age and very low birth weight, a more accurate cutoff value is needed to predict the timing of NEC surgery. In the case of acute kidney injury, this study only conducted a complete statistical analysis of serum creatinine, lacked a detailed analysis of urine creatinine and urine volume, and did not analyze the stage of acute kidney injury. Finally, this study lacks multicenter validation data. In the subsequent study of NEC combined with acute kidney injury, adding multicenter data may improve the accuracy and practicality of the model, thereby providing a reference for neonatal surgeons.

Conclusion

In the treatment of progressive NEC, the factors that determine surgical indications include continued deterioration of clinical symptoms, changes in imaging, and aggravation of infection indicators. We should also consider changes in serum creatinine. When serum creatinine continues to rise to 60.5ummol/l, surgical intervention should be considered to reduce mortality.
