Neutrophil Activation and Adhesiveness in Coronary Artery Disease: Results From the COLCHICINE‐PCI Biomarker Substudy
Authors: Nina Talmor, Michael H Pillinger, Yuhe Xia, Ana Leonard, Fatmira Curovic, Binita Shah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
RegistrationURL: https://clinicaltrials.gov. Unique identifier: NCT02594111.Keywords: adhesiveness, biomarkers, colchicine, coronary artery disease, neutrophil activation, percutaneous coronary interventionSubject Categories: Biomarkers

Inflammation and immune cell activation play effector roles in both the development of coronary artery disease (CAD) and during acute myocardial infarction (AMI). 1 , 2 , 3 Whereas prior studies have focused on macrophages and T lymphocytes in the development and destabilization of plaque, neutrophils are the most abundant white blood cells in the peripheral circulation and play a greater role in CAD than previously supposed. 4 Colchicine inhibits neutrophil chemotaxis, activity, and inflammasome activation, reducing interleukin‐1β and subsequent interleukin‐6 and CRP (C‐reactive protein) synthesis. 4 The randomized double‐blind COLCHICINE‐PCI (Colchicine in Percutaneous Coronary Intervention) trial demonstrated that compared with placebo, immediate preprocedural administration of colchicine did not decrease percutaneous coronary intervention–related myocardial injury or major adverse cardiovascular events but did attenuate the increased systemic inflammation in response to acute vascular injury via percutaneous coronary intervention at 24 hours. Given the potential importance of neutrophils, we now leverage the COLCHICINE‐PCI biorepository to characterize neutrophil activity in CAD with or without AMI, as well as by burden of atherosclerosis.

Detailed methods of the COLCHICINE‐PCI trial have been reported previously (NCT02594111, NCT01709981). 5 Our data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Of the 714 patients enrolled in the trial, 478 patients participated in the nested inflammatory biomarker substudy.

The COLCHICINE‐PCI trial was approved by the New York University Grossman School of Medicine and the Veterans Affairs New York Harbor Healthcare System institutional review boards. All participants provided written informed consent.

All participants in the COLCHICINE‐PCI trial (n=714) underwent baseline (immediately before colchicine/placebo administration) assessment of troponin‐I (Ultra assay, Siemens Centaur CP Chemistry Analyzer; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), complete blood count with differential (Sysmex XN‐1000 Hematology Analyzer; Sysmex, Kobe, Japan), and high‐sensitivity CRP (Siemens ADVIA 1800 Chemistry Analyzer, Siemens Healthineers). Participants in the nested inflammatory biomarker substudy (n=478) additionally underwent assessment of peripheral neutrophil and endothelial cell adhesiveness and migratory potential (soluble L‐selectin [CD62L; a transmembrane glycoprotein on neutrophils that is shed on activation], E‐selectin [CD62E; a cell adhesion molecule expressed on endothelial cells following cytokine activation], intercellular adhesion molecules, vascular cell adhesion molecules [endothelial cell surface proteins required for neutrophil adhesion and transendothelial migration]), neutrophil activation and degranulation (soluble lactoferrin, myeloperoxidase, and neutrophil extracellular traps [NETs]), and systemic inflammation (soluble interleukin‐6, tumor necrosis factor).

To assess soluble biomarkers, citrate‐anticoagulated blood was centrifuged within 30 minutes of collection at 2500 g for 10 minutes. To assess plasma NETs, EDTA‐treated blood was separated within 15 minutes by centrifugation at 2500 g for 10 minutes. All samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Biomarker concentrations were assessed using multiplex assays (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA; and R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) on the MAGPIX multiplex instrument (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX). NETs were assessed using a human neutrophil elastase–DNA ELISA. Wells were coated with human neutrophil elastase antibody and coincubated with the platelet‐poor plasma samples. Incubation buffer containing a peroxidase‐labeled anti‐DNA antibody was added and incubated for an additional 2 hours. After multiple washes, peroxidase substrate was added, and NETs were quantified via optical density measured at a 450‐nm wavelength using the MAGPIX multiplex instrument.

AMI was defined as a clinical diagnosis of AMI with elevated troponin concentration before percutaneous coronary intervention. Obstructive CAD was defined as >70% diameter stenosis in at least 1 epicardial vessel >2 mm in diameter or by positive invasive physiology.

Continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the approximate version of the Mann–Whitney U test. In this exploratory but prespecified analysis, statistical significance was set at a 2‐sided αlevel of 0.05. A Bonferroni correction with a 2‐sided αlevel of 0.0042 was used to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Baseline characteristics of the 714 participants in the COLCHICINE‐PCI trial were reported previously. 5 Of the 478 participants in the biomarker substudy, most were men (89.7%), 73% were of White race, and 23.4% were of Hispanic ethnicity. Cardiovascular comorbidities were common, with hypertension (86.8%), dyslipidemia (88.5%), prior or current tobacco use (68.6%), and diabetes (54%) being the most prevalent. Prior AMI had occurred in 29.7% of participants, and prior coronary revascularization was reported in 36.6%. Heart failure was present in 18.4%, renal dysfunction in 15.5%, and lung disease in 14.9%.

AMI with an elevated troponin at baseline was present in 23.8% (n=114) of the biomarker substudy cohort (median baseline troponin I, 0.48 ng/mL [interquartile range, 0.14–3.00] in the AMI group; and 0.01 ng/mL [interquartile range, 0.00–0.02] in the no‐AMI group). Only 8 (7%) of the patients with AMI in the biomarker substudy were referred in the setting of a ST‐segment–elevation MI. In the no‐AMI group of the biomarker substudy (n=364), patients were referred in the setting of asymptomatic ischemia (n=26), nonanginal symptoms (n=55), indeterminate chest pain (n=43), stable angina (n=139), unstable angina (n=65), and recent AMI but troponin in the normal range upon referral to the trial (n=36). Obstructive CAD was present in 79.5% (n=380) of the biomarker substudy cohort.

Soluble markers of systemic inflammation (interleukin‐6 and high‐sensitivity CRP) were higher in participants with versus without AMI. Additionally, increased neutrophil activity was present in participants with versus without AMI, as evidenced by increased neutrophil degranulation (increased myeloperoxidase and lactoferrin plasma concentrations). The other measured inflammatory markers were not different between the 2 groups (Table).

We next analyzed inflammatory states in participants by burden of atherosclerosis (with versus without obstructive CAD). Systemic inflammation, overall, was higher in patients with versus without obstructive CAD as evidenced by higher white blood cell concentrations, neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratios, and soluble tumor necrosis factor concentrations. However, in contrast to the AMI analysis, soluble interleukin‐6 and serum high‐sensitivity CRP concentrations did not differ between patients with versus without obstructive CAD, suggesting an absence of macrophage activation in this setting. Neutrophil activity was increased among participants with versus without obstructive CAD, as evidenced by increased soluble lactoferrin and NET concentrations. Neutrophils from participants with obstructive CAD demonstrated increased adhesiveness and migratory potential, as evidenced by higher concentrations of soluble L‐selectin. Additionally, we found increased adhesiveness of endothelial cells to neutrophils, including upregulation of E‐selectin, and intracellular and vascular cell adhesion molecules (Table).

The COLCHICINE‐PCI trial includes the largest neutrophil biorepository in patients with CAD with or without AMI. We are the first to describe in detail the presence of neutrophil activation and cellular interactivity across a range of cardiovascular phenotypes. These data suggest that neutrophils from patients with AMI and obstructive CAD have increased activation and adhesiveness compared with those without. Markers that were elevated in obstructive CAD, however, differed from those in AMI. Limitations of this study include that majority of patients had low‐acuity AMI as evidenced by a low median baseline troponin concentration. We would expect a greater difference in biomarkers in the AMI versus no‐AMI groups if a higher‐acuity cohort had been included. Another limitation was the use of peripheral neutrophil biomarkers rather than functional studies and analysis of frozen, rather than fresh, samples. Overall, our data suggest that inflammation and neutrophil activation are increased both in patients with obstructive CAD and patients with AMI. These different states of CAD may have overlapping but distinct pathways of inflammation. Our findings have important implications regarding the utility of anti‐inflammatory medications targeting neutrophil activity.
