Clinical utility of pembrolizumab in the management of orbital recurrence of uveal melanoma: case report
Authors: Diego Strianese, Maria Paola Laezza, Maria Laura Passaro, Andrea Elefante, Antonella D'Aponte, Claudio Barbato, Luigi Formisano, Adriana Iuliano, Ciro Costagliola, Francesco Matarazzo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrbital recurrence from uveal melanomas occurs in 3–23 % of enucleated eyes and poses a management challenge. Treatment options are disfiguring, and many tumors will progress despite management with surgery and radiation.Case reportThis case explores the use of pembrolizumab to an orbital recurrence of uveal melanoma. A 80-year-old man developed recurrent orbital disease 11 years after enucleation for uveal melanoma and refused exenteration. As alternate management option he received 200 mg every three weeks of pembrolizumab. The orbital recurrence was reduced in size by 60 % in dimension as measured by MRI and CT imaging. After 20 months of treatment, the patient experienced deterioration of the dementia and spontaneously discontinued the therapy.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that pembrolizumab therapy can result in tumor regression of recurrent orbital uveal melanoma and it eventually may be helpful as a neoadjuvant treatment.

Introduction

Uveal melanoma (UM) is the most common primary intraocular tumor in adults occurring in approximately 5 cases per million inhabitants per year.1 UM is usually unilateral, with an age of presentation of between 50 and 70 years.2

More than 90 % of the tumors involve the choroid, with only 6 % confined to the ciliary body and 4 % to the iris. Despite the relatively good initial response of primary UM to treatment, almost 50 % of patients develop metastatic disease over time.3 The most common site of metastasis is the liver (>80 %), followed by lungs and bone. Orbital recurrence of malignant choroidal melanoma in the ipsilateral anophthalmic orbit has been rarely reported.4 The treatment of such cases may be challenging because radical surgical control is always limited by the need to spare vital adjacent structures; therefore, prognosis is considered relatively unfavorable. In addition, extensive surgery, including exenteration, results in disfigurement with no solid evidence that it improves the prognosis.4 In recent years, immunotherapy has become one of the options available for cutaneous metastatic melanoma because of the significant improvement in prognosis and its relatively favorable toxicity profile.5 Pembrolizumab is a full-length human immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) monoclonal antibody directed against the immune checkpoint PD-1 to remove its binding with PD-L1 and thus restore the antitumor immune response of T cells. Pembrolizumab has already proven its efficacy and has thus obtained marketing authorization in so-called hot or hypermutated tumors or tumors expressing PD-L1, such as melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, urothelial carcinoma, and cervical cancer.5 However, its use in metastatic choroidal melanoma has not been fully established yet. Herein, we report a case of orbital recurrence of uveal melanoma treated with anti-PD1 pembrolizumab who responded to such therapy showing more than 50 % in mass reduction. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Case report

In 2020, an 80-year-old male patient presented sudden, severe pain in his right anophthalmic socket and difficulty in wearing his prosthesis. Clinical examination revealed a firm mass located in the deep part of the orbit which was palpable also under the lower eyelid (Fig. 1A). MRI revealed a large orbital mass filling almost entirely of the anophthalmic socket, which displaced the implant laterally (Fig. 1B). CT did not show any bone erosion. Relevant clinical history includes enucleation for choroidal malignant melanoma with a primary implant, nine years before, in 2011. The onset preceding enucleation was of three-month progressive vision loss and evidence of secondary retinal detachment due to a solid dark-grey mass. Ultrasound showed a dome-shaped nodular mass, 8 mm high and 15 mm wide, located in the superior nasal quadrant, with evidence of rupture of the Bruch membrane; the A-scan features of choroidal melanoma. Histopathological examination following enucleation revealed choroidal melanoma with a prevalence of epithelioid pattern and no infiltration of the sclera and optic nerve. No further therapy was performed and no evidence of metastasis or recurrence was reported until he developed the pain and the prosthesis fitting issue.

Fine-needle aspiration biopsy was performed percutaneously through the lower right eyelid, which resulted in recurrence of choroidal melanoma.

The oncology board recommended radical surgery, which the patient declined because of his concern for hospitalization during the COVID-19 pandemic and his concurrent psychological discomfort stemming from mild dementia. The patient was then offered alternative treatment options including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy. The patient and family agreed to pembrolizumab treatment after a wide discussion of the risks and benefit-risk ratio of such therapy. In 2020, the patient commenced treatment with a dose of 200 mg every three weeks. Within 12 weeks of initiating treatment, a notable decrease in the tumor burden was observed (Fig. 1C). After 3 months, a CT scan revealed a reduction of > 50 % in the orbital mass (Fig. 1D). The switch from MRI to CT was due to the onset of claustrophobia, but the images remained reasonably comparable. After 20 months of treatment, the patient experienced deterioration of the dementia and spontaneously discontinued the therapy.

Discussion

Orbital recurrence of uveal melanoma (UM) has been reported and may occur several years after enucleation in about 3 % of patients, with the longest disease-free interval being 42 years.3,6,7

It seems to occur in about 3 % of patients undergoing enucleation for large tumors and in 18 % of patients with extra-scleral extension of the tumor at the time of enucleation.6,7 Although orbital recurrence usually precedes or coincides with systemic dissemination, it may occur as localized relapse of malignant disease without generalized spreading.3 The present case did not show any distant metastasis, either at presentation or during the follow-up period.

Delayed recurrence of uveal melanoma could be postulated to be due to tumor dormancy.8 The immune system can play a central role in maintaining cellular dormancy or disrupting the dynamic equilibrium between the tumor and stroma.9,10 It has been reported that local tumor recurrence in patients with uveal melanoma significantly increased the risk of metastatic disease, with a reported hazard ratio of 6.28.4,11, 12, 13 However, orbital recurrence can be the only manifestation of disease reactivation, as was the case in the patient reported herein.

The treatment options for primary or secondary orbital melanoma are limited to either radiation or surgical excision, typically by exenteration, as no chemotherapy, biologic or targeted therapy has been fully evaluated. However, none of the aforementioned therapeutic options have proven to prolong survival. Indeed, both radiotherapy and radical surgery can be disfiguring and are not always effective at preventing progression of disease. Likewise, in one reported case, intra-arterial melphalan was not curative of disease, and the tumor recurred at 9 months of follow-up.14

Several approaches have been proposed for treating metastatic uveal melanoma. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as ipilimumab, pembrolizumab, and nivolumab, have been evaluated for uveal melanoma.15,16 Indeed, the anti-programmed death 1 (PD-1) antibody pembrolizumab allows for an objective response in metastatic uveal melanoma in a minority of patients. Intriguingly one study reported that patients with an interval from the diagnosis of the primary tumor to metastases longer than five years had a significantly prolonged free survival rate than those with an interval of < 5 years.5 Eventually, tumor recurrence later than five years from the diagnosis of the primary melanoma, might be a patient selection criteria for immunotherapy. Indeed, the patient herein described was eligible for immunotherapy since he experienced recurrence of uveal melanoma after 9 years. It has also been reported that patients with extrahepatic metastatic disease respond better to immunotherapy than those with liver metastases.5,16 This evidence also supports our choice to use pembrolizumab in this patient because he had the orbital location as the sole manifestation of recurrence.

When possible, surgical excision of recurrent melanoma remains a therapeutic option. However, the patient refused surgical treatment because of the concurrent Covid pandemic was a matter of concern for him for hospital inpatient admission.17,18 Radiotherapy, which was also offered to the patient, implies at least 10 or more days in a row of short-stay admissions; therefore, the patient was unable to accept it. The oncology board's decision to offer pembrolizumab to the patient was supported by evidence from the literature of having a case with extra liver metastasis presented later than 5 years from the initial onset of the disease. The treatment was considered successful because the patient had a reduction of almost 50 % in the orbital mass after four cycles of first-line pembrolizumab monotherapy, resulting in pain relief and improvement in the patient's quality of life. Indeed, the median survival time after metastasis for uveal melanoma is 6–12 months19,20, and the treatment for secondary orbital melanoma remains controversial and often palliative. The present cases showed that with this therapeutic regimen an acceptable disease control was achieved during almost two years of treatment. Unfortunately, the occurrence of the worsening of his dementia led to a spontaneous withdrawal of the therapy.

In conclusion it is an established fact that orbital recurrence of uveal melanoma may occur in approximately one-quarter of eyes with known extraocular extension on histopathology. Indeed, the treatment of this condition poses a management challenge because exenteration can be disfiguring and does not always control local disease. This report contains some interesting novel insights; particularly, immunotherapy can cytoreduce (but not cure) orbital recurrence of uveal melanoma. In this light, it may be helpful as a neoadjuvant treatment and for local disease control in patients whose local surgical control of the disease is limited by poor general health and tumor infiltration beyond the limits of vital structures. Although the clinical benefit of PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy is under debate in patients with uveal melanoma, Pembrolizumab may be considered a viable treatment option, in selected cases, for orbital recurrence of uveal melanoma.

Future studies will be useful to confirm the value of this approach.
