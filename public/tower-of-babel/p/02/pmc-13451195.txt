Privacy preservation of EHR by data anonymization and federated learning for IoT based smart city application in healthcare
Authors: Shaik Mahamad Shakeer, M Rajasekhara Babu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper addresses privacy and security challenges in smart cities, particularly in IoT applications that process sensitive data in sectors like healthcare and agriculture. Traditional security solutions often fail to meet the energy and resource constraints of IoT devices, creating a significant gap in data protection. To address this, we propose a two-phase lightweight privacy-preserving approach. In the first phase, DIVersed and Anonymized Instances (DIVA) are used to anonymize and diversify sensitive data, ensuring privacy while enabling efficient processing. This approach mitigates the risks of identifying individuals through sensitive information. In the second phase, Federated Learning (FL) is employed, a decentralized machine learning technique that allows IoT devices to collaboratively train models without sharing raw data, thus preserving privacy. The proposed approach is evaluated based on information loss, execution time, and scalability, with experiments varying key parameters such as the number of Electronic Health Records (EHR) and the value of k. The results show that our method significantly reduces information loss, improves data utility, and maintains efficient execution, even as the number of devices and data points scale. This demonstrates the feasibility and effectiveness of our approach for privacy-preserving IoT applications in smart cities. Our solution ensures data privacy while enabling energy-efficient machine learning, making it suitable for IoT devices with limited resources. It provides a scalable, practical framework for applications in critical sectors like healthcare, where privacy and data utility are of utmost importance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48031-2.Subject terms: Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The Internet of Things (IoT) signifies an important evolutionary period in technology that affects nearly every sector, including homes, transportation, cities, athletics, commerce, education, or industry. An IoT comprises of interconnected devices enabling users and organizations to be more productive, efficient and allow time, energy, and financial savings. By 2030, the total number of IoT devices is expected to reach nearly 50 billion, data generated from these devices is expected to reach 79.4 zettabytes by 2025, and worldwide revenue from IoT is expected to be $3 trillion in 20261–4.

Although more convenient, IoT also presents severe challenges, especially with security and privacy. Oftentimes IoT devices are limited in resources and do not offer security protections resulting in easily compromised devices5,6. As IoT uses a centralized server for consolidating and storing data, using a cloud-based service raises security concerns regarding attack targets7,8. Additionally, IoT systems often utilize machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) algorithms as part of their system to produce real-time decisions, which may also heighten vulnerabilities as these models face threats from multiple attacks9,10.

In order to tackle privacy issues, primarily in the healthcare context, this article proposes a cluster-based data anonymization algorithm, which is conjoined with Federated Learning (FL). This is important as sensitive healthcare data is derived from IoT devices, making this a concern with the possibility of privacy being breached11–13. The solution prioritizes IoT applications while harnessing the value of data analytics. As a result, this outlines an innovative approach to mitigating the risks involved with IoT adoption.

The fast-growing number of Internet of Things (IoT) devices in smart cities will generate tremendous amounts of sensitive data, with projected figures indicating that IoT devices will generate over 79.4 zettabytes of data by 202514. In spite of the promise from IoT, these devices are at risk for privacy breaches, with a large percentage of devices having security vulnerabilities, and many devices relying on cloud services increasing the likelihood of data leaks. Data privacy in a healthcare application is especially concerning, as patients have Electronic Health Records (EHRs) processed through these devices, and unauthorized access to data can have dire consequences. Traditional methods of ensuring security do not take into account the energy and computational limitations of IoT devices, often sacrificing privacy to do so. Additionally, while Federated Learning (FL) allows for a decentralized approach to data privacy, challenges to its scalability and privacy of data still exists, with a significant number of data breaches occurring inside poorly designed systems.

In this paper, we present a two-phase lightweight privacy-preserving framework that combines DIVersed and Anonymized Instances (DIVA) along with Federated Learning to achieve required data privacy and energy efficiency for IoT system deployments in smart cities15.

Review of literature

As privacy preservation is a growing concern in smart city applications, particularly in health settings, there has been much research devoted to finding solutions to such issues. This section highlights recent literature on privacy preservation and then summarizes some of the key progress being made with privacy preservation for machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and federated learning (FL).

Traditional methods

Traditional machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods represent the backbone of many smart city scenarios; however, their application does not effectively protect the sensitive data of people, devices, vehicles, and other stakeholders that interact with many smart city infrastructures16. Traditional approaches typically require transferring large amounts of private data to a remote, centralized cloud server, raising potential privacy and security concerns.

ML/DL methods

Numerous studies have examined the drawbacks associated with conventional Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) models, specifically, the lack of privacy preservation features that would limit the transfer of sensitive data during the communication of information between multiple professionals in healthcare17. Issues associated with fast decision-making in smart city applications complicate this issue further due to potential data latency from the cloud18,19. Furthermore, within smart cities, there are IoT sensors and devices that collect data at a high velocity resulting in large data volumes being generated20, with other issues developing due to data being lost, compute issues, and high energy consumption21.

Federated learning (FL) for privacy preservation

As a potential solution to these issues, federated learning (FL) has been gaining momentum. FL enables machine learning (ML) algorithms to be distributed across devices - rather than sending all raw data to a centralized server22. This may offer some protection against risks involved with storing personally identifiable data in the cloud. During training of a global ML model, only the parameters from the local devices are sent back to a central algorithm, retaining the sensitive data in a decentralized manner and offering robust protections23,24.

There is a great potential for Federation Learning (FL) within smart city use cases whereby FL would emphasize privacy while facing large amounts of data. As FL is based on the local storage of data on devices rather than centralized in the cloud or a server, this ensures that sensitive information stays on particular premises and does not leave a certain location, therefore, reducing security issues that are often associated with traditional ML approaches25. FL, even as it relates to privacy issues, has demonstrated an improvement regarding data protection by pointing out the separation of system data from ML models, collecting data through deep learning, at centralized servers26. The broader process of FL in decentralized training also adds to promising privacy protection, especially within these cases wherein very sensitive data is being handled27.

FL has shown application in smart cities across a variety of domains including but not limited to healthcare, transportation, communication, and the Internet for things (IoT). For instance, FL has been applied towards mobile edge computing28, smart city decision making29,30, and IoT based systems31,32. In addition, the combinations of FL with blockchain technology have been studied towards better security and privacy protection in IoT environments33–35.

Federated Learning (FL) is an encouraging way to enable privacy-preserving machine learning (ML) in the healthcare Internet of Things (IoT), but faces challenges. One challenge is device heterogeneity, which arises from a wide range of devices in healthcare IoT having varying computing ability, battery, and network bandwidth. Device heterogeneity can impact FL effectiveness, however, heterogeneous federated learning and client selection strategies to only use capable devices can be used to address this aspect of heterogeneity. Additionally, statistical heterogeneity arises due to non-IID data being collected and provided by devices or patients that complicate converging models and performance. Personalized federated learning and data normalization are examples of approaches that can mitigate impact non-IID data to allow federated learning model updates that can be generalizable and representative of diverse data. Lastly, communication limitations mean limited bandwidth and latency may occur in many healthcare IoT environments. Model compression, quantization, and sparsification are effective to avoid communication bottleneck and improve overall efficiency of federated learning in healthcare IoT.

Background

In this section, different kinds of attributes based on their sensitivity are given an outline. Basic attributes involved in any kind of health record can be classified into three categories such as Personal Identifiers (PI), Quasi Identifiers (QI) and Sensitive Identifiers (SI). Personal Identifiers (PIs) are distinct characteristics of patients that differentiate each individual, such as their name and ID. In order to safeguard the confidentiality of patients, it is necessary to either erase or substitute their identifying information with fictitious identifiers. Personal identities were deemed unnecessary for the data analysis. Quasi Identifiers (QI) are characteristics that, when paired with additional characteristics such as gender, age, and zip code, can be used to identify an individual. QIs are readily accessible to the public and serve as valuable tools for doing data analysis. The Sensitive Identifier (SI) of a patient, such as their diagnosis and medicine, is considered very private and must be safeguarded from any potential threat or unauthorized access. Hence, a health record is composed of all these kinds of identifiers and anonymized records are to be employed for treating with training model. This work involves two important phases such as data anonymization and classification model building based on FL.

The healthcare system is struggling with the ability to share the medical data of patients responsibly to maintain their privacy. A proposed methodology aims to achieve privacy protection while ensuring utility and limit data loss associated with the anonymization process. The process begins with raw collection of the hospital data, raw data analysis, and obfuscation of explicit identifiers. The process involves two major phases: the first phase includes using the Normal Distribution Function (NDF) to eliminate rare high-risk data points, and the second phase utilizes the DIVA algorithm to improve the clustering of the data. Overall, this provides increased confidence in privacy protection, decreases information loss, and increases the quality of the data. Finally, the data is anonymized using the DIVA with generalization method, ensuring the appropriate privacy preservation methods are used based on reunion data output and the data type.

This methodology removes or replaces Personal Identifiers (PIs) that can identify individuals directly, with a pseudonym, whenever applicable, in order to provide protection against re-identification. Quasi-Identifiers (QIs) that do not uniquely identify individuals but can identify individuals when they are aggregated/combined with other QIs, are generalized to the degree possible to decrease the likelihood of re-identification, while still being able to provide some level of utility. The link between QIs and Sensitive Attributes (SIs) is protected using methods like differential privacy, which distorts/masks sensitive information about the individual to provide privacy. Although this methodology focuses on generalizing QIs, stronger privacy models, such as l-diversity and t-closeness, could be implemented to provide additional privacy assurances. l-diversity ensures that each equivalence class has multiple values for its sensitive attributes in order to protect against attribute disclosure and t-closeness guarantees that the distribution of sensitive attributes in an equivalence class, is close to the distribution of the sensitive attribute in the overall dataset43. While these models provide an additional layer of privacy, they may create processing and information losses that could impact data utility for future analysis. The right privacy model should ultimately depend on the level of re-identification risk utility that is acceptable for the application.

The proposed privacy-preserving federated learning framework in this paper is a modular component within a broader smart city infrastructure. The trained model output can be securely shared among other related subsystems via standardized data interface and Application Programming Interfaces (API’s), without exposing the raw Electronic Health Records (EHR’s) used in the model training. For instance, systems used for emergency responsemay employ real-time risk indicators or anomaly alerts from the federated model to facilitate rapid triage and resource allocation, while urban analytics platforms can aggregate population-level health trends for infrastructure planning and policy evaluation. Similarly, public health monitoring systems can leverage longitudinal, anonymized predictions to track disease prevalence and support early warning mechanisms. By decoupling data ownership from analytics and enforcing privacy at the data source, the framework ensures seamless interoperability with heterogeneous smart city services while maintaining compliance with privacy and security requirements.

Threat model and privacy guarantees

In this work, a realistic honest-but-curious adversarial threat model was employed. This model is widely considered for data sharing in healthcare and federated learning environments. The participating entities such as aggregation servers are assumed to follow the protocol correctly but may still attempt to extract sensitive information from intermediate computations or shared model updates. Within this threat model, three primary types of attacks are considered: membership inference attacks, model inversion attacks, and poisoning attacks. A membership inference attack seeks to determine whether a particular patient record was included in the training dataset, which could compromise patient privacy. In contrast, a model inversion attack attempts to reconstruct sensitive attributes or input data by analyzing shared model parameters or outputs. The proposed architecture mitigates these risks by employing federated learning, where raw Electronic Health Records (EHR) remain stored locally on edge devices and are never directly shared. Instead, only aggregated model updates are transmitted to the central server, significantly reducing the exposure of sensitive data. Furthermore, the framework incorporates prior anonymization mechanisms, including k-anonymity and diversity constraints, to reduce the uniqueness of quasi-identifier attributes before any learning process occurs. This additional layer of protection makes it more difficult for attackers to reconstruct individual records from model outputs. Finally, poisoning attacks, in which malicious participants attempt to corrupt the training process by submitting manipulated updates, are partially mitigated by the distributed structure of federated learning. Because the aggregation server combines updates from multiple clients, the influence of any single malicious participant is limited, thereby improving the overall robustness of the learning process.

The adversarial model is considered to have access to the anonymized dataset released, which contains background knowledge. This knowledge include partial quasi-identifier (QI) information from external sources like voter list, public registers, which attempt record linkage or attribute inference attacks. However, the adversary lack any access to the private keys, internal state of the Internet of Things devices involved in the federated learning and the raw Electronic Health Records (EHRs). These exhibit resilience to membership attacks by ensuring that raw EHR data remain on the local IoT devices and by applying k-anonymity with diversity constraint prior to the training of the model. The absence of direct identifier information and the aggregation of local update in advance prior to model sharing and the minimization of extreme or unique feature patterns using clustering-based anonymization constrained model invasion attacks. With respect to poisoning attacks, the proposed model considers honest-but-curious participants and does not explicitly implement Byzantine-resilient defenses. However, the aggregation implemented across multiple clients and the distributed nature of model training minimizes the influence of isolated anomalous updates.

The proposed method employs a multi-layer privacy protection approach to mitigate re-identification risks. The initial phase completely removes direct identifiers, and then employs DIVA clustering mechanism to generalize quasi identifiers and enforce k-anonymity with diversity constraints. This ensures that there is no difference between each released record and k-1 other records. The next phase employs a Normal Distribution Function (NDF)-based filtering to eliminate outliers and low-frequency records, susceptible to uniqueness and background knowledge attacks. To ensure the security of raw patient’s data, Federated Learning (FL) is used, preventing local devices and healthcare institutions from releasing these data, thus minimizing linkage attacks and exposure to centralized leakage.

Conventional ε-differential privacy (DP) models provide strong theoretical guarantees against arbitrary auxiliary information. However, they completely rely on noise injection, which result to a substantial model performance and reduction in data utility, especially in healthcare environments that uses resource constrained IoT devices. In contrast, the proposed anonymization-driven strategy in this paper emphasizes on practical privacy utility trade-offs, providing robust empirical shield against known re-identification attacks while maintaining data usability for downstream learning tasks. The proposed framework is developed to be compatible with differential privacy mechanisms at the model update phase in subsequent extensions, enabling stronger formal guarantees without changing the main anonymization pipeline.

Application of normal distribution function (NDF)

The Normal Distribution Function (NDF) is employed to identify and eliminate low-frequency data points deemed high risk for privacy preservation owing to its reliance on the well-defined statistical properties of the normal distribution. The NDF recognizes that data points that are very far from the mean are more likely to represent either sensitive values or anomalous data. When data are normally distributed, which appears throughout healthcare and IoT datasets43, the NDF can be used to guide decision-making. The benchmarks of 68% (one standard deviation from the mean) and 95% (two standard deviations from the mean) reflect the Empirical Rule which is designed to identify outliers from the data and is a common statistical practice. By using this statistical approach to identify outliers, the proposed thresholds could appear arbitrary; however, they are common practices in statistics and have both theoretical and sensitivity analyses supporting the validity. Sensitivity analysis supports the use of NDF as a simple, efficient, and interpretable outlier identification method for outlier analysis while also having data utility to ensure it is applicable for data privacy applications.

The improved performance observed under both rigorous and moderate anonymization settings can largely be attributed to the diversity enforcement mechanism incorporated within the DIVA clustering algorithm. Unlike conventional anonymization techniques that depend primarily on the principles of k-anonymity, the proposed approach additionally enforces diversity among sensitive attributes within each cluster. This mechanism prevents attribute homogeneity and ensures that records grouped together do not share identical sensitive information. By maintaining sufficient variation in sensitive attributes, the method reduces the risk of attribute disclosure while simultaneously preserving the analytical value of the dataset.

Furthermore, the diversity constraint contributes to retaining meaningful statistical variability in the anonymized data. Machine learning models rely heavily on underlying data patterns and distributions to learn predictive relationships. When anonymization methods excessively generalize or suppress data, these patterns may be distorted, resulting in degraded model performance. In contrast, the DIVA-based clustering strategy balances privacy preservation with data fidelity by organizing records in a manner that maintains structural relationships within the dataset. Consequently, the anonymized datasets generated by the proposed method retain more informative patterns compared to baseline approaches such as cluster-based anonymization algorithm (CBAA) and Adaptive k-anonymity, thereby improving predictive performance in downstream machine learning tasks.

The results also highlight the inherent privacy–utility trade-off that characterizes privacy-preserving data publishing. While stronger anonymization mechanisms inevitably introduce some degree of information loss, the observed reduction in model accuracy remains relatively small and within an acceptable range for many healthcare applications. In practical clinical decision-support systems, a slight decrease in predictive accuracy is often considered a reasonable compromise when it substantially enhances patient privacy protection. This is particularly important in large-scale smart city healthcare environments, where massive volumes of sensitive medical data are continuously collected, shared, and analyzed. In such contexts, achieving an effective balance between data privacy and analytical utility is essential for ensuring both ethical data governance and reliable healthcare analytics.

Although differential privacy (DP) offers strong theoretical privacy guarantees by injecting calibrated noise into model parameters or gradients, it was not directly incorporated into the proposed pipeline because the primary objective of this work is to preserve high data utility and energy efficiency in resource-constrained smart city healthcare environments. The proposed framework emphasizes lightweight anonymization combined with federated learning to minimize computational overhead while maintaining reliable predictive performance. In such environments, excessive noise injection may increase training instability and reduce the effectiveness of collaborative model learning.

Previous studies have shown that introducing DP noise into federated learning updates can negatively affect predictive accuracy because the perturbation of gradient information interferes with the model’s ability to learn precise patterns from distributed datasets. Based on the accuracy trends observed in this study, as well as results reported in related federated learning–DP implementations, incorporating gradient-level noise would likely lead to an additional accuracy reduction of approximately 2–4%, depending on the selected privacy budget and the characteristics of the dataset. While such degradation may be acceptable in extremely privacy-sensitive applications, it may not always be desirable in clinical decision-support contexts where predictive reliability is critical.

For these reasons, the current study focuses on combining diversity-preserving anonymization with federated learning to achieve a practical balance between privacy protection and model performance. This approach maintains useful data patterns while avoiding the computational and accuracy costs associated with strong noise-based privacy mechanisms. Nevertheless, integrating formal differential privacy techniques within the federated aggregation stage remains an important direction for future work. Future research will explore adaptive privacy budgets and hybrid privacy-preserving strategies that combine anonymization and differential privacy while minimizing the impact on predictive accuracy and system efficiency.

Clinical utility and model accuracy analysis

In healthcare applications, aside from sensitive data protection, privacy preserving mechanisms should be able to maintain sufficient predictive performance that facilitate clinical decision making. To evaluate our model in respect to this aspect, we consider the impact of different anonymization levels on the model accuracy and other related performance evaluation metrics.

As shown in Table 10, the proposed framework showcaseda significantly high predictive performance under mild and moderate anonymization settings, with accuracy values of 0.89 and 0.84, respectively. These results suggest that the DIVA-based anonymization method effectively preserves clinically meaningful patterns in Electronic Health Records (EHRs), allowing reliable model training even under privacy constraints. However, applying stronger anonymization, while improving privacy, comes at the cost of losing important information, which can noticeably reduce predictive performance. This trade-off underscores the need to carefully balance privacy and data utility based on specific clinical requirements.

From a clinical standpoint, the maintained accuracy under moderate anonymization is sufficient for many decision-support tasks, including disease risk stratification, population health monitoring, and early diagnostic screening. Notably, the federated learning setup ensures that sensitive patient data remain within local healthcare institutions, while anonymization further reduces the risk of re-identification or attacks on model updates.

The accuracy versus information loss comparison in Fig. 9 shows that the proposed DIVA clustering approach strikes a better balance between privacy and clinical utility than baseline anonymization methods. These findings indicate that the system is well-suited for real-world healthcare applications, where protecting patient privacy and supporting reliable clinical decisions are both critical.

Ethical implication and algorithmic fairness

The deployment of privacy-preserving analytics in healthcare environments requires careful consideration of ethical implications and algorithmic fairness. Although anonymization techniques such as k-anonymity and diversity constraints are designed to protect patient identity, they may unintentionally affect minority populations or individuals with rare medical conditions. In many healthcare datasets, records associated with small demographic groups or uncommon diagnoses tend to be statistically unique. During anonymization procedures—particularly those involving filtering mechanisms such as NDF-based outlier detection—these records may undergo stronger generalization or even be removed in order to satisfy privacy constraints.

Such processes can reduce the representation of minority groups within the training dataset, potentially introducing bias into the resulting predictive models. When machine learning algorithms are trained on data that underrepresents certain populations, the models may exhibit lower predictive accuracy for those groups. In healthcare applications, this imbalance can lead to disparities in diagnostic performance or treatment recommendations, thereby undermining the fairness and reliability of clinical decision-support systems.

To mitigate this risk, the proposed framework incorporates the DIVA clustering algorithm to preserve diversity within anonymized clusters. By ensuring that sensitive attributes remain heterogeneous across grouped records, the approach maintains greater statistical variability in the dataset and reduces the likelihood that minority attributes are excessively generalized or suppressed during anonymization. This diversity-preserving mechanism helps retain informative patterns that are important for equitable model learning.

Nevertheless, fairness considerations remain essential when deploying such systems in real-world healthcare environments. Future work will therefore include evaluating model performance across different demographic subgroups, such as age categories, gender, and disease types, to identify potential disparities in prediction accuracy. Integrating fairness-aware machine learning techniques and implementing subgroup-specific performance monitoring will further strengthen the ethical reliability and societal acceptability of privacy-preserving healthcare analytics in large-scale smart city environments.

Conclusions

A privacy-preserving scheme utilizing Federated Learning (FL) for smart city health care applications is the subject of the proposed work and aims to respond to the rising issues with the privacy of electronic health records (EHRs). As the use of electronic health records increases, keeping sensitive records protected is of vital importance, particularly with the limited power available to Internet of Things (IoT) devices in smart cities. This approach is base…of using a weighted FL model, and it processed the anonymized records through a lightweight, cluster-based data anonymization using NDF (Neighbourhood Data Flow) and k-anonymity by generalization to. The proposed technique is shown to be superior to prior methods with respect to scalability, information loss, and execution time, and this is based on experiments using various k values and health record datasets43. This approach combines FL with lightweight anonymization to provide an effective and protective approach to privacy preserving in these resource deficient environments. Check whether these models indeed help preserve privacy and system functionality in a smart city context. Use these policy, legal and ethical requirements as a guide, to determine what evidence-based rules apply to data model constraints. Ensure that the techniques and technological systems proposed here add to the necessity for smart city function as a whole, using these fields to avoid redundancy and overlap. Building and operationalizing these considered systems can over time diminish data privacy issues in these regions, or at least help surmount the public consciousness of the challenges.Despite the promising performance showcased by the proposed method, the model has limitations that warrant further extension.While the combination of k-anonymity and diversity constraint mitigates identity disclosure, under strong adversarial assumptions, the approach cannot fully protect the system against attribute inference or background knowledge attacks. Also, the model experiment in this paper doesn’t fully capture large-scale heterogeneous IoT devices and network dynamics of the real smart city deployments. Ethical considerations in healthcare system remain vital, as anonymization options may influence data fairness and minority representation, especially when it’s poorly configured. In future, we will integrate formal privacy guarantee such as ε-differential privacy to handle regulatory compliance requirements including HIPAA and GDPR into the proposed anonymization and federated learning framework. Differential privacy techniques provide mathematically provable protection against inference and re-identification attacks by introducing carefully calibrated noise during model training, parameter updates, or aggregation processes. Incorporating such mechanisms would further strengthen the privacy guarantees of the system while maintaining the collaborative learning benefits of federated architectures. Another important direction involves validating the framework in real-world healthcare environments. Deploying the proposed system within operational settings such as hospital networks, regional health data infrastructures, or smart city health monitoring platforms would enable comprehensive evaluation of its scalability, reliability, and interoperability with existing health information systems. Practical deployments would also provide insights into computational requirements, communication overhead in federated learning networks, and the real-world impact of the framework on clinical decision-support applications.
