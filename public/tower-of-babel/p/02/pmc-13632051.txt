Microbial ecological mechanism of Blumea balsamifera residue promoting fermentation quality and improving feed value of Pennisetum silage
Authors: Xuejuan Zi, Shuo Wu, Kai Wang, Mao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Pennisetum is the most widely used forage in tropical regions around the world but its silage preservation is often challenged by high temperature, humidity, and the proliferation of undesirable bacteria. Blumea balsamifera (L.) DC., a native medicinal plant and industrial crop, produces residues with notable antibacterial and antioxidant properties, suggesting potential as a silage additive. However, its effects and underlying mechanisms on silage fermentation and feeding value are unclear.ResultsIn this study, we evaluated the impact of B. balsamifera residue on the fermentation quality, chemical composition, digestibility, antioxidant activity, microbial community succession, assembly processes, and ecological network characteristics of Pennisetum silage. Our findings demonstrate that adding 10% residue significantly (P < 0.05) improved fermentation quality, antioxidant capacity, nutritional composition, and in vitro dry matter digestibility. Specifically, the powder treatment increased lactic acid content (KG10P vs. CK, P < 0.05), while the fresh material showed similar improvements (KG10 vs. CK, P < 0.05). Moreover, microbial diversity and community composition were markedly altered: Lactobacillus became dominant (> 90%), while undesirable bacteria such as Aeriscardovia, Clostridium sensu stricto, and Pseudomonas were suppressed (< 1%). Further, the addition of B. balsamifera modified microbial succession patterns, increased the abundance of lactic acid bacteria, and reduced undesirable taxa. Co-occurrence network analysis revealed predominantly cooperative microbial interactions, with B. balsamifera enhancing network stability by reducing tightness, complexity, and natural connectivity. Community assembly processes also shifted: untreated silage was governed by deterministic processes, while residue addition introduced varying contributions of stochastic processes, with powder treatments largely stochastic and fresh treatments shaped by both deterministic and stochastic processes in a ratio-dependent manner.ConclusionsOverall, moderate addition of B. balsamifera residue improved Pennisetum silage fermentation and feeding value by optimizing microbial communities and their interaction networks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05368-y.Keywords: B. balsamifera residue, Feeding value, Microbial community succession, Assembly processes, Co-occurrence network

Background

Pennisetum is a high-quality forage and bioenergy crop broadly distributed across tropical regions, valued for its rapid growth, high biomass yield, and strong stress resistance [1, 2]. Ensiling is the primary means by which Pennisetum is preserved to ensure year-round forage supply and a stable bioenergy feedstock [3, 4]. However, preparing high-quality Pennisetum silage in tropical regions faces many challenges due to high temperature and humidity, excessive moisture content, insufficient epiphytic lactic acid bacteria, and rapid proliferation of undesirable microorganisms such as Enterobacter, Pseudomonas, and Clostridium [2, 3, 5]. To address these problems, various additives—including carbohydrates, organic acids, exogenous microbes, and enzymes—have been tested and shown to improve fermentation quality [2, 4–7]. Nevertheless, their broader application is limited by factors such as high cost, environmental constraints, and the need for technical expertise.

Blumea balsamifera (L.) DC. is one of the most important medicinal and industrial crops cultivated in Southeast Asia and southern China [8, 9]. A member of the Asteraceae family and the Blumea genus, B. balsamifera is rich in bioactive compounds such as l-borneol, volatile oils, flavonoids, alkaloids, terpenes, and chlorogenic acid. These compounds confer diverse biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, cytotoxic, and anti-obesity effects [8, 10–12]. Previous studies have also demonstrated strong antibacterial activity of B. balsamifera against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli [13], as well as improving skin health and promoting wound healing [14, 15]. In animal production, dietary supplementation with B. balsamifera has been reported to improve performance and enhance duck egg quality [16]. Consequently, B. balsamifera is widely used in the pharmaceutical, beauty, cosmetics, healthcare industries and animal production. After extraction of borneol flakes from its stems and leaves, large quantities of B. balsamifera residues remain, retaining significant amounts of flavonoids and other bioactive compounds [17, 18]. At present, much of this residue is discarded indiscriminately.

In addition, with global comprehensive ban on the use of antibiotics in animal feed industry, there is an urgent need to seek widely sourced, safe and reliable, multifunctional silage additives with alternative antibiotic and the ability to improve fermentation quality [19]. Plant residues are a promising option. Recent studies have shown that residues of medicinal plants such as astragalus, bupleurum, honeysuckle, and chrysanthemum can inhibit undesirable silage bacteria, stimulate the growth of lactic acid bacteria, improve fermentation quality, enhance antioxidant capacity, and ultimately promote animal health and productivity [20–22]. To date, however, the use of B. balsamifera residue in silage preparation has not been reported.

Given its abundance of bioactive compounds and functional properties, we hypothesized that B. balsamifera residue could serve as an effective silage additive, improving both the fermentation quality and antioxidant capacity of Pennisetum silage. To test this, we investigated its effects on fermentation characteristics, chemical composition, digestibility, antioxidant activity, microbial community succession, assembly processes, and microbial ecological network structure.

Materials and methods

Silage preparation

For this study, we collected Pennisetum (also known as king grass, KG) at the National Tropical Forage Germplasm Repository of the Tropical Crop Genetic Resources Institute, Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences (Danzhou, Hainan, China). B. balsamifera residue was obtained from Guizhou Miaoyao Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guiyang, Guizhou, China). The residue was divided into two portions: one half was dried at low temperature (65 ℃) and ground into powder, while the other half was stored fresh at 4 ℃.

The experiment followed a completely randomized design. Treatments were arranged according to the proportion of B. balsamifera residue (fresh weight basis). Trial 1 included powder additions of 0, 2, 5, and 10%, designated as CK, KG2P, KG5P, and KG10P, respectively. Trial 2 included fresh residue additions of 0, 10, 15, and 20%, designated as CK, KG10, KG15, and KG20, respectively. Each treatment was replicated three times.

Harvested Pennisetum materials were mechanically cut into 2 cm lengths, then mixed thoroughly with B. balsamifera residue and packed into specialized plastic silage bags (approximately 500 g per bag). The bags were vacuum-sealed and stored at room temperature. After 60 days of ensiling, the bags were opened and samples were collected for analyses of fermentation quality, antioxidant activity, chemical composition, digestibility, and microbial diversity metrics.

Fermentation quality, antioxidant activity, chemical composition and digestibility analysis

From each sample, 50 g of silage was removed and soaked in 200 mL of distilled water. The soaked silage sample was stored at 4 ℃ for 24 h, and subsequently filtered through four gauze layers so only the extract remained. Half of the extract was used in fermentation quality analysis, and the other half was stored at -80 ℃ until the silage microbiome could be analyzed.

The pH of the silage extract was determined using an electronic precision pH meter. High-performance liquid chromatography was performed to determine the contents of lactic acid, acetic acid, propionic acid and butyric acid in silage. The experimental steps were based on the method of Li et al. [23], with optimizing improvements made according to the experimental conditions. No addition treatment was used as the control group, and the antioxidant capacity was compared with the treatment added with B. balsamifera residue. The activities of total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazy(DDPH) were determined using a kit, and the specific operation procedure were referred to the report of Li et al. [23]. The dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) parameters of silage samples were measured following the procedure of the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 1990) [24]. The determination method of in vitro dry matter digestibility was optimized according to the procedure reported in Li et al. [25].

DNA extraction and sequencing

Microbial DNA was extracted from silage extracts using the Stool DNA Kit (OMEGA Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). A total of 156 microbial DNA samples were obtained, diluted to working concentrations, and stored at -20 °C. The full-length 16 S rDNA gene was amplified from the genomic DNA of each sample using the universal primer set 27 F: 5′-AGRGTTTGATYNTGGCTCAG-3′ and 1492R: 5′-TASGGHTACCTTGTTASGACTT-3′. Products from the PCR assay were then sequenced on a PacBio Sequel platform (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA) according to the standard procedures of Biomarker Technologies (Beijing, China). Raw sequence data was deposited in the NCBI, and accession number was PRJNA1291986.

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw reads were quality-filtered with Trimmomatic v0.33 and further processed with QIIME2 (https://qiime2.org/) [26]. The optimized sequence was then divided into operable taxonomic units (OTUs) based on 97% similarity. Taxonomic assignment was performed using the SILVA database and the Naïve Bayes classifier [27]. Alpha diversity (Shannon and Simpson indices) was calculated in QIIME2 and visualized in R. Beta diversity was assessed using principal components analysis (PCA) in QIIME2.

Bacterial functional profiles were predicted with PICRUSt2 against the KEGG database [28, 29], and functional differences were assessed by non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS). Molecular ecological networks were constructed using ggClusterNet (https://github.com/taowenmicro/ggClusterNet) to compare co-occurrence patterns between powder and residue treatments. Network topological features (edges, connectance, average degree, centralization) as well as complexity, stability, robustness, and natural connectivity were calculated following Yuan et al. and Wen et al. [30, 31]. Node roles were characterized by within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi). Based on Zi–Pi thresholds, nodes were categorized as module hubs, network hubs, connectors, or peripherals, with the first three considered keystone taxa [32].

Community assembly processes were evaluated using null models [33]. The β-nearest taxon index (βNTI) and Bray–Curtis-based Raup–Crick metric (RCbray) were used to quantify deterministic versus stochastic processes. βNTI values > 2 or <–2 indicated heterogeneous or homogeneous selection, respectively; values between − 2 and 2 indicated stochastic processes. RCbray values <–0.95, > 0.95, or intermediate indicated homogenizing dispersal, dispersal limitation, or drift, respectively.

Statistical analysis

SPSS (version 25, IBM SPSS, Chicago, Illinois) was used to carry out the statistical analysis. The data pertaining to silage quality and antioxidant activity for different residues addition ratio were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the effects of residue type(powder or fresh residue) and the different addition ratio on chemical composition and IVDMD were analyzed by two-way ANOVA. Duncan’s multiple-range method was used for pairwise comparisons of means, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant and P < 0.01 considered highly significant.

Results

Fermentation quality and digestibility

The addition of B. balsamifera residues, in either powder or fresh form, markedly improved the quality of Pennisetum silage fermentation parameters (Fig. 1). In the powder treatment, the KG10P group showed significantly lower pH, acetic acid, and butyric acid values (P < 0.05), alongside higher lactic acid content compared with the CK group (Fig. 1A–D). KG2P group had no significant effect on fermentation parameters, whereas KG5P group significantly (P < 0.05) increased the concentrations of acetic acid and butyric acid. Similarly, in the fresh residue treatments (Fig. 1E–H), the KG10 group exhibited significantly reduced pH, acetic acid, and butyric acid values (P < 0.05), as well as higher lactic acid levels (P < 0.05).

Antioxidant capacity also improved with residue addition. In contrast, fresh residue treatments displayed a fluctuating pattern (Fig. 2E–H), though the KG10 group consistently showed higher antioxidant activity (P < 0.05).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that appropriate levels of B. balsamifera residue addition significantly improved fermentation quality, antioxidant capacity, nutrient value, and digestibility of Pennisetum silage.

Microbial diversity and community succession

Residue treatments significantly altered microbial diversity and community structure. In the group receiving powdered B. balsamifera, Simpson index decreased while the Shannon index increased relative to CK (Fig. 3A). The KG10P group had the lowest Simpson index; the highest Shannon index was found in KG2P group (P < 0.05). In contrast, fresh residue treatments showed different trends, with the KG10 group having both the lowest Simpson and highest Shannon indices (P < 0.05; Fig. 3B). These results suggest that powder addition supported greater bacterial diversity compared to fresh residue addition.

B Simpson index and Shannon index of Pennisetum silage treated with fresh Blumea balsamifera. C Principal components analysis (PCA) of Pennisetum silage treated with Blumea balsamifera powder. D PCA of Pennisetum silage treated with fresh Blumea balsamifera

The PCA analysis revealed significant differences in silage bacterial community composition between the CK and the B. balsamifera residue treatments (Fig. 3C, D). Clear clustering patterns were observed among groups, reflecting distinct microbial assemblages associated with powder and fresh treatments. At the genus level, Lactobacillus dominated all silage samples, while some undesirable genera such as Aeriscardovia, Clostridium sensu stricto 12 and Pseudomonas were enriched in the CK treatment(Fig. 4A, C). Residue addition induced clear microbial succession. In powder treatments, Pseudomonas abundance increased significantly in KG2P and KG5P groups (P < 0.05), while Lactobacillus (up to 98%) almost completely dominated the KG10P group (P < 0.05). In fresh residue treatments, KG10 had the highest abundance of Lactobacillus (P < 0.05), whereas KG15 was dominated by Clostridium sensu stricto 12 (P < 0.05). Heatmap analysis revealed enrichment of undesirable bacteria in CK and KG5P (powder), and in CK, KG15, and KG20 (fresh), while Lactobacillus was enriched in KG10P (Fig. 4E–F).

At the species level, the succession of silage microbial community structure with different B. balsamifera residue addition treatments was more obvious. 6 species of LAB were detected in the CK group, including Lactobacillus vaccinostercus, L. plantarum, L. nasuensis, L. brevis, E. faecium and L. pantheris, alongside undesirable Bifidobacterium sp. PG18 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In KG2P, L. plantarum (35%) and L. secaliphilus (20%) became dominant, with P. aeruginosa (19%) persisting. In KG5P, L. plantarum (36%) and L. buchneri (23%) dominated, with P. aeruginosa (32%) as the main undesirable bacterium. KG10P showed a dramatic shift, with L. plantarum (46%), L. buchneri (24%), L. vaccinostercus (14%), and L. secaliphilus (10%) dominating, while undesirable taxa fell below 1%. Fresh treatments showed distinct patterns (Fig. 4D). In KG10, L. vaccinostercus (72%) became the predominant LAB, with undesirable bacteria nearly eliminated. KG15, however, showed L. vaccinostercus (45%) and L. nasuensis (20%) dominance, along with Clostridium sp. S1 (13%). KG20 resembled KG10 in composition. Overall, microbial succession patterns differed sharply between powder and fresh treatments, both at genus and species levels.

Cumulative abundance analysis (Fig. 5) confirmed that residue addition increased LAB (Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, Leuconostoc, Weissella) while reducing undesirable bacteria (Aeriscardovia, Clostridium sensu stricto, Mycoplasma, Pseudomonas). KG10P and KG10 groups showed the highest LAB abundance and lowest undesirable taxa (P < 0.05), consistent with their superior fermentation quality.

Microbial co-occurrence networks and stability of silage microbial communities

Network analysis revealed distinct differences in microbial interactions among treatments (Figs. 7 and 8). CK networks exhibited higher edge numbers, connectance, average degree, and centralization than residue treatments, indicating greater tightness and complexity. Residue addition reduced complexity while enhancing network stability. Increasing residue ratios further decreased positive edges and increased negative edges, suggesting stronger stability despite networks remaining predominantly cooperative (positive-edge dominated). Robustness and natural connectivity analyses confirmed higher stability in treated silages (Fig. 8). Although CK showed greater robustness, its network displayed weaker anti-interference capacity. In contrast, KG10P and KG15 showed the strongest robustness among powder and fresh treatments, respectively (Fig. 8A, B, E, F). Natural connectivity declined linearly with node removal, but residue treatments mitigated this effect compared with CK (Fig. 8D, H). The ratio of negative correlations was also higher in residue-treated groups, with KG10P and KG15 reaching the highest levels (Fig. 8C, G). Collectively, these results indicate that residue addition enhances silage microbial network stability by reducing complexity and improving resilience.

Assembly process of silage microbial community

The β-nearest taxon index (βNTI) was used to evaluate the assembly mechanisms of bacterial communities (Fig. 9). In CK, deterministic processes governed assembly, with variable selection (65%) and dispersal limitation (35%) as the main drivers (Fig. 9A–D). In contrast, stochastic processes dominated in powder treatments, with dispersal limitation as the main mechanism.

Analysis of assembly process in of Pennisetum silage microbial communities. A, B The β-nearest taxon index (βNTI) and ecological processes of silage treated with Blumea balsamifera powder, respectively. C, D The β-nearest taxon index (βNTI) and ecological processes of silage treated with fresh Blumea balsamifera, respectively

In fresh treatments, KG10 was governed by stochastic processes, mainly drift (65%) and dispersal limitation (35%). However, KG15 and KG20 were shaped primarily by deterministic processes, dominated by variable selection. Overall, B. balsamifera residue addition shifted microbial assembly mechanisms, with the balance between deterministic and stochastic processes varying by treatment type and inclusion level.

Discussion

In the current study, certain B. balsamifera residue treatments improved silage quality relative to the CK group, approaching the standard for high-quality silage. This improvement was primarily reflected in increased lactic acid content, reduced acetic acid and butyric acid content, and pH values significantly lower than the standard for high-quality silage [34]. These results are consistent with previous reports showing that silages supplemented with medicinal plant residues have better fermentation quality than alfalfa or corn straw ensiled alone [20, 22, 35]. This may be the result of the inhibition of harmful bacteria (Enterobacter or Clostridium, etc.) in silage and the promotion of lactic acid bacteria by residual active ingredients such as flavonoids, polysaccharides, and phenols in medicinal plant residues.

The positive impact of B. balsamifera residue on the nutrition and digestibility of Pennisetum was also evident. The increases in DM and CP likely reflect the natively significantly higher DM and CP contents in the residue relative to Pennisetum, as well as the inhibition of harmful bacteria that may otherwise compete for free C and N. These findings concurred with those of Mu et al. (2024), who reported that Lonicerae flos improved the DM and CP content of mixed silage [36]. The reductions of NDF and ADF in Pennisetum silage may result from both the proportion of raw materials, and the acidic environment formed by good fermentation, which accelerates the breakdown of structural carbohydrates. Similar effects have been observed in silages containing Chinese herbal residues [22, 36]. The results showed that as the proportion of B. balsamifera residue increased, digestibility improved, suggesting that changes in chemical composition enhanced nutrient utilization. Another main purpose of adding medicinal plants to silage is to improve the antioxidant capacity of feed, which may benefit animal health. We observed silage treated with B. balsamifera residues had higher antioxidant capacity, particularly in the powder treated group, where a clear linear relationship between powder addition and antioxidant capacity could be observed. These results are consistent with previous studies, such as Wang et al. (2023) and Mu et al. (2024), who reported enhanced antioxidant activity in silages supplemented with vine tea and Lonicerae flos, respectively [36, 37]. The high levels of flavonoids and other active compounds in B. balsamifera residues likely explain this effect [17, 18]. In addition, we also noticed that there were differences in the effects of adding powdered B. balsamifera residue and fresh samples. The possible reasons are as follows: Due to the high moisture content of Pennisetum, a higher proportion of B. balsamifera residue powder needs to be added to significantly change the moisture content of the silage material. Appropriate moisture is more conducive to the promoting effect of medicinal residue. Meanwhile, adding a lower amount of fresh B. balsamifera residue improves silage quality better. This may be because fresh medicinal residue already carries undesirable bacteria (such as Enterobacter and mold), and adding too much may lead to an excessively high abundance of undesirable bacteria in the silage environment, which in turn inhibits lactic acid bacteria fermentation and reduces fermentation quality. The form of addition of different herb residues in silage depends on the characteristics of the raw materials. Some use fresh samples [19, 37], while others predominantly use powder [20, 22, 23, 35, 36]. When considering improving the silage effect, it may also be necessary to consider the convenience of herb residues material storage, retrieval and addition.

Silage microorganisms play a critical role in fermentation, and understanding microbial diversity and succession is critical for optimizing silage quality. While previous studies report that herbal residues reduce microbial diversity, that implied high-quality silage exhibits relatively low microbial diversity, dominated by one or two lactic acid bacteria species [22, 36]. However, our results showed increased bacterial diversity in B. balsamifera residue-treated silages. The dominant species shifted from a single microbe to multiple Lactobacillus species, including L. vaccinostercus, L. plantarum, L. buchneri, L. nasuensis, and L. secaliphilus. The co-dominance of multiple lactic acid bacteria is relatively rare and may indicate that medicinal plant residues provide diverse active compounds that support the growth of different LAB types. This means that there may be two modes for medicinal plants residue to change the microbial community structure of silage, one is to promote the large growth of a few lactic acid bacteria species, and the other is to promote the common growth of multiple lactic acid bacteria. However, both modes promote silage fermentation by increasing the total abundance of lactic acid bacteria and inhibition of undesirable silage microorganisms. The phenomenon of the second mode need a warranting further study.

Harmful bacteria e.g., Enterobacter, Pseudomonas and Clostridium are typically abundant in Pennisetum silage, producing metabolites like acetic and butyric acids and consuming protein, thereby reducing fermentation quality and nutritional value [2, 3, 5]. Previous studies have shown that herbal residues increase Lactobacillus abundance while suppressing undesirable bacteria [20, 22, 35]. Similarly, in this study, B. balsamifera residue treatments substantially increased LAB abundance (over 90%), while reducing low-abundance undesirable bacteria, thereby improving silage quality. Ni et al. (2020) investigated the microbial composition of alfalfa silage treated with astragalus and hawthorn residue and confirmed that the abundance of Lactobacillus significantly increased, while Enterobacter, Clostridium, and fungi sharply decreased, resulting in a significant improvement in fermentation quality [20, 35]. Similar succession patterns of silage microorganisms have also been reported. Another study about adding more types of traditional Chinese medicine residue to alfalfa silage also found a significant decrease in the abundance of Enterobacter, while the types and abundance of lactic acid bacteria increased significantly [22]. Wu et al. (2025) showed that Bacillus was the dominant bacteria in corn straw silage, but five lactic acid-producing microorganisms, such as Levilactobacillus, were predominant in the herbal medicine residue added silage [25].

Lactobacillus vaccinostercus, one of the most abundant LAB in this study, is rarely reported in silage but has excellent fermentation characteristics in food applications. Pinillos-Miñano et al. (2022) reported that the production of “misha coffee” in Peru requires anaerobic fermentation with lactic acid bacteria, and Lactobacillus vaccinostercus is one of the potential application strains [38]. Meanwhile, L. vaccinostercus is one of the dominant bacteria found in anaerobic fermentation tea. It has been confirmed that it plays an important role in the metabolism of amino acids and flavonoids during tea fermentation [39]. Das et al. (2025) also discovered six beneficial microorganisms from fermented bamboo shoots food, including L. vaccinostercus, which has gene clusters associated with immune modulation, probiotic properties and stress tolerance. It can be developed and utilized as an emerging probiotic [40]. Given the widespread application and excellent probiotic function of L. vaccinostercus in food fermentation, and its status as a dominant microorganism of Pennisetum silage in this study, it may have great potential for the development of functional feed. L. nasuensis, first isolated from silage prepared with Sudan grass, was also abundant in our study, consistent with the work of Du et al. (2022), who found that L. nasuensis was prevalent in paper mulberry silage [41, 42]. Other LAB including L. plantarum, L. secaliphilus, and L. buchneri were commonly detected and have been reported in well-fermented silages [20, 22, 36, 43]. Meanwhile, undesirable bacteria such as Pseudomonas and Clostridium were significantly reduced, consistent with prior studies demonstrating that controlling these taxa improves fermentation [23, 44–46]. Pseudomonas are often blamed for the degradation of protein in silage materials, and may produce nitrogen-containing compounds with an odor that can reduce the palatability and nutritional value of silage [23, 44]. Clostridium mainly produces butyric acid, which can easily cause spoilage and deterioration of silage materials [45, 46]. Each of our methodologies (fresh and powder) appeared to inhibit the growth of both Pseudomonas and Clostridium.

Functional prediction analyses revealed significant differences between powder and fresh residue treatments. Key metabolic pathways related to nutrient metabolism and biosynthesis (e.g., amino acids, carbon, starch and sucrose, amino sugars) were enriched in powder-treated silage. These pathways, associated with well-fermented silage, likely reflect microbial responses to environmental changes during fermentation, including substrate availability, pH, and organic acid concentrations [20, 36]. The altered functional pathways were mainly concentrated in the biosynthesis or degradation of nutrients, metabolites, functional components or active substances, and these metabolic pathways are usually highly expressed in well fermented silage. The up-regulation of some similar metabolism pathways has been observed in alfalfa-mixed silages added Lonicerae flos residue compare with our study [36]. However, another study about adding more types of traditional Chinese medicine residue to alfalfa silage discovered that residue addition drove communities toward a more diverse functional profile of metabolic pathways, which may be due to differences in functional components of different traditional Chinese medicine residues [20]. In brief, the alterations of metabolic pathways may be attributed to the response of microorganisms that alter silage micro-environmental characteristics during the fermentation process, especially in response to fluctuations in substrate, pH and organic acids concentrations.

Molecular ecology network reflects the coexistence patterns between microbial communities and the potential interaction mechanisms of internal and external environmental factors [47, 48]. We observed B. balsamifera residue addition treatments enhanced network stability by decreasing network complexity and natural connectivity. Similar results have been reported for other herbal, Wu et al. (2025b) and Mu et al. (2024) suggested that Lonicerae flos or other residue added could weaken the complexity of silage microbial networks of alfalfa-mixed silage or corn straw silage [23, 36]. In addition, our previous research found that medicinal plants weaken the complexity of silage microbial networks while also enhancing network stability [23]. In summary, the above studies demonstrate that there is an inseparable interaction between the silage microbial network, microbial community structure and fermentation products, but the mechanism of action still requires further study.

Nowadays, there has been in-depth research on the assembly process and mechanism of microbial communities in the environment, rhizosphere and polluted water [49, 50]. However, there is little valuable information about the assembly process and ecological mechanisms of silage bacterial communities, which hinders a deeper understanding of the microbial processes in silage. In the present study, we confirmed that the assembly of the Pennisetum silage microbial community was governed by deterministic processes. The addition of B. balsamifera residue shifted the community assembly mechanism towards stochastic processes becoming dominant or co-dominant. Furthermore, the ecological processes and their relative proportions varied with the form and level of addition. Similar reports include, Dong et al. (2024) applied the null model to analyze the oat silage microbial community assembly mechanism, and the succession of dominant microbial communities was controlled by both deterministic and stochastic processes [51]. However, Wang et al. (2024) suggested that stochastic processes govern microbial community assembly overall in biofilters, while heterogeneous selection, dispersal limitation and homogeneous selection play important roles at different stages of fermentation [52]. However, drift, dispersal limitation and variable selection dominated in this study. These results indicate that key microbial taxa and diversity contribute to community assembly and, ultimately, silage quality, though further research is needed to clarify these mechanisms.

In this study, the microbial community structure, network characteristics, community assembly mechanism and other aspects of the micro-ecological mechanism of B. balsamifera residue to improve silage quality were preliminarily explored. However, there are still many deficiencies in the research, such as the lack of identification of key active ingredients at the metabolic level and the interaction between active ingredients and silage microorganisms. In the future, we will jointly apply metagenomic and metabolome technologies to establish regulatory networks for key active substances, microorganisms and functional genes, so as to achieve precise regulation of silage fermentation by medicinal plants.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of B. balsamifera residue on the fermentation quality, microbial community, and feeding value of Pennisetum silage. The addition of 10% B. balsamifera residue (either powdered or fresh B. balsamifera residue) improved fermentation quality, antioxidant activity, nutritional composition, and in vitro dry matter digestibility. It also increased the abundance of beneficial Lactobacillus while reducing undesirable bacteria such as Aeriscardovia, Clostridium sensu stricto 12, and Pseudomonas. Microbial co-occurrence networks were dominated by cooperative interactions, and residue addition enhanced network stability by reducing network complexity and connectivity. Community assembly was governed by deterministic and stochastic processes depending on both the residue form and addition ratio. Overall, appropriate B. balsamifera residue supplementation optimizes microbial communities and network properties, thereby improving silage quality and feed value. These findings provide a basis for precision regulation of silage fermentation.
