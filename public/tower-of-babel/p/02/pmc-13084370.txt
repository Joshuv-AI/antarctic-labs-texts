Lipemia-induced pseudohyponatremia: Analytical bias beyond manufacturer claims
Authors: Jeanne E. van Dongen, Volkher Scharnhorst, Nienke Geerts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLipemia-induced sodium underestimation can introduce a clinically relevant analytic bias in sodium measurements. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the clinical impact of lipemia on sodium measurements, investigate thresholds for sodium underestimation due to lipemia, and compare manufacturer-recommended interference limits with actual clinical data.MethodsIn vitro interference experiments were conducted with Intralipid® and measuring sodium via indirect ISE (Cobas Pro, Roche Diagnostics) in venous plasma and evaluated against analytical acceptance limits (±2.4 mmol/L) and the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) total allowable error (TEa; ±4.0 mmol/L). In parallel, we analyzed 32,603 paired arterial whole blood (direct ISE) and venous plasma (indirect ISE) sodium measurements from clinical samples collected over five years (in vivo interference). The relationship between sodium bias (ΔNa) and lipemic index was assessed.ResultsIntralipid®-spiked samples exceeded the analytical and CLIA limits at lipemic indices of ∼300 and ∼500, respectively, well below manufacturer-stated thresholds (>2000). In clinical samples, sodium bias was greater than in spiked plasma where ΔNa exceeded analytical limits at L-indices >100 and CLIA TEa at >200.ConclusionsEndogenous lipemia causes clinically relevant sodium underestimation when measured via indirect ISE at lipemic indices substantially lower than manufacturer thresholds and in in vitro interference experiments. We recommend that manufacturers update their interference studies, and that laboratories evaluate their lipemic thresholds through retrospective data analysis, in accordance with ISO 15189:2022.

Introduction

A recent case in our laboratory highlighted a critical risk that may easily go undetected in routine practice: A patient's sodium concentration was initially reported as 112 mmol/L, suggesting severe hyponatremia and raising immediate clinical concern. Yet, a repeat measurement on a new sample taken within a few hours showed 129 mmol/L, without any clinical intervention in between. The only notable difference was the lipemic index: 477 in the first sample, compared to 0 in the second, suggesting clinically relevant lipemic interference below the cut-off value used in our laboratory at the time (L-index <500). This 17 mmol/L difference between two measurements from the same patient in this short time interval raises an important question: how often does lipemia cause clinically significant sodium underestimation that we fail to detect?

Sodium is a key ion which is essential for many physiological processes in the human body, including maintaining fluid balance, blood pressure, and neuromuscular function [1]. Its narrow physiological range (135 –145 mmol/L) makes accurate measurements crucial in clinical practice. Routine analyzers, used in most central clinical laboratories, measure sodium concentration in diluted plasma via indirect ion-selective electrode (ISE) methods. To recalculate the original sodium concentration after measurements, these methods assume a plasma water fraction of 93% [2]. Hyperlipidemia and/or hyperproteinemia can reduce the plasma water fraction, which results in falsely low reported sodium concentrations when using indirect ISE methods, a phenomenon known as pseudohyponatremia [3].

This analytical artifact can lead to misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment, especially since sodium levels can directly guide fluid and electrolyte management [4,5]. To mitigate this risk, laboratories routinely monitor hemolysis, icterus, and lipemia via HIL indices. For lipemia, the lipemic index (L-index) flags potential interference. However, manufacturer-defined L-index thresholds are often based on spiking with synthetic exogenous lipid emulsions like Intralipid®, which do not fully replicate the complexity found in endogenous lipemia [[4], [5], [6]]. The L-index measures turbidity, providing a semi-quantitative result, but it does not account for differences in lipoprotein classes or other matrix effects, which can impact indirect ISE measurements in patient samples.

Consequently, these manufacturer-set fixed thresholds may underestimate the clinical risk of pseudohyponatremia, as suggested by recent case reports and small-scale studies [[4], [5], [6]]. However, these studies remain descriptive and often lack robust, clinical data. For instance, McCarron et al. (2022) demonstrated clinically relevant sodium underestimation in lipemic samples, even when L-index values were well below the manufacturer-recommended limit. However, their findings were based on a relatively small sample size of approximately 20 patients [4].

The primary aim of this study is to evaluate the clinical significance of lipemia-induced sodium underestimation and determine at what lipemic index sodium underestimation becomes clinically relevant. Our hypothesis is that lipemic interference, even at levels lower than the manufacturer-recommended thresholds, can lead to clinically significant sodium measurement biases. To address this evidence gap, we analyzed a large clinical dataset of over 32,000 paired venous plasma (indirect ISE) and arterial whole blood (direct ISE) sodium measurements collected at approximately the same time. Our aim was to determine at what lipemic index sodium underestimation becomes clinically relevant, using a data-driven approach that aligns with ISO 15189:2022 recommendations for interference testing and individualized quality assurance [7].

Materials and methods

Study population and data extraction

Data from March 2020 to March 2025 were extracted from the laboratory information system (GLIMS 10, Clinisys), yielding a total of 633,631 sodium measurements. From this dataset, 43,805 paired arterial–venous sodium measurements were included in the analysis, of which 32,603 met the criterion 135 ≥ Nadirect ≤ 145 mmol/L. Arterial sodium was measured using direct ion-selective potentiometry on the ABL90 Flex (Radiometer), and venous plasma sodium using indirect ion-selective potentiometry on the Cobas Pro platform (Roche Diagnostics).

Paired arterial–venous results were included only when both samples originated from the same clinical order. In our institution, such combined orders primarily occur in the ICU, Medium-Care (MC), Cardiology, and the Emergency Department (ED) (Table 1). In the ICU, MC, and Cardiology, arterial and venous blood are routinely drawn during the same sampling event from existing arterial and venous lines. Because these samples are requested under a single order and processed concurrently in the laboratory, reflected by identical receipt and measurement timestamps, they are assumed to represent the same clinical moment, and therefore the same physiological and lipemic state. Stand-alone point-of-care blood gas orders were excluded. In the clinical dataset, sodium bias was calculated as: ΔNadirect-indirect = Nadirect − Naindirect.

Analytical performance and thresholds

Although arterial whole blood and venous plasma samples collected from the same patient on approximately the same moment are expected to reflect comparable physiological states, identical sodium values are not necessarily expected. Even in the absence of clinical changes, small differences can occur due to analytical variation. Since we are working with paired samples from the same individual, inter-individual biological variation, which would typically be accounted for using the EFLM Biological Variation Database [8], is not relevant in this case.

Therefore, to estimate this expected variation, we used manufacturer-reported coefficients of variation for repeatability (CVs): 0.8% for direct ISE [9] and 0.3% for indirect ISE [10]. At a sodium concentration of 140 mmol/L, these correspond to standard deviations (SDs) of 1.12 mmol/L and 0.42 mmol/L, respectively. Since both platforms contribute to the variability, the combined SD was computed as:SDcombined=(1.12)2+(0.42)2≈1.20mmol/L

To define a practical threshold for analytical agreement, we applied a 2SD range, resulting in an analytical acceptance limit of ±2.4 mmol/L. This limit represents the variation expected purely from method-related variability when measuring the same sample on both platforms. Therefore, we consider any sodium bias (ΔNa) exceeding ±2.4 mmol/L to indicate potential analytically significant bias.

For clinical relevance, we compared the sodium bias (ΔNadirect–indirect) with the ±4.0 mmol/L total allowable error (TEa) criterion used by the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) [11]. Based on these thresholds, a ΔNadirect–indirect exceeding ±2.4 mmol/L suggests possible analytically significant bias, while values greater than ±4.0 mmol/L are considered clinically unacceptable and may impact patient management.

Intralipid® titration for exogenous interference lipemia analysis

To assess analytical significant bias from lipemia under controlled conditions, we conducted a spiking study using pooled patient plasma collected in lithium–heparin tubes. Three plasma pools with predefined sodium levels were prepared: low (134.5 mmol/L), normal (140.7 mmol/L), and high (148.2 mmol/L).

Each plasma pool was subdivided into 11 aliquots of equal volume. Lipemia was induced by using Intralipid® (240 mmol/L). A stock bag (500 mL) was obtained from the hospital pharmacy and subsequently diluted with 0.9% NaCl to generate a series of Intralipid concentrations. The first aliquot served as the unaltered baseline. From each dilution and the basefile, 20 μL was added to 180 μL plasma. The total added volume was kept constant across all aliquots to ensure identical sample dilution. Consequently, sodium concentrations remained stable while lipemia increased in a controlled, stepwise manner.

This titration approach produced samples spanning a L-index range from approximately 10 up to ∼1600, covering the clinically relevant spectrum of lipemia encountered in routine laboratory practice. All samples were analyzed in triplicate on a Cobas Pro (Roche Diagnostics) using the indirect ISE method for sodium. The analyzer-reported lipemic index was recorded for each measurement.

Data-driven approach for endogenous lipemia interference analysis

Sodium results, lipemia indices, and sample-level metadata were obtained from the GLIMS 10 (Clinisis) environment, as described in the Study population and data extraction section.

Statistical analysis was performed in R version 4.3.0, using the packages dplyr, lubridate, stringr, and patchwork for data cleaning, time-handling, string processing, and figure assembly.

Using this dataset of 32,603 paired arterial whole blood and venous plasma sodium measurements, we evaluated the relationship between sodium bias (ΔNadirect–indirect) and lipemia index to identify thresholds associated with analytically and clinically significant interference.

Ethics

This study used fully anonymized laboratory data extracted from the institutional laboratory information system. In accordance with institutional policy and national regulations governing the secondary use of de-identified medical data, this type of retrospective methodological evaluation does not require medical ethics committee (IRB) approval. No identifiable patient information was accessed, and no interventions or changes to clinical care were performed.

Results

In vitro Intralipid® interference

To systematically assess the interference threshold under controlled conditions, as typically done by manufacturers, we performed a spiking experiment using pooled patient plasma and incremental additions of Intralipid®. Sodium was measured before and after spiking using the indirect ISE method, with the sodium difference defined as: ΔNaintralipid = Nablank − Nameasured. The corresponding lipemic index was recorded for each sample. A summary of these in vitro spiking results across lipemic index thresholds is presented in Fig. S1.

Our results showed that at a lipemic index of approximately 300, the sodium bias exceeded the ±2.4 mmol/L analytical limit, and at a lipemic index of around 500, the sodium bias exceeded the ±4.0 mmol/L total allowable error (TEa) threshold. These findings suggest that sodium measurements in lipemic samples can exhibit significant underestimation even at lipemic indices far below the manufacturer's recommended interference threshold of 2000.

In vivo lipemic sodium interference

In our clinical dataset (Table 1) we analyzed 633,631 sodium measurements performed on the routine analyzer (Naindirect). Of these, 43,805 were performed simultaneously with a sodium measurement on the blood gas analyzer (Nadirect). Out of these paired samples, 8682 paired samples (19.8%) had a venous plasma sodium concentration (Naindirect) below 135 mmol/L. Of these, 30.8% (n = 2671) did not meet the hyponatremia criterion when measured by direct ISE (Nadirect ≥ 135 mmol/L), indicating false hyponatremia, i.e., pseudohyponatremia. This highlights the clinical impact of sodium underestimation induced by lipemia in routine indirect ISE measurements, potentially affecting diagnostic and therapeutic decisions in one-third of cases apparent hyponatremia.

To minimize the impact of physiological variability, we limited the scope of the subsequent analysis to 32,603 paired samples (see Fig. S2) for which the Nadirect value was normonatremic samples (i.e., sodium levels within the reference range of 135–145 mmol/L). This selection allowed for a more controlled analysis of lipemic interference on sodium underestimation by minimizing confounding factors related to underlying pathologies, such as pre-existing hyponatremia or hypernatremia, which could introduce additional variables affecting sodium measurement.

Fig. 1A shows the distribution of lipemic index values within the normonatremic paired dataset (n = 32,603), illustrating that most samples exhibit low lipemia and providing context for the analyses in Fig. 1B–D. As Fig. 1A illustrates, only a very small fraction of samples reaches L-index values above 500. Across the mid-range of lipemia (L-index 100–400), where the majority of lipemic samples are concentrated, the trend is far more robust. In this region, venous sodium concentrations measured by indirect ISE (Nadirect) show a clear, progressive decline with increasing lipemia (Fig. 1B). Correspondingly, the difference between arterial whole blood sodium (Nadirect) and venous plasma sodium (Naindirect) increases steadily with rising L-index (Fig. 1C/D), indicating a growing underestimation of sodium in venous plasma samples measured by indirect ISE. In contrast, arterial whole blood sodium (Nadirect) remains stable across varying degrees of lipemia (Fig. S3B), supporting that the observed effect originates from the indirect ISE method rather than true physiological variation.

This prompted us to investigate whether the observed bias could be due to differences in sample origin (venous vs. arterial) rather than lipemia itself. To address this, we refined the analysis to samples with low lipemia (L-index <10). This yielded 11,904 paired measurements with arterial sodium values in the reference range (135–145 mmol/L). Within this subset, the mean ΔNadirect–indirect was −0.098 mmol/L with an SD of 1.80 mmol/L, indicating a small yet statistically significant arterial–venous bias (paired t-test: p = 0.0084).

Importantly, 1.46% of measurements were classified as outliers using the IQR method (n = 174). Excluding these outliers reduced the SD to 1.65 mmol/L and shifted the mean only slightly to −0.073 mmol/L, demonstrating that the broad visual spread in Fig. 1C is partly driven by a small number of extreme values rather than true physiological or analytical differences. Although the absolute mean difference is small, the observed SD exceeds the variability expected from manufacturer-reported CVs. As a result, the empirical 2SD range (−3.7 to +3.5 mmol/L) extends beyond the theoretical analytical cut-off (±2.4 mmol/L; light-grey area in Fig. 1C/1D), indicating that the clinically observed variability between the two sodium measurement methods is greater than predicted. Yet, this observed distribution is, however similar to the CLIA TEa of ±4.0 mmol/L suggesting the clinical relevance cut-off is still appropriate.

Our comprehensive analysis, which includes over 625,000 Naindirect results collected over 5 years, demonstrates that lipemic interference is rare, accounting for just 0.13% of sodium tests with a lipemic index above 150. However, when lipemic interference occurs, its impact is significant. The proportion of paired-samples with clinically significant sodium differences (>4 mmol/L) increased sharply as lipemic index values rose, reaching over 50% at an L-index above 150. This underscores the need to revise flagging thresholds for lipemia in clinical laboratories to more accurately detect sodium underestimation caused by lipemia.

Discussion and conclusion

Our findings confirm that lipemia-induced sodium underestimation can cause both an analytical and clinically significant bias. In our analysis, we demonstrated that lipemia, even at lower levels than those typically flagged by manufacturers, can cause significant sodium measurement biases. This discrepancy is particularly alarming in cases where lipemic interference goes unnoticed, potentially leading to incorrect diagnoses and inappropriate treatment decisions. The current lipemic index thresholds, often based on synthetic lipid emulsions like Intralipid®, do not fully replicate the complexity of endogenous lipemia in patient samples [[4], [5], [6]]. As a result, they may underestimate the clinical risk of lipemic interference, as evidenced by our findings of significant sodium underestimation at lipemic indices well below the manufacturer-recommended limits.

The impact of this analytical artifact is far-reaching, especially in clinical scenarios where sodium measurements are pivotal for guiding treatment decisions related to fluid and electrolyte balance. Sodium concentration is a critical parameter in managing conditions like dehydration, heart failure, and kidney disease, and inaccuracies in its measurement can lead to inappropriate therapeutic interventions. Our comprehensive analysis, covering over 625,000 Naindirect results collected over 5 years, shows that while lipemic analytically significant bias is infrequent (0.13% of all sodium tests had a lipemic index above 150), its impact is substantial when it occurs. Specifically, more than 34% of cases with a high lipemic index were falsely labeled as hyponatremic when comparing indirect sodium measurements to the Nadirect measurements. This suggests that the recent case in our laboratory, where a substantial discrepancy in sodium measurements was observed due to a high lipemic index, is not an isolated incident. Rather, it highlights a tangible peril of undetected pseudohyponatremia in routine practice, especially when relying on in vitro thresholds provided by manufacturers. Our results underscore the need for clinical laboratories to revise their lipemic index thresholds, ensuring that sodium underestimation due to lipemia is detected even when the lipemic index is below current flagging cut-offs.

Despite the significant insights gained from this study, there are limitations to consider. We heavily rely on orders that contain both a direct and indirect sodium measurement, typically performed on a specific part of the hospital population. The retrospective nature of our analysis means that confounding factors, such as undiagnosed health conditions or unaccounted variations in laboratory procedures, may have influenced the results. Therefore, while our findings are based on a large dataset, future prospective studies are needed to assess the long-term clinical consequences of lipemic interference on patient outcomes and include a broader part of the hospital population. Additionally, the reliance on synthetic emulsions for modeling lipemic interference calls for further exploration of more representative models that better reflect the complex nature of lipemia in patient samples, a data driven approach as we used in this study could be helpful for labs to set their own thresholds for (lipemic) interference. Lastly, we did not apply total protein or albumin concentrations as inclusion or exclusion criteria. It is known that other matrix constituents like hyperproteinemia could also contribute to an underestimation of the sodium concentration. Therefore, if available in the same order, the effect of protein and albumin was also analyzed (Fig. S4). Total protein showed a modest positive correlation with ΔNa (r = 0.32), consistent with the electrolyte-exclusion effect in indirect ISE, whereas albumin contributed minimally (r = 0.05). Thus, while lipemia remains the primary determinant of sodium underestimation, elevated total protein among other factors can introduce additional variability in a subset of samples. However, the effect size was smaller than that of lipemia and does not alter our conclusion that lipemia is the dominant driver of clinically relevant pseudohyponatremia.

In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence that lipemia-induced sodium underestimation is a clinically significant concern that warrants immediate attention. The currently used threshold values for lipemic interference are manufacturer-recommended limits, which are typically derived from in vitro spiking studies using synthetic emulsions and therefore may not fully capture the risks present in endogenous lipemia. We recommend that laboratories revisit their lipemic index thresholds, incorporate patient-specific data into interference testing, and adopt more advanced methods for detecting lipemia-related bias. By doing so, we can ensure more reliable sodium measurements, reduce the risk of misdiagnosis, and ultimately improve patient care.
