Marked Underreferral to Structural Heart Specialists in Severe Tricuspid Regurgitation Despite High Clinical Burden: An Adjusted Registry Analysis
Authors: Prince Darko, John Costner, Brennan Carter, Baffour Otchere, Christopher Chinnatambi, Papa Beeko, Patrick Berchie, Oladipo Odeyinka, Eunice Hama, Swethapriya Chaparala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere tricuspid regurgitation (TR) carries mortality risk comparable to untreated severe aortic stenosis, yet specialist referral rates remain poorly characterized.MethodsWe analyzed 1000 patients with severe TR from CardioCare registry from a single center in Georgia. The primary outcome was the absence of structural heart disease (SHD) specialist evaluation. Multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of referral failure adjusted for age, heart failure (HF), kidney and liver disease, symptoms, right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP), and ejection fraction, with an extended model adding coronary artery disease, conduction delay, and New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III–IV. Kaplan-Meier and Cox analyses assessed survival by evaluation status and clinical subgroup.ResultsOnly 328 patients (32.8%) underwent SHD evaluation despite symptoms (86.4%), HF (86.5%), kidney disease (65.1%), and liver disease (32.0%). HF was the strongest referral predictor (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 0.20, 95% CI 0.11–0.36); NYHA class III–IV, coronary artery disease, and conduction delay were also independently associated, whereas kidney disease, liver disease, and symptoms were not, and higher RVSP paradoxically predicted lower referral (OR 1.02/mmHg; both p < 0.001). Only 13.2% of symptomatic patients without HF were evaluated, and those with combined HF, kidney, and liver disease had the highest mortality (22.4%) but no preferential referral. Survival was worse with HF, increasing organ burden, and RVSP ≥40 mmHg; these factors plus age remained independent mortality predictors on Cox analysis, though this association attenuated in sensitivity analysis.ConclusionsMore than two-thirds of patients with severe TR never reach an SHD specialist despite substantial clinical burden, highlighting the need for echocardiography-triggered referral pathways and quality metrics.Keywords: Care gap, Disparity, Specialist referral, Structural heart disease, Transcatheter tricuspid valve intervention, Tricuspid regurgitation

Introduction

Affecting approximately 1.6 million Americans with moderate-to-severe disease, tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is the most common valvular disease in adults.1,2 Once considered a benign secondary lesion, severe TR is now recognized as a major cause of morbidity and mortality, with reported 5-year survival near 50% and 10-year survival near 30%, comparable to untreated severe aortic stenosis.3,4 Elevated right atrial pressure causes venous congestion affecting the liver and kidneys, producing cardiorenal and cardiohepatic syndromes that independently worsen prognosis.5,6

The economic burden of TR is considerable. Largely due to cardiovascular hospitalizations, annual health care costs rise from roughly $31,000 before significant TR diagnosis to nearly $95,000 afterward.7,8 Surgical referral patterns further highlight delayed recognition. In a French nationwide database, patients undergoing isolated tricuspid surgery commonly presented with advanced New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III–IV symptoms; in-hospital mortality exceeded 10% and complication rates approached 20%, suggesting referral occurred too late.9,10 Additional disparities exist, with lower utilization of tricuspid surgery among Black, Hispanic, and female patients.11,12

The transcatheter era has substantially changed treatment options, with contemporary reviews emphasizing earlier referral and intervention before irreversible right ventricular remodeling develops.13, 14, 15 In 2024, the United States Food and Drug Administration–approved TriClip transcatheter edge-to-edge repair and the EVOQUE transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement system based on the TRILUMINATE Pivotal and TRISCEND II trials, respectively.16,17 These studies demonstrated major reductions in TR severity and significant improvements in quality of life compared with medical therapy alone.16, 17, 18 The TRI-SCORE model further demonstrated that low- and intermediate-risk patients derive meaningful benefit from intervention, emphasizing the importance of timely referral.19 Accordingly, the 2025 American College of Cardiology Expert Consensus Decision Pathway (ECDP) recommends heart team evaluation for all symptomatic patients with severe TR.20 We therefore sought to characterize patterns and independent predictors of structural heart disease (SHD) specialist evaluation in a contemporary real-world cohort of patients with severe TR.

Methods

We analyzed a 1000-patient real-world registry to characterize patterns of SHD specialist evaluation, identify independent associations with referral failure, and examine survival using Kaplan–Meier and Cox proportional hazards methods.

Study Design and Population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the CardioCare registry, a multisite, real-world registry of deidentified patients with structural and valvular heart disease. The present analysis used data from a single tertiary center in Georgia, United States, spanning 2016 through December 2025. This single-center cohort was selected because it represented the complete center-level data set available for analysis and allowed referral patterns to be evaluated within a defined health care system and referral pathway. Inclusion required adults 18 years or older with echocardiographic severe TR, defined per American Society of Echocardiography and European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging guidelines as the presence of at least 2 of the following criteria: vena contracta width ≥10 mm, hepatic vein systolic flow reversal, dense continuous-wave Doppler signal with early peaking triangular contour, proximal isovelocity surface area radius >9 mm at a Nyquist limit of 28 cm/s, effective regurgitant orifice area ≥40 mm2, or regurgitant volume ≥45 mL/beat. Kidney disease (KD) was defined by registry-based, International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision–coded diagnosis, captured as 2 hierarchical severity strata: non–end-stage renal disease (ESRD) chronic KD (“Renal Failure or KD”) and ESRD, with ESRD representing the more severe stratum. No laboratory-based estimated glomerular filtration rate thresholds or creatinine values were available in the registry. Patients with prior tricuspid intervention before registry entry were excluded. The final cohort included 1000 patients.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the absence of SHD specialist evaluation, defined as any documented encounter with an SHD cardiologist or multidisciplinary SHD team. Secondary outcomes included subgroup-specific referral rates, Kaplan–Meier survival by referral status and comorbidity profile, and Cox-adjusted mortality.

Statistical Analysis

Symptomatic status was defined using the registry-coded SHD symptom status variable. The registry did not contain symptom type, severity, timing, adjudication criteria, or relationship to TR. Heart failure (HF) was captured separately as a registry diagnosis/comorbidity variable. Because HF and symptomatic status may overlap clinically, both variables were retained as clinically relevant covariates but interpreted cautiously for potential collinearity. Comorbid KD and ESRD were identified using the registry’s predefined binary variables for each condition. Further stratification based on estimated glomerular filtration rate, chronic KD stage, dialysis timing, and longitudinal renal function was not available for analysis. Baseline characteristics were compared between evaluated and nonevaluated patients using chi-squared tests for categorical variables and independent t-tests for continuous variables to describe unadjusted clinical differences between referral groups.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate independent associations with referral failure because the primary outcome was binary absence of SHD specialist evaluation. The model adjusted for age, HF, KD, liver disease (LD), registry-coded symptom status, right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP), and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) (LVEF). Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance to severe TR referral patterns, comorbidity burden, and prognosis rather than by univariable statistical significance alone. The extended model additionally adjusted for coronary artery disease, conduction delay, and NYHA class III–IV to evaluate the contribution of markers of established cardiovascular engagement.

Subgroup analyses were performed separately from the multivariable logistic regression to describe SHD evaluation rates across clinically relevant phenotypes, including age strata, LVEF phenotype, KD, LD, combined organ burden, NYHA class, symptomatic status without documented HF, and elevated RVSP. These analyses were intended to characterize referral patterns across clinically recognizable risk groups rather than identify independent predictors.

Survival analysis was conducted in 961 patients with a valid index date—defined as the earliest documented clinical encounter date across all available registry diagnosis, comorbidity, and specialist encounter fields—and positive follow-up time. Twenty-four patients were excluded due to the absence of any documentable clinical encounter date, and 15 additional patients were excluded due to nonpositive follow-up time. The event was recorded death; patients were censored at the date last known alive, lost to follow-up, or registry closure (May 16, 2026), whichever occurred first.

Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank testing were used to describe unadjusted survival patterns by SHD evaluation status and clinical subgroups. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate adjusted associations with all-cause mortality. Cox covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance to TR prognosis and baseline mortality risk, including SHD evaluation, HF, KD, LD, RVSP ≥40 mmHg, NYHA class III–IV, and age. Because the registry does not contain cause-of-death data, competing-risks analyses using the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model could not be performed; this limitation is discussed in the Limitations section. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals for each covariate individually and globally; results are reported as per-covariate p-values alongside the global test. As a sensitivity analysis, the TR registry list date was used as an alternative index date/time zero. To address systematic baseline differences between evaluated and nonevaluated patients, inverse probability of treatment weighting was applied as a prespecified sensitivity analysis. A propensity score for SHD specialist evaluation was estimated using multivariable logistic regression incorporating HF, KD, LD, coronary artery disease, conduction delay, NYHA class III–IV, age, symptom status, RVSP, and LVEF. Stabilized weights were computed as p(SHD evaluation)/propensity score for evaluated patients and p(no SHD evaluation)/(1 − propensity score) for nonevaluated patients. Weights were truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles to limit the influence of extreme weights. Covariate balance after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (threshold <0.10). An inverse probability of treatment-weighted Kaplan–Meier analysis and inverse probability of treatment-weighted Cox proportional hazards model were used to reexamine the association between SHD evaluation and all-cause mortality in the weighted pseudopopulation.

RVSP, an echocardiography-derived estimate of pulmonary artery systolic pressure, was missing in 190 patients (19.0%). Patients with missing RVSP did not differ significantly from those with available values with respect to mortality, SHD evaluation rate, HF, KD, LD, or NYHA class (all p > 0.05), which supported the use of multiple imputation for missing RVSP values.

Multiple imputation by chained equations was performed using age, LVEF, HF, KD, LD, NYHA class, and SHD evaluation status as predictors across 20 imputed data sets. Results were pooled using Rubin's rules. Sensitivity analyses using cohort median imputation and complete-case analysis were performed to assess the robustness of RVSP-related findings.

Logistic regression results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs, and Cox regression results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

A total of 1000 patients with severe TR were included (mean age 72.9 ± 16.0 years). Clinical burden was substantial: HF in 865 (86.5%), symptoms in 864 (86.4%), KD in 651 (65.1%), ESRD in 329 (32.9%), and LD in 320 (32.0%). The triple comorbidity cluster (HF + KD + LD) was present in 245 patients (24.5%). Hypertension was near-universal (941, 94.1%) with coronary artery disease in 757 (75.7%) and anemia in 730 (73.0%). The mean RVSP was 47.3 ± 20.4 mmHg, and the mean LVEF was 41.4% ± 17.7%. By EF phenotype, 36.9% had HF with reduced EF, 14.4% HF with mildly reduced EF, and 48.1% HF with preserved EF. NYHA class III–IV was documented in 330 (33.0%). Recorded mortality was 151 (15.1%). Full baseline characteristics stratified by SHD evaluation status are presented in Table 1.

SHD Specialist Evaluation Rate

Only 328 of 1000 patients (32.8%) received SHD specialist evaluation, and 672 (67.2%) did not. Subgroup evaluation rates and the clinical burden among nonreferred patients are presented in Figure 1. In unadjusted comparisons, evaluated patients had significantly higher rates of HF (94.5% vs. 82.6%, p < 0.001), coronary artery disease (87.2% vs. 70.1%, p < 0.001), NYHA class III–IV (54.3% vs. 22.6%, p < 0.001), and conduction delay (35.4% vs. 21.0%, p < 0.001). The mean HF admissions were significantly higher in the evaluated group (3.9 vs. 3.2, p = 0.020). Paradoxically, the mean RVSP was significantly lower in evaluated patients (43.4% ± 19.6 vs. 49.2% ± 20.5 mmHg, p < 0.001). KD (p = 0.482), LD (p = 0.609), symptom status (p = 0.163), and LVEF (p = 0.766) did not differ. Among 865 patients with documented HF, 555 (64.2%) still never reached an SHD specialist.

Multivariable Predictors of Referral Failure

On primary multivariable logistic regression (Table 2, Figure 2), HF showed the strongest independent association with referral (aOR for no SHD evaluation: 0.20, 95% CI 0.11–0.36, p < 0.001). RVSP was independently associated with reduced referral (aOR 1.02/mmHg, p < 0.001). In the extended model, NYHA class III–IV (aOR 0.27, p < 0.001), coronary artery disease (aOR 0.39, p < 0.001), and conduction delay (aOR 0.54, p < 0.001) were also independently associated with evaluation, which suggests that markers of established cardiovascular engagement drive specialist access. KD (aOR 1.11, p = 0.511), liver disease (aOR 1.02, p = 0.890), symptoms (aOR 1.15, p = 0.559), and age (aOR 1.01, p = 0.212) were nonsignificant.

Subgroup Analysis

Referral rates were strikingly uniform across age strata (age <65: 32.1% vs. ≥65: 33.1%, p = 0.812), LVEF phenotype (HF with reduced EF 33.3%, HF with mildly reduced EF 36.1%, HF with preserved EF 31.8%; p > 0.5 for all), and the presence versus absence of KD or LD (p = 0.482 and p = 0.609, respectively). The 245 triple-burden patients had a referral rate of 33.1%, statistically indistinguishable from the overall cohort despite a recorded mortality of 22.4%. Among 68 symptomatic patients without a documented HF diagnosis, only 9 (13.2%) were evaluated (p < 0.001 vs. overall). NYHA class III–IV achieved the highest referral rate (53.9%), yet 46.1% of this highest-burden group were never evaluated. Higher RVSP (≥40 mmHg) was associated with lower referral (30.7 vs. 36.8%). SHD evaluation was associated with improved survival: 36-month survival 89.7% in evaluated patients versus 86.5% in nonevaluated patients (log-rank p = 0.032; Figure 5A). HF was the strongest survival discriminator: 36-month survival 96.4% without HF versus 86.5% with HF (log-rank p < 0.001; Figure 5B). Organ burden showed a graded survival relationship: 36-month survival declined from 92.7% (no kidney or LD) to 86.5% (KD or LD alone) to 85.0% (KD + LD), log-rank p = 0.003 (Figure 5C). RVSP ≥40 mmHg was associated with significantly worse survival (86.3 vs. 89.9% at 36 months, log-rank p = 0.003; Figure 5D).

On multivariable Cox analysis adjusted for SHD specialist evaluation, HF, KD, LD, RVSP ≥40 mmHg, NYHA class III–IV, and age (Table 2, Figure 6A), SHD evaluation was independently associated with 45% lower hazard of death (HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.38–0.79, p = 0.001). In the inverse probability of treatment weighting sensitivity analysis (n = 875, 119 events; median follow-up 19.3 months), propensity weighting achieved adequate balance on 9 of 10 covariates (all standardized mean differences <0.10 after weighting); coronary artery disease showed residual a standardized mean difference of +0.11. The inverse probability of treatment–weighted Cox HR for SHD evaluation was 0.72 (95% CI 0.49–1.06, p = 0.10), directionally consistent with the primary estimate but no longer statistically significant after group balancing. Inverse probability of treatment–weighted 36-month survival was 85.2% (95% CI 78.3%–90.1%) in evaluated patients versus 82.6% (95% CI 78.0%–86.4%) in nonevaluated patients (weighted log-rank p = 0.27). KD remained an independent mortality predictor in the weighted model (HR 2.03, 95% CI 1.23–3.34, p = 0.005) (Supplementary Table 1). Additional variables independently associated with mortality included HF (HR 3.07, 95% CI 1.21–7.77, p = 0.018), KD (HR 2.04, 95% CI 1.30–3.20, p = 0.002), and RVSP ≥40 mmHg (HR 1.54, 95% CI 1.07–2.24, p = 0.021). LD was not an independent mortality predictor on Cox analysis (HR 1.05, p = 0.761). The proportional hazards assumption was not violated on Schoenfeld residual testing. Per-covariate scaled Schoenfeld residual p-values were as follows: SHD evaluation p = 0.90, HF p = 0.41, KD p = 0.83, LD p = 0.15, NYHA class III–IV p = 0.07, age p = 0.69, RVSP ≥40 mmHg p = 0.12 (all p > 0.05). The global test p-value was 0.68. No covariate violated the proportional hazards assumption. The 12-month survival by comorbidity profile is shown in Figure 6B, demonstrating a gradient from ≥98% in patients without comorbidities to 92.3% in the KD + LD group.

Sensitivity Analysis

In 827 of 976 patients (84.7%) with both an earliest clinical encounter date and a TR registry list date available, the primary index date preceded the TR list date by a median of 550 days (interquartile range 135–1223 days); 485 patients (49.7%) had a primary index date more than 1 year before TR registry listing. In the sensitivity analysis using the TR list date as time zero (n = 869, 93 events, median follow-up 7.0 months), KD (HR 3.00, 95% CI 1.64–5.51, p < 0.001) and elevated RVSP ≥40 mmHg (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.13–3.01, p = 0.014) remained independent predictors of mortality, consistent with the primary analysis. The association between SHD evaluation and survival was attenuated in this model (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.57–1.39, p = 0.607), attributable to the substantially shorter follow-up time (median 28.7.0 vs. 21.3 months) and correspondingly fewer events (93 vs. 148), which limited precision for estimating survival differences between groups. Sensitivity analyses using cohort median imputation and complete-case analysis for missing RVSP values yielded results consistent with the primary multiple imputation analysis. Results of the inverse probability of treatment weighting sensitivity analysis are reported in the Survival Analysis section. An important methodological consideration is the potential for immortal time bias in the primary Cox analysis: SHD-evaluated patients must have survived long enough to attend the evaluation encounter, and any follow-up time accruing before that encounter is attributed to the evaluated group, which may artificially inflate the apparent survival benefit. Because the registry does not capture granular encounter-level timing relative to the survival index date for all patients, a time-varying covariate Cox model could not be fully implemented; the inverse probability of treatment–weighted analysis provides a complementary approach, and both analyses should be interpreted with this limitation in mind (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

This registry analysis of 1000 contemporary patients with severe TR establishes a convergent set of findings across logistic regression, Kaplan–Meier analysis, and Cox modeling: (1) 67.2% of patients never received SHD specialist evaluation; (2) referral is driven by HF labeling and established cardiovascular engagement—not TR-specific organ sequelae; (3) SHD evaluation is independently associated with 45% lower hazard of death; (4) KD doubles mortality risk on Cox analysis yet carries no independent referral weight; and (5) higher RVSP predicts both reduced referral and worse survival simultaneously. Together these findings define a referral ecosystem that is both irrational relative to clinical risk and inadequate relative to the therapeutic landscape established by TRILUMINATE and TRISCEND II.16, 17, 18

SHD Evaluation as a Survival Modifier

The Cox finding that SHD evaluation was independently associated with a 45% reduction in mortality hazard (HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.38–0.79, p = 0.001; Table 2, Figure 6A) elevates the referral gap from a process failure to an outcomes imperative. The Kaplan–Meier curves (Figure 5A) show widening survival divergence between evaluated and nonevaluated patients from 12 months onward, with 36-month survival of 89.7 vs. 86.5% (log-rank p = 0.032). The observed association between SHD evaluation and improved survival may reflect a combination of downstream effects, including initiation of transcatheter TR therapy (TriClip or EVOQUE), optimization of guideline-directed medical therapy under specialist supervision, and earlier recognition of end-organ progression. However, this association may also reflect selection effects, including the possibility that some nonreferred patients had advanced or end-stage disease at the time referral would have been considered. As an observational association, residual confounding cannot be excluded; evaluated patients were more likely to have coronary artery disease, conduction delay, and higher NYHA class, suggesting more complete cardiovascular engagement. However, the HR remains robust after multivariable adjustment and is directionally consistent with the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire improvements and favorable right ventricular remodeling documented in TRILUMINATE and TRISCEND II.16, 17, 18

The Referral-Mortality Juxtaposition: Kidney Disease

The most actionable finding in this analysis is the juxtaposition of KD between the 2 models. On logistic regression, KD was not an independent predictor of referral (aOR 1.11, p = 0.511; Table 2). On Cox analysis, KD independently doubled the hazard of death (HR 2.04, 95% CI 1.30–3.20, p = 0.002; Table 2, Figure 6A). The condition that most dramatically predicts premature death carries no influence on whether patients reach the clinician who might prevent that death. This discordance represents a structural failure of the care pathway.

The mechanism is important: in severe TR, chronic KD is predominantly cardiorenal in etiology, driven by elevated renal venous back-pressure impairing glomerular filtration.5,6 Transcatheter TR repair has demonstrated reversal or stabilization of estimated glomerular filtration rate postprocedure in multiple series, establishing that renal dysfunction is a target, not a contraindication, of TR intervention.5 The window for reversible benefit narrows with chronic KD progression. Our data show that patients with combined kidney and LD had a 36-month survival of 85.0% (Figure 5C), whereas the 245 triple-burden patients with HF, KD, and LD had the highest recorded mortality (22.4%; Figure 3) and a referral rate statistically identical to the overall cohort (33.1%, Figure 1). The sickest patients, those in whom earlier referral may have the greatest potential clinical relevance, receive no preferential access to structural heart care.

The RVSP Paradox: Confirmed From Both Directions

The RVSP paradox is confirmed bidirectionally in this data set: higher RVSP independently predicts reduced referral (aOR 1.02/mmHg, p < 0.001; Figure 2), and RVSP ≥40 mmHg is an independent Cox mortality predictor (HR 1.54, 95% CI 1.07–2.24, p = 0.021; Table 2, Figures 5D and 6A). The Kaplan–Meier curves for RVSP ≥40 vs. <40 mmHg (Figure 5D) diverge from 12 months, with 86.3% versus 89.9% 36-month survival (log-rank p = 0.003). These findings suggest patients with a more severe hemodynamic profile are simultaneously at higher risk of death and less likely to reach a clinician who can modify that risk.

The mechanism is clear: elevated pulmonary pressure is perceived as prohibitive procedural risk, deterring referral before specialist assessment can even occur. This calculus belongs to the surgical era.9,10 In the transcatheter era, both TriClip and EVOQUE have demonstrated favorable safety profiles in patients with pulmonary hypertension at baseline, and favorable right ventricular–pulmonary artery remodeling postprocedure supports earlier intervention before irreversible right ventricular remodeling.13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 The 2025 American College of Cardiology ECDP explicitly does not contraindicate evaluation for patients with elevated RVSP.20 Systematic re-education of managing clinicians about this paradigm shift is essential. Notably, RVSP in this study is an echocardiography-derived estimate that may be less accurate in the setting of severe TR; these findings should ideally be validated using invasively obtained hemodynamic data.

Survival Gradient by Organ Burden and Comorbidity Profile

The Kaplan–Meier organ burden analysis (Figure 5C) demonstrates a clear and statistically significant (p = 0.003) graded survival relationship: patients with no organ involvement have 36-month survival of 92.7%, those with KD or LD alone 86.5%, and those with both conditions 85.0%. The 12-month survival by comorbidity profile (Figure 6B) provides further granularity, showing a gradient from >98% in patients without comorbidities to 92.3% in the KD + LD group, with CIs that separate meaningfully by 12 months. This survival gradient was not accompanied by a corresponding referral gradient; triple-burden patients were not more likely to be evaluated than patients without organ involvement (33.1 vs. 32.8% overall).21 The TRI-SCORE framework, which explicitly incorporates glomerular filtration rate ≤30 mL/min and elevated bilirubin as indicators for intervention timing, much like right ventricular–pulmonary artery coupling–based risk scores developed for the same purpose, presupposes that patients have already reached a structural heart team, a presupposition our data show is unmet in the majority19,22

Toward a Referral Architecture for the Transcatheter TR Era

There is evedence that functional TR confers excess mortality independent of, and in addition to, underlying HF severity.23,24 The evidence in this paper defines the problem precisely, and the solution follows directly. An echocardiography-triggered SHD referral alert, activated at report finalization on detection of severe TR, independent of any downstream clinical filtering for comorbidity or perceived procedural risk, is the single highest-yield structural intervention. This model has successfully accelerated transcatheter aortic valve replacement referral in aortic stenosis programs.25 There is no technical barrier to extending it to TR. Under current practice, the referral decision is made by the managing physician applying an implicit calculus dominated by HF status and deterred by organ comorbidity and high RVSP, all 3 of which are discordant with our survival data.

Quality metrics tied to TR referral rates (e.g., proportion of patients with echocardiographically diagnosed severe TR receiving SHD evaluation within 90 days) would create institutional accountability. Clinical decision support embedded in electronic health records can flag nonreferred patients with severe TR at subsequent encounters. Continuing medical education must address the organ damage paradox, the RVSP paradox, and the survival data presented here.13, 14, 15 The 2025 American College of Cardiology ECDP provides the clinical rationale; this paper provides the outcomes evidence that its recommendations are not being followed.20

Conclusion

In a contemporary registry of 1000 patients with severe TR, more than two-thirds never received SHD specialist evaluation despite near-universal symptomatic status and profound multiorgan comorbidity. SHD evaluation was independently associated with a 45% lower hazard of death on multivariable survival analysis, establishing referral as an independent survival determinant rather than merely a process metric. KD, which doubled the hazard of death and was the strongest mortality predictor beyond HF, carried no independent weight on referral probability, and patients with higher RVSP faced both reduced referral likelihood and worse survival outcomes. Two United States Food and Drug Administration–approved transcatheter therapies now exist for severe TR, although the infrastructure to deliver them to patients who need them does not. Echocardiography-triggered referral pathways, institutional quality metrics, and sustained clinical education are urgently needed to bridge the gap between diagnosis and therapy in the transcatheter TR era.
