The role of splicing factor SRSF3 in cancer progression: Mechanisms, biomarkers, and therapeutic implications
Authors: Shidong Wang, Hong Teng, Hongyan Han, Kangjie Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•SRSF3 is a key splicing regulator that drives tumor progression through widespread alternative splicing reprogramming.•Dysregulated SRSF3 influences oncogenic signaling pathways, tumor metabolism, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance.•Targeting SRSF3-mediated splicing networks represents a promising strategy for cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy.

Introduction

SRSF3 (Serine/Arginine-Rich Splicing Factor 3) is a member of the SR protein family, which is known for its critical role in the regulation of RNA splicing [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. These proteins are involved in the recognition of splice sites in pre-mRNA and the regulation of alternative splicing events, which ultimately influence the expression of different protein isoforms [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. SRSF3 specifically plays a crucial role in the regulation of pre-mRNA splicing by interacting with RNA sequences that govern exon inclusion and exclusion. Beyond its role in splicing, SRSF3 has also been implicated in various cellular processes, including cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and cellular response to stress [9]. The functional versatility of SRSF3 makes it a key player in maintaining cellular homeostasis.

In the context of cancer biology, SRSF3 has emerged as an important regulator of tumorigenesis. It influences cancer progression by modulating various signaling pathways, including those involved in cell proliferation, metastasis, and immune evasion. SRSF3 contributes to cancer progression by promoting the expression of key oncogenes and proteins that are involved in tumor growth and survival. For example, it has been shown to regulate the splicing of genes involved in angiogenesis, cell migration, and immune suppression, which are all critical aspects of cancer development. Furthermore, SRSF3 has been linked to therapeutic resistance, as its aberrant splicing activity can contribute to the survival of cancer cells under therapeutic stress. SRSF3 is widely expressed in various types of tumors, including colorectal cancer, lung cancer, cervical cancer, and pancreatic cancer, among others. In these cancers, its expression levels are often elevated, and its dysregulation has been associated with poor prognosis and aggressive tumor characteristics [10]. The role of SRSF3 in regulating immune responses, metabolism, and the tumor microenvironment further emphasizes its relevance in cancer biology.

This review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of the role of SRSF3 in cancer progression. Specifically, it will explore the molecular mechanisms through which SRSF3 drives tumorigenesis and modulates critical cellular functions in cancer cells. We will also discuss the potential of SRSF3 as a cancer biomarker, focusing on its expression patterns in different cancers and its ability to predict prognosis and therapeutic response [11]. Finally, the review will examine the therapeutic implications of targeting SRSF3, highlighting the development of small molecule inhibitors and other strategies aimed at modulating its splicing activity to halt tumor growth and overcome resistance to treatment. By synthesizing the current understanding of SRSF3 in cancer biology, this review will provide insights into how SRSF3 can be utilized as both a diagnostic and therapeutic target, offering new avenues for personalized cancer therapies. Unlike previous reviews that primarily summarized the oncogenic functions and regulatory networks of SRSF3, this review aims to provide a more critical and translationally oriented perspective. We focus on three unresolved issues. First, although SRSF3 is frequently described as an oncogenic splicing factor, its function is not uniformly tumor-promoting across all cancer types, suggesting strong context dependence. Second, many reported SRSF3-regulated pathways, including PI3K/AKT, MAPK, and Hippo signaling, may represent downstream consequences rather than direct mechanistic effects, and the specific splice isoforms responsible for these phenotypes remain incompletely defined. Third, although SRSF3 has been proposed as a biomarker and therapeutic target, its broad physiological expression, essential role in normal RNA metabolism, and limited clinical validation create major barriers to translation. By integrating mechanistic evidence, contradictory findings, and translational limitations, this review provides a balanced framework for understanding when and how SRSF3 may be clinically actionable in cancer.

Overview of splicing factors in cancer

Splicing factors and their roles in RNA processing

Pre-mRNA splicing is a fundamental step of gene expression in eukaryotic cells, enabling the removal of introns and the joining of exons to generate mature messenger RNA (mRNA) [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. This process is catalyzed by a large ribonucleoprotein complex known as the spliceosome, which consists of small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs) and numerous auxiliary proteins called splicing factors. Among these, the serine/arginine-rich (SR) protein family and heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) represent two major classes of regulators that control splice-site recognition and exon selection [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. By binding to specific RNA sequences such as exonic splicing enhancers (ESEs) or silencers (ESSs), these proteins determine whether particular exons are included or skipped during mRNA maturation [22,23].

Alternative splicing is a highly dynamic regulatory mechanism that significantly expands proteomic diversity. A single gene can generate multiple transcript isoforms with distinct biological functions, allowing cells to adapt gene expression programs to developmental cues, tissue specificity, or environmental stress. It is estimated that >90% of human genes undergo alternative splicing, highlighting its importance in normal physiology. In cancer, dysregulation of RNA splicing has emerged as a critical layer of gene regulation that contributes to tumor initiation and progression [[24], [25], [26]]. Abnormal expression or mutation of splicing factors can lead to aberrant splice variants that promote oncogenic phenotypes. These cancer-associated splice variants may alter key biological processes, including cell proliferation, apoptosis resistance, metabolic reprogramming, angiogenesis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and immune evasion. For instance, aberrant splicing can generate protein isoforms that activate oncogenic signaling pathways or suppress tumor suppressor functions. Consequently, splicing factors are increasingly recognized as important regulators of tumor biology and promising targets for therapeutic intervention.

Biological features and functions of SRSF3

Among the SR protein family, Serine/Arginine-Rich Splicing Factor 3 (SRSF3)—also known as SRp20—is one of the smallest and most evolutionarily conserved members. Structurally, SRSF3 contains two major functional domains: an RNA recognition motif (RRM) located at the N-terminus and a serine/arginine-rich (RS) domain at the C-terminus. The RRM domain mediates the binding of SRSF3 to specific RNA sequences within pre-mRNA, whereas the RS domain facilitates protein–protein interactions with other components of the spliceosome and regulates subcellular localization through phosphorylation-dependent mechanisms [27,28]. Through these structural domains, SRSF3 participates in multiple steps of RNA metabolism. It regulates splice-site selection, promotes exon inclusion, and coordinates spliceosome assembly during pre-mRNA processing. Beyond splicing regulation, accumulating evidence suggests that SRSF3 also participates in other post-transcriptional processes, including mRNA export, RNA stability, translation regulation, and microRNA biogenesis. These diverse functions highlight SRSF3 as a multifunctional RNA-binding protein that integrates several layers of gene expression control [29]. In normal physiological conditions, SRSF3 plays an essential role in maintaining cellular homeostasis. It contributes to the regulation of cell cycle progression, genomic stability, stem cell maintenance, and embryonic development. Tight regulation of SRSF3 expression is therefore critical for normal cellular function. However, increasing evidence indicates that dysregulation of SRSF3 is frequently observed in multiple human cancers. In many tumor types, SRSF3 expression is significantly upregulated and correlates with enhanced cell proliferation, invasion, and poor clinical prognosis. Mechanistically, aberrant SRSF3 expression can reprogram alternative splicing networks to favor oncogenic isoforms of key regulatory genes. Through this mechanism, SRSF3 influences diverse oncogenic pathways, including those controlling cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and tumor metabolism [30]. Furthermore, SRSF3 has been implicated in shaping the tumor microenvironment and modulating immune-related signaling pathways, further highlighting its multifaceted role in cancer biology. Taken together, these findings suggest that SRSF3 acts as a central regulator linking RNA splicing to tumor progression. SRSF3 in cancer progression

SRSF3-Mediated regulation of cancer-related alternative splicing

Alternative splicing is a key mechanism that enables cancer cells to rapidly reprogram gene expression and adapt to oncogenic stress. As an important member of the SR protein family, SRSF3 plays a central role in controlling splice-site selection and exon inclusion, thereby influencing the generation of cancer-associated transcript isoforms. Dysregulated expression of SRSF3 can reshape the alternative splicing landscape of tumor cells and promote oncogenic transformation. Mechanistically, SRSF3 recognizes exonic splicing enhancer sequences within pre-mRNA and recruits components of the spliceosome to facilitate exon inclusion. Through this activity, SRSF3 regulates the splicing of numerous genes involved in tumor growth, survival, and metastasis. For example, SRSF3-mediated splicing can modulate genes associated with cytoskeletal remodeling, transcriptional regulation, and signal transduction, thereby enhancing the malignant phenotype of cancer cells [31]. Aberrant splicing driven by SRSF3 often results in the production of protein isoforms that favor oncogenic signaling while suppressing tumor-suppressive pathways. In addition to alternative splicing regulation, SRSF3 can influence RNA stability and translation efficiency, further amplifying its impact on gene expression programs associated with tumor progression. These regulatory functions collectively position SRSF3 as a key post-transcriptional regulator that facilitates tumor cell adaptation and survival.

SRSF3 in cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

A growing body of evidence indicates that SRSF3 plays a significant role in promoting cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, which are hallmarks of tumor progression. Elevated expression of SRSF3 has been reported in multiple malignancies and is frequently associated with increased tumor aggressiveness and poor clinical outcomes [32,33]. SRSF3 contributes to uncontrolled cell proliferation by regulating the expression or splicing of genes involved in cell cycle progression and growth signaling pathways. Through these mechanisms, SRSF3 promotes the transition of cells through key checkpoints of the cell cycle and supports sustained proliferative signaling. Furthermore, SRSF3-mediated splicing events can alter the activity of transcription factors and cytoskeletal regulators that drive cell motility. In addition to proliferation, SRSF3 also facilitates tumor cell migration and invasion, partly through the regulation of genes involved in cytoskeletal organization and extracellular matrix interactions [34,35]. These changes enhance the ability of cancer cells to invade surrounding tissues and disseminate to distant organs. Consequently, SRSF3 overexpression has been linked to enhanced metastatic potential in several tumor types.

Roles of SRSF3 in specific cancer types

Increasing experimental evidence demonstrates that SRSF3 plays tumor-promoting roles across a wide range of malignancies. In colorectal cancer, SRSF3 has been shown to regulate the expression and splicing of multiple genes involved in tumor growth and metastasis. For instance, SRSF3 can modulate the splicing of genes such as ArhGAP30 and SRF, thereby influencing cytoskeletal dynamics, angiogenesis, and metastatic behavior of colorectal cancer cells [36,37]. Additionally, the RBM4–SRSF3–MAP4K4 splicing cascade has been reported to regulate metastatic signatures in colorectal cancer [38]. In lung cancer, SRSF3 contributes to tumor progression by regulating splicing events associated with cell survival and immune responses [39]. Recent studies suggest that SRSF3 may also undergo liquid–liquid phase separation, which may influence transcriptional regulation and RNA processing in tumor cells. Furthermore, silencing SRSF3 has been shown to induce cellular senescence in non-small cell lung cancer, indicating its critical role in maintaining malignant proliferation [40,41]. In cervical cancer, SRSF3 promotes tumor progression by regulating the expression of oncogenic factors such as DDX5, thereby enhancing cancer cell proliferation and tumor growth [42]. Similarly, in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, SRSF3 regulates the alternative splicing of AMOTL1, which subsequently influences YAP1 nuclear translocation and activates oncogenic transcriptional programs [43]. SRSF3 also plays important roles in other malignancies, including pancreatic cancer, gastric cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and endometrial cancer, where it regulates the splicing of key genes involved in tumor development and progression.

SRSF3 in immune evasion, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance

Beyond its intrinsic effects on tumor cells, SRSF3 also contributes to shaping the tumor microenvironment and immune interactions. One important mechanism involves the regulation of immune checkpoint molecules through alternative splicing. For example, SRSF3-mediated splicing of B7-H3 in colorectal cancer has been shown to influence immune regulatory pathways and may contribute to tumor immune evasion [44]. SRSF3 also plays an important role in metastatic progression by controlling splicing events that affect cell adhesion, migration, and signaling pathways associated with epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT). Through these mechanisms, SRSF3 enhances the ability of tumor cells to invade distant tissues and establish secondary tumors. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that SRSF3 contributes to chemotherapy resistance. In pancreatic cancer, SRSF3 regulates the splicing of the m6A-related lncRNA ANRIL, which promotes resistance to the chemotherapeutic drug gemcitabine [45]. Similarly, suppression of SRSF3 expression has been reported to sensitize certain cancer cells to chemotherapeutic agents such as paclitaxel, indicating that SRSF3-mediated RNA processing may influence drug response. Collectively, these findings highlight the multifaceted role of SRSF3 in cancer progression. By regulating alternative splicing networks and interacting with multiple oncogenic pathways, SRSF3 acts as a critical driver of tumor growth, metastasis, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance across diverse cancer types. SRSF3 expression and functions in different cancers.

Current evidence linking SRSF3 to tumor immune evasion is suggestive but not yet definitive. In colorectal cancer, SRSF3 directly binds B7-H3 pre-mRNA and contributes to B7-H3 exon 4 retention, thereby increasing B7-H3 mRNA and protein expression. Because B7-H3 is an immune checkpoint molecule associated with immunosuppressive tumor phenotypes, this finding provides a mechanistic link between SRSF3-mediated splicing and immune checkpoint regulation [44]. However, the available study primarily examined B7-H3 expression and splicing in tumor cells, and did not fully establish functional immune escape using T-cell killing assays, immune co-culture systems, or immune-competent in vivo models. Therefore, SRSF3-mediated immune evasion should be described as a plausible and emerging mechanism that requires further functional validation. In addition, SRSF3 has been shown to regulate PD-1 exon 3 inclusion, thereby influencing the balance between full-length PD-1 and soluble PD-1 isoforms, which further supports a role f splicing.

Integration of SRSF3-regulated targets into a coherent oncogenic program

Although individual SRSF3-regulated targets are often described separately, these targets can be integrated into a coherent oncogenic program that links alternative splicing reprogramming to multiple stages of tumor progression. In colorectal cancer, SRSF3-mediated regulation of ArhGAP30 and MAP4K4 mainly converges on cytoskeletal remodeling, cell motility, and metastatic behavior. ArhGAP30 is associated with Rho GTPase-related cytoskeletal regulation, whereas the RBM4–SRSF3–MAP4K4 splicing cascade contributes to metastatic signatures and MAPK-related signaling [38]. Together, these events may enhance the ability of tumor cells to reorganize their cytoskeleton, migrate, invade surrounding tissues, and acquire a more metastatic phenotype. SRSF3 also contributes to the establishment of a supportive tumor microenvironment. The regulation of SRF splicing links SRSF3 activity to angiogenesis in colorectal cancer. Because angiogenesis supplies oxygen and nutrients and facilitates metastatic dissemination, SRF-associated vascular remodeling may cooperate with ArhGAP30- and MAP4K4-dependent motility programs to support both primary tumor expansion and metastatic spread. Thus, SRSF3-regulated splicing does not simply promote intrinsic cancer cell proliferation; it may also remodel the extracellular and vascular context required for tumor progression.

In addition to cytoskeletal and angiogenic regulation, SRSF3 influences oncogenic transcriptional and RNA-processing programs through targets such as DDX5. In cervical cancer, SRSF3-mediated regulation of DDX5 promotes tumor cell proliferation and tumor growth. As an RNA helicase and transcription-associated regulator, DDX5 may amplify SRSF3-driven effects by connecting alternative splicing with broader transcriptional and post-transcriptional control. This creates a feed-forward regulatory layer in which SRSF3 not only changes individual splice isoforms but also influences RNA-processing factors that further reshape oncogenic gene expression [42].

The AMOTL1/YAP1 axis provides another example of how SRSF3-regulated splicing can be connected to a broader signaling program. In nasopharyngeal carcinoma, SRSF3 regulates AMOTL1 alternative splicing, thereby affecting YAP1 nuclear translocation and Hippo pathway-related transcriptional activity. This mechanism links SRSF3-dependent splice-site regulation to transcriptional programs that support proliferation, survival, and metastatic potential [43]. In parallel, SRSF3-mediated regulation of the m6A-related lncRNA ANRIL in pancreatic cancer extends this oncogenic program to therapeutic resistance, particularly resistance to gemcitabine [45]. ANRIL-associated splicing changes may help tumor cells survive under chemotherapy-induced stress, indicating that SRSF3-regulated networks contribute not only to tumor expansion but also to treatment adaptation.

Taken together, these targets suggest that SRSF3 does not drive cancer progression through a single linear pathway. Instead, SRSF3 coordinates multiple interconnected modules: cytoskeletal plasticity and invasion through ArhGAP30 and MAP4K4, angiogenic support through SRF, transcriptional and RNA-processing amplification through DDX5, Hippo/YAP-dependent growth and survival through AMOTL1/YAP1, and chemotherapy resistance through ANRIL. This integrated view provides a mechanistic rationale for why SRSF3 dysregulation can simultaneously influence proliferation, metastasis, microenvironmental remodeling, and therapeutic resistance across different cancer contexts.

Mechanisms of SRSF3 in tumor biology

To avoid overinterpreting pathway-level observations, this review distinguishes direct SRSF3-mediated splicing regulation from indirect SRSF3-associated pathway changes. Direct regulation refers to cases in which SRSF3 binds a specific pre-mRNA and alters a defined splicing event, such as exon inclusion, exon skipping, or isoform switching. Strong evidence usually includes RNA immunoprecipitation or CLIP-based binding data, minigene or splice-reporter assays, isoform-specific validation, and rescue experiments using the relevant splice isoform. In contrast, indirect regulation refers to changes in pathway activity, gene expression, phosphorylation status, or cellular phenotype observed after SRSF3 knockdown or overexpression without evidence that SRSF3 directly binds and splices a pathway component. We therefore use the term “SRSF3-regulated splicing event” only for direct or mechanistically supported targets, whereas broader changes in PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK, immune, or metabolic pathways are described as “SRSF3-associated” unless causal splice isoforms have been defined.

Upstream regulatory mechanisms driving SRSF3 dysregulation in cancer

Dysregulation of SRSF3 in cancer is not only a consequence of altered splicing demand but is also shaped by multilayered upstream regulatory mechanisms. These mechanisms operate at transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and post-translational levels and may explain why SRSF3 displays tumor-type-specific expression patterns and context-dependent biological functions. At the transcriptional level, SRSF3 expression can be controlled by oncogenic transcriptional and epigenetic programs. In colorectal cancer, LINC01210 has been reported to promote malignant phenotypes by epigenetically upregulating SRSF3, suggesting that non-coding RNAs may regulate SRSF3 at the chromatin or transcriptional level [31]. HOXB9 has also been shown to promote colon cancer progression by targeting SRSF3, linking developmental transcriptional programs to SRSF3-driven splicing regulation [32]. In addition, hnRNP L is important for SRSF3 expression and oncogenic potential in oral squamous cell carcinoma cells, indicating that RNA-binding protein networks may act upstream of SRSF3 and reinforce oncogenic RNA-processing states [18]. UPF3A-mediated upregulation of SRSF3 in colorectal cancer further suggests that nonsense-mediated decay-associated factors may participate in compensatory regulation of oncogenic splicing factors [33].

At the post-transcriptional level, SRSF3 expression is regulated by microRNAs and non-coding RNA networks. miR-1908–5p has been reported to contribute to the oncogenic function of SRSF3, supporting a regulatory connection between microRNA signaling and SRSF3-dependent tumor biology [25]. Another example is the circRNA/miR-486–5p/SRSF3 axis in hepatocellular carcinoma, in which SCARB1-derived circ_0029343 promotes tumor growth and metastasis by modulating p73 splicing through the miR-486–5p/SRSF3 pathway [46]. These findings indicate that SRSF3 may be embedded within competing endogenous RNA networks, where circRNAs or lncRNAs indirectly alter SRSF3 abundance by sequestering SRSF3-targeting microRNAs. In addition, antisense oligonucleotides targeting SRSF3 regulatory elements can suppress SRSF3 expression, further demonstrating that SRSF3 levels can be modulated through RNA-level mechanisms. At the post-translational level, phosphorylation and protein–protein interactions are central to SRSF3 activity. As an SR protein, SRSF3 contains an RS domain whose phosphorylation status affects RNA binding, spliceosome assembly, nuclear speckle localization, nucleocytoplasmic shuttling, and interactions with other RNA-processing factors. In hepatocellular carcinoma, PPM1G promotes tumor progression by regulating the phosphorylation of SRSF3, providing cancer-specific evidence that post-translational control of SRSF3 can influence oncogenic splicing programs [47]. Kinase/phosphatase-mediated regulation of SRSF3 may also affect its interaction with export adaptors and RNA-binding partners such as NXF1, DDX5, and YTHDC1, thereby linking SRSF3 phosphorylation to RNA export, m6A-associated RNA fate, and non-splicing functions.

Together, these upstream mechanisms suggest that SRSF3 dysregulation in cancer results from coordinated control at multiple molecular layers rather than simple overexpression. Transcriptional activation, ncRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulation, and phosphorylation-dependent functional modulation may cooperate to determine SRSF3 abundance, localization, binding specificity, and downstream splicing outcomes. Understanding these upstream controls is essential for identifying tumor contexts in which SRSF3 is a driver, a compensatory factor, or a therapeutic vulnerability.

SRSF3-mediated alternative splicing and tumor cell fate regulation

SRSF3 functions as a critical regulator of alternative splicing networks that control key cellular processes involved in tumor biology. By binding to exonic splicing enhancer sequences within pre-mRNA transcripts, SRSF3 promotes exon inclusion and influences the generation of protein isoforms with distinct biological functions. Dysregulation of SRSF3 expression can therefore reshape transcriptomic programs that support tumor cell survival and adaptation [48,49]. One major mechanism through which SRSF3 contributes to tumor progression is the regulation of cell cycle control and proliferation-related genes. Aberrant SRSF3 activity can alter the splicing patterns of genes involved in cell cycle checkpoints, enabling tumor cells to bypass growth restrictions and sustain uncontrolled proliferation. In addition, SRSF3 has been shown to influence cellular senescence pathways; for instance, suppression of SRSF3 can induce senescence in certain cancer cells, suggesting that elevated SRSF3 expression helps maintain proliferative capacity in tumors.

Beyond cell cycle regulation, SRSF3 also participates in the regulation of tumor metabolism. Cancer cells frequently undergo metabolic reprogramming to support rapid growth and survival under stressful conditions. By modulating the splicing of metabolic regulators and signaling molecules, SRSF3 contributes to the metabolic flexibility of tumor cells. These splicing events may influence pathways involved in lipid metabolism, oxidative stress responses, and cellular redox balance, thereby facilitating tumor growth and survival.

Furthermore, SRSF3 has been implicated in immune-related regulatory mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment. Alternative splicing events controlled by SRSF3 can alter the expression of immune regulatory molecules, thereby influencing immune surveillance and tumor immune evasion [50]. Through these mechanisms, SRSF3 acts as a key post-transcriptional regulator linking RNA processing with multiple aspects of tumor cell biology.

Mechanistic evidence linking SRSF3-regulated splicing to immune regulation and oncogenic signaling

A major limitation in the current literature is that many studies report pathway changes after SRSF3 knockdown or overexpression without defining the precise splice isoforms responsible for these effects. Therefore, SRSF3-linked signaling pathways should be interpreted according to the strength of mechanistic evidence.

The best-supported immune-related mechanism involves the immune checkpoint molecule B7-H3 in colorectal cancer. Zhang et al. showed that SRSF3 knockdown markedly reduced B7-H3 mRNA and protein expression, whereas SRSF3 overexpression increased B7-H3 expression in CRC cells [44]. RIP assays further demonstrated direct binding of SRSF3 to B7-H3 mRNA, and minigene experiments showed that SRSF3 contributed to B7-H3 exon 4 retention by binding to exon 4 and/or exon 6. Because B7-H3 is an immune checkpoint molecule associated with immune suppression and poor prognosis, this mechanism provides a concrete example of how SRSF3-mediated splicing may influence tumor immune regulation. However, this study mainly demonstrated regulation of an immune checkpoint molecule rather than direct T-cell suppression or immune escape in an immune-competent model. Thus, SRSF3-dependent immune evasion remains aetely validated mechanism.

Another immune-related example is PD-1 isoform regulation. Wang et al. identified an exonic splicing enhancer in PD-1 exon 3 and showed that SRSF3 binds this element to promote exon 3 inclusion and full-length PD-1 expression. Blocking the SRSF3–ESE interaction with an antisense oligonucleotide increased exon 3 skipping and soluble PD-1 expression. This study provides a defined splicing event—PD-1 exon 3 inclusion versus skipping—but its relevance to tumor immune evasion requires further validation in immune interaction models [50]. For the MAPK-related pathway, the clearest mechanistic evidence comes from the RBM4–SRSF3–MAP4K4 splicing cascade in colorectal cancer. Alternatively spliced MAP4K4 variants showed different effects on JNK1 phosphorylation, which subsequently altered E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and vimentin expression, thereby influencing migration and invasion of CRC cells. Therefore, the pathway should be described more specifically as a MAP4K4–JNK1–EMT/migration axil “MAPK signaling” effect [38]. For Hippo/YAP signaling, Xu et al. identified the SRSF3/AMOTL1 splicing axis in nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Mechanistically, SRSF3 promotes the generation of the oncogenic AMOTL1-L transcript. AMOTL1-L preferentially localizes intracellularly and interacts with YAP1, thereby facilitating YAP1 nuclear entry and activation of YAP-dependent transcriptional programs. This provides a direct example in which SRSF3-regulated alternative splicing generates a specific isoform that cogenic signaling pathway [43]. For PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, available evidence is less direct. In gastric cancer, SRSF3 knockdown inhibited PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation and reduced proliferation and migration, while similar pathway suppression has been reported in cervical cancer models. However, these studies did not clearly define a specific SRSF3-regulated splice isoform that directly activates PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling. Therefore, PI3K/AKT/mTOR should be presented as an SRSF3-associated downstream signaling change rather than a fullicing-mediated pathway [51].

Collectively, current evidence supports a tiered model of SRSF3-mediated oncogenic signaling. B7-H3, PD-1, MAP4K4, SRF, and AMOTL1 represent relatively well-defined splicing-related targets, although their immune and pathway consequences differ in the depth of validation. In contrast, PI3K/AKT/mTOR activation remains mechanistically underdefined and requires future studies to identify the causal splice isoforms, upstream intermediates, and rescue experiments that link SRSF3 to pathway activation.

Interactions between SRSF3 and oncogenic targets

SRSF3 exerts its tumor-promoting effects through interactions with multiple oncogenic targets and RNA-binding partners. These interactions enable SRSF3 to regulate the splicing or expression of genes that are directly involved in tumor progression. One important example is the immune checkpoint molecule B7-H3, whose alternative splicing has been shown to be regulated by SRSF3 in colorectal cancer [[52], [53], [54], [55], [56]]. By modulating B7-H3 isoforms, SRSF3 may influence immune signaling pathways and contribute to tumor immune evasion. SRSF3 has also been reported to regulate the alternative splicing of ILF3, an RNA-binding protein involved in gene transcription, RNA stability, and cell proliferation [57]. Through this mechanism, SRSF3 promotes oncogenic transformation and supports sustained tumor cell growth.

Another important interaction involves the transcription factor SP4, whose alternative splicing can be influenced by SRSF3 in certain cancers. Regulation of SP4 isoforms may affect transcriptional programs associated with tumor progression, including genes involved in cell proliferation and survival [58]. In addition, SRSF3 can interact with other regulatory proteins such as NXF1 and DDX5, forming functional networks that coordinate RNA processing and gene expression [59]. These interactions highlight the complex regulatory landscape through which SRSF3 controls oncogenic signaling pathways.

Overall, SRSF3 acts as a central node in post-transcriptional gene regulation, integrating alternative splicing events with major oncogenic signaling pathways. Through its interactions with multiple molecular targets, SRSF3 orchestrates transcriptional and metabolic programs that promote tumor growth, metastasis, and immune escape. Emerging non-canonical functions of SRSF3: epitranscriptomics, phase separation, and non-splicing regulation

Although SRSF3 is best known as a regulator of splice-site selection, recent studies indicate that its function extends beyond canonical alternative splicing. These emerging areas are important because they suggest that SRSF3 may coordinate multiple layers of RNA regulation, including RNA modification-dependent processing, nuclear export, translational control, microRNA biogenesis, and biomolecular condensate formation.

SRSF3 and epitranscriptomic regulation

One important emerging direction is the interaction between SRSF3 and RNA modifications, particularly N6-methyladenosine (m6A). In pancreatic cancer, SRSF3 was shown to regulate both the splicing and m6A modification of the lncRNA ANRIL, thereby promoting gemcitabine resistance [45]. This finding links SRSF3-mediated splicing with epitranscriptomic regulation and therapeutic adaptation. Importantly, the m6A reader YTHDC1 can interact with SRSF3 and facilitate the nuclear export of m6A-modified mRNAs through the SRSF3–NXF1 pathway, indicating that SRSF3 may act as an adaptor connecting m6A recognition with RNA processing and export [60,61]. More recent work further showed that the phosphorylation status of the SRSF3 RS domain influences its interaction with YTHDC1, suggesting that post-translational modification of SRSF3 may regulate its epitranscriptomic functions. These studies collectively indicate that SRSF3 should not be viewed only as a splicing factor, but also as a component of m6A-dependent RNA fate regulation.

SRSF3 and liquid–liquid phase separation

Liquid–liquid phase separation is another recent area that may expand the mechanistic understanding of SRSF3. A 2025 study reported that SRSF3 contains intrinsically disordered regions and can form dynamic nuclear foci with phase-separation properties, supported by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching and fusion/fission behavior of SRSF3 puncta [39]. In lung cancer, SRSF3 expression was associated with immunity- and ferroptosis-related genes, and SRSF3 knockdown inhibited lung cancer cell proliferation and migration. These findings suggest that SRSF3-containing condensates may spatially organize RNA-processing machinery or signaling-related transcripts in cancer cells. However, whether SRSF3 phase separation directly controls specific oncogenic splice events, immune-related RNA programs, or ferroptosis pathways remains unresolved and requires further mechanistic validation.

Non-splicing functions of SRSF3

SRSF3 also performs several non-splicing functions that may be relevant to tumor biology. For example, SRSF3 participates in mRNA nuclear export, including m6A-modified transcript export through cooperation with YTHDC1 and NXF1. In addition, SRSF3 can regulate translation independently of splicing. It has been reported to repress translation of p21 mRNA, and SRSF3 depletion increases p21 expression and promotes cellular senescence, suggesting a splicing-independent mechanism by which SRSF3 supports proliferative capacity. SRSF3 also controls microRNA biogenesis. Recent work showed that SRSF3 binds CNNC motifs downstream of Drosha cleavage sites within the miR-17–92 cluster and promotes selective processing of miR-17 and miR-20a. Because the miR-17–92 cluster is closely linked to proliferation and oncogenic signaling, this provides a non-canonical route through which SRSF3 may influence cancer cell behavior [60].

Together, these findings broaden the conceptual framework of SRSF3 in cancer. SRSF3 may regulate oncogenesis not only by altering exon inclusion or skipping, but also by coordinating m6A-dependent RNA export, lncRNA modification and splicing, translational repression, microRNA maturation, and condensate-associated RNA organization. Future studies should determine whether these non-canonical functions act independently of splicing or cooperate with SRSF3-mediated alternative splicing to generate integrated oncogenic RNA programs.

SRSF3 as a biomarker in cancer

Current evidence supporting SRSF3 as a biomarker

Accumulating evidence suggests that SRSF3 has significant potential as a biomarker in cancer, reflecting its central role in alternative splicing regulation and tumor-associated gene expression programs [[62], [63], [64]]. Because RNA splicing is frequently dysregulated during tumorigenesis, splicing regulators such as SRSF3 often show altered expression patterns in malignant tissues compared with normal counterparts. These changes may influence not only tumor biology but also clinical outcomes, making SRSF3 an attractive candidate for diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive biomarker development [65]. From a diagnostic perspective, SRSF3 expression has been reported to be dysregulated across multiple tumor types. Elevated expression levels of SRSF3 have been observed in cancers such as colorectal cancer, cervical cancer, lung cancer, and pancreatic cancer, where it is often associated with increased proliferative capacity and tumor aggressiveness. In clinical specimens, detection of SRSF3 expression through transcriptomic analyses or protein-based approaches such as immunohistochemistry may provide useful information regarding tumor presence and biological behavior. Moreover, because SRSF3 regulates numerous cancer-related splicing events, its expression may reflect broader alterations in post-transcriptional gene regulation within tumors. These features suggest that SRSF3 could contribute to molecular classification systems that distinguish aggressive tumor phenotypes from less invasive disease.

Beyond diagnosis, SRSF3 has also been investigated as a prognostic biomarker. Several studies have demonstrated correlations between SRSF3 expression levels and patient survival outcomes across different cancer types. High SRSF3 expression is frequently associated with enhanced tumor proliferation, metastatic potential, and resistance to therapy, all of which contribute to unfavorable clinical outcomes [46,66,67]. Conversely, in certain cellular contexts, loss or suppression of SRSF3 activity may alter splicing patterns in ways that inhibit tumor progression. These observations highlight the complex and context-dependent roles of SRSF3 in cancer biology. Nevertheless, the overall trend across many malignancies indicates that dysregulated SRSF3 expression is linked to tumor aggressiveness and may therefore provide prognostic value in clinical oncology [68].

Importantly, emerging research also indicates that SRSF3 is closely connected to tumor immune regulation. Alternative splicing plays an increasingly recognized role in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment by generating isoforms of immune-related molecules that influence immune recognition and signaling. SRSF3 has been shown to regulate the splicing of immune-associated genes, including immune checkpoint molecules and cytokine-related pathways, thereby affecting immune cell interactions within the tumor microenvironment. For instance, SRSF3-mediated splicing of immune regulatory molecules such as B7-H3 can influence tumor immune escape mechanisms [[69], [70], [71], [72], [73]]. Through such processes, SRSF3 may contribute to the development of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that favors tumor survival and progression. The relationship between SRSF3 and immune regulation also raises the possibility that SRSF3 could serve as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response. Immunotherapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors rely on effective recognition of tumor cells by the immune system. Since SRSF3 influences the splicing and expression of immune regulatory molecules, variations in its activity may affect tumor immunogenicity and responsiveness to immunotherapy. Tumors with high SRSF3 activity may exhibit enhanced immune evasion mechanisms, potentially contributing to reduced therapeutic responses. Conversely, targeting SRSF3-mediated splicing networks could theoretically improve immune-mediated tumor clearance by restoring immune recognition pathways. Despite these promising observations, several challenges remain before SRSF3 can be widely applied as a clinical biomarker. One limitation is that SRSF3 is broadly expressed in many normal tissues, which may complicate the interpretation of expression levels in clinical samples. Additionally, because SRSF3 regulates a large network of splicing targets, its biological effects may vary depending on tumor type, genetic background, and microenvironmental conditions. Future studies integrating large-scale transcriptomic data, functional experiments, and clinical cohort analyses will be essential to clarify the precise biomarker value of SRSF3.

Overall, current evidence indicates that SRSF3 represents a promising biomarker candidate in cancer, with potential applications in diagnosis, prognosis, and prediction of therapeutic response. A deeper understanding of SRSF3-mediated splicing networks and their impact on tumor–immune interactions will further enhance its translational relevance and may open new avenues for precision oncology. Limitations of clinical validation and requirements for biomarker development

Although SRSF3 has been proposed as a candidate biomarker in multiple cancers, its clinical utility remains incompletely validated. Most available studies have reported associations between SRSF3 expression and tumor aggressiveness, survival outcomes, or immune-related features [4,74]. However, correlation does not necessarily establish diagnostic or predictive value. At present, there is limited evidence demonstrating that SRSF3 expression alone can distinguish cancer from non-malignant tissues with sufficient sensitivity and specificity, independently predict patient survival beyond established clinicopathological variables, or guide treatment selection in prospective clinical settings. Several issues limit the current biomarker interpretation of SRSF3. First, SRSF3 is broadly expressed in normal tissues and participates in essential RNA-processing events, which may reduce tumor specificity. Second, total SRSF3 expression may not accurately reflect functional SRSF3 activity, because its biological output depends on phosphorylation status, interacting RNA-binding proteins, subcellular localization, and available pre-mRNA targets. Third, SRSF3 may have context-dependent roles across cancer types, meaning that high expression may not carry the same biological or prognostic meaning in all tumors. Fourth, evidence supporting SRSF3 as a predictive biomarker for therapeutic response, especially immunotherapy response, remains largely indirect and requires validation using treatment-annotated clinical cohorts.

Therefore, future biomarker studies should move beyond measuring total SRSF3 abundance. More informative strategies may include isoform-level transcriptomics, SRSF3-dependent splicing signatures, integration with immune profiling, and multivariable models incorporating tumor stage, molecular subtype, and treatment outcome. Prospective cohorts, standardized immunohistochemistry scoring, independent validation datasets, and receiver operating characteristic or survival modeling will be required before SRSF3 can be considered a clinically actionable biomarker.

Therapeutic implications of targeting SRSF3

SRSF3 as a therapeutic target

Given its central role in regulating alternative splicing and oncogenic gene expression programs, SRSF3 has emerged as a promising therapeutic target in cancer treatment. Dysregulated SRSF3 activity can drive tumor progression by promoting oncogenic splice variants and enhancing signaling pathways associated with proliferation, survival, and metastasis. Therefore, strategies aimed at inhibiting SRSF3 or modulating its splicing activity have attracted increasing attention as potential anticancer approaches. One of the most direct strategies involves the development of small-molecule inhibitors that target SRSF3 activity. For example, the compound SFI003 has been identified as a novel inhibitor that suppresses tumor growth by interfering with the SRSF3/DHCR24/ROS regulatory axis in colorectal cancer [75]. In preclinical studies, SFI003 treatment reduced cancer cell proliferation and induced oxidative stress, ultimately leading to tumor cell death. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of pharmacologically targeting SRSF3-mediated splicing networks.

In addition to small-molecule inhibitors, other molecular strategies are being explored to suppress SRSF3 activity. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) and RNA interference technologies have been used to reduce SRSF3 expression, resulting in decreased tumor cell proliferation and increased sensitivity to chemotherapy in several cancer models [[76], [77], [78], [79], [80]]. For instance, downregulation of SRSF3 using antisense oligonucleotides has been shown to sensitize certain tumor cells to chemotherapeutic agents such as paclitaxel [81]. These results suggest that SRSF3 inhibition may enhance the effectiveness of conventional cancer therapies. Notably, although this study focused on SRSF7 rather than SRSF3, ASO-mediated disruption of the cALG8–CLK1–SRSF7 axis, combined with gemcitabine and anti-PD-1 treatment, reduced tumor burden in patient-derived xenograft models of drug-resistant pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. This finding suggests that phosphorylation-dependent SRSF splicing networks may be therapeutically targetable and provides a conceptual framework for developing SRSF3-directed splice-modulating strategies [82]. Emerging approaches also include gene-editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas systems, which provide opportunities to selectively disrupt SRSF3 expression or correct oncogenic splicing patterns regulated by SRSF3. Although these approaches remain largely experimental, they illustrate the expanding toolkit available for targeting splicing regulators in cancer. Overall, targeting SRSF3 offers a promising strategy for interfering with multiple oncogenic pathways simultaneously. Because SRSF3 functions as a master regulator of RNA processing, its inhibition may disrupt a broad network of tumor-promoting mechanisms, providing potential advantages over therapies that target single signaling molecules [83].

Proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) provide a potential strategy for targeting proteins that are difficult to inhibit with conventional small molecules [84,85]. Unlike classical inhibitors, PROTACs induce proteasomal degradation by recruiting an E3 ubiquitin ligase to the protein of interest. This event-driven mechanism may be relevant for RNA-binding proteins such as SRSF3, which lack conventional catalytic pockets and are therefore difficult to directly inhibit. For SRSF3, PROTAC-based degradation remains a conceptual but potentially valuable direction. A putative SRSF3 degrader would require an SRSF3-binding ligand or RNA-mimetic warhead, an E3 ligase-recruiting module such as CRBN or VHL ligand, and an optimized linker that enables productive ternary complex formation. In principle, SRSF3 degradation could suppress both canonical splicing functions and non-splicing activities, including RNA export, translational regulation, microRNA processing, and phase-separation-associated RNA organization [4,36,74]. RNA-PROTACs may also provide a useful framework for targeting SRSF3 and other RNA-binding proteins by using RNA or RNA-mimetic elements to engage the RNA-binding domain. However, no validated SRSF3-specific PROTAC has yet been reported. Major challenges include identification of selective SRSF3-binding ligands, nuclear delivery, tumor selectivity, off-target degradation, and potential toxicity caused by global depletion of an essential splicing factor. Therefore, PROTAC-based SRSF3 targeting should currently be considered a forward-looking therapeutic concept rather than an established treatment strategy. Future studies should define SRSF3-dependent tumor vulnerabilities and develop pharmacodynamic biomarkers based on SRSF3-regulated splice isoform changes.

Challenges in SRSF3 drug development

Despite growing interest in targeting SRSF3, drug development against this splicing factor faces substantial biological and pharmacological barriers. First, SRSF3 is not a tumor-specific protein. It is broadly expressed and participates in essential RNA-processing events in normal cells. Systemic inhibition of SRSF3 may therefore disrupt physiological splicing programs and cause toxicity, particularly in rapidly proliferating or highly transcriptionally active tissues. This raises an important therapeutic question: whether cancer cells are selectively dependent on SRSF3-regulated oncogenic splice isoforms, or whether SRSF3 inhibition would mainly produce nonspecific global splicing stress [39,42]. Second, SRSF3 is an RNA-binding protein rather than a conventional enzyme with a defined catalytic pocket. This makes selective small-molecule development more difficult than targeting kinases or proteases. Although SFI003 and other experimental approaches suggest that SRSF3-associated pathways can be pharmacologically perturbed, the specificity, direct binding mechanism, pharmacokinetic properties, and long-term safety of such strategies remain to be rigorously established [37,65]. Third, RNA-based strategies, including antisense oligonucleotides, siRNAs, and splice-switching oligonucleotides, offer a rational approach to modulate SRSF3 expression or SRSF3-dependent splicing events. However, these approaches face major delivery barriers in solid tumors, including poor tumor penetration, stromal restriction, endosomal trapping, off-target effects, and variable uptake across heterogeneous tumor cell populations. Tumor-selective delivery systems will therefore be essential for translating RNA-based SRSF3-targeted therapies. Fourth, compensatory splicing networks may limit therapeutic durability. Cancer cells may adapt to SRSF3 inhibition by altering the activity of other SR proteins, hnRNPs, spliceosome components, or RNA-binding proteins. Such compensation could reduce therapeutic efficacy or generate resistance. Future studies should use CRISPR screening, CLIP-seq, long-read sequencing, and patient-derived models to identify tumors that are specifically dependent on SRSF3-regulated splicing programs. Finally, clinical translation will require robust pharmacodynamic biomarkers. Instead of relying only on reduced SRSF3 expression, future trials should monitor target splice switching, downstream isoform changes, pathway inhibition, and tumor-selective biological effects. At present, SRSF3-targeted therapy remains a preclinical concept, and its successful development will depend on defining cancer-specific splicing vulnerabilities, improving delivery technologies, minimizing toxicity, and identifying patients most likely to benefit. Future research should also focus on identifying biomarkers that predict sensitivity to SRSF3-targeted therapies, as well as defining the specific tumor contexts in which SRSF3 inhibition is most effective. With continued progress in understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying SRSF3 function, targeted modulation of this splicing factor may eventually become a viable strategy for precision cancer therapy. Future directions and challenges

Context-dependent and controversial roles of SRSF3 in cancer

Although SRSF3 is commonly considered an oncogenic splicing factor, this interpretation may be overly simplified. In many cancers, including colorectal cancer, cervical cancer, lung cancer, and pancreatic cancer, SRSF3 overexpression promotes malignant phenotypes through regulation of tumor-associated splicing events [39,42,45,75,82,86]. However, studies in renal cell carcinoma and endometrial cancer suggest that SRSF3 may also participate in tumor-suppressive regulatory networks, particularly through interactions with SP4-related splicing programs [58,59]. These findings indicate that SRSF3 should not be viewed as a universal oncogene. Instead, its biological output depends on tumor lineage, upstream regulators, interacting RNA-binding proteins, and the repertoire of available pre-mRNA targets. A second unresolved issue is whether SRSF3 directly drives oncogenic signaling pathways or indirectly modulates them through upstream splicing events. Many studies report changes in PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK, or Hippo/YAP signaling after SRSF3 inhibition or overexpression [51,87,88]. However, pathway activation alone does not prove direct mechanistic regulation. Future studies should define the precise splice isoforms, RNA-binding sites, and causal links connecting SRSF3 to these signaling cascades. This distinction is essential because therapeutic targeting of SRSF3 will only be rational if cancer cells depend on specific SRSF3-mediated splicing events rather than broad and nonspecific splicing disruption. A third controversy concerns the biomarker value of SRSF3. Although high SRSF3 expression has been associated with aggressive tumor behavior in several malignancies, expression level alone may not accurately reflect functional SRSF3 activity. SRSF3-dependent splicing signatures, rather than total SRSF3 abundance, may provide more informative biomarkers for prognosis or treatment response. Therefore, future clinical studies should integrate SRSF3 expression with isoform-level transcriptomics, immune profiling, and treatment outcome data.

Translational hurdles for SRSF3-targeted therapy

Despite its therapeutic potential, targeting SRSF3 faces substantial translational barriers. First, SRSF3 is widely expressed and participates in essential RNA-processing events in normal cells. Systemic inhibition may therefore disrupt physiological splicing programs and cause toxicity in proliferative tissues, immune cells, or metabolically active organs. For this reason, future therapeutic strategies should ideally target cancer-specific SRSF3-dependent splicing events rather than globally suppressing SRSF3 expression. Second, SRSF3 is an RNA-binding protein rather than a conventional enzyme with a well-defined catalytic pocket. This makes selective small-molecule inhibition technically difficult. Although preclinical inhibitors and RNA-based approaches have shown encouraging activity, their specificity, pharmacokinetics, tumor selectivity, and long-term safety remain insufficiently validated. Antisense oligonucleotides, siRNAs, and other RNA-based strategies also face delivery challenges, especially in solid tumors with stromal barriers, heterogeneous vascularization, and inefficient intracellular uptake [[89], [90], [91]]. Third, tumor cells may compensate for SRSF3 inhibition by rewiring other splicing regulators, including SR proteins, hnRNPs, and spliceosome-associated factors. Such compensatory networks may limit the durability of SRSF3-targeted therapies and contribute to acquired resistance. Systematic CRISPR screening, single-cell transcriptomics, long-read sequencing, and patient-derived tumor models will be needed to identify tumors that are truly dependent on SRSF3-regulated splicing programs.

Future research priorities

Future research should focus on defining clinically actionable SRSF3-dependent splicing vulnerabilities. In immunotherapy, it will be important to determine whether SRSF3-regulated isoforms influence antigen presentation, immune checkpoint expression, T-cell infiltration, or resistance to immune checkpoint blockade. In combination therapy, SRSF3 inhibition should be evaluated together with chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy in molecularly defined tumor contexts rather than as a broad universal strategy. Multi-omics approaches will be particularly important for clarifying SRSF3 function. Integrating transcriptomics, proteomics, epigenomics, CLIP-seq, long-read sequencing, and clinical outcome data may help distinguish driver splicing events from passenger alterations. In parallel, patient-derived organoids, xenografts, and immune-competent models should be used to evaluate efficacy, toxicity, and resistance mechanisms. Ultimately, successful translation of SRSF3 biology will depend on identifying tumor types or patient subgroups that are selectively dependent on specific SRSF3-regulated isoforms and developing delivery systems capable of targeting these vulnerabilities with acceptable safety.

Conclusion

In recent years, growing evidence has highlighted the critical role of Serine/Arginine-Rich Splicing Factor 3 (SRSF3) in the regulation of cancer progression. As a key member of the SR protein family, SRSF3 acts as an important regulator of alternative splicing and post-transcriptional gene expression. Through its ability to control splice-site selection and regulate multiple downstream targets, SRSF3 influences a wide range of cellular processes that are essential for tumor development, including cell proliferation, migration, invasion, metabolic reprogramming, and immune modulation. Dysregulation of SRSF3 has been reported across multiple cancer types, where it contributes to oncogenic signaling networks and promotes tumor growth and progression.

Beyond its mechanistic role in tumor biology, SRSF3 also shows considerable promise as a cancer biomarker. Altered expression of SRSF3 has been associated with tumor aggressiveness, metastatic potential, and patient prognosis in several malignancies. Because SRSF3 regulates numerous cancer-associated splicing events, its expression may reflect broader alterations in RNA processing within tumor cells. Moreover, the involvement of SRSF3 in immune-related pathways and tumor–immune interactions suggests that it may serve as a predictive indicator of treatment response, particularly in the context of emerging immunotherapies. As high-throughput transcriptomic technologies continue to advance, integrating SRSF3 expression patterns and splicing signatures into clinical datasets may further improve cancer diagnosis, risk stratification, and therapeutic decision-making.

Importantly, the increasing recognition of SRSF3 as a central regulator of oncogenic splicing networks has stimulated interest in targeting SRSF3 for cancer therapy. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition or genetic suppression of SRSF3 can impair tumor cell proliferation, induce cellular senescence, and enhance sensitivity to chemotherapy. These findings suggest that SRSF3 represents a promising therapeutic target capable of simultaneously disrupting multiple tumor-promoting pathways. Furthermore, combining SRSF3-targeted strategies with conventional therapies, targeted agents, or immunotherapies may provide synergistic benefits and help overcome therapeutic resistance.

Despite these encouraging advances, several challenges must be addressed before SRSF3-targeted therapies can be translated into clinical practice. Because SRSF3 plays essential roles in normal RNA processing, achieving sufficient therapeutic specificity remains a major concern. Additionally, the context-dependent functions of SRSF3 across different tumor types and the technical difficulties associated with targeting RNA-binding proteins present further obstacles. Addressing these challenges will require continued efforts in understanding SRSF3-regulated splicing networks, developing more selective inhibitors, and identifying biomarkers that predict patient response to SRSF3-targeted interventions.

Looking forward, future research should focus on integrating multi-omics approaches, functional genomics, and clinical data to better define the role of SRSF3 in tumor biology and therapeutic response. Advances in RNA-targeting technologies, precision drug delivery systems, and splicing-modulating therapies may provide new opportunities to exploit SRSF3-dependent vulnerabilities in cancer cells. With deeper mechanistic insights and improved therapeutic strategies, targeting SRSF3 may ultimately contribute to the development of more effective and personalized treatments for cancer patients.
