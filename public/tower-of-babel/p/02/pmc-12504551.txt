Intelligent calibration method for microscopic parameters in the discrete element method based on ensemble learning
Authors: Yifan Jiang, Jiapeng Pu, Junfeng Sun, Zhigang Yao, Yong Fang, Qihang Ran, Yubo Wang, Zhibing Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Block Discrete Element Method is widely used in engineering research because it can accurately model fractured rock masses. However, the accuracy of simulations depends on selecting appropriate microscopic parameters, which cannot be directly obtained from macroscopic rock tests. Therefore, calibrating microscopic parameters is essential to ensure that the model’s macroscopic physical and mechanical states align with laboratory test results. Traditional trial-and-error calibration methods are highly inefficient and computationally demanding. To address this challenge, this study randomly generated microscopic parameters for discrete block elements and established computational models for uniaxial compression, Brazilian splitting, and triaxial compression tests. Maximum-edge length of the Voronoi Trigons and failure modes were analyzed to verify model reliability. Based on the results, a macroscopic-microscopic parameter dataset was constructed, and correlation analysis was performed to determine the relationship between microscopic parameters and macroscopic behavior. Subsequently, a Stacking ensemble learning model was developed, trained, and tested. The optimal Stacking ensemble model outperformed others across multiple tasks, achieving higher R2 values and lower MAE and RMSE. In practical applications, simulation results closely matched experimental values, with errors of 0.6% for uniaxial compressive strength, 6.6% for elastic modulus, 10.6% for indirect tensile strength, 8.6% for friction angle, and 5.1% for cohesion. These findings confirm the high accuracy and reliability of the proposed method for prediction of discrete element microscopic parameters, providing valuable support for engineering applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18817-x.

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of numerical simulation technology, various numerical analysis methods have been increasingly applied to rock mechanics calculations1–3. Among them, the finite element method, finite difference method, and discrete element method are widely utilized by researchers. Since natural rock masses contain numerous joints that significantly influence their mechanical properties4,5, selecting an appropriate numerical method is crucial. The finite element and finite difference methods, based on continuum theory, divide the objects into finite elements, with nodes connected through mathematical equations. These methods are more suitable for homogeneous materials. However, for discontinuous natural rock masses, the finite element method tends to produce significant errors. In contrast, the discrete element method (DEM) represents rock as a collection of discrete units bonded together6,7, effectively characterize the microstructural and mechanical characteristics of rock masses.

Currently, discrete element software primarily employs two approaches: the particle-based discrete element method and the block-based discrete element method. In the particle-based method, models are constructed using spherical particles bonded together, with model size and shape defined by boundary conditions8. Their mechanical behavior is determined by parameters such as stiffness, strength, and friction9,10. However, since the elements are spherical and the deformation behavior of balls is ignored, this method is more suitable for micro-scale simulations of granular materials like sand11,12. On the other hand, the block-based discrete element method divides materials into multiple independent blocks and simulates their overall behavior through inter-block interactions. With fully connected blocks, this approach enables the use of arbitrary polygonal shapes, offering better tessellation and a more accurate representation of rock microstructures. Compared to particle models, block-based discrete element models provide significant advantages in capturing rock behavior13.

The block-based discrete element method has been widely applied in rock damage mechanisms14, tunnel excavation15–19 and slope stability analysis20. Its computational efficiency has been significantly enhanced through parallel computing technologies21,22 enabling large-scale applications. However, in block-based discrete element simulations, accurately determining microscopic parameters is essential to ensure the simulation reliably reflects macroscopic rock behavior. Since these microscopic parameters cannot be directly obtained from macroscopic rock experiments, calibration is required to align the numerical model with laboratory test results23–25. Traditional studies often rely on trial-and-error calibration using experimental data26–28, which is computationally intensive and inefficient. To enhance calibration efficiency, researchers have explored methods such as orthogonal testing29 and response surface methodology23 to establish relationships between microscopic and macroscopic parameters. However, these approaches still face challenges related to high computational costs, inaccuracy and low efficiency.

In recent years, machine learning has been extensively applied in engineering numerical simulations30 and its powerful nonlinear fitting capability provides a new approach to rock microscopic parameter calibration31–34. Among various machine learning techniques, ensemble learning has gained increasing attention due to its ability to enhance model generalization and robustness35–37. By integrating multiple base models, ensemble learning effectively reduces prediction errors and improves accuracy38,39. Compared to traditional standalone machine learning models, the Stacking ensemble method leverages the strengths of different models, and reduce the uncertainty caused by the limitations of a single model. This multi-layer learning mechanism is particularly advantageous for capturing the complex nonlinear relationships in rock microscopic parameter calibration40,41.

To address the challenges of high computational costs and inefficiencies in block-based discrete element calibration, this paper proposes a microscopic parameter calibration framework based on Stacking ensemble learning. This method not only significantly enhances calibration accuracy but also surpasses the traditional trial-and-error approach in computational efficiency, providing new insights and methodologies for related research. We developed numerical models for uniaxial compression, Brazilian splitting, and triaxial compression tests, and randomly generated granular parameters for block-based discrete elements. To ensure the reliability of the model, the size of the mesh division and the failure form of the sample were analyzed. Each parameter set was used to compute macroscopic mechanical properties of the models, including uniaxial compressive strength, elastic modulus, indirect tensile strength, friction angle, and cohesion. A dataset of macroscopic and microscopic parameters was then constructed, followed by correlation analysis to identify the most relevant microscopic parameters. The Stacking ensemble learning model was subsequently trained and used for prediction, with model performance evaluated based on R2MAE, RMSE, and practical application results.

Model introduction

Introduction of models and working condition

Block discrete element models of uniaxial compression, triaxial compression, and Brazilian splitting tests for rocks were established, as shown in Figs. 1, 2 and 3. Voronoi Trigons were generated using Voronoi tessellation, creating randomly sized Voronoi Trigons. Each cell functions as an elastic block, with contact constitutive models assigned to the Coulomb slip (area) contact model. The Coulomb slip model provides a linear representation of contact stiffness and yield limit, and is based upon elastic stiffness, frictional, cohesive, and tensile strength properties, and dilation characteristics common to rock contacts. For the uniaxial and triaxial compression simulations, the rock specimens measured 80 cm in height × 40 cm in diameter, with Voronoi polygons having a maximum edge length of 2 cm. Loading was applied by assigning velocity to the grid points at the top of the sample while constraining displacement at the bottom. In the triaxial compression test, the model undergoes pre-compression by applying confining pressure in three directions (excluding the bottom surface) until equilibrium is reached, after which loading is initiated. For the Brazilian splitting test, the rock sample has a diameter of 30 cm, with a maximum Voronoi Trigon side length of 1.5 cm. A rigid plate is placed above and below the sample. The lower plate restricts displacement in the x and y directions, while the upper plate constrains displacement in the x direction and applies a downward velocity to control the load.

In the block discrete element calculations, key microscopic parameters of the rock include block’s elastic modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and contact’s normal stiffness, stiffness ratio, cohesion, friction angle, and tensile strength. Based on the “strong” and “weak” rock parameter sets provided by Stavrou and Murphy43, the range of parameter settings in this study was appropriately extended (Table 1). One control group was established, along with 28 groups under single-variable conditions and 300 groups with randomly assigned parameter values within the specified range. Uniaxial compressive strength, elastic modulus, indirect tensile strength, cohesion, and friction angle were obtained through simulations of uniaxial compression, triaxial compression, and Brazilian splitting tests.

Block size analysis

To investigate the impact of Voronoi Trigons’ edge-max (edge-max is the maximum-edge length of the Voronoi Trigons) on simulation results, multiple edge-max values were tested in addition to the previously mentioned working conditions. The microscopic parameters remained consistent with the control group, and the corresponding calculation results are presented in Fig. 5.

The influence of block zone size on macroscopic parameters: (a) The failure forms of different block sizes; (b) Analysis of the dispersion of calculation results.

In the uniaxial compression, Brazilian splitting, and triaxial compression tests, the block edge-max values were set to 1–8 cm, 0.5–4 cm, and 1–8 cm, respectively. The fractures of the rock samples are marked by red dashed lines (Fig. 5a). As shown, the uniaxial compression failure mode consistently exhibited multiple non-parallel crack failure under different edge-max values, indicating no clear relationship between failure mode and mesh size.

Multiple calculations were performed for the same parameters to analyze result variability (Fig. 5b). The uniaxial compressive strength ranged from 102 MPa to 124 MPa, indirect tensile strength from 2 MPa to 3.6 MPa, friction angle from 31° to 40°, and cohesion from 35 MPa to 41 MPa. Comparing the coefficients of variation (COV) across the three tests shows that the variability of macroscopic parameters decreases as edge-max is reduced. Indirect tensile strength exhibited the highest variability (COV > 8%), while other parameters remained relatively stable when edge-max was small.

Considering computational efficiency, result stability, and model size consistency, the final configuration was set with an edge-max to model length ratio of 1/20. Accordingly, the edge-max values were set to 2 cm for the uniaxial compression model and 1.5 cm for the Brazilian splitting model.

Analysis of rock failure form

Four distinct failure modes were identified for cylindrical rock samples: simple shear failure, multiple shear failure, multiple fracturing, and vertical splitting44. Simple shear failure occurs when cracks develop along a single plane or parallel planes. Multiple shear failure involves two non-parallel failure planes. Multiple fracturing is characterized by cracks forming on multiple planes, while vertical splitting results in fractures parallel to the loading direction.

As shown in Fig. 6, the model exhibits four corresponding damage modes: single-crack failure, V-shaped crack failure, multiple non-parallel crack failure, and parallel-loading crack failure. Different failure modes are primarily governed by the material’s mechanical properties and microstructural characteristics. Single-crack failure typically results from high tensile strength combined with low shear strength. V-shaped crack failure develops from the propagation of conjugate shear bands and is mainly influenced by the friction angle. Multiple non-parallel crack failure is associated with low cohesion, whereas Parallel-loading crack failure is driven by lateral tensile stress caused by a high Poisson’s ratio, along with relatively low tensile strength and high shear strength. Overall, the parameters such as elastic modulus, tensile strength, internal friction angle, and Poisson’s ratio play a decisive role in determining the failure mode. These failure modes align with those observed in actual rock samples, demonstrating that the model effectively reflects macroscopic failure behaviors.

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation coefficient analysis principle

To accurately assess the correlation between macroscopic and microscopic parameters, the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient (PPMCC) was used for correlation analysis. In statistics, PPMCC measures the linear correlation between two variables, X and Y, with values ranging from − 1 to 1, indicating the strength and direction of the relationship. A higher absolute value signifies a stronger correlation. The Pearson correlation coefficient is calculated using Eq. (6)45:

Pearson correlation analysis results

Pearson correlation analysis was performed on dataset conditions, and the results are presented in Fig. 7. The findings indicate that uniaxial compressive strength is primarily influenced by contact’s cohesion and friction angle, with correlation coefficients of 0.53 and 0.56, respectively. Indirect tensile strength is mainly affected by contact tensile strength, with r = 0.68. The elastic modulus is influenced by both the elastic modulus of the block and normal stiffness, with r = 0.75. Since normal stiffness is determined by the block’s elastic modulus, their correlation coefficient is 1.

Conversely, the Poisson’s ratio of the block and the contact stiffness ratio exhibit correlation coefficients below 0.2, indicating little to no correlation with macroscopic test parameters. As a result, these parameters will be excluded from the subsequent regression model.

Introduction to stacking ensemble learning

Stacking ensemble learning is a hierarchical hybrid structure that combines multiple heterogeneous models to enhance overall prediction accuracy46. Compared to traditional algorithms or other ensemble methods, it offers significantly improved generalization capabilities. This approach is widely used, not only in major data competitions but also across various industrial applications47. By integrating predictions from multiple base models, Stacking ensemble learning leverages the strengths of different models, leading to substantial improvements in both accuracy and generalization. Therefore, to achieve more accurate simulation results, this study employs a stacking-based ensemble learning method.

The implementation of ensemble learning using the Stacking method is illustrated in Fig. 8. The core process consists of two main steps. First, multiple base models are trained using the training data, generating individual predictions. These predictions then serve as input features for a meta-model, which learns to combine the strengths of the base models to produce the final prediction.

Model construction

The implementation of the microscopic parameter calibration model for the block discrete element method, based on stacking ensemble learning, comprises two main steps (Fig. 9). In the first step, we optimize hyperparameters and train three base models—Random Forest Regressor (RFR), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)—to obtain their respective predictions. In the second step, these predictions serve as inputs to train a genetic algorithm-optimized backpropagation (GA-BP) model. The final results are then generated by the GA-BP neural network. The entire modeling and algorithmic development process was implemented in Python.

Model evaluation

Result verification

The performance of the Stacking ensemble model, XGBoost, RFR, and LSTM in predicting the block’s elastic modulus, and contact’s cohesion, friction angle, tensile strength was evaluated using R2 (Coefficient of Determination), MAE (Mean Absolute Error), and RMSE (Root Mean Square Error) (Fig. 10). The results indicate that the Stacking ensemble model performs well across all target variables, achieving high R2 values (0.9605, 0.9195, 0.9516, 0.6517). It significantly outperforms XGBoost and RFR in predicting tensile strength of contact. LSTM demonstrates the best predictive accuracy for friction angle of contact (R2 = 0.9718) and elastic modulus of block (R2 = 0.9609), highlighting its capability to capture complex nonlinear relationships. In contrast, RFR performs the worst across all tasks, yielding the lowest R2values.

In terms of error analysis, LSTM exhibits the lowest errors in predicting contact cohesion (MAE = 2.4510, RMSE = 3.2291) and friction angle (MAE = 1.7060, RMSE = 2.2022). The Stacking ensemble model achieves the best results for elastic modulus (MAE = 2.958, RMSE = 3.9374) and tensile strength (MAE = 2.9854, RMSE = 4.2198), outperforming XGBoost and RFR. While XGBoost performs well in certain tasks, its errors remain slightly higher than those of LSTM and Stacking ensemble model. RFR consistently exhibits high prediction errors across all variables.

A comparative analysis between genetic algorithm-optimized and grid search-optimized BP neural networks demonstrates the superior performance of the genetic algorithm approach. For predicting elastic modulus, friction angle, and tensile strength, the GA-BP model yields slightly higher R2 values and lower MAE and RMSE. In contrast, grid search performs better for cohesion prediction. Both optimization methods outperform base models across most outputs, particularly in capturing the complex nonlinear relationships in friction angle prediction, where both stacked models attained R2 values above 0.95.

Overall, the Stacking ensemble model effectively balances high accuracy with low error across multiple target variables, making it a reliable and versatile predictive model. Although LSTM excels in specific tasks, its high computational complexity limits its practical application. XGBoost delivers competitive performance in some cases, whereas RFR consistently underperforms. Considering both accuracy and efficiency, the Stacking ensemble model is the optimal choice.

The triaxial compression test curve from the DEM simulation and laboratory test (Fig. 15) shows that the curves for rock specimens under confining pressures of 10, 15, and 20 MPa demonstrate a clear dependence of strength and deformation behavior on confinement, with both laboratory tests and discrete element simulations (DEM) showing increased peak deviatoric stress and enhanced ductility at higher pressures. While laboratory results exhibit higher peak strengths and more gradual post-peak softening, DEM simulations consistently underpredict strength by 10–15% and display abrupt softening, likely due to simplified contact models and inadequate microcrack representation. The rock failure mode of simulation and test results is oblique shear failure.

The results of the Brazilian splitting and triaxial compression tests under three confining pressures were analyzed to determine indirect tensile strength, friction angle, and cohesion (Figs. 16 and 17). The microscopic parameters predicted by the Stacking ensemble model closely match actual values. Specifically, the simulated indirect tensile strength is 8.74 MPa, with a 10.6% error. The simulated friction angle is 44°, compared to the actual 48.12°, resulting in an 8.6% error. The simulated cohesion is 32.74 MPa, with a 5.1% error. These results indicate that the Stacking ensemble model effectively predicts microscopic parameters within a reasonable accuracy range.

Despite the close alignment with actual values, some errors remain. The relatively high error in indirect tensile strength may stem from rock heterogeneity in the Brazilian splitting test or uncertainties in microscopic parameter predictions. The friction angle error may be due to the model’s limitations in high-angle regions, while the cohesion error is minimal, indicating stable model performance in cohesion-related predictions.

In conclusion, the integration of the ensemble learning model with discrete element simulation demonstrates strong potential for predicting rock mechanics parameters. While some errors persist, the close agreement between simulation and experimental results highlights the method’s reliability for engineering applications. Future research can enhance the model’s predictive accuracy and applicability by expanding the training dataset.

Conclusion

In block discrete element method, microscopic parameters play a crucial role in determining simulation reliability, yet they cannot be directly obtained from macroscopic experiments. Therefore, calibrating microscopic parameters is necessary to match experimental results. Traditional calibration relies on a trial-and-error approach, which is inefficient and computationally demanding. To address this issue, this study explores the correlation between macroscopic and microscopic parameters and establishes a microscopic parameters prediction model using the Stacking ensemble learning approach. The following conclusions are drawn:

In the uniaxial compression, Brazilian splitting, and triaxial compression tests, no clear relationship was observed between the failure modes, and mesh size. A comparison of the COV from the three tests shows that the variability of macroscopic parameters decreases as edge-max decreases. Among them, tensile strength exhibits the highest variability (COV > 8%), while the other parameters remain relatively stable at smaller edge-max values. Considering computational efficiency, result stability, and model size consistency, the final configuration adopts an edge-max to model length ratio of 1/20.
