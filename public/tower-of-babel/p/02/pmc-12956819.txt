Integration of learned artificial sensation with vision during freely moving navigation
Authors: Samuel J. Senneka, Maria C. Dadarlat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMultisensory integration of visual and proprioceptive information facilitates accurate and precise movements. Intracortical microstimulation (ICMS) elicits perceptions that could supplement visual information for patients controlling a prosthesis. Here, we developed a freely moving mouse behavioral task to examine how ICMS can be used to encode multivariable task-relevant information. Mice implanted with a cortical microwire array were trained to interpret patterned multichannel ICMS to navigate to targets upon the floor of a custom behavioral training chamber. Mice quickly learned to use the ICMS signal to locate invisible targets and integrated the artificial signal with natural vision, improving task performance. This protocol can be applied to efficiently develop and test algorithms to encode artificial proprioception for neural prostheses.

The native human somatosensory system has access to an array of complex, dynamic inputs whose informational content is constructed from the activation of sensors distributed throughout the body (1). The neural circuits underlying somatosensory processing remain plastic into adulthood, rapidly adapting to the statistics of sensory inputs: expanding the somatotopic representation of task-relevant information from the fingertips (2), developing novel multisensory responses to paired multimodal stimuli (3), and shifting receptive fields to incorporate extracorporeal objects such as tools (4). Similarly, cortical neurons plastically adapt their response properties when directly activated by electrical currents (intracortical microstimulation; ICMS): expanding local somatotopic representations in response to persistent stimulation (5), altering functional connectivity between neurons (6), and shifting motor neuron projection fields (7). Humans and animals can perceive neural activity elicited by artificial neuromodulation (8–14), making ICMS a promising tool for encoding artificial sensation to replace lost sensory function (15, 16) and for providing closed-loop feedback for neural prostheses (e.g., ref. 17).

Animals learn to detect and interpret distinct spatial and temporal patterns of electrical stimulation across an array of electrodes, ultimately using multichannel ICMS to guide perception and movement (11, 18–26). This is particularly important for brain–computer interfaces (BCIs), which establish an artificial sensorimotor system (27–31). Just like in natural sensorimotor function (32), BCI patients will require artificial somatosensation for fine control of a prosthesis (33, 34), which can be integrated with visual information to form a precise multisensory estimate (35, 36). Modern neural interfaces enable high-dimensional neuromodulation via multichannel ICMS—passing small electrical currents through chronically implanted electrodes to mediate an “artificial sensation” (8, 9, 11, 37–43). The prevailing philosophy is to pattern ICMS to evoke neural activity that closely resembles activity evoked by natural sensation. However, stimulation-evoked activity is state-dependent, and the required algorithms are computationally intensive, difficult to scale, and presume a known neural code (44–48). Additionally, there may be limited access to the population of neurons encoding the desired sensory variables (39, 49).

An alternative is to reconceptualize the engineering challenge for artificial proprioception by replicating the information encoded by natural proprioception rather than replicating a specific pattern of neural activity. For example, proprioceptive information in the primary somatosensory cortex arguably provides feedback on distance and direction of a movement (1, 50, 51); Fig. 1A]. These two variables can be adopted to describe a different type of movement: navigation to a target in an open field (Fig. 1B), which encodes the same set of parameters (distance and direction) with a frame of reference fixed on the target location rather than fixed on the animal’s body. Even if proprioception is instead encoded as a set of joint angles in the primary somatosensory cortex (52), the goal should be to encode a specific set of behaviorally relevant sensory variables (joint angles) rather than replicate specific neural activity patterns.

Past work showed that freely moving rodents learn to use ICMS to navigate through an open field or to a reward port (20, 21, 53–56). Nevertheless, movements guided by artificial sensation have so far failed to be as direct and precise as those guided by natural sensory signals (57). The major questions from the perspective of encoding artificial sensation then are 1) how to encode sensory information precisely, 2) how to encode multiple variables, and 3) how to encode artificial sensation so that the signal integrates with natural sensory information to improve artificial sensorimotor function? To address these questions, we developed a rapid and robust protocol by which to study encoding and integration of artificial sensation in freely moving mice. We showed that artificial sensation can be integrated with natural vision and that integration is learned rapidly and robustly across animals. These results demonstrate the surprising flexibility of neural circuits in adapting to and integrating novel (artificial) forms of sensory information, which is an important consideration in the development of brain–computer interfaces.

Results

Six C57BL/6J mice were trained to navigate to 2-cm diameter round targets upon the floor of a custom behavioral training chamber. The target could be set to one of nine possible locations, forming a three by three grid upon the cage floor (Fig. 1F). The target location was made known to the mice via two sensory modalities: 1) vision (a circle upon the floor), and/or 2) multichannel ICMS. The ICMS signal encoded a two-dimensional vector specifying 1) the angle between the mouse’s heading and the target (θ) and 2) the distance between the mouse’s head and the center of the target (δ; Fig. 1B). The ICMS signal encoding of target location imitates the cosine tuning of neurons in sensorimotor cortices to the direction of limb movements (51, 58, 59). A detailed description of ICMS encoding is provided in the next section. To reduce the saliency of the visual target, it was dim (6 lx) and red (630 nm). Although mice are dichromats (they have no red cones in the retina), they are not blind to red light (60). During each trial, the mouse’s head and body (Fig. 1F) were tracked using DeepLabCut Live on a 60 Hz video feed from a camera mounted on the top of the behavioral training chamber (61). Estimates of animal heading are thus updated at 60 Hz. A behavioral trial was considered successful if the mouse’s head entered the target during a 5 s trial period.

Encoding Algorithm for Artificial Sensation.

The artificial sensory signal was encoded via spatiotemporally patterned ICMS (Fig. 1D–F). The basic unit of electrical stimulation is a biphasic pulse of current (Fig. 1D), and the effect of stimulation on nearby neurons depends on the parameters used for stimulation, including the current amplitude, location of stimulation, and the frequency at which pulses are delivered (9, 38, 47, 62–64). To encode the location of the target relative to the mouse’s head, each electrode was assigned a tuning curve in the form of a von Mises distribution centered on a “preferred direction” (ϕ) (Fig. 1D), equally spaced around a circle from 0 to 2π. The direction of the target relative to the mouse’s heading could be decoded from the relative stimulation frequencies across electrodes (Fig. 1F). Similarly, the distance between the mouse’s head and the center of the target (δ) could be decoded by comparing stimulation frequencies across “proximal” and “distal” electrode groups (Fig. 1 E and F). Distance, δ, scales the amplitude of each electrode’s tuning curve (Fig. 1E). For example, an electrode may have a peak stimulation frequency of 100 Hz when the mouse was 10 cm away from the target and a peak stimulation frequency of 10 Hz when the mouse was 1 cm away from the target. The proximal group tuning curves were scaled by (1−δ/δmax), increasing average stimulation frequency as the mouse approached the target. The distal group tuning curves were scaled by (δ/δmax), decreasing stimulation frequency as the mouse approached the target.

Learning Artificial Sensory Information via Multimodal Stimulation.

Animals received multimodal sensory information (dim visual and ICMS encoding of target location) from the first day of training on the behavioral task. Mice quickly learned the multimodal behavioral task (Fig. 2B), locating the target on at least 75% of trials within the first seven days of training. A level of 75% is often used as threshold for proficiency on a behavioral task (65). Further training did not improve performance, as task completion on the last three days of testing was not statistically different from the first three days following proficiency (P≥0.05, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). In both early and late sessions, task completion was significantly better than the threshold set for proficiency (75%; P≤0.05, t test). Therefore, animals quickly learn and stabilize performance on this behavioral task.

Once the mice were proficient on multimodal trials, we introduced a series of probe trials intended to test learning and integration of the ICMS signal. To test performance with the ICMS signal, ICMS probe trials were presented on 15% of trials per session, during which the target location was invisible to the mouse. We also included vision-only probe trials that could have dim or bright visual target trials in order to directly compare behavioral performance guided by natural and artificial sensation (15% and 5% of trials). Probe trials also included a sham condition, where no sensory information was provided about target location and therefore showed success rates achievable by chance (5%).

To quantify behavioral performance beyond success rate, we calculated additional single-trial metrics: time-to-target, per-trial path efficiency, movement speed, and angular dispersion. The metrics were calculated for the last five behavioral sessions, at which point performance on multimodal trials had plateaued. These five stable sessions were used to strengthen the statistical comparisons of behavior guided by different sensory conditions. To ensure a fair comparison between vision and ICMS trial types, all analyses were performed on cropped trajectories—from the point the animals first faced the target and were less than 25 cm away from the target center. This was done to ensure that the trial statistics were not being calculated for periods during which ICMS or multimodal trials would provide additional information not present during vision trials, i.e., while the animal could not see the target and therefore could not make directed movements toward it.

Mice clearly learned to use the ICMS signal to locate the invisible targets within the training cage. Animals had stable average performance on ICMS trials, fluctuating around the 75% level set for proficiency (Fig. 2 B and C). In fact, behavioral performance guided by ICMS was qualitatively (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1) and quantitatively (Fig. 3B–E) similar to behavioral performance when navigating to visual targets (P≥0.05, Wilcoxon sign-rank test). Under all sensory conditions, mice completed significantly more trials than chance (sham), suggesting that mice are making directed movements toward the target on all other trial types (P≤1e−7; Wilcoxon sign-rank test, Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons; Fig. 3A). Based on these results, we conclude that animals quickly learn to decode task-relevant sensory information from the ICMS signal and that decoding is stable across time.

Integration of Natural and Artificial Sensation.

We hypothesized that, if natural visual information were being integrated with artificial sensory information (Fig. 2A), behavioral performance on multimodal trials would be significantly better than on unimodal trials. This is in fact the case (Figs. 2 and 3A–E). Mice completed significantly more trials (Fig. 2C) with more directed, efficient movements in multimodal than unimodal trials (Fig. 3). Notably, mice were able to reach the target in a shorter period of time in multimodal than unimodal trials (Fig. 3B), and moved faster in multimodal trials than in ICMS (but not visual) trials (Fig. 3C). While ICMS immediately modulates neural activity (37, 48, 66), detection of ICMS may take longer than natural sensation (67, 68), slowing the animal’s movement. Another possibility is that the 10 Hz update rate of target location encoded via ICMS led to the animals moving more slowly to attend to the signal. In addition to movement speed, animals made more directed movements when guided by multimodal sensory information than by either ICMS or vision alone, as evidenced by shorter total path lengths and lower variability in movement direction in multimodal trials (Fig. 3 D and E). In conclusion, while there is prior evidence of multisensory integration of natural and artificial sensation (17, 24, 43, 57), our current work highlights the speed and flexibility by which integration can be learned.

Decoding of Direction and Distance from ICMS.

Theoretically mice could have high success rates with ICMS just by being more active in ICMS trials than in the sham trials. However, the statistical equivalence between ICMS and visual trials in path efficiency, angular dispersion, movement speed, and time-to-target suggest that target localization guided by ICMS is a deliberate process. Even so, we have not yet explicitly examined how well ICMS encodes each of δ and θ—the distance and direction between the mouse’s current heading and the target (Fig. 1B). This question is challenging to address given the circuitous trajectory taken by the animals even to visual targets (Fig. 3A). We tested this idea in two ways. First, we used behavioral performance on visual trials to estimate the intrinsic variability of mouse trajectories (Fig. 4). Additional variability can then be assigned to an inability to extract task-relevant information about target location. Second, we explicitly perturbed ICMS encoding in a second cohort of mice. These results, with a detailed description of the experiments, are discussed below (Fig. 5).

To estimate decoding of movement angle and distance from the ICMS signal, we first identified discrete submovements in single-trial trajectories based on changes in velocity (69). The distinct submovements allow us to quantify changes in heading and distance relative to the target in response to sensory information available at the onset of the movement (Fig. 4A). At the onset of movement, the mouse is at some location (θonset, δonset) relative to the target (Fig. 4A, Top Left). If the animal can perfectly decode both direction and distance from the ICMS signal, an optimal movement would take the animal directly to the target, such that the change in angle over the submovement (the “estimated angle”, calculated as θonset−θoffset=θonset), could be compared to the true angle—the angle at movement onset. Similarly, the distance traveled in that submovement could be taken as the animal’s estimate of the true distance—the distance at movement onset. Only trials in which distinct submovements could be identified were included in this analysis. Furthermore, to remove noise from movements that were really just small twitches, only submovements that were of at least 200 ms duration were considered. Finally, this analysis was conducted on cropped trials, once the mice were facing the target, to make sure that the fist submovement was not just the mice rotating away from the reward port on the side of the behavioral training chamber.

For both visual and ICMS trials, we found that the estimated angle was linearly related to the true angle, although mice tended to underestimate the true angle (rdim visual2=0.52 and rICMS2=0.53; Fig. 4B). Even so, the strong correlation between the two suggests that mice were able to extract target direction information from ICMS similarly well to vision. In contrast, estimations of target distance were generally poor for both ICMS and dim visual trials, with little relationship to the true target distance at submovement onset (rdim visual2=0.05 and rICMS2=0.18; Fig. 4C). Perhaps this poor correlation is not surprising, because the use of distinct submovements to reach the target suggests that the animal has low certainty about the target location on those trials and is stopping to accumulate more information.

To further quantify how well δ and θ can be decoded from the ICMS signal, we performed three additional analyses. First, we hypothesized that, on average, the mouse’s movement should be turning its head toward the target. To test this idea, we separated each trial into segments of 6 cm (the median of the distribution of distances traveled during distinct submovements that lasted longer than 200 ms). We then calculated the fraction of these 6 cm segments during which the mouse was turning toward rather than away from the target direction. We found that, just as the behavioral metrics described in prior analyses, the mouse made just as many turns toward the target during ICMS trials as to visual trials (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, mice made more turns toward the target in multimodal trials than unimodal ICMS or dim visual trials (P≤0.02, Wilcoxon sign-rank test), again supporting the notion that artificial and natural sensation are integrated.

Fraction of turns toward the target primarily examines decoding of directional information. To examine decoding of distance information, we modified the analysis to ask on what fraction of movement segments did the mouse come closer to the target. If the mouse were moving randomly, as in the sham condition, movements would only bring the animal closer to the target approximately half of the time. As before, we considered movement segments of 6 cm. A movement was considered to be “toward” the target if the radial distance to the target at the endpoint of the segment was smaller than the radial distance at the start of the segment. As before, performance with ICMS was similar to that with the dim visual signal, and performance with the multimodal signal significantly exceeded unimodal trials (Fig. 4E).

Finally, we noted that mice slowed down as they approached the bright visual target, reflected by a positive correlation between target distance and movement speed (ρ=0.44; Fig. 4E). This behavior may reflect the well-known trade-off between the speed and accuracy of movements (Fitts’s law), suggesting that movements become slower and more precise as the animal knowingly approaches the target. Similarly, target distance and movement speed were positively correlated for ICMS trials (with correlations significantly greater than zero across trials, P=5e−8, t test). However, the correlation between target distance and movement speed was lower for ICMS trials than for either dim visual trials or bright visual trials (P=0.025 and P=0.001, Wilcoxon rank sum test, using Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons), suggesting that distance information was not as well encoded by the ICMS signal as by the visual signal.

Persistence of Multisensory Integration Following Extended Training.

Our analyses suggest that artificial sensation is integrated with vision, as performance with multisensory cues exceeds performance with unisensory information. However, one confound is that animals received more multisensory than unisensory trials. Superior performance on multisensory trials could be the result of insufficient exposure to unisensory stimuli. To address this question, we analyzed the behavior of a second cohort of mice (N=4) that underwent a longer period of training on ICMS (see Materials and Methods for details), and compared performance during earlier training sessions (exposure to 700 to 800 ICMS trials) to later training sessions (exposure to 1,300 to 1,5000 ICMS trials). We found that additional exposure modestly improved ICMS success rate: Mice successfully completed more ICMS trials than in the earlier testing session (P=0.044; SI Appendix, Fig. S4). However, there was no significant change at the level of sensory-guided single-trial trajectories (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). In contrast, additional training on multimodal trials and visual trials did not improve behavioral performance, suggesting that performance stabilized across stimulus conditions. At both the early and late stages of training, navigation guided by multimodal sensory information was superior to unisensory navigation (P≤0.009, Wilcoxon sign-rank test; SI Appendix, Fig. S4), supporting the hypothesis that the integration of ICMS with vision drives improved performance in multimodal trials over unimodal trials.

Perturbation of the ICMS Signal Impairs Behavior.

In the same group of well-trained mice described above, we explicitly disrupted ICMS encoding of θ and δ to test reliance on each variable during target localization. Probe trials to test reliance on distance encoding (δ; Fig. 5C) consisted of: 1) fixing the peak stimulation frequency of distal electrodes (80 Hz, 5% of trials), 2) fixing the peak stimulation frequency of proximal electrodes (80 Hz, 5% of trials), and 3) fixing the peak stimulation of all electrodes (both distal and proximal, 80 Hz, 5% of trials). A stimulation frequency of 80 Hz corresponds to a δ=10.6 cm (for proximal electrodes), which is approximately the average target distance at the start of a trial.

Probe trials to test reliance on directional information (θ; Fig. 5C) consisted of: 1) rotating the preferred direction (ϕ) of each electrode by 45°, or 2) shuffling (randomly reordering) the preferred direction of distal–proximal electrode pairs. Both perturbations maintain distance encoding, which is set by the relative stimulation frequency across distal–proximal electrode pairs. Together, these five experimental perturbations test the hypothesis that mice decode both distance and direction from the ICMS signal. If correct, mice should be able to locate the target on distance perturbation trials by relying on direction information and on direction perturbation trials by relying on distance information. In both cases, behavioral performance would be significantly better than chance (sham trials). Mice never received both distance or direction perturbation trials in the same session.

The results of the probe trials suggest that mice rely primarily on direction information encoded by ICMS to locate targets. Probe trials made up 10 to 15% of all trials presented during an experimental session, a total of 15 to 22 trials. When the encoding of distance was diminished or removed entirely, mice still performed to the same level of success as with the intact ICMS signal (Fig. 5B). Further, perturbing distance encoding did not alter time-to-target, average movement speed, path efficiency, or angular dispersion (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). In contrast, perturbing the encoding of direction impaired target localization. While rotating preferred directions by 45° did not affect success rate, mice took a longer time to reach the target (Fig. 5 D and E), and had longer path lengths with higher angular dispersion (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Shuffling preferred direction completely impaired behavior; success rates fell to those achievable by chance during sham trials (Fig. 5D). In summary, these perturbation trials suggest that the mice learn to decode the relative target direction from ICMS (θ) but have minimal ability to decode distance.

Discussion

Our major finding is that freely moving animals quickly integrate multivariate artificial sensation encoded by patterned ICMS with natural vision to improve behavioral performance on multimodal trials relative to unimodal trials. Furthermore, although we used a learning-based approach toward encoding the artificial sensory signal, the mice ultimately perform just as well on unimodal ICMS trials as on visual trials, as measured by task success rates, path efficiency, path variability, movement speed, and trial duration. A closer analysis of single-trial trajectories and experimental perturbations to ICMS encoding of distance/direction suggest that animals primarily learned to decode direction, rather than distance, from the ICMS signal. The speed and reliability of multisensory integration across animals emphasizes the flexibility of the nervous system in processing and adapting to novel forms of sensory information, including integrating information that is not available via natural sensors. Our results suggest that integration is a natural consequence of learning in the context of sensory-guided behavior (70). We propose that this protocol may be used to rapidly study the optimal methods for encoding artificial sensory information and for ensuring that artificial sensation is integrated into natural sensorimotor function.

Multisensory Integration Must Be Learned Through Experience.

Multisensory integration is characteristic of natural sensory processing and has been identified across a range of species, brain areas, sensory modalities, and behavioral tasks (35, 36, 71–78). Multisensory integration can and must be learned through experience of paired sensory information (70, 79–81). This can happen quickly—neurons can develop multisensory responses in naive adult animals in as few as 3,600 cross-modal exposure trials (six hours), even under anesthesia (79). Once established, multisensory responses are stable, even without subsequent exposure (82). Multisensory integration has also been shown in the context of artificial sensation, even when stimulation is not biomimetic (17, 57). However, when learning of integration was explicitly studied, it took an order of magnitude longer (60,000 trials) for integration of natural and artificial sensation to occur in head-fixed macaques. In contrast, the freely moving mice studied here integrated visual information with artificial sensation encoded by electrical stimulation by 3,000 trials (2 h of multimodal sensory experience). This speed may come from the simplification of the behavioral task (navigating to a visual target) and the improved ethological relevance of the behavior to the animal. The speed at which behavioral multisensory integration occurs suggests the ready integration of artificial sensation into natural sensorimotor circuits.

Neural Mechanisms of Learning and Integration of Artificial and Natural Sensation.

Two forms of integration occur during this sensory-guided navigation task: 1) integration of the ICMS signal with the mouse’s internal estimates of heading, and 2) integration of estimates of target location derived separately from visual and ICMS signals during multimodal trials (depicted in Fig. 3A). The parietal cortex, with its direct and prolific projections across the brain, is an integrative hub for both multisensory and sensorimotor function (32, 83, 84). Thus, electrical stimulation of the posterior parietal cortex (PPC), as in this experiment, might thus be expected to disrupt integration; yet we show both were intact (Fig. 3). Further, mice did not make more errors in estimating target direction encoded by the most posterior stimulation electrode (putatively in PPC) than encoded by more anterior electrodes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). One possibility as to why PPC stimulation was not detrimental is that, unlike in primates, rodent PPC seems to integrate prior knowledge and task context rather than directly processing sensory information (85, 86). Instead, circuits downstream of PPC, such as the frontal cortex, may play a direct role in integrating multisensory information (75).

An informal distinction in the field of artificial sensation is whether or not neuromodulation is “biomimetic.” This term conveys the idea that an optimal form of artificial sensation is one that takes no learning, by which electrical stimulation can directly replace natural sensation (e.g., refs. 10 and 12). Intuitively, the nervous system is optimized to interpret and generate specific activity patterns, and movement away from those patterns requires neural plasticity (87–89). Consequently, understanding how electrical stimulation can be patterned to evoke neural activity patterns similar to those induced by natural sensation is of interest (42, 44, 45, 90). These efforts have been particularly successful in the field of artificial touch, where neurons have readily identifiable receptive fields and electrical stimulation induces sensation on clearly demarcated parts of the body (11, 91, 92).

Achieving similar results for artificial proprioception is challenging, due to the complexity of the proprioceptive neural code (51, 58, 59) (but see refs. 49 and 93). Further, any finite number of electrodes is unlikely to be able to precisely access and modulate neurons with all of the desired encoding properties (49). Encoding the full range of artificial proprioceptive signals from even a single limb may thus require some level of learning from the subject. Encouragingly, the sensory perceptions elicited by peripheral nerve microstimulation shift to align to prosthesis sensor locations (94). However, persistent microstimulation may be required to engage plasticity, as transient stimulation evokes stable perceptions across time (95). Our approach investigates an extreme circumstance: What if almost everything has to be learned? Addressing this question sets a lower bound for behavior guided by electrical stimulation and demonstrates the extent to which learning and plasticity can be engaged by neural prostheses. Previous work in freely moving rodents has demonstrated speed of learning of ICMS in the context of behavior, suggesting that this model is ideal for early development of encoding strategies for artificial sensation (20, 21, 53–56).

Given that the algorithms used to encode behavioral variables were not matched to the tuning properties of local neural populations, and that the spatiotemporal neural response patterns to electrical stimulation are distinct from patterns evoked by natural sensation (19, 38, 47, 62, 64), some level of neural plasticity was necessary for both learning and integration of the artificial sensory signal (19). Improvements in detection of electrical stimulation requires that neural circuits adapt to recognizing specific patterns of activity, as there is a competition between an animal’s ability to detect electrical stimulation of neurons and its ability to detect a visual stimulus matched to those neuron’s tuning properties (19). Natural sensory signals modulate both the perceptual and functional impact of ICMS: the probability of eliciting a saccade from electrical stimulation of visual cortical areas interacts with specific behavioral conditions of the task, include the relative position of a visual target and the receptive fields of stimulated neurons (96, 97). Additionally the perceived timing of ICMS inputs is modulated by codelivery of a visual stimulus (98). These results suggest that integration of information encoded by ICMS is mediated by interactions with ongoing neural activity in local circuits (23, 48).

The spatial and temporal structure of stimulation may dictate how easily an artificial sensory signal may be learned (13, 55, 99). In this current study, both direction and distance are encoded via spatial maps along the cortical surface; yet one is learned while the other is not. In the case of distance, the spatial map is the shift in high-frequency stimulation from distal to proximal electrodes as the mice approach the target (moving stimulation from the lateral toward the medial stimulating electrode). However, electrodes along the A-P axis (encoding direction) are closer together (250 μm apart) than electrodes along the M-L axis (encoding distance, 500 μm apart). It is possible that this greater anatomical distance activates spatially distinct populations, which potentially project to spatially distinct downstream anatomical targets (100), which may complicate a direct comparison of activity across those two locations. We note that poor distance estimation may instead reflect the search strategies employed by mice during navigation, which seem equally poorly directed toward visual targets as toward ICMS targets (Fig. 4 C and E).

One confound in our study is that ICMS could trigger a global state of arousal (e.g., ref. 101), making the animals move more, increasing the likelihood of finding the target by chance than in sham trials. However, given that there is no difference in path efficiency between ICMS and visual trials (Fig. 3E), this scenario is unlikely. A contrasting concern is that ICMS may take longer to process than natural sensation, leading to slower movements than those guided by natural sensation. When a single electrode is used to encode a tactile sensation, the response time to report the stimulus is sometimes, but not always, longer for ICMS encoding than for vision or touch (67, 68). Response times speed up if stimulation is delivered with higher intensities or across more electrodes or if the stimulation amplitude is titrated to elicit a sensation of similar intensity to natural sensation (102, 103). However, in this study, the narrow tuning on the electrodes led to (mostly) a single distal–proximal pair stimulated at a time (Fig. 1D–F). Further, we found no difference in movement speed or time-to-target between visual trials and ICMS trials. Therefore, the ICMS signal seems as informative as natural vision during freely moving sensory-guided navigation. More work will be needed to address the question of whether it will be as informative in the context of BCIs, but our results are encouraging.

Materials and Methods

Mice.

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Purdue University. Ten C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Labs #000664) aged 3 to 4 mo were used in these experiments (7 males, 3 female). Six of the mice were used to examine short-term learning and integration. Four additional mice were used to examine extended training and ICMS perturbations. Each mouse was implanted with a 16-channel chronic microwire array prior to behavioral training (TDT OMN1010-16). Full details of the surgery can be found in SI Appendix.

Behavioral Training.

Mice were trained to locate a target that could appear in one of nine locations upon the floor of the behavioral training chamber (Fig. 1F), chosen randomly each trial. Visual targets were generated by turning on a small group of LEDs within a 32 × 32 LED RGB matrix (6 mm pitch) placed underneath the cage floor. Visual targets were shown in red (630 nm, 6 lx). The mouse’s position within the cage was recorded at 60 Hz with a camera mounted on the ceiling of the training chamber (Imaging Source monochrome camera DMK 37BUX287 used with an Imaging Source TPL 0420 6MP Lens). We trained a DeeplabCut pose estimation model and used the DeepLabCut-Live GUI to track the mouse’s movement on the real-time camera feed (61). A custom python script was used in conjunction with DeepLabCut-Live to randomly select target regions, provide reward, and update stimulation parameters during trials.

Testing ICMS learning and multisensory integration.

Mice were initially trained to navigate to multimodal targets. Once mice performed well (≥75% success rates) on multimodal trials, four additional trial types were introduced: ICMS-only, visual-only (dim and bright), and sham (no visual or ICMS). ICMS-only explicitly tests decoding of the artificial sensory signal, sham trials test success rates that are achievable by chance (setting a lower bound on performance), and the visual trials test baseline behavior guided by unimodal natural feedback when the signal is highly reliable (bright visual) or less reliable (dim visual). The first two mice were tested using a trial probability distribution of 80% multimodal, 7.5% ICMS only, 7.5% dim visual only, 2.5% bright visual only, and 2.5% sham. Four subsequent mice were tested using a trial probability distribution of 60% multimodal, 15% ICMS only, 15% dim visual only, 5% bright visual only, and 5% sham. The last mouse (M6) was tested with this distribution from the first day of training. See SI Appendix, Fig. S2 for success rates on each trial type as a function of training session.

Perturbations of distance and direction encoding.

A separate cohort of mice (N=4) were trained on the behavioral task described above. These mice were part of an experiment to test the impact of tuning curve width on ICMS decoding, but we found no difference in performance with ICMS due to this change. Therefore, these mice were used to study the impact of extended ICMS training and perturbations of ICMS encoding.

First, the distribution of trial types within a behavioral session was changed to: 45% intact ICMS trials, 10% probe trials (described below), 30% multimodal, 15% dim visual, 5% bright visual, and 5% sham. On five sequential behavioral sessions, we perturbed distance encoding on probe trials by fixing the height of electrode tuning curves to 80 Hz. The fixed tuning height was applied to either 1) the proximal electrode group, 2) the distal electrode group, or 3) both proximal and distal groups (Fig. 5C). A fixed rate of 80 Hz is approximately half of the maximum stimulation frequency the mice saw in practice (160 Hz) and is equivalent to a distance ratio of 0.4 (δ/δmax).

A separate set of five sessions included probe trials with altered direction encoding, by 1) offsetting the “preferred direction” of each electrode pair and 2) randomizing, or shuffling, preferred directions across the array. Proximal–distal pairs tuned to the same “preferred direction” were maintained in both of these trial types to provide an intact distance encoding signal. Trial types perturbing the distance and angle encoding accounted for approximately 10% of all trials across the 5 sessions they were included (5 to 6 trials for distance perturbations and 7 to 8 trials for angle perturbations).

Intracortical Microstimulation.

ICMS pulses were cathode-leading biphasic symmetric pulses with a phase length of 200 μs and interphase interval of 250 μs with a constant amplitude of 10 to 20 μA. The frequency and location of stimulation were used to encode the vector describing the relative position between the mouse’s forward heading and the target. For electrode i, stimulation frequency at time t was set to[1]fi(t)=200δrel(t)eκ(cos(ϕi−θ(t))−1),

where θ(t) is the target direction relative to the animal’s heading, ϕi is fixed for each electrode, δrel(t) scales with distance to the target, and 200 scales the curves to a maximum stimulation frequency of 200 Hz. The parameter κ sets the tuning curve width and was set to κ=16. δ(t) is the instantaneous distance between the mouse’s head and the target location, such that δrel(t)=δ(t)/δmax and δrel(t)=1−δ(t)/δmax for distal and proximal encoding electrodes, respectively, with δmax=26.5 cm (the diagonal distance across cage). Electrodes 1 to 8 (row 1) were set to be proximal encoding electrodes and electrodes 9 to 16 (row 2) were set to be distal encoding electrodes. Both electrodes located within each column of the 8 × 2 array had the same ϕi, a value that acted as a “preferred direction” for the electrodes as it is the direction at which the electrode had the highest stimulation frequency for fixed δ.

Analysis.

Trajectories recorded during individual trials were denoised prior to analysis due to variability in the DeepLabCut tracking algorithm by identifying time points with low probability of being correct (a measure provided by DeepLabCut) and averaging the value of the two surrounding, higher-probability points. To ensure a fair comparison between visual and ICMS trials, we cropped behavioral trajectories on each trial to the point at which the mouse is first facing the target, defined as the point at which the mouse’s heading (vector between the nose and back of the head; Fig. 1) is within ±123° of the target direction, θ. Subsequent trial metrics (described below) were calculated using these cropped trajectories. We then quantified behavioral performance under each sensory condition with five behavioral measures: success rate, path efficiency, angular dispersion, time-to-target, and average speed, as described in brief below. The equations used to calculate each of these metrics are described in more detail in SI Appendix.

Behavioral metrics.

Path efficiency is the ratio between the direct distance to the target at the beginning of the trial and the total path length traveled by the animal during the trial, where a path efficiency of 1 reflects a direct move to the target. Angular Dispersion measures the spread of turning angles around an average direction of movement and provides a second measure of path tortuosity. Time-to-target measures the total time between the start tone and the time the mouse’s head reaches the target. Finally, Average Speed is calculated across the entire trial (start tone to target acquisition).

Estimation of angle and direction from sensory information.

To estimate how well an animal could infer target angle and direction from each sensory modality, we isolated trials with a bell-curve shaped velocity profile, defined by “onset” and “offset” points equal to baseline speed plus 0.5σv, where σv is the SD of the cropped velocity trace. Only movement segments of at least 200 ms duration were considered, to reduce noise from small, unintentional head movements. On these trials, we used the first submovement following the first pause to assess decoding of θ and of δ. Fraction of turns and movements toward target. For trials without distinct submovements in terms of movement velocity, submovements were instead estimated as a function of distance traveled (6 cm duration, the median of the distance traveled during initial submovements identified from velocity). We then found the radial distance between the mouse’s head and the center of the target at the beginning and end of each submovement segment. If the radial distance decreased from movement onset to offset, that segment was labeled as a move toward the target. The total number of movements toward the target was normalized by the total number of segments on that trial to calculate a single-trial fraction of movements toward the target.
