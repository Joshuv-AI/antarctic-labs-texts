Cognition and coronary events: A narrative overview of neurocognitive impairment in ACS patients
Authors: Umaimah Batool Mirza, Faareah Mansoor, Umna Fnu, Summiya Riaz, Mehar Zaka, Ahmed Asad Raza, Abedin Samadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Neurocognitive dysfunction is a common but often overlooked complication in patients with Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS). This narrative review aims to provide a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence on the prevalence, mechanisms, clinical assessment, and management strategies of cognitive impairment in ACS patients. We highlight how neurocognitive deficits including memory loss, reduced attention, and executive dysfunction arise from cerebral hypoperfusion, systemic inflammation, microvascular injury, and post-infarct metabolic stress. These deficits arise from mechanisms including cerebral hypoperfusion, systemic inflammation, microvascular injury, and post-infarct metabolic stress. Such impairments are associated with poorer clinical outcomes, decreased treatment adherence, and increased mortality.Routine cognitive assessment remains absent from standard ACS management, despite the availability of effective tools such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), which can detect subtle cognitive deficits early in hospitalization. Integrating cognitive screening into clinical protocols enables timely interventions and better patient stratification. Management strategies should combine pharmacological treatment of cardiovascular risk factors with non-pharmacological interventions such as cognitive rehabilitation, mental health support, and lifestyle modification. Multidisciplinary collaboration between cardiology, neurology, psychology, and rehabilitation specialists is essential to address both cardiac and cognitive recovery.By integrating findings from clinical and epidemiological studies, this review underscores the need for routine cognitive screening, multidisciplinary care, and innovative interventions such as telemedicine to improve patient outcomes. Recognizing cognitive health as an integral part of ACS management offers a more holistic, patient-centered approach to recovery.

Acute coronary syndrome: mechanisms, risk factors, and diagnosis

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) refers to a group of ischemic heart diseases. It includes conditions such as unstable angina (UA), non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) [1]. Annually, over 7 million individuals are diagnosed with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) globally [2]. ACS is more prevalent in women as compared to men, with non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) and unstable angina being the most common diagnoses among them. Women experiencing ACS face a 20 % greater risk of adjusted mortality compared to men [3]. This is because women are considerably more prone to experiencing delays in receiving care and are less likely to undergo invasive treatments and medications, which results in worse clinical outcomes [4].

The fundamental issue in ACS is an imbalance between the oxygen supply to the myocardial tissue and its demand. The primary reason is coronary artery occlusion due to a disrupted atherosclerotic plaque accompanied by acute thrombosis, which significantly reduces coronary blood flow [1]. Other factors contributing to this issue include coronary artery spasm, severe narrowing of the lumen due to atherosclerosis following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and coronary dissection, along with external influences such as severe hypotension, tachycardia, anemia, and thyrotoxicosis) [1,5]. A 12‑lead electrocardiogram (ECG) is the single most important test in the initial evaluation of patients with ACS, it also distinguishes between STEMI and NSTEMI, STEMI arises from total blockage of a vessel, leading to myocardial damage and cell death, while UA/NSTEMI is caused by partial blockage [6].

The most frequently reported symptom in patients with ACS is typical chest pain, regardless of their age. Women tend to experience symptoms other than the typical chest pain more often than men, such as fatigue, difficulty breathing, and general discomfort [2].

Cognitive impairment (CI) and dementia are frequently observed in ACS patients. Vascular dysfunction is a significant contributor to CI, leading to the dysfunction of the neurovascular unit. This can lead to reduced blood flow to the brain and microvascular ischemia, enlargement of periventricular spaces, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy. After MI, lower blood pressure and diminished left ventricular ejection fraction can lead to ongoing cerebral hypoperfusion and brain infarction [7]. Arrhythmias, being one of the most common complications in post-MI patients, can lead to oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, which may induce systematic implications including cerebrovascular events and cognitive impairment [8].

Dementia and cardiovascular disease have several common risk factors, such as high blood pressure, obesity, cholesterol levels, diabetes, diet, and exercise [9]. This influence of CVDs on cognitive abilities is more evident in individuals of middle age. Because, during middle age, the brain remains relatively healthy. While elderly often face multiple health issues (such as chronic inflammation and oxidative stress) that may already be influencing the brain. This complicates the ability to identify the specific impact of CVD on cognitive function [9]. In light of these diverse presentations and outcomes, a growing body of evidence has begun to explore not only the cardiovascular but also the neurocognitive consequences of ACS.

This article is presented as a narrative review, synthesizing current evidence on neurocognitive impairment in ACS patients. Relevant literature published between 2000 and 2024 was identified through PubMed, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, and Scopus using combinations of terms such as ‘Acute Coronary Syndrome,’ ‘cognitive impairment,’ and ‘neurocognitive dysfunction.’ Eligible studies included peer-reviewed clinical studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and guidelines involving adult ACS populations.

While this approach allows for a broad and integrative overview of the topic, narrative reviews inherently lack the structured bias control and reproducibility of systematic reviews, and findings should therefore be interpreted in this context. Nonetheless, this methodology provides an accessible framework for synthesizing prevalence data, pathophysiological mechanisms, assessment strategies, and management approaches.

The purpose of this review is to provide a comprehensive synthesis of available evidence on neurocognitive dysfunction in ACS patients by examining its prevalence, pathophysiological mechanisms, clinical assessment approaches, and management strategies. In doing so, this review emphasizes the importance of integrating cognitive evaluation into routine cardiac care to achieve more comprehensive and patient-centered outcomes.

Neurocognitive dysfunction in acute coronary syndrome

Definition and classification of neurocognitive dysfunction

Neurocognitive dysfunction refers to a significant decline in one or more areas of cognitive function from a previous level of performance, typically caused by acquired conditions such as neurodegenerative diseases, stroke, brain injury, or infections. According to the DSM-5, neurocognitive disorders (NCDs) are classified as either “major” or “mild” based on the severity of impairment and its impact on independence in daily life [10].

The DSM-5 outlines six key cognitive domains involved in neurocognitive dysfunction: complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor function, and social cognition. Deficits in complex attention may present as distractibility or slow processing speed [11], while impaired executive function affects planning and decision-making [12]. Learning and memory issues result in difficulty retaining new information [13], and language dysfunction affects fluency and comprehension [11]. Perceptual-motor deficits interfere with coordination and navigation, and social cognition impairments can result in inappropriate social behavior or lack of empathy [11]. These domains provide a structured framework for diagnosing and assessing the extent of cognitive dysfunction.

Prevalence of cognitive impairment in ACS patients

Cognitive impairment is a common but often under-recognized comorbidity in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Findings from a cohort study indicates that cognitive dysfunction is present in approximately 20–30 % of older adults hospitalized with ACS [14], another prospective cohort reported that nearly half of hospitalized ACS patients screened positive for mild cognitive impairment (MCI), especially among older and unmarried individuals, which may contribute to delays in seeking care, poor treatment adherence, and increased mortality [15]. Moreover, new-onset or worsening cognitive dysfunction can occur after an ACS event, with estimates ranging from 10 % to 40 % depending on the population studied and the tools used for cognitive assessment [16].

The incidence of cognitive decline post-ACS is particularly notable within the first six months, with evidence suggesting a link to peri-infarct inflammation, hypoperfusion, and microembolism during interventions such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Some studies have demonstrated that up to one-third of ACS patients may experience declines in attention, memory, or executive function shortly after the event, even in the absence of overt stroke [16], independently associated with increased 1-year mortality, rehospitalization, and bleeding [17]. Furthermore, study within a hospital-based cardiac rehabilitation program found that 40 % of ACS survivors show memory deficits and 30 % experience executive function impairments, associated with poorer rehabilitation outcomes and reduced quality of life [18].

Role of atrial fibrillation in post-ACS related cognitive decline

Arrhythmias are common complications in post-ACS patients, a retrospective cohort study found out that cognitive decline was significantly more prevalent in atrial fibrillation (AF) patients as compared to patients without AF, especially in the visuospatial/executive domain (p = 0.0056). The mean MoCa scores were 22.52 ± 4.68 vs. 23.24 ± 4.47 respectively (p = 0.0320) [19].

Another study reveals that atrial fibrillation (AF) independently predicts long-term mortality in older patients with hip fractures and suggests that AF may relate to cognitive functioning in a more deleterious way. This is because AF tends to form microemboli, disrupts cerebral blood flow, causes microinfarcts, and leads to neuronal destruction over time. Thus, AF in these patients would not only increase mortality risk, but could worsen cognitive functioning, exacerbating health and quality of life [20].

Impact on functional outcomes and prognosis

Neurocognitive dysfunction significantly impacts patients' functional capacity, quality of life, treatment adherence, and long-term prognosis, particularly in those with chronic conditions like acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Studies have shown that patients with cognitive dysfunction are at greater risk for non-adherence to prescribed treatments, which can further worsen their health outcomes [21]. Cognitive deficits also have a profound effect on emotional well-being, leading to a lower quality of life and diminished social engagement [22]. Moreover, the long-term prognosis for patients with cognitive impairments tends to be poorer, with significantly higher rates of mortality (HR 1.30, 95 % CI 1.23–1.38) and re-hospitalization observed [23].

Given the strong link between cognitive dysfunction and adverse outcomes, early identification of cognitive impairments through routine assessments can help mitigate their impact. Targeted interventions aimed at addressing cognitive issues may improve adherence, functional outcomes, and overall quality of life for patients with ACS and other chronic conditions [24]. Recognizing the importance of cognitive health as part of comprehensive care can lead to better long-term health outcomes for these individuals.

Pathophysiology of neurocognitive dysfunction in ACS

The primary mechanism by which ACS develops is the rupture of atherosclerotic plaques, which causes partial or total occlusion of epicardial coronary arteries. The decrease in blood flow due to the blockage and/or the distal embolization of a thrombus into the coronary microcirculation reduces blood flow to every part of the body, with the brain being the most vulnerable, causing cerebral hypoperfusion. [5].

One of the significant ACS-related complications is heart failure, as it leads to myocardial damage and structural changes in the heart, causing reduced cardiac output and decreasing cerebral blood flow [25]. This decline in cardiac output initiates the dilation of brain arterioles as a compensatory reaction. However, this adaptation limits the ability for additional dilation, resulting in inadequate cerebral autoregulation and reduced cerebrovascular reactivity to CO₂ [25]. With altered cerebral autoregulation, cerebrovascular reactivity to CO2, and autonomic nervous system control, everyday tasks can put the patient at risk for a critical decrease of cerebral blood flow in crucial areas of the brain, resulting in loss of consciousness or, in intense conditions, cerebral ischemia and stroke [25].

In patients with atherosclerotic CAD, microcirculatory dysfunction is associated with cerebral small vessel disease, anomalous cerebral flow hemodynamics, and mild but substantial impairment of cognitive function. These results suggest that microvascular dysfunction in both the heart and brain is part of a linked pathological process [26].

Cerebral hypoxia and nutrient deficiency due to various heart conditions can begin abnormal reactions that can induce depolarization, protein misfolding, ion imbalance, and oxidative stress, with iron accumulation in the brain and apoptosis [27]. Oxygen deprivation, like nutrient deprivation, can cause the activation of transcription signals, such as sirtuin-1 (SIRT1), AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), and HIFs (especially HIF-2), which are required for the regulation of numerous genes and proteins needed for homeostasis and cell survival. Metabolic stress by lack of nutrients or pharmacological suppression of mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase) effectively stimulates autophagy in astrocytes and neurons [27].

Recent research has indicated that microglia, which are immune cells located in the brain, may have a significant role in modulating the nervous system and cardiovascular health by secreting a variety of mediators. Microglia are implicated in nearly all neurological disorders, including neurodegenerative illnesses, traumatic brain injuries, and mental health conditions. Consequently, myocardial infarction may trigger the activation of microglia, leading to heightened neuronal activity due to the actions of these activated microglia [28].

Moreover, in the surgical treatment options for ACS, including CABG, the data show that upon longitudinal assessment, there is a substantial correlation between a drop in cognitive ability directly after CABG surgery and an increase in the incidence and severity of cognitive dysfunction after five years. This connection demonstrates that due to perioperative injury, increased susceptibility to an injury, or reduced ability to make a recovery, individuals with perioperative decline are at significantly higher risk for long-term decline in cognitive ability (P < 0.001) [29]. Another study shows that elderly patients with ACS-related complications like heart failure are at an increased risk of functional and neurocognitive decline (HR: 1.33; 95 % CI: 1.02 to 1.73) and have a higher burden of comorbid conditions, which ultimately increases the mortality rate [30]. Cognitive assessment and screening in ACS patients

Early and routine cognitive screening in patients with ACS is essential, particularly in the elderly, where cognitive impairment is both common and prognostically significant. The patients with cognitive impairment are less likely to receive percutaneous revascularization and face higher short- and long-term mortality [31]. The findings suggest unrecognized neurocognitive impairment may contribute to undertreatment and poor adherence to therapy. Despite evidence linking cognitive decline to future dementia and impaired comprehension of medical instructions, it remains underdiagnosed and poorly characterized in ACS [32]. The absence of standardized screening tools and thresholds contributes to this gap, leaving vulnerable patients unidentified.

Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA): a preferred screening tool

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) has emerged as a preferred screening tool due to its brevity, high sensitivity (83 %), high specificity (80 %), ease of administration (∼10 min), and ability to assess multiple cognitive domains including visuospatial ability, executive function, attention, memory, and orientation [[33], [34], [35]].

In the ACS setting, an observational, cross-sectional study reported that 48.1 % of inpatients screened positive for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) using MoCA, with common deficits in recall, attention, and visuospatial function [15]. Comparative studies have demonstrated MoCA's higher sensitivity over the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) in detecting MCI in coronary artery disease (CAD) patients, recommending a lower cut-off score of 25 for this group [36].

MoCA's relevance extends beyond detection, Utami et al. found significantly lower MoCA-Ina scores in ST-elevation ACS patients compared to non-ST-elevation cases, with scores negatively correlated with myocardial injury biomarkers, highlighting its sensitivity to disease severity and prognostic value [37]. While tools like the MMSE remain in use, MoCA's broader domain coverage and greater sensitivity to subtle cognitive changes make it particularly well-suited for identifying high-risk ACS patients.

Advanced neuroimaging for detecting cognitive impairment

A growing body of evidence highlights the value of neuroimaging and circulating biomarkers in detecting and monitoring cognitive dysfunction in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) or coronary artery disease (CAD). Multimodal neuroimaging, particularly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), has shown potential in capturing functional alterations in brain networks, even in the absence of gross structural abnormalities. Different methods; voxel-based morphometry, tract-based spatial statistics, and resting-state functional connectivity have been utilized to reveal that executive dysfunction in ACS patients, and were associated with altered brain network activity, despite no evident structural damage on imaging, underscoring the sensitivity of functional MRI (fMRI) in capturing transient cognitive changes during post-ACS recovery [38]. Similarly, resting-state fMRI employment has demonstrated significant reductions in spontaneous brain activity (measured via amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations) in CAD patients with cognitive impairment, particularly in the precuneus, angular gyrus, and cingulate regions [39]. Diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI), an advanced MRI technique, has also been used to detect widespread microstructural white matter alterations in CAD patients, with clear correlations between reduced diffusion parameters and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores [40]. These findings are echoed in the Cerebral-Coronary Connection (C3) study, which comprehensively linked coronary microvascular dysfunction with brain atrophy, white matter damage on diffusion tensor imaging, and cognitive decline, emphasizing that cerebrovascular and coronary microcirculatory dysfunction may represent manifestations of a shared pathological process [26].

Structural imaging markers also play a significant role. In a large meta-analysis, it has been reported that patients with higher CAC scores had significantly elevated risks for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia, supporting the notion that CAC may reflect cerebrovascular burden [40]. Elevated CAC levels are associated with more severe brain MRI abnormalities, such as subcortical infarcts and white matter hyperintensities, which are themselves linked to cognitive impairment [41]. Interestingly, cognitive decline in CAD patients persists even after adjusting for structural MRI findings, indicating that traditional imaging markers may underestimate the true cognitive burden of vascular disease, and more sensitive imaging or functional measures may be required [42].

FDG-PET provides a functional outlook on numerous cerebral changes and helps in assessing brain activity and cerebral metabolic reactions, and is widely used for imaging cerebral blood flow. It has been extensively applied in the evaluation of cerebrovascular diseases. In neurocognitive impairment due to cerebral hypoxia, microglia are activated, indicating inflammation, which can be detected by PET [43]. Furthermore, PET imaging shows distinct uptake patterns in vascular cognitive impairment, which is crucial in the clinical assessment of cerebrovascular diseases [44].

Circulating biomarkers and their predictive value

From a biomarker perspective, several circulating molecules have been implicated in cognitive outcomes. Low levels of 25(OH) vitamin D and elevated concentrations of NSE, S100β, and GFAP have been identified as predictors of postoperative cognitive dysfunction in ACS patients following PCI. These findings suggest that both neuronal injury and glial activation may underlie cognitive vulnerability in this population. The potential of CAC to serve as both a cardiovascular and neurodegenerative marker is also strengthened by its correlation with cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) in MRI and cognitive decline [45]. According to an observational cross-sectional study, patients with ST-elevation ACS exhibited significantly lower cognitive scores compared to those with non-ST-elevation ACS and that elevated levels of cardiac biomarkers specifically CKMB and troponin were negatively correlated with cognitive performance, suggesting that the extent of myocardial injury in ACS may contribute to acute neurocognitive dysfunction [37].

Collectively, these findings argue strongly for integrating cognitive assessment as a standard component of ACS admission protocols to better stratify risk, tailor interventions, and ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Management strategies for neurocognitive dysfunction in ACS

Cardiovascular risk factor optimization and cognitive protection

The process of early risk stratification can help physicians identify the best pharmacologic treatments and decide whether to pursue an initial conservative approach or an early invasive management plan for acute coronary syndrome [46]. Optimizing cardiovascular risk factors is crucial in safeguarding cognitive health, as these conditions significantly contribute to vascular damage, neuroinflammation, and reduced cerebral perfusion, factors that accelerate cognitive decline. The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) has published clinical practice guidelines that emphasize important tasks like quitting smoking, maintaining a Mediterranean diet, engaging in regular physical activity that increases exercise capacity, closely monitoring risk factors for atherosclerosis (diabetes, hypertension, platelet hyperactivity, and hyperlipidemia), following ACS [47].

Patients are prescribed lifelong medications to prevent further attacks of ACS and decline in neurocognitive impairment. The American Heart Association (AHA) and American College of Cardiology (ACC) guidelines center on adjuvant pharmacologic treatments of ACS that aim to minimize ischemia (supplemental oxygen and nitrates), stabilize plaque (statins), control remodeling (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), and reduce myocardial oxygen demand and malignant arrhythmias (beta-blockers) [48]. Anticoagulants lower the chance of cerebral infarction, which may improve cognitive impairment, whereas it is shown that β-blockers impair memory function. Since it has been demonstrated that many patients endure untreated anxiety and depression following an episode of Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS), the patient should have received cognitive behavior therapy or psychotherapy as part of the early stages of treatment. During this time, posttraumatic stress disorder is also prevalent. Patients who suffer from anxiety or depression are less likely to follow their prescribed treatment plans and make the necessary lifestyle adjustments [32].

Non-pharmacological strategies for cognitive support

In addition to aggressive cardiovascular management, addressing modifiable lifestyle factors is crucial for better cognitive health outcomes. When assessing patients who exhibit symptoms of cognitive impairment, clinicians should follow a methodical process [49]. To ascertain whether functional impairments are present and whether cognitive domains are affected, this method entails speaking with an informed informant and conducting a comprehensive mental status assessment [49]. Non-pharmacological strategies focus on promoting physical, social, and cognitive activities to slow cognitive decline and enhance daily functioning [50]. Socio-psychological support plays a vital role in maintaining patient autonomy by encouraging social engagement, routine tasks, and mental stimulation while avoiding prolonged hospitalization [50].

Pharmacological interventions with cognitive implications

Pharmacological treatment includes the use of cholinomimetics (donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine) and the glutamate receptor blocker memantine to manage cognitive and behavioural symptoms. Additionally, choline alfoscerate (e.g., Cerpechol) can be used as adjunct therapy to improve cognitive function and reduce mood-related symptoms, particularly in patients with Alzheimer's disease (AD) and cerebrovascular disease (CVD) [50].

Managing ACS and its complications, such as heart failure and AF, can prevent tissue hypoxia, systemic oxidative stress, inflammation, and slow down cognitive impairment.

For instance, sacubitril/valsartan has been shown to decrease the heart rate and is effective in preventing AF recurrence. Sodium-Glucose Co-Transporter 2 Inhibitors (SGLT2i) have been effective in reducing HF hospitalization and improving cardiovascular health.SGLT2 inhibitors work through multiple mechanisms in AF patients, most notably, reducing cardiovascular risk associated with oxidative stress. Furthermore, the pleiotropic characteristics of SGLT2 inhibitors may have an advantageous impact on the incidence of AF. Newer drugs such as Vericiguat, which is a soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) stimulant, enhance myocardial function and overall heart performance [8].

Telemedicine and mHealth role in post-ACS followup and cognitive impairment

The importance of telemedicine and digital health interventions with respect to the management of acute coronary syndrome and cardiovascular risk reduction continues to increase. Meta-analyses show that the use of mobile applications, messages, voice and video calls, smart devices, and other digital tools dramatically lower an individual's risk of all-cause mortality, diminish systolic blood pressure, improve compliance with essential medications, and lower the rate of readmissions for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) patients [51]. The use of telemedicine such as pre-hospital tele-ECG, teleconsultation, and tele-rehabilitation assist in the early diagnosis of the patient, lessen the time from symptom to treatment for STEMI, and provide patient education, rehabilitation, and monitoring over an extended period [52].

Likewise, in a sub study of the LIGHT randomized clinical trial, the use of smart devices and mobile applications in addition to standard care among patients with high cardiovascular risk has been shown to significantly improve peak VO2 at one year (adjusted treatment effect: +1.1 mL·kg−1·min−1; 95 % CI: 0.8–1.4; pp. < 0.001). Improvement in smart devices and mobile applications in standard care have been shown to markedly improve cardiopulmonary fitness, a significant predictor of morbid cardiovascular events [53].

The potential benefits of integrating digital health and telemedicine into standard ACS care pathway along with pharmacologic therapy (e.g., dual antiplatelet therapy, beta-blockers, and statins), reperfusion strategies (PCI and thrombolysis), and cardiac rehabilitation access to more timely care can improve patients prognosis in ACS.

Interpersonal psychotherapy for mood-linked cognitive impairment

IPT can indirectly help people with cognitive impairments, particularly when such impairments are connected to mood disorders (such as sadness or anxiety), even if its primary focus is on mood and interpersonal functioning. A modified form of this known as “IPT-CI” (for cognitive impairment) is a patient/caregiver strategy that offers psychoeducation to both parties, individual problem-solving opportunities, and a forum for role-dispute resolution through collaborative meetings [54].

An interdisciplinary approach is essential in managing cognitive impairment following acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Collaboration among cardiologists, neurologists, psychologists, and other healthcare professionals ensures a comprehensive understanding of both the cardiovascular and neurological factors contributing to cognitive decline. This team-based strategy enhances patient outcomes, promoting both heart and brain health in ACS survivors.

Future research: advancing research in neurocognitive dysfunction post-ACS

To properly comprehend and treat cognitive impairment following acute coronary syndrome (ACS), more research is necessary. In order to determine the key times for intervention and the variables affecting long-term results, long-term studies are required to investigate the trajectory of cognitive decline in ACS patients. Furthermore, early detection and precise evaluation of cognitive deficiencies depend on the development of more sensitive and specific diagnostic tools. Clinical trials assessing the effectiveness of lifestyle, psychological, and pharmaceutical therapies can offer evidence-based methods to enhance cognitive health in this demographic. Furthermore, investigating a personalized medicine approach and developing interventions according to each patient's risk factors, genetic predispositions, and cognitive profiles may result in more focused and efficient treatment regimens, which would ultimately improve patient care and quality of life.

Conclusion

Neurocognitive dysfunction has an important role to play in ACS, yet, it is majorly overlooked in diagnosis with ramifications for patient recovery and long term outcome. Causative factors for the relevant pathophysiologic framework include cerebral hypoperfusion and microvascular injury resulting from systemic inflammation-the underlying intertwining of the cardiovascular and cognitive systems. A considerable number of ACS patients experience cognitive decline, and the time for intervention is now.

Thus, clinical care should expand its horizons from solely focusing on the heart to encompass cognitive function as well. Introductory-level cognitive screening approaches especially through instruments such as MoCA should be imbued into ACS management pathways for early detection. More importantly, screening must be followed by interventions from multiple disciplines including cardiology, neurology, psychiatry, and rehabilitation medicine to support recovery on both physical and cognitive levels.

Management should go beyond pharmacology to include lifestyle modifications, psychosocial support, and cognitive rehabilitation as potential interventions within post ACS care. Educating patients and caregivers, providing psychosocial support, and tailoring interventions to patients' individual risk profiles will be pivotal to enhancing adherence, maximizing independence, and promoting quality of life.

Now, the healthcare systems need to fill the gap between cardiac and cognitive care. Reduced neurocognitive burden during acute coronary syndrome can be achieved through increased research, standardized screening protocols, and interprofessional collaboration. The brain is recognized as a key part of cardiac recovery. More complete care can be provided that does not only save lives but really helps them live.
