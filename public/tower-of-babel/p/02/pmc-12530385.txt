Laparoscopic-assisted extraperitoneal ligation versus intraperitoneal suturing for pediatric inguinal hernia repair: a multicenter, observational study of recurrence
Authors: Chaosheng He, Yanbin Fang, Yusheng Chen, Guizhen Huang, Yi Su, Mao Ye, Suolin Li, Xuelai Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to retrospectively compare postoperative recurrence rates between laparoscopic-assisted extraperitoneal closure and intracorporeal suturing for pediatric inguinal hernia (PIH) repair across multiple centers, and to investigate the clinical causes of recurrence to inform surgical practice.MethodsThis is a retrospective review of 5244 PIH repairs performed using single-port laparoscopic percutaneous extraperitoneal closure (SPLPEC) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University and the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University between January 2017 and December 2020. In addition, 6054 PIH repairs were performed using two-port laparoscopic intracorporeal suturing (TPLIS) at the Capital Center for Children’s Health, Capital Medical University between January 2015 and December 2020. All patients were followed for 2 years with intraoperative findings at reoperation documented and causes of recurrence analyzed.ResultsThere was recurrence in 30 SPLPEC cases (27 males, 3 females; mean interval to recurrence, 7.6 months). There was a recurrence in 35 TPLIS cases (31 males, 4 females; mean interval, 12.5 months). There was no statistically significant difference in recurrence rates (p=0.966). Laparoscopic re-exploration identified omitted or torn peritoneum (n=42), lipidosis in the abdominal wall (n=7), and loose knots (n=16) as the primary causes of recurrence. No further recurrences were seen in the SPLPEC group after reoperation. In the TPLIS group, 33 patients had favorable outcomes, 2 patients experienced a second recurrence.ConclusionsThe incidence of PIH in females may be higher than previously recognized. Both SPLPEC and TPLIS demonstrated low recurrence rates, particularly among females and the second operation proved to be an effective treatment option for recurrent cases. For SPLPEC in obese children, secure ligation of the hernia internal ring is essential to prevent recurrence. TPLIS requires extended follow-up to detect possible late recurrence.Keywords: Surgery, Plastic; Technology

Introduction

Pediatric inguinal hernia (PIH) is a common surgical condition. Its anatomical basis is a congenital failure of the processus vaginalis to close.1 Therefore, high ligation of the hernial sac alone is sufficient to achieve a cure without the need for abdominal wall repair. This is in contrast to treatment in adults or the elderly patients.2,4 Laparoscopic surgery has become the standard approach for treating PIH due to its relative technical simplicity, avoidance of inguinal canal dissection, clear operative field, capacity to inspect the contralateral hernia internal ring (HIR) and favorable cosmetic outcomes.5 The two main laparoscopic techniques are single-port laparoscopic percutaneous extraperitoneal closure (SPLPEC) and two-port laparoscopic intracorporeal suturing (TPLIS).6 Based on current evidence, Morini et al. concluded that neither the laparoscopic extraperitoneal nor the transperitoneal approach demonstrated clear superiority in terms of intraoperative or postoperative complications and recurrence rates.6 However, Maat et al. reported a lower recurrence rate using the single-port extra-corporeal closing technique when compared with the intracorporeal purse-string suture technique (0.6% vs. 5.5%).7 Most current comparative studies of these two techniques focus mainly on comparison with open surgery, as well as on single-center or small-sample evaluations of postoperative complications.57,14 However, few multicenter studies have compared long-term postoperative outcomes in large patient cohorts.

In the present study, we conducted a retrospective analysis to compare postoperative recurrence rates following the two most representative laparoscopic techniques for PIH across multiple centers and to explore their causes to help guide practice.

Methods

Clinical data

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committees of the participating hospitals. All surgeries were performed either by attending physicians or residents under the supervision of senior surgeons. Between January 2017 and December 2020, a total of 5244 PIH repairs were performed using the SPLPEC technique at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University and Second Hospital Hebei Medical University. Among these cases, 30 experienced postoperative recurrence and were assigned to the SPLPEC group. From January 2015 to December 2020, 6054 PIH surgeries were performed using the TPLIS technique at Capital Center for Children’s Health, Capital Medical University. Of these, 35 cases developed postoperative recurrence and were assigned to the TPLIS group (figure 1). Clinical data from all participating hospitals were collected for retrospective analysis.

Inclusion criteria were pediatric patients with a confirmed diagnosis of inguinal hernia, including unilateral, bilateral or incarcerated cases.5 15Exclusion criteria were hydrocele of the canal of Nuck, indirect inguinal hernia associated with undescended testis, direct inguinal hernia or femoral hernia.

The postoperative follow-up period was 2 years.16 In the first postoperative week, patients were advised to avoid strenuous activities. Follow-up assessments were conducted at 1 month, 3 months and 2 years, either via telephone or during outpatient visits. Children with suspected recurrence underwent further diagnostic evaluation and extended follow-up.

Recurrence was defined as the reappearance of a mass in the inguinal region or scrotum (including communicating hydrocele), demonstrating bowel, omental, ovary or fluid within the mass confirmed by ultrasonography.

Surgical methods

SPLPEC procedure

The SPLPEC procedure was performed as previously described17 (figure 2A–D). In brief, a 3.5 mm trocar was inserted through the umbilicus and a 3 mm 30° laparoscope was introduced. A double-hook hernia needle (manufactured by Surgaid Medical Co Ltd, Xiamen, China) was used. One end of a 2–0 silk suture was placed in the hook groove, while the other end was attached to a syringe. The needle carrying the 2–0 silk suture was maneuvered within the extraperitoneal space between the vas deferens and the peritoneum along the inner aspect of the HIR (figure 2A). Saline was injected to elevate the peritoneum away from the vas deferens, allowing the needle to cross over the vas deferens and enter the peritoneal cavity, thereby prepositioning the suture intraperitoneally (figure 2B). The needle was then withdrawn into the extraperitoneal space of the anterior abdominal wall and advanced along the lateral side of the hernia ring to the space between the spermatic cord vessels and the peritoneum (figure 2C). Additional saline was injected to separate the posterior peritoneum from the spermatic cord vessels, after which the needle was passed over the vessels into the abdomen to retrieve the prepositioned suture. The suture was then tied extracorporeally to close the HIR (figure 2D). If a contralateral patent processus vaginalis was identified, it was ligated in the same manner. Hernias with an internal ring exceeding 15 mm in diameter were repaired by incorporating ipsilateral medial umbilical fold as reinforcement, with a 2–0 silk suture passed through the original puncture site to secure the fold to the lateral margin of the internal inguinal ring, thereby strengthening the closure.17

The procedure of single-port laparoscopic percutaneous extraperitoneal closure. (A) The needle crossed the vas deferens. (B) The suture was released from the needle and left intraabdominal cavity. (C) The needle was withdrawn and advanced along the lateral side of the hernia ring to retrieve the suture. (D) The suture was tied extracorporeally to close the internal ring.

TPLIS Procedure

The TPLIS procedure was as previously reported18 (figure 3A–D): a 5 mm trocar was inserted through the umbilicus for the introduction of a 5 mm 30° laparoscope. A small incision was made on the abdominal wall 3–4 cm to the right of the umbilicus, through which a second 5 mm trocar was inserted for the introduction of grasping forceps. A 2–0 silk suture with an attached needle was introduced percutaneously through the lower abdominal wall, leaving one end of the suture external to the body. Under laparoscopic visualization, the needle was manipulated to place a circumferential purse-string suture around the HIR (figure 3A,B), carefully avoiding injury to the vas deferens and spermatic cord (figure 3C). Intracorporeal knot-tying was then performed, leaving the suture knot inside the abdominal cavity (figure 3D). If a patent processus vaginalis was identified on the contralateral side, it was similarly closed. In cases where the HIR exceeded 1.5 cm, an additional reinforcing purse-string suture was placed over the previously closed peritoneum at the HIR to further strengthen the repair.

The procedure of two-port laparoscopic intracorporeal suture. (A, B) The needle was manipulated to place a circumferential purse-string suture around the internal ring. (C) The needle crossed vas deferens and spermatic cord. (D) Intracorporeal knot-tying was performed.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (V.4.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables with normal distribution (recurrence and age) were expressed as mean±standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Categorical variables (age and gender) are presented as number (percentage) and compared using the χ² test. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Across all three participating hospitals, the incidence of PIH was significantly higher in males than females. The male-to-female ratio was highest at the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University (7.3:1) and lowest at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University (2.2:1).

No significant differences were found in the age at recurrence between the SPLPEC (37±24 months) and TPLIS groups (35±26 months; p=0.350, K-S test). Recurrence rates did not differ significantly between the SPLPEC and TPLIS groups (p=0.966). No age-related differences in recurrence were observed within either group. However, recurrence rates differed significantly by sex, with females exhibiting a lower recurrence rate than males (p=0.032; table 1).

Discussion

We observed that the male-to-female incidence ratios of PIH varied among the participating hospitals. In previous reports, Kilda et al. reported a male-to-female ratio of 1.35:1,19 Petridou et al. reported a ratio of 2.55:120 and Dreuning and colleagues suggested a ratio of 4:1.21 We consider the male-to-female incidence ratio reported by the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University to be relatively accurate and reliable. In Xiamen, specialized maternal and child health physicians conduct comprehensive physical examinations for children entering kindergarten, as well as annual check-ups for school-aged children. Suspected female cases are promptly referred to the pediatric surgical outpatient clinic for further evaluation. This systematic screening ensures that girls who are previously thought to have a lower incidence are accurately diagnosed. Therefore, the 2.2:1 ratio reported by the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University may more accurately reflect the true epidemiological distribution. Given that the clinical manifestations of PIH in girls are often subtle and may go unnoticed by parents, early detection through school-based health screening by experienced physicians is essential. Timely diagnosis and intervention can prevent complications including ovarian strangulation.21

Although laparoscopic equipment and techniques have advanced considerably, postoperative recurrence rates of PIH after laparoscopic repair remain between 0.1% and 2.5%.6 8 10 12 17 21 22 In this study, which compared SPLPEC and TPLIS as representative laparoscopic approaches, postoperative recurrence rates were comparable between the SPLPEC (0.57%) and TPLIS groups (0.58%); this contrasts with findings from some previous reports.7 When performed by experienced surgeons, both techniques achieved low postoperative recurrence rates, supporting their reliability as laparoscopic options for PIH repair.823,25 Nevertheless, compared with TPLIS, SPLPEC may confer certain cosmetic advantages. The secondary incision in SPLPEC is smaller, and the postoperative appearance of the abdominal wall is often superior, likely because the diameter of the hook is only 1.6 mm, whereas the trocar diameter in TPLIS is 5 mm.

In our study, recurrence rates were notably higher among children aged 4–6 years. This may be attributable to reduced adherence to postoperative activity restrictions in this age group, as children at this stage often lack self-discipline and may engage in vigorous activities shortly after discharge.26 Such activities can increase intra-abdominal pressure during the early postoperative period, potentially leading to recurrence. In the TPLIS group, recurrence did not vary significantly with age, whereas in the SPLPEC group, recurrence risk decreased as age increased. These findings suggest that TPLIS may offer a lower recurrence risk for younger patients, especially infants with PIH, while SPLPEC may be a more suitable option for older children, independent of factors such as operative time and cosmetic outcomes.

In this study, recurrence rates among female patients in both groups were low, and this difference was statistically significant compared with males. A possible explanation is that during surgery in male patient, meticulous dissection is required to navigate around the vas deferens and spermatic vessels at the HIR to avoid inadvertent ligation or injury. This complexity may result in incomplete ligation or suturing of certain peritoneal areas at the HIR, predisposing to PIH recurrence. Surgeons should carefully inspect the integrity of the peritoneum at the internal inguinal ring after ligation or suturing to ensure complete closure and minimize the risk of recurrence.27 28 The mean recurrence intervals were 7.6 months for the SPLPEC group and 12.5 months for the TPLIS group, both longer than the 3.3 months and 6.5 months reported by Kilda et al.19 We speculate that this difference may be due to delayed parental detection of recurrence, as the recurrent mass in the inguinal region or scrotum is often smaller than the original hernia.

Our study indicated that the high-risk period for postoperative recurrence in both groups was within the first 12 months after surgery, followed by a marked decline. However, six cases (6/65) of recurrence were documented in the second postoperative year, accounting for 9.2%, while four cases were reported in the fifth postoperative year, representing 6.2% of the total. Recurrence rates beyond 5 years were unclear due to the lack of long-term follow-up data. In the TPLIS group, 14.3% (5/35) of patients experienced recurrences more than 2 years postoperatively, suggesting that extended follow-up is necessary after TPLIS for adequate monitoring

Laparoscopic observations identified marked laxity of the HIR as the most common clinical cause of recurrence in children, occurring in 42 of 65 cases. These cases frequently presented with numerous pleats in the posterior peritoneum, making it technically challenging to dissect and securely ligate the peritoneal folds from the spermatic structures during surgery. Consequently, portions of peritoneum at the HIR may have remained inadequately ligated, effectively converting a large defect into a small defect.13 This condition may have been further exacerbated by early postoperative activity and increased intra-abdominal pressure, ultimately leading to recurrence. Surgeons should thoroughly confirm the integrity of peritoneal ligation or suturing intraoperatively.29 When necessary, auxiliary surgical instruments may be employed to improve visualization of the HIR folds and to prevent needle misplacement and inadvertent injury to adjacent tissues.30 31 A smaller subset of cases (16/65) exhibited a large internal ring with a visible twisted ligature in the extraperitoneal space, suggesting recurrence due to loosening of the ligation. This may have resulted from tying a simple square knot or leaving excessively short suture ends, thereby increasing the risk of slippage.32 Pediatric surgeons must be proficient in applying appropriate tension during knot-tying. In the SPLPEC group, six cases (6/65) involved a small HIR with a complete ligation loop, all occurring in older and obese children with thick subcutaneous fat. In these patients, extracorporeal knot-typing was complicated by the thickness of the abdominal wall, causing the knot to adopt a teardrop-shaped configuration and slide into a loop too large to occlude the HIR. For obese children, the use of a push rod may assist in advancing the knot into the extraperitoneal space to achieve secure ligation.29

In the SPLPEC group, all 30 children who experienced recurrence underwent a second SPLPEC surgery with laparoscopic assistance; none experienced further recurrence after the second surgery. This finding supports SPLPEC as an effective treatment option for patients with recurrent PIH.8 20 32 Similarly, in the TPLIS group, all 35 children with recurrence underwent a second TPLIS procedure with laparoscopic assistance.33 Among these, 33 had a favorable prognosis with no further recurrence; however, two cases recurred following the second procedure. One child subsequently underwent a traditional open inguinal repair at Beijing Children’s Hospital and was successfully treated, while the other is awaiting a third surgery. These findings suggest that, for children with recurrence, surgeons should review surgical recordings, engage in multidisciplinary discussions and make informed decisions to minimize the risk of secondary recurrence. When necessary, traditional open inguinal repair should be considered as an alternative.

This study has several potential limitations. First, there was a significant difference in the incidence of PIH between genders across participating hospitals, which may have resulted in uneven group distribution. Second, intergroup comparisons may have been biased because they were conducted across different hospitals, introducing intercenter variability. Finally, as with all observational studies, the observed associations cannot establish causality and may be influenced by unmeasured population factors. Nevertheless, this study used a large and unique cohort in which recurrence was clearly diagnosed, effective surgical interventions were performed and detailed laparoscopic findings were documented.

In conclusion, both SPLPEC and TPLIS techniques were associated with low recurrence rates for PIH, with even lower rates observed in females. Children who experienced recurrence and subsequently underwent repeat laparoscopic repair generally achieved favorable outcomes. However, when performing SPLPEC in obese children, meticulous attention should be paid to ensuring secure ligation of the HIR to prevent recurrence. In addition, patients undergoing TPLIS should receive extended follow-up to monitor for potential recurrence.
