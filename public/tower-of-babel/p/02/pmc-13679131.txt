Quality of work life and professional ethics among occupational health professionals: a social exchange theory perspective
Authors: Omid Akbarzadeh, Roghayye Rasouli, Parisa Moshashaei, Rasoul Ahmadpour, Neda Gilani, Mehran Seif Farshad, Seyyed Shamseddin Alizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the increasing complexity of ethical challenges in occupational health settings, limited attention has been paid to the ethical conduct of occupational health professionals themselves. This study examined how Quality of Work Life is associated with professional ethics among occupational health professionals, guided by Social Exchange Theory.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 200 occupational health practitioners in East Azerbaijan. Data were analysed using SPSS and SmartPLS, involving Pearson correlation, t-tests, ANOVA, and structural equation modelling. Measurement models were validated for reliability, convergent and discriminant validity, and common method bias.ResultsThe findings showed a statistically significant but modest positive association between the total Quality of Work Life score and the total Professional Ethics score (r = 0.281, p < 0.01). The strongest domain-level correlation was observed between Human Capabilities Development and Professional Autonomy (r = 0.330, p < 0.01). Human Capabilities Development, Safe Work Environment, and Social Integration were positively associated with Professional Responsibility, Professional Autonomy, and Trustworthiness. However, the model explained only 21.4% of the variance in Professional Responsibility, 18.6% in Professional Autonomy, and 8.2% in Trustworthiness, indicating modest explanatory capacity and particularly limited explanatory power for Trustworthiness.ConclusionThe findings suggest that Quality of Work Life may be a relevant organizational factor associated with professional ethics in occupational health practice. The study highlights the potential importance of developmental and relational aspects of work in supporting moral responsibility, while acknowledging that causal conclusions cannot be drawn from the cross-sectional design. These findings are especially relevant for policymaking in developing countries where ethical conduct is central to workplace safety and public health credibility.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05226-6.

Introduction

Background

Professional ethics has become an increasingly important concern in modern organizations, particularly in sectors where professional decisions directly affect human health, safety, and organizational legitimacy. Ethical conduct is no longer limited to individual morality or normative professional codes; rather, it is shaped by the interaction between personal values, occupational responsibilities, organizational expectations, and institutional constraints [1, 2]. This issue is especially important in workplace health and safety (WHS), where ethical failures may expose workers to preventable harm, weaken trust in safety systems, and compromise the credibility of occupational health practice [3, 4]. Occupational health professionals often operate in an “interpretative space” in which they must balance their responsibility to protect workers with organizational pressures, resource limitations, and regulatory expectations [5]. Therefore, understanding the organizational conditions that support or weaken ethical behaviour among these professionals is an important research and practical priority.

Quality of Work Life (QWL) has been widely recognized as a multidimensional organizational construct that reflects the extent to which the work environment supports employees’ physical safety, psychological wellbeing, autonomy, development, fairness, and social integration [6–8]. In occupational settings, QWL is particularly relevant because employees’ professional conduct is shaped not only by formal rules but also by the quality of the environment in which they perform their duties. Supportive working conditions, fair treatment, safe work environment, opportunities for development, and constructive interpersonal relations may be associated with employees’ motivation, psychological resilience, and sense of professional responsibility [9]. Evidence from WHS-related contexts also suggests that organizational support, interpersonal trust, and psychosocial conditions influence safety-related behaviours, including compliance with protective practices and adherence to professional standards [10].

The relationship between QWL and professional ethics is particularly important among occupational health professionals because their work involves both technical and moral responsibilities. These professionals are expected to assess risks, protect worker wellbeing, communicate hazards honestly, and support compliance with occupational health and safety regulations. However, their ethical behaviour may be affected by the organizational conditions in which they work. Poor QWL, including limited development opportunities, unsafe or stressful working conditions, weak social integration, or lack of organizational support, may reduce professional motivation and weaken ethical commitment. Previous studies have shown that diminished QWL is associated with lower job satisfaction, higher burnout, and weaker professional commitment, particularly in high-pressure healthcare and industrial environments [11, 12]. These findings suggest that QWL may be meaningfully linked to ethical behaviour, but this relationship requires clearer theoretical and empirical examination among occupational health professionals.

Literature review

Previous research on professional ethics has examined ethical behaviour from several theoretical and empirical perspectives. Some studies have focused on the role of professional codes, ethical awareness, and formal ethics training, while others have emphasized the influence of organizational climate, leadership, institutional support, and peer norms. For example, Adams et al. showed that rapid technological and organizational changes can disrupt established ethical norms and create new ethical challenges [13]. Nikolaidis et al. argued that formal ethics enculturation alone may be insufficient to ensure ethical conduct in complex and dynamic work environments [14]. Similarly, Dejean et al., in a national study of medical physicists, highlighted the importance of institutional support and professional norms in sustaining ethical standards. These studies indicate that professional ethics is not only an individual-level characteristic but is also shaped by the broader organizational and social environment [15].

In healthcare and human-service settings, professional ethics has also been linked to important workplace outcomes. Asadi et al. examined the relationship between resilience and professional ethics among nurses in southern Iran and reported a statistically significant association between the two constructs [16]. Razavi et al. found that adherence to ethical principles among Iranian dentists was related to higher patient trust and satisfaction [17]. In addition, Asadi et al. found that resilience was associated with ethical commitment among nurses, suggesting that personal and contextual resources may influence professionals’ ability to maintain ethical conduct under occupational stress [16]. These findings collectively show that ethical behaviour is affected by both individual capacities and workplace conditions.

A growing body of organizational research has further emphasized the role of ethical leadership, workplace climate, perceived fairness, and organizational support in shaping ethical behaviour. Jha and Singh suggested that ethical behaviour may be understood as a response to perceived organizational justice and managerial sincerity, particularly when employees believe that their contributions and wellbeing are valued [18]. Mehra and Narwal found that employees’ ethical behaviour was shaped by ethical leadership and workplace climate [19]. Sha et al., drawing on Social Exchange Theory and affective-cognitive perspectives, showed that supportive and morally consistent organizational systems were associated with lower levels of unethical workplace deviance [20]. Similarly, studies demonstrated that ethical climate and leadership conditions influence employee behaviour through mechanisms of justice and confidentiality, trust, and perceived integrity. These studies support the view that ethical behaviour is co-produced through individual, interpersonal, and organizational mechanisms [21, 22].

Quality of Work Life provides a useful framework for examining these organizational and psychosocial conditions. QWL reflects the degree to which the workplace provides employees with safe conditions, opportunities for growth, social support, justice and confidentiality, professional autonomy, and meaningful participation [23]. Prior research has shown that QWL is associated with job satisfaction, organizational commitment, burnout, wellbeing, and work-related performance [7, 11]. From an ethical perspective, QWL may also influence whether professionals feel supported, respected, and morally obligated to act in accordance with professional standards. When employees experience fair treatment, safe conditions, opportunities for development, and positive social relationships, they may be more likely to reciprocate through responsible, autonomous, and trustworthy professional behaviour.

Social Exchange Theory (SET) provides a coherent theoretical foundation for explaining this relationship. SET proposes that workplace relationships are shaped by reciprocal exchanges between employees and organizations. When employees perceive that the organization provides support, fairness, respect, and resources, they are more likely to respond with positive attitudes and behaviours [24]. In this framework, QWL can be understood as an organizational resource offered to employees, while ethical behaviour can be interpreted as one possible form of reciprocal professional response. Dimensions such as human capabilities development, safe working conditions, and social integration may signal that the organization values employees and supports their professional role. In return, employees may show stronger professional responsibility, greater Professional Autonomy, and higher trustworthiness. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies showing that organizational support, ethical climate, justice and confidentiality, and perceived respect are associated with ethical conduct and professional commitment [18, 19].

Based on the reviewed literature and SET, the proposed conceptual framework assumes that selected QWL dimensions, including human capabilities development, safe work environment, and social integration, are positively associated with professional ethics dimensions, namely professional responsibility, professional autonomy, and trustworthiness (Fig. 1).

Despite these contributions, important gaps remain in the literature. First, many previous studies have examined either QWL or professional ethics separately, while fewer have examined how specific dimensions of QWL are associated with specific dimensions of professional ethics. Second, much of the existing evidence relies on descriptive statistics, simple correlations, or regression-based approaches, which may not fully capture the multidimensional relationships among latent organizational and ethical constructs. Third, occupational health professionals remain underrepresented in this line of research, despite their important role in protecting worker health, supporting safety compliance, and managing ethical tensions between workers, employers, and regulatory expectations. Therefore, further empirical work is needed to clarify how key dimensions of QWL are structurally associated with professional ethics among occupational health professionals. To address this gap, the present study uses Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) to examine the associations between selected QWL dimensions and professional ethics dimensions within a Social Exchange Theory framework.

Research hypotheses

Grounded in Social Exchange Theory, this study assumes that favourable work conditions may be associated with stronger professional ethics because employees who perceive support, justice and confidentiality, safety, and respect in the workplace are more likely to respond with positive professional attitudes and behaviours. Accordingly, three dimensions of QWL were selected as key organizational resources that may be linked to three dimensions of professional ethics: professional responsibility, professional autonomy, and trustworthiness. Human capabilities development reflects opportunities for learning, skill use, and professional growth. Previous studies indicate that professional development, perceived organizational support, and empowerment are associated with stronger work engagement, ethical commitment, professional responsibility, and responsible professional conduct [18, 23]. These findings provide empirical support for expecting human capabilities development to be associated with professional responsibility, professional autonomy, and trustworthiness among occupational health professionals. Therefore, the following hypotheses are proposed: H1: Human Capabilities Development is positively associated with professional responsibility. H2: Human Capabilities Development is positively associated with professional autonomy. H3: Human Capabilities Development is positively associated with trustworthiness.

Safe work environment reflects physical safety, psychological support, and protection of employee wellbeing. In occupational health settings, a safe and supportive workplace may reinforce consistency between organizational values and professional ethical standards, thereby supporting responsible, autonomous, and trustworthy conduct [7, 12]. This evidence supports the expectation that safe work environments may be linked to greater professional responsibility, professional autonomy, and trustworthiness. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed: H4: Safe Work Environment is positively associated with professional responsibility. H5: Safe Work Environment is positively associated with professional autonomy. H6: Safe Work Environment is positively associated with trustworthiness.

Social integration refers to respectful relationships, cooperation, inclusion, and trust within the workplace. Existing studies suggest that interpersonal trustworthiness, ethical climate, social support, peer norms, and workplace inclusion are associated with ethical behaviour, professional commitment, honest communication, and responsible conduct [15, 19]. These findings justify the expectation that social integration may be positively associated with professional responsibility, professional autonomy, and trustworthiness. Thus, the following hypotheses are proposed: H7: Social Integration is positively associated with professional responsibility. H8: Social Integration is positively associated with professional autonomy. H9: Social Integration is positively associated with trustworthiness.

Methodology

Research design

This study employs a quantitative, cross-sectional research design to empirically examine the structural relationship between QWL and Professional Ethics among occupational health professionals. The design was selected to capture real-time perceptions and behaviours in industrial settings where ethical performance may be linked to organizational context. A cross-sectional approach is particularly suitable for theory-driven modelling studies that focus on testing associations among latent constructs at a specific point in time, rather than inferring causality or temporal change. Moreover, this design enables efficient data collection in occupational environments where access to professionals is often limited, and longitudinal follow-up is not feasible.

The conceptual model is grounded in SET, which posits that employees’ attitudes and behaviours are shaped by the perceived reciprocity in their relationship with the organization. Within this framework, the various dimensions of QWL function as exogenous predictors, while components of professional ethics serve as endogenous outcomes. The model assumes that when employees perceive fairness, support, and opportunities for growth, they are more likely to demonstrate ethically aligned behaviours at work. To test this multidimensional theoretical structure, the study utilizes PLS-SEM. PLS-SEM was selected because it is suitable for estimating complex latent-variable models [25], with moderate sample sizes and non-normal data. Its use in the present study was intended to examine the proposed associations rather than to claim strong predictive performance [26].

Population and sampling

The study population consisted of occupational health professionals employed in medium- and large-scale industrial enterprises in East Azerbaijan Province, Iran. A census sampling strategy was employed due to the relatively small, well-defined, and accessible population of occupational health professionals within the selected industrial settings. This approach was chosen to maximize representativeness, reduce sampling bias, and ensure sufficient statistical power for PLS-SEM analysis. Given the specialized nature of the target group and their limited population size, probability-based sampling techniques were not considered practical or methodologically advantageous in this context.

A total of 230 eligible individuals were invited to participate. After screening the returned questionnaires, 30 responses were excluded due to incompleteness or inconsistent responses. Therefore, the final analytic sample consisted of 200 valid responses, corresponding to an effective response rate of 86.9%. Inclusion criteria were: (1) formal employment in an occupational health role (2), a minimum of one year of continuous work experience in the current setting, and (3) active involvement in WHS-related tasks such as risk assessment, compliance training, or health surveillance. The unit of analysis was the individual professional, and the unit of observation was the self-reported response to standardized questionnaires on QWL and professional ethics. This approach ensured that inferences were drawn at the individual level rather than institutional aggregates.

Respondents were drawn from petrochemical, mining, manufacturing, and food processing sectors. The gender distribution was relatively balanced, with a slightly higher proportion of female participants (53.5%) than male participants (46.5%). The dominant age group was 20–30 years, representing 63.5% of the sample, followed by those aged 31–40 years (27.5%). Most participants had 1–10 years of professional experience, and 71.5% held a bachelor’s degree.

Regarding sample adequacy for PLS-SEM, the final analytic sample size of 200 met the minimum requirements based on both the “10-times rule” and statistical power criteria recommended by Hair et al. [26]. Specifically, the model includes a maximum of six paths leading to any endogenous construct; therefore, the minimum suggested sample size is 10 × 6 = 60 observations. With 200 valid cases, the dataset satisfies the minimum criteria for path coefficient estimation, model convergence, and hypothesis testing in PLS-SEM analysis.

Instrumentation

This study employed a structured questionnaire composed of two psychometric tools: a standardized QWL instrument and a researcher-developed Professional Ethics scale. Both instruments were adapted and validated for the context of occupational health professionals and were designed for reflective measurement modelling in PLS-SEM analysis. Examples of questionnaire items and their corresponding conceptual dimensions are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Work life quality instrument

The QWL construct was measured using a Persian-translated version of Walton’s model [27], comprising 27 items across eight dimensions: Fair Compensation, Safe Work Environment, Growth Opportunities, Organizational Constitutionalism, Work-Life Balance, Social Relevance, Social Integration, and Human Capabilities Development. All items were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree), and all were positively worded. No reverse-coded items were included. Final scores were computed using arithmetic means for each dimension and the total construct.

The content validity of the QWL instrument was assessed by a 10-member expert panel evaluating item necessity, relevance, clarity, and contextual appropriateness. The dimension-level CVR and I-CVI values are presented in Table 1 and met the acceptable criteria. Minor wording modifications were made, but no items were removed.

Professional ethics instrument

The professional ethics questionnaire was a researcher-developed tool based on a synthesis of existing national professional ethics codes, guidelines published by Iran’s Ministry of Health [28], and previous literature [16]. It included 29 items across six conceptual domains: professional excellence (3 items), trustworthiness (5 items), Service Quality (3 items), Professional Autonomy (4 items), professional responsibility (8 items), and justice/confidentiality (6 items). All items used a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree), with no reverse-worded items.

The content validity of the tool was assessed by a panel of 10 experts using Lawshe’s method. The CVR values ranged between 0.88 and 0.94, and the CVI ranged between 0.92 and 0.97 (Table 2). In addition, interpretive validity was addressed by asking three domain specialists to review the items for alignment with the intended ethical constructs in clinical and occupational health settings.

Common method bias

The questionnaires were administered in Persian using a forward–backward translation procedure conducted by two bilingual experts. Cultural suitability was further assessed through a pilot test with 20 occupational health professionals, confirming item clarity, relevance, and readability. Common method bias was examined using Harman’s single-factor test and measurement-model VIF values. These procedures were treated as diagnostic checks rather than definitive evidence of the absence of common method bias.

Data analysis strategy

Data were analysed using SPSS and SmartPLS 4 through four sequential stages. First, the dataset was screened for incomplete responses, missing values, distributional characteristics, and extreme outliers. Descriptive statistics were then calculated, followed by independent-samples t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and Pearson correlation analysis as supplementary procedures for examining group differences and bivariate associations.

Second, the psychometric evaluation of the two instruments proceeded from EFA to reflective measurement-model assessment. The instrument-specific EFA, reliability, convergent-validity, and discriminant-validity procedures and criteria are described in "Work life quality instrument" section and "Professional ethics instrument" section. Common method bias was assessed using the procedures described in "Common method bias" section.

Finally, the structural model was evaluated using bootstrapping with 5,000 subsamples and bias-corrected confidence intervals. Hypotheses were assessed using standardized path coefficients, p-values, confidence intervals, and f² effect sizes. R² values were used to quantify explained variance, while blindfolding-based Q² values were used to assess in-sample predictive relevance. SRMR and NFI were reported as approximate model-fit indices. The structural model constituted the primary evidence for hypothesis testing, whereas group comparisons and correlations were treated as supplementary findings.

Ethical considerations

All procedures conducted in this study adhered to the ethical standards outlined by the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.TBZMED.REC.1398.1295). Prior to data collection, participants were fully informed about the purpose, scope, and voluntary nature of the research. A written informed consent form was distributed to all respondents, outlining their right to withdraw at any stage without consequence, as well as assurances of confidentiality and anonymity in data handling and reporting.

Discussion

Interpretation of findings and comparison with prior studies

The findings reveal a selective rather than uniform pattern of association between Quality of Work Life and Professional Ethics. Human Capabilities Development, Safe Work Environment, and Social Integration were more consistently associated with Professional Responsibility and Professional Autonomy than with Trustworthiness. This pattern suggests that ethical outcomes closely embedded in everyday organizational interactions may be more responsive to perceived workplace conditions, whereas Trustworthiness may depend more strongly on personal dispositions, professional socialization, ethical leadership, or organizational climate. The modest associations, generally small effect sizes, and limited explained variance support a cautious interpretation and indicate that Quality of Work Life provides only a partial account of Professional Ethics outcomes. Although all paths to Trustworthiness were statistically significant, their effect sizes were generally very small. The effects of Human Capabilities Development and Safe Work Environment fell below the conventional threshold for a small effect, while the effect of Social Integration was small. This suggests that Trustworthiness may be shaped by additional individual, organizational, or professional factors not fully captured in the present model.

These findings are consistent with SET, which suggests that supportive, fair, and development-oriented work environments may be associated with positive professional attitudes and behaviours through mechanisms of reciprocity, perceived organizational care, and moral obligation. However, because both Quality of Work Life and Professional Ethics were assessed through self-report at a single time point, the observed associations may partly reflect shared method variance, social desirability, or general response tendencies. The cross-sectional design also prevents establishing temporal ordering; for example, professionals with stronger ethical orientations may perceive their workplace conditions more favourably. The findings should therefore be interpreted as concurrent associations consistent with Social Exchange Theory rather than evidence that Quality of Work Life causes changes in Professional Ethics.

From a SET perspective, QWL may function as an organizational resource that supports Professional Ethics through reciprocal employee–organization relationships [29]. However, the weak association between Social Integration and Trustworthiness suggests that this relational influence is limited. More specifically, Human Capabilities Development may represent organizational investment, Safe Work Environment may signal institutional care, and Social Integration may strengthen belonging and shared ethical norms [30–37]. Their comparatively stronger associations suggest that workplace-based resources may be more relevant to Professional Ethics than broader conditions such as Work-Life Balance [38], consistent with previous evidence on dignity, empowerment, and inclusive organizational environments [3, 39].

Beyond the organizational factors captured in the model, educational attainment also appeared relevant to ethical behaviour. The significant association between educational attainment and ethical behaviour is consistent with Dejean et al. [15] and Omidi et al. [40], both of whom reported that higher academic qualifications were associated with ethical sensitivity and decision-making. Kaya et al. [41] also found that education was related to ethical responsiveness and caring behaviour among nursing students, while Kazemi et al. [42] linked moral reasoning and ethical behaviour with professional learning and training. In the present study, this pattern is consistent with the observed association between Human Capabilities Development and Professional Autonomy, suggesting that professional growth may be particularly relevant to autonomy-related ethical conduct. Safe Work Environment and Social Integration showed modest associations with Professional Ethics outcomes [43]. consistent with evidence on safety climate and inclusive workplace cultures [39, 41, 44]. In contrast, Work-Life Balance showed limited relevance.

However, unlike Nayak et al. [8], who found that income significantly influenced ethical behaviour among public health professionals, our study did not reveal a statistically significant relationship between income levels and ethical scores. A possible explanation lies in the structural detachment between compensation systems and professional responsibility mechanisms within Iran’s public-sector employment. In these systems, ethical behaviour may be guided more by deontological standards embedded in organizational codes than by material gain. Similarly, while our analysis showed no significant gender-based differences in ethical conduct, contrary to findings by Kaya et al. [41], this may reflect uniform codes of ethical regulation, standardized job descriptions, and similar supervision levels among male and female professionals. Moreover, studies in similar collectivist cultures such as Dehghani et al. [45] suggest that communal work norms and peer responsibility may reduce the influence of individual characteristics such as gender on ethical decision-making.

Professional Ethics did not differ by gender or age, suggesting that institutional norms may outweigh basic demographic traits [13]. Married participants reported higher ethical scores, contrary to previous evidence, potentially reflecting culturally shaped responsibilities [14, 46]. Work experience was also positively associated with both QWL and ethical behaviour, which is consistent with Lee et al. [47] and Sha et al. [20], who indicated that professional maturity is linked to judgment, self-regulation, and moral engagement in complex workplace settings. Although the present study did not directly examine ethical leadership, the findings resonate with Baydeniz et al. [21], who highlighted that experienced professionals may contribute to ethical work climates when supported by transparent performance appraisals and value-driven workplace systems. In contrast, variables such as year of graduation and holding a second job did not show significant associations with QWL or ethics in this study. This differs from Khanian et al. [11], who noted that job multiplicity may reduce ethical consistency due to role conflict and burnout. However, such effects may be less salient in occupational health settings where job scopes are relatively well-defined and workloads are systematically distributed.

Overall, the findings provide modest, context-specific support for SET, with developmental, relational, and safety-related QWL dimensions showing greater relevance to Professional Ethics. Differences from prior evidence highlight the need for culturally sensitive research and workplace ethics initiatives.

Scientific contributions and policy implications

This study offers several novel contributions to the interdisciplinary literature on professional ethics and QWL, particularly within the domain of WHS. While a growing body of research has examined ethical behaviour in professions such as nursing, education, and management, there is a notable scarcity of empirical studies focusing specifically on WHS professionals. A comprehensive review across major scholarly databases revealed that ethical behaviour among occupational health specialists remains a largely unexplored topic. As a result, many of the studies used for comparative discussion in this paper are drawn from adjacent fields. This contextual gap highlights the novelty of our research and underscores the need to expand ethical inquiry into WHS-related disciplines.

Theoretically, this study provides preliminary, context-specific evidence that selected Quality of Work Life dimensions are associated with particular dimensions of Professional Ethics in a manner consistent with Social Exchange Theory. The observed pattern suggests that developmental and relational resources may be more closely associated with the examined ethical outcomes than some structural or material conditions. However, the modest R² values indicate that Social Exchange Theory and the selected Quality of Work Life dimensions provide only a partial explanation of these outcomes. The findings should therefore be regarded as a cautious application of Social Exchange Theory rather than a comprehensive explanatory model of occupational ethics.

From a policy perspective, the findings provide context-specific insights that may be useful for organizational leaders, human resource practitioners, and regulatory bodies. Investment in capability development and inclusive social structures may be relevant to supporting ethical conduct, although the modest explanatory power of the model suggests that other individual and organizational factors should also be considered. Policymakers may therefore consider workplace-based strategies as one component of broader efforts to support ethical cultures, while recognizing that the present model explains only a limited proportion of variation in Professional Ethics. Moreover, the cultural and institutional specificity of the findings calls for context-sensitive ethics promotion strategies, especially in collectivist environments like Iran’s public health sector. In such settings, ethical engagement may be better sustained through institutional trustworthiness and shared norms rather than through individual traits or material inducements.

Overall, this study addresses an important gap in the empirical literature and provides a preliminary analytical framework for examining ethical conduct among WHS professionals, while recognizing that the model explains only a limited proportion of variance in some ethical dimensions, particularly Trustworthiness.

Limitations and future research directions

Despite offering valuable insights into the relationship between Quality of Work Life and professional ethics, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to draw causal inferences; therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as relational rather than directional or causal. As a result, it cannot be determined whether improvements in QWL lead to stronger ethical behaviour or whether ethically committed professionals perceive their work environment more positively. Future research employing longitudinal, or panel designs could better clarify the temporal ordering and potential causal pathways between these constructs.

Second, the study was geographically limited to occupational health professionals in Iran, which may constrain the generalizability of the findings. Cultural norms, regulatory frameworks, and institutional practices may shape both perceptions of QWL and ethical behaviour, meaning that the observed relationships may not fully transfer to other national or organizational contexts. Multi-site and cross-cultural studies are therefore recommended to assess the robustness of the proposed relationships across diverse settings and regulatory environments.

Third, reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of social desirability and common method biases, which may have influenced the observed associations between QWL and ethical constructs. Although procedural and statistical checks were applied, these checks should be interpreted as partial diagnostic evidence rather than definitive proof that common method bias was absent. Future studies could mitigate this limitation by integrating objective indicators, supervisor ratings, peer assessments, or qualitative observations to triangulate ethical behaviour and workplace conditions.

Finally, the present study did not explicitly incorporate organizational-level variables such as ethical leadership, organizational justice, or formal ethics training programs. The absence of these variables limits the ability to fully explain the mechanisms through which QWL may be linked to ethical behaviour. Future research should include these factors as potential mediators or moderators to develop a more comprehensive and explanatory model of ethical conduct in occupational health settings. Overall, these limitations indicate that the findings should be interpreted as context-specific associations rather than causal or universally generalizable conclusions. Addressing these limitations through longitudinal designs, multi-source data, cross-cultural samples, and broader theoretical models would enhance the explanatory power, generalizability, and practical relevance of future research on Quality of Work Life and professional ethics.

Conclusion

This study examined the associations between selected Quality of Work Life dimensions and Professional Ethics among occupational health professionals in Iran, using Social Exchange Theory as the guiding framework. Although several structural paths were statistically significant, the model explained only 21.4% of the variance in Professional Responsibility, 18.6% in Professional Autonomy, and 8.2% in Trustworthiness. Accordingly, the findings indicate modest and partial associations rather than strong explanatory or predictive capacity. The particularly low explained variance for Trustworthiness suggests that additional individual, organizational, and professional factors should be incorporated into future models.

From a practical perspective, the findings suggest that improving developmental, psychosocial, and supportive work conditions may be associated with stronger ethical orientations among occupational health professionals; however, the modest R² values indicate that these factors explain only part of the variation in ethical outcomes. For policymakers and regulatory bodies in the WHS domain, these results may inform training strategies, ethical supervision, and organizational support systems, particularly in collectivist and hierarchical work cultures. However, due to the cross-sectional design and regional scope of the study, the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Future longitudinal and mixed-method studies are recommended to examine potential causal pathways and mediating mechanisms, such as ethical climate, perceived justice and confidentiality, and organizational identification.
