Cultural innovation driven nonlinear impact: New quality productivity and cultural consumption’s high-carbon trap
Authors: Jiaxin Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAgainst global net-zero carbon goals and China’s “dual carbon” goals, cultural consumption remains trapped in a high-carbon trap (HCT), hindering the sustainable development of environmental and social systems. Using benchmark regression and a mediating effect model, this study explores how new quality productivity (NQP) impacts carbon emissions from cultural consumption. Findings reveal a non-linear “first promoting, then inhibiting” effect of NQP on such emissions. It is worth noting that cultural innovation (CI) serves as a transmission mechanism through which NQP exerts an inverted U-shaped relationship on the HCT in cultural consumption. In addition, energy transition (ET) plays a regulatory role in the inverted U-shaped outcome, which can enhance the inhibitory effect of NQP on carbon emissions from cultural consumption. Overall, this study extends the Environmental Kuznets Curve theory to cultural consumption, offering causal insights and actionable policy implications for the sustainable transformation of environment-social systems.

Introduction

Research background and literature review

As China advances its “dual carbon” goals and high-quality development strategy for the cultural industry, achieving low-carbon cultural consumption requires comprehensive empowerment from new quality productivity (NQP). Cultural consumption refers to the act of satisfying people’s spiritual needs through purchasing and using cultural products and services, encompassing education, entertainment, tourism, and other domains. Its service-economy-dominated nature is regarded as embodying the quintessential characteristics of a non-material economy and low-carbon economy, making it a vital component of sustainable consumption in daily life.1 However, in recent years, the rapid advancement of digital technology has led to an expansion of consumption patterns, trapping cultural consumption in a “high-carbon trap” (HCT). This phenomenon describes seemingly green consumption behaviors that actually generate substantial carbon emissions through production, transportation, and operational processes.2,3 Data from the World Tourism and Travel Council (WTTC) in 2021 indicates that carbon emissions from culture and tourism account for 8%–10% of global total emissions. By 2030, carbon emissions from the global tourism industry may increase by another 25% or more.4 This trend could hinder the achievement of global net-zero carbon goals and constrain the sustainability of cultural consumption.

The development of the cultural industry has driven cultural consumption, forming an economic sector primarily engaged in the production and provision of cultural products and services. It has gradually become a vital component of China’s national economy.5,6,7 As one of the four ancient civilizations with uninterrupted continuity, China possesses abundant cultural resources that currently face challenges such as uneven development, unscientific allocation, and insufficient industrial innovation. This has led to high-carbon phenomena, including cultural emulation, cultural imitation, and cultural waste, some of which are even more covert.8 Internal operations and processes within the cultural industry sector may also harbor significantly high-carbon practices. Among these, large-scale physical set construction, long-distance transportation for tourism, and high-energy-consumption operational activities, while satisfying people’s spiritual needs, consume substantial traditional energy, resources, and materials during production or generate significant carbon emissions during transportation and storage.

From the consumer perspective, many cities and tourist attractions have created “trendy blocks” and nighttime economies. The lighting equipment required for these activities leads to extremely high carbon emissions.9 At the same time, the public’s awareness and acceptance of cultural products are relatively low, and consumers lack understanding of cultural symbols, making it difficult for them to identify and purchase truly low-carbon cultural products. This has greatly weakened their enthusiasm for choosing cultural goods.10 The NQP emphasizes the synergistic effect of elements such as technological innovation,11 structural optimization, and green innovation, promoting industrial transformation to achieve high-quality development of productivity.12 In addition, the NQP itself is a green productive force. Some studies have shown that NQP significantly reduce regional carbon intensity and can improve the energy consumption structure,13 thus having significant advantages in terms of resource utilization efficiency, environmental protection, and sustainable development.14 Existing econometric studies mainly focus on constructing evaluation systems that reflect the external manifestations of NQP, such as technological productivity, green productivity, and digital productivity.15 The high-quality development logic represented by NQP has been specifically extended in the fields of cultural production, consumption, and exchange in the new era to form cultural new quality productivity. On the one hand, it constitutes a direct manifestation of NQP in the dimension of spiritual value creation; on the other hand, as a form of productive forces driven by innovation and oriented toward low carbon, its role in avoiding the HCT in cultural consumption has become increasingly prominent.

Motivation and contribution

Surveying prior research, the sustainability of cultural consumption is not an absolute attribute. The development of NQP has increased people’s research interest in mitigating potential high-carbon phenomena. However, research on the relationship between NQP and carbon emissions from cultural consumption remains relatively limited. Firstly, the establishment of NQP often focuses on aspects such as technological and digital development. Secondly, NQP research mainly concentrates on economic growth and industrial upgrading, neglecting its impact on the low-carbon transformation of cultural consumption. Furthermore, there is a lack of in-depth exploration into the paths for overcoming HCT in cultural consumption.

To address this gap, this study examines the impact of NQP on the HCT in cultural consumption based on environmental Kuznets theory. Drawing on data from 30 Chinese provinces (2013–2022) and employing a two-way fixed-effects econometric model, we find that NQP exerts a significant “initially promoting, then inhibiting” effect on the formation of the HCT in cultural consumption. Moreover, cultural innovation serves as a converter enabling NQP to break the HCT. This occurs because NQP facilitates the transformation of cultural innovation outcomes into low-carbon substitutes within cultural consumption, thereby circumventing the trap. Consequently, this study contributes to existing literature in three key ways. First, the concept of NQP, a development concept with Chinese characteristics, is systematically introduced into the research fields of cultural consumption and carbon emissions. This enriches the extension of NQP and provides an innovative analytical perspective for interdisciplinary research on NQP. Second, extending the application of the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) theory to the field of cultural consumption clarifies the inflection points and boundaries of NQP in promoting low-carbon transformation. Finally, by identifying cultural innovation as the key mechanism connecting NQP and HCT in cultural consumption, it enriches research in innovation management. It provides policy insights for China to promote cultural green consumption and achieve sustainable development through the development of NQP.

Theoretical analysis and hypotheses

New quality productivity is nonlinearly correlated with carbon emissions from cultural consumption

There may be an inverted U-shaped nonlinear relationship between NQP and carbon emissions from cultural consumption, similar to the EKC, characterized by a pattern of “initial promotion followed by suppression.” The EKC theory posits a non-linear inverted U-shaped relationship between economic growth and environmental pollution.16 Specifically, during the initial stages of economic expansion, an extensive growth model has been shown to result in increased carbon emissions alongside economic scale expansion. Once an economy has reached a certain level of development, factors such as technological progress, industrial upgrading, and heightened environmental awareness can collectively lead to a gradual decline in carbon emissions. Nevertheless, NQP is an inherent requirement and an important focus for promoting high-quality development. Relying on technological innovation17 and industrial upgrading,18 they have effectively promoted the optimization of the economic structure and the improvement of energy efficiency.19,20 This emergent productive force has precipitated the substantial proliferation of innovative cultural consumption models, thereby further stimulating latent energy consumption. It is important to note that the rapid growth in digital cultural consumption has resulted in a significant increase in energy consumption by data centers, network transmission, and terminal devices. This has led to an absolute increase in carbon emissions. Concurrently, there is an increase in demand for cultural activities requiring significant energy and expertise, such as air travel and live performances, which typically result in substantial carbon emissions. The creation of new demand and the subsequent boosting of purchasing power are the driving factors behind the rapid quantitative expansion in cultural consumption, which in turn leads to a persistent increase in total carbon emissions and forms the ascending branch of the EKC curve.

The continuous development of NQP promotes the maturity of green energy technologies and the extensive improvement of green power grid infrastructure. When the cost of green energy technologies is lower than that of traditional energy, it may break the traditional high-carbon model. The cultural product sharing model facilitated by digital platforms has been demonstrated to have a significant impact on the reduction of energy consumption and production losses in the manufacturing of cultural products and derivatives.21 Concurrently, the green attributes of NQP have profoundly permeated every link in the cultural industry chain. Facilities such as data centers and cultural venues are increasingly adopting renewable energy power supplies, while sectors including online education and digital entertainment are witnessing significant uptake. This innovation in circular economic development models has been shown to effectively reduce physical resource consumption and energy expenditure during commutes, thus minimizing the largest component of cultural consumption’s carbon footprint.22 The development philosophy of high technology, high efficiency, and high quality advocated by NQP will have a profound impact on the evolution of social consumption culture. There is an increasing tendency among consumers to identify with and support green, intelligent cultural products and services. This shift in cultural market demand is evident from a shift from quantitative expansion to qualitative enhancement. This approach circumvents the persistent escalation in carbon emissions peaks that is precipitated by demand saturation.

H1: New quality productivity first promotes cultural carbon emissions and then restrains them.

The mediating effect of cultural innovation

In the era of the knowledge economy, cultural innovation (CI), as a means of creativity, can promote the transformation of cultural resources into capital, form new factors of production, and facilitate the circulation of cultural resources, thereby effectively driving socio-economic development.23,24 At the same time, CI is an important driving force for the spread and development of green culture and is also a top priority in promoting the formation of green culture and a green low-carbon lifestyle.25

During the early stages of NQP development, a low NQP level may struggle to effectively drive CI, potentially even producing a certain inhibitory effect in the short term. On one hand, the costs of technological adaptation in the cultural sector, high upfront investments, coupled with limited market scale and low innovation conversion rates during the same period, lead to an imbalance between input and output. On the other hand, NQP resources are often prioritized for foundational digital infrastructure development, energy transformation, and other areas of greater foundational, strategic, and green significance. This objectively creates a crowding-out effect on factor inputs for CI, leading to insufficient cultural innovation supply. Simultaneously, the cultural market is flooded with homogenized products and services that fail to adequately meet diverse cultural consumption demands. There is a shortage of high-quality, highly creative, and aesthetically rich cultural products and services with high added value, and new scenarios and business forms for cultural consumption are also lacking.26 Moreover, the quality of cultural products and services is uneven, and the phenomenon of high prices still exists, which, to some extent, suppresses residents' willingness to consume. The carbon dioxide produced by the production of products and services cannot be transformed into economic benefits, resulting in a huge cultural waste of high carbon phenomenon.27

Based on innovation diffusion theory and cultural economics theory, NQP may drive CI to reduce carbon emissions from cultural consumption. NQP uses digital technology to reconstruct cultural production methods, while also promoting a shift in cultural consumption from a “physical goods-oriented” approach to a “service-oriented” approach. Specifically, firstly, NQP can accelerate cultural dissemination, generate new cultural consumption demands, and reshape consumption patterns. Innovative cultural products and services can attract consumers' attention, stimulate their cultural consumption tendencies, and further encourage enterprises to prioritize green innovation initiatives in product development and service provision. This, in turn, drives technological innovation and upgrading in related industries, enhances the production efficiency of the industrial chain,28 thereby reducing carbon emissions and escaping the HCT of cultural consumption.29 Secondly, NQP has injected technical momentum and the possibility of model innovation into CI, while CI helps optimize the production processes and resource allocation of the cultural industry.30 Innovative activities bring new technologies, new concepts, and new management models, thereby reshaping the traditional production processes of the cultural industry. Finally, NQP provides strong technical support, rich resource channels, and efficient collaboration platforms for CI, promoting CI to break through traditional forms and boundaries, expand into new industrial fields and business models,31 create more economic growth points, and provide a broader space for the development of NQP. For example, the integration of culture and technology has given birth to emerging industries such as the digital cultural industry and the animation game industry.11 These industries have achieved rapid development through innovative business models and technological applications.

H2: Cultural innovation is an intermediary variable for new quality productivity to avoid the “high-carbon trap” in cultural consumption. New quality productivity, through cultural innovation as a transmission mechanism, achieves an inverted U-shaped relationship in its impact on avoiding the “high-carbon trap” in cultural consumption.

The regulatory effect of the energy transition

Theoretically, the energy transition exerts a dual-channel influence on the carbon emission effects generated by the integration of NQP and cultural consumption.

On the one hand, the popularization of clean energy technologies and high-efficiency energy utilization technologies driven by energy transition (ET) has laid a foundation for low-carbon energy supply for the development of NQP.32 The core essence of NQP lies in realizing production efficiency improvement and development model innovation through digital technologies and intelligent technologies.33,34 The stable and low-carbon supply of clean energy can effectively reduce the carbon emission intensity of energy consumption in the process of NQP promoting the digitalization and intelligentization of cultural consumption scenarios.35,36 For instance, when cultural venues adopt photovoltaic power supply combined with intelligent energy management systems, the carbon emissions from energy consumption will be significantly reduced during the implementation of NQP driven cultural consumption activities such as digital cultural exhibitions and online cultural experiences.

On the other hand, ET directly affects carbon emissions from cultural consumption by altering the structure and efficiency of energy use in this domain.37,38 Under a high level of ET, the cultural consumption sector tends to adopt more energy-saving equipment and low-carbon operation models, such as the application of energy-efficient lighting and audio equipment in cultural performances, and the use of new energy vehicles in cultural tourism.39 In this context, the empowerment of NQP to cultural consumption is no longer a mere pursuit of formal innovation and scale expansion, but rather a deep integration with low-carbon energy use. This integration enables the innovative development of cultural consumption while effectively controlling or even reducing carbon emissions, thus avoiding the HCT. Conversely, under a low level of ET, due to the lack of support from low-carbon energy and guarantees of energy-saving technologies, NQP may rely more on traditional high-carbon energy sources to drive innovation in cultural consumption. This reliance will lead to an increase in carbon emissions along with the development of NQP, failing to avoid the HCT.

H3: Energy transition plays a positive regulatory role in the impact of new quality productivity on the avoidance of the “high-carbon trap” in cultural consumption.

Research design

Variable selection

Independent variable: new quality productivity

NQP represents the development and innovation of traditional productive forces. On the one hand, they embody the qualitative transformation and elevation of laborers, means of labor, and objects of labor. On the other hand, they function as a new driver for advancing the building of Chinese-style modernization. Based on the principles of data availability and completeness, and drawing on the connotation of NQP and related studies,36,40,41 a new quality productivity evaluation index system has been constructed from three dimensions: new-quality laborers, new-quality objects of labor, and new-quality means of labor. See Table S1 for details. Meanwhile, in this article, the entropy Weight-TOPSIS method is adopted to measure the development level of NQP, minimizing the loss of data and information to the greatest extent possible.

Dependent variable: high-carbon trap

In this study, the high-carbon phenomenon generated by cultural consumption that should have been low-carbon behavior is regarded as a trap, and it is measured by taking the logarithm of the carbon emissions generated by cultural consumption activities. Since the Chinese government does not officially track carbon emissions from cultural consumption, it is necessary to use a cultural consumption coefficient for calculation. Referenced the indicator measurement methods from relevant literature,4,42 first, carbon emissions data for Chinese provinces are extracted from EDGAR. Subsequently, this set of data is multiplied by the cultural consumption coefficient, and through this calculation process, the carbon emissions associated with cultural consumption are derived. It should be emphasized that the cultural consumption coefficient specifically denotes the ratio of per capita expenditure on cultural and entertainment activities to the total amount of consumption expenditure. And its calculation formula is expressed as:(Equation 1)HCTit=CEit∗CCCitwhere HCTit denotes the carbon emissions from cultural consumption in year t of province i; CEit denotes the total carbon emissions of the province, and CCCit denotes the cultural consumption coefficient. Higher values indicate higher HCT in the region.

Control variable

Drawing on the practices from relevant literature, a series of provincial-level control variables are introduced. Local government support (GOV) which plays a guiding role in low-carbon consumption. It may reduce carbon emissions by promoting low-carbon cultural projects, but it may also increase emissions in the short term due to the large-scale construction required for cultural events. Thus, it is represented by the proportion of cultural, tourism, sports, and media expenditures in the local general public budget.43 Industrial structure (IND) serves as the primary pathway for driving the development of the digital creative industry and stimulating public spiritual and cultural consumption, transitioning toward the tertiary sector will reduce the carbon emissions dependency of cultural consumption, as measured by the ratio of the added value of the tertiary industry to GDP.44 The urbanization level (URB) can to some extent promote new consumption patterns and encourage low-carbon and green consumption behaviors, as measured by the urbanization rate of the permanent resident population.45 The improvement of transportation infrastructure (TRAN) has promoted decarbonization.46 A well-developed transportation network has reduced residents' reliance on private cars, lowering the economic costs and carbon emissions of cultural and tourism trips, this is measured using the logarithm of highway mileage. Digital finance (DFI) is used by the Digital Inclusive Finance Index from Peking University,47 and it provides financing support for low-carbon innovation in cultural enterprises while promoting online consumption to reduce carbon emissions. The social consumption (SOC) drives cultural consumption growth and affects carbon emission patterns. Higher consumption may boost willingness to pay for low-carbon options. Therefore, the ratio of total retail sales of consumer goods to GDP serves as a suitable metric.

Mediating variable

The measurement of CI is a cutting-edge issue in academia. Currently, most academic research on CI focuses primarily on qualitative analysis, with a particular emphasis on “cultural inheritance and innovation,” “cultural integration and innovation,” “innovative culture,” and “cultural and corporate innovation.” However, there is a lack of quantitative measurement of CI. Existing literature primarily focuses on constructing an index for the high-quality development of the cultural industry. This article draws on relevant research,48 introducing two primary indicators, cultural innovation input and cultural innovation output—and employs the Super-Efficient SBM model to measure CI across all provinces and municipalities nationwide. (See Table S2 for indicator construction.).

Regulating variable

ET is defined as the process of transforming an energy production and consumption pattern centered on fossil fuels into an energy system led by renewable energy sources, and this transition is marked by higher levels of cleanliness, efficiency, and sustainability. The optimization of energy consumption structure serves as a crucial measure for attaining the “dual carbon” targets and promoting the green, low-carbon development of both the economy and society. What is more significant is that it offers a more adaptable energy base to support the development of NQP, and in turn, it exerts a moderating influence on the association between NQP and carbon emissions. Within the scope of this study, the degree of optimization in the energy consumption structure is evaluated using the ratio of clean energy power generation to the total volume of power generation.

Endogeneity test

To prevent bidirectional causality and omitted variable issues, endogeneity tests are required to ensure the robustness of the benchmark regression results. This study selects the logarithm of artificial intelligence patents lagged by one period as the instrumental variable. Artificial intelligence patents reflect a region’s innovative output in the field of intelligent technology, and the “lagged by one period” approach avoids bidirectional causality in the current period. Conclusions and policy recommendations

Conclusions

This study first clarifies the connotation of NQP. Then, it constructs a framework to measure the development level of new quality productive forces in 30 provincial-level administrative regions in China from the perspectives of new quality talent resources, new quality of labor object, and new quality of labor resources. Subsequently, using inter-provincial panel data from 2013 to 2022, it empirically examines the impact of NQP on the HCT in cultural consumption. The main conclusions are as follows:

Firstly, the entropy-weighted TOPSIS method is used to quantify the development level of NQP. The results show that Guangdong, Beijing, Shandong, Jiangsu, Shanghai, and Zhejiang rank among the top six; in contrast, Xinjiang, Ningxia, and Inner Mongolia lag significantly, presenting a pattern of gradient decline from the east to the west.

Secondly, the benchmark regression results indicate that the impact of the development of NQP on carbon emissions from cultural consumption is not simply emission inhibition, but follows the trajectory of “first promoting consumption, then inhibiting emissions,” showing a significant inverted U-shaped relationship, which is an expanded verification of the EKC theory in a specific field.

Thirdly, a cultural innovation index system is established to quantify the level of CI, and its spatial-temporal distribution pattern is similar to the development level of NQP. At the same time, innovation management is extended to the field of the low-carbon economy, verifying that cultural innovation plays a significant mediating role between NQP and HCT.

Finally, ET has a non-linear moderating effect on the relationship between NQP and HCT from cultural consumption, and also shows a certain positive moderating effect within the sample range. Energy transition provides a fundamental support for the low-carbon effect of NQP.

Policy recommendations

Drawing on the findings of this study, the following policy recommendations are put forward to effectively advance the development of NQP and provide robust support for the low-carbon transformation of the cultural consumption sector:

First, it is recommended that NQPs be vigorously supported and promoted in their development. It is evident that, on the one hand, NQP has been showing steady improvement on an annual basis across various regions. However, it is equally important to acknowledge the presence of significant imbalances. It is recommended that provinces leverage their respective strengths and formulate tailored pathways for enhancing NQP based on local conditions. To promote NQP, we should make full use of the radiating effect of these demonstration provinces. Provinces exhibiting comparatively diminished levels of NQP should assign priority to the enhancement of the caliber of nascent quality talent, the exploitation of the benefits attendant to novel quality means of production, and the acceleration of the transformation and upgrading of cultural industries. Conversely, targeted breakthroughs are imperative to achieve the low-carbon inflection point for regional NQP. In consideration of the divergent regional characteristics exhibited by the western regions, which are undergoing a process of catch-up, and the eastern regions, which are experiencing an intensification in their developmental efforts, it is recommended that low-carbon resources be allocated in a manner that is commensurate with regional differences. For the western provinces, priority should be given to supporting the digital and low-carbon transformation of traditional cultural industries, helping them rapidly approach and cross the inflection point. In the eastern provinces, the primary focus should be on R&D in cutting-edge technologies, including metaverse cultural tourism and virtual cultural experiences. This approach will serve to consolidate the low-carbon effects that have already been achieved.

Second, the strengthening of dual pathways for cultural innovation with the aim of empowering low-carbon development is recommended. It is important to implement targeted incentives for low-carbon-oriented cultural innovation projects. Policy subsidies should be provided to cultural enterprises that are developing technological innovations, such as digital cultural creativity, and model innovations, such as local micro-tourism. This will stimulate their innovative vitality. Conversely, the establishment of cultural innovation industrial parks guided by low-carbon standards is imperative. The integration of innovative components within these parks is expected to facilitate the effective allocation of cultural resources and optimize production processes within the cultural industry, thereby contributing to a reduction in carbon emissions during cultural consumption.

Third, efforts should be made to enhance the synergy between the energy transition and NQP. On the one hand, there is a necessity to increase the proportion of clean energy used in cultural consumption sectors, and to expand the adoption of low-carbon energy supply models, such as photovoltaic and wind power generation, in cultural venues. Conversely, the promotion of energy-efficient equipment and low-carbon operational models within cultural enterprises is recommended, including the increased utilization of energy-saving lighting and sound equipment in cultural performances. It is through the implementation of these dual measures that we will affect a profound shift toward low-carbon development in the field of cultural consumption. This will establish the energy foundation for NQP to curb carbon emissions, thereby amplifying the synergistic carbon reduction effects of both initiatives.

Limitations of the study

There are several aspects of this study that can be further developed in future research. First, this study examines provincial panel data, which may mask more micro-level regional heterogeneity. Since cultural consumption and innovative activities are often more active at the urban level, and different cities have varying endowments of cultural resources, the innovation levels of the cultural industry in different cities may vary significantly, requiring more detailed data for further research. Second, the estimation is made using the product of the cultural consumption coefficient and total carbon emissions. Although this method is practical, it may mask the differences in the internal structure of cultural consumption. Meanwhile, the measurement of cultural innovation mainly relies on data from cultural manufacturing enterprises, which may not fully cover activities in non-formal sectors such as cultural services and folk cultural innovation. In the future, we will conduct further research from an urban perspective, optimize the calculation method of carbon emissions from cultural consumption, construct a scenario-specific carbon emission accounting framework combined with the input-output method, and accurately capture the impact of differences in the internal structure of cultural consumption on carbon emissions to strengthen research in cultural economics, sustainable consumption theory, and other related fields.

Analysis of the entropy Weight-TOPSIS method for accounting new quality productivity

The evaluation index system of new quality productivity (NQP) reflects the qualitative transformation and improvement of laborers, means of labor, and objects of labor, and there may be non-linear relationships and information overlap between these indicators. The entropy weight method conducts objective weighting based on the information entropy contained in each evaluation index. Its advantages not only lie in effectively reducing the interference of subjective human judgment on weight distribution, but also in accurately representing the actual importance of different indicators through the quantitative differences of information entropy, and at the same time, it can dynamically capture the inherent laws of the evolution of indicator weights over time.52,53 The TOPSIS method is determined by calculating the relative distance between each evaluation object and the theoretical optimal solution and the theoretical worst solution based on the values of each evaluation object on each measurement index, measuring its relative proximity to the ideal point, so as to ensure the fairness and objectivity of the calculation results.54 Therefore, this study draws on previous research results to measure NQP.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical and empirical analyses were conducted using Stata software, with specific version information listed in the key resources table. The measures of central tendency and dispersion used in this study are the mean and standard error, respectively. Statistical significance is defined at conventional levels (∗∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗p < 0.05, ∗p < 0.1). Detailed empirical methods, including benchmark regression, mediating effect, and moderating effect models, are presented in the method details section.
