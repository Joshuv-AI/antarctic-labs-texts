Successful treatment of juvenile polyposis of infancy with sirolimus: a case report
Authors: Pei Xiao, Ting Zhang, Yizhong Wang, Yongmei Xiao, Xiaolu Li, Ruixue Wang, Youran Li, Ting Song
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfantile Juvenile polyposis of infantile (JPI) is a rare and aggressive form of juvenile polyposis syndrome (JPS) typically diagnosed in the first year of life. It often carries a poor prognosis due to chronic gastrointestinal bleeding, protein-losing enteropathy, malnutrition and immune deficiency.Case presentationWe report a case of a girl initially presented with pallor at 7 months of age, which progressed to gastrointestinal bleeding and protein-losing enteropathy. Background

Juvenile polyposis syndrome (JPS) is a rare autosomal dominant condition characterized by the presence of multiple distinct juvenile polyps in the gastrointestinal tract [1]. JPS occurs 1 in 100.000 people. JPS patients develop multiple juvenile polyps of the gastrointestinal tract, especially in the colon/rectum (98%), stomach (14%), duodenum (7%) and small bowel (7%) [2]. It has been clinically defined by the presence of 5 juvenile polyps in the colon, juvenile polyps throughout the gastrointestinal tract, or any number of juvenile polyps and a family history of juvenile polyposis [1]. Patients with JPS are typically asymptomatic, but when symptoms do occur, rectal bleeding accompanied by anemia is the most common presentation. This is often followed by abdominal pain, diarrhea, and potentially intussusception at the location of the largest polyps. It is important to note that JPS can also manifest with additional symptoms outside of the intestines, including cutaneous manifestations such as telangiectasia and pigmented nevi, as well as skeletal abnormalities like macrocephaly, hydrocephalus, cleft palate, polydactyly, and hypertelorism. These extra-intestinal manifestations are encountered in a significant percentage of JPS cases, with cutaneous manifestations seen in up to 56% of patients and skeletal stigmata in up to 70% [3].

Juvenile polyposis Infantile (JPI) is an extremely rare disease with limited reported cases in the literature. In contrast to other forms of juvenile polyposis, JPI typically presents early in life, leading to a severe clinical course and reduced life expectancy. Key features of JPI include disease onset in early childhood (often within the first 2 years of life) and severe gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhea, significant intestinal bleeding, protein-losing enteropathy, intussusception, rectal or polyp prolapse, and inanition, ultimately resulting in death during early childhood. JPI is not linked to a family history and has been associated with the deletion of a chromosomal region containing the phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) and bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 1A (BMPR1A) genes [4, 5].

This case study illustrates a positive clinical outcome achieved through the early administration of sirolimus in patients with JPI. While sirolimus shows promising benefits, it does not seem to replace the necessity of aggressive polypectomy.

Case presentation

A premature girl, delivered at 35 weeks’ gestation via cesarean delivery with a weight of 2700 g, presented at 7 months of age in June 2022 with severe anemia (hemoglobin of 3.4 g/dL). She had no family history of gastrointestinal disease. One week later, she experienced gastrointestinal bleeding and protein-losing enteropathy. An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy showed multiple polyps in the stomach. Colonoscopy revealed multiple colorectal polyps, leading to multiple polypectomies being performed (Fig. 1A). Histopathology of the resected polyps showed characteristic features of juvenile polyps with no dysplasia (Fig. 2A-B). Initially managed with endoscopic polypectomies, she later required frequent blood transfusions and albumin due to recurrent bleeding episodes causing severe anemia and hypoalbuminemia (Fig. 3A-B). Genetic testing through targeted microarray analysis identified a 2.1 Mb deletion in chromosome 10q23.2q23.31 involving both the PTEN gene and BMPR1A gene.

Albumin and heamoglobin level before and after sirolimus

At 10 months of age, specifically in September 2022, we commenced treatment with sirolimus at an initial dose of 1.5 mg/m2 per day administered twice daily (0.4 mg bid), a mammalian target of rapamycin protein (mTOR) Inhibitor. Prior to starting sirolimus, an informed consent was obtained from the parents, outlining details about the medication, treatment objectives in JPI, associated risks, side effects, alternatives, and potential benefits. The primary aim was to decelerate the proliferation of gastrointestinal polyps, or ideally, promote their regression. Before sirolimus therapy, the patient had an average albumin level of 2.5–2.9 g/dL and hemoglobin level of 3.4–7.1 g/dL, necessitating weekly albumin infusions and RBC transfusions every 1–2 weeks. Following 1 month of sirolimus treatment targeting a serum level of 6 to 8 ng/mL, there was no longer a need for albumin infusions or RBC transfusions. Albumin levels remained stable at 3.3–3.5 g/dL and hemoglobin levels improved to 10.5–12.1 g/dL.

At 14 months of age, following 4 months of sirolimus treatment, the patient experienced intussusception approximately 10 cm into the small intestine. The patient initially underwent gastroenteroscopy, revealing multiple polyps in the stomach and intestines, which significantly decreased compared to pre-sirolimus treatment. Subsequently, endoscopic polypectomy was carried out. The procedure then transitioned to the abdomen, where laparoscopic reduction of small intestinal intussusception and further polypectomy were conducted, eliminating the need for intestinal resection.

At 21 months of age, sirolimus was discontinued due to pneumonia. Two months post-discontinuation, the patient experienced aggravated intestinal bleeding, with stable hemoglobin levels but decreased albumin to 3.1 g/dL. Gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed numerous polyps of varying sizes, leading to another round of endoscopic polypectomy. Subsequently, sirolimus treatment was resumed. After a 15-month follow-up period, the patient has remained stable without requiring transfusions or albumin supplementation. Post-treatment growth improvement was observed, as well as enhanced quality of life for the patient and their family.

Although there is intermittent blood in the stool and the need for endoscopic polypectomy every 5 months, the patient has not experienced the common side effects associated with sirolimus use, such as lipid disorders, cytopenia, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, interstitial lung disease, or thrombotic microangiopathy.

Discussion and conclusions

JPS is a rare autosomal dominant disease characterized by hamartomatous growths that develop throughout the gastrointestinal tract of affected children. Diagnosis of JPS is based on the presence of ≥ 5 gastrointestinal polyps, or any polyps in a child with a family history of the syndrome. Individuals with JPS face an increased risk of colorectal and gastric cancers in the long term, as well as potential immediate impacts on health and development. The majority of patients with JPS exhibit a loss of function variant in either mothers against decapentaplegic homolog 4 (SMAD4) or BMPR1A. Furthermore, case studies have revealed a connection between chromosome 10q23 deletions involving BMPR1A and PTEN, and the rare onset of various phenotypically diverse polyposis syndromes [1].

JPI is a distinct and aggressive subtype of JPS characterized by severe gastrointestinal symptoms. JPI emerges during early infancy and is often linked to a poorer prognosis due to gastrointestinal blood loss and protein-losing enteropathy, leading to additional health issues such as malnutrition, severe anemia, and immune deficiency. The presence of polyps also raises the risk of intussusception and gastrointestinal obstruction, which may require urgent surgical intervention [6].

JPS has been associated with mutations in both SMAD4 on chromosome band 18q21.2 and BMPR1A on chromosome band 10q23.2, both integral components of the BMP/TGF-beta signaling pathway. BMPR1A is situated in close proximity to PTEN on the chromosome, and cases of larger deletions affecting both genes have been documented. Children carrying deletions in both BMPR1A and PTEN genes may exhibit symptoms of JPI. Delnatte et al [4] initially suggested a cooperation between PTEN and BMPR1A and the existence of a combined synergistic effect of the deletion of both PTEN and BMPR1A, leading to a severe form of JPS, i.e. JPI. The combined deletion of the PTEN and BMPR1A genes, which are pivotal players in the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation and apoptosis via PI3K/Akt and BMP pathways, respectively, could lead to more aggressive polyposis and development of malignancies [4, 7, 8].

Morbidity associated with JPI is significant. The standard treatment involves repeated endoscopic surveillance and polypectomy, often requiring red blood cell transfusions and albumin infusions. Advanced nutritional support may be necessary in cases of severe growth impairment. In the most severe instances, colectomy may be necessary to reduce blood and protein losses. Previous case studies have shown some success with surgical interventions such as bowel resection and multi-organ transplantation. Busoni et al [9] documented a case of delayed JPI diagnosis at the age of 6, where a 3.34 Mb de novo deletion was found at 10q23.1q23.31, involving the PTEN and BMPR1A genes. The disease progression was severe, presenting symptoms like diarrhea, abdominal pain, inanition, refractory anemia, rectal bleeding, hypoalbuminemia, and exudative enteropathy. A sub-total colectomy, along with intraoperative endoscopic removal of ileal and rectal stump polyps, was necessary for palliative disease management. Chandan et al [10] reported a case of JPI diagnosed at 9 months of age, where the patient underwent multiorgan transplantation, including liver, small bowel, pancreas, and colon transplants at 3.5 years old. Unfortunately, the patient succumbed to an invasive pneumococcal infection at the age of 5.

Frequent endoscopy poses a challenge for patients with gastrointestinal polyposis and other inherited gastrointestinal cancer syndromes. Moreover, many patients exhibit hesitancy and reluctance towards surgical resection of the affected organ once endoscopic management is no longer viable, despite guideline recommendations. Given the significant impact on quality of life and the lack of disease-modifying treatment options, there is a compelling need to explore new mechanisms for treating these diseases effectively and safely. Rapamycin is an inhibitor of mTOR, which shows strong expression in mouse small intestine polyps with APC defects, as well as in human colorectal adenomas [11] and colorectal cancers [12]. In animal models, the effectiveness of sirolimus to inhibit the formation of gastrointestinal polyps and increase the time of progression to dysplasia in patients with intestinal polyposis syndromes, including JPS [13].

In addition to animal models, an increasing number of studies have documented the use of sirolimus in JPS. Busoni, et al [14] initially reported a case of a patient who presented with gastrointestinal bleeding and protein-losing enteropathy at 4 months of age. A chromosomal microarray revealed a single allele deletion in chromosome 10q23, involving both the PTEN and BMPR1A genes. Despite undergoing subtotal colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis, the patient continued to experience protein-losing enteropathy and bleeding, necessitating ongoing blood transfusions and albumin infusions. Treatment with sirolimus was initiated at 20 months of age, and no further albumin infusions or red blood cell transfusions were required during the subsequent 27-month follow-up period. Another case report described an 11-year-old girl who had been monitored since birth for macrocephaly, mild hypotonia, motor and developmental delay. At 18 months of age, she experienced rectal bleeding, and colonoscopy revealed numerous juvenile polyps, some up to 3 cm in diameter. Genetic analysis identified a de novo germline deletion encompassing PTEN and BMPR1A. The patient exhibited failure to thrive, mild anemia, and protein-losing enteropathy. Following a colonic perforation during the resection of large polyps at 5 years old, she underwent emergency transient ileostomy. Sirolimus treatment was initiated at 6 years of age, and after more than 4 years of follow-up, she demonstrated weight improvement and normal serum albumin levels without recurrence of anemia or adverse events [15]. Our case aligns with those reported in the literature, involving deletions of the PTEN and BMPR1A genes, and suggests that sirolimus treatment can ameliorate protein-losing gastrointestinal disease. In our case, sirolimus therapy commenced at 10 months of age, just 3 months after diagnosis, marking the youngest reported use of the treatment in a child. Adverse effects of sirolimus that are clinically significant and require specific treatment, or have the potential to impact both short- and long-term patient health, include high levels of cholesterol and triglycerides, pneumonia (both infectious and non-infectious), anemia, formation of lymphoceles, and impaired healing of wounds. Less severe complications of sirolimus treatment can include temporary reductions in platelets and white blood cells, as well as gastrointestinal issues and ulcerations in the mucosal lining [18]. mTOR inhibitors, such as sirolimus, have a limited range of effective doses, with absorption rates that vary widely among individuals and within the same person over time. Due to this variability, it is essential to monitor sirolimus levels in the blood to ensure effective treatment. In our case, following the initiation of sirolimus administration, weekly monitoring of drug concentration was implemented, with subsequent dose adjustments based on the concentration levels. Once the blood drug concentration reached a stable state, the monitoring interval was gradually extended to 2 weeks, 4 weeks, and eventually 8 weeks. It was observed that sirolimus concentration typically ranged between 6-10ng/ml.

The increasing evidence supporting the use of sirolimus suggests it could be a viable alternative to endoscopic treatment for patients with JPS, potentially preventing the need for colectomy. We present a successful case of treating a girl with severe infantile juvenile polyposis using sirolimus, marking the first reported case from China. While sirolimus shows promising benefits, it does not seem to replace the necessity of polypectomy.
