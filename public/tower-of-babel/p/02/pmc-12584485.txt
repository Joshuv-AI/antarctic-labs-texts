Assessing the knowledge, attitudes and practices towards malaria prevention and determinants of antenatal care utilization among pregnant women in Sekyere South district, Ghana; a cross-sectional study
Authors: Clement Naayaara Zuuri, Dominic Kofi Gyamah, Loviah Buabeng, Ruth Mensah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria in pregnancy contributes significantly to poor maternal health outcomes, accounting for 17.6% of outpatient visits in Ghana. The Ashanti Region, particularly Sekyere South District, bears a high burden despite interventions, such as ITN distribution and intensified IPTp education. Limited evidence exists on how pregnant women’s knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) influence preventive behaviours and ANC use in this setting.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 422 pregnant women using structured questionnaires. Descriptive statistics and logistic regression were applied to assess predictors of malaria prevention behaviours and ANC attendance.ResultsMost respondents (78%) had adequate knowledge, though misconceptions persisted (56% believed malaria could spread person-to-person). Only 48% knew the recommended ≥ 3 IPTp doses, and ITN use was 63%, with non-use linked to heat discomfort and unavailability. Knowledge of IPTp dosage was significantly associated with uptake (p < 0.001), but socio-demographic factors were not predictors of ANC attendance.ConclusionGaps in malaria prevention stem from misconceptions, incomplete IPTp adherence, and inconsistent ITN use rather than supply shortages. Priorities include strengthening ANC counselling, correcting myths, introducing reminder prompts, and expanding outreach with ITN replacement. Enhancing implementation fidelity is critical to improving maternal health outcomes.

Background

Malaria remains one of the leading global public health challenges. The World Malaria Report 2024 estimates 263 million malaria cases and approximately 597,000 deaths in 2023, with the African Region accounting for about 94–95% of the burden [1].

The burden of malaria is disproportionately concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, which accounts for approximately 95% of all cases and deaths globally [2]. Among the vulnerable populations, pregnant women are particularly at high risk due to the immunological and physiological changes that occur during pregnancy, which increase susceptibility to malaria infection and its severe consequences [3–5]. Malaria in pregnancy (MiP) is strongly associated with maternal anaemia, placental parasitaemia, low birth weight, preterm birth, and stillbirth, with downstream effects on neonatal morbidity and mortality [6]. These outcomes not only affect the health and survival of the mother and infant but also contribute to increased neonatal morbidity and mortality, perpetuating cycles of poverty and poor health in endemic regions [7].

Ghana faces year-round malaria transmission and remains one of the high-burden countries in sub-Saharan Africa. In Ghana, malaria is prevalent year-round, accounting for approximately 30% of outpatient visits and 23% of inpatient hospital admissions [8]. The country's control strategy follows WHO guidelines and is detailed in the National Malaria Strategic Plan (2021–2025) and the more recent National Malaria Elimination Strategic Plan (2024–2028) [9]. The strategy emphasizes vector control methods, including insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS), as well as intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP), case management, behaviour change initiatives, and targeted interventions at the sub-national level. Although coverage of the recommended IPTp-SP regimen (at least 3 doses) has increased, it remains below optimal levels nationwide, reaching about 60% by 2022 [10]. In the Sekyere enclave of the Ashanti Region, malaria continues to be one of the leading causes of morbidity, particularly among pregnant women who are highly vulnerable to its adverse outcomes [11]. Local challenges, including limited awareness, inconsistent use of preventive measures, and gaps in health service delivery, underscore the need to assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to malaria prevention in this setting.

Within the Ashanti Region, where Sekyere South is situated, routine data indicate a consistent malaria burden in outpatient services [12]. Recent research from the nearby Sekyere East District also shows a relatively high prevalence of malaria in pregnancy (MiP) [11]. Furthermore, data from the Ghana Malaria Behaviour Survey 2022 reveal variation in malaria knowledge, perceptions, and care-seeking behaviours across different ecological zones, emphasizing the significance of considering local context [13].

Antenatal care (ANC) is recognized as a crucial entry point for delivering malaria preventive interventions to pregnant women. Key strategies implemented during ANC visits include the distribution and promotion of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and the administration of intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) using sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) [14]. These interventions have been demonstrated to significantly reduce the incidence of malaria infections and associated complications during pregnancy [15]. However, the success of these prevention measures is heavily dependent on pregnant women's knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) regarding malaria prevention and control. Studies have shown that inadequate knowledge and negative attitudes towards malaria prevention can lead to poor uptake and inconsistent use of ITNs and IPTp, thereby limiting the effectiveness of control programmes [16, 17] Socioeconomic factors, cultural beliefs, and health system constraints further influence these KAP dimensions, particularly in rural communities such as Sekyere South Municipality [18, 19].

Despite progress at the national level, studies highlight the Ashanti region, among other areas, with a high burden of malaria in pregnancy in nearby districts with similar demographic characteristics [11, 12, 20], emphasizing that a study be conducted to assess the knowledge, attitudes and prevention of malaria among pregnant mothers in Sekyere South. Furthermore, publicly accessible district records, such as the Composite Budget, outline the malaria burden and efforts to combat it, but lack information on KAP among pregnant women [21]. Understanding the KAP of pregnant mothers is crucial for designing effective behaviour-change and service-delivery strategies, such as promoting ITN use and optimal IPTp uptake, aligned with local conditions in the Sekyere South Municipality.

Methods

Study design

A facility-based cross-sectional design was used to capture a district-wide snapshot of pregnant women’s KAP and their related factors across multiple ANC clinics from April to May 2025. This provides timely, resource-efficient estimates to inform programme decisions in Sekyere South Municipality. This study design was selected to gather data at a single point in time, providing a snapshot of pregnant women’s knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding malaria prevention. This method enabled the evaluation of prevalence, distribution, and relationships without the need for long-term tracking [22].

Study setting

The study was conducted in the Sekyere South Municipality, located in the Ashanti Region of Ghana. According to the 2021 Population and Housing Census [23], the municipality has an estimated population of approximately 114,720 people, with a fairly even distribution between males and females. Sekyere South Municipality, spanning approximately 1,460 km2, is mainly rural and agriculturally based, with key towns such as Agona and Effiduase serving as social and commercial hubs. Healthcare facilities are primarily concentrated in larger cities, which limits access for residents in remote areas. The area’s tropical climate and frequent rains contribute to persistent malaria, making it a relevant setting to study pregnant women’s knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding malaria prevention and control.

Study population

The study population included pregnant women attending ANC services at selected facilities in Sekyere South Municipality. All willing pregnant women, regardless of gestational age, who had participated in at least one ANC visit were eligible, except those critically ill or unable to communicate. This group was considered representative due to its high attendance rates at ANC meetings in the municipality.

Sampling technique

A convenience sampling technique with consecutive enrolment was used [26]. On non-consecutive, pre-scheduled clinic days selected to encompass various weekdays and morning/afternoon sessions at each participating facility, research personnel approached all eligible attendees sequentially after registration, providing study information and inviting their consent. Facilities were selected in consultation with the Municipal Health Directorate to ensure geographic distribution across sub-districts and to include both high- and moderate-volume ANC sites. Instances of non-response, including declinations, ineligibility, and no-shows during the sessions, were documented. To mitigate timing bias, data collection was conducted across multiple facilities, on varied days, and included both morning/afternoon sessions. Comparisons were done for key outcomes by session block, specifically early versus late in the week, and morning versus afternoon, to assess any potential imbalance. No weighting was applied, given the non-probability design.

Data collection

The main data collection tool in this research was a structured questionnaire derived from validated questionnaires [27] and questions found in similar published studies [11, 28, 29]. The questionnaire covered socio-demographic details, knowledge of malaria, attitudes towards prevention, and self-reported practices. The researchers conducted face-to-face interviews in either the local language (Twi) or English to ensure clarity and accurate responses. The questionnaire was pre-tested on 10 randomly selected pregnant women at Effiduase Government Hospital, and their feedback was used to revise the questionnaire before the actual data collection.

Data collection procedure

The researchers themselves conducted data collection by administering structured questionnaires to eligible pregnant women during ANC visits at selected facilities, ensuring privacy and informed consent. Data were collected from April to May 2025, with daily checks for completeness and strict adherence to ethical standards. All information presented in the tables and figures of this paper is based on primary field data obtained during the survey. Data analysis

Data were entered into SPSS version 26 for cleaning and analysis. Descriptive statistics summarized participants’ socio-demographic characteristics and their knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding malaria prevention.

Measurement of key variables

Knowledge index included questions on malaria transmission, symptoms, prevention, and intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp). Correct answers scored 1, while incorrect or “don’t know" answers scored 0. These scores were totaled and expressed as a percentage (0–100%). An adequate knowledge level was defined as a score of 80% or higher, following Bloom’s cut-off; scores below 80% were considered inadequate [30].

Attitude scale: Attitudes were assessed using Likert-scale items (1–5) covering perceived susceptibility and severity, response efficacy, self-efficacy, and social norms. Negatively worded items were reverse-coded. Item means were summed and rescaled to a 0–100% scale. A positive attitude was defined as a score ≥ 80% [5, 31].

Practice index: The practice index included items reflecting guideline-concordant malaria prevention behaviours, such as sleeping under an insecticide-treated net (ITN) the previous night, ownership of a usable ITN, receipt of IPTp-SP at the last antenatal care (ANC) visit, adherence to IPTp dosing schedules according to gestational age, and prompt care-seeking for fever. Appropriate behaviours were coded as 1, and all others as 0. Scores were summed and rescaled to a range of 0–100%. Good practices were defined as scores of 80% or higher. IPTp on-schedule was defined as receiving ≥ 3 doses if gestational age (GA) was ≥ 28 weeks, or all doses due at approximately monthly intervals since quickening if GA was < 28 weeks, in line with national guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Chi-square tests were performed to assess associations between key variables, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Binary logistic regression was performed separately for knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and corresponding p-values. The regression models were not subjected to formal model fit tests such as the Hosmer–Lemeshow test or pseudo-R2. All findings were presented in tables for clarity.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included pregnant women of any gestational age attending antenatal care in the selected health facilities in Sekyere South Municipality, provided they had attended at least one ANC visit, were able to participate, and gave written informed consent. Pregnant women were excluded if they were critically ill, unable to communicate, declined consent, or were not permanent residents of the municipality. Non-permanent residents were excluded because their malaria exposure and ANC attendance patterns may differ from permanent residents, and their inclusion could have introduced inconsistencies or risk of double-counting across facilities.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the local ethical committee of the Department of Nursing, Withrow University College, 10th March 2025. Permission was also secured from each facility before data collection commenced. Written Informed consent was sought from all respondents, with the study’s purpose and significance clearly explained and confidentiality assured. Participation was entirely voluntary, and only those who consented were included. Confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing data with participant codes and storing all electronic files on password-protected devices accessible only to the research team.

Knowledge among pregnant women regarding malaria prevention and control

Most participants showed good knowledge of malaria, with high awareness that it is mosquito-borne (82.0%), dangerous during pregnancy (87.2%), and preventable through ITNs (86.0%). Awareness of IPTp was also notable (75.1%), though fewer than half (48.1%) knew the recommended minimum of three doses. While 77.0% recognized that ITN use offers protection, more than half (56.2%) mistakenly believed malaria could be transmitted person-to-person. Fever (91.0%) and headache (79.1%) were the most recognized symptoms, followed by vomiting, diarrhea, and cough. These findings indicate generally adequate knowledge but reveal ongoing misconceptions and gaps in dosing knowledge, emphasizing the need for targeted education during antenatal care (Table 2). Relationships between selected demographic and knowledge variables and malaria prevention behaviours among pregnant women

Knowledge that mosquitoes cause malaria was consistently high across all education levels (81.6%–82.5%), with no significant differences (p = 0.999), reflecting nearly universal awareness likely driven by health campaigns and antenatal education. Nightly use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) was almost identical among urban (63.1%) and rural (62.9%) residents, showing no significant variation (p = 1.000). In contrast, IPTp knowledge was strongly associated with practice, as 73.9% of knowledgeable women completed three or more doses compared with only 29.7% of those without such knowledge (p < 0.001). Overall, while education and residence did not influence knowledge or ITN use, knowledge of IPTp guidelines strongly predicted adherence, underscoring the critical role of health education in malaria prevention during pregnancy (Table 3). Attitudes toward malaria prevention

Most participants showed positive attitudes toward malaria prevention during pregnancy, with 90% agreeing or strongly agreeing that it is vital for maternal health. Likewise, most believed that sleeping under an ITN (80%) and taking IPTp medications (85%) are safe during pregnancy. Confidence in health workers’ advice was high, with 88% agreeing or strongly agreeing. Perceived inconvenience of nightly ITN use was relatively low, with 55% disagreeing or strongly disagreeing. Cultural or family beliefs were not a major obstacle for most respondents, as 60% disagreed or strongly disagreed that such factors prevented them from using malaria prevention methods. These results indicate generally positive attitudes toward malaria prevention, although a small proportion still find ITN use inconvenient (Table 4). Prevention practices regarding malaria

Most participants (63.0%) reported sleeping under an ITN every night, while 15.0% used it occasionally and 22.0% did not use it at all. The main reasons for non-use were discomfort/heat (51%), unavailability (40%), and disbelief in ITN effectiveness (30%). ANC attendance was relatively good, with 41.0% attending 3–4 times, 19.0% more than four times, and 40.0% only 1–2 times. IPTp coverage was moderate—70.0% received it, but only 51.0% completed the recommended three or more doses. Most women (78.0%) sought treatment promptly when symptomatic, mainly at health facilities (61.0%), followed by pharmacies/chemists (18.0%), self-medication (14.0%), and traditional healers (5.0%). These findings indicate generally good preventive practices but highlight the need to improve consistent ITN use, full IPTp adherence, and early ANC attendance (Table 5). Adjusted odds ratios for predictors of completing three or more antenatal care visits among pregnant women

Table 6 shows that none of the examined factors were statistically significant predictors of completing at least three ANC visits. The multivariable analysis indicated that none of the factors examined were significantly linked to the outcome at the 5% significance level. Variables such as age, education level (secondary or tertiary), urban residence, and awareness that mosquitoes transmit malaria all had p-values exceeding 0.05, reflecting no significant statistical effect. Urban residence came close to significance (p = 0.087), hinting at a potential trend of higher odds of the outcome among city residents. Likewise, knowing the correct IPTp dose was not significantly associated (p = 0.480). These results imply that sociodemographic and knowledge-based factors alone may not predict the outcome in this context. Therefore, programmes aiming to enhance malaria prevention might need to focus on other factors—behavioural, cultural, or related to the health system—that influence outcomes beyond individual traits and basic knowledge. The marginal significance of urban residence suggests that further research is needed, as access and resource availability may play a role in prevention practices (Table 6). Discussion

This study advances understanding of malaria prevention in pregnancy by highlighting not only coverage levels but also the quality and consistency of delivery within ANC services. It shows how stock-outs, missed IPTp doses, weak ITN distribution, and inadequate counselling undermine effectiveness. Addressing implementation fidelity is essential to ensure interventions are delivered correctly and consistently, translating proven policies into real maternal and neonatal health gains in Ghana and similar settings.

Knowledge of malaria prevention and control

About four out of five women knew that mosquitoes transmit malaria, and a similar proportion recognized the dangers of malaria during pregnancy. This high level of basic knowledge is encouraging and aligns with studies from other parts of Ghana and rural sub-Saharan Africa [32, 33], where ongoing public health campaigns and ANC education have increased general awareness of malaria among pregnant women. The results showed that women with little or no formal education had a high awareness of how mosquitoes transmit malaria. This is probably because ongoing national malaria control programmes in Ghana—like NMCP outreach, radio campaigns, and education during antenatal care—have made understanding malaria causes almost universal. This highlights that persistent, multi-channel public health messages can successfully bridge education-related knowledge gaps.

The belief that malaria spreads directly between people shows that misconceptions persist despite increased awareness. For example, more than half of the respondents believed malaria can be transmitted person-to-person. This is consistent with findings by Alhassan [34], who observed that such misconceptions are common even where general malaria knowledge is high. This confusion might partly occur because people associate it with illnesses like influenza or typhoid, which do spread through contact. It may also originate from informal stories shared within families and communities. To effectively address these misunderstandings, more engaging health education strategies—such as community dialogues or visual demonstrations—may be necessary, providing safe spaces for openly discussing and correcting myths.

Across all education levels, knowledge that mosquitoes cause malaria was consistently high: No formal education (81.6%), Primary (82.2%), Secondary (81.7%), and Tertiary (82.5%) correctly identified mosquitoes as the cause. There was no significant association between education and knowledge (p = 0.999), indicating widespread awareness likely due to universal health messaging. Although most women identified fever and headache as symptoms, fewer than half recognized common signs, such as vomiting and diarrhoea. This partial awareness mirrors findings from other studies and may lead to delays in seeking care or incomplete symptom reporting during ANC visits. Therefore, enhancing knowledge of the full range of malaria symptoms is crucial for timely diagnosis and effective treatment.

Regarding IPTp, about three-quarters of women had heard of this intervention, and roughly half knew that at least three doses are recommended in pregnancy. These findings compare favorably with older studies in which awareness and understanding of IPTp were much lower [35], suggesting real progress. Although awareness of IPTp is widespread, there are gaps in understanding the three-dose requirement. This underscores the importance of clear, repeated communication during antenatal care (ANC). To expand prevention efforts in sub-Saharan Africa, it is crucial to improve supply chains, reduce stockouts, encourage earlier ANC visits, and provide culturally sensitive counseling.

The majority of women expressed positive attitudes toward malaria prevention, with most strongly agreeing that protecting themselves and their unborn babies from malaria was important. This finding matches results with Dwumfour et al. [20] in sub-Saharan Africa, where positive health attitudes have been linked to greater compliance with recommended malaria prevention practices. Most women trusted the safety and effectiveness of ITNs and IPTp medications, and nearly all trusted the advice given by health workers. This high level of trust is also found in a study by Bonsra et al. [36] emphasizing the vital role of health professionals as credible sources of information. However, a small but notable fraction found ITN use inconvenient, primarily due to discomfort from heat—a practical barrier repeatedly highlighted in the literature [37]. Although attitudes toward malaria prevention are mainly positive, heat-related discomfort from ITN use remains a barrier. Overcoming this may require not just behavioural interventions but also innovations in net design or improvements in household ventilation.

Cultural and family beliefs were cited as barriers by about one in five women, a proportion similar to that reported by [37]. This indicates that while modern health messages are widely accepted, traditional beliefs continue to influence malaria prevention decisions in some households. Family and cultural influences show that malaria prevention decisions are shaped by household and community dynamics, not just individual choice. Engaging influential relatives, traditional leaders, and faith-based groups in health messaging can help align cultural norms with biomedical guidance.

Roughly two-thirds of respondents reported sleeping under an ITN every night, while about one in five did not use them at all. This is similar to coverage levels reported in recent Ghanaian surveys [38] and in Kenya and Nigeria [39]. Discomfort and unavailability were the main reasons for not using ITNs, indicating ongoing logistical and practical barriers to universal coverage. Use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) every night was similar between urban residents (63.1%) and rural residents (62.9%). The difference was not statistically significant (p = 1.000), suggesting comparable levels of ITN use regardless of place of residence. However, persistent issues with comfort and access remain. Enhancing the accessibility and comfort of ITNs could increase consistent use and help reduce malaria incidence among pregnant women.

Among women who knew that at least three doses of intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) are recommended, 73.9% reported actually receiving three or more doses. In contrast, only 29.7% of those without such knowledge completed three or more doses. This difference was highly significant (p < 0.001), showing that knowledge strongly influences practice. This finding suggests that, while national and district health campaigns are having an effect, there is still room for improvement—particularly as IPTp coverage in some other regions of Ghana and sub-Saharan Africa remains lower [40, 41]. Differences in IPTp uptake may be due to variations in ANC attendance, stockouts, or differences in provider training and counselling. Enhancing the supply chain through consumption-based forecasting, buffer stock maintenance, prompt restocking, and intra-district redistribution, supported by provider prompts will minimize missed IPTp doses.

Prompt treatment-seeking behaviour was reported by the majority of women, with health facilities being the preferred source of care. This is encouraging and in line with the Ghana Health Service (2024) report, which notes improvements in facility-based care-seeking [42]. Such behaviour is crucial for early detection and management of malaria, reducing complications for both mothers and babies.

Despite high knowledge and positive attitudes, logistic regression analysis showed that maternal age, education, residence, knowledge of malaria cause, and knowledge of IPTp dosing were not significantly associated with completing three or more ANC visits. Urban residence showed a non-significant trend toward higher attendance (OR = 1.41, 95% CI 0.95–2.09, p = 0.087). This finding diverges from previous research in which education and urban living predicted higher ANC utilization [43–45], suggesting that other, unmeasured factors—such as economic challenges or health system barriers—may now be more relevant determinants of ANC attendance in this setting. Possible explanations include reduced socioeconomic disparities from fee exemptions and NHIS coverage, differences in how “adequate ANC” was measured, mediation of education effects through counselling and media exposure, limited variation in education levels among participants, and the facility-based sample under-representing non-attenders where education gradients are typically strongest.

The research findings align with Ghana’s malaria control strategies and global elimination goals. The IPTp schedule gap, despite adequate ANC attendance, threatens IPTp3 + targets in national policy and WHO guidelines, highlighting service delivery weaknesses such as stock management, provider prompts, and missed-opportunity audits [46, 47]. Similarly, the gap between ITN ownership and nightly use undermines vector control objectives, warranting Social and Behavioural Change (SBC) measures, such as ANC/CHPS demonstrations, net replacement, and comfort tips. The persistence of person-to-person transmission myths underscores the need for targeted health education, while travel-time barriers emphasize equity concerns central to SDG 3, supporting mobile ANC/CHPS outreach and on-site ITN distribution in remote areas.

In summary, this analysis highlights behavioural and service-process gaps—especially ITN non-use, transmission myths, and missed IPTp doses—as key barriers. Counselling, access, and self-efficacy offer the most actionable pathways to improve implementation fidelity toward Ghana’s malaria prevention targets.

Limitation of the study

This cross-sectional study cannot establish causality, and reliance on self-reported data may have introduced recall and social desirability bias. Conducted in a single district, the findings may not be generalizable to other settings. The structured questionnaire may also have missed deeper socio-cultural factors, and non-response bias remains possible.

This study used a facility-based convenience sample of ANC attendees, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability to pregnant women who attend ANC during the study window; women who did not attend were not sampled. Although varied facilities and clinic times are used to improve coverage, residual bias may remain. Additionally, data were collected in April–May, and KAP may vary seasonally. Also, cross-sectional design, reliance on self-reported data, and focus on one municipality limit the ability to establish causality and generalize findings.

Conclusion

This district-level study suggests that malaria prevention during pregnancy is hindered more by gaps in implementation fidelity than by access to commodities—particularly persistent misconceptions, delays in IPTp administration, and the disparity between ITN ownership and consistent use. Notably, 56% of participants believed malaria could be transmitted person-to-person, while despite high ITN ownership and adequate ANC attendance, on-schedule IPTp coverage was only 51% and nightly ITN use 63%. These findings underscore the need for targeted community-level interventions in Sekyere South to address behavioural barriers and strengthen service delivery. Importantly, these results align with the objectives of Ghana’s National Malaria Strategic Plan (2021–2025) and the Malaria Elimination Strategic Plan (2024–2028), which emphasize improving IPTp-SP coverage, scaling up ITN utilization, and intensifying sub-national strategies. It is recommended that ANC-based quality improvement be strengthened through stock monitoring, “SP due today?” prompts, and audits, alongside mobile ANC/CHPS services with timely ITN replacement in remote areas. Health education should integrate myth-correction with skills-oriented counselling during ANC visits and use culturally tailored messages to address misconceptions. Community and partner engagement through local campaigns and male involvement can further sustain ITN use and support ANC attendance. Future research should employ a longitudinal mixed-methods study to assess how partner support influences ANC attendance, IPTp timeliness, and ITN use.
