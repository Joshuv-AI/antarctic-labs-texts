Cardiac Injury Causing Traumatic Ventricular Septal Rupture With Right Ventricular Pseudoaneurysm
Authors: Mahima Tyagi, Matthew Shotwell, Amelia E Power, Gurnoor Singh, Dinesh K Kalra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVentricular septal rupture (VSR) is a rare, potentially fatal consequence of blunt cardiac injury (BCI). Concomitant right ventricular (RV) pseudoaneurysm formation is even rarer, and the occurrence of both complications has not to our knowledge been previously reported.Case SummaryA 63-year-old man presented with a VSR and a torn tricuspid chord, flail leaflet, and severe tricuspid regurgitation after BCI due to a motor vehicle accident. He declined surgery initially and presented a month later with severe heart failure symptoms. Imaging at that time demonstrated a persistent VSR and a new RV pseudoaneurysm. His condition was not deemed to be amenable to percutaneous closure, and he again declined open surgical repair.DiscussionVSR after BCI results from acute mechanical forces and/or delayed necrosis, with RV pseudoaneurysm developing as a delayed complication likely due to inflammatory necrosis. Multimodality imaging provides comprehensive anatomical assessment and tissue characterization and guides accurate diagnosis, prognostication, and therapeutic planning.Take-Home MessageThis case emphasizes the importance of early recognition and the value of serial imaging in blunt cardiac trauma, with surgical repair recommended for significant defects and management tailored to the anatomy, timing of complications, and patient preferences.Key words: blunt cardiac injury, echocardiography, right ventricular pseudoaneurysm, tricuspid regurgitation, ventricular septal rupture

History of Presentation

A 63-year-old man was brought to the emergency department after a motor vehicle accident in which he was driving his car while wearing a seatbelt and collided at moderate speed with a tree owing to alcohol intoxication. Because of hypotension at the scene, emergency medical services administered intravenous fluids and transported him to our trauma center. On arrival at the emergency department, he reported substernal chest discomfort. Initial evaluation demonstrated a blood pressure of 90/50 mm Hg, heart rate of 106 beats/min, and normal oxygen saturation. He showed signs of alcohol intoxication and altered mental status, and multiple anterior chest wall abrasions and ecchymoses were found. A harsh holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border and a crisp mechanical A2 valve click were heard. There was no edema, and the rest of the examination was unremarkable.

Past Medical History

The patient had undergone a St Jude's bileaflet tilting disc mechanical aortic valve (AV) replacement with aortic conduit for a congenital bicuspid AV 15 years ago and was chronically anticoagulated with warfarin. Additional comorbidities included hypertension, dyslipidemia, alcohol use disorder (2 beers daily for 5 years), and a 55 pack-year history of cigarette smoking.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis consisted of myocardial contusion/injury including intramural hematoma, chordae tendineae injury/rupture, valve injury, cardiac trauma/rupture including free wall and/or papillary muscle rupture, pericardial injury or hematoma or tamponade, coronary injury including dissection or compression and ischemia/infarction, and acute aortic injury.

Investigations

Initial laboratory evaluation was unremarkable except for a supratherapeutic international normalized ratio (4.5) and an elevated high-sensitivity troponin I, which peaked at 46,467 ng/L. Toxicology was positive for a serum ethanol level of 231 mg/dL. An electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia with intraventricular conduction delay, without any ischemic changes. Chest x-ray confirmed prior sternotomy and AV replacement, without acute findings. Focused assessment with sonography for trauma was negative, and pelvic x-ray revealed no fractures. Non–cardiac gated chest computed tomography showed right third and fourth rib fractures and a possible interventricular septal defect. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) (Video 1) followed by transesophageal echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 60%, apical septal hypokinesis, and a 2.9 × 1.8 cm apical ventricular septal defect/rupture (VSR) with a shunt across the interventricular septum. The pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio was 1.24, and a 0.9 × 0.4 cm mobile echodensity was observed on the right atrial side of the tricuspid valve (TV), consistent with torn tricuspid chordae tendineae with flail anterior leaflet of the TV and severe tricuspid regurgitation. Cardiac catheterization was performed, and no evidence of occlusive coronary artery disease was found (Video 2).

Management

A multidisciplinary heart team reviewed the case and discussed percutaneous and surgical repair options, along with their associated risks and benefits, to guide the patient in making an informed decision. However, the patient declined intervention owing to procedural risks. He was discharged on oral furosemide, carvedilol, lisinopril, and warfarin, with follow-up in the structural heart clinic.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Five weeks later, the patient returned to the emergency department with worsening exertional dyspnea, bilateral lower extremity edema, and fatigue. Examination revealed a loud holosystolic murmur at the apex, diminished breath sounds over the right lung field, and 2+ edema extending to the buttocks.

The patient's serum B-type natriuretic peptide was 1,083 pg/mL and his high-sensitivity troponin I was 44 ng/L. Repeat TTE showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 55% and redemonstrated the complications of blunt cardiac injury (BCI) seen previously—namely, a 3.1 × 2 cm VSR with turbulent left-to-right shunting at the apex, severe tricuspid regurgitation, and a 0.8 × 0.3 cm anterior tricuspid leaflet flail (Figure 1, Video 3). Transesophageal echocardiography identified a 3 × 1.8 cm muscular VSR in the apical inferoseptum, with bidirectional but predominantly left-to-right shunting and a pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio of 1.53 (Figure 2, Video 4). Severe tricuspid regurgitation was observed, with 3 regurgitant jets secondary to a partially flail anterior leaflet and 0.9 × 0.3 cm mobile echogenic density consistent with torn chordae (Video 5). Retrospectively gated cardiac computed tomography angiography (CCTA) confirmed a serpiginous VSR with irregular margins extending from the mid inferoseptum to the apical septum, measuring 3.2 × 2 cm during the systolic phase. A poorly visualized 0.8 × 0.4 cm mobile mass was noted attached to the superior/atrial aspect of the anterior leaflet of the TV. Additionally, a new complication was noted: a right ventricular (RV) pseudoaneurysm just below the RV outflow tract, measuring 3.1 × 1.9 cm with a 1.3-cm neck (Figure 3, Video 6). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) was attempted to better characterize the extent of the biventricular myocardial injury (late gadolinium enhancement and myocardial edema), RV pseudoaneurysm, and shunt, but could not be completed owing to orthopnea (Figure 4, Video 7). According to the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma cardiac injury scale, developed to categorize both blunt and penetrating cardiac injuries (Table 1), he was classified as having grade IV injury.

The patient was started on medical therapy for acute decompensated heart failure, which included a furosemide intravenous drip and inotropic support with intravenous dobutamine. Percutaneous VSR closure was not an option owing to the apical, irregular, and serpiginous anatomy, nor would it be able to address the new complication of the RV free wall pseudoaneurysm. A multidisciplinary heart team was again consulted for shared decision-making about possible surgical repair, but the patient continued to refuse surgery. He was subsequently discharged to hospice care and unfortunately died 1 week later.

Discussion

BCI refers to a spectrum of injuries to cardiac and pericardiac structures after blunt chest trauma, with symptoms ranging from asymptomatic myocardial contusion to cardiac rupture. The true incidence of BCI is unknown, with literature reports ranging from 8% to 71% after chest trauma.1 Between 2017 and 2021 in the United States, the National Trauma Data Bank reported 14,219 cases of BCI among 4.8 million patients who experienced blunt trauma, accounting for a 0.3% incidence rate.2 Because many patients with mild chest wall trauma do not seek medical attention or get systematic cardiac evaluation with biomarkers and advanced imaging, it is likely that cases of mild cardiac injury are subclinical and missed with spontaneous recovery in the ensuing weeks. However, serious chest wall injuries that present to the emergency department are often evaluated with troponin, an electrocardiogram, and TTE and thus can show serious cardiac injury or complications when present.

Cardiac complications after BCI include cardiac contusions, myocardial rupture, arrhythmias, commotio cordis, myocardial infarction, and injuries occurring to the septum, valves, coronary arteries, and aorta. Sinus tachycardia is the most common electrocardiographic finding, with arrhythmias occurring in 1% to 6% of cases.3 Commotio cordis is the third leading cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes, after hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and congenital coronary anomalies.4 It is triggered by a blunt, nonpenetrating impact to the chest trauma during the RR interval phase of the cardiac cycle (upslope of T-wave), resulting in ventricular fibrillation. Cardiac chamber rupture is the leading cause of death after BCI, occurring in 64% of fatal cases.5 Valvular injuries occur in 0.5% to 1% of cases, primarily affecting the AV, followed by the mitral and TV; however, this pattern remains controversial, with one study reporting a higher rate of TV (12-fold) and AV (3-fold) injury and regurgitation, and other studies reporting that AV injury is the most common valvular injury after BCI.5 Septal injuries, such as VSR, are very rare, occurring in 0.01% of cases and resulting from high-energy deceleration injuries such as motor vehicle accidents.6 The incidence of RV pseudoaneurysms has not been systematically reported in the literature and is probably even rarer.

Two principal mechanisms classically cause myocardial rupture after nonpenetrating or blunt chest wall trauma (eg, receiving a blow to the chest wall or being hit with a baseball or baseball bat) (Figure 5): 1) an acute mechanical rupture occurring within minutes to hours of trauma due to direct compressive injury, and 2) delayed inflammatory rupture that develops over days after the acute injury due to an evolution of inflammatory injury and/or expansion of hematoma.

Pathophysiology

Blunt cardiac injury from the trauma of a motor vehicle accident or a baseball impact causes immediate injury that may lead to a contusion and myocardial rupture. In our patient, a ventricular septal rupture was followed by a delayed inflammatory complication, days to weeks later, that resulted in the development of a right ventricular (RV) pseudoaneurysm. IVS = interventricular septum.

During rapid deceleration, the interventricular septum is particularly vulnerable to VSR from compression between the sternum and vertebral column, especially at end-diastole, when myocardial wall tension is at its peak. This is exacerbated by the septum's thin apical musculature, lower vascularity, and longitudinal fiber orientation, which collectively reduce torsional resilience and increase susceptibility to shearing forces. The delayed development of an RV pseudoaneurysm a few weeks later in the clinical course of our patient likely represented the delayed inflammation and weakening of the myocardium due to multiple mechanisms, which led to transmural necrosis of the thinner RV wall. It involves the enzymatic breakdown of necrotic myocytes, interstitial hemorrhage with microvascular disruption, and progressive coagulative necrosis. Immune cell infiltration initiates a cytokine-mediated signaling cascade, involving tumor necrosis factor alpha and interleukins, and upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2 and MMP-9).7,8 Traumatic coronary artery injuries, such as dissection or thrombosis of the right coronary artery or its acute marginal branches, can potentially impair regional perfusion of the RV, predisposing it to necrosis and structural failure. However, we did not observe any gross stenosis or hematoma or luminal compromise of the right coronary artery at the time that the patient underwent the CCTA on day 56, although transient ischemia, spasm, distal embolization, or resolved hematoma around the right coronary artery or its acute marginal branch may have occurred acutely and not been detected on the electrocardiogram or cardiac catheterization. Pericardial thickening from his prior pericardiotomy at the time of AV replacement 15 years ago likely contributed to the containment of the pseudoaneurysm without frank rupture into the mediastinum.

In this patient, the VSR appeared early, as indicated by the chest computed tomography scan, loud murmur, and TTE showing shunting soon after trauma. Notably, he had prior echocardiograms demonstrating no septal abnormalities, and CCTA did not reveal any significant coronary artery stenosis or abnormalities in the left anterior descending artery or its septal branches that could have contributed to the septal weakening and VSR. TV involvement likely reflects its anterior location, closer to the sternum, and the relatively unsupported structure of the tricuspid apparatus, making it particularly vulnerable to sudden chest wall impact.

Multimodality imaging was pivotal in the initial diagnosis and subsequent anatomic characterization. TTE first identified the VSR, but acoustic window limitations and reduced spatial resolution in the setting of chest wall trauma hindered precise evaluation. Transesophageal echocardiography improved the delineation of the defect's oblique trajectory and size. However, retrospectively gated CCTA provided definitive functional anatomic detail, revealing the full extent of the VSR and identifying the RV pseudoaneurysm (Figure 3). CCTA was especially useful in evaluating the morphology, location, and neck dimensions of the pseudoaneurysm while simultaneously excluding additional structural injuries.

Although our patient did not tolerate a full CMR study, it would have offered valuable tissue characterization. CMR provides precise quantification of ventricular volumes and myocardial function, evaluates myocardial edema via T2-weighted imaging, assesses fibrosis/scar by late gadolinium enhancement, and helps distinguish stunned, viable myocardium from infarcted segments, aiding in prognostication and operative planning. The combination of structural and tissue characterization imaging offers a comprehensive understanding of BCI that echocardiography and CCTA cannot achieve.

Management of traumatic VSR is dictated by hemodynamic severity, anatomic suitability, and patient preference. Small defects may spontaneously close, but larger or symptomatic VSRs necessitate surgical repair. Percutaneous closure is not feasible in irregular, tortuous, or apically located defects. Surgical intervention is often the ideal choice for comprehensive repair, however it is complicated by friable tissue, ongoing inflammation, and the complexity of repairing multiple concomitant complications. Our patient declined surgical therapy on 2 separate occasions, ultimately transitioning to hospice; the family declined an autopsy.

Conclusions

VSR, TV injury, and pseudoaneurysms are rare but serious complications of BCI that may dynamically evolve. This case highlights the critical role of multimodality imaging for initial diagnosis, anatomical assessment, and ongoing surveillance (Figure 6). Delayed sequelae, such as the development of an RV pseudoaneurysm, may not be apparent on initial imaging and demonstrate the importance of serial re-evaluation. A comprehensive, longitudinal imaging strategy is essential for guiding timely intervention, evaluating structural disease progression, and adapting management as post-traumatic myocardial injuries evolve.
