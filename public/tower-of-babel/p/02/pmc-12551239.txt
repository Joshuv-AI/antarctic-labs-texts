Exploring the pathophysiological relationship between bisphenol A exposure and ischemic stroke risk using network toxicology and machine learning
Authors: Yongfeng Li, Xiuyang Chen, Peng Zhong, Yiming Xing, Peng Miao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the molecular mechanisms by which bisphenol A (BPA) exposure contributes to ischemic stroke (IS) risk via the induction of cellular senescence, using an integrative approach combining network toxicology, transcriptomics, and machine learning.MethodsBPA’s chemical properties and target genes were retrieved from PubChem, ChEMBL, STITCH, and SwissTargetPrediction databases, with toxicity validated via ProTox-3. Ischemic stroke (IS)-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified from merged GEO datasets (GSE16561, GSE37857) and validated in GSE58294. Cellular senescence-related genes (CSRGs) were obtained from CellAge. The intersection of BPA target genes, IS-DEGs, and CSRGs (BPA-CSRG-IS intergenes) was analyzed using protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks and machine learning algorithms (random forest, LASSO) to screen hub genes, with SHAP analysis applied to evaluate the optimal model. A risk prediction model was constructed via logistic regression and validated externally. Additionally, immune cell infiltration analysis, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), miRNA-TF-mRNA regulatory network construction, and molecular docking were performed to explore underlying mechanisms.ResultsBPA was confirmed to cross the blood-brain barrier and interact with 2,985 target genes. Merged IS datasets identified 708 DEGs, enriched in immune-related pathways. Intersection analysis yielded 17 BPA-CSRG-IS intergenes, with 2 hub genes (MMP9, HDAC1) identified via machine learning (KNN model, AUC = 0.940) and SHAP analysis (MMP9 as the top contributor). The risk model based on MMP9 and HDAC1 showed robust performance (training set AUC = 0.830; validation set AUC = 0.946). IS patients exhibited elevated immune cell infiltration (e.g., CD8 + T cells, M0 macrophages), with MMP9 and HDAC1 strongly correlated with immune cell subsets. ssGSEA revealed hub genes regulate pathways related to neuroinflammation, BBB disruption, and cellular senescence. Molecular docking confirmed strong binding affinity between BPA and MMP9 (–7.2 kcal/mol) or HDAC1 (–7.7 kcal/mol).ConclusionThis study identifies MMP9 and HDAC1 as key hub genes mediating BPA-induced IS via cellular senescence, highlighting their roles in regulating the “senescence-inflammation” cascade and immune cell infiltration. These findings provide mechanistic insights into BPA’s neurotoxicity and potential biomarkers/targets for IS prevention and treatment.Keywords: Bisphenol A, Ischemic stroke, Cellular senescence, Machine learning, Molecular docking

Introduction

Bisphenol A (BPA), one of the most widely produced industrial chemicals globally, is ubiquitously used in plastic packaging, food containers, and medical devices, rendering its environmental exposure a critical public health concern [1]. Substantial epidemiological evidence links BPA exposure to heightened risks of metabolic disorders [2], neurodegenerative diseases [3], and cardiovascular pathologies [4]. Of particular relevance, BPA acts as a potent endocrine disruptor, inducing cellular senescence through mechanisms such as estrogen receptor mimicry or oxidative stress pathway perturbation [5, 6].

Cellular senescence—a state of irreversible cell cycle arrest—is characterized by the secretion of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), comprising proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [7, 8]. These factors collectively exacerbate chronic inflammation and oxidative damage in tissue microenvironments, contributing to age-related pathologies [9].

Ischemic stroke, a leading cause of global disability and mortality, involves intricate pathological processes including blood-brain barrier (BBB) breakdown, neuronal apoptosis, and neuroinflammation [10, 11]. Emerging evidence highlights cellular senescence as a pivotal driver of stroke pathogenesis [12, 13]. For example, senescent glial cells—including astrocytes and microglia—exacerbate post-ischemic reperfusion injury by releasing SASP factors that amplify neuroinflammation and compromise BBB integrity [14]. Furthermore, accelerated biological aging, as measured by the PhenoAge biomarker derived from blood profiles, correlates strongly with adverse short- and long-term outcomes in ischemic stroke [15]. Despite these associations, whether BPA promotes ischemic stroke via induction of cellular senescence—along with the underlying molecular mechanisms and key signaling cascades—remains poorly understood.

Existing research demonstrates that BPA induces cellular senescence through multiple pathways, such as activating the p53/p21-dependent DNA damage response to enforce cell cycle arrest or disrupting mitochondrial dynamics to elevate reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and oxidative stress [16]. In the nervous system, BPA exposure disrupts neurotransmitter homeostasis, impairing dopaminergic and neuron function and inducing neurotoxicity [3, 17, 18]. Notably, two genes have emerged in recent literature as critical intermediaries in these pathways: Matrix Metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) and Histone Deacetylase 1 (HDAC1). MMP9, a key SASP component, is upregulated by BPA-induced oxidative stress—for instance, BPA-mediated ROS production in endothelial cells was shown to increase MMP9 expression, driving senescence and vascular dysfunction [6]. MMP9 also directly degrades BBB extracellular matrix proteins, linking senescence to stroke-relevant vascular damage [19]. HDAC1, by contrast, acts as an epigenetic brake on the p53/p21 senescence pathway: HDAC1-mediated deacetylation suppresses p53 transcriptional activity, and BPA-induced HDAC1 downregulation (via ubiquitination or direct binding) has been shown to enhance p53 acetylation, triggering senescence in renal and neuronal cells [20, 21]. Studies have shown that exposure to BPA can damage various brain structures, induce molecular changes, promote oxidative stress, disrupt neurotransmitter homeostasis, enhance neurotoxicity and neuroinflammation, impair blood-brain barrier function, and cause neuronal damage, along with other adverse effects [21]. Such exposure also increases the risk of neurological diseases. In addition, Cai et al. observed an association between urinary BPA levels and elevated stroke prevalence in the U.S. population, and this association was more pronounced in men [22]. However, the interaction between BPA-mediated cellular senescence and the pathophysiology of stroke—particularly the specific mechanisms driving ischemic brain injury—remains elusive.

Traditional reductionist toxicology approaches are insufficient to fully characterize BPA’s pleiotropic effects across multiple targets and signaling pathways. Given BPA’s potential to modulate cellular senescence and stroke risk through network-level interactions, single-gene or single-pathway studies fail to capture its systemic toxicological impacts. ‌Particularly‌, BPA acts as an endocrine disruptor with multi-target toxicity, while the pathological cascade linking BPA-induced cellular senescence to IS involves interconnected processes: immune cell infiltration, BBB disruption, and neuroinflammation. Traditional methods cannot integrate these disparate layers of biological information (e.g., BPA targets, IS-related gene expression changes, senescence-associated pathways) to identify core regulatory networks or resolve the “senescence-inflammation-IS” axis. Here, we employ an integrative framework combining network toxicology, transcriptomics, bioinformatics, machine learning, and molecular docking to systematically dissect the molecular network by which BPA induces ischemic stroke via cellular senescence. This study aims to identify key regulatory nodes and analyze protein-ligand interactions, providing insights into the mechanism by which BPA exposure promotes stroke susceptibility by increasing the risk of cellular senescence.

Methods

BPA compound and toxicity identification

Chemical structures and molecular properties of BPA were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Toxicity validation was performed using the ProTox-3 database (https://tox.charite.de/).

Compilation of BPA target genes

The potential target genes for BPA were retrieved from the ChEMBL database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/), STITCH (http://stitch.embl.de/), and SwissTarget Prediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) databases, with Homo sapiens as the condition. A prediction condition probability greater than 0 was set as the inclusion criterion. The results from all these databases were integrated to construct a comprehensive target dataset for BPA.

Identification of IS-associated differentially expressed genes

Analyses were performed utilizing three IS datasets sourced from the GEO database. The first dataset, GSE16561 (platform: GPL6883), comprised 24 control and 39 IS peripheral blood samples [23]. The second dataset, GSE37587 (platform: GPL6883), included 68 IS peripheral blood samples [24]. Background correction and normalization were applied to each dataset using the “limma” R package. For genes represented by multiple probes, the average value across all corresponding probes was calculated as the final expression value. The R package “SVA,” which includes the “ComBat” function, was utilized to adjust for batch effects [25]. Differences in sample distributions before and after batch effect removal were visualized through two-dimensional principal component analysis (PCA) clustering plots. For further investigation, the combined dataset functioned as the training set, while the GSE58294 dataset was designated as the validation set. Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was executed with the “limma” R package, employing a significance threshold of adjusted P-value < 0.05(Benjamini-Hochberg) and |log2 fold-change| >0.3.

Functional enrichment analysis

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were performed using the R packages clusterProfiler and org.Hs.eg.db to characterize the underlying biological pathways and molecular functions associated with the DEGs. For these analyses, an adjusted P-value < 0.05 (determined via the Benjamini-Hochberg method) was set as the threshold for statistical significance.

Genes associated with cellular senescence

The list of cellular senescence related genes (CSRGs) was derived from CellAge (https://genomics.senescence.info/cells/) [26], a curated database specializing in cellular senescence-related genes across aging and disease contexts. This resource systematically compiles manually validated human genes linked to cellular senescence, from which 278 genes were selected for subsequent analysis.

Application of SHAP machine learning in screening and validation of hub genes

First, the intersection of BPA target genes, DEGs, and CSRGs was identified using the “ggvenn” R package, which visualized and derived a set of BPA-CSRGs-IS-associated genes (intergenes) for subsequent analysis.

Subsequently, to identify potential hub genes associated with BPA-induced cellular senescence in IS, machine learning algorithms including Random Forest (RF) and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) were employed to screen intersected genes, with the intersection of results from the two algorithms selected for further verification.

For the LASSO analysis, the R package glmnet was utilized to incorporate intergenes into the model. The glmnet function was configured with an alpha value of 1 (corresponding to LASSO regularization) and family set to “binomial”. To determine the optimal regularization parameter λ, 10-fold cross-validation was performed with deviance as the evaluation metric (implemented via type.measure=’deviance’ and nfolds = 10 in the function call), and the signature genes were finally identified based on this optimal λ. For the RF algorithm, the model was constructed with an ensemble size of 500 decision trees to ensure the stability and predictive performance of the ensemble learning framework.

To enhance model accuracy and generalization, ten machine learning algorithms were applied to predict hub genes, including Logistic Regression (LR), RF, Decision Tree (DT), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), glmBoost, GBM, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Partial Least Squares (PLS), XGBoost, and Neural Network (NN). Furthermore, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis, grounded in cooperative game theory, was used to evaluate the optimal model. This analysis not only elucidated the model’s prediction mechanisms but also quantified the contribution and relative importance of each feature to the predictive outcomes, providing rigorous insights into the model’s decision-making process through this interpretable methodology.

Development and validation of a risk model

To estimate the regression coefficients of hub genes and derive individualized risk scores for each patient sample, a binary logistic regression (LR) model was constructed. To promote clinical applicability, the “rms” R package was employed to develop a nomogram integrating these key molecular markers. The nomogram’s predictive efficacy and clinical utility were systematically evaluated using calibration curve assessment, decision curve analysis (DCA), and ROC curve metrics. Moreover, external validation of the diagnostic model was performed using the independent GSE58294 dataset.

Immune cell infiltration analysis in IS patients

To investigate the composition of infiltrating immune cells, the CIBERSORT algorithm was applied to analyze gene expression profiles within the IS dataset [27]. This approach enabled quantitative estimation of the relative abundance of infiltrating immune cell subsets, thereby providing insights into their functional contributions.

Single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) of hub genes

The functional implications of hub genes were investigated via single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), implemented using the “clusterProfiler” package with the “C2.cp.kegg.v7.0.symbols.gmt” gene set as reference. To decipher the correlation between hub gene expression profiles and the composition of infiltrating immune cell populations, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed using R software.

Regulatory network of hub genes

The miRTarBase database was employed to screen for miRNAs targeting hub genes [28], while the Enrichr database was utilized to identify transcription factors (TFs) associated with these genes, using an adjusted p-value threshold of < 0.05. Following this, miRNA-TF-mRNA regulatory networks were constructed and subsequently visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.10.3), enabling systematic analysis of the regulatory interactions.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking analysis was performed using the CB-Dock2 platform [29]. PDB files of hub genes were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank, while the molecular structure of BPA was acquired from the PubChem database. Binding energy was employed as the evaluation metric to assess the interaction between ligands and receptors.

Results

Characterization of toxicological effects and identification of target genes for BPA

First, the 2D and 3D structures of BPA were obtained from the PubChem database, and its SMILES structure (CC(C)(C1 = CC = C(C = C1)O)C2 = CC = C(C = C2)O) was extracted for subsequent analysis. Subsequently, toxicity assessment via the ProTox-3 database revealed significant toxic activity of BPA across the blood-brain barrier (Fig. 1). Finally, a total of 2,985 BPA target genes were compiled from three databases: ChEMBL, STITCH, and SwissTargetPrediction.

Identification of DEGs in IS and enrichment analysis

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in ischemic stroke (IS) patients, two datasets (GSE16561 and GSE37857) were merged, yielding a total of 24 control samples and 107 IS samples (Fig. 2A). Prior to batch correction, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that samples clustered by dataset rather than by sample type, indicating that data source had a stronger influence on the results than sample type and confirming the presence of a batch effect. Following correction, samples predominantly clustered by IS or control status, suggesting that sample type became the primary source of variation (Fig. 2B). This batch effect adjustment resulted in the identification of 708 DEGs (Fig. 2C).

Functional analyses highlighted the significant enrichment of these genes in immune response-related pathways, including T cell receptor signaling, Th cell differentiation, and T cell activation, etc (Fig. 2D and E).

Identification of hub BPA-CSRG-IS-related genes via machine learning

By intersecting BPA target genes, DEGs, and CSRGs, we identified 17 BPA-CSRG-IS-related genes (intergenes) (Fig. 3A). These intergenes were then subjected to protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis using STRING database (Fig. 3B), and the PPI network was visualized via Cytoscape. Network degree values were calculated, and the top 10 genes were selected for further analysis (Fig. 3C). Using machine learning algorithms, LASSO identified 7 feature genes with an AUC value of 0.861 (Fig. 3D and E), while Random Forest (RF) yielded 5 feature genes (importance > 4) with an AUC value of 0.775 (Fig. 3F and G). The intersection of these two gene sets resulted in 5 potential hub genes (Fig. 4A).

SHAP explanatory analysis

Ten machine learning algorithms, including PLS, RF, DTS, SVM, LR, KNN, XGBoost, GBM, neural network, and glmBoost, were employed to validate the five identified potential hub genes (Fig. 4A and B). Among these algorithms, KNN demonstrated the optimal overall predictive performance, as reflected by the highest AUC value (AUC = 0.940) (Fig. 4B).

To interpret the prediction results of the KNN model and identify hub genes, SHAP values were calculated using the “shapviz” R package, and genes were ranked based on their contribution strength to the model. The bar plot of mean absolute SHAP values (Fig. 4C) demonstrated that MMP9 had the largest mean |SHAP value|, indicating its most substantial overall contribution to the KNN model’s predictions. HDAC1 also exhibited a high mean |SHAP value|, ranking second only to MMP9 in global contribution.

Regarding the impact on sample classification (Fig. 4D), beeswarm plots revealed that MMP9 and HDAC1 had SHAP values distributed across a broad range, with a distinct correlation between their expression levels (encoded by color) and SHAP values. This highlighted that changes in MMP9 and HDAC1 expression played more critical roles in distinguishing sample classes; notably, these two genes, together with three other genes, exerted positive effects on the model’s prediction results.

For the correlation between SHAP values and gene expression levels (Fig. 4E), scatter plots confirmed a positive correlation between the SHAP values of MMP9/HDAC1 and their respective expression levels. When the expression of MMP9 or HDAC1 increased, their SHAP values also rose—suggesting that high expression of these two genes enhanced their predictive contribution, thereby facilitating the differentiation between disease and control samples.

When examining local interpretability for individual samples (Fig. 4F and G), waterfall and force plots illustrated that MMP9 and HDAC1 contributed the most to cumulative contributions in the classification of individual samples. Variations in the expression of these two genes were the core drivers for the KNN model to determine whether a sample belonged to the disease or control group.

Collectively, given their most prominent contributions to the KNN model’s predictions and strong associations with disease status—supported by global contribution analysis, expression-SHAP correlation, and local sample classification results—MMP9 and HDAC1 were identified as the hub genes in this study.

Development and validation of the risk prediction model

First, we assessed the differential expression of the hub genes MMP9 and HDAC1 in the training set. Consistent with our mechanistic hypothesis, MMP9 was significantly upregulated in the IS group, while HDAC1 was significantly downregulated (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, a logistic regression-based risk model for IS was constructed using these two hub genes, and the final risk score equation was derived as follows: Risk score = (−1.5551 × HDAC1 expression) + (0.9836 × MMP9 expression).Notably, the coefficients of MMP9 and HDAC1 in this equation directly align with their expression patterns in IS and their proposed biological roles—confirming the model’s biological plausibility. For MMP9, the positive coefficient (+ 0.9836) reflects its pathogenic role in IS: as MMP9 is upregulated in the IS group (Fig. 5A) and acts as a pro-inflammatory mediator in IS, higher MMP9 expression levels increase the risk score, which is consistent with its pro-disease function. For HDAC1, the negative coefficient (−1.5551) corresponds to its protective role in IS: HDAC1 is downregulated in the IS group (Fig. 5A) and functions as a protective mediator; a decrease in HDAC1 expression reduces the value of the term “(−1.5551 × HDAC1)”, thereby increasing the total risk score. For instance, a 1-unit decrease in HDAC1 expression raises the risk score by approximately 1.5551, which confirms that reduced HDAC1 expression is associated with higher IS risk.

In the training set, IS patients had significantly higher risk scores than healthy controls (Fig. 5B). To enhance the clinical applicability of the model, a risk nomogram integrating MMP9 and HDAC1 expression levels was developed (Fig. 5C). Clinical calibration curve analysis (Fig. 5D) and decision curve analysis (DCA) (Fig. 5E) showed that the model exhibited robust predictive performance for IS; 10-fold cross-validation further confirmed its reliability, with an AUC of 0.830 (Fig. 5F).

For external validation, we used the GSE58294 dataset. Consistent with the results from the training set, MMP9 remained significantly upregulated and HDAC1 remained significantly downregulated in IS patients (Fig. 6A), and the risk score equation maintained its discriminative ability: IS patients had significantly higher risk scores than controls (Fig. 6B), and the model achieved an AUC of 0.946 (Fig. 6E). Calibration curves showed strong consistency between predicted risk probabilities and observed clinical outcomes (Fig. 6C), while DCA confirmed that the model provided a net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 6D).

Analysis of immune cell infiltration in IS

Immune responses play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of ischemic stroke (IS). Analysis of immune cell infiltration in the blood of IS patients and controls revealed substantially elevated levels of multiple immune cells in IS patients, including CD8 + T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), γ-δ T cells, activated natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, M0 macrophages, and neutrophils (Fig. 7A and B). These findings imply that dysregulated immune activity may contribute significantly to the onset and progression of IS. Notably, MMP9 exhibited strong correlations with immune cells such as M0 macrophages and activated NK cells (Fig. 7C), whereas HDAC1 was strongly associated with naive CD4 + T cells and neutrophils (Fig. 7D). Collectively, these results highlight the potential role of BPA-induced cellular senescence-mediated immune cell activation as a key pathological mechanism driving IS progression.

ssGSEA of the hub genes

The ssGSEA was performed to further explore the underlying mechanisms of hub genes MMP9 and HDAC1 in IS. Specifically, in the MMP9 high-expression group, gene set enrichment analysis revealed significant associations with the chemokine signaling pathway, complement and coagulation cascades, FCγR mediated phagocytosis, leukocyte transendothelial migration, and regulation of actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 8A). Conversely, the MMP9 low-expression group was primarily enriched in the allograft rejection, intestinal immune network for IgA production, oxidative phosphorylation, ribosome, and spliceosome pathways (Fig. 8B). For HDAC1, the high-expression group showed significant enrichment in the lysosome, primary immunodeficiency, proteasome, spliceosome, and T cell receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 8C), while the low-expression group was predominantly associated with the neuroactive ligand receptor interaction, olfactory transduction, p53 signaling pathway, regulation of autophagy, and ribosome pathways (Fig. 8D).

These findings indicate that MMP9 and HDAC1 may influence the pathogenesis of IS by regulating distinct biological pathways related to immune response, cellular metabolism, and cytoskeletal dynamics.

The miRNA-TF-mRNA regulatory network

To delve into the regulatory mechanisms, we performed a predictive analysis concentrating on miRNAs and transcription factors (TFs) that target the hub genes. Using Cytoscape, we then constructed a visual model of the complex regulatory network, which comprised 19 miRNAs, 109 TFs, and 2 genes (Fig. 9). Notably, the transcription factor SMAD3 was found to regulate both MMP9 and HADAC1, suggesting its potential pivotal role in this network.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking analyses revealed that two hub genes exhibited binding affinity to BPA, with vina scores below − 5 kcal/mol. Specifically, the minimum docking scores for BPA-MMP9 (Fig. 10A) and BPA-HDAC1 (Fig. 10B) interactions were − 7.2 kcal/mol and − 7.7 kcal/mol respectively. The results illustrate the binding sites of BPA within each hub gene structure. These results suggest that BPA may directly interact with MMP9 and HDAC1, potentially modulating IS-associated pathological processes.

Discussion

This study revealed the key molecular mechanism by which BPA promotes IS through inducing cellular senescence via multi - omics integrated analysis and machine learning modeling. The results showed that BPA may participate in the pathological process of IS by potentially interacting directly with MMP9 and HDAC1 (supported by molecular docking-derived computational evidence), thereby regulating the SASP and immune cell infiltration. This discovery provides new evidence for the association between environmental endocrine disruptors and neurological diseases.

As one of the highest produced industrial chemicals globally, the endocrine - disrupting properties of BPA have been widely confirmed [30]. Through molecular docking simulations, this study found that BPA exhibits strong binding affinities with MMP9 and HDAC1 (binding energies of −7.2 kcal/mol and − 7.7 kcal/mol, respectively)—a result that provides computational evidence for a potential direct targeting relationship between BPA and these two genes, which may be involved in the pathological process of IS. This result echoes the previous conclusion that BPA promotes cellular senescence by activating the p53/p21 pathway or inducing oxidative stress, further clarifying the molecular basis of BPA - mediated cellular senescence [16].

Cellular senescence, an irreversible state of cell - cycle arrest, secretes SASP factors (such as IL – 1β, TNF - α, and MMPs), which can exacerbate chronic inflammation and oxidative damage in the tissue microenvironment. In this study, MMP9, a key component of SASP [31], was significantly upregulated in IS patients and strongly correlated with immune cell infiltration (such as M0 macrophages and activated NK cells). This indicates that BPA may promote the release of SASP factors such as MMP9 by inducing cellular senescence, thereby disrupting the integrity of the BBB and amplifying the neuroinflammatory response, which is consistent with the report that senescent glial cells exacerbate ischemia - reperfusion injury by releasing SASP factors [32]. Notably, the degradation of the extracellular matrix by MMP9 can directly lead to the destruction of the BBB structure [19]. In this study, ssGSEA showed that the high - MMP9 - expression group was enriched in the “leukocyte trans - endothelial migration” pathway, further confirming its mechanism of aggravating neuroinflammation by promoting immune cell infiltration.

This study screened MMP9 and HDAC1 as core hub genes through machine learning algorithms, and they showed distinct regulatory patterns in the occurrence and development of IS. The upregulation of MMP9, a extracellular matrix-degrading factor, may represent a potential mechanism through which BPA contributes to the disruption of the blood-brain barrier structure facilitates immune cell infiltration, and exacerbates neuroinflammation; while the downregulation of HDAC1 may regulate the expression of cell - cycle - related genes by affecting histone deacetylation, promoting the process of cellular senescence [20]. This discovery provides key nodes for analyzing the molecular network of BPA - induced IS.

From the perspective of cellular senescence regulation, as a member of the histone deacetylase family, the downregulation of HDAC1 expression may lead to increased acetylation modification of senescence - related genes such as p53, thereby activating the cellular senescence program [21]. This mechanism is consistent with the results of functional enrichment analysis in this study - the low - HDAC1 - expression group was significantly enriched in the “p53 signaling pathway” and the “autophagy regulation pathway”, suggesting that HDAC1 deficiency may drive cellular senescence by inhibiting autophagy and enhancing p53 activity. Notably, this HDAC1-p53-mediated senescence process is not isolated; it directly fuels the production of pro-inflammatory SASP factors, including MMP9. Previous studies have confirmed that activated p53 can bind to the promoter region of MMP9 to promote its transcription [33], and our functional enrichment analysis further showed that the low-HDAC1 group was co-enriched in “SASP-related pathways” (e.g., “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction”) alongside p53 pathways. This indicates that HDAC1 downregulation-induced senescence may upregulate MMP9 expression through p53-dependent transcriptional activation—establishing a direct upstream regulatory link between HDAC1’s senescence-related function and MMP9’s pro-inflammatory role. The association between MMP9 and the “chemokine signaling pathway” and “complement cascade reaction” further supports its core role in the inflammation - senescence axis, that is, recruiting immune cells by releasing chemokines and forming a “senescence - inflammation” vicious cycle.

Analysis of immune cell infiltration showed that the levels of various immune cells such as CD8 + T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and γδ T cells were significantly increased in the blood of IS patients. MMP9 was strongly correlated with M0 macrophages and activated NK cells, and HDAC1 was correlated with naive CD4 + T cells and neutrophils. This suggests that BPA - induced cellular senescence may form a “senescence - immune activation - inflammation amplification” vicious cycle by activating immune cells.

Specifically, SASP factors (such as MMP9) secreted by senescent cells can recruit M0 macrophages and NK cells to the damaged site. After activation, these immune cells release pro - inflammatory factors (such as TNF - α and IL − 6), which can in turn induce the senescence of neighboring cells, further exacerbating BBB damage. In this study, the ssGSEA results showed that the high - MMP9 - expression group was enriched in the “complement and coagulation cascade reaction”, which may be related to the activation of the complement system and promotion of thrombus formation by SASP factors released from senescent cells, thus directly participating in the ischemic pathological process of IS. The negative correlation between HDAC1 and naive CD4 + T cells implies that the downregulation of HDAC1 may affect T - cell differentiation, weaken immune regulation function, and indirectly promote the inflammatory response.

The miRNA - TF - mRNA regulatory network constructed in this study revealed the dual regulatory effects of transcription factors such as SMAD3 on MMP9 and HDAC1, providing new targets for developing therapeutic strategies targeting BPA - related IS. For example, targeting SMAD3 may simultaneously inhibit the pro - inflammatory effect of MMP9 and restore the anti - aging function of HDAC1, thereby blocking the pathological cascade reaction induced by BPA. From the perspective of transcriptional regulation mechanism, as a key effector of the TGF - β signaling pathway, the regulation of MMP9 by SMAD3 may involve extracellular matrix remodeling [34–36], while the regulation of HDAC1 may affect the cellular senescence process through epigenetic modification—together, these provide a theoretical basis for combined intervention of the ‘inflammation-senescence’ axis, with the caveat that the proposed direct interaction between BPA and MMP9/HDAC1 remains to be validated experimentally.

In addition, the logistic regression model constructed based on MMP9 and HDAC1 achieved AUC values of 0.830 (training set) and 0.946 (validation set), respectively. These results provide preliminary computational evidence for the potential of MMP9 and HDAC1 as candidate diagnostic biomarkers for IS. Meanwhile, both can be detected using blood-based enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), a method that is widely employed in clinical laboratories owing to its rapid turnaround time and cost-effectiveness, thereby supporting its suitability for large-scale screening. However, this potential remains to be verified in larger, multi-center independent clinical cohorts to confirm their stability and generalizability. Combining the clinical calibration curve and decision curve analysis of the nomogram model, this model is expected to provide a quantitative tool for clinical assessment of the risk of BPA - exposure - related IS. Notably, the combined detection efficacy of MMP9 and HDAC1 was significantly higher than that of a single biomarker, which may be attributed to their synergistic effects as a pro - inflammatory factor and an epigenetic regulatory factor in the “BPA - cellular senescence - IS” path.

Although this study revealed the association mechanism of BPA - cellular senescence - IS through multi - dimensional analysis, there are still some limitations. First, the potential direct interaction between BPA and MMP9/HDAC1 proposed in this study is based on molecular docking, a computational prediction approach. While strong binding affinities support this hypothesis, the actual biological binding event (e.g., in vivo or in vitro) requires further verification via experimental techniques such as co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) or surface plasmon resonance (SPR) to confirm. In the future, animal models and cell experiments are needed to further confirm the causal relationship between MMP9 and HDAC1 in BPA - induced IS. For example, by knocking out or overexpressing the MMP9/HDAC1 genes, the changes in cellular senescence phenotypes and IS - related pathological indicators under BPA exposure can be observed to clarify their causal regulatory relationship. Second, the exposure dose and time - effect of BPA were not fully explored in this study. The long - term effects of low - dose BPA exposure relevant to the environment may be more clinically relevant. In subsequent studies, experiments with different dose gradients and exposure durations should be designed to analyze the dose - effect relationship.

In addition, this study only focused on the two hub genes MMP9 and HDAC1, while the BPA - induced cellular senescence network may involve more unknown targets. In the future, single - cell sequencing technology can be combined to deeply analyze the senescence trajectories of different cell types (such as neurons and glial cells) under BPA exposure, providing a more comprehensive theoretical basis for precise intervention. For example, through single - cell transcriptomics analysis, the specific expression and functional differences of MMP9 and HDAC1 in neurons, astrocytes, or microglia can be determined, providing a basis for developing cell - type - specific therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that MMP9 and HDAC1, as key hub genes, may play a core role in the BPA - mediated “senescence - inflammation” cascade reaction and participate in the pathological process of IS by regulating immune cell infiltration and the signaling pathway network. These findings not only expand the understanding of the association between environmental pollutants and neurological diseases but also provide new targets and biomarkers for the prevention and treatment of IS.
