Factors associated with late bedtimes, early wake-up times, and short sleep in adolescents on school nights
Authors: Rachel S Charoenthammanon, Joshua J Gooley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIdentifying factors that contribute to inadequate sleep in adolescents is important for developing interventions to improve sleep, learning, and well-being. The goal of our study was to estimate the strength of associations of individual and environmental factors with adolescents’ sleep timing and duration.MethodsDaily sleep onset, offset, and duration were measured by actigraphy in 133 Singaporean adolescents (13–18 years; 67 girls) during a typical school week in which individual students often had more than one school start time. Daily diaries recorded behaviors before and after nocturnal sleep. Linear mixed models were used to estimate associations of age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, chronotype, night of the week, bedtime reason, morning get-ready time, commute time, and school start time with nocturnal sleep.ResultsLater sleep onset was associated with older age, later chronotype, and homework/studying at bedtime. Earlier sleep offset was associated with longer get-ready and commute times and earlier school start time. Shorter sleep was associated with older age, later chronotype, homework/studying at bedtime, and earlier school start time. Adolescents obtained 45 min less sleep when their main reason for bedtime was finishing homework/studying versus feeling tired or sleepy. They obtained 50 min more sleep when school started from 08:30 to 08:59 am versus from 07:30 to 07:59 am.ConclusionsMultiple factors influence adolescents’ sleep on school nights. Modifiable factors that were strongly associated with short sleep included homework/studying at bedtime and early school start times. Avoiding schoolwork near bedtime and delaying school start time are potential strategies for improving sleep in adolescents.

Statement of Significance

Many adolescents do not get enough sleep on school nights because they go to bed too late or wake up too early. Our study tested the strength of association of several modifiable factors with adolescents’ sleep timing and duration in an academically competitive sociocultural context. Adolescents went to bed much later and obtained shorter sleep when their bedtime was determined by when they finished their homework or studying rather than when they felt tired or sleepy. Early school start times were associated with early wake-up times and short sleep. Minimizing schoolwork at bedtime and starting school at 08:30 am or later are potential strategies for improving the timing and duration of sleep in adolescents.

Introduction

Many adolescents struggle to obtain sufficient sleep during the school week [1, 2]. This is a major concern because inadequate sleep is associated with impaired performance and health. Repeated exposure to short sleep over the week results in cumulative deficits in attention, cognitive processing speed, and memory encoding and recall [3–8]. The impairing effects of inadequate sleep on alertness, cognitive performance, and motivation may erode learning and contribute to lower academic performance [8–10]. Additionally, short sleep in adolescents has been linked to absenteeism, behavioral problems, negative mood, emotional dysregulation, and depression symptoms [8, 11–15]. Moreover, short sleep is associated with other negative lifestyle behaviors including higher sedentary activity and increased dietary intake that may give rise to obesity and poorer physical health [16–19]. Treating inadequate sleep in adolescents is therefore a priority for improving their performance and well-being. This requires identifying and removing barriers to obtaining healthy sleep.

Multiple factors may contribute to short sleep during adolescence. Bedtimes tend to drift later due to biological changes and environmental pressures. A puberty-associated delay of the circadian timing system and slower buildup of homeostatic sleep pressure make it easier for adolescents to go to bed later compared with earlier in childhood [1, 2, 20–23]. Adolescents’ preferred sleep–wake timing (i.e. chronotype) during free days is therefore typically much later than their sleep pattern during the school week [24]. Keeping a later sleep schedule over the weekend also delays the circadian clock [25], which may make it more difficult for adolescents to fall asleep early and wake up early at the start of the school week. Adolescents’ waking activities also compete with time for sleep. School workload often intensifies during the high school years, where time spent on homework and studying may delay bedtime and displace sleep [26, 27]. In parallel, adolescents gain autonomy over how they spend their time before bedtime and may postpone sleep to pursue leisure activities, e.g., using e-devices [28–31]. Early school start times and long commute times may also contribute to early wake-up times that curtail nocturnal sleep [15, 32–36]. The convergence of these factors that delay bedtimes and advance wake-up times in adolescents has been described as “a perfect storm” that leads to short sleep during the school week [1, 2].

Measuring the effect sizes (i.e. the magnitude of change) of associations between bedtime- and wake-up time-related factors with adolescents’ nocturnal sleep is critical for determining what types of interventions are likely to have the greatest impact on sleep. However, most studies have focused on one or a few factors in isolation, e.g., media use before bedtime or school start time, rather than statistically adjusting for multiple sleep-associated factors in the same individuals – doing so should give a better picture of which factors are associated with meaningful changes in sleep duration (i.e. enough to impact daytime functioning or well-being) that should be targeted in intervention studies. The relative importance of each sleep-associated factor may also vary by the sociocultural context. For example, prior studies of associations between adolescent behavior and late bedtimes have focused primarily on screen use rather than schoolwork [28, 37–39]. In high-pressure academic environments, homework and studying may contribute substantially to late bedtimes and short sleep [15, 27]. Also, most research on the relationship between school start time and wake-up time has not accounted for adolescents’ commute time or morning preparation time. The degree to which adolescents can sleep in on school mornings is determined not only by their school start time but also by how long it takes to get ready (e.g. time spent on grooming, using the bathroom, eating breakfast, doing chores, etc.) and how long it takes to reach school from their residence; hence, these variables should all be considered when assessing adolescents’ wake-up behavior.

Past studies have typically aggregated sleep data, e.g., by averaging sleep over the week, or used one-time surveys to assess sleep-associated factors, rather than investigating how daily changes in adolescents’ pre-bedtime behavior or morning routine influenced their sleep. Adolescents’ reasons for going to bed may differ across the school week. The same individual may decide to go to sleep when feeling sleepy on some evenings and delay bedtime to finish schoolwork or leisure activities on other evenings. Additionally, wake-up time may vary depending on when adolescents need to reach school. In universities and pre-university educational systems such as the one in Singapore, it is common for students to have more than one school start time during the week. In these instances, students may wake up later and sleep longer on days when school starts later. In the present study, we addressed the aforementioned limitations by assessing both the impact of student-level factors and daily sleep-associated factors on the timing and duration of adolescents’ sleep.

The objectives of our study were to identify factors associated with late bedtimes and early wake-up times during the school week and to measure their strength of association with sleep duration in Singaporean adolescents. We focused primarily on individual factors and environmental factors that were associated with sleep timing and duration in our previous survey studies of adolescents in Singapore (e.g. age, chronotype, bedtime reason, commute time, and school start time) [15]. Building on this past work, we implemented linear mixed models in the present study to estimate effect sizes of student characteristics, pre-bedtime behavior, and morning routine on daily actigraphy-estimated sleep, adjusting for other factors. The study was conducted with the purpose of identifying modifiable factors (i.e. behavioral or environmental factors) that can be targeted to help adolescents go to bed earlier or wake up later so that they are more likely to achieve a healthy sleep duration on school nights. We therefore tested three sets of hypotheses (each tested by a different linear mixed model) relating to which factors would be associated with sleep onset, sleep offset, and sleep duration. First, we hypothesized that adolescents would go to sleep later if they were older, had a later chronotype, or if their main reason for going to bed was finishing homework/studying or using e-devices for leisure, as compared with going to bed when they felt tired or sleepy. Second, we hypothesized that adolescents would wake up earlier if they took longer to get ready in the morning, had a longer commute time, or if school started earlier. Third, we hypothesized that these factors would be associated with shorter nocturnal sleep. Based on our prior work in Singaporean adolescents [15, 27, 33], we predicted that among the modifiable factors included in the analysis, homework/studying and school start time would be strongly associated with sleep duration.

Methods

Ethics statement

Adolescents provided written informed assent to participate in the research, with at least one parent or guardian providing written informed consent. Research procedures were approved by the National University of Singapore Institutional Review Board (NUS-IRB-2021-316) and complied with ethical principles for human research in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participant recruitment and school characteristics

Participants were recruited from local schools in Singapore. Adolescents were eligible for the study if they were between 13.0 and 19.0 years at the time of enrollment, and they were attending a local secondary school (equivalent to middle school grades 7–8 and high school grades 9–10 in the American education system), or a local junior college (pre-university education equivalent to high school grades 11–12). Adolescents also needed to provide evidence that they were vaccinated for COVID-19. There were no exclusion criteria. In the Singapore local education system, there is no fixed school start time. Schools have the autonomy to decide their start time, and they may have more than one start time within the school week. The school day starts between 07:30 am and 08:30 am on most days (see Results).

The researchers contacted 14 schools, all of which agreed to facilitate student recruitment. A designated school representative (i.e. teacher or school leader) oversaw the dissemination of publicity and study materials to students. Each school managed recruitment internally and provided the researchers with a contact list of students who were interested in the study. Data on response rate were not provided by participating schools. Adolescents from several other schools also contacted the researchers directly to take part in the research after hearing about the study from their friends/peers. Among 322 students who expressed interest in the study by providing their contact information, 141 adolescents did not participate in the research (unresponsive or did not show up for consent, n = 87; ineligible based on age or vaccination status, n = 23; no longer interested, n = 12; eligible but waitlisted, n = 19). There were 181 adolescents who attended an online information session with their parent/guardian and agreed to take part in the research. Among these adolescents, there were 46 participants who were withdrawn from the study (unavailable or unresponsive, n = 26; non-compliant, n = 13; waitlisted and not studied, n = 7), and there were 2 participants who completed the study but they were excluded due to inconsistencies in their actigraphy record and daily diaries. The final sample in our analyses therefore included 133 adolescents aged 13–18 years from 20 local schools in Singapore.

Pre-study questionnaire

After consenting to participate in the research, adolescents completed a questionnaire that provided data on their demographics (age, sex, ethnic group, type of residence, and school), general health, and usual sleep habits (Table 1). Socioeconomic status (SES) was estimated by housing type, with up to 3-room public housing, 4- to 5-room public housing, and private housing corresponding to lower, middle, and higher SES. BMI categories (underweight, normal weight, overweight/obese) were determined from self-reported height and weight using pediatric growth-curve percentiles that were adjusted for age and sex. Pubertal category (pre-pubertal, early pubertal, mid-pubertal, late pubertal, or postpubertal) was determined by scores on the Pubertal Development Scale [40], which assessed self-reported changes in height, growth of bodily and facial hair, changes in dermatology, development of breast tissue, and the onset of menarche.

Demographic Characteristics of Adolescent Participants

Study protocol

Each adolescent’s sleep behavior was assessed during school periods and holiday periods between December 2021 and April 2023. In the Singapore local education system, there are two 5-month school semesters per year (January to May, July to November) with intervening holiday periods lasting 4–6 weeks (May to June, November to December). In Singapore (1.35°N, 103.82°E), the natural photoperiod is nearly 12 h year-round, the daily averages of temperature and humidity are nearly constant, and there is no daylight saving time. The researchers coordinated with adolescents and their parents to schedule a 1-week period during the school term, and a 1-week period during the holidays, to participate in the research. Adolescents were scheduled according to their availability; hence, the order of assessment (holiday period first, school period first) was not randomized. The first week of the school term and the first week of the holiday period were avoided due to possible transitional effects on sleep behavior. Adolescents were not studied during major examination periods.

During each week of sleep monitoring, adolescents were required to wear an actigraphy watch (ActiGraph wGT3X-BT; ActiGraph, Pensacola, FL, USA) on their non-dominant wrist. The actigraphy watch included an accelerometer for measuring rest–activity patterns and a photo-sensor for detecting light. Adolescents were instructed to wear the actigraphy watch continuously when asleep and awake except when engaging in activities that could damage the device (e.g. water activities or contact sports). Participants returned the actigraphy watch to the researchers at the end of the week (school periods, Friday night; holiday periods, Thursday or Friday night). Adolescents completed daily diaries before going to bed at night and after waking in the morning to collect data on their sleep habits and daily activities, and to document removals of the actigraphy watch. Adolescents were instructed to complete their bedtime diary between 09:00 pm and bedtime, and their morning diary within an hour of waking. The same diary instructions were given for school nights, weekends, and holiday periods. Diaries were hosted on an online survey platform (Qualtrics, Seattle, WA, USA). Participants were also instructed to send a “sleep” or “wake” text message to the researchers before going to sleep and after waking (Telegram Messenger, Dubai, United Arab Emirates).

The bedtime diary was used to collect information on time taken to get ready for school, commute time, and school start time. If participants had an in-person class that day and left directly from their residence to make their way to school, they were asked the following questions: (1) “How long did it take you to get ready before leaving your home/hostel?”; (2) “How long did it take you to reach class after leaving your home/hostel?”; and (3) “What time did your first class start today?”. Participants entered a numerical free-response for get-ready time and commute time (in hours and minutes) and for school start time (in 12-h am/pm format). Other questions on the bedtime diary (e.g. related to daytime sleepiness or mood) were not used in the present study. Fewer bedtime diaries were completed for Friday night because most adolescents did not fill out the diary after returning the actigraphy watch earlier in the day.

The morning diary was used to collect data on bedtime and wake-up time for the previous night in free-response format (12-h am/pm format). Bedtime was assessed with the question “What time did you go to bed last night? (i.e. the time you tried to fall asleep).” Next, adolescents were asked “What was the main reason you went to bed at that time last night?” Response options included “I set my bedtime,” “My parents set my bedtime,” “I started to feel tired or sleepy,” “I finished my studying/ homework,” “I finished hanging out with friends or family members (without using media/ electronic devices),” “I finished using media/ electronic devices for leisure (e.g. cell phone, iPad/tablet, computer, TV, video games, etc.),” or “Others.” The question on bedtime reason was modified from the School Sleep Habits Survey [41], where students could choose only one response option as “the main reason.” Participants chose either schoolwork or media use as the main reason if they were performing the activity after the point when they started to feel tired or sleepy, i.e., instead of going to bed. By comparison, participants chose “I started to feel tired or sleepy” if they decided to go to bed when they started to feel tired or sleepy, irrespective of what they were doing at the time. The different response options for bedtime reason were reviewed with adolescents during their information/consent session. Wake-up time was assessed with the question “What time did you wake up today? Your wake-up time is the last time that you woke up without going back to sleep.” Other questions in the morning diary (e.g. related to sleep quality and morning mood) were not used in the present study.

Analyses of actigraphy-estimated sleep and daily diaries

Actigraphy data for each 1-week recording were binned in 30-s epochs and plotted as actograms. Bedtimes and wake-up times from participants’ daily diaries and time-stamped text messages were used to mark the time-in-bed intervals on the actograms. Epochs within these time-in-bed intervals were then scored as either sleep or wakefulness using the Sadeh algorithm (ActiGraph ActiLife software, version 6.13.5) [42]. These data were used to derive the sleep onset and sleep offset for each daily nocturnal sleep episode. Sleep duration was defined as the time elapsed from sleep onset to sleep offset. Secondary measures included the bedtime and rise time that marked the beginning and end of each time-in-bed interval, the sleep midpoint (halfway point between sleep onset and sleep offset), and the total sleep time (time spent asleep, excluding nocturnal awakenings).

Chronotype was estimated by first computing the median of adolescents’ actigraphy-estimated sleep midpoint during holiday weekdays (from Sunday night to Thursday night) when sleep behavior was not constrained by school start time. Adolescents were then categorized as early-type or late-type if their sleep midpoint was in the lowest or highest quintiles of the distribution [43], respectively (i.e. the 20% with the earliest and the 20% with the latest sleep midpoint values). The remaining 60% of adolescents were defined as intermediate-type. This method of categorizing adolescents into chronotype groups ensured that early-type and late-type adolescents were phenotypically distinct with a sufficient number of students in each group for making meaningful statistical comparisons.

Daily diaries were analyzed only for school days with in-person classes (536 total nights). Responses on the daily diary were combined for some categories based on the distribution of the data. For the question on bedtime reason, responses for “I set my bedtime” and “My parents set my bedtime” were combined into one category referred to as “Set bedtime.” The response for “I finished hanging out with friends or family members” was rarely chosen (12 instances) and was therefore combined with “Others.” In analyses where all factors were treated as categorical (see below), morning get-ready time and commute time were binned by time categories (<15 min, 15–29 min, 30–44 min, 45–59 min, ≥60 min) because adolescents usually rounded off their responses. School start times were binned by ranges (earlier than 07:30 am, 07:30 am to 07:59 am, 08:00 am to 08:29 am, 08:30 am to 08:59 am, and 09:00 am or later) to facilitate comparisons with recommendations by health organizations for setting healthy school start times (e.g. starting no earlier than 08:30 am) [44, 45]. In separate analyses, time variables (get-ready time, commute time, and school start time) were treated as continuous variables using adolescents’ original responses provided in their daily diaries (i.e. without binning; see below).

Statistical comparisons

Descriptive statistics and statistical tests were performed using Python (version 3.9.8) software, with the packages “pingouin” (version 0.5.3), “dabest” (version 2023.2.14) and “Pymer4” (version 0.8.1). The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. SigmaPlot software (version 14.5; Systat Software, Inc., San Jose, CA) was used to plot the data. Demographic variables were compared between age groups using two-sided Chi-squared tests for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis tests for non-categorical variables.

Estimation statistics (effect sizes and their precision) were performed to test for differences in sleep behavior (sleep onset, sleep offset, sleep duration) between school and holiday periods. Estimation statistics were also used in univariate analyses to test for differences between the categorical subgroups for each potential sleep-associated factor (age at enrollment, sex, ethnicity, SES, chronotype, order of study visit, day/night of the school week, bedtime reason, morning get-ready time, commute time, and school start time). The purpose of these univariate analyses was to select for predictor variables that should be included in linear mixed models (see below). In these analyses, the reference groups were 13 or 14 years, girls, Chinese, middle SES, intermediate chronotype, holiday period first, Tuesday night/Wednesday morning, felt tired or sleepy as the main reason for bedtime, morning get-ready time of 30–44 min, commute time of 30–44 min, and school start time between 07:30 am and 07:59 am. Most of these reference categories were chosen based on the subgroup with the highest frequency, except for age, sex, and night/day of the week. The bedtime reason “I started to feel tired or sleepy” was chosen as the reference category because it was the most frequently cited reason for going to bed in our dataset (55.2% of nights), whereas having a set bedtime (by self or parent) was relatively uncommon (17.6%). Additionally, adolescents should presumably go to sleep if they feel signs of sleepiness, as opposed to staying awake to pursue other activities (e.g. schoolwork or media use). We did not include the pubertal development category in our analyses because most adolescents (89.2%) were categorized as late- or postpubertal, and self-reported data were missing from 22 participants. The mean difference in hours (simple effect size) and Cohen’s d (standardized effect size) with 95% CIs were computed using a bootstrap resampling method with 5000 samples [46]. For each comparison, a two-sided permutation t-test was also performed to obtain a P-value (threshold P < 0.05) that reflects the likelihood of obtaining the observed effect size should the null hypothesis be true (i.e. zero effect size with no difference between the two samples).

Linear mixed models were used to test the associations between each predictor variable with sleep onset, sleep offset, and sleep duration. The predictor variables entered into each model included student-level characteristics and daily self-reported variables (e.g. bedtime reason and morning routine) that were identified as being associated with sleep in univariate analyses. Fixed effect factors in the various linear mixed models included age at enrollment, sex, ethnicity, SES, chronotype, order of study, day/night of the school week, bedtime reason, morning get-ready time, commute time, and school start time. Individual participants were included in each model as a random effect factor. These models were used to estimate the change/difference (i.e. effect size) in sleep onset, sleep offset, and sleep duration (expressed in hours, with 95% CIs) associated with each fixed effect factor, adjusting for all other factors. Satterthwaite’s method was used to perform two-tailed t-tests for fixed effect factors. In instances where there was evidence of a non-zero effect size, multiple comparisons between subcategories for a given fixed effect factor were performed using Tukey’s test. In separate linear mixed models, time-related variables including age, chronotype, morning get-ready time, commute time, and school start time were entered as continuous variables using participants’ original responses in their pre-study questionnaire and daily diaries (i.e. without binning responses into categories). Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to confirm that age and chronotype (holiday sleep midpoint) were correlated with sleep onset (age, r = 0.21, P < 0.0001; chronotype, r = 0.28, P < 0.0001), and get-ready time, commute time, and school start time were correlated with sleep offset (get-ready time, r = −0.25, P < 0.0001; commute time, r = −0.21, P < 0.0001; school start time, r = 0.33, P < 0.0001).

Results

Participant characteristics and sleep behavior

Our study population (n = 133, mean age ± SD = 15.87 ± 1.55 years) was approximately balanced by age group (13 or 14 years, n = 44; 15 or 16 years, n = 45; 17 or 18 years, n = 44) and biological sex (boys, n = 66; girls, n = 67) (Table 1). Most participants were Chinese (77.4%), resided in public housing (69.5%), and had a normal weight based on self-reported BMI (67.4%). Among adolescents with a valid score on the Pubertal Development Scale (n = 111), most were in mid-to-postpubertal stages of development (early, 0.9%; mid, 9.9%, late, 56.8%; post, 32.4%). Adolescents were recruited from a mix of local secondary schools (54.1%) and junior colleges (45.9%), and their order of assessment was approximately balanced (school period first, 44.4%; holiday period first, 55.6%). The average time interval between sleep assessments was about 2 months (mean ± SD = 58.3 ± 32.0 days). Based on daily diaries, most adolescents reported more than one school start time during their school week (72.9%, n = 97), and nearly all of these adolescents (n = 93) had school start times that fell within more than one time category (i.e. earlier than 07:30 am, 07:30–07:59 am, 08:00–08:29 am, 08:30–08:59 am, 09:00 am or later).

Adolescents’ actigraphy-estimated sleep behavior was much earlier and shorter during the school week compared with the weekends or holidays (Figure 1, Table 2). During holiday weekdays, participants fell asleep after 01:00 am (Mean = 01:22 am, SD = 1.62 h) and woke up shortly after 09:00 am (Mean = 09:11 am, SD = 1.92 h), resulting in an average sleep duration of 7.85 h (SD = 1.33 h). During the school week, participants fell asleep near midnight (Mean = 11:59 pm, SD = 1.17 h) and woke up shortly after 06:00 am (Mean = 06:10 am, SD = 0.55 h), resulting in an average sleep duration of 6.23 h (SD = 1.07 h). Within-participant comparisons showed that adolescents went to sleep 1.38 h earlier (95% CI = 1.15 to 1.64 h), woke up 3.00 h earlier (95% CI = 2.71 to 3.32 h), and obtained 1.62 h less sleep (95% CI = 1.39 to 1.86 h) on school nights compared with holiday weekdays (Table 2). Differences in sleep during the school week relative to holiday weekdays were associated with large effect sizes for sleep onset (d = −0.98, 95% CI = −1.16 to −0.80), sleep offset (d = −2.13, 95% CI = −2.40 to −1.87), and sleep duration (d = −1.34, 95% CI = −1.57 to −1.12) (Table 2).

Factors associated with sleep onset on school nights

In univariate analyses, factors that were associated with sleep onset on school nights included age, ethnicity, SES, chronotype, night of the school week, and bedtime reason (Figure 2A, Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Figure S1). Sleep onset was earlier in adolescents with a lower SES or early chronotype, and on days when the main reason for going to bed was having a set bedtime. Sleep onset was later in adolescents who were older, non-Chinese, or categorized as a late chronotype. Sleep onset was also later on Sunday night, and on nights when adolescents reported their main reason for bedtime was finishing homework/studying or using e-devices for leisure. Sleep onset did not differ between boys and girls (Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Figure S1).

Next, we tested which factors were associated with sleep onset when all variables were entered simultaneously in a linear mixed model (Supplementary Table S2). Sleep onset was associated with age, chronotype, night of the school week, and bedtime reason (Figure 2B, Supplementary Table S2), but not sex, ethnicity, or SES. Relative to adolescents aged 13 or 14 years, sleep onset was 28 min later in individuals aged 15 or 16 years (β = 0.46 h, 95% CI = 0.03 to 0.89 h), and 41 min later in individuals aged 17 or 18 years (β = 0.69 h, 95% CI = 0.26 to 1.12 h). However, differences in sleep onset between age groups did not reach statistical significance when adjusting for multiple comparisons (Tukey’s test, P > 0.05 for each pairwise comparison). Relative to adolescents categorized as an intermediate chronotype, early-type individuals went to sleep 40 min earlier (β = −0.67 h, 95% CI = −1.13 to −0.21 h), and late-type individuals tended to go to sleep later by 28 min (β = 0.46 h, 95% CI = −0.003 to 0.93 h). Multiple comparison tests indicated that sleep onset was statistically different between early-type and late-type individuals (Tukey’s test, P < 0.001), but not for the other pairwise comparisons (P > 0.05). On Sunday night, adolescents went to sleep 23 min later compared with the middle of the school week (β = 0.39 h, 95% CI = 0.16 to 0.62 h), and multiple comparison tests showed that sleep onset was later on Sunday night compared with Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday nights (Tukey’s test, P < 0.05 for each pairwise comparison).

Adolescents went to sleep 49 min later if their main reason for bedtime was finishing homework or studying, as compared with going to bed when they felt tired or sleepy (β = 0.81 h, 95% CI = 0.52 to 1.10 h; Tukey’s test, P < 0.0001) (Figure 2B, Supplementary Table S2). Adolescents went to sleep 28 min later if their bedtime reason was using e-devices for leisure (β = 0.47 h, 95% CI = 0.12 to 0.81 h); however, the difference relative to going to bed when tired or sleepy did not reach statistical significance when correcting for multiple comparisons (Tukey’s test, P = 0.21).

In a separate linear mixed model, the factors age and chronotype (i.e. holiday sleep midpoint) were entered as continuous variables (Supplementary Table S3). In this model, the same factors were associated with sleep onset on school nights, i.e. age, chronotype, and bedtime reason. Sleep onset was 11 min later for each 1-year increase in age (β = 0.18 h, 95% CI = 0.07 to 0.30 h), and 15 min later for each 1-hour-later difference in chronotype (β = 0.25 h, 95% CI = 0.14 to 0.36 h). Adolescents went to sleep later if their bedtime reason was homework/studying compared with feeling tired or sleepy (β = 0.81 h, 95% CI = 0.52 to 1.10 h; Tukey’s test, P < 0.0001). Sleep onset also tended to be later when their bedtime reason was using e-devices for leisure, but did not reach statistical significance when adjusting for multiple comparisons (β = 0.48 h, 95% CI = 0.14 to 0.82 h; Tukey’s test, P = 0.13).

Factors associated with sleep offset on school mornings

In univariate analyses, factors that were associated with sleep offset on school mornings included age, SES, chronotype, day of the school week, morning get-ready time, commute time, and school start time (Figure 3A, Supplementary Table S4, Supplementary Figure S2). Sleep offset was earlier in adolescents with a higher SES or early chronotype, and in individuals who took at least an hour to either get ready or commute to school. Adolescents also woke up earlier when school started before 07:30 am, as compared with 07:30 am to 07:59 am. Sleep offset was later in adolescents who were older or had a short commute time (<30 min), and in instances where school started at 08:30 am or later. Sleep offset was also later on Thursday and Friday mornings, which were days when some schools in our dataset had a later start time compared with the rest of the week. Sleep offset did not differ between boys and girls, or between Chinese and non-Chinese adolescents (Supplementary Table S4, Supplementary Figure S2).

In a separate linear mixed model that included time-related factors entered as continuous variables, morning get-ready time, commute time, and school start time were associated with sleep offset (Supplementary Table S6). Adolescents woke up 29 min earlier for a 1-h increase in morning get-ready time (β = −0.48 h, 95% CI = −0.73 to −0.24 h), and 27 min earlier for a 1-h increase in commute time (β = −0.45 h, 95% CI = −0.74 to −0.16 h). Adolescents woke up 27 min later for every hour that school started later (β = 0.45 h, 95% CI = 0.35 to 0.55 h).

Factors associated with sleep duration on school nights

In univariate analyses, factors that were associated with sleep duration on school nights included age, ethnicity, chronotype, night of the school week, bedtime reason, morning get-ready time, and school start time (Figure 4A, Supplementary Table S7, Supplementary Figure S3). Sleep duration was shorter in adolescents who were older, non-Chinese, or categorized as a late chronotype. Nocturnal sleep was also shorter on Sunday night, and in instances where adolescents’ main reason for going to bed was finishing homework or studying. Sleep duration was longer in adolescents categorized as an early chronotype, and in instances when they had a set bedtime (by self or parent). Adolescents also obtained more sleep if they got ready quickly in the morning (<15 min) and when school started at 08:30 am or later. There were no differences in sleep duration between boys and girls, across SES categories, or for different commute times (Supplementary Table S7, Supplementary Figure S3).

Sleep duration was 32 min shorter on Sunday night compared with the middle of the school week (β = −0.53 h, 95% CI = −0.78 to −0.28 h), and multiple comparison tests showed that sleep duration was shorter on Sunday night compared with all other school nights (Tukey’s test, P < 0.05 for each comparison).

They also tended to get less sleep (by 20 min) when their main reason for going to bed was using e-devices for leisure, but the difference relative to feeling tired or sleepy was not statistically significant in the linear mixed model (P = 0.071) or in multiple comparison tests (Tukey’s test, P = 0.37). Relative to days when school started from 07:30 am to 07:59 am, sleep duration was 51 min longer when school started from 08:30 am to 08:59 am (β = 0.85 h, 95% CI = 0.45 to 1.25 h; Tukey’s test, P < 0.001). Sleep duration tended to be shorter when school started earlier than 07:30 am but the difference did not reach statistical significance when adjusting for multiple comparisons (β = −0.60 h, 95% CI = −1.17 to −0.04 h; Tukey’s test, P = 0.24) (Figure 4B, Supplementary Table S8). In contrast to univariate analyses, starting school at 09:00 am or later (relative to 07:30 am to 07:59 am) was not associated with a statistically significant increase in sleep duration when adjusting for other factors (P = 0.071).

Discussion

In this study, we identified several factors that were associated with adolescents’ sleep behavior during the school week, and we estimated their strength of association with daily sleep onset, sleep offset, and sleep duration using linear mixed models. We found that adolescents went to sleep later on school nights if they were older or exhibited a later chronotype during the holidays, or if their main reason for going to bed was finishing homework/studying rather than going to bed when they started to feel tired or sleepy. Adolescents woke up earlier if they took longer to get ready for school, if they had a longer commute time, or if their school started earlier. Among the factors that were associated with sleep onset or sleep offset, adolescents obtained shorter sleep on school nights if they were older or exhibited a later chronotype, if it was Sunday night, if their reason for going to bed was finishing homework/studying versus feeling tired or sleepy, or if they had an earlier school start time. Modifiable factors that were strongly associated with sleep behavior included homework/studying before bedtime and school start time, suggesting that interventions for improving adolescents’ sleep should target these factors.

Most adolescents obtained short sleep on school nights

Most adolescents in our study failed to get a healthy amount of sleep on school nights. The average sleep duration during the school week was 6.23 h (SD = 1.07 h), which is much shorter than the amount recommended (8–10 h) for optimal performance and health in adolescents [47, 48]. Adolescents were capable of getting more sleep, however, as evidenced by their behavior on the weekends and during the holidays when their average sleep duration was 7.85 h (SD = 1.33). These findings are consistent with previous studies of self-reported sleep and actigraphy-estimated sleep in Singaporean adolescents whose average sleep duration was about 6.5 h on school nights and about 8 h on weekends [15, 49, 50].

Adolescents in our study went to sleep much later and obtained shorter sleep on school nights compared with their peers in most other sociocultural settings (predominantly Western societies) whose sleep was assessed by actigraphy [51]. Previous cross-cultural comparisons of sleep have also shown that adolescents in Asian countries, including Singapore, tend to go to bed later and get less sleep compared with their peers in Western societies [52–54]. Nonetheless, across cultures, it has been consistently shown that adolescents are more likely to achieve a healthy sleep duration during their holiday periods compared with the school term [52, 55, 56]. Hence, short sleep in adolescents is a global problem. As discussed below, this can be explained by going to bed too late and waking up too early.

Factors that were associated with late bedtimes

Student characteristics that were associated with sleep onset on school nights included age and chronotype. Ethnicity and SES were associated with sleep onset in univariate analyses, but not when adjusting for other predictors in the linear mixed models. Consistent with previous cross-sectional and longitudinal studies [15, 41, 57], sleep onset delayed by about 40–60 min from early to late adolescence. The difference in sleep onset between late-type and early-type students was also about an hour in our study, indicating that adolescents’ chronotype was an important determinant of when they fell asleep on school nights. In contrast to most prior work, we estimated chronotype by taking the midpoint of adolescents’ sleep during the holidays. However, earlier studies that defined chronotype based on preferred sleep timing or morningness–eveningness scores also showed that late-type adolescents went to sleep about an hour later during the school week compared with early-type adolescents [15, 58–60].

Adolescents’ sleep onset differed according to their bedtime reason. We found that adolescents went to sleep nearly 50 min later if their reason for bedtime was finishing homework/studying, as compared with feeling tired or sleepy. These results extend previous survey findings where adolescents reported going to bed about 30 min later on school nights if homework/studying was the main reason for their bedtime versus feeling tired [61], or versus any reason other than schoolwork [27]. Our results also extend previous findings that showed adolescents who spent more time on homework had later bedtimes [15, 62]. The association between homework/studying and sleep onset could be explained by difficulties with time management (e.g. adolescents started their work too late in the evening) or difficulties coping with after-school workload (e.g. too much assigned work and/or too much time spent on other after-school activities).

Although adolescents in our study tended to go to sleep later when their main reason for going to bed was determined by when they finished using e-devices for leisure, the difference relative to going to bed when tired or sleepy did not reach statistical significance when adjusting for multiple comparisons. It is possible that adolescents who used e-devices for leisure before bedtime were able to stay awake longer without feeling tired because the activity was cognitively engaging. In such instances, adolescents may have indicated that they went to bed when they felt tired or sleepy, even though they would have fallen asleep earlier if they had not used e-devices for leisure. Our results may therefore underestimate the effect of pre-bedtime media use on sleep behavior. Prior studies in other sociocultural contexts found that electronic media use near bedtime was associated with later sleep in children and adolescents [28, 29, 58], which could be explained by sleep displacement, increased cognitive arousal, or a circadian-phase delay of sleepiness caused by screen light [31, 63]. In univariate analyses of bedtime reason, adolescents with a set bedtime (by parent or self) went to sleep nearly 40 min earlier. Although this finding did not extend to our model that adjusted for other predictor variables, it is nonetheless consistent with previous studies showing that adolescents go to sleep earlier if they have a parent-set bedtime [15, 61, 64, 65].

Factors that were associated with early wake-up times

The factors that were associated with sleep offset on school mornings were get-ready time, commute time, and school start time. While univariate analyses provided some evidence that student characteristics (age, SES, chronotype) and day of the school week were associated with sleep offset, these relationships were non-significant or marginal in our models that adjusted for other predictor variables. Adolescents who took a long time to get ready in the morning (≥60 min) woke up about 30 min earlier compared with their peers who got ready quickly (<15 min). Similarly, adolescents whose commute time was long (≥60 min) woke up about 30 min earlier compared with those whose commute time was short (<15 min). When treated as continuous variables, a 1-h increase in either get-ready time or commute time was associated with an earlier sleep offset by nearly half an hour. These findings suggest that wake-up time is strongly dependent on how long it takes adolescents to mobilize and reach school. While other studies have not investigated the association of morning get-ready time with sleep offset, our findings for commute time are consistent with previous work showing that adolescents who spend more time on travel wake up earlier and get less sleep on school nights [15, 66–68]. The strength of the relationship was also similar to prior survey studies, where adolescents woke up about 30–40 min earlier if they were categorized as having a long versus short travel time [15, 68].

As expected, wake-up time was associated with school start time. In analyses where school start time was treated as a categorical variable, wake-up times were more than an hour later on days with the latest (09:00 am or later) versus the earliest (before 07:30 am) school start times. These results are consistent with previous studies on effects of delaying school start time, where adolescents woke up 20–60 min later in accordance with the magnitude of change in their school start time [34]. In earlier work conducted in a high school in Singapore, we found that adolescents woke up about 30 min later after their school implemented a 45-min delay in school start time [33]. We also showed that adolescents’ average self-reported wake-up time at 8 different schools in Singapore closely tracked differences in school start time [15]. When school start time was treated as a continuous variable, sleep offset was 27 min later for every hour that school started later. By comparison, a study performed in American adolescents found that actigraphy-estimated sleep offset was 39 min later for every 1-h delay in school start time [69]. Between-study differences in the magnitude of change could be related to characteristics of the study population and covariates that were included in the statistical models. Nonetheless, both studies demonstrate that adolescents wake up substantially later when school starts later.

Factors that were associated with short nocturnal sleep

Student characteristics that were associated with later sleep onset on school nights were also associated with shorter nocturnal sleep, i.e., older age and later chronotype (treated as continuous variables). Sleep duration was about 30 min shorter in older (17 or 18 years) versus younger (13 or 14 years) adolescents, and decreased by about 8 min for every 1-year increase in age. Comparable results were reported in previous cross-sectional studies where sleep duration decreased by about 30–60 min from early to late adolescence [41, 70, 71]. We found that sleep duration decreased by about 10 min for every 1-hour-later difference in chronotype, which is consistent with previous findings showing that late-type students (adolescents or university students) obtained shorter sleep than early-type students when classes were held in the morning [60, 72–76]. In our study, sleep duration was about 30 min shorter on Sunday night compared with other school nights, which may be related to the tendency to go to sleep later on Sunday night compared with other weeknights.

The only bedtime reason that was associated with a shorter sleep duration compared with feeling tired or sleepy, when adjusting for other predictor variables, was homework/studying. Adolescents obtained about 45 min less sleep on nights when their main reason for going to bed was finishing homework/studying versus feeling tired or sleepy. These findings extend prior survey studies showing that adolescents’ sleep duration was about 30 min shorter on school nights when their primary reason for bedtime was homework rather than feeling tired or sleepy [27, 61]. In previous work, we also showed that time spent on homework/studying was inversely related with time-in-bed for sleep [27], suggesting that adolescents’ schoolwork may displace time for sleep. Relatedly, a longitudinal study in adolescents found that more time spent on homework was a risk factor for obtaining inadequate sleep [26]. Together, these studies suggest that pre-bedtime schoolwork is an important determinant of sleep duration on school nights.

In our study, adolescents’ sleep duration did not differ when their main reason for bedtime was using e-devices for leisure rather than going to bed when they felt tired or sleepy. As highlighted earlier, adolescents who used e-devices before bedtime may have reported that they went to sleep when they felt tired or sleepy, even though they would have gone to bed earlier and obtained more sleep if they had done something else. Our study did not address whether using e-devices may delay the onset of sleepiness, which may contribute to later bedtimes and shorter sleep. By comparison, previous studies showed that digital media use at bedtime was associated with shorter sleep in children and adolescents [28–30, 37]. In our study, having a set bedtime was associated with an increase in sleep duration by about 30 min in univariate analyses but not in the linear mixed models that adjusted for other predictor variables. Previous studies showed that adolescents obtained more sleep when their parents were involved in setting their bedtime [15, 61, 64, 65]. Given that parental supervision of bedtime was rare in our study population (3% of all nights), we did not have sufficient data to test whether parent-set bedtimes were associated with longer sleep (parent- and self-set bedtimes were combined). Nonetheless, our findings in univariate analyses are consistent with prior work suggesting that having a planned bedtime may benefit sleep.

School start time was the only morning factor that was significantly associated with sleep duration when adjusting for other factors. Sleep duration was about 50 min longer when school started from 08:30 am to 08:59 am versus 07:30 am to 07:59 am, but did not differ significantly when school started from 08:00 am to 08:29 am. This result is generally consistent with a prior meta-analysis showing that delaying school start time by more than an hour was associated with an increase in total sleep time by about 50 min, whereas smaller delays in school start time were associated with an increase in total sleep time by about 20 min [35]. Comparable results were obtained in a large-scale survey study that showed delaying school start times by 70 min in high school students and 40-60 min in middle school students increased self-reported sleep duration by 45 min and 29 min, respectively [77]. Relatedly, in a Singapore high school, delaying school start time by 45 min from 07:30 am to 08:15 am resulted in an increase in time-in-bed for sleep by about 20 min [33]. These studies show that the gain in sleep duration is dependent on the magnitude of change in school start time [34, 35, 78].

In univariate analyses, we found that sleep duration was about 45 min longer when school started at 09:00 am or later; however, this association was not statistically significant in the linear mixed model that included other predictors. Hence, while adolescents obtained more sleep on days with late school start times (i.e. 09:00 am or later), the underlying reasons may have extended beyond the start time itself in our dataset. In a prior study where adolescents could choose to start school at either 08:00 am or 09:00 am on a daily basis, sleep was about an hour longer on days that students chose the later start time [79]. Hence, there are benefits of late start times on sleep duration within the school week, as also suggested in our prior work in university students with day-to-day variation in their school start time [80].

In analyses where school start time was treated as a continuous variable, we found that sleep duration was 23 min longer for every hour that school started later. This result closely resembles the Fragile Families & Child Wellbeing Study, whereby actigraphy-estimated total daily sleep was 21 min longer for every 1-h delay in school start time [69]. Our findings add to the large body of evidence suggesting that school start time is an important determinant of adolescents’ sleep duration, even when adjusting for student characteristics including age and chronotype, day of the school week, bedtime reason, and time needed to get ready and reach school.

Limitations and considerations

Our study focused on a relatively small set of factors that may impact the timing and duration of adolescents’ sleep. The factors that we chose were largely based on our prior surveys in Singaporean adolescents demonstrating associations between age, chronotype, bedtime reason (parent-set bedtime, homework/studying, digital media use), commute time, and school start time with sleep behavior [15]. We did not investigate other factors related to sleep hygiene that may delay sleep onset, including evening caffeine use or naps [81–83]. We also did not include factors related to the sleeping environment, such as room-sharing, noise, or pre-bedtime light exposure. The student characteristics that we measured were limited to demographic factors (age, sex, ethnicity, SES) and chronotype. We did not consider psychological characteristics or behaviors (e.g. self-regulation or self-control) that may contribute to bedtime procrastination [84, 85], nor did we include measures of mental health (e.g. anxiety or depression) that may contribute to difficulty falling asleep [86].

Although we identified homework/studying as a reason for bedtime that was associated with later and shorter sleep, the underlying explanation for why adolescents were engaging in schoolwork at bedtime is unclear. As highlighted earlier, homework/studying at bedtime could be explained by poor time management or not having enough time to complete the work earlier, e.g., due to other commitments or too much schoolwork. We also did not collect data on whether adolescents were spending time on assigned work, or if they were studying independently to keep up with material they had learned in school. In future work, it will be important to collect more information on why adolescents are doing homework/studying at bedtime, including tracking after-school time-use, workload, and self-regulation skills.

Although our dataset comprised 536 school nights with repeated sampling of self-reported bedtime reason and school start time in the same group of adolescents, the sample size (n = 133) was relatively small for the number of predictor variables that were included in the statistical models. The fixed effect factors in our linear mixed models (8–11 factors per model) were chosen based on theoretical reasons and their associations with sleep behavior in univariate analyses. Our study design was sufficient for identifying variables that were associated with sleep timing and/or duration with relatively large effect sizes (e.g. changes by more than 30 min), but may not have been adequate for detecting weaker statistical associations. Increasing the sample size and the duration of recording per student may reveal other factors that have a meaningful impact on nocturnal sleep behavior beyond those identified in our study (e.g. media use before bedtime or parental supervision). Additionally, it should be emphasized that the effect size for a given categorical predictor variable was measured relative to the chosen reference category for that factor (e.g. homework/studying was compared with feeling tired or sleepy for the factor “bedtime reason”). Effect sizes cannot be compared directly between factors in our study (e.g. between bedtime reason and school start time) because the predictor variables were expressed in different units (e.g. behavior for bedtime reason, and clock time for school start time). However, the relative importance of a given predictor variable can be inferred by its strength of association, i.e., the magnitude of change, in nocturnal sleep behavior.

Implications of the research

Our findings suggest that efforts to improve adolescents’ sleep timing and duration on school nights should seek to minimize homework/studying at bedtime. There were strong associations of homework/studying with sleep onset (50-min delay) and sleep duration (45-min decrease) relative to nights when adolescents went to sleep when they felt tired or sleepy. Hence, interventions that target schoolwork at bedtime could lead to substantial gains in nocturnal sleep. The relationship between homework/studying and sleep may depend on the sociocultural context. Cross-cultural comparisons indicate that adolescents in Singapore spend more time on homework compared with most other countries [87], which is consistent with the emphasis placed on academic achievement. In a previous time-use survey study, we found that adolescents in Singapore spent more time on homework/studying (about 3 h) compared with media use (about 2 h) on school nights [27]; however, only time spent on homework/studying was associated with shorter self-reported sleep [15]. These survey results are consistent with findings in other Asian populations (Korean and Chinese) showing that sleep duration was more strongly associated with time spent on homework compared with media use or sleep hygiene [62, 88–90]. Cross-cultural comparisons within the same school have also provided evidence that Asian students were more likely to go to bed after finishing homework compared with their Caucasian classmates, and they had later bedtimes and shorter sleep [91]. Together, these studies suggest that reducing adolescents’ schoolwork, especially near bedtime, may permit more time for sleep in academically competitive cultural settings. This may require engaging schools and parents to ensure that adolescents have a manageable after-school workload (homework plus other time commitments). In the Singapore context, schools have the flexibility to design their homework policies and can decide whether it may be necessary to adjust the amount of assigned schoolwork. Improving adolescents’ time management skills and increasing parental supervision may also be necessary to avoid starting homework too close to bedtime.

Our study supports health recommendations and position statements from major national organizations (e.g. American Academy of Pediatrics, American Academy of Sleep Medicine, National Sleep Foundation, USA) that high schools should start no earlier than 08:30 am to ensure that adolescents have an opportunity to obtain a healthy amount of sleep [44, 45]. On days that school started before 08:30 am, adolescents in our study obtained about 6.2 h of sleep, which is far short of the recommended 8–10 h for optimal performance and health [47, 48]. By comparison, adolescents obtained about 7 h of sleep when school started at 08:30 am or later, hence partially closing the gap relative to holiday periods when they slept for nearly 8 h per night. School started earlier than 08:30 am on 80.6% of all school days (432 out of 536 days) in our dataset. It is therefore likely that early school start times were a major contributing factor to insufficient sleep in our study population. In Singapore, schools have the autonomy and flexibility to adopt later school start times; however, there are substantial barriers to change associated with parental feedback, school end time, transport, and the potential impact on after-school programs. Nonetheless, as described in the present study, some schools have already adopted a later start time (i.e. 08:30 am or later) on at least one day of the school week. The degree to which this allows adolescents to recover their sleep debt is unclear, however, and having more than one school start time during the week may contribute to sleep irregularity.

There is substantial evidence that permanently delaying school start time can improve adolescents’ sleep and daytime functioning. Most studies have shown that later school start times are associated with later wake-up times, hence resulting in longer nocturnal sleep [33, 34, 69, 77]. By extending sleep later in the morning, adolescents may also awaken at a more biologically appropriate circadian phase relative to their melatonin rhythm. Importantly, delaying school start time has been shown to decrease daytime sleepiness and improve mood [32, 34, 35]. In previous work in a Singapore secondary school, we showed that after a 45-min delay in school start time (07:30 am to 08:15 am), larger improvements in sleep duration among individual students were associated with larger improvements in alertness and mental well-being [33]. Even 9 months after the change in school start time, adolescents reported lower levels of sleepiness, fewer problems staying awake during the day, feeling more refreshed after waking on school days, and fewer depressive symptoms. Comparable results were obtained for a residential high school in Hong Kong, where a 1-h delay in school start time (07:30 am to 08:30 am) was associated with improved sleep duration and sleep quality, lower daytime sleepiness, and fewer symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress for at least 7 months after the change in school start time [92]. When viewed in the context of the present study, such findings suggest that delaying school start times may be a key strategy for improving adolescents’ sleep and wellbeing in sociocultural contexts where short sleep and high academic pressure are the norm.

Conclusion

Most adolescents in our study did not obtain a healthy amount of sleep on school nights. We investigated several modifiable factors that may contribute to inadequate sleep. Adolescents went to sleep substantially later if their main reason for going to bed was finishing homework rather than going to bed when they felt tired or sleepy. Additionally, adolescents woke up earlier if they took more time to get ready for school, had a longer commute, or had an earlier school start time. Modifiable factors that were strongly associated with sleep duration were homework/studying at bedtime and school start time. Our findings suggest that minimizing schoolwork at bedtime and delaying school start time are potential strategies for improving sleep duration in adolescents.
