Construction and application of an intelligent tutoring system for resident physicians’ medical record writing based on large language models
Authors: Lin Yang, Dan Zhao, Jinyu Zhao, Hao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop and evaluate a large language model (LLM)-based intelligent tutoring system for medical record writing in residency training and to assess its effectiveness in improving documentation quality and documentation-related learning outcomes. Methods: A single-center quasi-experimental controlled before-and-after educational evaluation was conducted from January to December 2025 at a tertiary teaching hospital. Residents (PGY1-PGY3) were allocated to the intervention cohort (LLM-based tutoring system plus conventional teaching) or the control cohort (conventional teaching alone) according to existing teaching arrangements. The system used pseudonymized historical real patient cases to provide structured documentation tasks, automated multidimensional assessment, and personalized feedback. The primary outcome was the overall medical record writing score (0-100). Secondary outcomes included domain-specific documentation scores, documentation error rates, completion time, and resident satisfaction. Statistical analyses included t tests, chi-square tests, effect-size estimation with 95% confidence intervals, and multivariable linear regression. Results: A total of 240 residents were included in the analysis (120 per cohort), with comparable baseline characteristics. After 6 months, the intervention cohort demonstrated significantly greater improvement in overall medical record writing scores compared with the control cohort (between-group mean difference in change, 9.0 points; 95% CI, 7.5–10.5; Cohen’s d = 1.56; P < 0.001). Improvements were also observed in documentation domains related to history taking, diagnostic analysis, differential diagnosis, and treatment planning. Documentation error rates decreased significantly in the intervention cohort, and residents reported high satisfaction with the system. Conclusions: The LLM-based intelligent tutoring system was associated with improved medical record writing quality, documentation efficiency, and resident satisfaction in a residency training setting. These findings support its use as a scalable supplement to conventional documentation training while requiring further validation for broader educational outcomes beyond documentation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09888-z.

Introduction

Standardized training for resident physicians is a key stage in the transition from medical students to qualified clinicians [1, 2]. Proper and standardized medical record writing is a fundamental skill requirement throughout residency training [3, 4]. A qualified medical record must comply with medical standards and support continuity of care. It also documents the resident’s history taking, diagnostic analysis, differential diagnosis, and treatment planning. Therefore, medical record writing training is an important entry point for improving documentation quality and for making residents’ documented clinical assessment and management planning more explicit in written records.

Research subjects and methods

Research subjects

Inclusion criteria: ① Residents in standardized training in internal medicine and surgery programs, class of 2025 (PGY1–PGY3); ② Completed at least 6 months of clinical rotations; ③ Ability to write inpatient medical records independently; ④ Complete routine pre/post documentation assessment records were available. Exclusion criteria: ① Completion of or withdrawal from training during the study period; ② Total leave exceeding 3 months; ③ Previous use of similar intelligent systems; ④ Withdrawal from the study midway.

Cohort allocation and intervention measures

After stratifying by enrollment time and training year, residents were allocated to the intervention cohort or the control cohort according to existing teaching arrangements rather than by individual randomization. The intervention cohort used the LLM medical record writing intelligent tutoring system in addition to standard education, while the control cohort received standard education alone.

Data sources for this evaluation included routine teaching records, standardized medical record writing scores before and after the 6-month intervention, documentation error audits, writing-time records, and the post-intervention resident satisfaction survey. These records were collected within the residency training program before de-identification and analysis for this manuscript.

Components of the intelligent tutoring system

The system is based on real historical cases and the case studies that residents are expected to learn and master according to the “Standardized Training Content and Standards for Residents (2022 Edition)”. After de-identification, a case database was constructed, providing a structured medical record writing interface. The system integrates an evaluation form for the quality of inpatient medical records and LLM semantic analysis to conduct multidimensional automated evaluations of records, generate real-time personalized feedback and improvement suggestions, and perform multidimensional analysis of the corresponding data. Evaluation indicators

The primary outcome was the total medical record writing score. Secondary outcomes included domain-specific documentation scores, the incidence of common documentation errors, writing time, and trainee satisfaction. The documentation domains included history taking, diagnostic analysis and differential diagnosis, and treatment planning; no independent validated clinical reasoning instrument was used in this study.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using Python. Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and between-group comparisons were conducted using t tests. Categorical data were analyzed using chi-square tests. For the primary outcome, between-cohort differences in change scores were reported as mean differences with 95% confidence intervals, and standardized effect sizes were estimated using Cohen’s d. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with score improvement. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of baseline characteristics

A total of 247 resident physicians were enrolled, with 7 participants withdrawing during follow-up, resulting in 240 individuals being included in the analysis. Among them, 120 were in the intelligent tutoring system group and 120 were in the traditional teaching control group. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, gender, educational background, training year (PGY1–PGY3), specialty composition, and baseline medical record writing scores (P > 0.05 for all), indicating comparability (see Table 1).

Changes in total scores of medical record writing

Before the intervention, the total medical record writing scores for the two groups of residents were (68.4 ± 6.7) points and (67.9 ± 7.1) points, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.68).

After the 6-month educational intervention, the total score for the intelligent tutoring system cohort increased to (82.6 ± 5.9) points, representing an improvement of (14.2 ± 6.1) points from baseline. The control cohort’s score increased to (73.1 ± 6.8) points, representing an improvement of (5.2 ± 5.4) points from baseline. The between-cohort mean difference in change scores was 9.0 points (95% CI, 7.5–10.5), corresponding to a large standardized effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.56). The difference in improvement between cohorts was statistically significant (t = 7.89, P < 0.001). After the intervention, total scores also differed significantly between cohorts (P < 0.001) (see Table 2).

Comparison of medical record writing quality across dimensions

After the intervention, the intelligent tutoring system group scored significantly higher on several core dimensions of medical record writing compared with the control group, including completeness of medical history collection [(14.8 ± 1.6) points vs. (12.9 ± 1.8) points, P < 0.001], diagnostic analysis and differential diagnosis [(17.2 ± 1.9) points vs. (14.3 ± 2.1) points, P < 0.001], and the reasonableness of treatment plans [(16.9 ± 1.7) points vs. (14.8 ± 1.9) points, P < 0.001]. Between-group differences in medical record formatting standards, physical examination, and auxiliary examination records were also statistically significant, but of a relatively smaller magnitude (P < 0.05) (see Table 3).

Incidence of common medical record writing errors

Before the intervention, there was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of common medical record writing errors between the two groups. After the intervention, the incidence of missing key historical elements in the intelligent tutoring system group decreased from 32.1% to 10.7%, the incidence of insufficient diagnostic basis decreased from 41.1% to 14.3%, and the incidence of incomplete differential diagnosis decreased from 46.4% to 16.1%, all of which were statistically significant (P < 0.01). Although there was a slight decrease in the incidence of these errors in the control group, the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05) (see Table 4).

Writing efficiency and learning experience

Compared with the control group, the average time required by residents in the intelligent tutoring system group to write a complete inpatient medical record was shortened from (48.3 ± 9.6) min to (39.7 ± 8.4) min, while the control group showed no significant change [(47.5 ± 10.1) min to (45.9 ± 9.8) min, P = 0.21]. The satisfaction survey showed that 92.9% of residents considered the system “helpful” or “very helpful”, 90.2% believed the feedback was timely and targeted, and 87.5% were willing to continue using the system in future clinical learning (see Table 6).

Multifactor regression analysis

Using the change in the total medical record writing score as the dependent variable, multiple linear regression analysis was conducted with variables including use of the intelligent tutoring system, training year, baseline score, and educational level. The results showed that using the intelligent tutoring system was an independent factor associated with improvement in medical record writing performance (β = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.31–0.65, P < 0.001), and the baseline score was negatively correlated with the magnitude of improvement (β = -0.29, P = 0.004). Training year and educational level were not significantly associated with the outcome (see Table 7).

Discussion

This study developed and evaluated an intelligent tutoring system for resident medical record writing based on large language models. The findings indicate that the system was associated with improved documentation quality, fewer documentation errors [5], shorter writing time, and high resident satisfaction.

Documentation-related educational value of the system

The intervention cohort showed significantly greater improvement in total medical record writing scores (14.2 points vs. 5.2 points, P < 0.001) compared with the control cohort. The largest gains occurred in documentation domains related to diagnostic analysis, differential diagnosis, and reasonableness of diagnostic and treatment plans. These findings indicate that repeated structured documentation practice and multidimensional feedback can improve the written representation of clinical assessment and management planning [6, 7]. Because this study did not use an independent validated instrument for broader trainee learning outcomes beyond documentation, the results should be interpreted as documentation-related educational outcomes rather than direct evidence of broader clinical competence.

Synergistic effect of standardized evaluation and personalized guidance

The system established a standardized evaluation framework based on the “Content and Standards for Resident Standardized Training (2022 Edition)“ [8] and achieved multidimensional quality assessment of medical records through semantic analysis. In addition, personalized feedback based on error types and learning trajectories allowed each trainee to receive targeted improvement plans. This “standardized evaluation + personalized guidance” model may help standardize training quality while accounting for individual differences [9], and may promote residents’ practice of question-driven learning, which aligns with modern medical education concepts [10–13].

Balance between learning efficiency and clinical practice

The intervention cohort showed significantly reduced medical record writing time (from 48.3 min to 39.7 min, P < 0.01) while documentation quality improved. This finding suggests that the intelligent tutoring system may improve learning efficiency for structured documentation tasks. Under high clinical work pressure, the system provided a safe and repeatable training environment in which residents could practice writing and receive targeted feedback [7, 14]. Satisfaction surveys showed that 92.9% of trainees considered the system helpful, and 90.2% recognized the timeliness and relevance of feedback, indicating good user acceptance and potential as an autonomous learning tool [13, 15].

Documentation training and educational significance

This study suggests that the LLM-based intelligent tutoring system can serve as a useful teaching support tool for standardized residency training. Its standardized evaluation, real-time feedback, and personalized guidance are consistent with trends in digital medical education. The system may help supervising physicians assign documentation tasks that match residents’ learning stages and provide targeted feedback more efficiently [7, 10, 15]. Residents trained with both the LLM-based intelligent tutoring system and conventional teaching demonstrated better documentation performance, which may support improvements in medical record writing quality.

Conclusion

The intelligent tutoring system for resident medical record writing based on large language models was associated with improved medical record writing quality, documentation efficiency, and resident satisfaction. These results suggest that the system may provide a scalable supplement to conventional residency documentation training. Future studies should use randomized or multicenter designs and validated assessment instruments to determine whether documentation improvements translate into broader trainee learning outcomes.
