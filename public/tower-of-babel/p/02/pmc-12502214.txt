Effectiveness of “colored teeth and digital evaluation” on preclinical tooth preparation training
Authors: Yanglin Zeng, Ya Wang, Bo Dong, Liu Liu, Yubin Cao, Dingming Huang, Shida Wang, Qinghua Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIn dental education, it is challenging for novices to practice independently after watching the teacher demonstrating. Additionally, feedback during the training process tends to be untimely, subjective, and varied. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of integrating a colored model tooth with a digital assessment system to enhance cavity preparation skills, as well as the role of this approach in identifying challenges during practice and students' perceptions of their learning experience.Material and methodsA total of 464 fourth-year undergraduate dental students participated in two 4-h lessons. Students (n = 231) from the 2023 academic year received traditional training (traditional group), which involved using standard model teeth of a single color with instructions solely from the instructor. In contrast, students (n = 233) from the 2024 academic year comprised the digital group. After observing a demonstration conducted by the instructor, participants in the digital group prepared one standard model tooth and engaged in peer evaluation. Subsequently, they prepared a colored tooth to better understand the cavity outline and practiced incorporating feedback obtained from both digital evaluations and instructors. At the conclusion of this process, all students completed an examination with standard model teeth as well as self-evaluation of their clinical skills. Furthermore, the perception of students in digital group towards both the colored tooth and digital evaluation was assessed through a questionnaire. Statistical analyses of scores were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, while questionnaire data were presented as percentages and means.ResultsPeer assessment scores in the digital group exceeded those of digital self-evaluations across seven out of eight rating criteria, indicating a limited capacity for self-assessment among novices. Following the implementation of colored tooth and digital evaluation methods, students in the digital group achieved higher exam scores compared to their counterparts in the traditional group (p < 0.05) and demonstrated significantly greater clinical confidence than those in the traditional cohort. The majority of students provided positive feedback regarding both the colored tooth and digital evaluation systems. A substantial 76% expressed a preference for utilizing these evaluation tools as guidance, while 73% indicated that they found the colored teeth and digital evaluation system beneficial as a complement to teacher instruction during practice.ConclusionsThe phased training framework that integrates colored teeth with digital evaluation enhanced training effectiveness and students’ confidence in the preclinical tooth preparation training. These educational tools are potential for broader implementation within dental education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07963-5.Keywords: Colored tooth, Digital evaluation, Pre-clinical education, Cavity preparation

Introduction

Operative dentistry is a highly skilled discipline usually involving invasive and irreversible procedures, which requires dental practitioners to create geometric shapes in small dimensions safely and effectively, with limited workspace and visibility [1, 2].

Simulation-based preclinical training serves as a critical foundation for dental students to develop psychomotor competencies prior to patient care, particularly given the irreversible nature of most dental interventions [3].

Kovacs suggested a four-step procedural skill training paradigm for practical guides on teaching medical procedures. According to Kovacs, students will successively go through “acquiring requisite cognitive knowledge through didactic education (learn), observation of the procedure (see), deliberately practicing the procedure on a simulator (practice) and finally performing the procedures on patients (do) [4]. Expanding this model, Sawyer et al. developed an evidence-based six-step pedagogical approach- “Learn, See, Practice, Prove, Do, and Maintain” to optimize procedural competence and patient safety [5]. Dental preclinical training falls within the realm of “learn” to “practice”, and then the students undergo assessment on a simulator to ensure that procedural competency has been achieved, before treating the live patient [5]. Dental preclinical curricula predominantly focus on the initial three phases, employing simulator-based competency assessments as gatekeepers for clinical patient care [5].

As a core preclinical competency, tooth preparation involves controlled tissue removal to facilitate restorative rehabilitation while preserving anatomical integrity [2, 6]. Traditional training follows a three-step model: theoretical knowledge delivery via slide presentations ("learn"), live step-by-step demonstrations with verbal narration ("see"), and supervised practice until competency is achieved through teacher’s assessment [7, 8].

Optimal tooth cavity preparation requires dental students to possess a foundational understanding of both the geometric configuration and three-dimensional architecture of proposed cavity designs. However, novice practitioners often can’t identify what is good or poor work, due to their underdeveloped self-assessment capabilities [9]. Feedback is considered essential for cultivating students' evaluative judgment skills, enabling them to become self-regulated lifelong learners [9]. In conventional training, instructors are expected to provide feedback on students’ tooth preparation during practice, after which students correct their errors and gradually make progresses. However, due to the limited number of teachers, students frequently lack timely feedback. Additionally, traditional assessment methods, which rely primarily on instructors’ visual evaluation, are prone to subjective variability, potentially undermining students’ self-judgment and confidence during the practice [7, 10, 11]. Furthermore, for beginners, oral guidance from teachers may be overly abstract, making it difficult for some students to fully grasp the instructions. Therefore, more objective and intuitive feedback regarding the tooth preparation needs to be provided. Recent advancements in dental education have witnessed integration of digital evaluation systems into preclinical curricula, such as PrepCheck, Dental Teacher, CPSES, RDTES, DCARER and so on [2, 10, 12, 13]. These systems are vivid and objective to improve educational satisfaction, which are also beneficial for improving students' performance [14–16]. These systems can also reduce faculty members' working hours by providing timely and objective feedback, which plays a positive role in optimizing preclinical training outcomes [3, 6, 8]. However, existing assessment systems primarily present three-dimensional topographic maps by comparing prepared teeth with standard model, yet lack detailed quantitative parameters. This deficiency renders it difficult for students to fully understand the results and areas for improvement [17, 18].

In this study, we designed a tooth model featuring a colored target region to guide tooth preparation and developed a digital evaluation system incorporating both 3D topographical mapping and detailed quantitative parameters of cavity preparation characteristics. The colored tooth model and digital evaluation system were integrated into the training of Class II cavity preparation to: 1) identify challenges in students' practical performance; 2) assess the effectiveness of this novel teaching model in facilitating their transition from the observational "see" stage to the operational "practice" stage; and 3) explore students’ perceptions of their learning experience.

Methods

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (WCHSIRB-D-2024–373). The schema of this study was shown in Fig. 1.

The schema of this study.

Design and production of the colored tooth

The colored model tooth was developed based on the #19 (#36 in the FDI numbering system) standard model tooth (A5SAN-500, Nissin). Standard reference Amalgam Class II cavities were prepared by an experienced dentist on the #19 standard model tooth. Three dimensional scan data of both prepared and original #19 teeth were exported in STL (standard tessellation language) format (A5SAN-500, NISSIN). The prepared teeth were fitted to the #19 standard model tooth image data to design colored tooth models for Class II cavity preparation. The target area—defined as the region requiring tissue removal—was colored red, with the remaining anatomical structures rendered in white (Fig. 2A). These models were then produced using injection molding techniques.

The colored tooth and digital evaluation software. (A) tooth number, (B) colored tooth, and (C) digital evaluation software.

Participants

This study was conducted at the West China School of Stomatology, Sichuan University. This study included fourth-year undergraduate dental students in the 2023 and 2024 academic year who had never prepared Class II cavity and excluded individuals who did not participate in the entire study and those who had received similar training. All 231 undergraduate dental students (110 male and 121 female) in the 2023 academic year, and all 233 dental students (109 male and 124 female) in the 2024 academic year enrolled in this study. Informed consent to participate in this study was obtained from all participants. All students received initial training in Class II cavity preparation in this study through a structured curriculum involving two weekly 4-h lessons.

Training of the control group

Students in the 2023 academic year, as a traditional group, received traditional training with only teachers’ feedback. Following faculty demonstrations, students prepared the standard model tooth in a simulated phantom head. Upon completing the training, they completed the dental preparation exam and answered the questionnaire 1 to assess their perception of skills acquisitions. The questionnaire 1 item—"I have mastered the skill of tooth preparation", rating in grades (Strongly Agree, Agree, Neutral, Disagree, Strongly Disagree).

Training of the digital group

Students in the 2024 academic year, as a digital group, practiced with the guidance of colored tooth and the feedback of the teacher and digital evaluation system. Firstly, students prepared a standard tooth at the beginning of the course and then made peer evaluation by a different student according to the criteria of the preparation (Table 1) in Questionnaire to show their ability to assess. Fully meeting the standard was evaluated as “Yes”, while not within the reference range was evaluated as “NO”. After peer evaluation, they submitted this tooth for digital evaluation as a comparison.

Then, each student was provided with a colored tooth model as a guidance for Class II cavity preparation training, with the red area of the tooth indicated tissue need to be removed. Later, students practiced Class II cavity preparation with standard teeth model in a simulated phantom head (Dr.Morita, Japan). In the training of preparation, students can use the dental preparation evaluation software (instructor-student ratio:1:10) at any time, and they must use it every session. They had completed training in the use of an intraoral scanner (SHINING3DDENTAL, China) and evaluation software before performing the task. Also, students could get the instructor's feedback.

In the end, students completed the preparation of a standard tooth model as an exam, which was graded by the teacher. Then, they also answered the Questionnaire 1 to assess their perception of skills acquisitions like the traditional group. Also, students were asked to complete Questionnaire 2 (Table 2) to assess perception of the effectiveness of the color tooth and the digital evaluation system, including a free-text section designed to collect students' perspectives on the innovative teaching aids through open-ended questions.

The digital evaluation system for tooth preparation

Before evaluation, a prepared #19 model tooth and an unprepared #19 model tooth were scanned by an intraoral digital scanner (SHINING3DDENTAL, China). The scanning data were exported in Polygon File Format (PLY) and then imported into the evaluation software (Fig. 2A) to measure the cavity parameters.

The prepared model teeth also should be scanned to exported in Polygon File Format, and then imported into the evaluation software. This software was specifically designed for tooth preparation assessment by our team and developed by a domestic company (Chengdu Zhuoma Technology Co., Ltd.). The evaluation process involved manual orientation adjustment of the prepared teeth to approximate the alignment of the reference Class II cavity model tooth, followed by automatic 3D registration using crown morphology as a superimposition reference. Within seconds, the system generated quantitative assessments includes: (1) color-coded deviation maps on 3D models indicating over-preparation (positive deviations) and under-preparation (negative deviations), and (2) the text showed the preparation parameters and reference parameters (Table 1) with corresponding preparation grades and scores (Fig. 2B). The grading system categorized preparation quality based on deviation percentages from reference values: Grade A (≤ 10% deviation), Grade B (10–20%), Grade C (20–30%), and Grade D (> 30%).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California USA, www.graphpad.com). Descriptive statistics were calculated for the characteristics of questionnaires. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare performance scores between the traditional group and the digital group. A p-value under 0.05 was set as statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of peer assessment and digital evaluation

We collected peer evaluation of digital group (n = 233) and the digital evaluation (n = 226) for the first preparation of standard tooth (Fig. 3). The results showed that more than 90% of the tasks were deemed to meet the feature of the cavity in peer evaluation. However, the digital evaluation showed that only the shape of the proximal box met the criteria in 90% of the works. Other parameters showed varying compliance rates: the smoothness of the lateral wall was achieved in 76.55% of preparations, the width in the proximal box in 57.96%, the width in the gingival wall in 56.64%, the dovetail shape in 55.75%, the distance from the gingival wall to CEJ in 43.36%, and the dovetail width in 35.84% (Fig. 3).

Comparison between peer evaluation and digital evaluation.

Enhanced cavity preparation ability through “colored teeth and digital evaluation” instructional assistance

Quantitative analysis of final assessments on standard tooth revealed significantly higher scores in the digital group (85.85 ± 7.791) compared to the traditional group (82.73 ± 8.465) (p < 0.05), demonstrating the efficacy of color guidance combined with digital feedback in developing cavity preparation skills (Fig. 4A).

The comparison between traditional group and digital group. (A) Score comparison, and (B) clinical confidence comparison.

Questionnaire 1 were obtained from 186 students in the 2023 academic year (questionnaire recovery rate = 80.52%), and 233 students in 2024 academic year (questionnaire recovery rate = 100.00%). Questionnaire data indicated stronger self-confidence among digital group students (n = 233) versus traditional group students (n = 186). In the traditional group, 33.87% of the students strongly agreed and 59.14% of the students agreed that they had mastered the cavity preparation skill. However, 83.69% of the students in the digital group strongly agreed and 15.88% agreed that they had mastered the preparation skill, while only one (0.43%) disagreed (Fig. 4B).

Assessment of the colored tooth and digital evaluation system

One hundred and thirty-five valid questionnaires were collected from students for Questionnaire 2, and the questionnaire recovery rate was 57.94%. As for the colored tooth, students showed a positive attitude toward it. A total of 92% of students strongly agreed, and 7% agreed that the colored tooth was helpful for cavity preparation (Fig. 5A). Most of the students have positive comments on this digital evaluation system. Specifically, 92% regarded the digital evaluation results as easy to understand, 95% thought these results helped them identify insufficiencies, and 92% considered the system helpful for improving their Class II cavity preparation skills. Regarding scoring fairness, 60% of students expressed support for the digital evaluation system (Fig. 5B).

Assessment of the colored tooth and digital evaluation system. (A) Evaluation of colored teeth, and (B) Evaluation of digital evaluation.

Colored tooth and digital evaluation application preference

In addition to surveying students' assessment of the evaluation system, the preference for a digital evaluation system was also investigated. As for the application stage of the evaluation system, 35% of students were in favor of the novice level, 38% of students were in favor of the advanced beginner level, and 27% of students were in favor of the competent level (Fig. 6A). For function preference, the results showed that 76% of students preferred the evaluation system for process guidance in training, while only 4% of students preferred it for test scoring, and 20% of students preferred it for process guidance and test scoring (Fig. 6B).

Colored tooth and digital evaluation application preference. (A) Stage preference of digital evaluation, (B) Function preference of digital evaluation, and (C) Teaching model preference.

Besides, in terms of training mode preference, 73.33% of students chose “Teacher + Digital evaluation + Colored tooth” as their preferred teaching mode (Fig. 6C).

Free-text for the evaluation system

Students expressed their opinions concerning the digital evaluation system, which were collected to be analyzed. The most significant advantage of the evaluation system was that it could intuitively display the cavity preparation, and objectively measure the parameters of the cavity (20.74%). Therefore, it was helpful for students to recognize their strengths and weaknesses in preparation so that they can make targeted improvements. However, it also had some shortcomings, especially too strict standard (16.30%), so students preferred it as a teaching aid rather than a grading tool. While, advice on improvements of the digital evaluation system included: (1) adding equipment to improve efficiency (3.70%); (2) teacher assistance in explaining the evaluation result (2.96%); (3) application in other types of dental preparation (0.74%).

Discussion

Assessment and feedback are crucial for developing students' evaluative judgment and promoting self-regulated, lifelong learning [9, 19]. However, we found that novice learners consistently overestimated their tooth preparation quality compared to objective digital assessments, suggesting limited self-assessment ability among novices [20, 21]. The observed overestimations indicated that novices may not independently recognize their errors due to limited knowledge and clinical experience, underscoring the need for guidance and feedback [22, 23]. To address these pedagogical challenges, we implemented a dual-modality intervention incorporating colored tooth and digital evaluation within preclinical training frameworks.

In traditional education, students initially acquired theoretical knowledge in class and observed faculty demonstrations of tooth preparation to reinforce conceptual understanding, subsequently attempting to independently apply these techniques during practice, which presented substantial challenges for novices during early stages. Students have to engage in extensive trial-and-error practice, progressively dampening their initial confidence during the process. Therefore, attention should be paid to strengthening students' cognition of the Class II cavity morphology during the initial practice phase, thereby facilitating their transition from “see” stage” to “practice” stage. The colored tooth implemented in current study demonstrated enhanced stereognostic perception through three-dimensional tactile-visual integration, offering advantages over conventional two-dimensional schematic representations in textbooks and didactic presentations [24]. This tactile-visual reinforcement significantly enhanced learners' comprehension of cavity geometry and critical preparation landmarks, probably improving the ability of self-assessment [24–26]. In this study, students gave positive comments on the integration of colored tooth in preclinical training. This aligns with our previous study that colored teeth could increase students' clinical confidence [26].

In the deliberate practice phase, students traditionally acquire tooth preparation skills through face-to-face teaching involving direct faculty-student interaction. However, feedback in this conventional mode was often untimely, subjective, inconsistent, and abstract, potentially undermining teaching efficiency and learning outcomes [6, 7, 10, 11, 27]. To address this, a digital evaluation system was implemented, distinguishing itself from existing assessment tools that rely solely on 3D topographical maps to visualize over-preparation or under-preparation. By integrating evaluations of cavity features with precise measurements, the system delivers real-time, objective feedback, enabling students to gain immediate, actionable insights into their performance during the improvement process. This approach enhances students’ ability to regulate preparation techniques effectively with the average score increasing from 82.73 to 85.85. Also, previous study indicated that a significant improvement in the total scores of the digital group than those of the traditional group [28]. However, several studies showed that there was no significant difference between the scores of the digital group and the traditional group, but students still expressed a great demand for digital aids in dental education [6, 12, 16]. Additional, the use of colored teeth models combined with digital assessment enhanced students' confidence, demonstrating subjective effectiveness of this educational approach.

Besides, the self-directed application of this digital platform during skill acquisition actively cultivates autonomous learning behaviors while facilitating competency-based self-evaluation mechanisms, which exerts a positive impact on preclinical training [10, 12]. However, this study unfortunately lacked data on self-assessment and longitudinal data for clinical operation. In the future, researches can be conducted to explore the relationship between clinical courses and preclinical training with these aids or without, while also examining self-assessment abilities.

This assessment system was designed as a teaching auxiliary tool to provide supplementary feedback and guidance alongside teacher feedback, rather than generating scores or replacing teachers. This design enriches the feedback, alleviates the challenges posed by high student-to-teacher ratios, and reduces faculty workload [12].

Furthermore, the digital evaluation system was based on data from the oral scanner, a technology widely adopted in the dental field, rendering the system readily accessible, mobile, and cost-effective. Moreover, the system can also be applied to the evaluation of other tooth preparations such as metal crowns, highlighting its broad application scope and high feasibility. On the other hand, this evaluation system also guided teachers to know the difficulties in preparation by analyzing data from the evaluation software, so they could allocate more attention to areas where students exhibit weaker proficiency.

The majority of students gave positive comments on the digital evaluation system. However, some students perceived the evaluation results as inconsistent with their expectations and unfair, likely due to their lenient assessment criteria conflicting with the system's rigidity, where even a 0.1 mm deviation was deemed unacceptable [27]. Nevertheless, the evaluation system undeniably demonstrates greater objectivity than teacher assessments. Most students preferred using it for guidance during training, which aligns with our expectations. Interestingly, a subset of participants (20%) advocated for dual implementation of the system in both training and scoring, reflecting growing demand for objective evaluation in dental education. As for the application stage of the evaluation system, students had different tendencies. The evaluation system's functionality aligns with learners' developmental progression: providing foundational guidance during imitation stages (initial), enabling skill refinement during proficiency development (intermediate), and supporting precision optimization at mastery levels (advanced) [5].

For preference for teaching models, 73.33% of students favored the integrated “Teacher + Digital evaluation + Colored tooth” approach, indicating the continued necessity of teacher involvement in learning process. While the colored tooth and digital evaluation system enhance skill acquisition, teachers should maintain primary instructional responsibility [2, 15, 18]. This aligns with prior research highlighting that assessment systems, which primarily measure quantitative performance, cannot replicate the nuanced "feedback in action" inherent in teacher’s instruction [12]. The "feedback in action" was define as “feedback or correction sequences occurring in context at the time that an error is made” [29].

In the free text, some students also mentioned that there might be difficulties in understanding the results, and they expressed a desire for teachers to assist in interpreting results and offering “feedback in action”. These findings reinforced that digital evaluation cannot replace teachers, but is suitable as a complementary teaching tool to improve teaching efficiency.

The assessment system also exhibited notable limitations. It lacked the capability to evaluate critical components of the clinical preparation process, such as sitting posture, fulcrum stabilization, and potential damage to adjacent teeth. It was also undeniable that the scanning of the prepared tooth was time-consuming, which may increase students’ workload. Lastly, current digital evaluations performed poorly in providing "feedback in action", which should be focused on in the future.

Conclusion

This study introduced a hybrid pedagogical approach that integrates conventional instruction with self-developed colored tooth model and digital evaluation systems for stepwise guidance. This approach enhanced training effectiveness and students’ confidence in the preclinical tooth preparation training, primarily through two mechanisms: providing visual-spatial cues via colored guidance that facilitated the transition from the "observation" to "practice" phases, and refining operation skills through timely objective digital evaluation in conjunction with traditional assessment. Significantly, this pedagogical framework demonstrated adaptability across diverse operative dental training scenarios, suggesting potential for systematic integration into dental training programs.
