Development and validation of a machine learning model for individualised meropenem dosing in CRRT patients
Authors: Dandan Li, Zhenzhen Qiao, Xiangzun Xiong, Qin Zhang, Yao He, Zhijie Yu, Xi Zheng, Xiaoyuan Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptimizing meropenem dosing in critically ill patients undergoing continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) remains challenging due to significant pharmacokinetic variability. This study aimed to develop and validate a machine learning (ML)-based clinical decision support system (CDSS) for individualizing meropenem dosing in this population. A large-scale virtual cohort (n = 48,000) was generated using a published population pharmacokinetic (POPPK) model to simulate diverse dosing regimens. Various ML models were trained to predict the probability of achieving two stringent pharmacodynamic (PD) targets: 100% fT>MIC and 100% fT>4×MIC. Feature importance was interpreted using SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) analysis. External validation was conducted using clinical CRRT patient data, and predictive performance was compared with that of the traditional POPPK model. The top-performing model was deployed into a Streamlit-based web CDSS, named “MerDose” for clinical use. The ML models exhibited excellent predictive performance on the simulated test set, with accuracy and F1 scores predominantly above 0.95. Notably, in the external validation cohort, the Gradient Boosting (GB) model robustly outperformed the conventional POPPK model across stringent pharmacodynamic targets (100% fT>MIC and 100% fT>4×MIC), achieving AUC values of 0.884 and 0.929, respectively, versus 0.823 and 0.871 for the POPPK model. Interpretation via SHAP analysis confirmed that the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), dosing interval, dose amount, CRRT clearance (CLCRRT), and creatinine clearance (CRCL) were the primary determinants of target attainment. This high-performing, interpretable GB model was subsequently integrated into the “MeroDose” CDSS. This web-based tool translates the model’s predictive capability into clinically actionable decision support for initial dose selection. The ML-driven CDSS ‘MerDose’ provides a clinically applicable tool for optimizing meropenem dosing in CRRT patients, potentially overcoming limitations of conventional approaches. Introduction

Sepsis represents a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients1. Approximately half of critically ill patients develop acute kidney injury (AKI), and about 20% require continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT)2,3. β-lactam antibiotics, particularly meropenem, are frequently administered as empirical therapy for sepsis in this population4. However, optimizing antibiotic dosing in critically ill patients receiving CRRT presents a major therapeutic challenge due to profound pharmacokinetic (PK) variability5.

Critically ill patients undergoing CRRT frequently exhibit pronounced variability in pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD)6,7. Such variability is attributable primarily to increased volume of distribution, altered plasma protein binding, and impaired endogenous clearance secondary to organ dysfunction, each of which can substantially influence systemic drug exposure8,9. CRRT further amplifies this variability by altering drug clearance, which depends on the CRRT modality, operational parameters, and hemofilter characteristics, including filter type, membrane pore size, and drug adsorption10–12.

The pharmacodynamic driver of meropenem efficacy is the duration of time that unbound plasma concentrations remain above the minimum inhibitory concentration (fT>MIC)13. In critically ill patients, more stringent PK/PD targets—such as 100% fT>MIC or even 100% fT>4×MIC—are advocated to maximize therapeutic efficacy and mitigate the emergence of resistance14,15. During CRRT, rapid meropenem clearance, especially in high-flow settings, can make maintaining therapeutic concentrations challenging. Ex vivo investigations demonstrated that meropenem undergoes rapid extraction within CRRT circuits, yielding an average recovery of merely 2.36%, compared with 93.0% in control conditions16. This accelerated clearance often necessitates higher or more frequent dosing regimens to attain pharmacodynamic targets, especially in patients with augmented renal clearance or those receiving high-intensity CRRT17. However, the potential for toxicity, particularly neurotoxicity and nephrotoxicity, necessitates careful consideration. Adverse events have been reported when trough concentrations exceed 45 mg/L17. Collectively, these factors contribute to marked variability in drug exposure among CRRT patients, thereby complicating the optimization of individualized dosing strategies in this population.

Although conventional population pharmacokinetic (POPPK) models provide insights into dosing determinants, their predictive accuracy and precision at the individual level remain limited18–21. Hence, the development of advanced approaches capable of integrating patient- and treatment-related variables to inform optimal, individualized dosing is critical. In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a promising tool in clinical pharmacotherapy22,23, with the capacity to capture complex nonlinear relationships within multimodal clinical data and to surpass conventional models in predictive accuracy24–28. The flexibility of ML approaches in accommodating diverse influencing factors renders them particularly well-suited to addressing the complexities of individualized dosing in critically ill CRRT patients.

The present study aimed to develop a clinical decision support system, “MeroDose” integrating machine learning with patient characteristics, dosing regimens, and CRRT parameters, to support clinicians in optimizing meropenem dosing strategies.

Methods

Machine learning dataset construction

A large-scale virtual dataset was constructed using the meropenem POPPK model reported by Peng et al. (Model 1)29. The corresponding model parameters are detailed in Table 1. To capture clinical heterogeneity and avoid overly constrained covariate distributions, continuous covariates were simulated using a mixture-based approach: 80% of values were drawn from a central normal distribution, and 20% from the lower and upper extremes (± 2.5 SD). Standard deviations (SD) were estimated as (Q3–Q1)/1.349.

Simulated patient covariates included age, body weight (WT), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, sex, and creatinine clearance (CRCL). Age (median 58 years, IQR 54–71), WT (median 65 kg, IQR 60–70), and APACHE II score (median 19, IQR 15–26) were sampled via truncated normal distributions. Sex was assigned according to published proportions (76.2% male, 23.8% female). CRCL was calculated from total body weight using the Cockcroft–Gault equation30 and sampled from a truncated normal distribution (median 13.6 mL/min, IQR 6.9–22.2).

The sieving coefficient (Sc) or saturation coefficient (Sd) for meropenem was defined as the ratio of drug concentration in the effluent to the mean pre-filter and post-filter plasma concentrations. CRRT clearance (CLCRRT) was then calculated using modality-specific formulas.

Previous in vitro and clinical studies indicate that the sieving or saturation coefficient of meropenem approximates its unbound fraction and is similar across CRRT modalities30. In the simulation framework, Quf (mean 2477.5 mL/h, SD 112.95) and Sc (mean 0.75, SD 0.12) were sampled from truncated normal distributions to derive CLCRRT, consistent with the approach used for other covariates.

Eight meropenem dosing regimens (0.5 g q6h or q8h; 1.0 g or 2.0 g q6h, q8h, or q12h) were simulated across three infusion durations (0.5, 2, or 3 h), yielding 24 dosing scenarios. For each scenario, 2,000 virtual patients were simulated, assuming steady state at the fifth dose, with intensive sampling every 15 min to generate detailed concentration–time curves. Two pharmacodynamic endpoints—100% fT>MIC and 100% fT>4×MIC—were calculated to reflect stringent therapeutic targets in critically ill CRRT patients31,32. These thresholds were derived from EUCAST distributions of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii, serving as representative spectra of clinically relevant pathogen susceptibilities33. MIC distributions were derived from EUCAST data for Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii34.

Target attainment rate (TAR) was defined as a binary variable: 1 if the patient maintained concentrations above the PD threshold throughout the dosing interval, 0 otherwise. For example, under 1 g q8h, if %fT>MIC = 100% but %fT>4×MIC = 95%, TAR = 1 for %fT>MIC and 0 for %fT>4×MIC. Overall, 48,000 virtual patients (2,000 × 24 regimens) were simulated to construct the ML dataset, enabling probabilistic prediction of PD target attainment in critically ill CRRT patients.

Machine learning analysis

In the ML framework, TAR served as the binary prediction outcome. Two separate ML models were trained independently for the two pharmacodynamic targets, 100% fT>MIC and 100% fT>4×MIC, using the same model architectures and hyperparameters to ensure consistency. Predictor variables comprised patient covariates (age, WT, CRCL, CLCRRT, APACHE II score, sex), dosing parameters (dose amount (AMT), infusion rate (RATE), dosing interval(TAU)), and MIC values from simulated scenarios. Categorical variables (e.g., sex) were one-hot encoded, and continuous covariates were standardized using z-scores. Baseline differences between training and test sets were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

The dataset was randomly partitioned into training (80%) and test (20%) subsets. To mitigate class imbalance, synthetic minority oversampling (SMOTE) was employed to generate a balanced training dataset. The algorithms evaluated included random forests (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), decision trees (DT), gradient boosting (GB), and logistic regression (LR). Hyperparameters for each algorithm were systematically optimized via grid search coupled with five-fold cross-validation. The workflow of the five-fold cross-validation procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Schematic representation of five-fold cross-validation. In each iteration, a different subset of the data is used for training while the remaining subset is used for testing. After all iterations, performance metrics are averaged to obtain the final evaluation results.

The predictive performance of the model was assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score (the harmonic mean of precision and recall), and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) were applied to interpret the model, visualize feature contributions, and evaluate their relative importance in predicting pharmacodynamic target attainment. All machine learning analyses were performed in Python 3.7 within an Anaconda environment, using Scikit-learn, SHAP, and other relevant machine learning libraries.

Real-world data-based external validation

Real-world data for external validation were obtained from the meropenem meta-analysis POPPK model (Model 2) reported by Peng et al.17. This dataset included critically ill patients receiving continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) across multiple modalities, including the major CRRT modalities. These patients were compiled from nine published studies30,34–41. Patient data were collected based on the variables used to train the ML models. For patients with incomplete PK sampling, full individual PK profiles were reconstructed using the POPPK model (Table 1) via Bayesian estimation, serving as reference curves for validation. Missing covariate values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbor (KNN) algorithm to ensure dataset completeness for external validation.

Since the real-world dataset lacked MIC values for specific pathogens, Bayesian-predicted individual PK profiles were used to evaluate pharmacodynamic target attainment across predefined MIC scenarios (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 mg/L). For each MIC scenario, the percentage of the dosing interval during which free drug concentrations exceeded MIC (%fT>MIC) and 4×MIC (%fT>4×MIC) was computed, and attainment was assessed against preset thresholds, forming the reference targets (TAR) for the external validation dataset. Subsequently, the trained ML models predicted individual target attainment under the actual dosing regimens, and these predictions were compared to the reference TAR derived from Bayesian-predicted PK profiles to assess model performance.

Comparison of the predictive performance of ML models versus the POPPK model

In this study, the meropenem POPPK model (Model 1) reported by Peng et al.29 was employed to predict individual drug concentrations using patient clinical features and dosing information from the external validation dataset. Predictions were generated using only population PK parameters, without MAP Bayesian individualization for each patient. TAR was calculated from these predicted concentrations, and the POPPK model predictions were compared against those of the ML models.

Streamlit-based web deployment tool

To enable clinical implementation, the final predictive model was incorporated into a Python web application built with the Streamlit framework, providing software to support individualised dosing decisions in practice. The software enables the entry of patient characteristics and dosing regimens. It provides predicted pharmacodynamic target attainment across various MIC scenarios, with model metrics to guide real-time dosing adjustments.

All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Simulation data

Steady-state concentration–time profiles were generated for 48,000 virtual patients across 24 simulated scenarios (Supplementary material 2–4). The primary characteristics of the simulated patients are presented in Table S1 (Supplementary material 1), with no significant differences in features between the training and testing sets (all p > 0.05).

Predictive performance of ML models

In the test set, most ML models demonstrated good to excellent predictive performance for both pharmacodynamic targets (Table 2). For the prediction of 100% fT>MIC, the DT and GB models achieved the highest overall performance, with accuracies of 0.978 and 0.965 and AUCs of 0.997 and 0.998, respectively. RF and XGBoost also performed well, with accuracies above 0.95 and AUCs exceeding 0.99. In contrast, AdaBoost showed comparatively lower performance, particularly in accuracy (0.827) and AUC (0.921). For the more stringent target of 100% fT>4×MIC, DT and GB again showed the strongest performance, with accuracies of 0.979 and 0.976 and AUCs of 0.998. RF, XGBoost, and LR maintained high predictive ability, whereas AdaBoost remained less accurate than the other models.

Comparison of ML and PopPK model predictive performance

In the external validation dataset, all ML models except LR outperformed the POPPK model for both PD targets. For 100% fT>MIC, POPPK achieved an accuracy of 0.802, F1 of 0.876, and AUC of 0.823, while ML models exceeded these metrics, with GB showing the best overall performance (accuracy of 0.848, F1 of 0.834, and AUC of 0.929). For 100% fT>4×MIC, POPPK performance was similarly lower (accuracy 0.814, F1 0.802, AUC 0.871). These results indicate ML models provided superior predictive performance for stringent PD targets.

Streamlit-based web deployment and clinical application example

Among all ML models, this study ultimately chose the GB model as the prediction engine and incorporated it into the Streamlit-based web CDSS “MeroDose” (Fig. 4). In the system, users can enter patient characteristics (age, sex, WT, APACHE II score, CrCL, CLCRRT), dosing plan (dose, interval, infusion duration), and target MIC; the system then provides real-time predictions of achieving 100% fT>MIC or 100% fT>4×MIC, indicating target attainment, thus supporting individualized meropenem therapy for CRRT patients in critical care. For real-time prediction, if patient-specific Sc or Sd values are unavailable, the system uses median values derived from the modelling dataset (Sc = 0.75) as substitutes. Discussion

This study is the first to develop an ML-based CDSS specifically tailored to optimize meropenem dosing in critically ill patients receiving CRRT. Compared with traditional pharmacokinetic modelling approaches, ML methods offer greater flexibility in handling complex interactions among patient characteristics, treatment parameters, and extracorporeal support settings42–44. Nonetheless, building models that accurately capture the impact of patient characteristics, dosing regimens, and CRRT settings on pharmacodynamic targets demands substantial high-quality data. Given the scarcity of real PK data in CRRT patients, a virtual patient cohort was generated using reported meropenem POPPK models that encompass diverse dosing regimens and MIC conditions, and was validated against external real-world datasets.

Several studies have applied ML methods to optimize antibiotic doses. For example, Tang et al.45 developed an ML-based CDSS for β-lactam dosing in neonates, demonstrating superior prediction accuracy compared with conventional POPPK models. Additional research, including Bououda et al.46 and Huang et al.47, primarily focused on predicting vancomycin steady-state AUC or trough concentrations. However, most existing ML studies have been conducted in standard treatment populations and primarily support dose adjustment after therapeutic drug monitoring. Research specifically targeting critically ill patients receiving CRRT remains limited. Time-dependent β-lactam antibiotics such as meropenem require maintaining free drug concentrations above the MIC, and ML-based prediction of pharmacodynamic target attainment in this context has been insufficiently explored. By shifting the modelling objective from concentration estimation to the direct prediction of clinically actionable PD target attainment, the present framework broadens the scope of ML in antimicrobial dosing. It aligns model outputs more closely with therapeutic decision-making.

A central strength of this framework lies in its direct modelling of PD target attainment—a clinically actionable endpoint—rather than intermediate PK parameters. In contrast to POPPK models, which are constrained by prespecified structural assumptions48, the ML model establishes a data-driven mapping from patient characteristics, dosing regimens, and CRRT settings directly to the PD target probability. By bypassing intermediate PK estimation, the model is optimized for the ultimate therapeutic objective, which likely explains its superior discrimination in external validation. This conceptual distinction distinguishes the present approach from prior studies reporting that PK/PD-based synthetic data has neutral or detrimental effects on ML performance49,50. Those investigations primarily focused on reconstructing concentration–time profiles or augmenting large observational datasets, potentially reinforcing existing PK structural assumptions.

In contrast, synthetic data were specifically generated to support the prediction of PD target attainment, and independent external validation confirmed the model’s robustness. Thus, the present strategy can be viewed not as a replacement of structural modelling, but as a complementary translational layer that bridges mechanistic simulation and bedside decision support. From a clinical perspective, this distinction extends beyond methodological refinement. In critically ill patients receiving CRRT, early underexposure to meropenem may lead to therapeutic failure and promote antimicrobial resistance. Given the substantial interpatient variability and the absence of immediate therapeutic drug monitoring results at treatment initiation, initial dosing decisions are often made under considerable uncertainty. In this context, a model capable of prospectively identifying patients at high risk of insufficient target attainment may reduce empirical dosing variability and narrow the critical window of vulnerability during early therapy. Therefore, accurate identification of insufficient target attainment at therapy initiation is more consequential than marginal differences in global predictive metrics. By directly targeting clinically meaningful PD outcomes, the GB model represents a practical, potentially useful tool for individualized initial dosing in this high-risk population.

In pharmacodynamic target definition, the critical parameter for time-dependent β-lactam antibiotics is %fT>MIC. For adult patients, 40–50% fT>MIC is typically recommended; however, critically ill CRRT patients need higher targets due to severe disease and compromised immunity to enhance therapeutic effect and lower resistance risk51,52. Accordingly, 100% fT>MIC and 100% fT>4×MIC were chosen as stringent thresholds for training and predicting with the ML model. SHAP analysis indicated that MIC was the most influential feature. At the same time, the significant contributions of CLCRRT and residual CRCL highlight the model’s ability to integrate both extracorporeal and native renal clearance pathways—an aspect often oversimplified in conventional dosing for CRRT patients and thus a critical consideration. This aligns with established pharmacokinetic principles. It should be noted that the synthetic dataset used to train the machine learning models only included CLCRRT, CRCL, dosing regimen, and MIC information. Consequently, it is expected that only these covariates had a substantial impact on model predictions, whereas other variables (e.g., WT, age) had minimal influence, consistent with the dataset design.

During validation using real-world clinical data, this study encountered a more complex picture than in the training set. This complexity arose not just from a wider spread of individual patient variables, but also from a greater diversity in how these characteristics combined within patients. In the simulated cohort, these combinations were relatively uniform, resulting in consistent pharmacodynamic outcomes across patients with similar profiles. However, in actual clinical populations, the interaction of heterogeneous traits led to greater variability in drug responses. This variability is a plausible explanation for the model’s somewhat lower predictive performance observed in the external validation set. Furthermore, because a structural POPPK framework generated the training data itself, the ML model essentially internalized the relationships predefined by that simulation. Consequently, the external validation served as a test of how well the model could generalize beyond the specific assumptions and controlled conditions of its training environment. This process offers a more rigorous assessment of its robustness for real-world applications.

Nevertheless, the ML models maintained strong predictive performance and consistently outperformed the POPPK model, with the GB model demonstrating superior performance across both pharmacodynamic endpoints. These results suggest that ML models incorporating patient features, dosing information, and CRRT settings can accurately predict whether planned doses achieve pharmacodynamic targets across various MIC scenarios, providing reliable guidance for initial clinical dose selection. Moreover, as illustrated in Fig. 5, the final model was deployed within the Streamlit web-based CDSS “MeroDose,” allowing real-time entry of patient data and dosing regimens, and outputting predicted probabilities and achievement status across various MIC conditions. This setup improves both the convenience and practicality of model application in clinical settings, allowing physicians to swiftly determine initial dosing and adjust regimens flexibly based on clinical feedback.

Although several POPPK studies of meropenem in CRRT patients have been published, their direct use for model integration or external validation was constrained by unavailable covariates, structural incompatibility, and modality mismatches (Table S4). Consequently, an independent real-world dataset was used for external validation.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the model itself did not account for several dynamic clinical variables, such as concomitant drug use, evolving organ function, or filter-specific characteristics, which may influence meropenem exposure, particularly in unstable patients. Second, limitations inherent to the data of this study should be acknowledged. The simulation framework was derived from CVVH parameters, while the external validation cohort included patients receiving CVVHD and CVVHDF. Although sieving coefficients are reportedly comparable across modalities, this study’s dataset was insufficient to conclusively rule out modality-specific effects. Furthermore, the external validation relied on published datasets, in which PK profiles for a small subset of patients (n = 8) were reconstructed via Bayesian estimation; future studies with larger cohorts and complete sampling are needed to verify that this imputation does not introduce bias. Finally, regarding clinical applicability, the use of a binary target attainment outcome, though practical, may not fully capture the exposure–response gradient, as scenarios near the threshold (e.g., 90% fT>MIC) may still be therapeutic. Incorporating continuous PK/PD measures in the future could provide more nuanced guidance. Prospective clinical evaluation will ultimately be required to determine whether integrating this CDSS into routine CRRT management translates into improved therapeutic precision, reduced antimicrobial resistance risk, or better patient-centered outcomes.

Conclusion

This study developed an ML-based CDSS (“MeroDose”) to predict pharmacodynamic target attainment for meropenem dosing in critically ill patients receiving CRRT. The web-based implementation may support individualized initial dosing decisions. Further prospective clinical validation is warranted to confirm its clinical utility.
