Early surgical intervention for neonatal syndactyly in amniotic band sequence
Authors: Aidan Shulkin, Laurent Tessier, Emilie Bougie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Amniotic Band Sequence is a rare congenital disorder that can result in a vast array of limb deformities, including pathognomonic fenestrated syndactyly. While current surgical approaches prioritize the early release of constriction bands to address ischemia and lymphoedema, syndactyly release is often delayed until the child is 3–6 months old. This letter advocates for earlier (less than 3 months) fenestrated syndactyly release when feasible to enhance joint mobility, encourage finger growth, and support overall hand development in affected newborns.We present the minimally invasive procedure used at our university-affiliated center to release fenestrated syndactyly in newborn patients with Amniotic Band Sequence. The technique, conducted under topical and local anesthesia, involves sharp transection of skin bridges, while bleeding is managed through manual pressure. Suturing is typically not required and a small bandage is applied for 48 h.Early intervention in this population is crucial, as it significantly reduces the risk of asymmetric finger growth, impaired function, and suboptimal aesthetic outcomes. Additionally, it promotes fine motor development and psychosocial well-being during a critical developmental stage. This technique shows great potential, given its minimally invasive nature, limited need of topical and local anesthesia, short recovery time, and potential for improved functional and aesthetic results. It also represents a particularly valuable option in resource-limited settings, where access to operating theatres and systemic anesthesia is restricted. However, further research is necessary to compare outcomes with delayed procedures to establish standardized treatment protocols.

Introduction

Amniotic Band Sequence (ABS), also referred to as Constriction Ring Syndrome, is a rare congenital condition that primarily affects the extremities. Although its etiology is debated, it is theorized to be secondary to the in-utero entanglement of foetal parts in amniotic membrane remnants around the sixth gestational week, following early amnion rupture.1 In the upper extremities, this can manifest as a vast array of deformities ranging from minor skin indentations, severe constriction with distal lymphoedema or ischemia, pathognomonic fenestrated syndactyly with proximal sinuses and distal fusions, to transverse amputation. In the hand, the distal central digits are most commonly affected, while the thumb and structures proximal to the constriction bands are typically spared.

Given the variability of ABS deformities, surgical planning must be adapted to each patient’s unique presentation. Specifically, urgent cases involving deep constriction bands that threaten vascularity require immediate intervention in the neonatal period. In contrast, syndactyly release is often delayed, with some authors recommending it around 3–6 months,2 and others suggesting between 6 months and 1 year.3

In addition to constriction band release, we advocate for early (less than 3 months) fenestrated syndactyly release when feasible. Amenable syndactyly may be between adjacent or nonadjacent digits, with a skin bridge with a base of less than 2 mL. We perform the procedure in the newborn nursery or in a minor procedure room for patients referred from outside our center (Supplementary Video 1, Supplementary Image 1). Oral sucrose and topical/local anesthesia are administered to limit procedural pain. Nevertheless, it is common practice to perform the procedure without local anesthesia, as the injection may cause more discomfort than the procedure itself. We avoid alcohol preparation and use a diluted chlorhexidine solution for disinfection in newborns. The skin bridge is sharply transected using scissors at the border of the digits. Manual pressure is applied for 1–2 min to limit blood loss, an important factor in neonates with limited circulating volume. Suturing is usually not required but may be used if necessary. A small bandage is applied for 48 h and thereafter bathing is permitted. It is important to note that more complex presentations involving multiple digits or deeper structures require meticulous surgical planning and may not be suitable for the simplified approach described.

It has been reported that delaying surgery increases the risk of complications, including asymmetric finger growth and joint angulation, which could lead to reduced function, stiffness, and suboptimal aesthetic outcomes.4 Furthermore, early intervention plays a crucial role in promoting the development of fine motor skills during a critical period in infancy, as well as supporting psychosocial well-being.5 Timely correction facilitates normal hand function, which is essential for achieving key milestones such as grasping and manipulation.

Our pragmatic approach eliminates the need for systemic anesthesia, which poses considerable risks in newborns, and allows interventions to be performed in the newborn nursery, reducing the need for subsequent hospital visits.6 This also offers particular value in resource-limited settings, such as low-and middle-income countries, where access to operating theatres is often restricted.7

Despite the clear benefits of early intervention, there remains a lack of comprehensive studies evaluating the outcomes of fenestrated syndactyly release in those with ABS performed before versus after 3 months of age. Given the procedure’s minimal invasive nature, limited need of topical and local anesthesia, short recovery time, and potential for improved functional and aesthetic results, early intervention emerges as a highly compelling approach. However, further research is needed to provide robust evidence supporting this practice and to guide clinical protocols.
