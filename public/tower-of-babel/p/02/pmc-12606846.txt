Association between controlled mechanical ventilation and systemic inflammation in acute hypoxemic respiratory failure: an observational cohort study
Authors: M. Consuelo Bachmann, Martín Benites, Vanessa Oviedo, Niki Hamidi Vadeghani, Dagoberto Soto, Roque Basoalto, Pablo Cruces, Yorschua Jalil, L. Felipe Damiani, Guillermo Bugedo, Alejandro Bruhn, Jaime Retamal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn patients with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, spontaneous breathing efforts may contribute to patient self-inflicted lung injury through increased ventilation inhomogeneity and systemic inflammation. Whether early transition to controlled mechanical ventilation (CMV) mitigates these effects remains uncertain.MethodsThis observational, prospective cohort study included 40 ICU patients with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure who initially breathed spontaneously. Based on clinical decisions, patients were managed with either continued spontaneous breathing (SB group, n = 12) or transitioned to CMV (CMV group, n = 28). Arterial blood gases, hemodynamics, plasma cytokines (IL-6 and IL-8), and ventilation distribution via electrical impedance tomography (EIT) were recorded at baseline and after 24 h. In the CMV group, intermediate time points (T2, T6, T12) were also assessed after intubation. The trial was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03513809).ResultsIn the CMV group, respiratory rate and heart rate decreased significantly over time. IL-6 levels dropped markedly from 305 ± 938 pg/mL at baseline to 27 ± 58 pg/mL at 24 h (p = 0.0195), accompanied by a significant improvement in oxygenation (PaO₂/FiO₂ from 140 ± 51 to 199 ± 67, p = 0.0004). EIT data showed improved ventilation distribution with increased end-expiratory lung impedance, decreased global inhomogeneity, and a shift in the center of ventilation toward dorsal regions. In contrast, the SB group showed no significant changes over 24 h in gas exchange, systemic inflammation, or EIT-derived parameters.ConclusionsIn patients with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure initially breathing spontaneously, transition to CMV was associated with reduced IL-6 levels and improved ventilatory homogeneity over 24 h. These exploratory findings indicate that connection to controlled mechanical ventilation was associated with reduced systemic inflammation, a relationship that warrants confirmation in larger prospective studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05727-7.

Introduction

Patients with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure (AHRF) frequently exhibit increased respiratory drive, characterized by intense inspiratory efforts, elevated respiratory rates, rapid inspiratory flows, and larger tidal volumes [1]. These respiratory patterns, when superimposed on a reduced, unstable, and heterogeneously aerated lung, result in increased pulmonary strain and stress [2]. The interplay between heightened respiratory drive, regional lung mechanical inhomogeneities, and local and systemic inflammation may ultimately contribute to the development of patient self-inflicted lung injury (P-SILI) [3, 4].

Observational studies have demonstrated consistent associations between increased inspiratory effort, negative intrathoracic pressure, elevated tidal volume, and failure of non-invasive ventilation and the subsequent need for invasive mechanical ventilation (MV) [4, 5]. Experimental research has further highlighted the deleterious effects of spontaneous ventilation in both injured and healthy animal models [6, 7]. A key study by Mascheroni et al. showed that spontaneous breathing with high minute ventilation caused lung edema and hypoxemia in healthy sheep [8]. More recently, in a porcine model of lung collapse, our group observed that spontaneous ventilation promoted uneven distribution of ventilation, marked swings in intrathoracic pressure, and subsequent lung injury [7]. In line with these findings, Hurtado et al. demonstrated in a murine model that spontaneous ventilation following lung injury leads to a progressive increase in regional strain and heterogeneity. They propose that this maldistribution of ventilation facilitates mechanical phenomena such as local overdistension, tidal recruitment, and pendelluft, which act as amplifiers of tissue damage, so-called ‘stress raisers’, particularly in regions bordering collapsed and aerated areas [9]. Lung inhomogeneities are associated with overall disease severity and mortality [10].

Despite the biological plausibility and growing experimental evidence, the clinical relevance of P-SILI remains incompletely understood. In particular, the early inflammatory response to spontaneous ventilation in AHRF and its potential modulation by subsequent passive mechanical ventilation have not been systematically studied [11].

To explore this issue, we designed a prospective, observational clinical study where we hypothesize that spontaneous ventilation during the initial phase of AHRF may exacerbate lung injury due to uneven ventilation distribution. Furthermore, we propose that intubation and connection to protective mechanical ventilation, using low tidal volume and moderate positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), may be associated with attenuation of inflammatory markers.

This study aims to investigate the evolution of inflammatory mediators and distribution of ventilation during the first 24 h in patients admitted with AHRF who initially breathe spontaneously and could be connected to MV or to continue breathing spontaneously based on the attending physician’s decision.

Methods

This prospective clinical protocol was conducted between 2018 and 2021 at the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) of Hospital Clínico UC-Christus in Santiago, Chile. Approval for this study was granted by the institutional ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained from the patients’ next of kin (170315007). The trial was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03513809).

Study population

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they presented with acute respiratory symptoms for less than seven days and fulfilled the criteria for acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, defined as PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio ≤ 300 mmHg, while receiving oxygen with FiO₂ ≥0.30. In addition, participants were required to demonstrate increased work of breathing, defined by either a respiratory rate > 25 breaths per minute or clinical signs of intercostal or supraclavicular retraction, with both criteria being present for less than 24 h prior to enrollment [12]. Exclusion criteria included acute respiratory failure due to exacerbation of chronic respiratory disease or cardiogenic pulmonary edema, hypercapnia (PaCO₂ > 45 mmHg), impaired consciousness (Glasgow Coma Scale < 13), immediate indication for endotracheal intubation, prior decision to withhold resuscitation, or refusal to provide informed consent.

Included patients were followed during their ICU stay. Based on the decision of the treating team, patients were categorized into two groups based on whether they subsequently needed MV or not:

1) Spontaneous Breathing (SB) group: Patients who completed 24 h of spontaneous breathing since the first assessment.

2) Controlled Mechanical Ventilation (CMV) group: patients who required connection to CMV (< 24 h) with variable periods breathing spontaneously before tracheal intubation (Fig. 1).

As this was an observational study, the decision to intubate, initiate controlled mechanical ventilation, and select ventilator settings was made by the attending clinical team, in accordance with ICU protocols for intubation in AHRF and for mechanical ventilation in ARDS, and was entirely independent of the study protocol.

Clinical data

After obtaining consent, demographic data, including age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores were recorded.

At baseline (during spontaneous breathing), we measured vital signs, blood arterial gases, electrical impedance tomography (EIT) data, and plasma samples of proinflammatory cytokines. For spontaneously breathing patients (SB group), the same data were collected at 24 h after inclusion (T24) to favor patient comfort. In the CMV group, additional intermediate time points were obtained. The Borg scale was also used to quantify the patients’ sensation of breathlessness at the baseline assessment. Patients were asked to rate their perceived dyspnea on a scale from 0 (no breathlessness) to 10 (maximal breathlessness).

If patients required connection to CMV, EIT data, vital signs, and respiratory mechanics were recorded immediately at the time of intubation (T0), and at hours 2, and 6 after connection to MV (T2, and T6). Subsequent assessments were performed at hours 12, and 24 (T12 - T24). Arterial blood gases were analyzed at T2, T6, T12, and T24, and EIT data at T2, T12, and T24. The inflammatory parameters, like those in the SB group, were evaluated at T24.

In spontaneously breathing patients, FiO₂ was estimated according to the oxygen delivery device used (Venturi mask, HFNC, or NIV), based on standard reference values provided by the device or manufacturer. PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio were therefore considered estimations, as exact FiO₂ measurement is not feasible in routine bedside practice. This estimation error was systematic and applied uniformly across all patients [13–16].

Inflammatory cytokines analysis

To determine the plasma concentrations of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-8 (IL-8), blood samples were collected at baseline and 24 h after baseline. Samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min and stored at − 80 °C until processing [22]. The solid-phase enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) used was based on the quantitative immunometric sandwich enzyme immunoassay technique on a microtiter plate (R&D Systems Inc, Minneapolis USA) [23]. The results are expressed in pg/mL of plasma.

Study endpoints

Primary Endpoint: Change in plasma levels of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and IL-8) between baseline (during spontaneous breathing) and after 24 h of spontaneous breathing or connection to MV.

Secondary Endpoints: Evolution of respiratory and hemodynamics parameters, and regional ventilation parameters assessed by electrical impedance tomography global Inhomogeneity Index, impedance ratio, and center of ventilation.

The primary outcome of this study is defined as within-group changes over time (from enrollment to 24 h), and not as formal comparisons between the SB and CMV groups. Rather, the analysis focuses on the temporal evolution of inflammatory markers, gas exchange, and ventilation distribution within each group.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was estimated based on the study by Sellarés et al., which evaluated changes in IL-6 levels during spontaneous breathing trials in mechanically ventilated patients [24]. Given the observed within-subject increase in IL-6 in the subgroup of patients with SBT failure (from a median of 60 to 72 pg/mL, p = 0.02), a within-group effect size (dz) of 0.53 was conservatively estimated. Using G*Power 3.1.9.7, a two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test (matched pairs) was selected under the Exact test family, with the parent distribution set to Laplace. Assuming an alpha level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, the required sample size was calculated to be 20 subjects per group (i.e., intubated and non-intubated patients, analyzed independently). This sample size allows for the detection of within-group changes in IL-6 concentrations over a 24-hour period. However, due to recruitment limitations and the observational nature of the study, where the decision to intubate depended on the treating clinical team, 28 patients were included in the CMV group and 12 in the SB group. For patients who did not complete the 24-hour follow-up, available data up to the last recorded time point were included in the analysis.

Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess the distribution of data. Normally distributed data were compared using Paired Student’s T-test and one-way (repeated measures) analysis of variance (ANOVA). Data that were not normally distributed were compared using the Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis tests. Bonferroni adjustment for multiple tests was applied for post-hoc comparisons.

Exploratory analyses were additionally performed to assess predictors of intubation and the association between EIT-derived measures and inflammatory biomarkers. A multivariable logistic regression model was built using baseline characteristics to estimate the probability of intubation and reduced by parsimony criteria. Furthermore, we assessed the association between interleukins (IL-6, IL-8) and EIT metrics (IR, GI, EELI, TVI, and CoV) using classical ANCOVA fitted separately for each cytokine. The outcome was log(ILℓ at 24 h); models adjusted for log(ILℓ at 0 h) (baseline persistence), the baseline value of the EIT marker, and its 0–24 h longitudinal summaries: AUC (time-averaged exposure, trapezoidal integral/24) and slope (global linear trend, h − 1). Values below the LOD were set to LOD/2 before log-transform. Models were estimated by OLS; we report coefficients with classical standard errors.

Analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and RStudio version 4.3.1 (Integrated Development Environment, Boston, MA, USA). Data are expressed as either mean ± SD or median (25–75% percentile). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Quantitative variables were analyzed as continuous data. No arbitrary cutoffs or categorizations were applied.

Results

A total of 40 patients were enrolled (28 in the CMV group and 12 in the SB group). One patient in the CMV group died 6 h after intubation; data up to that time point were included in the analyses. Overall, the cohort had a mean age of 65 ± 15 years, an APACHE II score of 13 ± 6, and a SOFA score of 6 ± 2 (Table 1). All patients were included at the time of admission to the ICU. Baseline diagnoses, including bacterial, viral, fungal, and COVID-19 pneumonia, as well as treatments (vasopressors and corticosteroids), are detailed in Table 1. The distribution of these variables was similar between groups. In addition, thirty patients received oxygen supply through a high-flow nasal cannula, while seven patients received conventional oxygen therapy, and three patients received non-invasive ventilation.

Demographic characteristics of the included patients

Decisions regarding initiation of mechanical ventilation were the responsibility of the attending physician and were guided by the ICU’s internal protocols. Patients were categorized according to the criteria of connection to MV into two groups: (1) the SB group (n = 12), and (2) the CMV group (n = 28). The mean age for the SB group was 67 ± 10 years, while the CMV group had an average age of 65 ± 12 years (p = 0.54). There were no significant differences in the severity of the critical condition assessed by SOFA and APACHE II scores. Additionally, the body mass index exhibited similarity between the two groups, with values of 26.2 ± 2 in the SB group and 28.3 ± 4 in the CMV group (p = 0.29).

Analysis of the CMV group over time

Cardiorespiratory variables

Connection to invasive mechanical ventilation in the CMV group was associated with a significant decrease in respiratory rate (32 ± 7 at baseline vs. 24 ± 3 at T24, p < 0.0001), heart rate (102 ± 19 at baseline vs. 86 ± 22, p < 0.0001), and mean arterial pressure (85 ± 19 at baseline vs. 78 ± 10 at T24, p = 0.011) compared to baseline values (Fig. 2). These changes were consistent across all analyzed time points. A reduction in body temperature was also observed (37 ± 0.8 at baseline vs. 36.5 ± 0.6 at T24, p = 0.030).

Arterial blood gases

After intubation, a significant increase in PaCO₂ was observed (35.3 ± 7.6 at baseline vs. 42.5 ± 7.4 at T2, p = 0.0007), accompanied by a decrease in pH (7.41 ± 0.06 at baseline vs. 7.34 ± 0.08 at T6, p = 0.005). Furthermore, the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio significantly improved and remained elevated throughout the mechanical ventilation period (140 ± 51 at baseline vs. 173 ± 77 at T2 and 199 ± 67 at T24, p = 0.0004) (Fig. 3) The breakdown of PaO₂, FiO₂, and the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio over time for each group is presented in Supplementary Table 1 (table S1).

Electrical impedance tomography

An increase in the impedance ratio was found at T2 and T12, indicating a shift in ventilation toward the ventral lung regions (0.61 ± 0.5 at baseline vs. 1.3 ± 0.7 at T2 and 1.1 ± 0.4 at T12, p = 0.0004). This finding was supported by a significant decrease in the center of ventilation at all time points (p < 0.0001). Additionally, a significant decline in the inhomogeneity index was observed (1.13 ± 0.3 at baseline vs. 0.9 ± 0.1 at T2 and T12 and 0.9 ± 0.2 at T24, p = 0.015), suggesting a more homogeneous ventilation distribution. An increase in EELI was also observed at T2 and T12 compared to baseline (6.8 ± 40 vs. 32 ± 50 and 22 ± 51, respectively, p = 0.044) (Fig. 4). No significant changes were detected in the TVI index.

A significant decrease in plasma IL-6 concentration was observed at 24 h (305 ± 938 at baseline vs. 27 ± 58 pg/mL at T24, p = 0.0195), while IL-8 concentrations showed no significant changes (Fig. 5). Baseline comparison between groups

Hemodynamics and arterial blood gases

At baseline, the SB group had a significantly lower heart rate than the CMV group (81 ± 19 vs. 102 ± 19, p = 0.004). Additionally, there was a trend toward a higher respiratory rate in the CMV group (32 ± 8) compared to the SB group (28 ± 3, p = 0.054). No differences were observed in other hemodynamic variables or arterial blood gases. Significantly higher Borg scale score was observed in the CMV group (8 ± 2 in CMV vs. 6 ± 2 in SB, p = 0.013).

Cytokines

Plasma IL-6 concentration was significantly higher in the CMV group (593 ± 1307) compared to the SB group (504 ± 1560, p = 0.038).

Comparison between groups at 24 h (T24)

Hemodynamics and arterial blood gases

No significant differences in hemodynamic variables were observed between the groups. However, the CMV group had a significantly lower pH (7.37 ± 0.06 vs. 7.42 ± 0.03, p = 0.005) and better oxygenation (PaO₂/FiO₂ of 199 ± 67) compared to the SB group (155 ± 44, p = 0.041).

EIT

The inhomogeneity index was higher in the SB group (1.36 ± 0.3 vs. 0.98 ± 0.18, p = 0.0003), indicating a more heterogeneous ventilation distribution in patients not connected to mechanical ventilation. No differences were found in the other evaluated EIT indices.

Cytokines

No significant differences were found in IL-6 or IL-8 concentrations between the groups at 24 h.

Clinical outcomes

Only one patient in the SB group required intubation during their ICU stay, which occurred more than 48 h after admission. There were no significant differences between groups in the duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU or hospital length of stay, nor in 28-day or 1-year mortality (all p > 0.05).

Exploratory analyses of predictors and associations

In exploratory logistic regression models, the complete model included sex, age, APACHE II score, PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio, respiratory rate, heart rate, mean arterial pressure, dyspnea (Borg score), and corticosteroid use. After stepwise reduction, a more parsimonious model retained PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio, respiratory rate, and heart rate. In the reduced model, higher respiratory and heart rates were independently associated with increased probability of intubation (p = 0.051 and p = 0.011, respectively), whereas lower PaO₂/FiO₂ demonstrated a non-significant trend toward higher risk (p = 0.077). Model performance was superior for the reduced model (AIC 38 vs. 44.5).

Regarding biomarkers, ANCOVA models consistently showed that for IL-6, the baseline term was positive and statistically significant (evidence of persistence), and the IR slope showed a negative association: a more pronounced increase in IR over 24 h was linked to lower IL-6 at 24 h, suggesting that dynamic improvement in ventilation distribution accompanies reductions in inflammation; neither IR baseline nor its AUC displayed conclusive effects.

Discussion

The main findings of this study indicate that, in this cohort of AHRF patients, the transition from spontaneous breathing to controlled mechanical ventilation resulted in a shift in ventilation distribution toward the ventral regions, along with improved overall ventilatory homogeneity. Furthermore, connection to mechanical ventilation was accompanied by a marked reduction in plasma IL-6 levels, suggesting a potential modulatory effect on systemic inflammation. Importantly, this transition also occurred alongside significant changes in hemodynamics and gas exchange, as well as the introduction of concomitant interventions such as sedation, neuromuscular blockers, and vasopressors, underscoring the multifaceted impact of mechanical ventilation in this clinical context [25].

This study adopts an observational design, using the decision for tracheal intubation and initiation of controlled mechanical ventilation as the exposure. This approach enabled the functional classification of patients into two essentially distinct groups: those continuing with spontaneous breathing (SB group) and those transitioned to MV (CMV group). A key strength of this design lies in its capacity for within-subject analysis, allowing comparison of physiological and inflammatory parameters before and after the intervention in the same individual. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly assess the impact of transitioning from spontaneous breathing to mechanical ventilation on factors potentially implicated in patient self-inflicted lung injury, including ventilation distribution and systemic inflammation.

In a previous closely related preclinical study, we observed that spontaneous ventilation with vigorous respiratory efforts led to ventilation heterogeneity and lung injury progression. Notably, transitioning these animals to controlled mechanical ventilation corrected the ventilation distribution heterogeneity [7]. Building on these findings, the present clinical study aims to further explore the early changes induced after connection to mechanical ventilation, extending the observation period to up to 24 h. By doing so, we seek to capture a more consolidated biological signal that will provide comprehensive insights into the complex interaction between spontaneous ventilation, mechanical ventilation, and the inflammatory response.

The structural organization of our hospital, where the ICU functions primarily as a ventilatory unit, largely explains the high intubation rate observed in this cohort. Patients are typically transferred for advanced management of respiratory failure, most often requiring intubation and invasive mechanical ventilation. This pattern was further accentuated during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the ICU was converted into a dedicated ventilatory unit.

Hemodynamic and respiratory effects

The CMV group demonstrated significant changes in respiratory and cardiovascular parameters over time, including reduced respiratory rate, heart rate, and mean arterial pressure. These changes suggest that MV and ICU-cointerventions alleviate the physiological burden associated with the increased work of breathing in critically ill patients breathing spontaneously, potentially contributing to better hemodynamic stability.

In contrast, the SB group exhibited minimal changes in hemodynamic variables, with only a small decrease in respiratory rate at 24 h. These findings emphasize the potential limitations of SB in reducing the physiological stress in patients with acute respiratory failure.

Gas exchange and ventilation distribution

Furthermore, the oxygenation levels showed a progressive and significant improvement during the early hours following the initiation of mechanical ventilation. This improvement likely reflects the benefits of controlled ventilation strategies, particularly the use of optimized tidal volumes and PEEP, in enhancing alveolar recruitment and reducing ventilation-perfusion mismatch. Specifically, using EIT, we observed a more homogeneous distribution of ventilation, reflected by a reduction in the global inhomogeneity index. In parallel, there was an early and sustained increase in EELI following the initiation of mechanical ventilation. These EIT findings reinforce the notion that appropriate PEEP settings can reverse the collapse of unstable alveoli after connection to MV, thereby increasing end-expiratory lung volume and reducing putative mechanisms of VILI. This leads to improved stabilization of dependent lung regions and overall ventilation efficiency [26]. Several preclinical studies have reported that CMV, compared to SB, leads to improved oxygenation and lung aeration, reduced regional ventilatory heterogeneity, and attenuated progression of lung injury [7, 9, 27].

On the other hand, we observed a redistribution of tidal ventilation toward the ventral regions of the lungs, as indicated by changes in IR and CoV. This shift, together with the increase in EELI while TVI remained stable, supports the hypothesis that controlled mechanical ventilation, and the associated diaphragm paralysis, may contribute to the stabilization of dependent yuxta-diaphragmatic lung regions and potentially reduce tidal recruitment phenomena. In acutely injured subjects breathing spontaneously, ventral regions are subjected to the highest regional strain [9], with respiratory rate acting as a multiplicative factor by increasing the frequency of strain cycles, and consequently overdistension events, per unit of time, thereby exacerbating strain-related inflammation [28].

Conversely, the SB group showed no significant changes in EIT indices, highlighting the challenges of achieving optimal ventilation distribution without mechanical support.

Inflammatory response

We observed a decrease in plasma concentration of IL-6 after 24 h of connection to controlled MV compared to the period of spontaneous breathing. This finding suggests that connection to mechanical ventilation, in addition to other ICU-cointerventions, may play a role in modulating the inflammation associated with spontaneous ventilation, also known as P-SILI [4]. The absence of significant changes in cytokine levels in the SB group may reflect ongoing lung mechanical stress together with other systemic pathophysiological mechanisms (e.g., dysoxia, hyperadrenergic activity), which could contribute to the persistence of systemic inflammation.

Our findings conceptually align with the work of González-López et al., who reported that, in patients with acute lung injury, higher lung strain (strain > 0.27) was associated with a fourfold increase in IL-6 concentrations in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid compared to patients with lower strain or without acute lung injury [29]. While our study did not directly assess lung strain or alveolar cytokines, the reduction in systemic IL-6 observed after initiation of controlled ventilation may be related to a decrease in global mechanical stress, particularly in patients exhibiting high stress and strain at baseline. Supporting this interpretation, other studies have shown that high regional strain in acutely injured lungs breathing spontaneously is associated with localized inflammatory responses and activation of mechanotransduction pathways [30], reinforcing the broader concept that excessive lung deformation, whether from patient effort or ventilator settings, amplifies systemic inflammation. In our study, the improvement in inflammatory markers was paralleled by more homogeneous ventilation distribution on EIT. The reduction in the GI index and redistribution of ventilation toward dependent regions suggest that controlled ventilation, combined with moderate levels of PEEP and low tidal volumes, may have contributed to stabilizing previously collapsed or heterogeneously ventilated lung units.

Baseline comparisons revealed higher heart rates and respiratory rates, as well as greater subjective breathlessness (Borg scale), in the CMV group compared to the SB group. These findings suggest that patients requiring MV had a more severe clinical presentation at baseline. At 24 h, the CMV group demonstrated better oxygenation and a more homogeneous ventilation distribution (GI index), compared to the SB group. While these associations should be interpreted with caution given the observational design of the study, our findings suggest that timely intubation and controlled ventilation, together with concomitant ICU interventions such as sedation, neuromuscular blockade, corticosteroids, antibiotics, and hemodynamic support, may contribute to both physiological stabilization and a reduction in systemic inflammation in patients with severe AHRF. Notably, the sickest patients (CMV group), who not only had greater clinical lability but also higher baseline levels of inflammation, were more responsive to these interventions. This raises the hypothesis that mechanical ventilation and other intensive treatments may act synergistically to reduce systemic inflammation. Importantly, baseline covariates related to these interventions were similar between groups, which supports the internal consistency of our findings despite the limited sample size.

Beyond these primary results, we also performed exploratory analyses to better understand factors associated with intubation and systemic inflammation. In line with our interpretation above, these analyses suggest that baseline physiological severity, as reflected by higher respiratory and heart rates and lower PaO₂/FiO₂ ratios, was strongly associated with the need for intubation. These variables capture both the burden of respiratory effort and the degree of gas exchange impairment, reinforcing their clinical value as bedside predictors of deterioration. Moreover, the observed association between improvements in impedance ratio over time and lower IL-6 concentrations supports the hypothesis that homogenization of ventilation may contribute to mitigating systemic inflammation. Taken together, these findings strengthen the concept that both mechanical ventilation and its associated interventions act synergistically, not only to stabilize gas exchange and hemodynamics but also to attenuate inflammatory responses.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged: (1) The sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the results. In particular, the smaller-than-planned sample size in the SB group reduced the ability to detect statistically significant within-group changes. One patient in the CMV group died after 6 h of follow-up; data up to that point were included, but subsequent measurements were unavailable, this may have influenced variability in the results. (2) The research protocol did not include a study-driven standardized protocol for intubation. Instead, the decision to initiate mechanical ventilation was left to the treating clinical team. Although our ICU follows established internal protocols for AHRF and ARDS management aligned with evidence-based recommendations, this may have introduced variability in decision-making and affected outcomes. (3) The evaluation period was limited to 24 h. A longer follow-up (e.g., 48 h) would have been desirable; however, this was not feasible due to the difficulty of maintaining continuous monitoring with minimal technical failures and ensuring patient comfort. As a result, longer-term outcomes such as ventilator-associated pneumonia or ventilator-induced lung injury were not assessed, and their inclusion could provide a more comprehensive perspective. (4) Missing data were present in some variables. Arterial blood gases were obtained as part of routine ICU management, typically every 6 h during the first 24 h after intubation, but not all planned measurements could be collected because of clinical priorities. Similarly, some EIT data were lost due to technical issues (e.g., noise, disconnections), and a small number of cytokine samples could not be reliably quantified. (5) Finally, concomitant interventions such as corticosteroid therapy, deep sedation with neuromuscular blockade, and protocolized hemodynamic management may have influenced inflammatory and physiological outcomes. Nevertheless, these measures represent the standard of care for ARDS patients and thus enhance the generalizability of our findings to real-world practice.

In conclusion, this observational cohort study provides new insights into the physiological changes that occur when patients with AHRF transition from spontaneous to controlled mechanical ventilation. We observed associations between connection to controlled MV and improvements in oxygenation, ventilation homogeneity, and systemic inflammation, whereas such changes were not evident in spontaneously breathing patients. These findings, while exploratory, suggest that both mechanical ventilation and concomitant ICU interventions may play a synergistic role in improving ventilatory distribution and decreasing systemic inflammation. Further research with larger sample sizes and controlled interventional studies are needed, to validate these findings and guide evidence-based clinical practice in the management of AHRF.
