Neonatal units as physical spaces for newborn care delivery in Kenya: A qualitative study
Authors: Caroline Waithira, Nancy Odinga, Gloria Ngaiza, Justina Maluni, Michuki Maina, Kenneth Karumba, Mike English, Edna Mutua, Sassy Molyneux, Caroline Jones, Dorothy Oluoch, HIGH Q Hospital Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal units offer specialized environments for caring for small and sick newborns. However, inadequate consideration of layout and organization of space can hinder care delivery and the implementation of key interventions such as Kangaroo Mother Care, supportive care for exclusive breastfeeding, and respectful communication. While efforts are being made to improve the infrastructure and technical capacities of newborn units, it is important to consider human-cantered design aspects to ensure that the spaces meet the needs of healthcare providers and mothers. This study examines the impact of the layout and organization of three key spaces in Kenyan neonatal units on care delivery: the nursing station, the Kangaroo Mother Care ward, and the breast milk expression areas. We aim to contribute evidence on how the space and layout of neonatal units can influence care delivery in resource-limited settings, such as Kenya.MethodsWe employed an ethnographic approach (non-participant observation, in-depth interviews, and informal conversations) to explore the layout and organization of neonatal units and how these influence care delivery in four county referral hospitals in Kenya. Data were collected between January 2022 and April 2023, cumulatively capturing over 600 hours of observations per hospital. We analyzed the data thematically.ResultsWe found substantial variation in the layout and organization of space in the neonatal units. Nursing stations emerged as pivotal hubs for communication, with their location and layout either facilitating or hindering the orientation of new mothers and the sharing of essential and confidential information. Kangaroo Mother Care spaces were often inadequate, limiting the number of babies who could benefit from continuous Kangaroo Mother Care. Designated breastmilk expressing areas in some hospitals provided mothers opportunities for peer support and bonding, but space limitations and overcrowding negatively impacted mothers' comfort and privacy when utilizing the spaces. Expressing areas also offered healthcare providers opportunities to support and supervise mothers collectively, which was helpful given staff shortages.ConclusionThe layout of neonatal units and the organization of spaces influence care delivery. Limitations in layout and space require healthcare providers to adopt practices that can inhibit the provision of respectful communication and maternal participation in care. Careful planning of the layout and organization of spaces in the neonatal units can facilitate these practices and support the needs of neonates, healthcare providers, and mothers.Keywords: Space, Layout, Neonatal units, Communication, Participation, Mothers, Healthcare providers

Background

Neonatal mortality remains a significant global health concern. Globally, approximately 2.4 million neonates die annually (WHO, 2020). Sub-Saharan Africa bears the largest burden of deaths, estimated at 27 deaths per 1000 live births, translating to 43 % of the global share (WHO Immediate KMC Study Group, 2021). Kenya faces a similar challenge, with the latest national survey indicating that the neonatal mortality rate stands at 21 deaths per 1000 live births, with 51 % of the total under-five deaths attributed to neonatal mortality (KNBS and ICF, 2023). Although there has been a persistent increase in hospital-based deliveries and subsequently increased utilization of neonatal care, Kenya is not on track to meet Sustainable Development Goal 3.2, which aims to reduce neonatal mortality to at least 12 deaths per 1000 live births by 2030 (KNBS and ICF, 2023; WHO, 2024).

Advances in neonatal care, driven by medical technology, have significantly reduced neonatal mortality and morbidity (Taha et al., 2023). However, while technologies play a critical role in neonatal care, other factors, such as the physical environment, are also important and contribute to the quality of care delivered to newborns. Neonatal units are the first extra-uterine settings that sick and small newborns experience (Lester et al., 2011; Wigert et al., 2014). This makes the neonatal units an important component in service delivery for small and sick newborns (Sheikh et al., 2011; WHO, 2007).

The admission of a baby to the neonatal unit is an unexpected occurrence for mothers, often leading to stress, fear, and anxiety (Wang et al., 2021; Joshi et al., 2024). However, the benefits of maternal participation in neonatal care are widely recognized, such as early hospital discharge and parent empowerment (Nyqvist and Engvall, 2009; Gómez-Cantarino et al., 2020). However, mothers' effective participation depends on both provider support and adequate physical space to enable participatory practices (WHO Immediate KMC Study Group, 2021).

Studies on parental experiences of neonatal care emphasize the importance of timely and adequate communication in alleviating these fears and anxiety (Wigert et al., 2014; Labrie et al., 2021). Beyond timely and effective communication, the neonatal intensive care unit's communication framework developed by Wreesmann et al. (2021) highlights other critical aspects of communication, including supporting decision-making, building and maintaining relationships, and empowering parents to participate in the care of their babies (Horwood et al., 2019). The orientation of mothers can also foster their participation in care and improve their experiences (O'Brien et al., 2013). It is therefore imperative that the design of the units takes into consideration the needs of mothers and care providers in ways that facilitate supportive care and promote mother participation.

The World Health Organization has established standards for improving the quality of care for small and sick newborns in hospitals (WHO, 2020). These standards outline the importance of appropriate physical environments. Standard 8.1 specifically highlights the need for safe, secure, and well-maintained environments including designated spaces to support key interventions such as Kangaroo Mother Care rooms, and layouts that allow respectful communication and privacy. Additionally, recognizing the important role of participation and involvement of mothers, standard 8.5 emphasizes the need for dedicated spaces for Kangaroo Mother Care and other spaces that ensure privacy for mothers to express breast milk. Guidance is also provided by the Consensus Committee on Recommended Design Standards for Advanced Neonatal Care of the United States of America, which outlines minimum requirements for neonatal unit designs (White, 2020, White, 2007; White et al., 2013). They recommend layouts that facilitate identifiable entrances for families, noise reduction, infection control, and adjustable lighting for babies. Spaces for administrative work and ancillary services for health providers, and a strategic location of the units near birthing areas, are also covered in the recommendations (White, 2007, White, 2020; White et al., 2013).

In Kenya, the Ministry of Health has adopted the World Health Organization standards and other standards to develop the Comprehensive Newborn Care Protocols (MOH Kenya, 2022). Newborn and Child Health Strategic Plan for 2022–2026, and the clinical guidelines for implementation of Kangaroo Mother Care (MOH Kenya, 2016, MOH Kenya, 2022, MOH Kenya, 2023) and the Comprehensive Newborn Protocols support effective communication with mothers and encourage parental involvement in care. It highlights the need for well-maintained and organized environments that are appropriately designed for family-centered care and interventions such as Kangaroo Mother Care (MOH Kenya, 2022). Further, the Newborn and Child Health Strategic Plan and the clinical guidelines for Implementation of Kangaroo Mother Care both support the need for appropriate infrastructure to enable the provision of services and sufficient space for Kangaroo Mother Care respectively (MOH Kenya, 2016, MOH Kenya, 2022, MOH Kenya, 2023). We however note that all of these Kenyan documents are silent on neonatal unit designs.

While standards and recommendations for neonatal unit design exist, much of the evidence is from high-income settings (White, 2007, White, 2020; White et al., 2013) including comparisons on the impacts of neonatal unit designs on staff, neonates, and families (Shahheidari and Homer, 2012; Doede et al., 2018; Feeley et al., 2019, Feeley et al., 2020). However, as neonatal units continue to be constructed, in settings such as Kenya, many are repurposed spaces not built with the needs of staff and mothers in mind (Feeley et al., 2019). There is limited research from low- and middle-income countries exploring how the organization of spaces and the wider physical environment within units affects the quality of care provided, the implementation of key neonatal interventions, associated supportive care, and the overall care experience.

In this paper, we draw on an ethnographic study to address this gap by exploring how the layouts and organization of three key spaces in Kenyan neonatal units - nursing stations, kangaroo Mother Care wards, and breast milk expressing areas – influences care delivery. The purpose is to identify areas for improvement, contributing to the development of evidence-based design principles for resource-limited settings.

Study setting

This study was nested within a larger project (HIGH-Q: Harnessing Innovation in Global Health to Improve Quality Care), which was implemented in four county referral hospitals in Kenya. The larger project aimed to explore strategies for implementing technologies and innovations in a limited-resource setting in a way that minimizes waste and harm while maximizing the benefits of the interventions. Two of these hospitals were located in urban areas, while the remaining two were situated in semi-urban regions (Maina et al., 2025). A summary of hospital characteristics is provided in Table 1. The study hospitals were located in Nairobi, Eastern, and the Central parts of Kenya.

Methods

We employed an ethnographic approach to allow a detailed exploration of the layout and spaces of the units and how they influence care delivery, with particular interest in the day-to-day practices, experiences, and perspectives of mothers and healthcare providers of neonatal care within their natural, real-life settings (Herbert, 2000; Higginbottom et al., 2013; Reeves et al., 2013). Data collection involved non-participant observations, combined with informal conversations and in-depth interviews with mothers and healthcare providers. The study included a focus on neonatal unit routines, layouts, and how spaces were organized and utilized across different shifts, during the day and night, and on weekdays and weekends. Data were collected by CW and NO, who are female social scientists with experience in qualitative research. CW has a background in B. A Sociology and Economics, while NO has a background in M.A Sociology, Gender and Development Studies.

Data collection in each of the four neonatal units involved in the HIGH-Q intervention arm commenced with a one-week familiarization process during which the researchers focused on developing relationships and acquainting themselves with the neonatal unit's environment, routines, mothers, and healthcare providers. Subsequently, there were four weeks of dedicated primary data collection in each hospital. Observations extended beyond 200 hours per hospital, and across all four hospitals, we conducted over 100 in-depth interviews face-to-face with purposively selected mothers and healthcare providers. The semi-structured interview guides developed by DO, NO, and CW were in Swahili and English. The Interviews were conducted in either English or Swahili, dependent on participants' preference, and audio-recorded using password-protected, encrypted recorders. The interviews with the healthcare providers were conducted within the newborn units, scheduled to accommodate the healthcare providers' availability, while the interviews with mothers were conducted within two weeks of discharge. Informed consent, voluntary participation, and confidentiality of information were observed by the researchers throughout data collection. The interviews conducted in Swahili were later translated into English for analysis. Data collection took place in three phases between January 2022 and April 2023.

Data management and analysis

Field notebooks were used by the researchers CW and NO to document daily observations. These field notes were subsequently typed into a Word document and uploaded onto NVivo 12 for management and analysis. Audio-recorded in-depth interviews were transcribed, and quality checks were conducted by CW and NO. Identifiers were removed from the transcripts. The typed-out field notes and de-identified transcripts, and audio recordings were stored on protected KEMRI Wellcome Trust Research Programme servers, on a OneDrive folder accessible only to the research team in accordance with data management protocols. The field notes and deidentified scripts were uploaded into NVivo 12 for management and analysis.

Data analysis was an iterative process with collaborative weekly debrief meetings involving CW, NO, GN, DO, EM, and CJ conducted for continuous reflection on the data, identification of emerging themes, and reflection on issues requiring further exploration until the team reached data saturation. We used a thematic approach to analyze the data, focusing on delineating the layout of the four neonatal units and describing the consequences for care delivery in three key spaces: the nursing station, the kangaroo mother care ward, and the expressed breast milk areas.

Ethical considerations

This study received ethical approval from the Kenya Medical Research Training Institute (KEMRI) Scientific and Ethical Review Committee (SERU) under the reference number KEMRI/SERU/CGMR-C/229/4203. In addition, we received approval from the National Commission for Science, Technology, and Innovation (NACOSTI/P/22/21337) and the Oxford Tropical Research Ethics Committee (Reference- 26-21). Formal approvals to execute the HIGH-Q project were diligently sought from each of the four study Counties and the respective participating hospitals (Karumba et al., 2024). For the observation in the neonatal units, we relied on oral consent. All the healthcare workers and mothers were sensitized about the observation work during the familiarization period through ward-specific engagement talks, and study information sheets containing the study details were posted on the wards. Participants who were not willing to be observed or to engage in any informal conversation were allowed to decline or withdraw their participation or choose not to be observed or approached by the study team. New mothers were continuously sensitized by the team on the purpose of the observers presence on the ward. For the formal interviews, we obtained individual written informed consent.

Results

Following a brief overview of mothers' access to neonatal units and the layout, use of space, and capacities of units, we present findings on care delivery in three key spaces in turn - nursing stations, Kangaroo Mother Care wards, and breast milk expressing areas. There was considerable variation among the hospitals in layout, uses of space, and neonatal unit capacities (Supplementary material). H1 housed the largest neonatal unit, with H3 and H4 having only about half the capacity of H1. All the neonatal units except for H3 had Kangaroo Mother Care beds, which allowed some mothers to continuously practice Kangaroo Mother Care. Despite having fewer or no Kangaroo Mother Care beds, the two smaller capacity hospitals (H3 and H4) did have an area for expressing breast milk. In three of the hospitals (H2, H3, and H4), the nursing station was in an open space within the neonatal unit, while in H1, the nursing station was in an enclosed office. This enclosed office was not visible from the neonatal unit entrance, and neither was the nursing station in H2. However, in both H3 and H4, the nurses' station was visible from the main entrance (Supplementary material).

Regarding maternal access to neonatal units, in all four study hospitals, mothers were accommodated in the postnatal ward (see Supplementary material). They followed a routine that allowed one-hour visits to their babies in the neonatal unit every three hours. Each visit lasted for approximately 1 hour. The morning visit was the busiest of all the visits because, other than feeding and diaper changing, mothers were expected to clean the babies, and in H1, they superficially cleaned the cots or incubators.

We present results based on the areas and how their organization and space influence care delivery by mothers and nurses.

The nursing station

Our observations revealed that the nursing station was one of the key hubs for information exchange between healthcare providers and mothers, occurring through group or individual interactions. However, variations in nursing station spatial arrangements and available space influenced communication practices across the study hospitals.

Group health talks

In all four neonatal units, group health talk sessions followed a one-way communication model, with mothers responding to the nurse facilitating the session only when directly requested. Group health talks were one of the primary ways in which the mothers received information about their expected roles in the units, how they were expected to behave, and when they were required to perform specific tasks. Roles such as diaper changing and cleaning the baby were commonly performed by mothers in neonatal units in Kenya (Oluoch et al., 2023). The talks were often used by the nurses to inform and reinforce the requirement for mothers to follow the scheduled feeding times and to stress the need for maintaining infant hygiene.

In H3, the group health talks at the nursing station were also used to orient mothers new to the unit. During these sessions, nurses identified new mothers and provided general information about unit routines:

“The nurse would come [to the place outside the nursing station where mothers gathered for health talks] …talk to mothers and tell them what was going on, then we enter the ward…You first learn what things you are supposed to carry when going to the ward because there were some things that you were supposed to have in the ward and how you are supposed to be dressed.”

(IDI_ Mother, H3)

In two of the largest units (H1 and H2), where the number of mothers discharged daily was very high, nurses held group talks at the nursing station as part of the discharge process. However, if there was a need to share additional information that was specific to a mother, the nurses would share that with the individual mother as they provided the hospital discharge summaries.

While all of the neonatal units had some designated space adjacent to nursing stations that could be used for group health talks, the size of these areas and the location of the nursing station significantly impacted their suitability for these talks. In H1, the narrow, long space adjacent to the nursing station also served as an admission bay, limiting its potential for group gatherings. Mothers sometimes congregated in this area during discharge, but due to the small space, some were forced to line up along the corridor leading to the nursing station, hindering the ability of those at the back to hear the information being conveyed during discharge.

In H4, the nursing station was also located at the end of a busy corridor where meals were served to mothers, and there was little space for them to gather. This space also served as a path for mothers and healthcare providers accessing various rooms in the unit, further compromising its suitability for group health talks. In H2, the space adjacent to the nursing station was a convergence point for critical areas such as the High Dependency Unit, acute rooms, and a corridor leading to numerous other rooms. Additionally, a working table accommodating several clinicians was situated nearby, further constricting the available space and rendering it unsuitable for group health talks.

By contrast, in H3, there was ample space next to the nursing station where mothers could gather for health talks. There was also minimal disruption in this area, and thus, the nursing stations served as a focal point for these sessions. Its strategic location near the neonatal unit entrance and the mothers' changing room facilitated the convenient assembly of mothers for group health talks on critical topics such as baby hygiene, cord care, and general health practices. It is also likely that the norm of conducting these talks in the morning was strategic, as it was during the busiest feeding hour of the day when mothers participated in various caring activities for their babies.

“When we talk with mothers… you've not come in the morning when I'm teaching there... when they are coming in, we teach them about breastfeeding, and personal hygiene, and we also encourage them especially those who have premature [babies], I also want to say, that I may be the nurse but there is nothing as good as hearing it from experience. So, you introduce this mother who is coming [new mother]. Like one who had three premature, I literally told her, ‘Please speak to someone who had a child less than 1 kg [kilogram]’, and who is already in kangaroo [practising kangaroo mother care] so that they also see it from experience, it's possible. Don't think that it's not possible. Wholesomely, we teach them about anything. We teach them about family planning and ask them to ask questions as long as they are here [in the neonatal units] in the morning before we start our routine.”

(IDI_ Healthcare provider, H3)

One-on-one communication

In addition to group health talks, individual communication between nurses and mothers was observed across all sites, including the nursing station. However, privacy-related challenges emerged. For instance, discussions regarding infant mortality and grief counseling often took place at the nursing station, despite it often being a high-traffic area with multiple simultaneous activities. A healthcare provider highlighted this limitation during an interview and emphasized the need for dedicated spaces that facilitate confidential communication.

“We don't have a place where we can sit down and talk with the mother and tell the mother one, two, three… we don't have a place where we can go inside in a room, talk with the mother, because our reception area [nursing station], it's where we sit, and it's a place where the mother are passing up and down….”

(IDI_ Healthcare provider, H4)

In H1 and H3, maintaining privacy during sensitive conversations was facilitated by the strategic location of the nursing station away from the care rooms. However, in the other two hospitals, the open design of the nursing station, situated at the intersection of multiple corridors, or its proximity to storage areas, compromised privacy. The constant flow of healthcare providers and other staff members engaged in various tasks frequently disrupted sensitive discussions, hindering effective communication, as illustrated in Box 1.

Orientation and regulation of entry to the neonatal unit

The visibility and strategic placement of the nursing station were crucial factors in regulating access to the neonatal unit and the orientation of mothers. Orientation involves providing mothers with essential information about procedures, expectations, and directions to key areas such as care rooms and pantries, as well as where to obtain feeding cups and gowns. In the study hospitals, there was no formally structured orientation practice, and the visible nursing station allowed healthcare providers to easily identify incoming mothers, facilitating timely information provision and orientation.

In H3 and H4, where the nursing stations were highly visible, nurses could easily monitor entry points and regulate access to care spaces. In contrast, H1 and H2, which had less visible nursing stations, presented challenges in maintaining oversight. Specifically, new mothers, unaware of the specific location of their infants, often followed other mothers or wandered through the unit in search of their babies. In some instances, mothers would manage to identify their babies but struggled to keep up with the routines for the unit. The mothers would rely on other mothers to learn and practice the unit's routines. When these attempts were unsuccessful, some struggled to seek assistance from the nursing staff, as demonstrated by the quote from a healthcare provider.

“…sometimes you may notice a mother has entered the room without you knowing …like X [another mother] can see her before then they [other mothers] tell her what to do then she comes to me so that I may show her, her child [baby]. Assuming she [the other mother] was not there, maybe the mother would have gotten lost in the ward [since she is new in the ward and does not know what to do or where to go].”

(IDI_ Healthcare provider, H2)

H1 supplemented its enclosed central nursing station with secondary nursing desks strategically located within the Acute and Preterm Babies rooms, enhancing accessibility and workflow efficiency within the unit. Nurses positioned at the desks could readily recognize and assist new mothers. Even if a new mother chose to locate her baby independently, the prominent location and design of the nursing station served as a clear point of reference for seeking assistance. The presence of patient files on the nursing station's table enabled nurses to accurately verify the baby's location and direct the mother accordingly.

Kangaroo Mother Care space and layout

Across the four neonatal units, Kangaroo Mother Care was implemented with varying degrees of intensity, ranging from intermittent to continuous. Three neonatal units (H4, H1, H2) had designated Kangaroo Mother Care rooms with limited capacity of 2, 3, and 4 beds, respectively. However, H3 had no beds in the designated Kangaroo Mother Care room, thus mothers practiced intermittent Kangaroo Mother Care during scheduled feeding times (Supplementary material). KMC spaces were highly valued as they allowed the mother to spend more time with her baby.

“I would have preferred to stay in the kangaroo [Kangaroo Mother Care ward], except the baby started vomiting [baby moved from Kangaroo Mother Care to the preterm room], it was better to be in the kangaroo. With the kangaroo, you sleep with your baby, you know? You place your baby on your chest and bond with your baby.”

(IDI_ Mother, H1)

They were also told of the benefits of Kangaroo Mother Care by the healthcare providers.

“She [healthcare provider] told me that Kangaroo Mother Care helps with the growth of the baby because of the body heat [from mothers contact with the baby]. And then it also helps them to add weight and improves the bonding between the mother and the baby.”

(IDI_ Mother, H2)

“…I heard that children develop fast while in that room under that care [Kangaroo Mother Care] …”

(IDI_ Mother, H4)

However, due to the small number of available beds, the optimal implementation of continuous Kangaroo Mother Care was limited in all three hospitals. In H3, where there were no Kangaroo Mother Care beds, intermittent Kangaroo Mother Care took place in a small, overcrowded room. There were two room heaters in the room which made it stuffy. Mothers attempted to cope by sitting some distance away from the heater, however, this led to overcrowding in small spaces, away from the effects of the heater.

“…the problem was sitting when we were doing kangaroo, so we didn't fit because everybody was afraid to stay where there is a heater.”

(IDI_ Mother, H3)

In addition, without a dedicated Kangaroo Mother Care room, mothers had to balance feeding, cleaning the babies, and practicing Kangaroo Mother Care within a tight schedule, often leading to compromised duration of practicing.

The nurse on duty puts pressure on us (mothers) so we cannot practice Kangaroo Mother Care for long. I wish Kangaroo was scheduled [continuous]. Although the sister [nurse] told me we should practice it for 45 minutes, we are pressed for time here…

(Field notes, H3)

The need for dedicated Kangaroo Mother Care wards with adequate space and conducive environments that would allow mothers to be with their babies was frequently emphasized by both mothers and healthcare providers in H3.

“If it was full time, if these mothers had a ward which they were sharing with their baby's full time, … She [mother] would be sleeping with the baby. You know now the mother would be on a bed with her baby.”

(IDI_ Healthcare provider, H3)

Expressed Breast Milk spaces, and layout

Mothers with babies on nasogastric tubes and cup feeding were required to express milk before proceeding to feed their babies. Of the four neonatal units, H4 and H3 had well-secluded milk-expressing areas containing chairs, a sink, and a table for the mothers to use. In H3, the space was at the farthest side of the unit, away from the rooms, while in H4, it was a part of the preterm babies' room. These designated milk-expressing areas within the units provided a conducive environment for the mothers to interact with each other.

“Sometimes you will hear them talking about the nurses. Sometimes they just talk about life.”

(IDI_ Mother, H3)

These spaces also facilitated the interaction between mothers and healthcare providers. Since all mothers involved in expressing were congregated in a single space, this allowed a healthcare provider to more easily support a group of mothers and identify mothers experiencing challenges in expressing milk, enabling timely and targeted assistance to be provided.

“It's when they come to express the milk I [healthcare provider] go around, I look for mothers who are maybe stranded then for those who look concerned, then we reassure them [offer support].”

(IDI_ Healthcare provider, H3)

“…when they [mothers] congregate together as they express breast milk, you're able to demonstrate to them how to do it [express milk].”

(IDI_ Healthcare provider, H3)

In units where there were no dedicated expressing spaces, mothers engaged in expressing milk would do so while seated or standing near their baby's cots or incubators. With this arrangement, and with mothers spread across several rooms together with few healthcare providers available to monitor the various spaces, it was difficult to identify mothers who were struggling to express milk.

Discussion

In this study, we have explored the layout and organization of three key spaces in Kenyan neonatal units: the nursing station as a space for communication, and how availability, size, and layout of the Kangaroo Mother Care and milk-expressing rooms influence care delivery in a resource-limited setting where mothers contribute immensely to caring for their babies. Neonatal unit designs have changed over time (Doyle and Darlow, 2021). Additionally, recommended standards and guidelines for the layout and design of neonatal units exist (White et al., 2013; WHO, 2020).However, due to challenges in resource availability and infrastructure, adopting and implementing these standards remains a constraint in resource-constrained settings, including Kenya (Barría, 2024). The results of this study from four neonatal units in Kenya highlight how the design and layout of physical spaces in resource-constrained settings can hinder effective nurse-mother communication and mothers' participation in the care of their babies (Beck et al., 2009; Domanico et al., 2010; Fay et al., 2022), in turn, compromising the quality of care delivered.

Our observations revealed that nursing stations functioned as central hubs for information exchange between healthcare providers and mothers. Communication was through both group health talks and one-on-one interactions. However, variations in the location and space available around the nursing stations across the study hospitals significantly shaped these communication practices. In all four units of this study, group talks played a pivotal role in nurse-mother communication, partly due to staff shortages. Group talks have been widely adopted as a standard method for sharing information with mothers in hospitals in Kenya (Doede et al., 2018). Our data suggest that the design, size, and location of the nursing station where these group talks take place impact the effectiveness of this information sharing, with some mothers missing out on critical information.

We found that nurses sometimes shared specific information with individual mothers. This communication often took place at the nursing station and - in the three neonatal units where the nursing station was in an open space - this compromised privacy. Previous studies have demonstrated that communication in open spaces or corridors can hinder privacy and breach confidentiality (White, 2011; Fay et al., 2022). These findings underscore the need for context-appropriate communication strategies that balance efficiency with the individualized, respectful, and private interactions that are essential to offering high-quality, family-centred neonatal care (Wigert et al., 2014). Investing in spatial redesigns could help bridge this gap and enhance the overall delivery of care.

The nursing station has also been shown in other studies to be central to monitoring the entry of mothers, staff, and visitors into the neonatal unit. As such, the location of the nurses' station affects the ability of nurses to identify and orient new mothers who come into the neonatal unit. For mothers entering the neonatal unit for the first time, the experience is often unexpected, disorientating, and frightening (Wigert et al., 2014). In our study, we observed that new mothers who had not been identified or picked up at entry struggled to locate their babies and to find their way around the neonatal unit, adding to their stress and anxiety.

Spatial constraints not only limits communication with and monitoring of mothers, but also mothers' involvement in key interventions such as Kangaroo Mother Care, which is closely linked to supportive care and positive care experiences (Seidman et al., 2015). This study adds to the evidence by showing how unit layout affects both maternal participation and healthcare providers' ability to offer support. While both intermittent and continuous Kangaroo Mother Care were observed, continuous KMC was more feasible in settings with sufficient bed space (Yue et al., 2020; Morgan et al., 2018), facilitating uninterrupted bonding, monitoring, and integration into care (Beck et al., 2009; Morgan et al., 2018; Foong et al., 2023; Sjömar et al., 2023). Expressing milk is a necessity for many mothers with a baby in the neonatal unit. The World Health Organization provides recommendations for neonatal units to include spaces that support mothers in expressing milk privately, as well as adequate spaces for expressing milk next to the baby (WHO, 2020). Research suggests that the availability of dedicated spaces makes mothers feel more comfortable and encourages them to express milk (Thompson and Heflin, 2011)and that the lack of private space is a barrier to breastfeeding support (Chabeda et al., 2021). Our findings support this research. We documented variations in the spaces provided for expressing across our study hospitals. In hospitals with dedicated shared spaces, and as Oluoch (2020) highlights, mothers were better able to form bonds and connections for peer support, and healthcare providers were better able to identify mothers who needed help.

The above findings highlight the importance of layouts, as well as considerations for effective nurse-mother communication and the privacy and comfort needs of mothers. This underscores the importance of tailoring neonatal unit designs to the specific needs of mothers and healthcare providers.

Strengths and limitations

This study used an ethnographic approach, which allowed us to collect rich contextual data using multiple methods (non-participant observations, informal conversations, and in-depth interviews from four neonatal units located in public referral hospitals in Kenya. Time spent collecting data across different shifts covering day and night and weekdays and weekends in the units contributes to a nuanced understanding of the contexts of the four neonatal units in semi-urban and urban regions in Kenya.

Regarding the limitations, we note the subjective nature of observations. Each researcher collected data in two hospitals however we utilized organized weekly debrief sessions where we shared data with the larger project team. These sessions helped maintain rigor. The findings from this study cannot be generalized due to the small number of hospitals studied. However, key learnings can be transferred to similar settings.

Conclusion

This study highlights how layouts and organization of spaces affect care delivery by either facilitating or hindering mothers' participation in caring for their hospitalized neonates. These constraints also impact communication between healthcare providers and mothers and limit the support offered to mothers. There is a need for careful planning of layouts and spaces in neonatal units to support the users' and healthcare provider needs. Further, the designs and layouts must be flexible based on the contexts for optimal care delivery in neonatal units in low-resource settings.
