Inhaled formulations for bacterial pneumonia: Strategies and advances in drug delivery
Authors: Yayun Wu, Weinan Zhang, Yechun Jiang, Wanyue Fu, Yutao Zha, Nian Liu, Ming Fang, Zhaoyou Chu, Min Shao, Haisheng Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInhaled formulations have garnered significant interest in the treatment of bacterial pneumonia, as they deliver the drug directly to the site of infection in the lungs, providing high local drug concentrations that are typically unattainable with oral or intravenous therapies. Technologies for inhalation formulations that enable efficient sterilization, effective nebulization, maximized lung deposition, and reduced macrophage uptake are critical for successful inhaled drug delivery. Consequently, formulation strategies for inhalation drug delivery aimed at treating bacterial pneumonia are of paramount importance, encompassing factors such as the selection of bactericidal agents, drug carriers, aerodynamic diameter of particles, and particle morphology. The appropriate selection of formulation strategies is essential to overcoming the barriers of the respiratory tract, reaching the infected sites, releasing the bactericides, and penetrating deep into the biofilm to achieve effective eradication of bacterial pneumonia. This paper aims to classify recent inhaled preparations for bacterial pneumonia and to provide insights and perspectives for the development of novel inhaled delivery formulations targeting this condition.Keywords: Bacterial pneumonia, Inhalation drug delivery, Drug carriers, Inhaled formulations, Pharmaceutical strategies

Introduction

According to the World Health Statistics 2024 report by the World Health Organization (WHO), lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) ranked as the fifth leading cause of death worldwide in 2021. The African region was identified as the primary contributor to this mortality, a distinction it shares with low-income areas [1]. LRTIs encompass a variety of pathologies, including tracheobronchitis, bronchitis, and pneumonia [2,3]. Among these conditions, pneumonia is associated with the highest morbidity and mortality rates. Pneumonia can be classified by pathogen type into viral infections (e.g., COVID-19, H1N1 influenza), bacterial infections (e.g., tuberculosis, bacterial pneumonia), fungal infections (e.g., pulmonary candidiasis, pulmonary aspergillosis), and mycoplasma infections [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. The prevailing treatment strategy for bacterial pneumonia involves the administration of antibiotics, either orally or intravenously [9]. However, only a limited amount of the drug reaches the deep alveoli or epithelial lining fluid (ELF) following traditional oral or intravenous antibiotic administration [10]. Consequently, elevated dosages are required to maintain therapeutic drug levels at the site of infection above their minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). Paradoxically, when administered at doses that achieve effective therapeutic concentrations in the lungs, these therapeutic drugs can exhibit significant systemic toxicity. For instance, aminoglycoside antibiotics may cause ototoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and neurotoxicity at high doses [11].

Inhaled drug delivery has garnered significant attention in the treatment of pulmonary diseases due to the substantial surface area of the lung, which can span up to 100 m2, and the relatively thin alveolar epithelium layer that facilitates the absorption of therapeutic agents [12]. Inhalation delivers the drug directly to the site of infection in the lungs, bypassing first-pass metabolism in the liver and resulting in high localized drug concentrations that are not typically achieved with oral or intravenous therapy. This approach reduces systemic drug concentrations and minimizes exposure of the drug to the gut microbiota [13,14]. Compared to conventional drug delivery methods, the inhalation route for administering pharmaceuticals offers numerous advantages, including rapid onset of action, enhanced efficacy, minimized systemic side effects, and direct targeting of the organ of interest. This method is particularly prevalent in the treatment of critically ill patients suffering from respiratory diseases [15]. However, despite the numerous benefits of inhaled drug delivery, the natural barriers and clearance mechanisms in the airways that protect the lungs from inhaled irritants can also hinder drug deposition [16]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, these mechanisms include mucosal barriers, ciliary clearance, macrophage phagocytosis, and lymphatic transport.

Respiratory barrier and clearance mechanisms.

The engineering of technologies for inhalation formulations that facilitate efficient sterilization, effective nebulization, maximization of lung deposition, and reduction of macrophage uptake is imperative for the successful development of pulmonary inhalation drug delivery systems [17]. Consequently, formulation strategies for inhalation drug delivery in the treatment of bacterial pneumonia are of significant importance. These strategies encompass the selection of bactericides, drug carriers, aerodynamic diameter of particles, particle morphology and density, solubility, surface tension, and other relevant factors. The selection of one or more combinations aims to overcome the natural and pathological barriers of the respiratory tract, reach infected sites, release bactericides, and penetrate deep into biofilms to achieve effective eradication of bacterial pneumonia. Drug carriers, which are diverse as illustrated in Fig. 2, play a pivotal role in pulmonary delivery by not only loading biocides and improving their solubility but also reducing airway irritation and protecting them from degradation. Additionally, they perform multiple functions, including group modification, targeting specific lesion sites, and enabling environmentally triggered release.

Bacterial pneumonia inhaled formulations classified by carriers.

This paper first provides an overview of clinical treatment regimens and administration routes for bacterial pneumonia. Subsequently, it discusses the pathophysiological barriers and challenges associated with pulmonary inhalation delivery in this condition. Finally, it reviews the inhalation therapies that have emerged in recent years for the treatment of bacterial pneumonia. The classification of inhaled drugs is based on the type of carrier used for the microbicide. Carriers include substances such as lipids, polymers, and metal-based nanomaterials that can encapsulate or absorb the antimicrobial agents. It is important to note that solutions for aerosols and amino acid or sugar-based dry powders for inhalation are considered excipients that enhance the formulation, rather than serving as carriers. This review classifies inhalation formulations based on carrier type, and summarises the latest advancements in inhalation therapy for bacterial pneumonia. The aim is to provide insights and inspiration for the development of novel inhalation drug delivery systems targeting bacterial pneumonia.

Bacterial pneumonia treatment options

The selection of clinical treatment routes (oral, intravenous, or inhaled) for bacterial pneumonia is influenced by the characteristics of the pathogen and the severity of the disease. Initially, the severity of patients with bacterial pneumonia is assessed and scored using rating scales such as CURB-65, CRB-65, or the pneumonia severity index (PSI). These instruments are designed to stratify patients into three distinct risk categories (low, intermediate, high) to guide therapeutic decisions, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Patients classified as low risk were prescribed oral medication on an outpatient basis [18].

Bacterial pneumonia: stratified therapy.

Oral or intra-venous routes

According to clinical guidelines, the treatment of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in young adults without underlying diseases, caused predominantly by bacterial pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae) and Haemophilus influenzae (H. influenzae), should involve oral antibiotics that exhibit good bioavailability. The recommended treatments include oral penicillin/enzyme inhibitor complexes and first/second generation cephalosporin antibiotics. In contrast, the most common bacterial pathogens in CAP among individuals with underlying medical conditions or in older adults are S. pneumoniae and Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae). For these cases, the use of oral penicillin/enzyme inhibitor complexes, second/third generation cephalosporin antibiotics, and respiratory quinolones is advised. This category of treatment is considered moderate and may require hospitalization, with antibiotics administered either orally or intravenously. For severe cases necessitating admission to intensive care units (ICU), the predominant pathogens include S. pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Legionella, K. pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa). Therapy for these cases is recommended to include third generation cephalosporins, carbapenems, quinolones, and the combination of macrolides or aminoglycoside antibiotics [19,20].

Inhaled treatment routes

There are relatively few approved and marketed inhaled formulations for the treatment of pulmonary infections. The first inhaled formulation approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for pulmonary infections is tobramycin (TOB) aerosol, which is used in conjunction with the specialized Air 360 series mesh nebulizer, as shown in Table 1. This formulation is specifically indicated for bronchiectasis associated with pulmonary P. aeruginosa infection in adults and received approval from the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) in China in 2022 [21]. Innovations such as amikacin liposome, which is encapsulated in liposomes to reduce airway reactions and toxicity, are utilized in nebulized inhalation therapy [22]. Recent clinical trials have demonstrated that nebulized antimicrobial inhalation has a positive impact on the eradication of respiratory pathogens and enhances patient prognosis [[23], [24], [25]]. Accordingly, the management guidelines for adults with hospital-acquired and ventilator-associated pneumonia recommend the use of antimicrobial nebulized inhalation therapy for pneumonia caused by gram-negative bacilli that are susceptible only to aminoglycosides or polymyxins, or for patients who cannot tolerate intravenous infusions. Additionally, for pneumonia caused by Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii) that is susceptible only to polymyxins, the guidelines suggest intravenous administration of polymyxin supplemented by nebulized inhalation of polymyxin [26]. Inhalation therapy may serve as a last-resort treatment for bacterial pneumonia when intravenous injections prove ineffective.

Difficulties and challenges in inhalation therapy for bacterial pneumonia

Respiratory pathophysiological barrier

The respiratory tract consists of the conducting airways, which include the nose, mouth, pharynx, larynx, trachea, and bronchi, as well as the respiratory zone, which encompasses the respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, and alveoli [27]. The pulmonary airways exhibit a bronchial structure akin to a tree, with the airways progressively narrowing as they extend from the trachea. A total of 24 structural levels can be identified from the trachea to the alveoli, with terminal alveoli measuring approximately 0.2 mm in diameter. An adult possesses approximately 300 to 400 million alveoli, collectively covering an area of nearly 100 m2 [28].

The respiratory tract defends against the invasion of harmful substances through a multi-layered composite system. The nasopharynx, oropharynx, and branching airways constitute the primary filtration barrier, specifically targeting larger particles. In conjunction with this, the mucociliary clearance system of the airway epithelium effectively removes deposited particles [29]. The mucosal barrier of the airway epithelium serves a crucial role as an innate defense mechanism, preventing the entry of particulate matter, pathogens, and toxic substances. Phagocytic cells, such as macrophages, can digest potentially toxic substances that breach these initial barriers and transport some intra- and extra-alveolar components via the lymphatic transport system. During bacterial infections in the lungs, goblet cells in the airway mucosa produce abundant mucus [30]. However, the excessive accumulation of mucus, along with the resultant thickened mucus layer, hinders the effective delivery of active ingredients in airway diseases [31]. Furthermore, the biofilm produced by bacterial pathogens in the respiratory tract not only exacerbates mucus secretion but also creates another dense barrier that impedes the penetration of bactericidal agents [32].

Challenges in inhalation delivery formulations for bacterial pneumonia

Particle/Droplet diameter: Currently, powder inhalation formulations for pulmonary delivery can achieve suitable aerodynamic diameters of particles through techniques such as grinding, freeze-drying, spray drying, and spray freeze-drying [33,34]. Furthermore, inhalation solutions can modulate the aerodynamic diameter of inhaled droplets by utilizing ultrasonic nebulizers and vibrating mesh screens [35].

Mucociliary clearance: The airway epithelial mucosal barrier serves as a critical innate defense mechanism that restricts the efficiency of inhaled medication delivery by preventing the entry of particulate matter, pathogens, and toxic substances. This barrier facilitates the rapid clearance of foreign substances through the mucociliary clearance system. In various pulmonary diseases, excessive mucus accumulation and the resultant thickening of the mucus layer impede the effective delivery of active ingredients in common airway disorders [38].

Biofilms: The formation of biofilms by microorganisms associated with pulmonary infections significantly reduces the efficacy of antibiotics, making treatment exceedingly challenging [39]. As shown in Fig. 4A, bacterial biofilms are multicellular aggregates formed by highly organized microbial communities encased in an extracellular matrix. This structure acts as a physical barrier that inhibits the penetration of bactericidal agents [36]. In comparison to planktonic bacteria, bacterial communities within mature biofilms can demonstrate resistance to antibiotic treatment that is up to 1000-fold greater [40].

Macrophage clearance: The bifurcated tracheal anatomy and the terminal alveolar diameter of the respiratory tract dictate that the optimal diameter of drug particles or droplets capable of reaching the terminal alveoli via airflow ranges from 1 to 5 μm. Particles larger than 5 μm tend to deposit in the upper airways due to impaction, while those within the 1-5 μm range effectively reach the deep lungs through inertial impaction and sedimentation. Although particles smaller than 1 μm can penetrate deep into the lungs via Brownian diffusion, they are often exhaled due to insufficient deposition time [41]. Notably, particles within the 1-3 μm size range are particularly susceptible to phagocytosis by alveolar macrophages, resulting in premature clearance and diminished therapeutic retention [42].

Drug safety: High doses of drugs and excipients in inhalation formulations present unique challenges, particularly concerning irritant cough, throat irritation, and bronchospasm (Fig. 4B) [37,43]. The physicochemical properties of bactericides, such as lipophilicity, water solubility, and stability in airway surface liquid, directly influence their suitability for inhalation administration [44].

In light of the aforementioned challenges, we have summarized recent advancements in inhaled formulations for bacterial pneumonia and highlighted the characteristics of various inhaled formulations. The characteristics of these formulations in addressing key biological barriers are summarized in Table 2. Regarding size control, inhaled formulations that utilize polymers, inorganic nanomaterials, or liposomes as carriers offer the advantage of controllable size. Various types of carriers exist, including inorganic nanomaterials, biomimetic carriers, and polymeric carriers, each exhibiting distinctive advantages due to their structural design, environmentally responsive release properties, and targeted efficacy against biofilms and bacteria. Furthermore, these carriers have been shown to effectively overcome mucus barriers and penetrate deeply into biofilms. Bionic structures enable these formulations to actively evade macrophage phagocytosis, while polymers achieve similar outcomes through programmable surface designs that either evade or specifically target macrophages. Nonetheless, biosafety remains a paramount consideration. Non-carried formulations and lipid-carried formulations are universally recognized as having superior safety profiles compared to other carrier systems. A classification and summary of inhaled formulations for treating bacterial pneumonia has been conducted, along with a detailed critical analysis of the latest developments in the field. The objective of this publication is to provide its readership with a prospective viewpoint.

Bacterial pneumonia inhaled formulations

In recent years, significant advancements have been made in the research domain concerning inhalation formulations for bacterial pneumonia. In addition to those already available on the market, a substantial body of research has been conducted on inhalation formulations targeting various bacteria and employing diverse carriers. This review systematically analyzes the current inhalation drug delivery systems for the treatment of bacterial pneumonia, with particular reference to the classification of carriers utilized for antimicrobial agents. Inhaled formulations can be categorized into two primary types based on the carrier type for the antimicrobial agent: non-carried inhaled formulations and carried inhaled formulations. Inhaled delivery of non-carried formulations

Currently, the available pulmonary inhaled anti-infective formulations are predominantly focused on antibiotic preparations, which involve reformulating approved antibiotics into inhalation-compatible forms, such as nebulized solutions and dry powder formulations, for localized respiratory delivery. For instance, TOB inhalation formulations include aerosols and dry powder inhalers (DPIs), while polymyxins are developed as colistimethate sodium aerosols and DPIs [69,70]. Moreover, existing inhaled antibiotic applications demonstrate significant clinical utility in managing P. aeruginosa infections associated with cystic fibrosis (CF), with amikacin liposome inhalation suspension being applicable for Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) infections. However, the therapeutic efficacy remains suboptimal against other clinically relevant respiratory pathogens. One current development pathway involves the use of pre-existing antibiotics that are directly sprayed with excipients (e.g., mannitol, lactose, L-leucine, etc.) to create carrier-free inhalable anti-infective formulations [45,48]. The strategic repurposing of established antibiotic agents through pulmonary delivery optimization presents a cost-effective and scientifically viable approach to broaden antimicrobial coverage against diverse pulmonary pathogens.

Macrolides inhaled formulations

Azithromycin (AZM), a macrolide antibiotic with a broad spectrum of activity, has been demonstrated to be effective against a variety of gram-positive, gram-negative, and atypical pathogens associated with pneumonia, including S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (M. pneumoniae) [71]. While conventional oral or intravenous administration achieves systemic therapeutic effects for bacterial pneumonia, these routes are hindered by non-targeted biodistribution and limited pulmonary bioavailability, often necessitating escalated doses to attain effective drug concentrations in critical lung compartments, such as the ELF [72]. The development of AZM-loaded DPIs represents a targeted pulmonary delivery strategy. Fumaroyl diketopyrimidine (FDKP) serves as an excipient, and upon co-spraying with AZM, it forms AZM@FDKP-MPs powder. Following inhalation, FDKP rapidly dissolves under physiological pH conditions. The AZM@FDKP-MPs prepared via spray drying exhibit excellent atomization performance and enhanced moisture resistance. Compared to intravenous and oral administration, AZM@FDKP-MPs delivered via the airway have been shown to provide significantly higher local concentrations and longer retention times [45]. To enhance the deposition efficiency of inhaled dry powder in the alveoli and achieve rapid release of AZM, the formulation was optimized by co-spray drying with citric acid and sodium carbonate, resulting in inhalable effervescent powders (AZM@FDKP-E-MPs). This DPI system utilizes effervescent reactions to actively promote antibiotic release, avoid macrophage phagocytosis, and ensure uniform distribution in the deep lung regions. The inhalable effervescent powder demonstrated potent antibacterial activity against S. pneumoniae and exhibited excellent therapeutic effects in a pneumococcal infection animal model, significantly reducing dosing frequency and improving safety compared to non-effervescent microspheres and intravenous administration [73].

Polymyxins inhaled formulations

Polymyxin B (PMB) sulfate is a last-resort therapeutic option for treating gram-negative bacterial infections, particularly those caused by multi-drug resistant (MDR) strains [74]. Clinical guidelines recommend that patients with hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) or ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) caused by extensively drug-resistant (XDR) Gram-negative bacteria, who require intravenous polymyxin therapy, should also receive adjunctive polymyxin aerosol therapy [75]. However, its clinical use is limited due to the nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity associated with both oral and intravenous administration [76]. Nevertheless, the development of PMB-loaded inhaled formulations for effective pulmonary infection management faces a fundamental biological barrier: the presence of pathogenic biofilms, particularly those formed by P. aeruginosa, which significantly hinder drug penetration through their structural matrices. To address this challenge, Zhou et al. employed a strategic approach by targeting the extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) components present within bacterial biofilms. High-lectin-affinity (HLA) sugars, such as raffinose, exhibit dual functionality as both biofilm-disrupting adjuvants and efficient DPI excipients. Regulating the construction of surface microstructures can improve the aerodynamic performance of DPI and enhance aerosolization. By spray-drying PMB with raffinose into inhalable composite particles, the developed system leverages raffinose's ability to penetrate biofilm matrices and bind competitively to lectins, thereby enhancing PMB access to biofilm-embedded bacterial populations (Fig. 5A). This synergistic mechanism facilitates concurrent biofilm structural destabilization and improved eradication of matrix-protected pathogens. The optimized PMB-HLA DPI formulation demonstrates clinically relevant anti-infective efficacy against P. aeruginosa pulmonary infections through targeted interference with the biofilm life cycle [46]. The strategic integration of biofilm-modulating agents with antimicrobial compounds establishes a viable pathway to enhance the therapeutic scope of inhaled formulations against resistant respiratory pathogens.

Aminoglycoside inhaled formulations

Chronic respiratory infections caused by bacterial biofilms present significant therapeutic challenges due to barriers in drug penetration into complex biofilm structures and the high resistance of biofilms [77]. Intravenous drugs can reach pulmonary infection biofilms by utilizing cell membrane modifications and targeting the infected microenvironment [78,79]. However, there are limited options for excipients in inhalation formulations that specifically target bacterial biofilms. OligoG (Oli), a low-molecular-weight alginate oligosaccharide, has been shown to possess significant disruptive activity against mucoid biofilm matrices. Researchers engineered an innovative inhaled powder that combines ciprofloxacin (CIP) with Oli, exploiting their cationic charge complementarity to achieve synergistic mucoid biofilm lysis and antimicrobial action against respiratory pathogens [47]. Parallel investigations explored N-acetylcysteine (NAC)-an established mucolytic agent-in combination with AZM, CIP, and TOB. These combinatorial systems yield multifunctional DPI integrating biofilm matrix destabilization with targeted antibacterial activity [80]. While the Tobi® Podhaler DPIs hold US regulatory approval for patients older than 6 years, its pulmonary delivery efficiency in children remains relatively low. Researchers have enhanced the DPI; under pediatric testing conditions, the pulmonary delivery efficiency of a model drug utilizing excipient-enhanced growth (EEG) technology reached up to 73.4% of the loaded dose [81]. The Rudra team has developed a novel TOB dry powder aerosol employing EEG technology. The TOB powder formulations have a micron size of less than 1 μm, which minimizes oropharyngeal deposition during inhalation. As illustrated by the mouse lung imaging in Fig. 5B, the TOB powder formulations, administered via the trachea at loading doses of 2 mg and 5 mg, achieved lung deposition rates of approximately 65% and 70%, respectively. Animal studies demonstrated that the new TOB EEG powder formulation exhibits similar therapeutic efficacy and antimicrobial potency compared to the Tobi® Podhaler powder formulation in inhibiting the growth of P. aeruginosa [82]. Combining colistin (COL) with TOB at mass ratios of 1: 1 and 1: 5 significantly enhances the in vitro aerosolization performance and stability of the combined dry powder formulation compared to TOB DPI. Furthermore, given the established synergistic antibacterial effects of COL and TOB, the developed COL-TOB spray-dried compound formulation presents substantial application prospects for the treatment of pulmonary infections caused by multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria [83].

Beta-lactam inhaled formulations

As one of the earliest and most widely used β-lactam antibiotics, first-generation cephalosporins, such as cefazolin (CE), remain critical first-line agents for treating common infections and surgical antimicrobial prophylaxis [84]. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, pharmacokinetic data reveal that following nebulized inhalation in mice, serum concentrations of CE peaked after an 8-min delay before gradually declining. In a mouse model of K. pneumoniae infection, the nebulized administration of CE exhibited significantly greater antibacterial efficacy compared to intraperitoneal dosing [85]. However, CE's limited gram-negative coverage necessitated development of broader-spectrum third-generation cephalosporins exemplified by cefixime (CFM), which targets both gram-negative and gram-positive pathogens. Despite achieving adequate oral absorption, CFM suffers from suboptimal bioavailability attributable to poor aqueous solubility. To circumvent this limitation, investigators implemented particle size reduction through nanoprecipitation coupled with spray-freeze-drying (SFD) to generate respirable CFM microparticles. By incorporating various excipients and subjecting the CFM nano-suspension to SFD processing, the resulting particles demonstrate an ideal spherical morphology, as evidenced by the scanning electron microscope (SEM) image presented in Fig. 6B. Excipient system evaluation revealed that formulations based on trehalose and raffinose had lower drug loadings, whereas leucine-based excipients had higher antibiotic loadings. Nanosuspensions of SFD can be used as a platform for pulmonary delivery of difficult water-soluble CFM molecules [48]. The escalating global crisis of antibiotic resistance, exacerbated by the extensive use of intravenous, topical, and inhaled antibiotics, underscores the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies. Cephalosporins exemplify the ongoing arms race between the development of antimicrobials and the evolution of bacterial resistance. Carbapenems, such as meropenem (MEM), serve as last-line therapeutics against MDR bacterial strains [86]. Similar to other β-lactams, MEM exhibits chemical instability in aqueous environments due to the hydrolysis of the β-lactam ring under acidic and alkaline conditions [87]. To address its rapid degradation profile, high therapeutic dosing requirements, and short plasma half-life, researchers have engineered a DPI formulation using lactose co-processed with L-leucine and magnesium stearate. This excipient combination stabilizes MEM's molecular integrity while facilitating efficient pulmonary deposition, thereby preserving its bactericidal activity against drug-resistant pathogens (Fig. 6C) [88].

Tetracyclines inhaled formulations

The global crisis of antimicrobial resistance has prompted the emergence of tigecycline (TGC), a third-generation tetracycline antibiotic, as a potential treatment for clinically challenging MDR bacterial infections. However, TGC's instability in aqueous media presents significant challenges for its delivery. Moreover, intravenous administration of TGC frequently results in severe adverse effects, such as gastroenteritis, which often necessitates the discontinuation of treatment. To address these limitations, pulmonary-targeted delivery via DPI has been strategically explored. Utilizing a Design of Experiments (DoE) methodology, nine variable combinations were randomly assigned. Following preparation, the morphological characteristics were analyzed using SEM, as shown in Fig. 6D. Additionally, the fine particle fraction (FPF) was measured to determine the optimal parameter combination. The innovative DPI formulations exhibit enhanced hygroscopic stability, improved aerosolization efficiency, and precisely engineered particle size distributions within the respirable range of 1-5 μm [49].

Antitubercular inhaled formulations

Another critical frontier in inhaled antibiotic research focuses on pulmonary delivery strategies targeting Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis) the etiological agent of tuberculosis (TB). Infection is initiated when airborne M. tuberculosis is deposited in the alveolar regions and subsequently undergoes phagocytosis by alveolar macrophages [89]. M. tuberculosis subverts the lysosomal degradation mechanism, establishes intracellular ecological niches, and disrupts host cell signaling pathways, thereby inhibiting lysosomal biogenesis and apoptosis while promoting macrophage necrosis [90]. Pulmonary administration circumvents first-pass metabolism, thereby enhancing target bioavailability while minimizing systemic toxicity. To address the solubility limitations of rifampicin (RIF), Freeman et al. engineered cyclodextrin-based inclusion complexes, which were co-processed with RIF nanoparticles through spray-drying into nanocomposite microparticles. This formulation demonstrated excellent aerosol performance, achieving a FPF of 89% and optimal alveolar deposition, with a mass median aerodynamic diameter (MMAD) of 4.0 μm. This design facilitated dual-phase RIF release, providing immediate dissolution followed by sustained release at infection sites [91]. Similarly, a “Trojan horse” strategy has been employed to enhance the pulmonary delivery of RIF [92]. In addition to improving antibiotic delivery, enhancing immunomodulation and targeting M. tuberculosis are critical. β-glucan, a biocompatible and biodegradable polysaccharide with immunomodulatory properties, has garnered significant attention. RIF encapsulated within inhalable β-glucan microparticles, is illustrated in Fig. 7A, which depicts both the preparation process and the drug image. This formulation facilitates targeted delivery to the primary site of pulmonary tuberculosis infection, a method that not only enhances patient compliance but also reduces systemic toxicity [93].

Antitubercular inhaled formulations. (A) Rifabutin loaded inhalable β-glucan microparticle based drug delivery system. Reproduced with permission from Ref. [93]. (B) CLZ pulmonary inhalation formulation. Reproduced with permission from Ref. [50].

MDR tuberculosis (MDR-TB) and extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis (XDR-TB) continue to pose a major public health challenge. Clofazimine (CLZ) is a potent therapeutic agent for the treatment of MDR-TB. Krishna Jadhav utilized microfluidizer high-pressure homogenization to produce CLZ nanocrystals, which were subsequently spray-dried to form micron-sized CLZ nanoclusters. This engineering approach combines the advantages of micron-sized particles (∼5 μm) for optimal aerodynamic lung deposition and nanocrystals (∼600 nm) to enhance the dissolution of poorly soluble CLZ. Following the administration of inhaled therapy, the pulmonary load of M. tuberculosis was reduced by approximately 1.95 Log10 colony forming units (CFU) times. Furthermore, a histological assessment of the lungs indicated significant improvements in both improved pathogenesis and inflammation after 30 days of inhaling CLF nano-clusters (Fig. 7B) [50]. In 2019, the U.S. FDA approved pretomanid (PTM) tablets in combination with bedaquiline (BDQ) for the treatment of highly drug-resistant TB. In addition to single-drug formulations, an inhaled triplet powder (PTM, moxifloxacin, and pyrazinamide) demonstrated in vitro synergism against MDR-TB and reduced dose requirements compared to single drugs [94].

Non-carried formulations are primarily produced using spray-drying technology, which is well-established in industrial applications, offering convenience and enabling continuous large-scale production. The incorporation of multi-drug combinations allows for the co-delivery of various medications [94]. DPI formulations require protection against moisture absorption, crystallization, and aggregation, and generally exhibit good long-term stability. In terms of biosafety, high concentrations of antibiotics may lead to airway irritation, coughing, and bronchospasm, with an incidence of approximately 3-5% observed in TIS trials [23]. Some drugs can be absorbed into the bloodstream, necessitating an evaluation of plasma exposure levels and potential organ toxicity, as seen with TOB. Excipients like leucine and mannitol are considered safe at inhalation doses; however, high doses may lead to changes in osmotic pressure. Furthermore, the inhalation formulation itself can provoke immune responses, potentially being recognized by macrophages and inducing inflammatory reactions, which necessitates the evaluation of pulmonary cytokine levels, including TNF-α and IL-6.

In terms of translational potential and regulatory aspects, non-carried inhaled formulations require preclinical evaluation of local and systemic toxicity, with a particular emphasis on repeated-dose toxicity and immunogenicity. Clinical evidence from the TOB Phase III trial demonstrates its efficacy in significantly reducing sputum bacterial density and improving the quality of life in patients with bronchiectasis complicated by P. aeruginosa infection, exemplifying a successful model of translation. Non-carried inhaled formulations display high translational potential, especially for chronic pulmonary bacterial infections and infections caused by resistant bacteria. These formulations typically involve inhalation modifications of marketed antibiotics, which fall under the FDA's 505(b)(2) pathway, providing a relatively clear regulatory route.

In summary, spray drying technology for non-carried inhaled formulations has matured, providing scalable control for multi-drug combinations. Particle engineering is well-established, and inhalation devices are readily available. The regulatory pathways and application procedures are clearly defined and currently supported by relevant preclinical and clinical evidence, which demonstrates established translational feasibility. A key focus is on modifying formulations to reduce local airway irritation and systemic exposure, alongside robust quality control, preclinical toxicology, and clinical endpoint design. This framework offers substantial large-scale clinical evidence for converting more antibiotic formulations into non-carried inhaled formulations, thereby enriching the therapeutic arsenal against bacterial pneumonia.

Inhaled delivery of carrier formulations

Lipid-carried inhaled formulations

The majority of antimicrobial agents exhibit high water solubility, a property that facilitates rapid absorption into the vascular system and subsequent systemic clearance. To maintain drug concentrations within the respiratory tract above the MIC, liposomal delivery systems are strategically employed to achieve sustained drug release. This approach extends the antibacterial duration at the infection site [95]. The controlled release mechanism has been shown to reduce dosing frequency while enhancing therapeutic adherence. Furthermore, liposomes enable precision-targeted delivery through macrophage-mediated phagocytosis upon airway deposition, allowing for intracellular antibiotic liberation, which is a critical advantage in managing intracellular pathogens [96]. In addition to encapsulating antibiotics, lipid-based carriers can also incorporate siRNAs and natural anti-inflammatory compounds, which have been utilized in the treatment of pulmonary bacterial infections, inflammatory diseases, and vaccine development [[97], [98], [99], [100]]. A multitude of liposomal formulations have been clinically validated as tolerable inhalation therapies, demonstrating excellent safety profiles [101].

Lipid-carried quinolone antibiotic inhaled formulations

Currently, several approved quinolone inhalation therapies exist, including an aerosolized liposome suspension formulation of amikacin, which has demonstrated clinical efficacy when administered directly to the pulmonary system. However, nebulized systems encounter operational limitations, such as antibiotic formulation leakage and the need for demanding equipment maintenance. Paul et al. utilized spray-drying technology to develop a DPI formulation of solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) loaded with levofloxacin (LEV). The manufacturing process is illustrated in Fig. 8A. By precisely controlling the aerosol characteristics, they achieved optimized pulmonary delivery [51]. The spray-drying process facilitated particle size reduction while preserving the nanostructural integrity of SLNs, significantly enhancing aerosol dispersion through improved mucus penetration dynamics. For CIP, a third-generation quinolone with poor lung targeting following oral administration, optimized inhalable nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) were developed to enhance deep lung deposition and drug retention. The CIP-loaded NLCs were fabricated via hot homogenization using stearic acid and oleic acid matrices, which were subsequently processed with chitosan (CS) into nanocomposite microparticles through spray-drying for DPI applications. These optimized CIP-NLCs exhibited enhanced physical stability (>92% encapsulation efficiency) and sustained release profiles [102]. Furthermore, an advanced formulation strategy, ultrasonic spray freeze drying (USFD) technology, was employed to co-encapsulate CIP and COL in a liposomal carrier, resulting in the formation of a Cip-Col-Lips DPI system [103]. These engineered formulations effectively penetrate airway mucus barriers, accumulate at biofilm interfaces, neutralize bacterial toxins, protect the pulmonary epithelium, and induce synergistic antibiofilm activity through controlled spatiotemporal drug release (Fig. 8B).

The active targeting mechanism surpasses the passive deposition strategy by utilizing the homologous targeting capability of bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), which are naturally secreted by gram-negative bacteria. Peng et al. developed lipid nanoparticles (LNP@HMVs) that are coated with neutrophil-bacterial hybrid membrane vesicles (HMVs). The membrane fusion process is illustrated in Fig. 8C, and these nanoparticles are utilized to deliver norfloxacin precisely to the site of infection. The LNP@HMVs exhibited dual-targeting capability: they selectively bind to inflamed vascular endothelial cells and specifically recognize gram-negative pathogens, thereby enabling localized antibiotic accumulation at infection sites. This platform demonstrated potent therapeutic outcomes against systemic and respiratory infections while concurrently activating adaptive humoral and cellular immune responses to prevent bacterial reinfection [104]. Collectively, LNP@HMVs represent an innovative therapeutic platform for effective infection management and prophylaxis.

Liposome-carried macrolides antibiotic inhaled formulations

A DPI liposomal formulation of AZM was developed for the treatment of chronic respiratory diseases, including CF and bronchiectasis. The liposome-encapsulated AZM exhibited enhanced activity against clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa growing in biofilms, demonstrating rapid delivery to bacterial cells with over 75% uptake within 1 h [52].

Liposome-carried polymyxins inhaled formulations

PMB effectively neutralizes lipopolysaccharides (LPS) released during bacterial lysis, thereby preventing endotoxin-mediated complications. To address PMB resistance and systemic toxicity, advanced spray-dried liposomal encapsulation technology has been explored to mitigate adverse effects while enhancing therapeutic efficacy. PMB, engineered into inhalable liposomal dry powders through optimized thin-film hydration and spray-drying processes, has demonstrated significant potential in managing MDR gram-negative respiratory infections [53].

Lipid-carried beta-lactam antibiotic inhaled formulations

Nebulized aztreonam (AZT) lysine has received regulatory approval for the management of P. aeruginosa infections in CF patients aged 7 years and older. However, the thrice-daily dosing regimen highlights the urgent need for advanced sustained-release delivery systems. To maintain pulmonary drug concentrations above the MIC, researchers have engineered inhalable AZT-encapsulated lipid nanoparticles using an integrated approach that combines hot-melt extrusion and probe ultrasonication technology. This innovative method achieves both targeted pulmonary deposition and sustained therapeutic release against P. aeruginosa pathogens [54].

Lipid-carried aminoglycoside antibiotic inhaled formulations

TOB has limited efficacy against P. aeruginosa biofilms due to its inability to penetrate biofilms and thick layers of mucus [106]. As a concentration-dependent antimicrobial agent, TOB necessitates sufficiently high concentrations for bactericidal activity; however, elevated TOB concentrations often lead to an increased incidence of irreversible nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and ototoxicity in patients [107]. Liposomal formulations represent the most popular and successful modification strategy, as they have the potential to fuse with bacterial cell membranes, facilitate targeted delivery, and increase antibiotic concentrations within bacterial cells [108]. Lipids can further self-assemble into more highly ordered non-lamellar structures, commonly referred to as liquid crystals, which are of great interest for drug delivery due to their ability to provide a larger surface area within the lipid bilayer and accommodate more amphiphilic, hydrophilic, and lipophilic drugs. Thorn developed TOB-loaded liquid crystalline nanoparticles (TOB-LCNP), wherein drug release is triggered by bacterial lipases. The lipid liquid crystal structure, submicron particle size, and neutral surface charge contribute to enhanced penetration of TOB across the biofilm barrier. The LCNP structure as shown in Fig. 8D–TOB encapsulated in TOB-LCNPs achieved a transport rate of 99.2% across the biological membrane and into human bronchial epithelial cells within 2.5 h post-nebulization. Additionally, the LCNP can fuse with individual P. aeruginosa cells, resulting in improved antimicrobial effects. In contrast to CIP-LCNP, TOB-LCNP demonstrated a 1000-fold increase in antimicrobial activity compared to unformulated TOB solutions. Importantly, TOB-LCNP can be aerosolized without affecting particle size, effectively delivering TOB into bacterial biofilms, which suggests its suitability as an advanced topical pulmonary inhalation therapy [105].

Lipid-carried antitubercular inhaled formulations

Pulmonary antibiotic delivery for TB presents distinct therapeutic advantages over conventional oral administration by facilitating direct targeting of M. tuberculosis-harboring alveolar macrophages. This approach not only reduces therapeutic dosages but also minimizes systemic toxicity. PTM/RIF-encapsulated lipid microparticles have been formulated as carrier-free inhalable dry powders through optimized spray-drying processes [109,110]. The advent of jet-milling technology has enabled the development of a novel and streamlined method for producing anti-TB drug dry powder formulations from liposomal precursors in a single step [55]. BDQ, a novel therapeutic for drug-resistant TB, has been successfully developed. It consists of liposomes decorated with fucose on their surface, facilitating C-type lectin receptor-mediated macrophage targeting and uptake, and is loaded with a second-line antimycobacterial drug. As shown in Fig. 8E, C-type lectin receptor (CLR)-targosphere liposomes overcome the respiratory barrier and interact with epithelial cells and pulmonary macrophages. This formulation is specifically designed for treating pulmonary infections caused by intracellular mycobacteria. Spray drying has been shown to enhance the stability and aerodynamic properties essential for effective pulmonary drug delivery, while simultaneously preserving the size, drug loading, and targeting capabilities of the liposomes. The fucose-dependent targeting of nanocarriers to alveolar macrophages, in contrast to conventional liposomes, suggests that this dry powder formulation has the potential to combine the aerodynamic advantages of microparticles with the targeted delivery capabilities of nanocarriers to alveolar macrophages [96].

Lipid-carried other antibiotic inhaled formulations

Berberine hydrochloride (BH) is a major biologically active alkaloid extracted from Coptis chinensis, known for its antibacterial effects, which are mediated by disrupting bacterial cell membranes, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), and decreasing intracellular adenosine triphosphate levels [111]. Xie et al. demonstrated that BH-encapsulated liposomes exhibit cholesterol concentration-dependent antibacterial efficacy against S. aureus and its biofilms, with liposomes containing cholesterol-enriched microdomains showing superior bactericidal activity and biofilm eradication capacity. This enhancement arises from optimized kinetics of liposome-biofilm fusion, high-affinity interactions between cholesterol microdomains and bacterial virulence factors, and rapid toxin-triggered drug release within biofilm matrices [112]. Resveratrol, originally identified as a phytoalexin, has emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate due to its potent antibacterial activity against A. baumannii. However, its clinical application is hindered by poor aqueous solubility, limited oral bioavailability, and rapid metabolic clearance. To address these limitations, resveratrol-loaded liposomal macroporous microparticles were developed as a novel pulmonary delivery system for the treatment of A. baumannii-associated pneumonia [113].

The cationic antimicrobial peptide (AMP) AA139, a 21-residue amphipathic hairpin-structured AMP, exhibits a dual mode of action: it directly binds to membrane phospholipids and disrupts phospholipid transport pathways, leading to membrane destabilization and bacterial death. For the targeted treatment of K. pneumoniae pneumonia, AA139 has been nanosized into lipid-core micelles suitable for nebulized inhalation therapy [56]. To eradicate P. aeruginosa biofilms, SLNs co-loaded with a quorum sensing inhibitor to disrupt bacterial communication, CS to enhance internalization, and alginate lyase to degrade alginate biofilm matrices were spray-dried into inhalable SLN microparticles [114].

The complexity of lipid-carried inhaled formulations is evident in their multi-level structural design and functional synergy. Firstly, the composition of liposomes requires precise regulation, which involves the selection of phospholipid types, the cholesterol ratio, and the methods for drug loading. These factors collectively influence particle size, surface charge, encapsulation efficiency, and release kinetics. Secondly, when processing liposomes into DPI, it is crucial to maintain their structural integrity while ensuring that the particles achieve MMAD and a high FPF. Furthermore, the scalability of surface modifications necessitates the introduction of functional ligands or enzymes to facilitate targeted delivery or to overcome biological membrane barriers, thereby complicating formulation design [96]. Long-term stability remains a core challenge for liposome-carried formulations. Liquid liposomes are prone to aggregation, fusion, drug leakage, and phospholipid hydrolysis/oxidation, often requiring conversion to solid forms to extend shelf life.

Biosafety challenges associated with lipid-based inhalation formulations include: 1) Local irritation, where high concentrations of lipids or drugs may lead to airway irritation, coughing, or bronchospasm, necessitating evaluation through animal models; 2) Immunogenicity, as liposomes may be recognized by alveolar macrophages, triggering inflammatory responses, thereby requiring monitoring of cytokine levels; 3) Long-term toxicity, since accumulation of lipids in the lungs may lead to phospholipidosis or granuloma formation, highlighting the need for long-term inhalation toxicity studies. Regarding translational potential, lipid-based carrier inhalation formulations have shown significant prospects for clinical translation. The successful market approval of amikacin liposome inhalation suspension (Arikayce®) serves as robust proof-of-concept. Furthermore, liposomes can function as immunomodulators or as multi-drug co-delivery platforms, providing innovative strategies for addressing drug-resistant bacterial infections. With a deeper understanding of the in vivo fate of liposomes and their interactions with biological barriers, it is anticipated that more candidate drugs will enter clinical development stages.

The advantages of this delivery system: 1) Targeted delivery: it achieves specific targeting of alveolar macrophages or biofilms through surface modification; 2) Controlled-release properties: this prolongs drug retention in the lungs, maintains effective concentrations, and reduces dosing frequency; 3) Enhanced bioavailability: it improves the solubility and membrane permeability of poorly soluble drugs; 4) Versatile platform: it enables the co-loading of multiple drugs or functional molecules for synergistic therapy; 5) Overcoming biological barriers: modified liposomes can penetrate mucus and biofilms to deliver drugs to deep infection sites. Conversely, the disadvantages are: 1) Complex preparation: This requires precise control of lipid composition, particle size, and surface properties, making large-scale production challenging; 2) Stability challenges: The liquid form is prone to degradation, while solidification necessitates process optimization and the use of protective agents; 3) Limited drug loading: There is low encapsulation efficiency for hydrophilic drugs, and the stability of the lipid bilayer may be compromised by lipophilic drugs; 4) Limitations on high-dose administration: The single-dose administration of liposome dry powder is constrained by device capabilities and pulmonary tolerance.

Polymer-carried inhaled formulations

Polymer nanoparticles are at the forefront of next-generation drug delivery systems due to their ability to manipulate physicochemical parameters (e.g., size distribution, morphological characteristics, structural architecture, surface charge, and functional group engineering) with exceptional precision. This capability, combined with their unique biological barrier navigation abilities for precision targeting, represents a significant advancement in the field of pharmaceutical delivery [115]. The encapsulation of therapeutic payloads, including peptides, recombinant proteins, and nucleic acid constructs, within polymeric matrices provides molecular stabilization for fragile biologics while enabling high-concentration delivery and synergistic co-delivery of multimodal therapies [116,117]. Their structural adaptability allows for precise traversal of complex biological barriers and spatiotemporally controlled drug liberation through stimulus-responsive mechanisms, such as pH/redox/enzyme-triggered release. In comparison to conventional antibiotic formulations, polymer nanoparticles have been shown to enhance therapeutic outcomes through several mechanisms, including the ability to shield drugs, enable combinatorial strategies, and leverage programmable targeting for site-specific action. Polymers have demonstrated numerous advantages as carriers, conferring a natural benefit when employed for pulmonary inhalation delivery. The release of this treatment system in the microenvironment of lung infections is characterized by its responsiveness, which enhances therapeutic precision.

Responsive polymer-carried inhaled formulations

As a pivotal endogenous stimulus, pH has inspired the design of sophisticated polymer nanoparticles that leverage pH-responsive structural transitions-such as size modulation, morphological reconfiguration, and phase transformation-to achieve spatiotemporally controlled drug release. Antibiotic PMB, an amphiphilic cationic peptide, has been engineered into pH-responsive polyion nanocomplexes by Chai et al. through the formulation of PMB with 2,3-dimethylmaleic anhydride (DA)-functionalized CS, as illustrated in Fig. 9A. At a physiological pH of 7.4, cationic PMB undergoes electrostatic complexation with anionic DA moieties. Conversely, acidic infection-site conditions (pH 5.5) trigger the dissociation of the nanocomplex, releasing PMB while maintaining potent antibacterial efficacy comparable to that of the free drug, thereby enabling targeted PMB delivery for acute pulmonary infections with reduced toxicity [118]. Jiang et al. developed AZM-encapsulated zwitterionic polymer micelles (PPEMA-b-PPOEMA) via reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer polymerization. These intelligent micelles exhibit environment-adaptive charge reversal: a neutral surface charge facilitates mucus and biofilm penetration, while acidic microenvironments induce positive charge exposure and pH-triggered AZM release, synergistically enhancing antimicrobial activity (Fig. 9B) [119].

Another innovative approach employed electrostatic assembly between anionic sodium alginate, cationic quaternized polyethyleneimine (QPEI-C6), and AZT to fabricate inhalable nanocomplexes engineered for dual mucus and biofilm penetration. The structure is shown in Fig. 9C, under acidic infectious conditions, nanocomplex disassembly releases AZT and QPEI-C6, generating combinatorial bactericidal effects against MDR pathogens through β-lactam antibiotic action and membrane-disrupting polymer activity [57]. Zhou designed acid-labile polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) microgels for the nebulization therapy of piperacillin (PIP), the synthesis process as shown in Fig. 9D. The UV-crosslinked vinyl ether methacrylate-modified PVA microgels (G@PIP) undergo acidic degradation in inflammatory environments, facilitating the release of PIP to combat E. coli. Medium-diameter G@PIP microgels (3.52 ± 0.15 μm) exhibited optimized pulmonary pharmacokinetics, characterized by reduced exhalation loss and enhanced alveolar deposition, ultimately achieving superior in vivo therapeutic outcomes [120].

Bacterial pulmonary invasion induces hyperactivation of macrophage and neutrophil recruitment, leading to the production of excessive ROS that mediate oxidative damage to cellular components. This pathophysiological cascade exacerbates the progression of bacterial pneumonia and the pathogenesis of acute lung injury [121]. Capitalizing on this ROS-rich microenvironment, Wang et al. engineered ROS-responsive amphiphilic nanoparticles by conjugating poly(L-lysine) with 4-carboxyphenylboronic acid pinacol ester (CDIPB) for the encapsulation of doxycycline (DOX) [58]. The boronate ester moieties in CDIPB undergo ROS-triggered cleavage through specific oxidation reactions, initiating the degradation of the nanoparticle matrix and resulting in a burst release of DOX, which effectively eradicates pathogen accumulations.

P. aeruginosa exhibits elevated esterase activity, which motivates the rational design of enzyme-responsive nanocarriers featuring TPGS (D-α-tocopheryl polyethylene glycol 1000 succinate) shells and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) cores [122]. The TPGS shell enhances nanoparticle penetration through pulmonary mucus and biofilm barriers due to polyethylene glycol (PEG) chain-mediated mucoinert surface properties. Within P. aeruginosa biofilms, bacterial esterases enzymatically cleave TPGS, resulting in the generation of hydrophobic, vitamin E-rich surfaces that anchor nanoparticles as localized reservoirs for AZM, thereby sustaining antibiotic retention. This platform was further optimized through the co-encapsulation of docosahexaenoic acid and TOB, achieving synergistic anti-infective and anti-inflammatory effects via nebulization delivery [59]. Guerreiro investigated konjac glucomannan (KGM) as an inhalable, macrophage-targeting carrier for the anti-tuberculosis drugs isoniazid (INH) and RIF [123]. Spray-dried KGM microparticles exhibited optimal aerodynamic properties with high drug loading efficiencies. In simulated lung fluid, the formulation achieved 50% drug release within 45 min, reaching 84.3% at 12 h and 96.7-97.4% at 24 h. Hydration-induced swelling preceded β-mannosidase-mediated biodegradation, confirming enzymatic responsiveness. This study highlights KGM's dual functionality as a natural biodegradable polymer and a macrophage-targeting vehicle for respiratory therapeutics.

Natural polymer-carried inhaled formulations

Natural polymer products remain pharmacologically indispensable due to their evolutionarily optimized chemical diversity and multi-target antimicrobial mechanisms [124]. Botanical derivatives particularly drive innovation in inhaled antimicrobial engineering [125]. The team led by Wu utilized tea leaf extracts along with traditional Chinese herbal extracts to develop a series of nanoclusters, which demonstrated promising results in the treatment of bacterial pneumonia via inhalation therapy [[126], [127], [128]]. Tannic acid (TA) demonstrates broad-spectrum bactericidal activity against E. coli and S. aureus through competitive binding to membrane porins and denaturation of extracellular proteins. A hydrogen-bonded ternary drug delivery system comprising poly(2-ethyl-2-oxazoline), TA, and CIP exhibited dose-dependent efficacy against MDR lower respiratory infections [129]. Fang et al. engineered a mucoadhesive TA/CS nanocomposite platform for sustained pneumonia management. Utilizing PMB as a model drug, CS-coated TA/PVA/PMB nanoparticles (Fig. 10A), along with electrostatic interactions, facilitated sustained PMB release over 24 h in P. aeruginosa-infected murine models, achieving complete bacterial eradication within 72 h through mucin-specific adhesion and prolonged pulmonary retention [130]. Many bioactive natural monomers encounter challenges such as poor water solubility and short half-lives. To address these issues, the researchers synthesized quercetin nanoclusters (CMNCs) through polyphenol-mediated self-assembly, utilizing intermolecular hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking. Fig. 10B illustrates transmission electron microscope (TEM) images of various nanoclusters along with their molecular structures during the formation process. CMNCs enhanced mucosal permeability and achieved pathogen-specific targeting via surface-exposed polyphenolic motifs, demonstrating improved survival rates in murine models of S. aureus pneumonia [131]. Hypericin (HP), isolated from Hypericum perforatum, exhibits multimodal bioactivity through its potent inhibition of protein kinase C, monoamine oxidase, and dopamine-β-hydroxylase. Li et al. developed ATP/ROS-dual responsive biomimetic nanosystems that integrate components from bacterial outer membranes and macrophage membranes, specifically a macrophage membrane, and loaded hypericin along with an ATP-responsive dibenzyloxalate nanoparticles(MMHP NPs). The mechanism by which MMHP NPs precisely treat pneumonia as shown in Fig. 10C. These intelligent nanosystems release HP payloads in response to cues from the infectious microenvironment, exerting dual antimicrobial action through Mg2+ chelation-mediated bacterial membrane destabilization and nuclear-targeted inhibition of viral replication. In co-infection murine models, MMHP NPs demonstrated improved survival rates through synergistic pathogen clearance and CD8+ T cell activation, thereby validating their combined antimicrobial and immunotherapeutic potential [132].

CS, one of the most widely used natural polymers, combines biodegradability, intrinsic antimicrobial activity, and immunostimulatory properties, establishing itself as a multifunctional biomaterial for pulmonary therapeutics. In respiratory drug delivery applications, as illustrated in Fig. 10D, electrospray-engineered CS-CIP nanocomposites (195 ± 80 nm) achieved targeted alveolar deposition with rapid antibiotic release kinetics, demonstrating a release of 50.2% within 30 min [60]. Chauhan et al. validated the CS/bovine serum albumin (BSA) layer-by-layer (LBL) coated microparticles through comprehensive in vitro-in vivo correlation studies, confirming their suitability for non-invasive pulmonary administration [133]. Furthermore, phytoglycogen and chondroitin sulfate have been shown to function as structurally essential excipients within the formulation of DPIs, leveraging their hygroscopic and mucoadhesive characteristics as key constituents of fine particles. This integration enhances the aerodynamic stability of the inhalation system [134,135].

Artificial polymer-carried inhaled formulations

In recent years, graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4), a prototypical polymeric n-type semiconductor, has exhibited remarkable electron reduction capacity within its conduction band, alongside substantial catalytic potential. However, its inefficiency in separating photo-generated electron-hole pairs, combined with slow carrier transport, results in diminished catalytic activity. In this study, Li et al. designed asymmetric Fe and Cu nanocluster-doped materials (DCN-FeCu), which significantly enhance microwave catalytic performance. In an animal model of pneumonia, following intratracheal inhalation of DCN-FeCu and subsequent microwave irradiation, a notable reduction in bacterial load within the lungs was observed [136]. In addition to the sterilization process initiated by external energy through catalytic effects, the utilization of synthetic polymer carriers for the delivery of antibiotics warrants consideration.

AZM was nanoencapsulated in dipalmitoylphosphatidylglycerol (DPPC)-functionalized polycaprolactone (PCL) nanoparticles and subsequently spray-dried with lactose monohydrate to generate respirable powders demonstrating optimal aerosol performance in next generation impactor (NGI) analyses [137]. Arbekacin (ABK) was surfactant-engineered with ER-290, lyophilized, and co-processed with mannitol to form ABK-SD/MAN nanocomposites [138]. Ultrasonic SFD of polymyxin nanosuspensions containing distearoyl phosphatidylglycerol-polyethylene glycol-polyoxymethylene dimethyl ethers (DSPG-PEG-OMe) and ivacaftor (IVA) yielded dry powders exhibiting enhanced bactericidal activity against polymyxin-resistant P. aeruginosa in CF models [139]. The research team led by Rudolph successfully prepared amorphous nanoparticles of the lipophilic anti-TB drugs BDQ and BTZ-043 with high drug loading using an optimized mixture of surfactants. As demonstrated in Fig. 11A, the synthesis process for BDQ and BTZ nanoparticles resulted in optimal drug loading (69% for BDQ and >99% for BTZ) and the production of stable nebulization suspensions, thereby enhancing the efficacy of tuberculosis treatment [140]. The development of new inhalable particulate formulations utilizing spray drying to bind a variety of drugs, including antibiotics, NAC, and curcumin (Cur), has been undertaken. The incorporation of PLGA nanoparticles facilitates the spray drying process and modifies the release profiles of certain compounds. PLGA incorporated with Cur-loaded PLGA demonstrated a higher potential to inhibit the release of TNF-α, IL-8, and IL-1β [141]. PLGA nanoparticles loaded with pirfenidone and nintedanib create a nebulizable inhalable microgel, leading to an inhalation formulation that exhibits enhanced anti-fibrotic efficacy [142]. Core-shell particles comprising PCL-coated Cur and PVA-stabilized CIP were engineered via coaxial electrospraying to optimize pulmonary deposition [143].

Finbloom et al. constructed a LbL self-assembled polyelectrolyte nanocomposite (AgTOB-NC) through the sequential assembly of dextran sulfate (DS), TOB, and silver nanoparticles (AgNPs). As demonstrated in Fig. 11B, the complexation of TOB (positively charged) with the dextran sulfate (DS) polymer (negatively charged) results in the formation of nanocomplexes, while the LbL incorporation of trimethyl chitosan (TMC) and AgNPs enables the embedding of the antimicrobial agent within the nanocomplexes. A high loading capacity was achieved (>75% TOB and >95% AgNPs loading), thereby exerting a synergistic antibacterial effect [61]. PEGylated TOB (mPEG-SA-TOB) demonstrated improved biofilm penetration by neutralizing electrostatic interactions between TOB and anionic alginate (Fig. 11C) [144]. Biodegradable polymers, including poly(L-lactide) and polylactic acid, are being investigated for controlled TB drug delivery [145,146]. For anti-inflammatory and pulmonary repair applications, poly(β-L-malic acid)-budesonide nanoconjugates (80-100 nm) co-spray-dried with L-leucine (∼1.0 μm) sustained drug release (∼73.4% loading) while maintaining anti-inflammatory potency [147]. γ-Polyglutamic acid/epsilon-poly-L-lysine (γ-PGA/ɛ-PLL) wet-adhesive hydrogels loaded with epidermal growth factor (EGF) demonstrated enhanced tracheal epithelial regeneration [148].

Phages, which are obligate bacterial viruses that exhibit strict parasitism, immunogenic antigenicity, and host-specific lytic activity, represent precise antimicrobial agents targeting bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms [150]. Compared to traditional antibiotics, phages offer distinct advantages: they exhibit high specificity by selectively targeting recognized pathogens, replicate within host bacteria without infecting mammalian cells or causing side effects, and demonstrate superior safety and tolerability. With the rise of MDR bacteria due to antibiotic overuse, phage therapy has regained attention in recent years [151]. Clinical case reports indicate that nebulized phages administered via the airway can ameliorate P. aeruginosa lung infections [152]. However, engineered phage delivery systems are critically needed to overcome airway clearance mechanisms. While phage monotherapy or endolysin Cpl-1-loaded DPIs effectively treat bacterial pneumonia, concerns remain about reduced efficacy due to bacterial evolution and phage resistance, akin to antibiotic resistance [153]. Studies also show that combining nebulized phage inhalation with intravenous phage administration improves survival rates in murine models of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) pneumonia [154]. Lytic phages not only kill bacteria but also synergize with antibiotics by exerting selective pressure. A co-spray-dried powder of phage PEV20 and CIP demonstrated synergistic potential against P. aeruginosa respiratory infections [155]. Research into phage stability in spray-dried powders has shown that immobilizing phages within rigid glassy sugar matrices significantly enhances their preservation [156]. These findings provide critical guidance for the production and storage of inhalable phage powders intended for clinical translation. Rachit Agarwal describes the development of phage-loaded microparticles (phage MPs) for pulmonary delivery via dry powder inhalation, aiming to deliver therapeutic doses of active phage to the site of infection. The study illustrates that phage-loaded polymeric particles are effectively deposited throughout the lung via dry powder inhalation and can deliver active phage, which ameliorated P. aeruginosa infection and associated inflammation in mice, ultimately rescuing them from pneumonia-related death. These polymeric particles may represent a clinically translatable therapeutic approach to eradicate hospital-acquired lung infections and infections associated with CF [157]. Liu et al. developed an inhalable DPI, as shown in Fig. 11D, consisting of porous microspheres of PLGA internally loaded with indocyanine green (ICG)-modified heat-resistant phages, which we evaluated for its high efficacy against MRSA. This formulation can deliver therapeutic doses of ICG-modified active phages to the site of infection in deep lung tissue, thereby avoiding rapid clearance by alveolar macrophages. When combined with the synergistic treatment of phage therapy and photothermal therapy, this formulation exhibits potent bactericidal activity in acute MRSA pneumonia. Given its long-term stability at room temperature and inhalable properties, this formulation has the potential to serve as a promising agent for the clinical treatment of MRSA pneumonia [149].

Inhalation formulations prepared using polymer carriers offer significant complexity and design flexibility, and can achieve multifunctional integration through advanced molecular engineering (such as environmental responsiveness, responsive release, and targeting) [57]. Additionally, the modifiability of polymers facilitates the surface grafting of mucus-penetrating molecules, enabling the targeting of ligands or cationic groups with antimicrobial properties. This approach effectively overcomes the respiratory tract mucus barrier and enhances biofilm penetration [119]. Complex, multi-component systems present significant challenges for large-scale production. With respect to long-term stability, encapsulating active ingredients within responsive polymers imposes stringent environmental requirements.

From a regulatory perspective, the development of polymer-based inhalation formulations is subject to stringent requirements regarding excipient safety and formulation performance. The primary biosafety challenges associated with cationic polymers primarily relate to their potential toxicity, propensity to induce oxidative stress, and immunogenic reactions. Additionally, the in vivo degradation kinetics of these polymers and the long-term effects of their metabolites necessitate thorough evaluation. Therefore, the transition from laboratory discovery to clinical translation requires not only the validation of their superior therapeutic efficacy but also the provision of comprehensive toxicological data to confirm their safety for pulmonary epithelial cells, macrophages, and systemic organs at effective therapeutic concentrations. This step is crucial for assessing their clinical value and facilitating widespread adoption.

In summary, the primary advantages of polymer-based inhaled formulations lie in their exceptional design flexibility and functionality. These formulations incorporate intelligent response mechanisms that facilitate targeted delivery and controlled release, significantly enhancing drug bioavailability. Moreover, these mechanisms effectively navigate complex physiological barriers, such as mucus and biofilms, ensuring the effective delivery of the drug substance to the desired location. However, these formulations are not without their drawbacks, which are equally significant. The synthesis and preparation processes are often complex, presenting technical obstacles to large-scale production. Furthermore, the long-term biosafety of these novel polymers requires additional validation, posing a substantial regulatory challenge.In conclusion, polymer-based inhaled formulations represent a promising therapeutic strategy that balances complex functionality with superior therapeutic efficacy. The success of their clinical applications hinges on achieving an optimal equilibrium between innovative design and the development of robust, scalable, and safe medicinal products.

Peptide/protein-carried inhaled formulations

Antimicrobial peptides-carried inhaled formulations

Natural antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), particularly those derived from bacteria, have gained prominence as innovative biomaterial solutions against MDR pathogens. Conventional antibiotics are known to target specific biochemical pathways. In contrast, AMPs primarily exert bactericidal effects through non-receptor-mediated membrane disruption. This process induces destabilization of the phospholipid bilayer and leakage of cytoplasmic contents, thereby significantly reducing the potential for resistance development. It has been determined that these molecules, which have been optimized through the course of evolution, are now recognized as paradigmatic next-generation antimicrobial candidates due to their broad-spectrum potency and non-specific membrane lytic mechanisms. The nanoconfinement of AMPs within dextran-based nanoparticles via ionic gelation significantly prolongs pulmonary retention following aerosol administration [158]. However, native AMPs encounter pharmacological challenges, including environmental sensitivity (such as proteolytic degradation in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, cation-mediated inactivation, and pH-dependent loss of activity) and suboptimal pharmacokinetics. To address these limitations, engineered cationic AMPs have been developed through rational stereochemical engineering, incorporating D-amino acid substitutions that enhance protease resistance while preserving membrane selectivity. The antibacterial activity of WLBU2 and its D-enantiomer as shown in Fig. 12A, the D-enantiomer of WLBU2 (D-WLBU2) has demonstrated enhanced stability and superior antibacterial efficacy, achieving an improved survival rate in P. aeruginosa-infected murine models via intratracheal delivery. This approach effectively treats lung infections by controlling inflammation and mitigating the cytokine storm induced by the gram-negative bacterium LPS through the LPS-TLR4 signaling axis [159]. Chen et al. engineered modular self-assembling β-hairpin peptides that integrate membrane-permeable motifs with truncated SAAP-148 sequences (LKR) to construct macrophage-targeting nanoparticles (SPRAY-BLKR-NPs). Molecular design and discovery of a biocompatible spray with dual M2 macrophage polarization and antibacterial activity, as illustrated in Fig. 12B. These intelligent nanoparticles employ a quadruple antimicrobial mechanism: disruption of gram-negative bacterial membranes via amphipathic helix insertion, lysosome-triggered payload release through membrane fusion, sustained anti-inflammatory reprogramming that mimics the effects of IL-10/IL-4 cytokines, and promotion of efferocytosis in apoptotic neutrophils. Compatible with dry powder or nebulized delivery, SPRAY-BLKR-NPs demonstrated enhanced lung bioavailability without any observed pulmonary toxicity in preclinical models [64]. Furthermore, synergistic therapeutic strategies utilizing coaxial electrospraying technology to co-encapsulate AMPs with anti-TB drugs into composite particles enable a synergistic delivery mechanism against M. tuberculosis through concurrent membrane disruption and metabolic inhibition [160].

Protein-carried inhaled formulations

Similar to pulmonary surfactants, proteins exhibit solubilizing properties when utilized as drug carriers. Chauhan et al. successfully engineered LbL polyelectrolyte coatings on polystyrene particles using CS and BSA. Their analyses using NGI demonstrated that both 0.5 μm and 4.5 μm LbL-assembled particles met inhalability criteria, thereby validating their potential for non-invasive pulmonary targeting [133]. Kabra formulated inhalable particles utilizing a ternary combination of BSA, L-leucine, and antibiotics (CIP, IVA, and albuterol) to achieve optimized aerodynamic parameters and high drug loading efficiency for anti-infective therapy in CF [161]. Zhu et al. developed a dual-drug DPI co-delivering hydrophilic COL sulfate and hydrophobic IVA for the treatment of MDR gram-negative pneumonia. The formulation involved encapsulating IVA in BSA nanoparticles via desolvation, followed by spray freeze-drying with COL sulfate and L-leucine to produce respirable composite microparticles. The optimized particles exhibited a corrugated morphology with superior aerosol performance, achieving FPF of 73.8 ± 5.2%. Amorphous IVA demonstrated a dramatically enhanced solubility (66-fold compared to its crystalline form), while the DPI achieved near-complete drug release (>95% within 3 h) compared to jet-milled physical mixtures, establishing an excipient-minimized platform with enhanced dissolution kinetics [63]. Human serum albumin (HSA) was utilized as a multifunctional carrier for the pulmonary delivery of benzothiazinone, demonstrating enhanced antimycobacterial solubility (>15 mg/ml vs 0.3 mg/ml of the free drug) [162]. In a pioneering study, Li developed a novel inhalation-loaded PMB-containing albumin nanoparticle (PEG-pHSA@PMB) capable of penetrating airway mucus and responding to the infectious microenvironment. As demonstrated in Fig. 12C, the pH-responsive molecule and amine-PEG-thiol were sequentially modified on HSA and loaded with PMB via electrostatic interactions. The sulfhydryl groups of PEG-pHSA@PMB interacted with mucin, contributing to the penetration of mucus after inhalation. In an acidic environment, the protonation of the tertiary amino group within the albumin nanoparticle results in a change in charge, leading to the release of PMB. Inhalation of PEG-pHSA@PMB has been shown to significantly reduce the bacterial load in the lungs of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae pneumonia mice and to attenuate the inflammatory response. This novel strategy for drug delivery by inhalation represents a significant advancement in the field [163]. Robla et al. prepared RIF-loaded protamine nanocapsules using solvent displacement nanoprecipitation, yielding monodisperse particles (∼200 nm) with 54.2% drug loading, which can be stabilized as suspensions or DPIs. Aerodynamic characterization showed that the FPF was approximately 30%, and the MMAD was about 5.0 μm, making it suitable for deep lung drug delivery [164]. Lin designed inhalable CIP hydrochloride composite microparticles using silk fibroin and D-mannitol through spray-drying. Silk fibroin enhanced drug loading via electrostatic complexation, while D-mannitol optimized mucosal permeability. Surface nanostructuring achieved optimal aerosol performance (FPF 45.04%) [165]. Baldry et al. demonstrated that nebulized flagellin, delivered via vibrating mesh technology, enhances innate immunity and synergizes with amoxicillin, leading to significantly improved treatment outcomes in models of drug-resistant S. pneumoniae pneumonia [166].

Enzyme-carried inhaled formulations

S. pneumoniae secretes hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as a key virulence factor, which induces alveolar epithelial apoptosis and contributes to the pathogenesis of acute lung injury. Dong's team engineered an inhalable catalase-TA nanoassembly (CT@LVX) for localized delivery of LEV, coupled with H2O2 detoxification [62]. The nanoassembly complex exhibited optimal aerodynamic properties for nebulization. By leveraging the high-affinity mucoadhesion of TA, the inhaled nanoassembly achieved sustained pulmonary accumulation. Post-treatment with CT@LVX resulted in a 2.5-fold reduction in H2O2 levels in infected lungs, eradication of 99.99% of invading S. pneumoniae, significant alleviation of lung injury in mice, and a 100% survival rate (Fig. 12D). Enzymes not only degrade bacterial metabolic toxins but also lyse respiratory mucus, enhancing drug penetration through the mucosal barrier. For instance, a team led by The-Thien Tran integrated papain (PAP) into the fabrication of nano-complexes, creating antibiotic nanocarriers with intrinsic mucolytic activity. Utilizing CIP as the antibiotic model and DS as the polyelectrolyte facilitated superior bactericidal activity against clinical P. aeruginosa biofilms, even in the presence of mucus barriers [167].

The formulation development of peptide/protein-carried inhaled formulations is highly complex, primarily due to the diversity in material selection and structural design. While this modular design offers versatility-such as the ability to target macrophages and respond to the inflammatory microenvironment-it also presents significant challenges for scaling up production processes [64]. Additionally, long-term stability poses another critical challenge. Spray drying, which involves temperature fluctuations and shear forces, can lead to irreversible denaturation of the tertiary structure of peptides and proteins, resulting in a loss of biological activity. Although the incorporation of excipients like mannitol and leucine can partially improve solid-state stability, the risks of aggregation, degradation, or crystallization remain during long-term storage. Therefore, maintaining the original structure through complex processes and stringent packaging measures is essential for ensuring the product's efficacy throughout its shelf life.

In terms of biosafety and toxicity, the primary challenges are immunogenicity and local toxicity. Although many proteins (e.g., albumin) and endogenous peptides (e.g., antimicrobial peptides) demonstrate favorable biocompatibility, their introduction as exogenous materials or through chemical modification may lead to recognition and phagocytosis by alveolar macrophages, potentially triggering cytokine storms or chronic inflammation (e.g., granulocyte infiltration). Furthermore, while the introduction of enzymatic carriers and mucolytic agents can assist in degrading toxins or mucus, precise control of their enzymatic activity in vivo and the potential for proteolytic side effects remain critical considerations for safety assessment. Peptide/protein-carried inhaled formulations represent complex combination drugs for which dedicated guidance principles are currently lacking. Their approval often requires consideration of combined requirements for biological products, nanomedicines, and inhalation formulations. These challenges significantly impact the translational potential of formulations; despite substantial animal studies demonstrating superior efficacy, only a limited number (e.g., WLBU2 advancing to Phase I clinical trials) progress to clinical stages [159]. This is primarily due to the complexities of the human immune environment, unresolved long-term toxicity risks, and significant challenges in quality control for large-scale production, which severely limit commercialization prospects.

In conclusion, peptide/protein-carried inhaled formulations represent a highly promising therapeutic frontier, balancing superior bio-inspired functionality with potent antibacterial and immunomodulatory efficacy. However, their clinical success depends on achieving a critical equilibrium between innovative structural design and the development of robust, scalable, and safe manufacturing processes, while also navigating a complex regulatory landscape.

Inorganic nanomaterials-carried inhaled formulations

The rapid development of metal-based nanomaterials with multiple antimicrobial mechanisms, such as drug delivery, oxidative stress, membrane disruption, and interference with bacterial metabolism, has garnered significant attention [[168], [169], [170]]. These materials have been investigated for pulmonary drug delivery to address antibiotic misuse and biofilm-associated resistance in pulmonary infections, owing to their excellent antimicrobial properties and low propensity to induce resistance [171]. A notable example involves lauric acid-coated magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles, which were electrostatically functionalized with vancomycin (VAN) and subsequently processed into inhalable dry powders using spray-drying technology, demonstrating effective eradication of MRSA in pulmonary tissues (Fig. 13A) [172]. These engineered nanoparticles employ magnetic targeting mechanisms to optimize the pulmonary deposition efficiency of inhaled particulates [173]. Miranda et al. developed magnetically responsive inhalable composite microparticles (MPs) through a calcium carbonate-templated microcasting approach, where gelatin and BSA were structured into spherical porous matrices. MPs were fabricated using a casting method that employed a calcium carbonate sacrificial template, incorporating superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles to facilitate on-demand drug release when subjected to an external alternating magnetic field. A high-resolution SEM image of the MPs is presented in Fig. 13B, illustrating that the roughness of the porous structure of the sacrificial template is well retained within the gelatin microgel. The incorporation of superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) endowed the system with magnetic steering capabilities, facilitating external magnetic field-directed drug accumulation in specific pulmonary regions and enabling macrophage-specific delivery of the TB therapeutic candidate P3. This targeted delivery paradigm enhances treatment precision while minimizing off-target effects [174].

Reproduced with permission from Ref. [175]. (C) Hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles-carried ceftazidime aerosol. Reproduced with permission from Ref. [66].

In another formulation breakthrough, COL-conjugated CS-coated gold nanoparticles were successfully incorporated into metered-dose inhaler (MDI) devices. This nanoformulation exhibited remarkable bactericidal activity against pulmonary E. coli infections within 12 h post-administration, establishing its clinical potential for managing complex respiratory infections [175]. Clofazimine (CLZ), an effective treatment for MDR-TB, has been encapsulated in mesoporous silica particles (MSPs). Upon contact with lung fluid, the micron-sized MSPs dissolve into nanoscale carriers, creating a dual delivery platform: microparticles for deep lung deposition and nanoparticles for cellular targeting [176]. Zhu's research team developed dual-penetrating immuno-antimicrobial agents (IMAMs) comprising ceftazidime (CAZ)-encapsulated hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles (HMSNs) that are functionalized with smart peptide coatings. The anionic surface charge of IMAMs facilitates penetration through mucus and biofilms by overcoming glycoprotein/polysaccharide electrostatic repulsion [66]. When exposed to acidic biofilm microenvironments, pH-responsive carboxyl group cleavage triggers a structural transformation in the peptides, converting them from flexible negatively-charged random coils to rigid positively-charged α-helices with membrane-lytic capability. This conformational transition concurrently induces the extension of helical peptides from HMSN surfaces, thereby unsealing nanopores to enable controlled CAZ release (Fig. 13C).

However, the high oxygen content of the pulmonary environment provides aerobic bacteria with a significant tolerance to oxygen and ROS. This phenomenon, in turn, imposes limitations on the therapeutic effectiveness of ROS-dependent metallic nanomaterials. Capitalizing on the susceptibility of aerobic respiratory cells to cuproptosis, Hua et al. engineered a copper-manganese antibacterial nanosystem (Cu-Mn NPs) through a sequential fabrication process: gallic acid-copper complexes (GA-Cu) were first synthesized via metal-phenolic network (MPN) assembly, and were subsequently coated with cationic acid-responsive CS and manganese dioxide to form composite nanoparticles [177]. Surface modification with hydrophilic PEG significantly enhances the system's ability to penetrate the mucus layer. As illustrated in Fig. 14A, experiments involving intratracheal administration in a mouse model demonstrated that Cu-Mn NPs can accumulate in the lungs. This nanosystem is capable of rapidly traversing the mucus layer to penetrate deep into the biofilm, where it exerts a pH-activated, cuproptosis-like death effect on MRSA, effectively clearing the biofilm. Concurrently, it releases manganese ions to enhance both immunomodulatory and antibacterial effects, ultimately resulting in an antibacterial therapeutic effect against acute bacterial pneumonia.

(B) Polysaccharide-camouflaged gallium-polyphenol nanoparticles aerosol. Reproduced with permission from Ref. [65]. (C) FeIII-TA aerosol. Reproduced with permission from Ref. [179]. (D) Zinc prussian blue analogue nanocatalysts (ZnPBA NCs) aerosol. Reproduced with permission from Ref. [183].

In a parallel development, Han et al. created an inhalable microbial capsular polysaccharide (CP)-integrated gallium-polyphenol metal-organic network (MON) for comprehensive lung microbiota eradication [65]. The liberated Ga3+ ions function as biochemical “Trojan horses”, effectively sabotaging bacterial iron respiration through metallomic mimicry to neutralize diverse pathogens (Fig. 14B). A comparable therapeutic strategy was employed to address biofilm infections caused by P. aeruginosa (PAO1). Gallium-quercetin nanoparticles (GEQ NPs) were synthesized through self-assembly, driven by the coordination of GA3+, quercetin, and ε-polylysine, which dissociate specifically in the acidic microenvironment of the infection site. These GEQ NPs have been engineered as a multifaceted nanoplatform to combat refractory P. aeruginosa biofilm infections by simultaneously impeding the electron transport chain and disrupting signal transduction pathways. In a mouse model of invasive pulmonary infection, nebulized GEQ NPs exhibited exceptional anti-biofilm efficacy in vivo [178].

Addressing the limitations of conventional metal-based nanomaterials with fixed dimensions that restrict drug-loading capacities and release kinetics, Ju et al. fabricated FeIII-tannic acid (FeIII-TA) nanocapsules with precisely adjustable shell thicknesses through controlled coating cycles on CaCO3 sacrificial templates [179]. By repeating the metal-phenolic networks (MPNs) coating cycles, capsules of (FeIII-TA)1, (FeIII-TA)3, and (FeIII-TA)6 with varying shell thicknesses were prepared, as illustrated in Fig. 14C. Thick-shell FeIII-TA nanocapsules exhibit superior drug retention and tunable aerodynamic properties, with diameters ranging from 1.31 to 2.54 μm. This enables site-specific pulmonary deposition, allowing for a shift from alveolar regions to terminal bronchioles. MPNs synergistically integrate metallic functionalities with the surface engineering capabilities of polyphenols, facilitating multifunctional biomedical applications. Another innovative category, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), features crystalline porous architectures constructed through coordination bonding between metal ions and organic ligands, which offer exceptional adsorption capacities and controlled release characteristics. For volatile therapeutics such as eugenol (Eug), γ-cyclodextrin MOFs (γ-CD-MOFs) were utilized through gas-solid adsorption to stabilize Eug in inhalable formulations (Eug@γ-CD-MOF), effectively preventing volatilization while enhancing aqueous solubility [180]. Fernández-Paz developed an advanced delivery system by encapsulating INH within iron (III) trimesate MOF (MIL-100 NPs), followed by microencapsulation into mannitol microspheres (Ma MS), achieving a high drug payload and controlled dual-release of nanoparticles and INH across aqueous environments [181]. Similarly, Fe-MIL-101-NH2 MOF nanoparticles loaded with INH and RIF-loaded ZIF-8 MOF (RIF@ZIF-8) enable personalized pulmonary delivery [67,182]. Liu et al. reported the development of self-assembled zinc-doped Prussian blue-analogue zinc hexacyanoferrate nanocatalysts (ZnPBA NCs) [183]. The mechanism by which ZnPBA NCs address acute bacterial pneumonia is depicted in Fig. 14D. The release of accumulated antimicrobial cations has been shown to exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against E. coli, S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa. Furthermore, Zn2+ has been demonstrated to reduce inflammatory factors. ZnPBA NCs also display remarkable mimetic activity for superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), effectively scavenging superoxide anion radicals, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals. Consequently, the synergistic antioxidant and antibacterial properties of ZnPBA NCs can effectively mitigate acute bacterial pneumonia, highlighting their significant potential for further clinical applications.

UiO66 MOF nanoparticles, loaded with dexamethasone (DEX) and coated with neutrophil membranes (UiO66-DEX@NMP), were developed for the targeted therapy of severe pneumonia [184]. By selectively delivering DEX, the inflammatory cascade associated with severe pneumonia was inhibited, leading to an amelioration of the condition. Furthermore, the innovative inhalation formulation combining UiO66 with RIF and LysB D29 enzyme shows promise for effectively managing MDR-TB [185].

Novel cyclic peptide-modified extracellular vesicles loaded in borated cyclodextrin frameworks exhibit sustained release characteristics and pH-responsive properties, rendering them suitable for therapeutic use in inflammatory lung diseases [186]. Certain metallic ions, including copper and gallium, demonstrate intrinsic antibacterial capabilities. For instance, CIP-copper ion complexes engineered into inhalable dry powders through spray-drying technology exhibit enhanced antimicrobial potency and extended pulmonary residence time [187]. The viability of bacterial pathogens fundamentally depends on enzymatic metabolic pathways requiring essential metal ions such as iron, magnesium, and calcium. Experimental evidence indicates that bismuth-based therapeutics combined with antibiotics achieve complete eradication of MDR P. aeruginosa, where intratracheal co-administration of bismuth-antibiotic formulations significantly improves survival outcomes in murine infection models [188]. The bactericidal mechanism operates through dual pathways: extracellularly, bismuth disrupts iron homeostasis via competitive siderophore binding; intracellularly, it compromises iron-sulfur cluster enzymes, inhibits electron transport chain function, dissipates transmembrane proton gradients, and impairs efflux pump activity, collectively enhancing antibiotic accumulation and therapeutic efficacy. Similarly, gallium ions target P. aeruginosa iron metabolism (via siderophores), suggesting nebulization as a viable alternative for treating pulmonary infections [189].

The development of inhaled formulations utilizing inorganic nanomaterial carriers illustrates the intricate nature of formulation engineering and the precise controllability of scale [179]. To surmount the pulmonary biobarrier and achieve targeted drug delivery, researchers have devised a variety of complex structures, these structures often necessitate multi-step synthesis, surface modification, and the incorporation of smart responsive elements, leading to a complicated preparation process [66]. Methods that involve multi-step self-assembly, precision coating, or cell membrane extraction/fusion continue to encounter challenges regarding consistency and cost-effectiveness for large-scale production. Furthermore, during extended storage, it is essential to manage drug leakage from inorganic nanocarriers as well as the degradation behavior of the carriers themselves.

Due to their distinctive composition and structure, inorganic nanomaterials face substantial challenges regarding regulatory approval and biosafety assessment. Currently, only a limited number of inorganic materials have set a precedent for approval by the FDA. These include iron oxide, which is utilized as a dietary supplement or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agent, and Prussian blue. A significant barrier to the clinical translation of most novel inorganic nanocarriers is the lack of safety data. The challenges related to biosafety primarily include: Potential long-term toxicity: Although metal ions exhibit antibacterial activity, their release may lead to toxicity in host cells. A careful balance must be maintained between effective therapeutic doses and host cell toxicity. Furthermore, the prolonged retention of nanoparticles in the lungs may result in chronic inflammation, granuloma formation, or fibrosis. Secondly, achieving a balance between targeting and toxicity is imperative. Acute irritation of the airways and alveoli following inhalation, along with the resulting inflammatory response and oxidative stress, are key toxicological endpoints that require thorough evaluation. Comprehensive in vivo pharmacokinetic studies, tissue distribution analyses, and long-term toxicity assessments are essential for the translational medical advancement of these formulations.

In summary, inorganic nanomaterials-carried inhaled formulations present several advantages. Firstly, they exhibit multi-modal antimicrobial activity. These formulations not only serve as delivery vehicles for conventional antibiotics but also exert potent antimicrobial effects through synergistic or independent mechanisms. These mechanisms include the release of metal ions, the induction of copper-mediated cell death, the disruption of bacterial metabolism, and the elimination of bacteria via enzyme-like activity. Such actions effectively minimize the development of drug resistance. Secondly, the phenomenon of barrier penetration is a critical consideration. The use of surface engineering techniques and smart, responsive designs has been shown to effectively penetrate the pulmonary mucus layer and bacterial biofilms, facilitating drug delivery to deep-seated lesions. Thirdly, the concepts of controlled release and targeted delivery are imperative. The precise release of pharmaceutical agents is initiated by external magnetic fields or the pathological microenvironment, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while concurrently reducing systemic exposure. Despite these encouraging prospects, significant biosafety concerns remain. This is a result of the lack of clear regulatory pathways and comprehensive long-term clinical safety data. The prolonged transition poses significant challenges for regulatory approval.

Biomimetic-carried inhaled formulations

The advent of biologically inspired microrobots capable of autonomous propulsion in biological fluids has positioned them as transformative biomedical platforms due to their unique dynamic locomotion capabilities [190]. The administration modalities can be meticulously customized to target specific organs or pathological sites through rational design optimization, thereby enhancing functional specificity and therapeutic outcomes. The potential of these micro-robotic systems to revolutionize therapeutic and diagnostic applications is substantial. For example, microneedle robots may facilitate gastrointestinal drug delivery [191], bionic soft micro-robots can be controlled by precise magnetic collectives for microvascular thrombolysis [192], and smart patch micro-robotics could be employed for the treatment of myocardial infarction [193].

The escalating global burden of respiratory pathologies has propelled pulmonary microrobotic systems to the forefront of drug delivery research. Professor Joseph Wang's team pioneered a biohybrid microrobotic platform (algae-NP-robot) that utilizes Chlamydomonas reinhardtii microalgae engineered with neutrophil membrane-camouflaged drug-loaded polymeric nanoparticles for in vivo pulmonary infection therapy, as illustrated in Fig. 15A. This algal chassis combines scalable cultivation, sustained flagellar motility (>110 μm/s), intrinsic autofluorescence tracking capability, and prolonged self-propulsion (>48 h). Through bioorthogonal click chemistry, CIP-loaded neutrophil membrane-coated polymeric nanoparticles (150 ± 20 nm) were site-specifically conjugated to the microalgal surfaces, creating actively propelled antibiotic delivery systems. In vitro evaluations demonstrated rapid mucosal penetration (>110 μm/s in simulated lung fluid) and reduced alveolar macrophage clearance (phagocytosis rate <15% at 48 h post-intratracheal administration). In P. aeruginosa-induced murine pneumonia models, these microrobots achieved a 4.3-log colony-forming units (CFU) bacterial reduction, a 100% survival rate (compared to 40% in controls), and exhibited no observable hepatorenal toxicity over 7 days [68].

Reproduced with permission from Ref. [194]. (C) Probiotics-based OASCLR nanoparticle aerosol. Reproduced with permission from Ref. [195]. (D) Nucleic acid-loaded TOB by inhalation. Reproduced with permission from Ref. [196].

Building on this research, the team further optimized the system by employing Micromonas pusilla as a propulsion source [194]. The model drug VAN was encapsulated within PLGA nanoparticles that were coated with platelet membranes. These nanoparticles were then conjugated to the surface of the algal robot through click chemistry, resulting in the formation of an algal-VAN-robot composite, as illustrated in the pseudo-colour SEM image presented in Fig. 15B. With a diameter ranging from 1 to 1.5 μm, the algae-van-robot was efficiently encapsulated into aerosol particles, achieving 98.6% of particles measuring less than 10 μm, thereby facilitating deposition in the alveoli and terminal bronchioles. After nebulization, the microrobots exhibited stable distribution and retention in murine lungs for over five days, a phenomenon attributed to their self-propulsion-mediated evasion of macrophage phagocytosis. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of algae-nanoparticle hybrid microrobots for pulmonary delivery.

An alternative microbial modification strategy involves engineering probiotics. Lactobacillus rhamnosus (L. rhamnosus), one of the most prevalent live bacteria in humans, exhibits immunomodulatory and antimicrobial properties. Through microencapsulation, L. rhamnosus was surface-modified with CS, hyaluronic acid (HA), and saponins to form nanoparticles known as OASCLR [195]. As shown in Fig. 15C, the therapeutic effects of OASCLR on primary and secondary pneumonia are as follows: First, OASCLR retains the innate poverty of LR, allowing it to compete with various pathogens and eliminate them. Additionally, OASCLR modifies the composition of the lung microbiome. Furthermore, OASCLR targets M1 macrophages through HA-CD44 interactions, thereby modulating the overactive immune response of these macrophages. These OASCLR nanoparticles specifically target the proinflammatory state associated with primary bacterial pneumonia, thereby restoring the balance of pulmonary microbiota. This probiotic engineering strategy may also serve as a promising platform for treating diseases beyond bacterial pneumonia. In a similar vein, microbial modification engineering has found applications in the field of fungi as biomimetic delivery carriers. Tong et al. drew inspiration from spores to create a biomimetic carrier known as carbonized Ganoderma lucidum spores (BUD-cGLS) through a precise carbonization process. This method not only preserves the natural morphological advantages of spores but also minimizes the immune response and enhances the drug-loading capacity. GLS provide dual delivery advantages; their natural morphology facilitates bronchoalveolar deposition while preventing phagocytosis by macrophages, thereby improving pulmonary retention. When loaded with budesonide, these particles have been shown to significantly reduce airway resistance, inhibit mucin secretion, and decrease inflammatory cytokines [197].

Bionic approaches are inspired not only by microorganisms in nature but also by cellular structures in animals, which serve as bionic carriers for drug delivery. Bio-inspired cellular structures have the ability to target specific cells effectively. To achieve this, bio-inspired nanoparticles, which are engineered with a neutrophil-derived membrane, can alleviate asthma symptoms by modulating various pathological cells [198]. Zhang et al. successfully designed and engineered a biomimetic nanocarrier for targeted antibiotic delivery and toxin/bacterial clearance. As illustrated in Fig. 15D, the inhalation formulation features a sophisticated structure composed of tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs) encapsulated with TOB. This complex is further enveloped by a hybrid membrane formed from neutrophil vesicles (NVs) and platelet vesicles (PVs), resulting in the creation of a neutrophil-platelet hybrid membrane (NPM). By coating the hybridized membranes on the exterior of the shell, the nanocarriers were endowed with neutrophil-like chemotaxis and platelet-like bacterial adhesion for phase I inflammatory targeting. The specific binding of bacterial toxins to the hybridized membrane triggers toxin-mediated membrane lysis, releasing the antibiotic-loaded scaffold nucleic acids for the second phase of toxin neutralization and bacterial killing. Meanwhile, the immunomodulatory potential of the backbone nucleic acids allows the nanocarriers to complete the third phase of reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment. In mouse models of acute and chronic P. aeruginosa pneumonia, these nanocarriers effectively reduced bacterial burden and mortality at low doses with negligible toxicity. As demonstrated by existing research, inhalation formulations employing bionic strategies have the potential to provide a broader therapeutic platform for the treatment of bacterial lung infections [196].

Biomimetic-carried inhaled formulations epitomize innovation in the field of modern nanomedicine. These systems are defined by complex multi-step structural assembly processes that are both intricate and demanding. Such precision in preparation imposes stringent requirements on process control and batch consistency, thereby significantly increasing the difficulty and cost associated with large-scale production. The multifunctional properties of biomimetic carriers are enhanced through the incorporation of hybrid cell membranes or surface modifications [194]. However, the long-term stability of biomimetic formulations poses a significant challenge. During storage, the integrity of the cell membrane coating may be compromised due to factors such as oxidation and enzymatic degradation. Additionally, maintaining specific storage conditions (e.g., temperature and nutrients) is crucial for the survival and motility of microorganisms, which further complicates the development of inhalation formulations.

From a regulatory perspective, bio-mimetic inhalation formulations—especially those involving live microorganisms or human cell derivatives—pose unique challenges within the existing regulatory framework. Currently, the lack of a cohesive and standardized regulatory approach impedes the transition of these innovations into clinical practice. Several biosafety challenges have been identified, including the following: Immunogenicity and inflammatory responses: Although the aim of cell membrane coating is to avoid immune clearance by camouflaging nanoparticles, allogeneic or xenogeneic cell membranes may still elicit unpredictable immune responses. Additionally, the potential risks associated with the use of live microorganisms must be thoroughly evaluated. The colonization of live microalgae and engineered bacteria in immunocompromised hosts, the risk of horizontal gene transfer, and their behavior in an inflammatory pulmonary environment all present significant safety concerns. Furthermore, the long-term toxicity of these substances requires careful assessment. The cumulative effects of the biodegradation products of biomimetic carriers, particularly non-human proteins and polysaccharides, necessitate further investigation. These complex biosafety issues greatly increase the uncertainty and risks associated with clinical translation, making the journey from laboratory concept to clinical application challenging and fraught with difficulties.

Conclusions and outlooks

In exploring the route of inhalation formulations for the treatment of bacterial pneumonia, the selection of bactericidal agents is based on existing antibiotics with established antimicrobial efficacy and a defined bactericidal spectrum. The use of non-carried antibiotic inhalation formulations presents a rapid solution for developing programs to combat bacterial infections of the lungs in light of changes in dosage forms. However, most antibiotics are primarily available in injectable or oral forms and cannot be directly inhaled. There are numerous solutions and options for pulmonary delivery through the use of various carriers. Due to their natural amphiphilic properties, liposomes can carry both hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs, enhanced drug delivery efficiency while protecting drugs from degradation. Polymer carriers offer flexible design capabilities, including pH-sensitive, ROS-sensitive drug release, and enzyme-responsive triggered release for precise drug administration. Peptide and protein carriers exhibit functional diversity due to the structural variability of proteins, allowing for targeted modifications. The use of inorganic metallic nanomaterials as inhalation delivery carriers for the treatment of bacterial pneumonia presents several advantages, including the potential for multi-targeted antimicrobial activity and precise control over size and morphology. Additionally, other lung-delivered therapies, such as bionic treatments, are at the forefront of research.

In the selection of inhalation formulation carriers, each of the aforementioned formulations possesses distinct advantages. While non-carried inhaled formulations are easy to transform and have long served as the cornerstone of inhalable drugs, they struggle to meet the increasing demand for precise and multifunctional therapies. Polymer-carried inhaled formulations signify a paradigm shift from “drug delivery” to “intelligent intervention in the disease microenvironment,” yet they require patients to wait for synchronous breakthroughs in materials science and bioengineering. Although inorganic nanomaterial-carried inhaled formulations and biomimetic-carried inhaled formulations can theoretically overcome multiple physiological barriers efficiently, challenges regarding their biosafety and large-scale production hinder their ability to bridge the clinical translation gap in the short term. Currently, lipid-carried inhaled formulations represent the most balanced solution between efficacy (overcoming barriers) and translatability (scalability and regulatory compliance), thanks to their endogenous phospholipid components, relatively mature preparation processes, and existing approved products. They are the most promising direction for bacterial pneumonia inhalation therapy and are expected to continue leading in clinical translation and multifunctional enhancements.

In the design of inhaled formulation carriers, although drugs can reach the lungs through inhalation, their delivery is often hindered by the lung's powerful clearance mechanisms and various pathological and physiological barriers. These factors result in limited drug delivery efficiency, inadequate targeting, and a short duration of drug action. To overcome these challenges, the design of inhalable drugs must consider several factors, including the bactericidal agent, the drug carrier, modifications to the carrier, and the diameter of the particles. These considerations are essential to achieving effective deposition, long-term release, and the eradication of bacteria. Combination inhalation delivery systems with responsive release, long release cycles and pathogen-targeted carriers carrying safe and effective bactericides should be the focus of future research. Given the recent advancements and potential in this field, we remain optimistic that inhaled drug delivery can provide innovative solutions to alleviate the global burden of respiratory diseases.
