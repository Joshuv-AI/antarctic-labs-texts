Burden of permanent and deciduous dental caries in middle East and North Africa (MENA): an epidemiological analysis of global burden of disease study from 1990 to 2021
Authors: Negar Khorasani, Mahsa Nikaein, Sina Khayatzadeh, Amirmohammad Ansari Feyzabadi, Narges Panahandeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental caries, a prevalent non-communicable disease (NCD), remains a significant health challenge worldwide, especially in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. In this study, we analyzed and compared the burden of dental caries in the MENA region from 1990 to 2021 based on Global Burden of Disease (GBD 2021) database.MethodsData from the GBD 2021 study database was utilized to assess the incidence, prevalence, and years lived with disability (YLD) for dental caries across 21 MENA countries. The study estimates age-standardized rates, considering variables such as age, gender, and region. Additionally, the relationship between dental caries and the Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) was examined.ResultsThe age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of permanent dental caries (PDC) in the MENA region increased from 31,592.38 per 100,000 in 1990 to 31,920.12 in 2021 with total percentage change (TPC) of 0.01 (-0.01-0.03). The ASIR of deciduous dental caries (DDC) in MENA region decreased from 19388.28 in 1990 to 16170.92 with TPC of -0.17 (-0.38–0.15). Additionally, the age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) of PDC in the MENA region decreased from 1990 to 2021 with the TPC of -0.04 (-0.06 - -0.02). Likewise, the ASPR of DDC decreased from 1990 to 2021 with the TPC of -0.06 (-0.24- 0.21). The age-standardized YLD rate (ASYR) for both PDC and DDC decreased from 1990 to 2021 with the TPC of -0.05 (-0.07- -0.02) and − 0.04 (-0.36- 0.38), respectively.ConclusionsThe findings underscore the need for enhanced preventive efforts and healthcare access in the MENA region, particularly in low-income countries. Further attention is required to address age and gender disparities, with a focus on the most vulnerable populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06774-w.Keywords: Dental caries, Middle east and north africa, Global burden of disease, Preventive health, Age-standardized rates, Socio-economic factors

Introduction

Dental caries is caused by a dynamic process in which the tooth structure undergoes alternating phases of demineralization and remineralization leading to localized dental destruction [1].It is the impact of fermentable carbohydrates on the bacterial biofilm that forms on the hard tissue of the teeth, gradually over time [2]. Several key risk factors for dental caries have been identified, which include biological, behavioral, and socioeconomic factors, such as high sugar consumption, lack of sufficient saliva production, poor oral hygiene, and poverty [3, 4]. Untreated dental caries can cause discomfort in many aspects, and over time, it can extend to the pulp and lead to spontaneous pain [1, 5]. Untreated dental caries can have a negative impact on the quality of life related to oral health across various age groups. However, it is possible to prevent dental caries effectively. To accomplish this, primary prevention programs should focus on models of behavioral change and patient empowerment [6, 7]. Epidemiological studies, such as ours, are crucial for raising awareness about disease burden, which is a fundamental step in emphasizing the importance of preventive policies.

Untreated dental caries of permanent teeth is the most prevalent non-communicable disease (NCD), affecting 2.3 billion people worldwide [8]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), dental caries is ranked among the top 10 most costly chronic diseases to treat. The estimated expenditure for dental caries treatment in the United States alone in 2014 amounted to $122 billion [9]. If dental caries remain untreated, they can lead to significant consequences, like social and financial, on individuals and the healthcare system [10]. In addition, a higher number of dental cavities are left untreated in low-income countries [11]. Therefore, considering the growing burden of dental caries, it can impose significant challenges on the healthcare systems, especially in low-income countries [8].

The countries in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region share a similar and close culture characterized by a common religion, language, and racial affinity, distinguishing them from other parts of the world [12]. Countries in the MENA region share similar dietary habits due to cultural influences, such as religion. As a result, dietary patterns act as a significant common risk factor for NCDs, like tooth decay [13–16]. Several studies have assessed burden of dental caries in individual MENA countries. Recent studies conducted from 2017 to the present indicate a rising trend in the prevalence of dental caries among young children in Iran during the past 15 years [17–19]. Similar findings of this increasing trend have been observed in other countries, such as Saudi Arabia [20]. Multiple studies indicate a growing prevalence of dental caries in most MENA countries, which necessitates the need for enhanced research in the field of oral health and the implementation of the findings into national and regional health policies [21, 22]. Despite the numerous studies conducted on individual countries in the MENA region, there has been no comprehensive study comparing the burden of dental caries across MENA countries and years. Therefore, in this study, we analyzed and compared the burden of dental caries in the MENA region from 1990 to 2021 based on Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database.

Methods

Over view

The data utilized in the current study were obtained from the GBD 2021 database performed by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME). IHME is a global health research center based at the University of Washington, dedicated to providing reliable and comparable data on the world’s most significant health issues and assessing the effectiveness of the strategies used to address them. It makes this information freely accessible to students, researchers, and policymakers, ensuring they have the evidence required to make informed decisions on how to allocate resources to optimize population health [23]. The GBD project aimed to assess epidemiology and burden of 87 risk factor and 368 diseases and injuries in 204 countries from 1990 to 2021 [24]. Current study used GBD data to assess the burden of permanent and deciduous dental caries in MENA region, that involve 21 countries: Afghanistan, Algeria, Bahrain, Egypt, Iran, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Oman, Palestine, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Sudan, Syria, Tunisia, Turkey, United Arab Emirates (UAE), and Yemen.

Definition of condition & modeling

Dental caries classified under the ICD-10 code K02-K02.9 is defined as “teeth with unmistakable coronal cavity at dentin level, root cavity in cementum that feel soft or leathery to probing, temporary or permanent restorations, or missing teeth extracted due to a caries lesion” [25]. In GBD 2021, the modeling technique was employed to analyze caries in permanent and deciduous teeth separately, considering along fundamental distinctions such as age patterns, length of cavity presence, and contributing risk factors. Briefly, the modeling method utilizes tools such as DisMod-MR 2.1, a Bayesian meta-regression tool, to estimate epidemiological parameters based on year, age, gender, and region. The data input relies on the existing scientific literature and survey data [24].

Indices

We documented the incidence, prevalence, and years lived with disability (YLD) across all MENA countries for various age and gender groups from 1990 to 2021 using the GBD results tool. Moreover, rates age-standardized rates per 100,000 for these parameters were also reported. The estimated Age-Standardized Incidence Rate (ASIR), Age-Standardized Prevalence Rate (ASPR), and Age-Standardized YLD Rate (ASYR) were reported alongside their 95% uncertainty intervals (95%CI). Age-standardized rates (ASRs) are generally calculated by multiplying the age-specific rate for each age group () by the corresponding weight () of that age group in a standard reference population. The sum of these products is then divided by the total size (or sum of weights) of the standard population. You can see the formula below:

We also calculated the percentage change (%C) between 1990 and 2021. The severity of dental caries was classified into three levels: asymptomatic cases with no disability, mild cases experiencing pain for one hour per day during the initial phase of the disease, and severe cases experiencing constant pain during the initial and terminal phases. The calculation of YLD involved the multiplication of prevalence, duration, proportion, and disability weight for each age, country, sex, and year.

The Sociodemographic Index (SDI) is a combined indicator used to evaluate the level of socioeconomic development in a given location. SDI is derived from per capita income, average years of education, and the total fertility rate (TFR) among females under 25 years of age. It is expressed on a continuous scale from 0 to 1, representing a spectrum of development. An SDI value of 0 corresponds to the lowest income and educational attainment and the highest fertility rate observed across GBD locations, while a value of 1 reflects the highest income and education levels and the lowest fertility rate. As the SDI increases from 0 to 1, it indicates a transition toward higher socioeconomic development [24].

Statistical analysis

All figures and choropleth maps were created using Python 3.8 with the Folium 0.12.0, Numpy 1.21.2, Matplotlib 3.4.3, Statsmodels 0.13.0, and Pandas 1.3.3. libraries offering support. To explore the association between age-standardized rates related to Permanent Dental Caries (PDC) and Deciduous Dental Caries (DDC) and the SDI, we applied locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) regression to plot the predicted trends.

We calculated the percentage change in age-standardized rates between 1990 and 2021 to evaluate temporal trends. To assess the statistical significance of these changes, we examined the corresponding 95% CIs. Based on standard statistical interpretation, a change was considered statistically significant if the 95% CI did not include zero.

National level

The ASPR of PDC among MENA countries was highest in Afghanistan (39316.36 [95%CI: 34146.33–45080.81]), Saudi Arabia (39279.33 [95%CI: 33844.28–44915.00]), and Sudan (38272.86 [95%CI: 32833.53–44500.75]) in 2021, respectively. On the other hand, the lowest ASPR belonged to Iran (31184.50 [95%CI: 27490.59–35341.85]), Egypt (30753.40 [95%CI: 25620.07–37026.87]), and Morocco (19482.87 [95%CI: 16184.73–23308.40]). These three highest and lowest ASPR countries were the same in 1990. (Table 1 and Fig. 4)

Age and gender disparities

The highest incidence rate of PDC belonged to the 20–24 years age-group both in 1990 (50668.65 [95%CI: 39288.82–62071.81]) and 2021 (51701.63 [95%CI: 39976.51–62937.97]). Also, the age-group 40–44 years had the highest prevalence rate of PDC both in 1990 (46697.50 [95%CI: 31634.37–60262.35]) and 2021 (45031.52 [95%CI: 30606.73–58694.65]). It is noteworthy that the highest YLD rate of PDC also belonged to the 40–44 years age groups in 1990 (45.78 [95%CI:18.40–89.77]) and 2021 (44.08 [95%CI:18.00–86.84]). Discussion

This study provides a thorough evaluation of the burden of PDC and DDC in the MENA region from 1990 to 2021. All ASRs for DDC and PDC decreased from 1990 to 2021, with the exception of the ASIR for PDC, which had an upward trend throughout this period of time. The highest incidence of DDC was observed among children aged 5–9, while the highest incidence of PDC belonged to the 20–24 age group. Saudi Arabia was among the top three countries with the highest ASPR of both PDC and DDC in 1990 and 2021.

In this study we found that from 1990 to 2021, in MENA region, the ASIR of PDC slightly increased with almost a stable trend, while both ASPR and ASYR had a downward trend. The increase in ASIR may be attributed to a higher detection of new cases, driven by improved access to healthcare services, greater awareness of the importance of dental checkups, and lifestyle changes such as increased sugar consumption [8, 26, 27]. Furthermore, the stable trend with a slight increase in ASIR, alongside decreasing ASPR and ASYR, suggests an increasing treatment rate, but also highlights a deficiency in preventative efforts, as the majority of systems prioritize treatment above population-level prevention [28]. On the other hand, the reduction in ASPR and ASYR signifies enhanced treatment coverage and less long-term disability from caries, probably attributable to improved access to dental care [28]. Compared to global trends, the burden of PDC in the MENA region shows higher amounts in overall. This higher burden of PDC is likely linked to the region’s lower SDI and can be attributed to a combination of demographic, socioeconomic, and lifestyle factors that differ from other global scales [8, 29]. Dietary and lifestyle practices highly influence oral health, with sugar being the primary contributor to caries formation [30, 31]. The average consumption of added sugar in the MENA region is approximately 85 g/day, far exceeding the WHO’s recommended average of 50 g/day [32, 33]. This issue can be addressed through public health measures, including targeted sugar taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB), which are the primary source of sugar consumption, as outlined in the guideline released in 2022 by the WHO to guide SSB taxation [33]. Only seven out of the 21 MENA nations (36.8%)—Morocco, Tunisia, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, and Oman—have implemented taxation specifically targeting SSB [34]. More MENA countries should implement such policies in order to reduce sugar consumption and the burden of dental caries in the region.

A significant portion of the MENA region is made up of lower SDI Arab countries, many of which face serious challenges that contribute to the increasing burden of dental caries. Most of these countries are developing countries, or they still face some negative aspects of it. Healthcare systems face many challenges in developing countries, such as inadequate oral health personnel, and a lack of facilities and equipment [35]. In these countries the ratio of dentists to the general population is lower compared to industrialized countries [36, 37]. Also, dental services are often not included in health insurance plans or are limited to basic care only [38]. Furthermore, the heavy concentration of dentists in urban areas greatly restricts access to dental services for people living in rural regions [39]. For example, even wealthy countries such as Saudi Arabia show significant gaps in rural dental access [40]. To address this issue, policies encouraging the distribution of dental professionals to underserved rural areas are essential to reduce inequalities in oral healthcare. In Iran, for instance, many health-related graduate students, including dentists, are legally required to provide services for a certain period in underserved areas where healthcare workers are in high demand. This policy helps reduce healthcare disparities between rural and urban regions [41].

We found that all ASRs of DDC decreased from 1990 to 2021, both in the MENA region and globally. This suggests that global policies have been more effective in controlling the burden of DDC. Interestingly, unlike PDC, the burden of DDC in the MENA region was lower than the global average. This could be attributed to several factors, including genetics and nutrition. Research has shown that low-carbohydrate diets and better maternal care may contribute to this difference, as caries continue to occur at high rates in both developing and developed countries despite the presence of advanced healthcare systems in higher SDI regions [42, 43]. In contrast, the higher burden of DDC observed in more developed countries may be linked to increasing sugar consumption as economic growth leads to more access to processed foods, highlighting the global challenge of diet-related dental health issues [8]. Given the higher burden of PDC in MENA countries, there should be a greater focus on addressing the challenges associated with PDC, particularly in terms of prevention, early diagnosis, and access to quality dental care.

Among MENA countries, Saudi Arabia and Iran had the highest ASIR for PDC. The high ASIR of PDC in Iran could be related to the high prevalence of NCD risk factors in Iran, such as obesity and tobacco use, which can affect the incidence of new dental caries [44]. Interestingly, despite Iran’s high incidence, it is also among the countries with the lowest ASPR for PDC. A key reason for this could be Iran’s notably high “priority to health” indicator—defined as domestic general government health expenditure (GGHE-D) as a percentage of general government expenditure (GGE). This means that, compared to other MENA countries, Iran devotes the largest share of its government budget to healthcare [45]. Additionally, Iran implemented two major health sector reforms in 2004 (the Universal Rural Health Insurance) and 2014 (the Health Transformation Plan), which led to improvements in the health insurance system [46]. According to official data, more than 90% of the Iranian population is covered by at least one type of health insurance. This results in greater and more affordable access to treatment and may lead to a lower prevalence of dental caries [41]. On the other hand, Saudi Arabia—despite being a high-income country and a member of the oil-producing Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries—continues to face significant healthcare challenges. One major issue is the need to provide adequate health services to a large population of economic migrants, who make up 60–90% of the workforce and have considerably impacted the country’s population size and structure. This demographic pressure has increased the burden on the healthcare system to meet the needs of both migrant workers and their families [47]. Another major issue in Saudi Arabia is the disparity in healthcare services between rural and urban areas. The uneven distribution of healthcare professionals may contribute to a higher prevalence of dental caries, particularly in rural regions [40]. Although GCC countries still underperform in certain aspects of healthcare delivery, Saudi Arabia has launched numerous reforms aimed at improving health outcomes by 2030 [48, 49].

Another major issue that still challenges MENA region, is prolonged conflicts that some of them still is going on in different countries of the region. The World Bank classified eight MENA countries as being conflict-affected or fragile contexts in 2018 [50]. In 2010–2011, as a result of a wave mass of uprisings, the so called the “Arab spring”, several MENA countries such as Libya, Syria, Iraq and Yemen have been suffering from continued violent conflicts [51]. Major parts of the population of these conflict affected countries have faced migration to other countries including healthcare professionals [50]. As a result, countries like Sudan and Yemen have particularly poor dental service infrastructure [52, 53]. This migration also has affected those countries that host all these refugees. The MENA region hosts the largest refugee population in the world. These forced displacement has challenged countries hosting the refugees such as Lebanon and Jordan and has created regional refugee crises and sustained impacts and pressures on the health systems [50, 51, 54]. MENA region will continue to suffer from these challenges, until some stability begin to rule over the region.

The highest ASPR of DDC was observed in the 2–4 years age group. Healthcare delivery for specific age groups, particularly young children and the elderly, presents unique challenges. Managing dental treatment in very young children can be especially difficult for dentists, often requiring specialized approaches such as treatment under general anesthesia [28]. These additional care needs can place a significant financial and emotional burden on families, sometimes leading to the unintentional neglect of children’s dental treatment [1, 55]. The highest ASIR of DDC belonged to the 5–9 years age group, probably related to the higher number of deciduous teeth present in their mouth. After reaching the age 9, the incidence and prevalence of DDC began to decline probably due to deciduous tooth loss by getting older [56, 57]. The incidence and prevalence of PDC reached another peak in the 20–24 age range, most likely due to limited access to oral health school-based programs, unstable lifestyles, and excessive SSB consumption [58, 59]. Importantly, early childhood oral health behaviors have a major impact on adult outcomes [60]. To lower the long-term caries burden in young adults, targeted public health initiatives that promote food control, regular dental checkups, and consistent preventative habits are required. About gender disparities, the ASPR of PDC among females was higher than males both on the global scale and in the MENA region in 2021. As the eruption of teeth is earlier in women than in males, the exposure time to the risk factors for dental caries like sugar is longer in females. Also, females’ teeth get affected by hormonal changes like menstruation, puberty, pregnancy, and menopause that change the oral environment (composition, quantity, and flow rate of saliva) [61]. Furthermore, there is a shortage of female health workers in MENA nations, which can be a main access issue because female staff are required to care for female patients due to cultural norms in these countries [47]. This could indicate a lack of sufficient policies and regulations to assist the development and distribution of healthcare professionals in the MENA area [62].

Limitations

This study also has some limitations. Even with the GBD 2021’s methodological advancements, our study’s largest drawback was still the attainability of the source data. Inadequate reporting and data input can reduce the accuracy of the information acquired. Insufficient availability of high-quality data can also distort research results, which would impact the strength of the evidence. The impacts might be more severe in countries with lower SDI, such as the majority of MENA countries, and ongoing conflicts because there might be fewer healthcare facilities starting the epidemiological investigations [63].

Estimation relies on covariates and statistical modeling when data is limited. Large 95%CI measure uncertainty at several estimation phases. Discarding subtle variations between groups, locations, or time points in these broad user interfaces is more difficult. It is difficult to capture all sources of uncertainty during the estimation process, even though our research attempts to account for uncertainty from various data sources and procedures such as age-sex splitting. In addition, the descriptive nature of the analysis, which does not allow for causal inference, is an inherent limitation of this study, similar to other epidemiological studies, as our primary aim was to introduce the burden and epidemiological patterns of dental caries.

Conclusion

In the MENA region, while treatment has improved, substantial regional disparities persist. Although higher SDI countries showed some improvement, the burden remains high in low-income nations. Public health policies, such as sugar taxation, expanded dental care access, and oral health education, are essential to mitigate this issue. Future efforts should focus on reducing disparities and implementing effective prevention and treatment strategies to improve oral health outcomes across the MENA region.
