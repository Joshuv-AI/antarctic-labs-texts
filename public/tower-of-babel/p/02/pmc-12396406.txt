Acute pyelonephritis complicated by subcapsular and perinephric hematoma: Diagnostic role of CT scan and 6-month follow-up
Authors: Maryam Shahali Ramsheh, Reza Zahedpasha, Amin Ghaemi Amiri, Fereshte Maghsoudlou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acute pyelonephritis is a common renal infection that can become life-threatening when complicated by structural abnormalities, resistant pathogens, or systemic comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus. While common complications include renal abscess and sepsis, spontaneous subcapsular hematoma is a rare but serious manifestation that requires prompt recognition and intervention. This article reports the case of a 54-year-old female with a history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia, who presented with fever, flank pain, vomiting, and gross hematuria. She was diagnosed with complicated pyelonephritis and sepsis. Laboratory workup revealed leukocytosis, anemia, and thrombocytopenia. Coagulation studies were within normal limits, and the patient was not on any anticoagulant therapy. Initial abdominal CT showed left renal parenchymal inflammation without fluid collection. Follow-up imaging revealed a subcapsular hematoma compressing the left kidney, with associated extension into the left psoas muscle. The patient received broad-spectrum antibiotics, ICU-level care, and multiple transfusions. Over the course of hospitalization and follow-up, she required percutaneous drainage and was eventually scheduled for nephrectomy due to significantly reduced left renal function. This case documents the radiologic changes of subcapsular, perinephric, and psoas hematomas over 2- and 6-month intervals, allowing detailed evaluation of their progression through serial imaging follow-up.

Introduction

Acute pyelonephritis is an infection of the renal parenchyma and collecting system, affecting 15-17 per 10,000 females and 3-4 per 10,000 males annually [1]. The disease is more commonly observed in patients with diabetes, urinary obstruction, pregnancy, vesicoureteral reflux, neurogenic bladder, and immunosuppression. These risk factors contribute through mechanisms such as impaired immunity, urinary stasis, and increased bacterial growth or reflux.redominant pathogens include Escherichia coli, Klebsiella, and Proteus species [2]. Complications of pyelonephritis include renal abscess, sepsis, chronic kidney disease, papillary necrosis, emphysematous pyelonephritis, and renal scarring leading to hypertension and, in rare cases, renal hematoma. Renal hematoma, a collection of blood within the kidney or perinephric space, is a rare but serious complication that can lead to bleeding, occurring in approximately 0.1%-0.4% of pyelonephritis cases and is associated with trauma, vascular diseases, or severe infections [3]. Diagnosis relies on imaging, with contrast-enhanced CT demonstrating parenchymal hypodensities, striated nephogram, and potential abscess formation [4]. For renal hematomas, CT detects high-attenuating fluid collections, while ultrasound shows heterogeneous echogenic masses with variable appearances depending on hematoma age [5]. This case highlights a rare but potentially serious complication of pyelonephritis that warrants clinical attention and timely diagnosis.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old female patient with a medical history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia was initially presented to a lower-level hospital and subsequently transferred to a higher-level tertiary care center. The patient presented with a 5-day history of fever, chills, multiple episodes of non-bloody vomiting, left flank pain, and gross hematuria. On presentation, vital signs revealed blood pressure of 105/70 mmHg, pulse rate of 108 beats per minute, and temperature of 38.2°C. Physical examination demonstrated pale sclera, slow capillary refill time, and positive costovertebral angle tenderness on the left side.

Initial laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis, anemia, and thrombocytopenia accompanied by elevated inflammatory markers. The fecal occult blood test was negative, and all coagulation studies were within normal limits. Based on the clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and imaging studies, a diagnosis of acute pyelonephritis was established, complicated by sepsis. Given the patient's septic state, underlying diabetes mellitus, and classification as a case of complicated pyelonephritis, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU). Empirical triple therapy with meropenem, vancomycin, and ceftriaxone was initiated due to the severity of illness and concern for multidrug-resistant pathogens. One unit of packed red blood cells was transfused in response to her severe anemia (hemoglobin: 7 g/dL), accompanied by gross hematuria and clinical signs of hemodynamic compromise, including tachycardia and poor capillary refill. An abdominal CT scan was requested for further evaluation.

A CT scan performed at the higher-level center 4 days later revealed a subcapsular hematoma compressing the left renal parenchyma, measuring 30 mm in thickness, 83 mm in transverse diameter, and 110 mm in craniocaudal length (Figs. 2A and B). In addition, a perinephric hematoma was identified in the medial portion of the perinephric space, adjacent to the superficial surface of the psoas muscle. This hematoma measured 66 × 38 mm transversely and 130 mm in craniocaudal length. Multiple wedge-shaped hypodense areas were observed throughout the left renal parenchyma, associated with inflammatory changes, fat stranding, and fascial thickening in the perinephric space. During this hospitalization, the patient remained in the ICU for 7 days and received a total of 7 units of blood transfusion. After 22 days, she was discharged from the hospital in stable condition.

After being discharged in stable condition, the patient developed gross hematuria on the same day he presented for his previously scheduled follow-up imaging. The perinephric hematoma, measuring 110 × 82 mm transversely and 150 mm in craniocaudal length, also showed signs of liquefaction. Additionally, the psoas muscle hematoma was more conspicuous on this CT scan, measuring 62 × 52 mm transversely and 110 mm craniocaudally, exhibiting hyperdensity. Discussion

Acute pyelonephritis represents a significant infection of the renal parenchyma and collecting system, constituting approximately 250,000 office visits and 200,000 hospitalizations annually in the United States [1]. The condition demonstrates marked gender disparity, with females experiencing 15-17 cases per 10,000 population compared to 3-4 per 10,000 in males [2]. This disparity primarily reflects anatomical differences, including shorter urethral length in females. Age-stratified data reveals bimodal distribution peaks among reproductive-age women and elderly populations of both genders [3].

Complicated pyelonephritis is a severe form of kidney infection that occurs in patients with underlying risk factors such as diabetes, urinary tract obstruction, immunosuppression, or anatomical abnormalities. It is often associated with resistant pathogens and may present with systemic symptoms, requiring hospitalization, imaging studies, and broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. Early recognition and appropriate management are essential to prevent serious complications, including renal abscess, sepsis, acute kidney injury, and chronic scarring, potentially leading to long-term renal impairment or hypertension.

Renal hematoma, characterized by blood accumulation within the kidney or surrounding tissues, represents an uncommon complication of pyelonephritis. Epidemiological data suggests an incidence of 0.1%-0.4% among pyelonephritis cases, with higher prevalence among patients with comorbidities such as coagulopathies, diabetes mellitus or vascular disorders [4]. Spontaneous renal hematomas unrelated to trauma occur at an estimated rate of 0.3-0.6 per 100,000 person-years in the general population [5]. The presented patient had a history of diabetes mellitus. There was no evidence of coagulopathy, with normal PT and PTT levels; however, thrombocytopenia was noted, likely related to sepsis. Regarding vascular comorbidities, the patient had a history of hyperlipidemia and hypertension.

The microbiological profile of pyelonephritis is dominated by gram-negative enteric organisms, with Escherichia coli responsible for 70%-80% of cases. Other common pathogens include Klebsiella species (5%-10%), Proteus mirabilis (3%-5%), and Enterococcus species (3%-5%) [6]. Antimicrobial resistance patterns have evolved significantly since 2015, with extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) producing organisms increasingly identified in community-acquired infections [7]. Given the concern regarding antimicrobial resistance, and considering that the patient was classified as having complicated pyelonephritis, broad-spectrum antibiotics including meropenem, vancomycin, and ceftriaxone were initiated.

The pathogenesis of renal hematoma in the context of pyelonephritis involves several mechanisms. Severe inflammation can cause microvasculature disruption, while bacteremia-associated coagulopathy may predispose to bleeding [8]. Hematomas may develop spontaneously or following interventional procedures, with anticoagulation therapy representing a significant predisposing factor, increasing risk approximately 4-fold [9]. It is noteworthy that the presented patient was not receiving any anticoagulant or antithrombotic therapy.

Imaging plays a critical role in diagnosing both pyelonephritis and associated hematomas. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) remains the gold standard, demonstrating sensitivity of 92%-97% and specificity of 93%-97% for pyelonephritis [10]. Characteristic findings include wedge-shaped or striated nephrogram patterns, parenchymal hypodensities, and perinephric stranding. Advanced disease may present with abscess formation or emphysematous changes.

For renal hematomas, CT demonstrates excellent diagnostic performance with sensitivity exceeding 90% and specificity of 98% [11]. Acute hematomas appear as high-attenuating (45-90 Hounsfield units) collections, while subacute and chronic hematomas demonstrate progressive density reduction. CT also provides critical information regarding hematoma size, extension, and mass effect on surrounding structures.Ultrasound, while less sensitive (sensitivity 68%-87%, specificity 78%-96% for pyelonephritis), offers advantages including bedside availability, lack of radiation, and utility for serial monitoring [12]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrates comparable sensitivity (90%-95%) to CT for pyelonephritis while offering superior tissue characterization. T2-weighted sequences reveal edematous regions with high signal intensity, while diffusion-weighted imaging shows restricted diffusion in affected parenchyma (sensitivity 95%, specificity 92%) [13]. For hematomas, MRI excellently characterizes evolution through various hemoglobin degradation stages, though its practical application is limited by cost and availability.Nuclear medicine studies, particularly 99mTc-DMSA scans, offer high sensitivity (87%-98%) for pyelonephritis through demonstration of photopenic defects but with lower specificity (77%-89%) and limited anatomical resolution [14]. While historically valuable, these studies have been largely supplanted by cross-sectional imaging in contemporary practice.

Conclusions

This case underscores the importance of considering rare complications such as subcapsular renal hematoma in patients with complicated pyelonephritis, particularly those with risk factors like diabetes and sepsis. Despite the absence of anticoagulant use or overt coagulopathy, the patient developed significant hemorrhagic complications. Early and accurate diagnosis using imaging—particularly contrast-enhanced CT—played a crucial role in identifying the hematoma and guiding further management. Timely recognition and multidisciplinary care are essential to preventing renal deterioration and optimizing outcomes in complex presentations of pyelonephritis.
