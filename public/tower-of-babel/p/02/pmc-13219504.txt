Plasma and aqueous humor levels of Maresin-1 in patients with diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Sabiha Güngör Kobat, Fatih Cem Gül, Fatih Çelik, Seda Liman Uzun, Fatma Savur, Kader Kasar, Sara Koylu Güngör, Mehmet Balbaba, Süleyman Aydın
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to evaluate plasma and aqueous Maresin-1 (MaR1) levels in patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR) and to examine the ability of these levels to distinguish proliferative diabetic retinopathy. The study included three age and gender-matched groups of 25 cataract patients with no diabetes mellitus (DM) or additional disease (C), 25 cataract patients with diabetes and no retinopathy (DM + C), and 25 cataract patients with diabetic proliferative retinopathy (DR + C). All the patients were examined with respect to age, gender, fasting plasma glucose and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c). Phaco + IOL implantation was applied to all patients in all the groups, and aqueous samples were taken during the operation. The plasma and aqueous MaR1 levels were analyzed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays. The diagnostic performance of plasma and aqueous MaR1 levels in distinguishing the DR + C group from the C group was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A statistically significant difference was determined between the groups with respect to fasting plasma glucose, and HbA1c levels (p < 0.0167 for all parameters tested). The plasma MaR1 levels in group DR + C were determined to be statistically significantly lower compared to the C and DM + C groups (p < 0.001). The plasma MaR1 levels in group DM + C were determined to be statistically significantly lower compared to the C group (p < 0.001). The aqueous MaR1 levels in group DR + C were determined to be statistically significantly higher compared to the C and DM + C groups (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, respectively). No statistically significant difference was determined between the DM + C and C groups in respect of the aqueous MaR1 levels (p = 0.590). ROC analysis showed excellent discriminative performance of both plasma and aqueous MaR1 levels in distinguishing the DR + C group from the C group (AUC = 0.968 and 0.974, respectively; both p < 0.001). According to the results of the study, MaR1 levels were significantly lower in plasma and significantly higher in the aqueous humor in the DR + C group. ROC analysis indicated that both plasma and aqueous MaR1 levels showed good discriminative ability in distinguishing the DR + C group from the control group. These findings suggest that alterations in MaR1 levels may be associated with the inflammatory processes involved in diabetic retinopathy and may reflect disease-related biological changes in both systemic circulation and ocular tissues.Keywords: Diabetic retinopathy, Anti-inflammatory mediators, Plasma MaR1, Aqueous MaR1Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Endocrinology, Medical research

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a systemic disease that develops as a result of insulin deficiency or tissue resistance to insulin and progresses with hyperglycemia. All micro and macro systems can be affected, primarily the eyes, kidneys, heart, and peripheral nerves1,2. Diabetic retinopathy is the most important microvascular complication of DM. Several vascular, inflammatory, and neuronal mechanisms have been held responsible in the pathogenesis of DR3. Inflammation is the response given by the body to exogenous and endogenous threats to the organism. Although inflammation is useful in the acute period, unwanted effects emerge in the chronic period. DR is accepted as a low-grade inflammatory disease4. Hyperglycemia itself forms a pro-inflammatory environment5. Exposure of retinal cells to a high level of glucose causes an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines. Clinical studies have shown increased level of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the vitreous of patients with DR, and that this is correlated with the progression and severity of retinal damage6,7.

MaR1, which has been defined as a new anti-inflammatory mediator, is synthesised in macrophages from docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)8. During inflammation, it limits polymorphonuclear neutrophil (PMN) tissue infiltration, and helps to reduce and prevent local tissue damage. It has been found to contribute to resolving inflammation and promotes tissue regeneration9–11. In recent studies, MaR1 has been shown to be associated with obesity, hepatic steatosis, inflammatory arthritis, acute pancreatitis, neurodegenerative disorders, and diabetic foot12–16. MaR1 has also been shown to reduce levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, which cause inflammation in DR17–19.

The aim of this study was to compare plasma and aqueous MaR1 levels between patients with and without diabetic retinopathy and to evaluate the discriminative ability of these levels for proliferative diabetic retinopathy using ROC analysis.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Firat University Faculty of Medicine as a prospective study (approval no: 2022/09–44). This study was designed as a cross-sectional observational analysis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Patients who presented to the Eye Diseases Polyclinic of Firat University with complaints of decreased vision, were diagnosed with cataract following a comprehensive ophthalmological examination, and subsequently underwent cataract surgery were included in the study. Diabetes mellitus (DM) was diagnosed by a specialist in endocrine and metabolic diseases according to the American Diabetes Association guidelines. Cataract and diabetic retinopathy (DR) diagnoses were established by ophthalmologists based on the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) criteria20,21.

All participants underwent a complete ophthalmological examination prior to cataract surgery, including assessment of refraction and best-corrected visual acuity, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, intraocular pressure measurement using Goldmann applanation tonometry, and dilated fundus examination. Patients with glaucoma, corneal disease, uveitis, a history of ocular trauma, previous ocular surgery, or prior laser treatment were excluded.

According to the ETDRS criteria, patients exhibiting retinal or optic disc neovascularization and/or associated vitreous hemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment, or fibrovascular membrane formation were classified as having proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR). Only patients with PDR were included in the DR group; non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy cases were excluded.

Participants were divided into three age- and sex-matched groups, each comprising 25 patients: cataract patients without DM or systemic disease (C group), cataract patients with DM but without diabetic retinopathy (DM + C group), and cataract patients with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (DR + C group). Patients who experienced intraoperative complications (such as posterior capsule rupture, nucleus drop, or zonular dehiscence) or had additional ophthalmological or systemic diseases (including glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration, systemic arterial hypertension, or autoimmune diseases) were excluded.

To minimize potential confounding factors, participants with similar durations of diabetes and comparable treatment regimens were included. Antidiabetic treatments consisted of diet and exercise alone, diet and exercise plus metformin (2000 mg/day) and gliclazide (30 mg/day), or diet and exercise plus metformin (2000 mg/day) and insulin (0.3–0.4 IU/kg/day). Patients had no comorbidities other than diabetic retinopathy. Individuals with involvement of other organ systems, such as diabetic nephropathy, were excluded. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were measured, and patients with elevated inflammatory markers were excluded.

A 10 ml blood sample was taken from all the study subjects in the morning after 8–12 h fasting into a tube containing aprotinin (BD Vacutainer SST II Advance, BD, Plymouth, UK). All the patients were examined in respect of fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and HbA1c. The blood samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min and the obtained plasma was placed in small volume tubes and stored at -80 °C until assay for MaR1. All the patients were applied with Phaco + IOL implantation, and during the cataract operation, 50–100 µL aqueous samples were taken and stored at -80 °C until the testing day.

Measurement of hormones in biological fluids

Plasma MaR1 levels were examined using the Human MaR1 Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) Kit (Cayman Chemical, Michigan, USA). The ELISA measurements were made in accordance with the procedures stated in the kit catalogue. An automatic washing device (Bio-Tek ELX50, BioTek Instruments, USA) was used for plate washing, and the absorbance readings were made using the ChroMate, Microplate Reader P4300 device (Awareness Technology Instruments, USA). The assay range of the MaR1 ELISA kit was 64–1000 pg/mL, with sensitivity of 9.6 pg/mL, and lower limit of detection, 5.8 pg/mL. Intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) were not reported by the manufacturer. However, based on our in-house laboratory analyses, the intra-assay CVs were below 10%, and the inter-assay CVs were below 15%. In addition, based on our laboratory assay validation experiments, the ELISA kit was found to measure MaR1 in aqueous humor with the same sensitivity as in plasma. Aqueous humor samples were analyzed without additional dilution, and all measured concentrations were corrected using the reported dilution factor.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained in the study were analyzed statistically using SPSS version 22.0 software (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 22.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The sample size was calculated as n = 75 using G*Power 3.1.9.4, with Effect size f = 0.47, Err prob = 0.05, Power = 0.95, and Number of groups = 3. Also, a priori power analysis was apllied and calculated as a priori power = 0.9553677. Because the sample sizes in the study groups were relatively small, the normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As at least one group did not demonstrate a normal distribution, comparisons among the three groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When a statistically significant difference was identified, pairwise comparisons between groups were carried out as post-hoc analyses using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction applied. A p value < 0.0167 (0.05/3) was considered statistically significant. Diabetes duration was also evaluated between the DM + C and DR + C groups, and since this variable showed a normal distribution, the comparison between the two groups was performed using the independent samples t-test. ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of plasma and aqueous MaR1 levels in discriminating patients with DR + C from C. Area under the curve (AUC) values, cut-off points, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated. The optimal cut-off value was determined using the Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1), which maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity.

The group of DR + C comprised 12 females (48%) and 13 males (52%) with a mean age of 67.12 ± 2.02 years. No statistically significant difference was determined between the groups in respect of age and gender (p > 0.0167 for all) (Table 1).

Discussion

Hyperglycemia is an important but modifiable risk factor in the development of DR22. Hyperglycemia itself forms a pro-inflammatory environment. Exposure of retinal cells to a high level of glucose causes an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines. DR is accepted as a low-grade inflammatory disease4. In patients with DR, there has been seen to be an increase in leukocyte counts in peripheral blood associated with insulin resistance23. Previous studies have also reported increased neutrophils in the choroid and retinal vessels in diabetics, together with increased expression of ICAM-1, CD18, and CD1124,25.

Leukostasis causes endothelial dysfunction mediated by inflammatory cytokines26. Increased leukocytes in choriocapillaris have been found to be correlated with increased capillary obstruction of leukocyte clustering within the lumen and reduced numbers of endothelial cells27. Following capillary obstruction, there is an increase in VEGF secondary to hypoxia. VEGF causes cytokine production and leukocyte activation by increasing the expression of ICAM-1 in endothelial cells. The cytokines released into the environment stimulate the inflammatory response and cause a greater expression of VEGF28,29. In patients with diabetic macular oedema, intravitreal steroid injection has been shown to decrease the level of inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, MCP-1, and VEGF30. Moreover, the determination of a lower incidence of DR development in diabetic patients with rheumatoid arthritis who are using salicylate suggests that an early start of anti-inflammatory treatment could prevent the development of DR31.

The newly identified MaR1 is the first reported member of the maresin family, and shows extensive anti-inflammatory effects under physiological conditions in endothelial cells and vascular smooth muscle cells. MaR1 is synthesised in macrophages from docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). During inflammation, it limits polymorphonuclear neutrophil (PMN) tissue infiltration, and helps to reduce and prevent local tissue damage. It has been found to contribute to resolving inflammation and promotes tissue regeneration8–11. In recent studies, MaR1 has been shown to be associated with obesity, hepatic steatosis, inflammatory arthritis, acute pancreatitis, neurodegenerative disorders, and diabetic foot12–16. MaR1 has also been shown to reduce levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, which cause inflammation in DR17–19.

In patients followed up because of diabetic foot, the serum MaR1 levels have been determined to be lower than those of healthy control subjects, and it has been reported that MaR1 levels are affected by several clinical factors such as glucose and insulin secretion and resistance14. In the current study, the plasma MaR1 levels were determined to be significantly lower in the DM and DR groups compared to the control group. The MaR1 levels in DM, which can be characterised as a chronic inflammatory disease, were low as in other chronic inflammatory diseases, consistent with findings in literature32. The low plasma levels of the MaR1 molecule, which has known effects in insulin and glucose homeostasis, obtained in the current study in the chronic disease of DM, suggest that this molecule is important in the continuation of glucose homeostasis of the body. In our study, although patients had comparable treatment regimens and similar durations of diabetes, the DR group exhibited markedly elevated HbA1c values accompanied by substantially dysregulated blood glucose profiles. This group also demonstrated the lowest plasma MaR1 concentrations. Reduced MaR1 levels were consistently associated with poor glycemic control. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential significance of MaR1 in the pathophysiology of diabetes and align with previously reported evidence in the literature. However, it cannot be definitively concluded that the association between MaR1 levels and proliferative diabetic retinopathy is entirely independent of differences in glycemic control.

In a study by Martinez-Fernandez et al., it was determined that increased TNF-α in type 2 diabetic rats blocked the stimulator efficacy of insulin and MaR1 eliminated this blockage of TNF-α15. White adipose tissue and skeletal muscle affect glucose uptake and are thereby the tissues responsible for insulin resistance33. GLUT4 glucose transport is required for the transfer of insulin-dependent glucose to tissues34. Akt phosphorylation is required to be able to study GLUT435. MaR1 has been determined to mediate insulin-mediated glucose transport by causing Akt phosphorylation. Following MaR1 treatment in rats, there has been determined to be an increase in the transfer of glucose to tissues and a decrease in insulin resistance15. It has also been reported that MaR1 treatment caused activation in the AMPK pathway, which improved adipose tissue dysfunction and inflammation in insulin resistance formed because of obesity, and therefore, MaR1 could be a new therapeutic agent for the control of obesity and inflammation. There has also been determined to be a decrease in insulin resistance following protectin DX treatment, which is another specialized proresolving mediators (SPMs) synthesised from DHA36. Sugimoto et al. demonstrated in their study that obesity and insulin resistance improved through the anti-inflammatory effects of Maresin37. In addition, Li et al. reported that MaR1 prevents the development of DR in diabetic rats38. In our study, the low plasma MaR1 levels we detected, together with the previously described protective effects of maresin against insulin resistance and DR development, suggest that maresin may have potential therapeutic value in DM.

A significant increase has been determined in SPM levels in samples taken from around a diabetic wound in diabetic patients. It has been emphasised that this increase contributed to tissue regeneration in patients, and the elevated levels determined in the wound site showed the importance of SPMs in inflammation in humans39. Although the plasma MaR1 levels were determined to be low in the current study, the MaR1 levels were determined to be high in the presence of DR in the anterior chamber. DR is experienced as an active inflammatory process within the eye. As MaR1 is known to be an important molecule in acute inflammation in particular, the high levels of MaR1 determined only in the anterior chamber in DR patients suggest that the levels of MaR1 molecule are increased as an anti-inflammatory response. MaR1 is a specialized pro-resolving mediator with well-characterized anti-inflammatory activity. In our study, the markedly elevated intraocular concentrations of MaR1 are most plausibly attributable to inflammation-driven upregulation of local synthesis. Although the eye is an immune-privileged organ, the integrity of the blood–retina barrier is compromised in proliferative retinopathy. Despite relatively low systemic MaR1 levels, the disproportionately higher concentrations detected within the eye point toward enhanced intraocular production rather than passive diffusion from the circulation. Although plasma MaR1 concentrations were substantially higher than those measured in the aqueous humor, the magnitude of the difference does not convincingly support the notion that elevated intraocular levels result from barrier leakage. The present findings may be compatible with the possibility that local biosynthetic mechanisms are activated in response to ocular inflammation; however, the current study design does not allow definitive determination of the source of intraocular MaR1. Although local ocular synthesis of MaR1 has not yet been definitively demonstrated in humans, its detection in aqueous humor in our study suggests two possible sources. First, MaR1 may diffuse from the systemic circulation through the blood–aqueous barrier, albeit at substantially lower concentrations than in plasma. Second, ocular tissues—including the retina, retinal pigment epithelium, and immune-active glial cells—are rich in docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and possess the cellular machinery necessary for pro-resolving lipid mediator biosynthesis, raising the possibility of local production. In the context of diabetic retinopathy, such local synthesis could have important implications for the resolution of retinal inflammation and tissue homeostasis. Future mechanistic studies are required to elucidate whether MaR1 is produced in situ in the eye or primarily originates from systemic sources. Furthermore, the levels of MaR1 detected in aqueous humor were above the assay’s detection limit, providing additional evidence for its presence in this fluid. Future studies involving diabetic patients with multi-organ involvement (e.g., nephropathy) may help further elucidate the interplay between systemic inflammation, barrier integrity, and ocular MaR1 dynamics. Additionally, any discrepancies in the analytical units used for plasma versus aqueous humor measurements should be carefully considered, as such differences may confound interpretations related to intraocular accumulation.

Of the ω − 3 and ω − 6 free fatty acids, SPMs synthesised with precursors such as DHA and AA increase secretion in conjunctival goblet cells under physiological conditions. SPMs such as RvD1, RvD2, MaR1, RvE1, and LXA4 are synthesised in similar ways, and can form an effect by affecting the receptors of each other. If a defect forms in the synthesis of any of the above-mentioned molecules in the conjunctiva, the other molecules can become dominant to provide homeostasis40–43.

Previous studies have determined a DHA defect and an associated reduction in resolvin and protectin levels in patients with age-related macular degeneration (AMD)44. There is no study of resolvin in the treatment of AMD, but it is a molecule that has been trialled as a drug in ocular surface diseases. RvE1 has been determined to increase tear production and corneal integrity and to decrease COX-2-related inflammation45. Drug studies of resolvin have been made by the Resolyx pharmaceuticals company in several inflammatory diseases such as dry eye, retinal disease, asthma, inflammatory bowel disease, cardiovascular diseases, and rheumatoid arthritis (RX-10001 (RvE1), and RX-10045 (synthetic resolvin analogues). The topical formulation of RX-10045 is at the phase 2 stage for dry eye, a disease in which inflammation is known to have an important role in the pathophysiology. Positive results have been obtained in dry eye with RX-1004546. There has also been determined to be a decrease in histamine-mediated secretion in rat conjunctival goblet cells incubated with MaR1. This finding shows that MaR1 could be a new treatment option in the treatment of allergic conjunctivitis47. In the current study, aqueous MaR1 levels were determined to be higher in the DR group than in the DM and C groups. The results obtained in this study suggest that MaR1 molecule, which is synthesised in the same pathway as resolvin, could be a potential molecule that could be used in the treatment of eye diseases.

Our findings on elevated intraocular MaR1 levels are in line with accumulating evidence that SPMs actively modulate retinal inflammation and neurovascular integrity. Experimental DR models have demonstrated that RvD1 attenuates retinal inflammation and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, as well as VEGF-related miRNA dysregulation under hyperglycemic conditions. Likewise, MaR1 has been shown to protect RPE cells from high-glucose–induced ferroptosis via Nrf2/HO-1/GPX4 signaling. Beyond DR, NPD1 and MaR1 exert neuroprotective and glia-modulating actions in photoreceptors, RPE, and blast-injured retina. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate the presence of MaR1 in aqueous humor. Furthermore, when compared with plasma concentrations, MaR1 levels in aqueous humor were approximately 35-fold lower. These data support the concept that altered MaR1 dynamics in our cohort may reflect a disturbed resolution phase of inflammation in DR rather than a purely passive epiphenomenon48–50.

The ROC analysis findings obtained in this study indicate that plasma and aqueous MaR1 levels exhibit high discriminative performance in distinguishing the DR + C group from the C group. The consistently high AUC values observed in both biological samples suggest that alterations in MaR1 levels may be closely associated with the inflammatory processes accompanying diabetic retinopathy. However, rather than being interpreted as a stand-alone diagnostic marker, these findings are more appropriately considered as supportive indicators reflecting disease-specific biological changes and reinforcing the existing pathophysiological evidence. In addition, given the relatively small sample size and the absence of external validation, these ROC results should be interpreted as exploratory findings rather than definitive evidence of clinical biomarker performance.

This study has several limitations. Foremost among these are the relatively small sample size and the cross-sectional nature of the design, both of which restrict the strength of the inferences that can be drawn. Moreover, the non-randomized allocation of participants may have introduced selection bias, despite efforts to match the groups according to age, sex, and diabetes-related characteristics. In addition, although age and sex were matched across groups, glycemic control parameters—particularly HbA1c levels—were higher in the proliferative diabetic retinopathy group, which should be considered a relevant limitation. Given the established associations between metabolic control, inflammation, and specialized pro-resolving lipid mediators, differences in HbA1c may have acted as a potential confounding factor influencing MaR1 levels. Therefore, the associations observed between MaR1 levels and proliferative diabetic retinopathy should be interpreted as unadjusted findings and may partly reflect differences in glycemic control. Only patients with proliferative diabetic retinopathy were included in this study, while non-proliferative stages were not evaluated. Therefore, the findings should not be generalized to early stages of diabetic retinopathy and should be interpreted as specific to the proliferative stage. Furthermore, the absence of vitreous MaR1 measurements and the lack of a detailed lipid profile limit the ability to achieve a more comprehensive biochemical assessment of the study population. Future investigations incorporating larger cohorts, longitudinal designs, and randomized methodologies will be essential to substantiate and expand upon the present findings.

Conclusion

Plasma MaR1 levels were significantly lower in patients with diabetes mellitus and diabetic retinopathy compared to healthy controls, reflecting the chronic systemic inflammatory state of diabetes. Interestingly, MaR1 concentrations were elevated in the aqueous humor of DR patients despite low plasma levels, suggesting a localized anti-inflammatory response within the eye. These findings suggest that systemic alterations in MaR1 levels may be associated with the inflammatory processes involved in diabetic retinopathy. The elevated aqueous MaR1 levels observed in the present study may reflect a local anti-inflammatory response within the eye; however, the current study design does not allow determination of the exact source or mechanism of this increase.
