Effects of dietary ergosterol supplementation on growth performance, antioxidant capacity, and intestinal health in weaned pigs
Authors: Weidong Cai, Kunhong Xie, Yan Li, Bing Yu, Yuheng Luo, Ping Zheng, Xiangbing Mao, Jie Yu, Junqiu Luo, Hui Yan, Jun He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractErgosterol (ERG), a bioactive fungal sterol, possesses potent antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties, but its potential benefits on the intestinal health of weaned pigs remain largely unknown. To explore the effects of ERG on growth performance, antioxidant capacity, and intestinal health, 180 weaned pigs (21-d-old, initial body weight 7.26 ± 0.26 kg) were randomly divided into 5 treatments with 6 replicate pens per treatment (6 pigs per pen) and fed with a basal diet (CON) or basal diet supplemented with 100, 200, 400, and 800 mg/kg ERG, respectively. The experiment lasted for six weeks. Results showed that, dietary ERG significantly decreased the diarrhea incidence (P < 0.001) and the feed-to-gain ratio (P = 0.038). Compared with the CON group, ERG supplementation at 200 and 400 mg/kg significantly decreased the serum concentration of urea (P = 0.025). While dietary ERG quadratically decreased the concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) (P = 0.027). Dietary ERG supplementation linearly increased the serum concentration of immunoglobulin (Ig) G (P = 0.044) and quadratically increased the serum level of IgM (P = 0.007). In addition, compared with the CON group, ERG supplementation at 400 mg/kg significantly increased the serum concentration of glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px; P = 0.025). Compared with the CON group, ERG supplementation at 200 mg/kg significantly increased the villus height, the abundance of ZO-1 protein, and the total antioxidative capacity (T-AOC) in the jejunal epithelium (P < 0.05). The ERG supplementation at 200 and 400 mg/kg increased the abundance of jejunal secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA)-positive cells compared with that in the CON group (P < 0.05). Moreover, ERG elevated the abundance of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, and the concentrations of major short-chain fatty acids in the caecum (P < 0.05). Importantly, ERG downregulated the expression level of IL-6 in the jejunum relative to the CON group (P = 0.014). ERG supplementation at 200 mg/kg also decreased the expression levels of Caspase-9 and NF-κB in the duodenum and ileum, respectively (P < 0.05). These results suggest a beneficial effect of ERG on the growth and intestinal health in weaned pigs, and such an effect may be closely associated with improved immunity, antioxidant capacity, and intestinal epithelial barrier function, as well as improved intestinal microbiota.Keywords: Ergosterol, Growth performance, Antioxidative capacity, Intestinal function, Weaned pig

Introduction

Weaning represents one of the most significant challenges in pig farming, as the phenomenon known as “weaning stress” often results in reduced growth performance and impaired intestinal function (Kim et al., 2012; Liang et al., 2021; Moeser et al., 2017; Wang and Ji, 2019). Over the last few decades, various antibiotics have been used to mitigate the effects of weaning stress in pigs. This approach has been favored due to antibiotics' ability to suppress harmful bacterial growth and enhance feed utilization. However, continuous or excessive administration of these agents has promoted bacterial resistance and caused residue retention in products of animal origin (Lourenco et al., 2021). Consequently, the search for innovative alternatives to conventional antibiotics has garnered significant attention from researchers across the globe.

Sterols are a class of naturally occurring organic compounds characterized by a cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene backbone, consisting of four fused rings (three six-membered rings and one five-membered ring), a hydroxyl group (-OH, usually at the C-3 position, conferring weak hydrophilicity), a side chain at C-17 composed of 8 to 10 carbon atoms, and possible double bonds (e.g., a double bond between C-5 and C-6 in cholesterol). They are widely distributed in animals, plants, and microorganisms and exhibit various biological activities, such as antitumor (Rajavel et al., 2017), anti-inflammatory (Hu et al., 2017), and immunomodulatory effects (Spann and Glass, 2013). According to their biological origin, sterols can be classified into animal-derived cholesterol, plant-derived sterols (e.g., β-sitosterol and stigmasterol), and fungal sterols (e.g., ergosterol [ERG] and lanosterol).

Ergosterol is one of the major natural sterols present in fungal cell membranes and also serves as a precursor of vitamin D2 (Marucci et al., 2020). Research evidence indicates that ERG is a key component of fungal cell membranes, playing a crucial role in maintaining membrane structure, influencing the function of membrane-associated enzymes, regulating membrane fluidity, sustaining cell survival, and facilitating nutrient transport by adjusting lipid bilayer fluidity (Kang et al., 2013). Furthermore, ERG exhibits multiple physiological regulatory functions, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, lipid-lowering, and anti-tumor effects (Corrêa et al., 2018). For instance, ERG pretreatment was found to attenuate hepatic cell apoptosis in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-challenged rats by activating the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant enzyme regulatory network (Xu et al., 2018). Furthermore, ERG also interferes with NF-κB activation in murine macrophage RAW 264.7 cells stimulated by LPS, leading to a decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, notably TNF-α and IL-6 (Kuo et al., 2011). Moreover, earlier research has shown that supplementing the diet with ERG markedly lowered serum levels of total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), while simultaneously enhancing cholesterol excretion in the feces of rats (He et al., 2019). Notably, ERG peroxide has been shown to mitigate porcine deltacoronavirus (PDCoV) infection in piglets (Duan et al., 2021) and to attenuate porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) proliferation in Vero cells through suppression of reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation and inhibition of p53 activation (Liu et al., 2022).

Although numerous investigations have emphasized ERG's beneficial effects on inflammatory regulation and immune activity, its influence on livestock, especially weaned pigs, has not been well explored. This study aimed to examine the effects of dietary supplementation with ERG on growth performance, antioxidant capacity, and intestinal epithelial function in weaned piglets. Additionally, the underlying mechanisms by which ERG modulates these biological processes were investigated. The findings of this research are expected to offer a new approach for mitigating growth delays and intestinal issues caused by weaning in pigs, while also contributing to the broader implementation of ERG in animal nutrition and feed production.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The experimental protocols employed in this study were reviewed and granted official authorization by Sichuan Agricultural University's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC approval number: SICAU20240042). All research activities were conducted within the Animal Research Facility of Sichuan Agricultural University, adhering strictly to established institutional guidelines for ethical treatment and welfare of laboratory animals.

Preparation of ERG

The ERG used in the animal experiments was sourced from Inner Mongolia Maiwei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Bayannur, Inner Mongolia, China) and had a purity of 95%.

Animals, experimental design, and diets

This study utilized 180 hybrid piglets (Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire) 21 d post-weaning. Following a three-day adaptation phase, animals averaging an initial body weight of 7.26 ± 0.26 kg were randomly allocated into five experimental groups, each containing six replicate pens with six animals per enclosure. The six-week trial period involved administering distinct dietary regimens: the control group received a standard basal diet (CON), while intervention groups were provided equivalent rations supplemented with varying concentrations of ERG (100, 200, 400, or 800 mg/kg). Ambient temperature was maintained at 26 to 29 °C with 50%–65% relative humidity. Each experimental unit was equipped with free-access feeding stations and dual nipple drinkers to ensure continuous nutrient and water availability. Dietary formulations (Table 1) were developed in accordance with the NRC (2012) guidelines to meet or exceed the established nutritional requirements.

Chemical analysis and calculation

The diet were evaluated for dry matter (DM) using an electric blast drying oven (101-3A, Shanghai Lunan Electric Furnace Oven Factory, Shanghai, China), and crude ash was determined using a muffle furnace (SGM·M30/10, Luoyang Sigma High Temperature Furnace Co., Ltd., Luoyang, Henan, China) according to standard AOAC procedures (AOAC, 2007). Organic matter (OM) was calculated as DM minus crude ash. The diet was evaluated for crude protein (CP) using an automatic Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (K1100, Hanon Instruments, Jinan, Shangdong, China) according to method 976.05 (AOAC, 2007), and for ether extract (EE) using a Soxhlet extractor (SN-00051B, Chengdu Shuniu Technology Co., Ltd., Chengdu, Sichuan, China) according to method 922.06 (AOAC, 2007). An automated oxygen bomb calorimeter (Model 6400, Parr Instrument Company, Moline, IL, USA) was used to evaluate the gross energy (GE) of the diet according to the standard ISO 9831:1998 procedure (ISO, 1998). The diet was analyzed for calcium and total phosphorus contents using an atomic absorption spectrometer (contrAA 700, Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany) (method 985.01, AOAC, 2007). The calculated nutrient levels of the diet were obtained from the Feed Database in China Feed Database (2020).

Sample collection

On d 43, one representative piglet from each replicate, whose body weight was closest to the average weight of its respective pen, was selected for biological sampling. Blood collection from the jugular vein utilized 10-mL sterile vacuum tubes for subsequent hematological assessment. After 30 min of coagulation at ambient temperature, samples were centrifuged at 3500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain serum, which was immediately frozen at −20 °C until biochemical analysis. Following humane euthanasia, intestinal tissues were promptly harvested with duodenal, jejunal, and ileal sections thoroughly cleansed using chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Central 4-cm segments from each intestinal region were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological examination, while mucosal layers from the remaining tissues were preserved at −80 °C. Intestinal luminal contents from cecal and colonic regions were portioned into sterile cryovials and snap-frozen at −80 °C for microbial community analysis.

Growth performance and diarrhea incidence

Daily feed consumption was monitored for each replicate group. Body weight measurements were conducted after a 12-h fasting protocol on specified days (d 1, 21, and 43) to evaluate key performance indicators, including average daily feed intake (ADFI), daily weight gain (ADG), and feed:gain ratio (F:G).

Throughout the study period, diarrheal occurrences within each pen were documented twice daily. Fecal consistency was calculated using a four-tier scoring system: 0, normal solid pellets; 1, pliable semi-formed stools; 2, unformed semi-liquid discharge; and 3, watery diarrhea. Blood parameters analysis

Serum biochemical indices were measured using an automated biochemistry analyzer (Model 7150, Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) to assess multiple parameters including total protein (TP), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CRE), albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bilirubin (TBILI), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and glucose (GLU). Pro-inflammatory cytokines (interleukin [IL]-1β [MM-0422O1], tumor necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α; MM-0383O1], and IL-6 [MM-0418O1]) and immunoglobulins (IgA [MM-090502], IgG [MM-040302], and IgM [MM-040202]) were analyzed through ELISA kits procured from Jiangsu Enzyme-Linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Antioxidant capacity was evaluated using commercially available detection kits (A015-1-2 for total antioxidative capacity [T-AOC]; A005-1-2 for glutathione peroxidase [GSH-Px]; A007-1-1 for catalase [CAT]; A001-1-1 for superoxide dismutase [SOD]; A003-1-2 for malondialdehyde [MDA]) produced by Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). The absorbance of all samples was measured using a UV–Vis spectroscopic instrument (Model SFZ 1606017568, Shanghai Cold Light Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). All experimental procedures were performed in strict compliance with the manufacturers' guidelines.

Intestinal morphology analysis

For intestinal morphology evaluation, duodenal, jejunal, and ileal tissue samples were collected and promptly fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde. Tissue samples were subjected to systematic processing steps encompassing tissue trimming, progressive dehydration using an ascending ethanol gradient, and subsequent embedding in paraffin blocks. Histological sections of 5-μm thickness were precisely cut with a rotary microtome (RM2235, Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany), followed by xylene-mediated paraffin removal and gradual rehydration through a descending series of alcohol concentrations. After the standard hematoxylin-eosin staining protocol implementation, the prepared slides underwent comprehensive microscopic evaluation. Quantitative analysis focused on ten distinct microscopic fields that exhibited properly oriented villus-crypt architectures. Morphometric analysis of villus height and crypt depth was conducted employing ImageJ analytical software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), with subsequent computation of villus height/crypt depth (V/C) ratios according to previously validated methodology (Wan et al., 2018).

Immunohistochemistry analysis of the secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA)-positive cells

Intestinal tissue samples were fixed by immersion in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4-μm slices using a microtome. For optimal antigen presentation, microwave-assisted epitope retrieval was performed in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Following three PBS washes, endogenous peroxidase was neutralized with 3% H2O2 solution. Tissue sections received a 30-min blocking with 10% normal goat serum to prevent nonspecific binding. Primary anti-sIgA antibodies diluted in bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution were applied for overnight incubation at 4 °C, followed by 1-h room temperature incubation with biotin-conjugated secondary antibodies. Chromogenic development was performed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate with hematoxylin counterstaining for nuclear visualization. Finalized sections underwent sequential ethanol dehydration, xylene clearing, and permanent mounting with neutral balsam. Imaging was performed at 200 × magnification using a digital microscopy system (Model BA210, Motic Group Co., Ltd., Xiamen, Fujian, China) to evaluate sIgA immunoreactivity levels. Quantitative measurements of the captured images were then performed using densitometric evaluation tools from ImageJ analytical software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunofluorescence analysis of the tight junction proteins

Intestinal tissue samples underwent post-fixation in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution over 24 h, then cryoprotected through immersion in 30% sucrose solution at 4 °C. The preserved specimens were cryosectioned at 5-μm thickness using a microtome maintained at −20 °C. After antigen retrieval, tissue slices were treated with 0.5% Triton X-100 for membrane permeabilization and blocked with 10% normal goat serum. Immunofluorescence staining involved overnight incubation at 4 °C with anti-ZO-1 primary antibodies (dilution ratio of 1:100) in a humidity-controlled environment. Following PBS washes, sections were treated with fluorophore-labeled secondary antibodies for 60 min at room temperature (25 °C) under light-protected conditions. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) solution before mounting with anti-fade medium. Fluorescent signals were documented utilizing an Olympus FV1000 confocal laser microscope (FV1000, Olympus Corporation, Shinjuku, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with the appropriate filter sets.

Determination of the enzyme activities of the intestinal mucosa

Intestinal mucosal samples were thawed and subjected to tissue disruption using sterile physiological saline at a 9:1 volume-to-mass ratio. Following homogenization, centrifugation was performed under refrigerated conditions (4 °C, 3500 × g, 15 min), with the resultant supernatant cryopreserved at −80 °C for subsequent analytical procedures. Disaccharidase activities (lactase [ml712060], sucrase [ml712026], and maltase [ml712030]) were assayed using ELISA kits (Jiangsu Enzyme-Linked Biotechnology Co., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Oxidative stress biomarkers including SOD (A001-1-1), MDA (A003-1-2), GSH-Px (A005-1-2), CAT (A007-1-1), T-AOC (A015-1-2), and ALP (A059-1-1) concentrations were measured with standardized assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Optical density measurements were conducted employing a UV–Vis spectroscopic instrument (SFZ 1606017568, Shanghai Cold Light Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Analysis of the intestinal microbiota

Following thawing procedures, approximately 0.2 g cecal and colonic digesta specimens were precisely weighed for genomic DNA extraction using the EZNA Stool DNA Kit (D4015-02, Omega Bio-tek, Inc., Doraville, GA, USA) according to manufacturer specifications. Quantitative DNA analysis was performed using the QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) through real-time PCR methodology, employing a 10 μL reaction system containing 5 μL SYBR Green PCR master mix, 3 μL molecular-grade water, 1 μL DNA sample, and 0.5 μL each of the forward/reverse primers. Thermal cycling parameters followed established protocols (Wu et al., 2018), with threshold cycle (Ct) measurements derived through automated system calculations. Absolute quantification was achieved using a standard curve prepared from 10-fold serial dilutions spanning 1 × 101 to 1 × 109 copies/μL. Detailed sequences of the oligonucleotide primers are provided in Table S2.

Determination of the short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)

The SCFA quantification was conducted via gas chromatography (CP-3800, Varian, Inc., Walnut Creek, CA, USA) following established methodology (Berni Canani et al., 2016). Defrosted cecal and colonic chyme specimens (500 mg) were processed by vortex-assisted homogenization in 1.2 mL ultrapure water, then centrifuged at 8000 × g for 10 min. From the resultant supernatant, 1 mL portions received 23.3 μL crotonic acid (210 mmol/L, internal standard) and 0.2 mL pyrophosphoric acid (25%), followed by 30-min incubation at 4 °C. After the second centrifugation, 0.3 mL acidified supernatant was combined with 0.9 mL high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-grade methanol, centrifuged again, and filtered through 0.22-μm membranes. The final processed extracts were injected into the GC system for analytical measurement of SCFAs.

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis

Intestinal mucosal samples (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum) weighing approximately 0.1 g were processed for RNA isolation by homogenization in 1 mL TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) according to standardized protocols. RNA quality control involved spectrophotometric measurement, with specimens demonstrating optimal purity (A260/280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0) proceeding to downstream applications. Reverse transcription was performed with PrimeScript RT reagent kit (TaKaRa Bio Inc., Dalian, Liaoning, China) through sequential incubations at 37 °C (15 min) and 85 °C (5 s). Quantitative PCR analysis was conducted using the CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) with SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus, RR820A, TaKaRa Bio Inc., Dalian, Liaoning, China). Reaction mixtures (10 μL total volume) comprised 4 μL cDNA template, 0.4 μL forward and reverse primers each, 5 μL SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus), and 0.2 μL nuclease-free water. Thermocycling conditions included an initial denaturation phase (95 °C for 30 s) preceding 40 cycles of amplification (95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 30 s per cycle). Primer sequences appear in Table S2, with β-actin serving as the reference genes for normalization, and quantitative gene expression analysis was conducted through the 2–ΔΔCt calculation approach (Fleige et al., 2006).

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluations were performed in SPSS 27.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) utilizing a one-way ANOVA methodology. Variance homogeneity was examined using Levene's test, with group comparisons analyzed via ANOVA. Duncan's multiple range test facilitated post-hoc analyses. Meanwhile, with dietary ERG supplementation, the activity of CAT in serum increased quadratically (P = 0.031), and the activity of GSH-Px increased linearly (P = 0.025). However, dietary ERG supplementation had no significant influence on the serum concentrations of IgA, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (P > 0.05).

Effects of ERG on intestinal morphology and mucosal enzyme activities

As shown in Table 5 and Fig. 1, ERG supplementation quadratically increased (P < 0.01) the V/C of the small intestine. Meanwhile, with dietary ERG supplementation, the height of jejunal villi increased quadratically (P = 0.012), while the depths of duodenal and ileal crypts decreased quadratically (P < 0.05). Supplementation of ERG in the diet linearly increased (P = 0.044) the activity of sucrase in the ileum. Discussion

Ergosterol, a sterol predominantly found in fungal membranes, functions as a structural component and has been reported to exert diverse physiological effects, such as antioxidative, antimicrobial, lipid-modulating, and anticancer activities (Liang et al., 2020). In the present trial, dietary addition of ERG was associated with a trend toward improved feed efficiency, as indicated by a lower F:G and a marked reduction in diarrhea cases among weaned piglets. These improvements may be linked to ERG's role in limiting the overgrowth of detrimental microbes and strengthening immune responses, thereby enhancing intestinal resilience and decreasing susceptibility to post-weaning enteric disorders (Hu et al., 2017).

During the weaning phase, piglets undergo substantial dietary, environmental, and immune-related changes, which collectively increase oxidative stress in the gut (Kim et al., 2012). To counteract this imbalance, antioxidant enzymes such as T-SOD and CAT participate in detoxifying reactive oxygen species (ROS), converting them into harmless metabolites (Inanan et al., 2018; Rakotoarisoa et al., 2019). Previous in vitro evidence demonstrated that ERG from Monascus purpureus exhibits potent antioxidant activity, reducing intracellular ROS accumulation, reversing H2O2-induced oxidative damage, and enhancing cell viability (Zheng et al., 2020). In the present trial, dietary ERG supplementation led to marked improvements in antioxidant enzyme activities. Specifically, serum levels of CAT and GSH-Px were elevated, CAT activity increased in both the duodenum and jejunum, and higher T-SOD and T-AOC were observed in the ileum and jejunum, respectively. Moreover, ERG administration lowered the content of MDA—a recognized indicator of lipid peroxidation and oxidative injury (Wang et al., 2012)—in serum and ileal samples. These outcomes are consistent with previous reports (Sun et al., 2019) and collectively suggest that ERG strengthens the antioxidant defense capacity in weaned piglets. Enhanced antioxidant capacity may further contribute to the observed improvements in intestinal morphology and immune parameters, as reduced oxidative stress is known to maintain epithelial integrity and modulate inflammatory signaling. This suggests that the improvements in antioxidant balance caused by ERG may underlie the subsequent positive effects on intestinal structure, immunity, and growth performance.

Villus length and the V/C ratio are widely used indicators of intestinal absorptive efficiency, where greater values generally reflect enhanced nutrient uptake (Cairo et al., 2018; Tucci et al., 2014). In the present trial, ERG supplementation led to shallower crypts in the duodenum and ileum, together with longer villi in the jejunum and a higher V/C ratio, suggesting improved absorptive function in weaned piglets. In line with these morphological improvements, ERG also increased the activities of ileal disaccharidases, such as lactase and maltase, which are essential for carbohydrate hydrolysis (Guo et al., 2014). Additionally, ERG supplementation enhanced AKP activity in the ileum. AKP acts as an endogenous detoxification factor, and plays a vital role in removing endotoxins such as LPS secreted by pathogenic bacteria in the intestine, thereby protecting intestinal health (Geddes and Philpott, 2008). The enhancements in intestinal morphology and enzyme activities appear to be driven by the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of ERG, suggesting that these biochemical improvements translate directly into functional benefits for gut health.

It is well-known that mucosal immunity relies heavily on sIgA, antimicrobial peptides, and mucin secretion which together form a front-line defense at the epithelial interface. However, the immunological immaturity of the gut following weaning exacerbates its vulnerability to microbial invasion and inflammatory damage (Obianwuna et al., 2023; Seikrit and Pabst, 2021). In the present study, ERG supplementation significantly increased jejunal sIgA levels, suggesting enhanced mucosal immune exclusion capacity. This finding gains particular relevance as elevated postnatal sIgA is associated with improved intestinal homeostasis and enteric disease resistance (Seikrit and Pabst, 2021), indicating that ERG may prime the mucosal immune system during a critical developmental plasticity window. In parallel with enhanced immune secretion, ERG also increased the expression levels of ZO-1, a key tight junction protein. Tight junction integrity is critical for preserving the intestinal barrier's function as a selective filter, and disruption of ZO-1 is known to facilitate translocation of luminal antigens and bacteria, leading to mucosal inflammation and systemic immune activation (Li et al., 2021). Thus, the simultaneous increase in sIgA and ZO-1 expression in weaned piglets suggests a coordinated reinforcement of both secretory and structural components of the mucosal barrier. Overall, these findings suggest that ERG reinforces the intestinal barrier through two complementary mechanisms: enhancing mucosal immunity and strengthening epithelial tight junctions, both of which are likely mediated by its antioxidant effects via the NF-κB/Nrf2 signaling pathway.

Intestinal epithelial homeostasis depends on balanced cellular turnover and inflammatory control. Disruption during developmental transitions, particularly post-weaning stress, triggers apoptosis and excessive inflammation, compromising mucosal integrity (Julien and Wells, 2017; Molnár et al., 2021). The present study demonstrated that ERG supplementation reduced intestinal apoptosis, indicated by decreased expression of Caspase-3 and Caspase-9, which are key apoptosis mediators in both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways (Khalil et al., 2012). Concurrently, ERG exerted broad anti-inflammatory effects, reducing the expression of IL-6, TNF-α, and NF-κB across multiple intestinal segments. These cytokines and transcription factors are well-established drivers of mucosal inflammation (Qin and Qiu, 2018), with NF-κB serving as a central node that integrates oxidative stress and immune signaling (Rogero and Calder, 2018). Importantly, these effects were accompanied by an upregulation of redox-sensitive transcription factor Nrf2 that represents enhanced antioxidant defenses. Interestingly, Nrf2 is reported to negatively regulate NF-κB activity, suggesting crosstalk between redox and inflammatory pathways (Fernández-Mendívil et al., 2021; Zuo et al., 2022). The coordinated activation of Nrf2 and suppression of NF-κB implies that ERG may recalibrate epithelial stress responses via redox-dependent transcriptional reprogramming. This observation indicates that ERG mitigates epithelial injury through a unified mechanism involving redox stabilization, immune modulation, and apoptosis inhibition. This integrated mechanism helps explain how ERG simultaneously improves oxidative status, reduces inflammation, and enhances intestinal morphology, highlighting the interconnected nature of these physiological responses.

The gut microbiota plays a foundational role in shaping intestinal development, immune, and metabolic signaling, particularly during early life (Hills et al., 2019). Disruptions in microbial composition due to post-weaning stress are closely associated with impaired barrier function, heightened inflammation, and reduced production of beneficial microbial metabolites (Ghosh et al., 2022; Lallès et al., 2007). In the present study, ERG supplementation significantly enriched the relative abundance of beneficial genera such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium in the cecum, along with an increase in total bacterial load. Although the mechanisms by which ERG regulates gut microbiota remain unclear, previous studies have suggested that phytosterols may modulate microbial ecosystems through metabolites (Feng et al., 2020; Obianwuna et al., 2024). In this study, it was found that these compositional shifts were accompanied by elevated concentrations of major SCFAs, which are recognized for their ability to energize epithelial cells, facilitate regulatory T cell differentiation, and strengthen tight junction integrity (Lv et al., 2023; Miszczuk et al., 2024). In addition, SCFAs are known to stimulate sIgA production and promote the survival of epithelial cells (Tan et al., 2023), suggesting that the microbiota-modulating effects of ERG may further reinforce the mucosal immune and structural improvements described above.

Conclusion

In summary, these results indicate that ERG enhances growth performance and intestinal health through an integrated mechanism involving antioxidant protection, immune and microbial modulation, and maintenance of epithelial integrity. Moreover, the superior responses observed at 200 to 400 mg/kg suggest this range as an optimal supplementation level, whereas higher doses may surpass physiological tolerance, resulting in feedback inhibition or metabolic burden.
