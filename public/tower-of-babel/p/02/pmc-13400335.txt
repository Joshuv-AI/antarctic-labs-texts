Analysis of nutritional components and flavor compounds in naturally fermented, fermentation agent-fermented, and unfermented multi-grain pancakes
Authors: Xiaoyue Ma, Xijie Zhang, Yanhua Du, Haitao Zhu, Bingwen Zhang, Chi Wang, Shanlong Mao, Shanshan Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional northern Chinese pancake fermentation faces challenges such as inconsistency, prolonged processing duration, and safety hazards. A controlled co-fermentation system (YL-7) was applied to multigrain pancakes for the first time, comparing with unfermented (UNF) and naturally fermented (NF) samples using e-nose, e-tongue, GC-IMS, nutritional analysis, and sensory evaluation. The YL – 7 system notably elevated the content of the buttery aroma compound 3 - hydroxy - 2 - butanone and esters, diminished the levels of grassy aldehydes, and decreased the total quantity of free amino acids. The sensory scores for aroma, taste, and overall acceptability were the highest among the samples fermented by the YL - 7 system. Co - fermentation reduced the processing time, improved flavor complexity and nutrient utilization, and offered a scientific foundation for quality enhancement.Keywords: Pancake, Fermentation, Volatile flavor compounds, Amino acids, GC-IMS

Introduction

Pancakes, a traditional staple food in northern China, are prepared through griddle - cooking fermented or unfermented batters crafted from coarse cereals such as millet, buckwheat, and sorghum. Due to their whole - grain composition, these products have high nutritional value and hold significant cultural heritage value (Fusco et al., 2023). Despite its significant role in the diet, the standardization of the quality of multi - grain pancakes still encounters persistent challenges stemming from processing methods. Current production techniques can be categorized into unfermented and fermented methods. The latter can be further subdivided into natural fermentation and controlled fermentation with the utilization of defined starter cultures. Although fermentation is generally acknowledged to enhance texture, enrich nutritional attributes, and develop characteristic flavors when compared with unfermented products, natural fermentation is afflicted by significant drawbacks: extended processing time, inconsistent quality across batches, vulnerability to environmental contamination, and potential safety risks stemming from uncontrolled microbial growth (Huiya et al., 2022). These limitations not only undermine product quality and consumer safety but also hinder the consistent production of this nutritionally optimized traditional food.

Microbial interactions are central to ecological fermentation, propelling the succession of microbial communities, the synthesis of metabolites, and the formation of flavors. Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and yeasts are the predominant functional microorganisms involved in cereal fermentation. LAB produce organic acids through either homofermentative or heterofermentative pathways and generate bioactive peptides and free amino acids via proteolysis. Moreover, certain strains also exhibit lipolytic capabilities, which contribute to a complex metabolite profile (Ke et al., 2023). Yeasts generate aromatic compounds, such as ethanol, higher alcohols, esters, and organic acids, via sugar metabolism. The synergistic co - culture of LAB and yeasts has been proven to enhance the nutritional value, prolong the shelf life, and improve the sensory attributes of diverse food matrices (Du et al., 2025; Duan et al., 2020). Recent studies further demonstrate that fermentation is not simply a preservation method but a precise bioprocessing instrument capable of fundamentally transforming food functionality. Solid - state fermentation using Antrodia cinnamomea mycelia has been proven to modify the structure and immunomodulatory characteristics of cereal polysaccharides, thus augmenting their bioactive potential (Kaewkumsan et al., 2025). Probiotic-mediated fermentation is capable of producing bioactive metabolites that possess well-defined antimicrobial and health-promoting effects, presenting a transformative approach to address antimicrobial resistance (Magalhães, 2025). Moreover, the selection of specific fermenting strains exerts a decisive influence on peptide profiles and functional outcomes in fermentation systems, which directly impacts the nutritional quality and bioactivity (Gabrilyants et al., 2025). Collectively, these findings indicate that controlled fermentation can be deliberately designed to regulate not only nutritional and sensory characteristics but also functional and health - related properties. Nevertheless, in traditional multi – grain pancake systems, where natural fermentation leads to uncontrolled microbial variability and the application of co - cultures remains largely unexamined, whether and how these mechanistic understandings can be applied has not been systematically studied. The imperative of bridging this knowledge gap is three - fold: from the perspective of food safety, unregulated natural fermentation poses unforeseeable risks; from the aspect of quality consistency, batch - to - batch variation obstructs reproducible preparation; and from the angle of nutritional optimization, the potential of well - defined co - cultures to enhance flavor and bioactive compound profiles remains untapped. This knowledge gap impedes the evidence - based optimization of pancake fermentation and restricts our comprehension of how specific microbial consortia can concurrently enhance flavor, nutrition, and safety in this culturally significant staple food.

To address this gap, a comprehensive characterization of fermentation-induced changes necessitates integrated analytical approaches that are capable of simultaneously capturing nutritional transformation and flavor modulation. Taste perception results from complex synergistic and inhibitory interactions among taste-active compounds, which makes subjective evaluation inadequate. The electronic nose (E-nose), the electronic tongue (E-tongue), and gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) offer complementary and objective tools for deciphering volatile and non-volatile flavor profiles: the E-nose detects volatile organic compounds (VOCs) through metal oxide sensors, the E-tongue mimics human taste responses by using cross-sensitive taste sensors (Lu et al., 2022), and GC-IMS combines high-resolution separation with high-sensitivity detection for trace volatile analysis (Capitain & Weller, 2021). Despite the availability of these techniques, extant studies on pancake fermentation have mainly concentrated on single - strain or natural fermentation systems. Moreover, no study has systematically compared well – defined co - fermentation systems with natural fermentation and unfermented controls. Significantly, the specific influence of LAB - yeast co - fermentation on the formation of volatile flavor compounds in pancakes, as well as the inherent relationship between amino acid metabolism and flavor regulation in multi - grain pancake matrices, remains completely uncharacterized.

Therefore, this study, for the first time, utilized a well – defined LAB - yeast co - culture system (YL - 7) in the production of multi - grain pancakes under controlled laboratory conditions. By employing integrated E - nose, E - tongue, and GC - IMS analyses, we comprehensively compared the nutritional composition and flavor profiles of pancakes that underwent co - fermentation (YL - 7), natural fermentation (NF), and unfermented treatment (UNF). The specific objectives were as follows: (i) to compare the nutritional components of the three types of pancakes, including protein, fat, reducing sugars, and amino acids; (ii) to characterize and distinguish the volatile flavor compound profiles using E - nose, E - tongue, and GC - IMS in combination with multivariate statistical analysis; and (iii) to establish a correlation between the instrumental data and sensory evaluation. Overall, through a systematic elucidation of the nutritional transformation and flavor regulation under the three processing methods, this study aims to bridge the research gap concerning the application of co - culture systems in traditional staple foods and to offer a scientific foundation for the rational design of pancake fermentation processes.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Rice, soybeans and corn grits were purchased from Shandong Yuezhong Food Company (Shandong, China). The seven lactic acid bacterial strains (Lactobacillus plantarum CICC 25125, Lactobacillus casei CICC 6112, Lactobacillus acidophilus CICC 20250, Lactobacillus rhamnosus CICC 6137, Lactobacillus bulgaricus CICC 20253, Bifidobacterium lactis CICC 21709, and Streptococcus thermophilus CICC 20378), along with Saccharomyces cerevisiae CICC 32883, which were utilized in the YL-7 starter culture, were sourced from the laboratory's culture collection. The standard amino acid reference substances were obtained from Tanmo Quality Inspection Technology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). N-alkanones (2-butanone, 2-pentanone, 2-hexanone, 2-heptanone, 2-octanone, and 2-nonanone) from Aladdin (Shanghai, China), used for GC-IMS analysis. All chemical reagents employed in the experimental procedures were of analytical grade.

Pancake preparation

The sample preparation process is depicted in Fig. 1. After being ground into a batter, the samples underwent various fermentation treatments. To elucidate the experimental rationale, three primary treatment groups were established:

The Preparation Process of Pancake Samples.

(1) UNF (unfermented control): The batter was processed promptly without undergoing any fermentation. This group was utilized to establish the baseline nutritional composition and flavor profile of the raw ingredients in the absence of microbial interference.

(2) NF (natural fermentation): The batter was fermented at room temperature (approximately 25 °C) for 10 h without the addition of any starter culture. This group represented the traditional artisanal process commonly employed in northern China.

(3) YL-7 (defined co-culture fermentation): The batter was inoculated with a 1:1 (w/w) mixture of Saccharomyces cerevisiae and seven lactic acid bacterial strains, which were collectively designated as YL-7. The inoculated batter was fermented under controlled conditions at a temperature of 44 °C and a relative humidity of 70% for a duration of 4 h. The specific parameters (44 °C, 4 h) were experimentally determined to be the optimum growth conditions for the YL-7 co - culture, enabling it to reach the target acidity within a reduced time frame. Notably, a temperature of 44 °C also acts as a selective pressure that inhibits mesophilic contaminating microorganisms, ensuring the dominance of the YL-7 consortium and minimizing batch – to - batch variability.

An intermediate time point, denoted as t/2 (i.e., half of the specified fermentation period: 5 h for NF and 2 h for YL - 7), was sampled exclusively for the determination of protein and amino acid content to monitor the progress of proteolysis during fermentation. This intermediate sampling was carried out solely to validate the fermentation kinetics and was excluded from the main comparative analyses of volatile compounds, nutritional composition, and sensory evaluation presented in this study.

Upon the completion of fermentation, all batter samples (including UNF) were adjusted to a pH of 6.1 with sodium bicarbonate prior to spreading and griddle - cooking. This pH adjustment procedure was uniformly applied to all groups to eliminate the confounding influence of pH on the subsequent flavor analyses and sensory evaluation, ensuring that any observed disparities could be ascribed to fermentation biochemistry rather than to variations in acidity.

Identification and quantification of flavor compounds

Electronic nose analysis

The measurement was performed using a PEN3 portable E-nose (Airsense Analytics GmbH, Schwerin, Germany), which is equipped with 10 metal oxide semiconductor sensors (TGS series). The sensor array consists of 10 MOS sensors, each sensitive to specific classes of volatile compounds (Table S1). The procedure followed the method described by Song et al. (2025), with several modifications. Specifically, 3 g of the sample was placed into a 100 mL beaker, the opening of which was tightly sealed with two layers of plastic wrap. The sample was then allowed to equilibrate for 2 h. The instrumental parameters were set as follows: sampling time was 1 s per group; sensor self-cleaning time was 80 s; sensor zeroing time was 5 s; sample preparation time was 5 s; injection flow rate was 400 mL/min; and analysis sampling time was 80 s.

Electronic tongue analysis

The TS-5000Z taste analysis system (INSENT Inc., Kanagawa, Japan) was used for the measurement. The system employs an artificial lipid membrane sensor array that detects taste substances via electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions with the membrane, causing a change in membrane potential that is quantitatively measured. The test conditions were adopted according to the method described by Wu et al. (2025), with appropriate modifications. 15 g of pancake samples were taken, 150 mL of pure water was added, and the mixture was stirred in a blender for 30 s. Then, it was poured into a centrifuge tube, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min, and the filtrate was obtained by filtering through filter paper for the test. Each group was tested four times, and the initial experimental data was discarded. The average value of the remaining three data sets was calculated.

Test Solution: Reference solution (reference saliva): 30 mM potassium chloride with 0.3 mM tartaric acid; Negative electrode cleaning solution: 100 mM HCL with 30% (v/v) ethanol; Positive electrode cleaning solution: 10 mM potassium hydroxide with 100 mM potassium chloride and 30% (v/v) ethanol.

GC-IMS analysis

A GC-IMS instrument (FlavourSpec®, G.A.S. Gesellschaft für analytische Sensorsysteme mbH, Dortmund, Germany) which was equipped with a CTC-PAL 3 static headspace autosampler (CTC Analytics AG, Zwingen, Switzerland) was used to determine the samples. The FlavourSpec® operates with a detection limit for volatile organic compounds in the low ppb (μg/L) range and enables isothermal operation in the range of 40–80 °C. The analytical conditions were adapted from the method described by Song et al. (2025), with minor modifications. A 3.0 g sample was placed into a 20 mL headspace vial and incubated at 60 °C for 15 min with continuous agitation at a speed of 500 rpm. Subsequently, 500 μL of the headspace gas was injected into the GC inlet. The analysis was carried out using a CTC-PAL 3 static headspace autosampler (CTC Analytics AG, Switzerland). Nitrogen was employed as both the carrier gas and purge gas. The carrier gas flow rate was initially maintained at 2.00 mL/min for 2 min, followed by a linear increase to 10.00 mL/min over 8 min, then further increased linearly to 100.00 mL/min within 10 min, and held constant for an additional 20 min. The purge gas flow rate through the drift tube was maintained at 150.00 mL/min.

Analysis of nutritional components

Determination of protein, fat, and carbohydrate content

The protein content was determined using the Kjeldahl method specified in GB5009.5–2016, following the procedure described previously for cereal-based foods (Batool et al., 2022). The fat content was determined using the Soxhlet extraction method outlined in GB5009.6–2016 according to the protocol of Karuppuchamy and Campanella (2026). The reducing sugar content was determined using the direct titration method described in GB5009.7–2016.

Determination of amino acid content

The amino acid composition and content were analyzed using a Hitachi LA8080 automatic amino acid analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) based on ion-exchange chromatography with post-column ninhydrin derivatization. The determination of free amino acid and hydrolyzed amino acids content was conducted based on the method described by Li et al. (2022), with certain modifications. Specifically, 0.1 g of the sample was accurately weighed and mixed with 6 mL of analytical-grade hydrochloric acid diluted to a 1:1 ratio. The mixture was purged with nitrogen gas for 15 min and then sealed. Subsequently, the sealed sample was placed in an oven at 110 °C for hydrolysis over a period of 24 h. After hydrolysis, the sample was removed, cooled, and unsealed. The volume was adjusted to 100 mL, and 2 mL of the solution was transferred to a rotary evaporator at 60 °C to remove the acid until dryness. The residue was reconstituted with 2.0 mL of 0.02 mol/L HCl, vortex-mixed, and filtered through a 0.22 μm aqueous filter prior to instrumental analysis, which was performed to determine the content of hydrolyzed amino acids.

For the determination of free amino acids, 2.0 g of the sample was weighed and dissolved in 0.02 mol/L hydrochloric acid, and the volume was adjusted to 250 mL. The solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm aqueous filter and analyzed using the same instrument.

Instrumental parameters: separation column: 4.6 mm ID × 60 mm; resin: Hitachi dedicated cation exchange resin; detection limit: 2.5 pmol; detection wavelengths: 570 nm and 440 nm; column temperature: programmed from 55 °C to 65 °C and then to 77 °C; reaction temperature: 135 °C; injection volume: 20 μL.

Sensory analysis

Sensory evaluation procedure

This study established a descriptive sensory analysis panel in accordance with the national standard “Sensory Analysis—Guidelines for the Control of Sensory Quality of Food” (GB/T 29605–2013) and the international standard ISO 8586:2012. Panelist selection and training followed a rigorous, three-phase protocol, adapted from previously established procedures for cereal-based products (Callejo et al., 2015): (i) Initial screening of 20 graduate students majoring in food engineering was conducted based on fundamental taste identification (sweet, sour, bitter, salty, umami) and olfactory threshold assessment; (ii) A 14-day intensive training program was implemented, encompassing standardized attribute definitions (shape, texture, color, aroma, taste, and overall acceptability), scale calibration using reference standards to anchor points at 0, 5, and 10, and repeatability evaluation via two blind duplicate samples per session; (iii) Final qualification required an intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) ≥ 0.75 for duplicate samples, resulting in a certified panel of 12 assessors (6 male and 6 female, aged 20–30 years). During evaluation, samples were randomly assigned three-digit codes; presentation order was balanced using a Latin square design; assessments were conducted in individual, ISO 8572:2012–compliant sensory booths; assessors rinsed their mouths with distilled water and observed a 2-min rest interval between samples; and consumption of flavor-active foods was prohibited prior to and during testing.

Ethical statement for sensory analysis

Sensory evaluation at the University of Jinan does not require ethical approval from the Ethics Committee. All panelists participated voluntarily and provided their written informed consent before the study commenced. In addition, appropriate protocols were implemented to protect the rights, safety, and privacy of all participants throughout the research process. Personal information of the panelists was kept strictly confidential, and all data were anonymized prior to analysis to ensure privacy protection. Participants were informed of the study objectives, procedures, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was conducted based on three biological replicates, and each biological replicate was technically repeated three times. The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism 10 was utilized for graphical representation, while IBM SPSS Statistics 27 was employed for statistical analysis. Partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS—DA) was conducted using SIMCA-P software (version 14.1) to evaluate group separation, and variable importance in the projection (VIP) scores were calculated for biomarker identification. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA and Tukey's HSD test at P < 0.05. Cluster analysis was carried out using the software provided with the equipment system.

Results and discussion

Analysis of electronic nose data

Principal component analysis (PCA) of the volatile profiles obtained via E-nose detection revealed distinct clustering among the three pancake samples (Fig. 2A). The first two principal components, PC1 and PC2, accounted for 97.53% and 2.07% of the total variance, respectively, cumulatively explaining 99.6% of the overall variability. As shown in the loading plot (Fig. 2B), sensors W1S (responsive to short-chain alkanes), W1W (responsive to inorganic sulfides), W5S (responsive to nitrogen oxides), W2W (responsive to aromatic compounds and organic sulfides), and W2S (responsive to alcohols, ethers, aldehydes, and ketones) exhibited substantial loadings on these components, indicating their dominant contribution to the discrimination of volatile composition across samples (Guan et al., 2025). Notably, the high contribution of the W1W sensor reflects changes in sulfur-containing compounds during fermentation (e.g., sulfides derived from the degradation of sulfur-containing amino acids), which are key products of the Maillard reaction and Strecker degradation and contribute substantially to the baked aroma of pancakes.

(A) Principal component analysis (PCA) diagram of the electronic nose (E-nose) system. (B) Sensor contribution rate loading diagram of the E-nose system. (C) Cross-validation plot of the E-nose system. (D) Permutation test plot of the E-nose system.

A partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS—DA) model was developed using sample type (UNF, NF, YL-7) as the categorical response variable. Cross-validation results (Fig. 2C) demonstrated robust predictive performance, with Q2 = 0.974, R2Y = 0.996, and an R2Y − Q2 difference of 0.022, well below the commonly accepted threshold of 0.3, indicating minimal risk of overfitting (Yun et al., 2021). Permutation testing (Fig. 2D) confirmed the statistical robustness of the PLS—DA model: the Q2 value of the original model significantly exceeded all permuted Q2 values (p < 0.001), indicating strong predictive ability and model validity. The R2 intercept was positive (0.398), whereas the Q2 intercept was negative (−0.187); critically, the Q2 regression line intersected the vertical axis below zero, consistent with established criteria for absence of overfitting. These validation metrics collectively support the model's reliability for discriminating among pancake aroma profiles.

Analysis of electronic tongue data

E-tongue analysis successfully discriminated among the three pancake samples (NF, UNF, YL-7). PCA (Fig. 3A) revealed distinct clustering of the samples along the PC1, which accounted for 99.68% of the total variance, while PC2 contributed only 0.32%. Radar plot analysis (Fig. 3B) further highlighted quantitative differences in taste profiles. Specifically, the highest level of sourness in UNF might stem from the endogenous organic acids that naturally occur in the cereal/soybean flour (e.g., phytic acid and phenolic acids). These acids are partially metabolized and consumed during the fermentation process (Magala et al., 2015). The simultaneous increase in umami, bitterness, and saltiness in YL - 7 might be related to proteolysis and the subsequent amino acid metabolism. This process releases soluble peptides and free amino acids, thus synergistically enhancing these taste perceptions. (Huang et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2016).

(A) Principal component analysis (PCA) diagram of the electronic tongue (E-tongue). (B) Radar chart representing the response profile of the E-tongue. (C) Cross-validation plot of the E-tongue. (D) Permutation test plot of the E-tongue.

The PLS—DA model exhibited exceptional predictive and explanatory performance, as evidenced by cross-validation (Fig. 3C): R2 = 0.999 (indicating near-perfect model fit) and Q2 = 0.999 (indicating excellent predictive ability). To rigorously assess the validity of this performance and rule out overfitting, a permutation test was performed (Fig. 3D), comprising 200 random permutations of class labels. The resulting distribution of permuted Q2 and R2 values (mean Q2 = −0.248; mean R2 = 0.0722) was significantly lower than the original model metrics (p < 0.001), thereby confirming the statistical robustness and non-spurious nature of the model. These findings collectively demonstrate that the E-tongue signals provide a reliable and discriminative basis for distinguishing among the three pancake sample types.

Analysis of GC-IMS results

GC-IMS map analysis of different types of pancake samples

GC-IMS analysis enabled robust discrimination among pancake samples by leveraging the compound-specific retention times and ion mobility signatures of VOCs (Xie et al., 2025). The three-dimensional principal component analysis plot (Fig. 4A) and corresponding topographic contour maps (Fig. 4B and C) demonstrate distinct clustering and separation of VOC profiles across the UNF, NF, and YL-7 samples. In the differential VOC map (Fig. 4C), where UNF serves as the reference condition, red and blue regions denote statistically significant increases and decreases in VOC abundance relative to UNF (Dai et al., 2025). All three pancake samples exhibit pronounced and reproducible deviations from the UNF profile. The fingerprint chromatogram (Fig. 4D) provides a visual representation of the VOCs present in the samples. Color intensity is semiquantitatively indicative of VOC abundance: brighter hues correspond to higher relative concentrations, whereas darker hues indicate lower relative concentrations (Xuedan et al., 2022). The volatile compound profiles revealed distinct compositional patterns among the three pancake types: UNF exhibited relatively higher concentrations of (E)-2-octenal, pentanal, and heptanal; NF was characterized by elevated levels of 2-pentanone, furfural, and benzaldehyde; and YL-7 showed comparatively greater abundances of propanol, 3-hydroxy-2-butanone, and ethyl propionate. Collectively, GC–IMS analysis enabled clear discrimination of the three pancake types based on both qualitative composition and quantitative intensity of volatile compounds.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis of VOCs in samples

The volatile flavor compounds present in the pancake samples were identified based on their retention time, migration time, and retention index. The results of the identified aroma compounds are summarized in Table 1. A total of 45 volatile flavor compounds (57 GC-IMS signal peaks) were identified and classified into eight chemical classes: 16 aldehydes, 10 alcohols, 8 ketones, 7 esters, 2 carboxylic acids, 1 monoterpene and 1 furan. It is worth mentioning that due to the high concentration of volatile components, certain single volatile compounds may produce multiple dimer signals in the ionization region, such as the monomer and dimer signal peaks detected for (E)-2-Heptena (Wang et al., 2026). The dimer peak represents hydrated ion clusters (M·H+(H₂O)ₙ) formed during ion mobility separation and is not treated as an independent chemical entity. These compounds collectively contribute to the distinctive flavor profiles of multi-grain pancakes produced through different processing techniques. Notable differences were observed in the composition and content of flavor compounds among the pancake samples, particularly between the UNF and the two types of fermented pancakes. These variations can be primarily attributed to the combined effects of batter fermentation, the Maillard reaction, and lipid oxidation during the pan-frying process (Pradal et al., 2025). These chemical transformations influence not only the diversity of flavor compounds but also their relative concentrations, ultimately shaping the distinct flavor characteristics of each pancake type.

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) identified by gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS).

Aldehyde compounds, as key contributors to the flavor profile of pancakes, are primarily generated through lipid oxidation and amino acid reactions. Owing to their low odor detection thresholds, aldehydes exert a pronounced and distinctive influence on the overall sensory characteristics of pancakes, enhancing both flavor complexity and depth. During fermentation, the concentration of 2-furaldehyde increases, imparting a sweet and aromatic note that contributes to the mellow aroma of fermented pancakes (Linfen et al., 2023). In contrast, the levels of other aldehyde substances tend to decrease, a reduction that may be attributed to two mechanisms: (i) the conversion of aldehydes to alcohols or acids via microbial aldehyde reductase and dehydrogenase activities (Estelle et al., 2022), and (ii) chemical addition reactions between aldehydes and nucleophilic groups such as amino acids (Liu et al., 2016). Notably, nine aldehyde compounds in sample YL-7 were found at higher concentrations compared to those in NF, with 1-hexanal showing a particularly marked increase. 1-hexanal is associated with a grassy and apple-like fragrance and is naturally present in fruits such as apples and strawberries (Xiaowei et al., 2023).

Ketone compounds constituted 26.1% of the identified components and played a secondary role in flavor contribution, following only alcohols, thereby representing a significant source of flavor in pancakes. Following fermentation, with the exception of a notable reduction in the content of 2-propanone, the levels of other ketone compounds increased. The most pronounced change was observed for 3-hydroxy-2-butanone, whose relative abundance in YL-7 (15.18%) was 38.9-fold and 4.9-fold that in UNF (0.39%) and NF (3.07%), respectively. This compound is primarily associated with a milky aroma and is commonly utilized in the formulation of cream and dairy products (Gou et al., 2023). The substantial accumulation of acetoin in YL-7 indicates that the fermentation conditions of this co - culture system (44 °C, 4 h) might have selectively enhanced this metabolic pathway. The elevated temperature is likely to favor the activity of acetolactate decarboxylase (Yuxing et al., 2015). Meanwhile, cross - feeding between lactic acid bacteria and yeast may further enhance the flux through this pathway (Ponomarova et al., 2017).

The formation of alcohol compounds typically involves the degradation of peroxides in fatty acids and the reduction of carbonyl compounds. These alcohols often exhibit characteristic aromas, such as floral and herbaceous notes, which interact synergistically with other flavor components to enhance the overall flavor complexity of pancakes (Wang et al., 2025). Although alcohol compounds generally have relatively high odor thresholds, they can react with acidic substances to form esters, thereby contributing to a more intricate and appealing flavor profile. Among the three pancake samples, ethanol was found to be the most abundant alcohol compound, imparting a distinct aromatic or wine-like fragrance (Feng et al., 2015). During pancake processing, ethanol is primarily produced through alcoholic fermentation by yeast. Post-fermentation, the concentration of 2-methyl-1-propanol-D was notably increased, contributing a wine-like aroma (Zhang et al., 2025). Significant variations in alcohol content were also observed between the NF and YL-7 samples.

Ester compounds play a crucial role in the aromatic profiles of numerous flowers and fruits, serving as primary contributors to these natural fragrances. However, these compounds are susceptible to various chemical reactions, including hydrolysis, ammonolysis, alcoholysis, and reduction, under specific conditions. These reactions may lead to fluctuations in ester content during different stages of production or storage, significantly influencing the flavor and quality of the final product. Several ester compounds were identified in the samples. Following fermentation, most ester compounds exhibited an increase in concentration, with propyl acetate and ethyl acetate showing the most pronounced enhancement. The small quantity of lactic acid produced during Lactobacillus plantarum fermentation can promote the synthesis of ethyl acetate by yeast (Reyes et al., 2021). Ethyl acetate is characterized by a pleasant, slightly fruity wine-like aroma, while propyl acetate possesses a soft, fruity scent and naturally occurs in strawberries, bananas, and tomatoes (Qian et al., 2021). Together, these compounds contribute to a more desirable flavor profile in the fermented pancakes. Additionally, the NF sample exhibited higher levels of certain esters, such as methyl propionate, butyl acetate, and ethyl acetate. This observation may be attributed to the higher concentration of acidic precursors in NF, which facilitates ester formation.

Aromatic compounds are recognized as significant contributors to the flavor profile of pancake products. Among the three pancake samples analyzed, only 2-pentylfuran was identified. This compound is characterized by a fruity and grassy aroma and exhibits a relatively stable presence, with a slight increase observed after fermentation. This variation may be attributed to the fermentation process itself and the influence of differing fermentation conditions.

Correlation analysis between volatile compound abundances and sensory perception

The PLS-DA model identified the volatile compounds that have the greatest impact on distinguishing UNF, NF, and YL-7 pancake samples, as indicated by the VIP scores (Fig. 5). Compounds with VIP > 1.0 contribute significantly to the separation between groups. However, statistical significance alone does not equate to sensory relevance. Furthermore, pairwise comparisons among the three pancake treatments (UNF vs. NF, UNF vs. YL-7, and NF vs. YL-7) were performed to identify up- or down-regulated compounds using a fold change (FC) > 2 or < 0.5 with p < 0.05. Compounds with FC values between 0.5 and 2 (log2FC > −1 and log2FC < 1) were considered non-significant. This strategy has been applied in GC-IMS flavor research, where differential compounds are screened by VIP > 1 combined with FC > 2 or < 0.5, and their sensory relevance is assessed by referencing literature-reported aroma attributes (Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025).

Variable importance in the projection (VIP) scores of key volatiles differentiating fermentation types in pancakes.

Using UNF as the control, the 12 compounds with VIP > 1 were further filtered by FC analysis (threshold: FC > 2 or < 0.5) (Table 2). Between NF and UNF, 10 compounds passed the FC filter; between YL-7 and UNF, 8 compounds passed the FC filter. Both fermentation methods induced significant changes in most volatile compounds, although the magnitudes of change differed markedly. When NF was used as the control, the direct comparison of YL-7 with NF revealed that among the 12 compounds with VIP > 1, only 3-hydroxy-2-butanone (FC = 4.94) passed the FC filter, indicating that this compound is the key chemical feature distinguishing YL-7 from NF samples.

Compared with UNF, the relative abundance of 3-hydroxy-2-butanone (acetoin) in YL-7 increased by approximately 39-fold (0.39% → 15.18%). Acetoin is widely reported as an important contributor to creamy and buttery aromas in fermented cereal products, and its formation is closely linked to the diacetyl/acetoin metabolic pathways in lactic acid bacteria and yeasts (Muzi et al., 2023). Although acetoin also increased approximately 8-fold in NF (0.39% → 3.07%), the magnitude of accumulation in YL-7 (FC = 38.92) was substantially higher than that in NF (FC = 7.87), demonstrating the unique advantage of the YL-7 co-fermentation system in promoting acetoin accumulation. Concurrently, ethyl acetate and propyl acetate in YL-7 increased approximately 28-fold (0.21% → 6.02%) and 17-fold (0.36% → 6.05%), respectively. These esters are widely recognized as possessing fruity, sweet, and wine-like notes and are important ester contributors to the aroma of bread, wine, and traditional fermented cereals (Kh et al., 2024). Notably, the fold increases of ester compounds in NF (ethyl acetate FC = 42.14; propyl acetate FC = 29.75) were generally higher than those in YL-7 (ethyl acetate FC = 28.67; propyl acetate FC = 16.81), indicating that natural fermentation has certain advantages in ester production. Nevertheless, the YL-7 samples still received higher “aroma” and “overall acceptability” scores in sensory evaluation (Fig. 8A), which may be associated with the unique enrichment of acetoin and a more pronounced reduction of aldehydes.

Hexanal, an aldehyde with an extremely low odor threshold (0.28 μg/L) and characteristic green/fatty notes, decreased by approximately 67% in YL-7 compared with UNF (10.74% → 3.54%). In addition, lipid oxidation-derived aldehydes such as (E)-2-hexenal (FC = 0.22) and (E)-2-heptenal showed similar decreasing trends (Table 1). These aldehydes are associated with green, grassy, and fatty odors in cereal products (Kai et al., 2022). Their marked reduction after fermentation aligns with the attenuation of undesirable off-odors in the aroma profile of YL-7 samples.

Acetic acid accumulated to the highest level in NF (9.12%), whereas in YL-7 it decreased to 5.85% (Table 1). FC analysis further revealed that acetic acid increased 2.64-fold in NF (passing the FC filter) but only 1.70-fold in YL-7 (not passing the FC filter). This trend is consistent with the sourness response detected by the E-tongue (Fig. 3B), indicating that different fermentation processes differentially affect organic acid production (Haram et al., 2022). The YL-7 controlled fermentation effectively prevented excessive sourness while maintaining appropriate levels of organic acids.

Collectively, the aforementioned results illustrate that the significant accumulation of 3 – hydroxy – 2 - butanone in YL - 7 (FC = 38.92, approximately five - fold that of NF), in conjunction with the accumulation of esters and a considerable reduction of aldehydes, forms the crucial chemical characteristics that distinguish YL - 7 from UNF and NF samples. These alterations are in line with the aroma attributes of the corresponding compounds reported in the literature and with the outcomes of sensory evaluation. It is important to note that the GC - IMS data employed in this study are semi - quantitative relative abundances, from which odor activity values (OAVs) cannot be computed. Moreover, the direct applicability of literature threshold values in the complex pancake matrix necessitates further verification. Therefore, the associations between differential compounds and flavor attributes discussed herein represent reasonable inferences based on the available data, and their actual sensory contributions require further confirmation through GC - O and aroma recombination experiments.

Multivariate statistical analysis

PCA revealed distinct clustering patterns among the samples, with the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explaining 56% and 35% of the total variance, respectively, yielding a cumulative explained variance of 91% (Fig. 6A). This indicates that PCA effectively captured the major sources of variation in the volatile flavor profiles. Subsequently, a PLS-DA model was developed using the GC-IMS peak areas of individual volatile compounds as predictor variables (X) and sample origin (i.e., fermentation method) as the categorical response variable (Y). Model performance was rigorously evaluated: cross-validation (Fig. 6B) demonstrated excellent predictive capability (Q2(cum) = 0.996; R2Y(cum) = 0.998; R2Y − Q2 = 0.002 < 0.3), and 200 permutation tests (Fig. 6C) confirmed statistical robustness and model validity (p < 0.001; Q2 intercept = −0.267; R2 intercept = 0.137). Collectively, these validation metrics substantiate that the observed group separation reflects genuine biological differences rather than overfitting or stochastic noise, thereby establishing a statistically sound foundation for discriminating pancake aroma profiles according to fermentation methodology.

Analysis of nutritional components and potential mechanisms underlying flavor formation

Analysis of fat and reducing sugar content

When identical raw materials are employed, the initial total fat content of unfermented and fermented pancakes is anticipated to be comparable. However, the experimentally determined fat content of the fermented samples (NF and YL-7) was significantly higher than that of the UNF, as shown in Fig. 7A. This observed difference likely reflects enhanced lipid extractability induced by fermentation, potentially attributable to structural reorganization of the dough matrix or partial hydrolysis of lipoprotein complexes, rather than a genuine increase in total lipid mass. Notably, Soxhlet extraction efficiency is highly dependent on both the physical state of lipids and their molecular accessibility within the sample matrix (Gad et al., 2024).

Comparison of various nutrient contents. (A) Fat. (B) Carbohydrates. (C) Protein. (D) Hydrolyzed Amino Acids. (E) Free Amino Acids. (F) Amino Acid and Its Corresponding Flavor Substance Taste Profile.

Significant differences in reducing sugar and fructose concentrations were observed across the sample groups (Fig. 7B). The UNF sample exhibited the lowest values, consistent with the expectation that minimal starch hydrolysis occurs under non-fermented conditions. In contrast, both NF and YL-7 showed elevated reducing sugar levels—aligning with established knowledge that microbial metabolism during grain fermentation facilitates starch degradation (Li et al., 2024). Notably, YL-7 displayed lower residual sugar content than NF, suggesting enhanced sugar utilization efficiency attributable to the defined starter culture. However, due to the absence of direct measurements of enzymatic activity and microbial population dynamics, these observed trends are interpreted as compositional correlations rather than mechanistic evidence of specific enzymatic or microbial kinetic processes.

Analysis of protein and amino acid composition

The protein content in the fermented samples (NF and YL-7) showed a trend of initially increasing and then decreasing (Fig. 7C), a pattern that has been documented in the literature for cereal fermentation systems (Ricardo et al., 2022), likely reflecting transient accumulation of soluble peptides during active proteolysis prior to microbial assimilation. Hydrolyzed amino acids (HAAs) and free amino acids (FAAs) showed time- and treatment-dependent changes (Fig. 7D–E). Essential amino acids accounted for 32.22–35.58% of the total hydrolyzed amino acids and 21.40–33.40% of the total free amino acids.

Comparative analysis shows that the flavor composition of pancake prepared using different processing methods (UNF, NF, YL-7) varies, which corresponds with changes in the degree of protein hydrolysis and the free amino acid profile. To visually present the relationships between components, quantitative data of volatile compounds (Table 1) were combined with the free amino acid measurement results (Fig. 7E) to generate Fig. 7F, which shows the relative abundance of major volatile compounds and the free amino acids that may serve as their precursors. This presentation method aims to highlight coexistence patterns rather than establish exact biochemical pathways.

The hydrolyzed amino acids reflect the compositional characteristics of the raw material's protein matrix (Fig. 7D), while the free amino acids exhibit a distinct biphasic temporal trajectory during fermentation (Fig. 7E): microbial proteases (derived from the indigenous microbiota in NF or the YL-7 co-culture) hydrolyze storage proteins—rice glutelin, soybean glycinin/β-conglycinin, and maize zein—releasing free amino acids. Among the hydrolyzed amino acids, glutamic acid is the most abundant (1.152–1.480 g/100 g), consistent with the naturally high glutamic acid content of these composite storage proteins (Tsukasa, 2019). Cysteine emerges as the most abundant free amino acid. The accumulated free amino acids are gradually metabolized by microorganisms, serving as nitrogen sources and direct precursors of flavor compounds. For example, threonine, lysine, and arginine are detected only in the unfermented group and in the early stage of NF (t/2), indicating their rapid consumption during active fermentation; the absence of glycine, alanine, and proline in the YL-7 samples reflects strain-specific catabolism. Additionally, the decline in free amino acid levels correlates with the increase in volatile compounds (e.g., aldehydes, esters).

UNF samples have not undergone microbial fermentation, and the degree of protein hydrolysis in the flour is very low, resulting in a very limited release of free amino acids. Therefore, their flavor profile mainly comes from the endogenous amino acids inherent in the raw materials. For example, the main umami substance, glutamic acid, mainly exists in a bound form (peptide-bound), with very low free content, contributing little to the umami taste. The absence of microbial activity also means that flavor precursors cannot undergo biochemical transformation. This results in UNF samples exhibiting a relatively simple steamed rice aroma, lacking the complexity and layers characteristic of fermented products. Similarly, arginine accumulates significantly in the UNF, reflecting that microbial metabolic activity is inhibited or absent. In contrast, in the fermentation system, arginine is rapidly consumed to support bacterial growth and the synthesis of secondary metabolites, including aromatic compounds (Izabela et al., 2010).

NF pancakes involve the native microbial communities present in the raw materials (such as wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria). Compared with UNF, NF samples indicate active protein hydrolysis and metabolic activity during fermentation. NF exhibited an increase in organic acids and esters (such as ethyl acetate and ethyl propionate). The presence of esters is associated with wild yeast activity recorded in other fermentation systems, where reactions mediated by alcohol acyltransferase produce fruity volatile compounds (Zhou et al., 2024). The YL-7 co-culture demonstrated enhanced process reproducibility and superior flavor development compared to NF. These attributes—reduced batch variability, simplified microbial control, and reduced reliance on exogenous flavor additives—suggest practical advantages for potential scale-up. In the YL-7 system, free amino acids such as Asp, Pro, and Ser are efficiently consumed by microorganisms to generate key flavor substances like aldehydes, ketones, and esters, significantly enhancing dairy, fruity, and ester aromas while stabilizing the flavor.

In summary, the different flavor characteristics observed in UNF, NF, and YL-7 pancake samples correlate with systematic differences in protein hydrolysis levels and the resulting patterns of free amino acids and volatile compounds. These changes can be descriptively summarized into three observational dimensions: (i) substrate breadth (the range of detectable free amino acids), (ii) reaction depth (the correspondence between amino acid consumption and volatile compound accumulation), and (iii) product diversity (the types and relative abundances of identified volatiles).

Analysis of sensory evaluation differences among samples

To assess the reliability of the sensory evaluation panel, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Cronbach's α were calculated for each sensory attribute (Table 3). The results showed that the ICC values for all sensory attributes were above 0.910, indicating a high level of agreement among the evaluators. Meanwhile, the Cronbach's α values for each attribute ranged from 0.920 to 0.968, indicating excellent internal consistency of the scores. These results collectively verify the reliability of the sensory evaluation panel. Based on the sensory evaluation results, a radar chart was plotted (Fig. 8A). The sample scores showed a distinct gradient, with YL-7 pancake receiving the highest score in sensory evaluation. Partial least squares regression (PLSR) models were constructed based on the instrumental response data from the E-nose, E-tongue, and GC-IMS (X variables) and the sensory evaluation scores (Y variables), systematically revealing the quantitative relationship between physicochemical indicators and sensory perception. The prediction scatter plots (actual sensory values vs. model-predicted values) (Fig. 8B) showed that all three models had excellent fitting and predictive capabilities (E-nose: R2 = 0.973, RMSECV = 0.293; E-tongue: R2 = 0.988, RMSECV = 0.217; GC-IMS: R2 = 0.985, RMSECV = 0.173), with data points tightly distributed around the ideal regression line (y = x), indicating that the comprehensive instrumental responses can accurately quantify variations in sensory attributes.

VIP analysis precisely identifies the key physicochemical indicators driving sensory prediction (Fig. 8C). Among the E-nose sensors, the W1W sensor (VIP = 1.81) exhibits the highest contribution, with its high sensitivity to sulfur-containing volatiles indicating that sulfur-containing Maillard reaction products are the core chemical basis for the characteristic aroma developed during fermentation. E-tongue analysis reveals that sourness (VIP = 1.80) and saltiness (VIP = 1.30) are the primary determinants of taste, reflecting the critical roles of organic acids (lactic acid, acetic acid) and ionic balance in shaping overall taste perception. This indicates that controlled acidification combined with optimized mineral/ion profiles is essential for achieving a harmonious taste profile. In GC-IMS profiling, 2-butanone emerged as the strongest predictor (VIP = 3.63), displaying a concentration gradient (UNF < NF < YL-7) with a dose-dependent positive correlation to sensory scores. As a sulfur-free fatty ketone (C₄H₈O) biosynthesized via pyruvate metabolism and moderate lipid oxidation by lactic acid bacteria, 2-butanone functions as a pivotal flavor skeleton compound, imparting creamy and nutty notes. Notably, W1W response (sulfurous aroma signature), sourness parameters (organic acid metabolism), and 2-butanone content (central carbon metabolite) all ranked among the highest VIP values. Although originating from distinct biochemical pathways, these three indicators collectively form a synergistic index system for predicting overall flavor scores. This tripartite optimization underpins the sensory superiority of YL-7 fermentation: simultaneous enhancement of roasted aroma clarity, balanced sour-salty taste profile, and pronounced creaminess. In contrast, NF samples exhibited lower response intensities and greater fluctuations in key variables (e.g., elevated 1-hexanal, VIP = 1.10, associated with grassy off-notes), leading to compromised sensory score stability. UNF samples showed minimal responses across all key variables and baseline sensory scores, further corroborating the decisive role of fermentation in flavor development. This work not only validates multimodal instrumental data as an effective proxy for subjective sensory evaluation but also elucidates, from the “physicochemical indicator → sensory perception” dimension, the quality advantage mechanism of YL-7 over NF. It provides a quantifiable scientific foundation for precise flavor regulation and process optimization in fermented wheat-based products.

Whether the observed differences originate from fermentation parameters (time/temperature/humidity) or from the YL-7 strain itself. Although detailed microbial community analysis is lacking, the following phenomena indicate that YL-7 is the main driving factor: (i) 2-butanone (VIP = 3.64) identified by PLSR analysis is a key compound associated with a “creamy aroma” (R2 = 0.9848), which is a metabolite closely related to YL-7; (ii) under the same substrate conditions, undesirable flavor substances commonly found in uncontrolled fermentation (such as geosmin and H₂S) were not detected in YL-7 samples. Therefore, parameter differences are not a confounding factor, but rather an enabling condition: 44 °C created a niche that allowed YL-7 to express its functional phenotype, thereby directly shaping the volatile profile and sensory outcomes.

This technological challenge is not merely a food processing issue but is also embedded in broader agricultural and rural development contexts. As highlighted by studies on climate-smart agriculture, farmers' access to information and adaptive capacity are critical determinants of resilience in rural farming systems (Amara et al., 2024). Similarly, integrated natural resource management and ecosystem-based approaches have been shown to underpin sustainable livelihoods and community resilience in agricultural contexts (Bera & Nag, 2025). The transition from spontaneous to controlled fermentation signifies a parallel shift, from unpredictable and resource – intensive practices to knowledge - driven and reproducible processes, which can enhance both product quality and production efficiency. Consequently, the development of defined starter cultures for traditional pancake fermentation not only addresses concerns regarding food quality and safety but also aligns with the broader goals of sustainable agricultural development.

Conclusion

This study reveals that the fermentation of a well – defined Lactobacillus - yeast co - culture (YL - 7) effectively enriches the flavor complexity of multi - grain pancakes. It achieves this by facilitating protein degradation, amino acid release, and the synthesis of esters and ketones, as verified by comprehensive analyses using E - nose, E - tongue, and GC - IMS. In sensory evaluations, YL - 7 pancakes demonstrated better aroma, taste, and overall acceptability compared to unfermented and naturally fermented samples. Nevertheless, the present findings are derived from laboratory - scale experiments. The stability of the strain, shelf - life, and cost - effectiveness under industrial conditions necessitate further validation. Future research should concentrate on pilot - scale confirmation, assessment of storage stability, and techno - economic analysis to promote practical implementation.
