Long-Term Proton Pump Inhibitor Use and Gastric Cancer Risk: Interpreting Population-Based Evidence
Authors: Hae Won Yoo
Published: 2026

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are among the most commonly prescribed medications worldwide. Their indications include gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcer disease, and gastroprotection in patients receiving non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or antiplatelet agents.1 Long-term PPI prescribing occurs across diverse clinical settings, yet the appropriateness of continued therapy is not always reassessed.2 Consequently, concerns regarding the long-term safety of PPIs remain clinically relevant, particularly for outcomes that develop over many years.

Prolonged acid suppression provides a biologically plausible basis for these concerns. Chronic acid suppression increases gastrin levels, which exert trophic effects on the gastric mucosa and may promote enterochromaffin-like cell hyperplasia.3 It has also been associated with the accelerated development of corpus-predominant gastritis and intestinal metaplasia, as well as alterations in the intragastric bacterial environment that may promote the formation of carcinogenic N-nitroso compounds.4 These mechanisms have raised the question of whether long-term PPI use may be associated with gastric cancer development.

The findings of previous population-based studies have been inconsistent. Cheung et al.5 reported an increased risk of gastric cancer after Helicobacter pylori eradication among long-term PPI users, and Abrahami et al.6 observed a modest excess risk in a U.K. new-user active-comparator cohort. Seo et al.7 also reported a positive association using Korean nationwide data. In contrast, Shin et al.,8 using the same national claims database but requiring normal baseline endoscopy, found no significant difference between long-term PPI and H2-receptor antagonist (H2RA) users. More recently, Duru et al.9 reported no association between long-term PPI use and non-cardia gastric adenocarcinoma across five Nordic countries after adjustment for H. pylori-related variables. These divergent findings underscore the uncertainty that remains in this field and may reflect differences in study design, baseline gastric cancer risk, or residual confounding that is difficult to fully capture in observational data.

In this issue, Huh et al.10 report a propensity score-matched cohort analysis using UK Biobank data. New PPI users had a higher risk of gastric cancer than non-users (hazard ratio [HR], 2.324; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.646 to 3.282) and a higher risk than new H2RA users, who served as an active-comparator group (HR, 5.343; 95% CI, 1.557 to 18.335), over a median follow-up of 6.7 years. The authors also observed a trend toward increasing risk with longer cumulative duration and higher cumulative dose, and the findings were consistent across several sensitivity analyses. These features add important evidence to the ongoing debate, particularly because the study was conducted in a Western population with a relatively low incidence of gastric cancer.

However, these findings require careful interpretation before causal conclusions are drawn. The key issue is not simply whether PPI users experience more gastric cancer, but whether the observed excess reflects a drug effect or the clinical context in which PPIs are prescribed and gastric cancer is detected. PPI use may partly function as a marker of preceding upper gastrointestinal symptoms, underlying gastric risk, or more intensive diagnostic evaluation. Such patterns can produce an apparent association that may not reflect a direct drug effect. This difficulty is well recognized in pharmacoepidemiologic studies of acid-suppressive therapy and long-term outcomes, in which treatment indication, healthcare utilization, and outcome detection are not easily separated.6-9

Residual H. pylori confounding deserves particular attention. Although Huh et al. identified H. pylori infection using diagnostic codes and eradication regimens, prior, untreated, or undocumented infection may not be fully captured. Because H. pylori is the strongest established risk factor for non-cardia gastric cancer, the incomplete ascertainment of infection status leaves some uncertainty when interpreting the observed estimates.

A related but distinct concern is differential diagnostic intensity. UK Biobank participants may differ from the general population in healthcare engagement, and long-term PPI users may undergo more frequent clinical assessment or diagnostic testing. Such patterns may lead to differential cancer detection and could partly account for the observed association.

Despite these concerns, the study remains valuable. By leveraging primary care prescription records within the UK Biobank, the authors defined new PPI use and assessed cumulative exposure rather than relying solely on baseline medication history. The inclusion of both a non-user comparison group and an active-comparator group of H2RA users, together with propensity score matching, broad covariate adjustment, calendar time-specific matching, lag-time analyses, and multiple sensitivity analyses, suggests that the authors attempted to address several important sources of bias. These features do not eliminate uncertainty, but they make the study a useful contribution to a field in which risk estimates are highly sensitive to design choices.8,9

Taken together, these findings support periodic reassessment of long-term PPI therapy but do not establish PPIs as gastric carcinogens. PPIs should not be withheld from patients with clear indications; however, long-term therapy should be periodically reviewed, particularly when prescribed for nonspecific or historical symptoms. In patients with additional risk factors for gastric cancer, treatment decisions should balance symptom control, preventive indications, baseline gastric cancer risk, and the feasibility of dose reduction or step-down therapy.
