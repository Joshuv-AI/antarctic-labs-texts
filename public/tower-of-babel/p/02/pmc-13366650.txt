Early risk factors and six month posttraumatic stress disorder symptom severity following trauma
Authors: Xia (Sebastiane) Chen, Farah Harb, Terri A deRoon-Cassini, Christine L Larson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a significant public health concern associated with persistent psychological impairment following trauma exposure. Early identification of risk factors is critical for timely intervention and prevention of chronic symptoms.Objective: This study examined early post-trauma cognitive, emotional, trauma-related, and socioeconomic factors associated with PTSD symptom severity at six-month follow-up, with sleep quality and self-perceived pain as potential mediators.Method: A total of 178 adults were recruited from an emergency department and assessed approximately two weeks following trauma exposure. Partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) was used to evaluate cognitive attributions, trauma history, emotion responding and regulation, and socioeconomic factors as predictors of six-month PTSD symptom severity. PTSD symptom severity at two weeks post-trauma was included as a covariate to account for early symptom levels.Results: Maladaptive cognitive attributions demonstrated strong direct associations with later PTSD symptom severity and significant indirect associations through sleep quality and self-perceived pain. Trauma history showed a modest association with PTSD symptoms. Emotion responding and regulation was indirectly associated with PTSD symptom severity through sleep quality, suggesting a sleep-mediated pathway. Socioeconomic factors were not significantly associated with PTSD symptom severity. These effects were observed after accounting for PTSD symptom severity at two weeks post-trauma.Conclusions: Early maladaptive cognitive attributions and sleep disturbances represent key clinical targets for early post-trauma intervention. Addressing these factors promptly after trauma may help reduce the risk of chronic PTSD.KEYWORDS: Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), partial least squares structural equation modeling, cognitive attributions, trauma history, emotion responding and regulation, socioeconomic factors, sleep, pain

Introduction

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a debilitating mental health condition that can develop after traumatic events, including experiencing or witnessing natural disasters, accidents, physical or sexual assault, or military combat (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Development of PTSD following trauma exposure has been related to the severity of the traumatic event (Rubin & Feeling, 2013), individual perception of the event (Boals et al., 2019), the duration and frequency of previous traumas (Ogle et al., 2013a), and personal vulnerability and coping mechanisms (Biro et al., 1997; Castro-Vale et al., 2020). PTSD is linked to decreased quality of life and social and emotional difficulties (Breslau et al., 1991; Kessler et al., 1995; Koenen et al., 2007). Given the prevalence of trauma and its long-term consequences, understanding the risk factors for PTSD is essential for improving early identification and informing future intervention research aimed at supporting recovery (McNally et al., 2003).

Despite numerous studies examining risk factors that can be assessed in the acute post-trauma period, there remains limited clarity regarding which early predictors are most robustly associated with PTSD symptom severity at later follow-up. Many studies have examined individual risk factors in isolation (Da Silva et al., 2024; DiGangi et al., 2013; Erving et al., 2024; Harnett et al., 2024; Mirabile et al., 2023; Seah & Berle, 2022). Examining multiple risk and resilience factors simultaneously shortly after trauma may provide a more comprehensive understanding of differential risk for persistent PTSD symptoms. Prior research has identified PTSD risk factors operating at both individual and contextual levels, including cognitive attributions (Ehlers & Clark, 2000), trauma history (Brewin et al., 2000), Emotion responding and regulation (Ehring et al., 2010), and socio-economic factors (Galea et al., 2007). However, these factors have often been investigated in isolation, and their relative contributions to PTSD symptom severity when examined simultaneously remain uncertain. This study aimed to examine the associations of these constructs, measured soon after trauma, with severity of PTSD symptoms six months post-trauma. Understanding these associations across multiple key risk factors may help inform future research on early identification and intervention development.

Moreover, more research is needed on mediators that influence the relationship between risk factors and PTSD symptom severity over time. Mediators are important for clarifying psychological and biological processes through which early risk factors are associated with later PTSD symptoms. If such processes are modifiable, they may represent potential targets for future intervention research aimed at attenuating the association between early risk factors and later PTSD symptom severity (Gasperi et al., 2021; Kind & Otis, 2019; Maher et al., 2021; Qing et al., 2025). Sleep quality (Insana et al., 2011) and self-perceived pain (Sharp & Harvey, 2001) were examined as potential mediators. These factors are shown to be linked with PTSD in previous studies and may mediate the relationship between initial risk factors and PTSD symptoms. By exploring sleep quality and self-perceived pain as mediators, this study aims to clarify their potential roles in pathways linking early risk factors to later PTSD symptom severity.

Cognitive attributions

Cognitive attributions refer to individuals’ beliefs and interpretations about the cause and impact of a traumatic event. Studies show that negative interpretations and self-blame significantly contribute to PTSD symptoms (Ehlers et al., 2004; Meiser-Stedman et al., 2009a,b). Cognitive attribution theory (Heider, 1958) suggests that perceptions of control and personal agency are shaped by these beliefs. When a traumatic event becomes integral to an individual's identity and life story, PTSD symptoms can be severe (Lanius et al., 2020; Ogle et al., 2013b). Attributing trauma to factors beyond one's control also increases feelings of helplessness and, in turn, exacerbates PTSD risk (Brewin et al., 2000; Resick et al., 2008). Self-blame can lead to lower self-esteem, higher anxiety and depression, and severe PTSD symptoms (Kip et al., 2022). Cognitive reframing, which helps individuals adopt more adaptive interpretations of traumatic experiences, has been shown to alleviate negative emotional responses and PTSD-related symptoms (Mueser et al., 2015; Nakao et al., 2021). Together, these findings underscore the relevance of cognitive attributions in shaping PTSD symptom severity.

Trauma history

Trauma history includes the number and severity of previous traumatic events. Studies consistently show that exposure to multiple and severe traumatic events is associated with a higher likelihood of PTSD following a subsequent potentially traumatic event (Brewin et al., 2000; Kessler, 2017). Accumulated traumatic experiences may tax coping mechanisms, contributing to elevated psychological distress and increased vulnerability to PTSD symptoms (Breslau et al., 1998; Cloitre et al., 2009; Ozer et al., 2003). Understanding the contribution of trauma history to later symptom severity remains an important component of identifying individuals at heightened risk for persistent psychological distress.

Emotion responding and regulation

Emotion responding and regulation involves managing and reducing emotional responses, often employing different coping styles, such as passive coping (e.g. avoidance or suppression) and active strategies (e.g. problem-solving or cognitive reappraisal; Tan et al., 2023; Weiss et al., 2019). Difficulties in emotion responding and regulation are linked to increased PTSD severity (Ehring et al., 2010; Tull et al., 2009). Trauma-related triggers can impair managing distressing thoughts and memories, leading to heightened PTSD symptoms (Berntsen et al., 2012; Gratz et al., 2006). Acute post-trauma emotional dysregulation has been shown to prospectively relate to later PTSD symptom severity (Pencea et al., 2020). This study further examines emotion responding and regulation assessed soon after trauma in relation to PTSD symptom severity at follow-up. Emerging neurodevelopmental research suggests that cortical thickness alterations and developing brain asymmetry may serve as broader biological vulnerability markers for internalising psychopathology including PTSD (Kuang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2023), and that early social environments shape the neural circuits underlying emotional reactivity and regulatory capacity (Liu et al., 2025).

Socioeconomic factors

Socioeconomic factors include the social and economic context of an individual's life. Access to healthcare, educational opportunities, safe recreational spaces, and neighbourhood characteristics like community cohesion and crime rates are linked to more PTSD symptoms (Gapen et al., 2011; Johnson et al., 2020; Roberts et al., 2011; Weisburd et al., 2018). For example, research shows that low-income urban neighbourhoods have higher PTSD rates (Parto et al., 2011). Individual-level socioeconomic position, such as income and education, has been associated with variability in PTSD symptom severity across populations (Cruz-Gonzalez et al., 2023; El Zouki et al., 2022; Harnett et al., 2024; Kraft & Kraft, 2021; Rahman et al., 2022). The interplay between individual and community socioeconomic factors is considered important for understanding heterogeneity in PTSD symptom expression and recovery trajectories (Fusco et al., 2021; Ghafoori et al., 2012; Lenart et al., 2021).

Synthesising findings on factors associated with risk for PTSD

Despite well-documented associations between these factors and PTSD symptom severity, evidence remains mixed regarding which constructs are most strongly associated with persistent or non-remitting PTSD over time, and relatively few studies have modelled multiple constructs simultaneously to address this question. For example, some studies suggest that negative cognitive attributions are associated with PTSD symptoms, whereas trauma history and socio-economic factors may show stronger associations in certain samples (Boscarino et al., 2013). In contrast, other work has emphasised cognitive attributions and emotion responding and regulation as more salient correlates of PTSD symptom severity than environmental factors (Karam et al., 2014; Tull et al., 2010). While neighbourhood resources may buffer some adverse effects of trauma exposure, existing research suggests that community-level factors alone may be insufficient to account for PTSD symptom persistence, particularly in socioeconomically disadvantaged settings with elevated trauma exposure (Bird et al., 2021; Ventimiglia & Seedat, 2019; Webb et al., 2022). The literature further indicates that associations between trauma history and PTSD symptoms may vary depending on individual resilience and characteristics of the traumatic exposure (Fink & Galea, 2015; Lee, 2019; Schock et al., 2016). Thus, although numerous studies have examined individual risk and protective factors, relatively few have directly compared multiple constructs within the same analytic framework. Among those that have, conflicting findings regarding the relative salience of socio-economic factors, cognitive attributions, and emotion responding and regulation – and the role of trauma characteristics – underscore the complexity of PTSD symptom development. These inconsistencies suggest that the relevance of specific risk factors may depend on both intrapersonal processes (e.g. cognitive attributions, emotion responding and regulation) and contextual conditions (e.g. trauma history, socioeconomic context), highlighting the need for integrative, multivariable approaches. To address this gap, the present study adopts an integrative, multivariable approach that simultaneously models cognitive attributions, trauma history, emotion responding and regulation, and socio-economic factors to estimate their unique associations with PTSD symptom severity assessed at follow-up.

Potential mediators: sleep and pain

Trauma survivors are more likely to suffer from sleep disturbances and chronic pain (Belleville et al., 2009; Insana et al., 2011; Krakow et al., 2000). A more comprehensive understanding of how trauma affects both body and mind may be gained by considering both physiological and psychological recovery pathways as potential mediators. Sleep disturbance is consistently associated with PTSD severity, and improvements in sleep quality may be beneficial for PTSD treatment (Germain et al., 2017). However, some studies report little or no evidence for mediation by sleep quality and self-perceived pain in the relationships between PTSD risk factors (e.g. cognitive attributions, trauma history, emotion responding and regulation, and socio-economic factors) and PTSD symptom severity (Babson et al., 2012), indicating the need for further investigation. Self-perceived pain co-occurs with PTSD symptoms, each exacerbating the other (Sharp & Harvey, 2001). Pain has been linked to elevated PTSD and depression risk following trauma (O’Donnell et al., 2004), particularly among veterans. By examining sleep quality and self-perceived pain as potential mediators, this study aims to clarify their roles in pathways linking early post-trauma risk factors to later PTSD symptom severity (Otte et al., 2007; Tang et al., 2017).

Current study

This study addresses a gap by examining previously identified risk factors and their associations with non-remitting PTSD symptom severity following a traumatic event. Guided by a multilevel perspective, we conceptualise risk as arising from intrapersonal mechanisms (cognitive attributions, emotion responding and regulation), historical factors (trauma history), and contextual conditions (socio-economic factors). Sleep quality and self-perceived pain are modelled as parallel mediators representing psychological and physiological recovery-related processes. Injured patients from a local Level 1 trauma centre completed self-report measures at two weeks post-trauma (cognitive attributions, emotion responding and regulation, trauma history, socio-economic factors) and PTSD symptoms six months post-injury. Using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) (Hair et al., 2014, 2019), we examined the relationships between variables assessed soon after trauma exposure and PTSD symptom severity at six-month follow-up, with sleep quality and self-perceived pain examined as parallel mediators assessed two weeks post-trauma. This study aims to enhance understanding of the complex relationships among these factors in relation to PTSD symptom severity, with implications for the identification of individuals at elevated risk and for informing future intervention research.

Method

Participants

These data were collected as part of a larger study, the Imaging Study of Trauma and Resilience (iSTAR) (Tomas et al., 2022a). Participants were recruited from an emergency department in southeastern Wisconsin between 2016 and 2019. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Medical College of Wisconsin. Participants consented at every visit and were able to withdraw from the study at any time. The study aimed to examine factors influencing mental health post-trauma through psychophysiological, neurobiological, behavioural, and self-report data (Bird et al., 2021; Webb et al., 2022). Inclusion criteria required participants to have a score of 3 or higher on the Predicting PTSD Questionnaire (Rothbaum et al., 2012), report a perceived threat to life during the traumatic event, demonstrate English proficiency, score at least 13 on the Glasgow Coma Scale (Teasdale & Jennett, 1974), be between 18 and 60 years old, and meet MRI safety criteria. Participants were excluded if they exhibited symptoms of psychosis or mania, had a spinal cord or traumatic brain injury, were seen in the emergency department due to self-harm, or tested positive for alcohol, illicit drugs, or narcotics at the time of their visit. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Medical College of Wisconsin. Participants consented at every visit and were able to withdraw from the study at any time.

Of 215 participants, 37 were excluded from the analysis: 24 due to missing PTSD symptom severity data and 13 due to insufficient data for at least two observed variables for each latent variable. The final sample comprised 178 participants who completed self-report measures at two weeks and six months post-trauma. The majority were involved in motor vehicle collisions (77.52%). Other mechanisms of injury included assault/altercation (11.8%), falls (1.69%), stabbings (1.12%), domestic violence (1.12%), gunshots (0.56%), and other injuries (6.18%). The sample was 43.82% male and 56.18% female, with an average age of 33.31 years (SD = 10.75). Racial composition included 57.63% Black/African American, 27.12% White, 1.69% Asian/Asian American, and 6.78% identifying as more than one race. Regarding ethnicity, 89.33% were non-Hispanic/Latino and 10.11% Hispanic/Latino. Income data revealed that 58.42% had an annual household income below $40,000 (Table 1).

Procedure and overview of the PLS-SEM analytic model

This longitudinal study analyzed self-reported data collected at two weeks and six months post-trauma. The structural model specifies hypothesised directional associations among exogenous and endogenous latent variables, estimated within a path analytic framework (Deng et al., 2018). The model included four exogenous latent variables: cognitive attributions, trauma history, emotion responding and regulation, and socio-economic factors. Observations were based on self-reported data collected two weeks post-trauma, with socio-economic factors (measured using the Area Deprivation Index, or ADI) derived from geocoded participant addresses. We modelled sleep quality and self-perceived pain as parallel mediators. Sleep quality and self-perceived pain were modelled as parallel mediators, representing distinct psychological and physiological recovery pathways; their potential interrelation was not explicitly modelled. PTSD symptom severity at six months post-trauma was used as the outcome variable (Figure 1).

Model indicators and measures

Cognitive attribution

Centrality of Event

The Centrality of Event scale measures the significance of trauma in shaping self-identity (Berntsen & Rubin, 2006). Participants rate 7 items on a 5-point scale from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree). The scale has high reliability (Berntsen & Rubin, 2006, α = 0.94).

Injustice Experience Questionnaire (IEQ)

The IEQ measures thoughts and feelings about loss, perceived unfairness, and externalised blame post-injury (Sullivan et al., 2014). Participants rate 12 items on a 5-point Likert scale. Example items include ‘Most people do not understand how severe my condition is’ and ‘I am suffering because of someone else’s negligence.’ Scores were summed, with missing data handled by mean imputation if one or two items are missing. The IEQ has been shown to demonstrate high reliability with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.89 (Philippus et al., 2021).

Just World Beliefs – Personal (JWB-P

The Just World Beliefs scale assesses belief in the fairness of life events (Dalbert, 1999). This scale uses seven items rated on a 6-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). The measure includes Personal and General Just World Belief Scales. The Personal Just World Belief items were used in this study. An example item is, ‘Overall, events in my life are just.’ The JWB-P has been found to have high reliability (α = 0.79; Dalbert, 1993).

Trauma history

Life Events Checklist (LEC)

The LEC assesses types and proximity of traumatic events (Blake et al., 1998). Participants report on 16 types of traumatic events and their proximity to the event (e.g. ‘Happened to me,’ ‘Witnessed it’). A weighted cumulative score was calculated, with high reliability (α = 0.87; Weis et al., 2022).

Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ)

The CTQ measures five types of childhood maltreatment (Bernstein & Fink, 1998). Each of the 28 items is rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (Never True) to 5 (Always True). Scores for childhood deprivation experiences (emotional and physical neglect) and childhood threat experiences (emotional, physical, and sexual abuse) were calculated. The CTQ has demonstrated reliability coefficients ranging from α = 0.79 to 0.86 (Bernstein et al., 2003).

Emotion responding and regulation

Emotion Dysregulation Scale (EDS)

The EDS assesses emotion cognition, expression, and regulation strategies (Powers et al., 2015). Participants rate 12 items on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all true) to 7 (very true). Example items include ‘It’s often hard for me to calm down when I’m upset.’ The EDS shows good internal reliability and temporal stability (Cohen’s d = 3.69) (Raimondi et al., 2022; Van Wijk, 2023).

Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS)

The SHAPS measures loss of interest and sense of pleasure (Snaith et al., 1995). Participants rate 14 items on a scale from 1 (totally disagree) to 4 (totally agree). Higher scores indicate greater disinterest and joylessness. The SHAPS showed high reliability with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.94 (Franken et al., 2007).

State-Trait Anger Expression Inventory (STAXI-2)

The STAXI-2 measures feelings of anger (Spielberger et al., 1999). We used the state anger measure in which participants rate 5 items on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very much). A Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.89 was found in a past study of participants recruited from the emergency department (deRoon-Cassini et al., 2010).

Socio-economic factors

Area Deprivation Index (ADI)

The ADI is a geocoded measure of relative resource advantage and disadvantage of a neighbourhood at the level of the census block group (Kind & Buckingham, 2018; Singh, 2003). Scores range from 1 to 100, with higher scores indicating more resource disadvantage. The ADI incorporates 17 variables from the American Community Survey (US Census), including metrics like employment, housing and household characteristics. These factors are weighted to determine the neighbourhood's socioeconomic status in comparison to all other block groups across the United States (Hu et al., 2018; Kind & Buckingham, 2018).

Income and educational attainment

In addition to neighbourhood socioeconomic position, we also measured the individual socioeconomic position of the participants. Income was measured in 11 levels from $0 to $100,000, with each level representing an increment of $10,000. Education was measured on a 21-level scale from no education to doctoral degree.

The selection of the Area Deprivation Index (ADI), income, and educational attainment was guided by their theoretical relevance and representativeness of neighbourhood- and individual-level socioeconomic disadvantage, as well as data availability within the present study; more granular neighbourhood-level indicators were not collected in this dataset.

Endogenous latent outcome variable: posttraumatic stress symptom severity

PTSD symptoms were measured using the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Weathers et al., 2013), encompassing four main symptom clusters: re-experiencing, negative mood and cognitions, hyperarousal, and avoidance. Each cluster was entered as a separate indicator of the latent variable representing overall PTSD symptom severity, allowing for analysis of how each symptom cluster contributes to the overall PTSD severity score in the model. In our sample, the PCL-5 showed high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95 (Bird et al., 2021).

Mediating variables

Mediators included sleep quality and pain self-report at two weeks post-trauma.

Insomnia Severity Index (ISI)

The ISI measures sleep quality (Bastien et al., 2001). Participants rate seven items on a 5-point scale from 0 (Not at all Noticeable) to 4 (Very Much Noticeable). Higher scores indicate more severe insomnia. The ISI demonstrates excellent internal consistency (Cronbach α = 0.92) and validity (Gagnon et al., 2013).

Visual Analog Pain Scale (VAS)

The VAS assesses physical pain severity (Holdgate et al., 2003). Participants rate their pain on a scale from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain). The Visual Analog Pain Scale has been shown to have strong reliability and validity when compared with other pain rating scales (Williamson & Hoggart, 2005).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (2020). Given the study's focus on examining theoretically informed predictors and predicting endogenous variables, partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) was utilised to test the research model (Hair et al., 2014). PLS-SEM is particularly suitable for this research due to several factors: it accommodates smaller sample sizes, does not require normally distributed data, and is effective for models with multiple predictors and mediation paths. Furthermore, PLS-SEM is advantageous for exploratory research and predictive analysis, and it enables the use of latent variable scores for further analysis (Hair et al., 2019; Hwang et al., 2019; Khan et al., 2019; Shiau et al., 2019). Data were analyzed using SmartPLS version 4.1.0.3 (Ringle et al., 2024). PTSD symptom severity at six months post-trauma served as the primary outcome; PTSD symptom severity assessed at two weeks post-trauma was included in the structural model as a single-indicator covariate, with a direct path to the six-month PTSD symptom severity outcome, to account for the potential confounding influence of early symptom severity on later PTSD outcomes. Prior to evaluating the structural model, the measurement model was assessed to ensure adequate reliability and validity of the latent constructs, including internal consistency reliability and indicator adequacy. Only after establishing acceptable measurement properties were the structural paths, indirect effects, and predictive relevance examined. The analysis focused on standardised path coefficients, effect sizes, and predictive relevance of the endogenous constructs. Bias-Corrected and Accelerated (BCa) Bootstrap resampling with 5000 resamples was applied. The model focused on estimating parallel direct and indirect effects among predictors within a mediation framework. PLS-SEM does not assume multivariate normality, and the use of BCa bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples provides robustness against non-normal distributions in self-reported data. Remaining item-level missing data were handled via mean imputation when no more than two items were missing on a given scale; cases with missing data on the latent variable indicators were excluded from the analysis as described above (Figure 2).

Results

Path model and mediation analysis

Cognitive attributions

Cognitive attributions exhibited a significant direct effect on PTSD Symptom Severity (β = 0.616, p < .001), with a large effect size (f² = 1.086, p < .001), indicating a strong association between adverse cognitive attributions and increased PTSD symptoms. Additionally, cognitive attributions had a significant direct effect on both Self-Perceived Pain (β = 0.443, p < .001; f² = 0.175, p < .001) and Sleep Quality (β = 0.310, p < .001; f² = 0.198, p < .001), suggesting that negative cognitive attributions were associated with higher levels of perceived pain and poorer sleep quality.

Furthermore, the total indirect effect of cognitive attributions on PTSD symptom severity, mediated through Sleep Quality and Self-Perceived Pain, was significant (β = 0.109, p < .001). These findings suggest that both sleep quality and self-perceived pain partially mediate the relationship between cognitive attributions and PTSD symptom severity, further highlighting the critical role of these mediators in the model.

Trauma history

Trauma history had a significant direct effect on PTSD Symptom Severity (β = 0.090, p = .006; f² = 0.041, p < .001), indicating a modest yet notable impact of trauma history on increased PTSD symptoms. However, trauma history did not significantly affect Self-Perceived Pain (β = 0.079, p = .267) or Sleep Quality (β = −0.014, p = .774).

The total indirect effect of trauma history on PTSD symptom severity was not significant (β = 0.012, p = .385), suggesting that the impact of trauma history on PTSD symptoms is primarily direct, with no mediation through sleep quality or perceived pain.

Emotion responding and regulation

Emotion responding and regulation did not have a significant direct effect on PTSD Symptom Severity (β = 0.076, p = .129) or Self-Perceived Pain (β = −0.003, p = .969). However, emotion responding and regulation had a significant and large effect size on Sleep Quality (β = 0.620, p < .001; f² = 0.914, p < .001).

The total indirect effect of emotion responding and regulation on PTSD symptom severity, mediated by Sleep Quality, was significant (β = 0.068, p = .041). This finding indicates that the relationship between emotion responding and regulation and PTSD symptoms is entirely mediated by sleep quality. Improved emotion responding and regulation is associated with better sleep quality, which in turn was associated with lower PTSD symptom severity, underscoring the essential role of sleep quality in mitigating the effects of emotional dysregulation on PTSD.

Socioeconomic factors

Socioeconomic factors did not have significant direct effects on PTSD Symptom Severity (β = 0.026, p = .454), Self-Perceived Pain (β = 0.048, p = .481), or Sleep Quality (β = 0.044, p = .329). The effect sizes for socioeconomic factors were also non-significant across all outcomes.

The total indirect effects of socioeconomic factors on PTSD symptom severity were not significant, suggesting that socioeconomic factors did not play a substantial role, either directly or indirectly, in influencing PTSD symptoms in this model.

Two-week PTSD severity

Two-week PTSD symptom severity, included as a single-indicator covariate, showed a significant direct effect on six-month PTSD symptom severity (β = 0.075, p = .026), with a small effect size (f² = 0.026). This indicates that, after accounting for all other predictors in the model, early PTSD symptom severity was associated with later PTSD symptom severity. Notably, the effect size of the baseline PTSD covariate was substantially smaller than that of cognitive attributions (f² = 1.086), indicating that early cognitive processes accounted for a considerably larger portion of variance in later PTSD symptom severity than did baseline symptom levels alone.

Predictive relevance

Predictive relevance (Q²) was calculated to assess the impact of each predictor on the endogenous constructs and the model's predictive accuracy (S. Geisser, 1974; Khan et al., 2019; Stone, 1974). The model explained 83.3% of the variance in PTSD Symptom Severity (R² = 0.833, R²adj = 0.826), 24.3% in Self-Perceived Pain (R² = 0.243), and 67.2% in Sleep Quality (R² = 0.672). The Q² values for PTSD Symptom Severity (0.584), Self-Perceived Pain (0.209), and Sleep Quality (0.653) were all greater than zero, indicating that the model has adequate predictive relevance (Hair et al., 2019). Model fit indices indicated acceptable model fit (SRMR = 0.032; NFI = 0.896).

Discussion

This study aimed to address a gap in the literature by comparing previously identified risk factors measured soon after trauma and jointly evaluating their associations with non-remitting PTSD symptom severity six months later. Using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM), we analyzed four latent variables – cognitive attributions, trauma history, emotion responding and regulation, and socioeconomic factors – as well as two mediators, sleep quality and self-perceived pain, and included PTSD symptom severity assessed at two weeks post-trauma as a covariate. By comparing these risk factors simultaneously, this study highlights complex relationships among these factors in relation to subsequent PTSD symptom severity (Kul & Gündoğmuş, 2020; Meyer et al., 2019; Sareen, 2014). Trauma history was directly associated with PTSD symptom severity. Emotion responding and regulation showed no direct association with PTSD symptoms but was associated with PTSD symptom severity through sleep quality. Sleep quality and self-perceived pain partially accounted for the association between early post-trauma cognitive attributions and PTSD symptom severity at six months. Socioeconomic factors were not significantly associated with PTSD symptom severity within this multivariable framework. Notably, these associations were observed after accounting for PTSD symptom severity at two weeks post-trauma, which itself showed a modest but significant direct association with six-month symptom severity (β = 0.075, p = .026), supporting the independent predictive value of the early cognitive, emotional, and trauma-related factors beyond baseline symptom levels. Thus, while trauma history was directly associated with later PTSD symptom severity, early post-trauma cognitive attributions also showed robust associations with PTSD symptoms, both directly and indirectly through sleep quality and self-perceived pain.

In the context of chronic PTSD, cognitive attributions and emotion responding and regulation are consistently linked to the persistence of symptoms over time. Research has shown that maladaptive cognitive processes and difficulties with emotion responding and regulation are linked to greater severity and persistence of chronic PTSD, making recovery more challenging (Miethe et al., 2023; Szabo et al., 2017; Timmer-Murillo et al., 2023). During the acute post-trauma period, these constructs have been related to variability in longer-term PTSD symptom trajectories. Disruptions in sleep quality and the onset of self-perceived pain may serve as early indicators of ongoing cognitive and emotional difficulties and have been linked to subsequent PTSD symptom severity (Asmundson et al., 2002; Defrin et al., 2008; Lancel et al., 2021; McCall et al., 2019).

Cognitive processes play an important role in PTSD’s manifestation and maintenance (Gower et al., 2022; Paunovic, 1998; Wiedemann et al., 2023), reflecting individual differences in how traumatic events are interpreted (Carlson & Dalenberg, 2000; Ouhmad et al., 2023). Negative cognitive attributions measured acutely after trauma, such as feelings of helplessness or self-blame, have been associated with elevated risk for persistent PTSD symptoms (Beierl et al., 2019; Meiser-Stedman et al., 2009a; Sheynin et al., 2024). Cognitive reframing has been effective in reducing PTSD symptoms by altering negative interpretations (Mueser et al., 2015; Nakao et al., 2021). The current study found that negative attributions assessed soon after trauma were associated with subsequent non-remitting PTSD symptom severity, consistent with prior research linking adverse cognitions to PTSD outcomes (Dunmore et al., 2001; Resick et al., 2014). Together, these findings underscore the potential clinical relevance of interventions that emphasise cognitive reframing, restructuring, and reappraisal for trauma survivors.

In addition to the direct association between cognitive attributions and PTSD symptom severity, the present findings suggest that sleep quality may function as an important intervening factor in this relationship. Individuals with elevated PTSD symptoms frequently report co-occurring sleep disturbances, which have been linked to greater symptom burden overall (Arditte Hall et al., 2024; Messman et al., 2023; So et al., 2023). Prior research indicates that negative cognitive attributions (e.g. self-blame) are related to poorer sleep patterns, which have in turn been associated with PTSD symptom severity over time (Aaron et al., 2019; Woodward et al., 2019). Together, these findings are consistent with the interpretation that maladaptive cognitive attributions evident in the acute post-trauma period may be accompanied by disruptions in sleep quality, potentially reflecting heightened cognitive and emotional arousal. From a clinical perspective, early interventions that target maladaptive cognitions and support sleep health may help reduce the risk of persistent PTSD symptoms (Borders et al., 2015; Espie et al., 2016; Germain et al., 2017; Pace-Schott et al., 2015; Rossman, 2019).

Self-perceived pain, like sleep quality, emerged as a crucial mediator in the relationship between cognitive attributions and PTSD development. Persistent pain may exacerbate PTSD symptoms and negative thinking, contributing to a cycle of symptom maintenance (Agar et al., 2006; Carty et al., 2011; Jenewein et al., 2009; M. E. Geisser et al., 1996; Palyo & Beck, 2005). Negative cognitive attributions not only heighten the perception of pain but also intensify its impact on PTSD symptoms (Beck & Clapp, 2011; Kragel et al., 2018; Kummer et al., 2020). These findings suggest that targeting maladaptive cognitive processes may help disrupt this reciprocal cycle, with potential benefits for both pain and PTSD symptom severity (Dow et al., 2023; Galatzer-Levy et al., 2014). Addressing self-perceived pain through mind–body interventions may also support psychological recovery (Hassed, 2013; Zeidan & Vago, 2016). This pattern may be particularly relevant in the present sample of traumatic injury survivors (e.g. motor vehicle collisions). In addition, understanding the interplay between sleep, pain, and cognitive attributions can help address PTSD more effectively (Zanza et al., 2023).

In this study, trauma history was significantly associated with PTSD symptom severity six months following the index trauma (e.g. motor vehicle collision). This aligns with previous findings that cumulative traumatic experiences increase PTSD symptom severity (Breslau et al., 1999). This study did not find a direct relationship between trauma history and sleep quality or pain perception. While previous research indicates trauma victims are more likely to suffer from sleep problems and chronic pain, our use of self-reported measures of sleep and pain may have influenced these results (Gasperi et al., 2021; Levin & Nielsen, 2007; Saranteas et al., 2019). Factors such as lifestyle, health status, and environment can also affect sleep and pain, indirectly influencing PTSD symptoms (Stiltner et al., 2024; Rauvola et al., 2019; Sareen, 2014). Future research may benefit from incorporating more objective assessments of sleep quality, such as actigraphy, pulse oximetry, or bed sensor systems. Moreover, incorporating objective indicators such as electromyography, heart rate variability, or infrared thermography may allow for more comprehensive assessment of pain beyond self-report (Bryant, 2021; Cohen et al., 2023; Weber & Wetter, 2022). Despite the lack of mediating effects of sleep and pain, recognising the cumulative impact of past traumas can help clinicians identify individuals at heightened risk for long-term PTSD and tailor interventions accordingly.

There was no direct association between emotion responding and regulation and PTSD symptom severity. However, this association was indirectly related to PTSD symptom severity through sleep quality, suggesting a sleep-mediated pathway linking emotion dysregulation to PTSD outcomes (Nicholson et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2023). Emotion responding and regulation deficits can significantly impact sleep quality in adults with PTSD, as challenges in managing negative emotions often lead to heightened arousal and persistent rumination, interfering with both sleep initiation and continuity (Woodward, 2022; Xu et al., 2022). Individuals with poor emotion responding and regulation skills may experience elevated physiological activation, including increased cardiac activity and heightened alertness, which hinder relaxation and sleep onset (Bhatia et al., 2018). Disruptions in restorative sleep may impair the body's natural recuperative processes and contribute to greater PTSD symptom burden by sustaining elevated stress levels and diminishing the individual's capacity to process traumatic memories effectively (Colvonen et al., 2019; Koffel et al., 2016; Richards et al., 2020). It is also worth noting that the Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale, used as an indicator of emotion responding and regulation, assesses anhedonia, a construct that conceptually overlaps with the negative mood and cognitions symptom cluster of the PCL-5. This overlap may have contributed to suppression of the direct path from emotion responding and regulation to PTSD symptom severity in the structural model. Addressing both mood and sleep disturbances acutely following trauma, with a focus on emotion responding and regulation and sleep patterns, may reduce PTSD risk (Bryant et al., 2010; Repantis et al., 2020; Swift et al., 2022).

Although socioeconomic factors did not significantly impact PTSD symptom severity in this study, their role in mental health cannot be ignored. Lower levels of education and income have been associated with a higher risk of mental illness, including PTSD (Knifton & Inglis, 2020; Reiss et al., 2019; Thomson et al., 2022). Neighbourhood disadvantages can also affect a wide range of health outcomes, including neurobiological responses to trauma, as well as both psychological and physical recovery challenges, particularly in the context of social vulnerability and firearm-related injuries (Tomas et al., 2022b; Tomas et al., 2024). While this study did not find a significant effect of socioeconomic factors on PTSD symptom severity, this may be due to differences in sample characteristics or the analytic approach used, which may capture associations differently. Specifically, the lack of significant associations may reflect limited variability in socioeconomic status within the sample, given that most participants reported low-income levels, which likely reflects restricted variability in socioeconomic indicators within this sample rather than an absence of meaningful associations. Future research may explore how socioeconomic factors interact with other risk factors, such as trauma type, resilience, and social support, to influence PTSD development.

One strength of this study is the use of partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) to examine multiple prospective predictors and mediating variables of PTSD symptom severity simultaneously (Hair et al., 2014, 2019). This multidimensional perspective provides a comprehensive understanding of individual predictors, including cognitive attributions, emotion responding and regulation, trauma history, sleep, and pain, and allows for an integrated examination of how these constructs relate to one another across different stages of PTSD symptom progression.

The novel contribution of this study lies in its prospective assessment of these predictors during the acute post-trauma period. By examining cognitive attributions, emotion responding and regulation, and related mediators like sleep and pain soon after trauma, this research provides important insights into mechanisms that may contribute to the transition from acute stress reactions to PTSD symptom severity six months post-trauma. This prospective approach is particularly valuable because it identifies key intervention points that could be targeted to mitigate the risk of developing chronic PTSD. For instance, timely interventions aimed at correcting maladaptive cognitive attributions and improving emotion responding and regulation may not only alleviate immediate distress but also reduce the likelihood of persistent PTSD symptoms over time. Early intervention strategies that prioritise sleep quality enhancement and effective pain management are crucial, particularly after assessing previous trauma exposure and cognitive attributions related to the traumatic event (Germain, 2013; Ruddock et al., 2023). Such assessments may aid in identifying individuals at higher risk for maladaptive sleep patterns or heightened pain perception associated with negative cognitive attributions, thereby supporting more targeted intervention strategies (LaMotte et al., 2017; Pace-Schott et al., 2015). These factors may function as important mediating pathways associated with greater PTSD symptom severity if left unaddressed. By addressing these mediators early, it is possible to disrupt the pathways that contribute to chronic PTSD, thus providing a more comprehensive approach to prevention.

By examining the interactions among these constructs, the study underscores the importance of individual pathways while also highlighting the value of considering the overall model. Importantly, this approach allows for identification of risk processes that remain robust when considered concurrently. This holistic approach offers a more nuanced understanding of PTSD development and suggests that early interventions may benefit from being multifaceted, addressing not only cognitive and emotional processes but also the physiological disruptions that often accompany trauma. Together, these findings point to multiple potential avenues for early intervention. Our findings highlight that cognitive attributions, trauma history, emotion responding and regulation, and socioeconomic factors play important and complex roles in PTSD symptom severity (Calhoun et al., 2022; Charuvastra & Cloitre, 2008). Early interventions such as cognitive behavioural therapy, emotion responding and regulation training, and interventions targeting sleep and pain may help mitigate PTSD symptoms effectively (Espie et al., 2016; Fenn & Byrne, 2013; Hassed, 2013; Ouhmad et al., 2023; Rossman, 2019; Darnell et al., 2017; Zeidan & Vago, 2016). Taken together, the present findings support early assessment and the potential implementation of these interventions in acute post-trauma care.

Several limitations should be considered. This study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall and social desirability biases (Althubaiti, 2016; Latkin et al., 2017). The sample was drawn from a specific geographical and cultural context (southeastern Wisconsin in the United States), limiting the generalizability of the findings. Specifically, as the sample primarily consisted of individuals who experienced motor vehicle crashes, these results may not be generalisable to survivors of other types of traumatic events. Additionally, while we considered multiple risk factors and potential mediating variables, other factors such as genetic predisposition or biomarkers may also significantly impact PTSD development (Schultebraucks et al., 2021). Future research should employ more objective measures and explore a broader range of influences on PTSD.

In summary, this study underscores the importance of screening and early intervention focusing on negative cognitive attributions, trauma history, sleep quality, and pain management to prevent non-remitting PTSD. A holistic approach that addresses psychological, physiological, and socioeconomic dimensions can promote recovery. Integrating targeted cognitive-behavioural interventions (e.g. cognitive restructuring), sleep-focused treatments (e.g. CBT-I), and evidence-based pain management strategies into early post-trauma care may help reduce the risk of persistent PTSD symptoms. Policymakers may benefit from continued investment in acute post-trauma mental health services to ensure accessibility and support for trauma survivors.
