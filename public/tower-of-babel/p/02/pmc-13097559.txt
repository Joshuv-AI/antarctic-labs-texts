Collision-free morgan fingerprints: a principled approach to enhance machine learning performance and interpretability in chemistry
Authors: Jibai Li, Xintong Qu, Wenlong Zhang, Shifa Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe success of machine learning in chemistry is fundamentally underpinned by the information fidelity of molecular representations. Despite their widespread adoption for efficiency and interpretability, Morgan fingerprints harbor a long-overlooked and fundamental flaw: bit collisions. This phenomenon erroneously maps distinct chemical substructures to identical positions, systematically corrupting structure–property relationships and severely compromising model interpretability. To address this challenge, we introduce Collision-Free Morgan Fingerprints (CF-MF), a principled framework that guarantees the integrity of substructure information through an adaptive, data-driven sizing mechanism. Through a comprehensive evaluation across 25 diverse datasets (> 50,000 molecules) and multiple machine learning paradigms, we demonstrate that CF-MF delivers consistent and significant performance gains up to 16.81% RMSE reduction in regression and 11.1% accuracy increase in classification. More critically, by eliminating attribution errors caused by collisions, CF-MF fundamentally restores chemical interpretability and expands the reliable prediction domains of models by 60–100%. Our information-theoretic analysis reveals a strong correlation between collision-induced entropy loss and performance degradation (R2 = 0.854, p < 0.001), establishing information fidelity as a fundamental design principle for next-generation molecular representations. It also achieves performance competitive with state-of-the-art deep learning models while retaining the simplicity and intuitiveness of traditional fingerprints. This work provides a more reliable and trustworthy foundation for AI-driven drug discovery, materials science, and environmental assessment. Scientific contribution While bit collisions in Morgan fingerprints have been acknowledged for decades, this study is the first to systematically quantify their impact on machine learning performance and provide a principled, reproducible solution applicable to any molecular dataset. We establish a novel information-theoretic framework that directly links collision-induced entropy loss to predictive degradation, offering the field a quantitative criterion for evaluating molecular representation fidelity. Beyond performance gains, our work uniquely demonstrates that eliminating collisions restores chemically valid SHAP attributions—addressing a critical but previously unrecognized barrier to trustworthy AI interpretation in chemistry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13321-026-01170-0.

Introduction

Machine learning models are now widely used to predict molecular properties across chemistry, materials science, and environmental applications [1–4]. Their success relies not only on model architecture or training strategy, but fundamentally on how molecular structures are represented [5–7]. Incomplete or distorted structural information at the representation stage imposes a fundamental bottleneck on downstream learning algorithms, as their performance becomes inherently constrained by the quality of the molecular description rather than the sophistication of the model itself [8–11].

Morgan fingerprint is one of the most commonly adopted molecular representations because they provide a compact, computationally efficient encoding of local atomic environments [12–14]. Owing to these advantages, they have become a popular choice in many quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) [15] and quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) studies [16, 17], particularly in large-scale screening tasks where interpretability and computational efficiency are important [18–20].

Standard Morgan fingerprints compress molecular substructures into a fixed-length bit vector through hashing. Although this design ensures compact representations, it comes at a cost: chemically distinct substructures can collide onto the same bit index, becoming indistinguishable in the fingerprint space (Fig. 1A) (hash collisions). Consequently, models must use shared parameters to represent fundamentally different structural motifs, which limits their ability to learn precise structure–property relationships.

The implications of hash collisions extend beyond predictive accuracy [21–24]. Feature importance analysis, substructure attribution, and similarity-based procedures such as applicability domain (AD) estimation all implicitly assume that individual fingerprint bits correspond to unique structural information. When this assumption is violated, model interpretation becomes less reliable and similarity measures may no longer faithfully reflect structural relatednes [25–27]. Despite these issues, fingerprint length is often selected using heuristic or conventional values, without explicit consideration of whether the chosen dimensionality is sufficient to avoid collisions for a given dataset and radius [17, 28, 29]. Crucially, even in seemingly sparsely populated or longer fingerprints, collisions persistently occur due to the deterministic nature of the underlying hashing function [30].

In this work, we propose an approach to deliver Collision-Free Morgan Fingerprints (CF-MF). Instead of fixing the fingerprint length a priori, CF-MF determines the minimal length required to eliminate bit collisions at a given radius for a chemical dataset, ensuring a one-to-one correspondence between fingerprint features and extracted molecular substructures. This strategy removes representational ambiguity while maintaining compatibility with standard machine learning workflows. Here, we focus specifically on eliminating folding-induced collisions, namely those arising from mapping distinct substructure identifiers to the same bit positions via modulo operations during the construction of fixed-length fingerprint vectors. Importantly, the pre-hashing procedure is kept identical to that of conventional Morgan fingerprints, ensuring a fair comparison and maintaining practical compatibility with existing workflows.

We systematically evaluate CF-MF across 25 chemically diverse datasets using multiple machine learning models. The results demonstrate that eliminating bit collisions leads to improvements in prediction accuracy, more stable feature attribution, and more reliable applicability domain estimation. Furthermore, we analyze the effect of bit collisions from an information-theoretic perspective, showing that collision-induced entropy loss provides a coherent explanation for the observed degradation in model performance. Together, these results highlight the importance of representation fidelity and suggest that addressing information loss at the fingerprint level is a practical and effective way to improve molecular machine learning models.

Results

Prevalence of bit collisions and CF-MF framework

A systematic survey across all 25 diverse chemical datasets highlights the widespread and non-uniform nature of bit collisions (Fig. 1B). At a fixed radius of 3, increasing the fingerprint length effectively reduces the bit-collision rate. For a length of 512 bits, collision rates across all datasets ranged from 11 to 75%. This range decreased to 3%–51% for 1024 bits. However, even at 2048 bits, collisions were not fully eliminated, with rates still ranging from approximately 1%–23%. Notably, these collisions are not randomly distributed across the fingerprint space. As summarized in Table S1, the fingerprint positions with the highest collision probabilities are associated with a limited set of chemically informative substructures, including secondary and tertiary amines, ester and lactone motifs, peroxy and sulfur-containing linkages, alcohol functionalities, and halogenated alkyl fragments. These motifs correspond to high-information-density chemical features that frequently occur in chemically diverse datasets and play an important role in determining molecular properties [31, 32].

To address this pervasive issue, we introduce a principled, adaptive mechanism to determine the minimal fingerprint length required to eliminate all bit collisions for a given radius across an entire molecular dataset. While increasing fingerprint length is intuitively essential to avoid collisions, systematically verifying sufficiency remains challenging. As an illustrative example, consider 2-nitropropane (Fig. 1C). When generating count-based Morgan fingerprints with a radius of 1 and a length of 100 bits, inspection of bit position 15 revealed a collision: it contained multiple distinct atom groups with different radii (e.g., (3,0) versus (0,1) and (2,1)). This demonstrates that chemically distinct substructures were erroneously mapped to the same bit position. By extending the fingerprint length to 300 bits, this specific collision was resolved: position 15 then contained only (3,0), while (0,1) and (2,1) were remapped to position 115. This example underscores that verifying bit-collisions at a given fingerprint length involves meticulously checking whether atom groups with different radii are mapped to the same bit position.

Building upon this principle, our framework systematically processes an entire molecular dataset. We begin by generating fingerprints for all molecules using an initial length (e.g., 100 bits) at the specified radii (radius = 1, 2, or 3). Each generated fingerprint is then meticulously inspected for the presence of bit collisions. If any collisions are detected for any molecule within the dataset, we iteratively increase the fingerprint length in predefined increments (e.g., 10 bits) and regenerate fingerprints for all molecules. This iterative process continues until a fingerprint length is identified at which no collisions occur for any molecules in the entire dataset. Once this optimal collision-free length is determined, the final CF-MFs are generated (Fig. 1D). Our results show that these collision-free lengths across these 25 datasets range from 1000 to 8000 bits for radius 1, and extend up to 240,000 bits for radius 3 (Fig. 1E). To further enhance computational efficiency for subsequent machine learning model training, unoccupied bit positions are removed, thereby reducing the fingerprint vector dimensionality. The resulting compact fingerprints constitute the final CF-MFs used for model development.

The dropped bit positions are not constant and could not be described by a single fixed range. Using the codes we provided can see the final bit positions in the generated CF-MF. It should be noted that the removed bit positions may differ between datasets, as each dataset has its own unique minimal length for CF-MF. Table S2 reports the number of dropped bits across all datasets under different radius settings (see Figure S1). CF-MF is conceptually equivalent to an unfold count-ECFP representation. To explicitly verify this equivalence, we compared the dimensionality of CF-MF with the number of unique substructure identifiers observed in each dataset. As shown in Table S2, the feature dimensionality of CF-MF after removing all zero-valued columns exactly matches the corresponding number of unique substructures. This result confirms that CF-MF is equivalent to an unfolded count-ECFP representation.

Systematic performance gains and competitive benchmarking of CF-MF

Our comprehensive evaluation, spanning 25 chemically and structurally diverse datasets and employing three distinct machine learning paradigms, unequivocally demonstrates that CF-MF consistently and substantially outperform traditional collision-affected fingerprints. The chemical structural diversity is characterized by the average Tanimoto index, as shown in Fig. 1F. Most datasets exhibit an average Tanimoto index below 0.15, indicating that molecules within each dataset are highly diverse and structurally distinct from one another. This low similarity score reflects the rich chemical diversity present within individual datasets [33–36]. We also performed t-SNE dimensionality reduction on standard MF fingerprints to check the molecular diversity between datasets. As shown in Fig. 1G, molecules from different datasets occupy varying regions of the chemical space, highlighting the structural differences between datasets. It should be noted that t-SNE is presented here as an intuitive visualization tool rather than a rigorous metric for quantifying diversity, given the inherent information loss during dimensionality reduction. Nevertheless, we include this visualization as a supplementary reference, as it may help readers gain a preliminary understanding of how molecules from different datasets are distributed in the reduced chemical space.

Figure 2A and Figure S2-S9 presents a heatmap visualization comparing the predictive performance of CA-MF-based and CF-MF-based models on the test sets (R2-test and F1-test score) across both regression and classification datasets. The details of each model performance are listed in “ModelPerformance.csv” in SI The results demonstrate that model performance varies considerably depending on the selected radius and fingerprint length for each dataset. Notably, models utilizing CF-MF consistently got higher R2test and F1 scores compared to their CA-MF counterparts, clearly demonstrating the superiority of CF-MF. This enhancement in predictive accuracy is observed consistently across both regression and classification tasks, highlighting the universal advantage of mitigating bit-collision effects in molecular fingerprint-based machine learning models.

The observed performances exhibited distinct and interpretable patterns across different machine learning algorithm classes (Fig. 2B, C and Tables S3-S8). Linear models, exemplified by Ridge regression, demonstrated the most pronounced improvements, achieving a mean RMSE reduction of 16.81% at radius 3 and 512-bit length (Fig. 2B and Table S3). This significant enhancement reflects their inherent limitations in correcting for feature conflation through nonlinear transformations, making them highly sensitive to the quality of the input representation. Support Vector Regression (SVR) achieved intermediate gains, with a mean RMSE reduction of 10.43%, indicating a partial compensation through kernel mappings, yet still constrained by the underlying residual information loss. Conversely, tree-based ensemble methods, such as CatBoost, yielded smaller but consistently significant improvements, with a mean RMSE reduction of 8.55%. This suggests that while their robust feature interaction modeling offers some resilience to representational noise, their performance remains sensitive to the fundamental quality of the input data.

Regression tasks, which inherently demand precise numerical estimation, consistently exhibited substantially greater improvements with CF-MF compared to classification tasks (Tables S3-S8). Across the evaluated regression datasets, RMSE reductions ranged from 0.21% to 16.81%, while the results for the classification dataset were more volatile. In some cases, eliminating bit collisions led to a slight decrease in model accuracy, but the overall results showed that the classification dataset had the highest improvement in accuracy, up to 11.1%. These findings highlight the relatively higher tolerance of categorical outputs to feature ambiguity, in stark contrast to the stringent demands on the representational accuracy of continuous-value prediction. Furthermore, the potential of performance enhancement consistently scaled with increasing fingerprint radius (Fig. 2B, C, S2-S9), which directly correlates with increasing substructure complexity and the inherent likelihood of bit collisions at larger radii. For example, the improvements at radius 1 averaged between 0.21% and 7.79% across algorithms, but were increased markedly to 4.66%–16.81% at radius 3. It should be noted that all-zero columns were removed for both CF-MF and CA-MF during preprocessing, ensuring that the performance improvement observed with CF-MF is attributable to the elimination of bit collisions rather than the removal of sparse features.

These performance improvements were rigorously validated using comprehensive statistical analysis following Ash et al. (2025) [37]. Cross-dataset analysis across 20 independent benchmarks demonstrates highly significant CF-MF advantages for all algorithms (all p < 0.001, Table S9). All results remain highly significant after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (α_adjusted = 0.0167). The consistently large effect sizes (d = 1.52–4.07), coupled with 95% confidence intervals excluding zero and replication across 90–95% of datasets, provide strong evidence for the substantial practical advantages of CF-MF fingerprints.

To robustly contextualize CF-MF within the broader landscape of molecular machine learning, we expanded the benchmark in Fig. 2D to include representative baselines spanning graph neural networks (GCN and GIN), directed message passing (DMPNN), a classical graph-kernel method (WL-subtree kernel with SVR; WL-SVR), and a pretrained Transformer (ChemBERTa). All non-Transformer baselines were evaluated under a unified model-selection protocol with systematic hyperparameter optimization, thereby reducing sensitivity to manual parameter choices and enabling a more faithful comparison of modeling capacity.

Across the 10 diverse regression datasets, CF-MF shows overall better performance (Table S10). In terms of per-dataset best results, CF-MF attains the lowest RMSE on 7/10 datasets, whereas DMPNN achieves the best RMSE on the remaining 3 datasets. Notably, when aggregating performance across all datasets, the mean RMSE further supports this conclusion: CF-MF (r = 2) achieves the best overall mean RMSE 0.91294, closely followed by CF-MF (r = 3) 0.91348, and then DMPNN 0.93441. In contrast, the tuned GNN baselines GCN and GIN yield higher mean RMSE values (1.18054 and 1.20638, respectively), while ChemBERTa and WL-SVR perform substantially worse on average (1.59322 and 1.91483, respectively). Together, these results indicate that collision-free fingerprinting provides a highly competitive representation for molecular property prediction, even relative to carefully tuned deep message-passing models.

Importantly, ChemBERTa leverages large-scale external self-supervised pretraining and is fine-tuned on each target dataset [38, 39], whereas CF-MF paired with classical machine learning models is trained solely on the target data without any external pretraining. Therefore, our goal is not to claim superiority under matched pretraining budgets, but to benchmark CF-MF against widely used off-the-shelf representation paradigms as they are commonly applied in practice. Within this setting, the strong mean and per-dataset performance of CF-MF, particularly with simple linear models, highlights a practical advantage: it can deliver high accuracy without reliance on complex architectures or external pretraining, while preserving interpretability and supporting transparent applicability-domain analysis.

Overall, Fig. 2D demonstrates that CF-MF alleviates a fundamental representational bottleneck of conventional hashed fingerprints, namely bit collisions, thereby narrowing the performance gap between classical and modern approaches. This offers a scalable, interpretable, and computationally efficient alternative that retains the simplicity of fingerprint-based learning while achieving performance comparable to, and often exceeding, state-of-the-art deep molecular models.

The effect of bit-collision on model performance in terms of contaminants

We meticulously investigated the differential impact of CF-MF on various contaminant groups within machine learning model development. Utilizing the ESOL dataset as a representative case study, we observed that CF-MF-based models consistently yielded lower RMSEtest values compared to their CA-MF counterparts across all contaminants, ML algorithms, and tested fingerprint configurations (radius 1; lengths 512 and 1024; Fig. 3C). To gain deeper insight, compounds were categorized into two groups: those inherently contain bit-collisions (CA-compounds) and those do not (CF-compounds). Unexpectedly, both CF-compounds and CA-compounds demonstrated reduced RMSE values when modeled with CF-MF. This crucial finding indicates that CF-MF significantly enhances prediction accuracy not only for compounds whose representations are directly compromised by bit-collisions (as anticipated), but also, surprisingly, for those compounds that were already collision-free, suggesting a broader, more fundamental improvement in representation quality.

Restored chemical interpretability through collision elimination

A pivotal advantage of CF-MF lies in their capacity to fundamentally restore and significantly enhance chemical interpretability, a crucial aspect often severely compromised by bit collisions in traditional molecular representations. Our detailed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis unequivocally reveals that bit collisions systematically distort learned structure–property relationships, thereby compelling machine learning models to infer patterns that can directly violate fundamental chemical principles [40].

For Niridazole, the ureido group (–NH–CO–NH–) and its adjacent nitrogen atoms are well-established as potent hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, which significantly enhance aqueous solubility (Fig. 4A) [41, 42]. When utilizing CF-MF, our model accurately assigns positive SHAP values (+ 0.12, + 0.10) to these chemically relevant substructures, precisely reflecting their positive contribution to solubility. In stark contrast, when these distinct substructures undergo bit collision within CA-MF, the model is erroneously compelled to assign a single, misleading negative attribution (−0.026). This incorrect assignment suggests that these polar groups decrease solubility—a conclusion that directly contradicts fundamental chemical principles governing intermolecular interactions and highlights the severe interpretability failures of collision-affected representations.

D The percentage of cases where the applicability domain (AD) of CF-MF-based models is larger than that of CA-MF-based models across different machine learning algorithms for both regression and classification datasets

In the case of m-Chlorobromobenzene, both chlorine (Cl) and bromine (Br) substituents are known to decrease aqueous solubility, with bromine typically exerting a more pronounced effect due to its higher lipophilicity (Fig. 4B) [43]. Our CF-MF-based model precisely captures this subtle yet chemically critical distinction by assigning appropriately scaled negative SHAP values (−0.01 for Cl, −0.59 for Br). This accurate differentiation reflects their individual contributions to lipophilicity. Conversely, traditional CA-MF-based model conflates both halogens into a single, undifferentiated attribution (−0.66), thereby completely losing this crucial chemical rank ordering and fundamentally misrepresenting their distinct individual contributions to the molecular property.

Monotropitoside, a representative glycoside, inherently contains both hydrophilic phenolic groups and hydrophobic aromatic carbons, which are known to exert opposing effects on aqueous solubility (Fig. 4C) [44]. Our CF-MF-based model accurately differentiates these contrasting contributions, correctly assigning positive SHAP values (+ 0.01) to the hydrophilic hydroxyl (OH) groups and negative SHAP values (−0.28) to the hydrophobic aromatic carbons. This precise attribution reflects their true chemical roles. In stark contrast, the presence of bit collisions in CA-MF compels the model to erroneously treat both types of substructures as equally hydrophobic (−0.58). This fundamentally violates the well-established chemical principle that phenolic groups enhance aqueous solubility, thereby demonstrating how collisions can lead to directly contradictory, chemically unsound, and misleading attributions.

To rigorously validate the reliability of the SHAP-based chemical interpretations presented in Fig. 4A–C, we conducted comprehensive stability and faithfulness analyses for all three fingerprint methods employed in our study: CF-MF, CA-MF-1024, and CA-MF-512.

We assessed the consistency of SHAP feature attributions by computing SHAP values for the three representative compounds (Niridazole, m-Chlorobromobenzene, and Monotropitoside) across our 5-fold cross-validation framework. The mean Jaccard similarity of top-10 SHAP-identified features was 0.693 ± 0.075 for CF-MF, 0.715 ± 0.068 for CA-MF-1024, and 0.675 ± 0.060 for CA-MF-512 (Table S11). These results demonstrate reasonable attribution stability across all three methods, with Jaccard similarities exceeding 0.65 [45]. The consistent values across different data partitions indicate that SHAP reliably identifies similar important features regardless of the specific train-test split, validating the robustness of our feature attribution methodology.

We performed both removal and sufficiency tests to validate that SHAP values accurately reflect the genuine influence of features on model predictions. In removal tests, masking the top-10 SHAP-identified features resulted in substantial prediction changes across all methods, with mean ΔPred values of 1.47 ± 0.75 (CF-MF), 1.43 ± 0.70 (CA-MF-1024), and 1.48 ± 0.69 (CA-MF-512), confirming that the identified features substantively influence model predictions (Table S12). These large prediction changes when key features are removed demonstrate that our SHAP attributions genuinely capture features that drive model behavior.

Sufficiency tests, which measure how well the top-10 features alone can reproduce original predictions, revealed an interpretable pattern reflecting molecular structural complexity. For simpler molecules such as m-Chlorobromobenzene (compound 49), all methods achieved high sufficiency scores (≈0.79–0.80), indicating that the top-10 features capture most of the predictive information. In contrast, structurally complex molecules like Monotropitoside (compound 120), a glycoside containing multiple diverse functional groups, exhibited lower sufficiency across all methods (0.16–0.39). This pattern is chemically intuitive: simpler molecules with fewer structural motifs can be well-represented by a small number of key features, while complex molecules with multiple interacting functional groups require a broader set of features to fully capture their structure–property relationships. The intermediate complexity molecule Niridazole showed correspondingly intermediate sufficiency values (0.12–0.76, depending on method), further supporting that our faithfulness metrics appropriately reflect task complexity rather than methodology artifacts.

These comprehensive validation results confirm that the SHAP-based chemical interpretations presented in Fig. 4A–C are derived from stable and faithful attribution methods. The consistent stability metrics and substantial removal effects across all three fingerprint approaches support the reliability and trustworthiness of our mechanistic chemical insights, thereby strengthening the foundation for our claims regarding the restoration of chemical interpretability through collision-free molecular fingerprints.

Expanded applicability domains enable broader reliable predictions

Beyond significantly enhancing predictive accuracy and interpretability, the systematic elimination of bit collisions facilitated by CF-MF profoundly expands the applicability domains (AD) of machine learning models. The AD, a critical concept in cheminformatics, is rigorously defined as the specific chemical space within which model predictions can be considered statistically reliable (Fig. 4D) [46]. This crucial expansion of the AD directly stems from CF-MF's ability to enable more accurate and unambiguous molecular similarity calculations. When distinct substructures are forced into collision in traditional representations, the assessment of molecular similarity becomes inherently ambiguous, which consequently leads to an artificially restricted and less reliable AD.

Across all evaluated datasets, CF-MF-based models consistently demonstrated larger applicability domains compared to their CA-MF-based counterparts across different machine learning algorithms, fingerprint radii, and lengths. The percentage of datasets where CF-MF-based models exhibited superior applicability domains ranged from 60 to 100% (Tables S13-S14). This phenomenon was most pronounced at higher fingerprint radii, precisely where substructure complexity and bit collision rates are inherently highest. For instance, at radius 3, a remarkable 100% of the regression datasets showed improved AD for both Ridge and SVR models, with 95% demonstrating improvement for CatBoost. This trend unequivocally highlights the increasing and critical benefit of CF-MF as molecular complexity and, consequently, collision probability increase. The substantial expansion of AD carries profound practical implications for real-world chemical discovery and assessment. In drug discovery, an expanded AD enables the reliable evaluation of a greater number of chemical candidates with the same training data, thereby accelerating lead optimization and candidate selection [47]. Similarly, for environmental assessment, this translates to robust predictions across broader ranges of potential contaminants, all while maintaining stringent statistical rigor [48].

Discussion

Our findings indicate that bit collisions in Morgan fingerprints impose a non-negligible limitation on molecular machine learning models, and that this effect can be substantially reduced by eliminating folding-induced collisions. By systematically eliminating these pervasive information bottlenecks through our proposed methodology, we achieve consistent and significant improvements across crucial aspects of molecular machine learning: enhanced model performance, restored interpretability, and expanded AD. These compelling results, observed across diverse chemical domains and machine learning algorithms, hold profound and transformative implications for the entire field of AI-driven chemical discovery.

Methodological implications for molecular representation learning

The differential performance benefits observed across various machine learning algorithm types provide profound insights into how distinct approaches contend with corrupted molecular representations. CatBoost, despite its advanced regularization capabilities and sophisticated handling of overfitting through gradient boosting techniques, consistently exhibited performance improvements when utilizing CF-MF. This critical finding strongly suggests that even highly sophisticated algorithms with robust overfitting control cannot fully overcome intrinsic information loss that originates at the foundational representation level—a principle that is likely to extend beyond Morgan fingerprints to encompass other molecular encoding schemes.

Our meticulously developed information-theoretic framework provides a principled and quantitative approach to understanding and predicting the precise impact of representation corruption. The exceptionally strong correlation observed between collision-induced entropy loss and performance degradation (R2 = 0.854) compellingly suggests that information preservation should be elevated to a fundamental design principle for all molecular representations. This principle holds broad applicability, extending beyond traditional fingerprints to encompass graph-based and other advanced encoding schemes, thereby guiding the development of future, more robust molecular AI.

Implications for trustworthy AI in chemistry

The interpretability enhancements provided by CF-MF directly address a critical and escalating need as machine learning increasingly transitions towards more explainable, transparent, and inherently trustworthy predictions in chemistry. Our in-depth analysis unequivocally reveals that bit collisions are not merely a source of noise; instead, they systematically corrupt the learned structure–property relationships, compelling models to derive patterns that directly violate fundamental chemical principles [49]. This profound finding is particularly significant for the advancement of trustworthy AI in chemical sciences, especially for the high-stakes applications where model transparency, reliability, and accountability are absolutely essential for regulatory approval, scientific acceptance, and critical decision-making. In domains such as drug safety assessment, environmental regulation, and advanced materials certification, the ability to trace predictions back to chemically meaningful substructures is indispensable. CF-MF's unique capacity to provide such clear, accurate, and chemically sound attributions builds crucial confidence in model reasoning, facilitates expert review, and thereby fosters greater trust in AI-driven chemical insights.

Limitations and future directions

While CF-MF significantly advance molecular representation learning, inherent limitations of our current approach also highlight promising future research avenues. Firstly, concerning dataset scale, for exceptionally large molecular datasets, while CF-MF utilizes efficient sparse representations, the iterative determination of optimal collision-free lengths can be computationally intensive. Future work could explore more efficient length determination algorithms or adaptive encoding strategies to further optimize performance for ultra-large datasets. Meanwhile, future research efforts can also compare various fingerprint types (RDK, atomic pairs, topological torsions, MACCS and so on) to examine molecular representations from broader perspective. Secondly, in dynamic chemical spaces, adding new molecules may necessitate recalculating optimal collision-free lengths, though dynamic updating mechanisms could mitigate this minor overhead. We recommend monitoring OOV rates as an AD metric and periodically rebuilding the vocabulary when significant new chemical space is encountered. The trade-off between vocabulary coverage and retraining cost should be considered based on the specific application requirements.

Beyond our fixed radii (1–3), future research should explore adaptive radius selection, dynamically determined by dataset complexity and target property characteristics. CF-MF also holds substantial potential as a high-fidelity input for advanced deep learning architectures (e.g., GNNs, transformers), synergistically combining information fidelity with powerful pattern recognition for more robust models. Developing hierarchical CF-MFs could capture both localized and global features, offering richer representations. Investigating CF-MF for transfer learning to smaller, specialized datasets could significantly improve performance and data efficiency. This study employed random data splits to isolate the effect of bit collisions on model performance. While scaffold splits are commonly used to evaluate generalization to structurally novel compounds, they introduce additional variability that may confound the specific effect under investigation. Future work could extend this analysis using scaffold splits to assess whether the advantages of collision-free fingerprints persist under more challenging generalization scenarios. Our validation protocol employs 5 independent train-test splits per dataset, providing adequate statistical power (0.46–0.67) for detecting the large effect sizes observed (Cohen's d = 1.52–4.07). The cross-dataset analysis across 20 independent benchmarks serves as the primary statistical inference (all p < 0.001), with per-dataset analysis providing supporting evidence for consistency. Recent research recommend 20–25 samples from 5 × 5 repeated cross-validation for optimal statistical power [37]. While our two-level testing framework balances statistical rigor with computational feasibility across diverse datasets and multiple algorithmic paradigms, future work could employ the recommended repeated cross-validation protocol to further strengthen statistical inference, particularly for detecting smaller effect sizes. Finally, expanded AD enable sophisticated uncertainty quantification and active learning strategies, while automated optimization of CF-MF parameters based on task requirements could lead to highly tailored representations.

Methods

Dataset curation and characterization

We meticulously assembled a comprehensive collection of 25 diverse datasets from publicly available and peer-reviewed sources, specifically chosen to encompass a broad spectrum of chemical applications (Table S15) [17, 50–56]. This collection included 20 regression tasks and 5 classification tasks, spanning critical areas such as environmental fate properties, biological activities, physicochemical characteristics, and analytical measurements. The total dataset size comprised 52,847 unique molecules, ensuring a realistic representation of chemical diversity encountered in research.

CF-MF generation algorithm

The CF-MF proposed in this study is generated using the standard ECFP algorithm, which has been well-established and formally described in previous literature [30]. Rather than introducing a new fingerprint encoding scheme, our contribution focuses on determining the appropriate fingerprint length to avoid bit collisions. Specifically, we developed an iterative procedure to identify the minimum length at which no two distinct substructures are mapped to the same bit position.

For each dataset and a specified radius (1,2,3), we implemented an iterative length-determination algorithm designed to identify the minimum required fingerprint length that guarantees the complete absence of bit collisions. The algorithm proceeds as follows:

All chemical information processing was performed using RDKit (version 2023.09.1). Both CF-MF and CA-MF were generated with RDKit’s GetHashedMorganFingerprint function, which encodes substructure identifiers as 32-bit unsigned integers in counting mode (fromCounts = True) and with chirality disabled (useChirality = False). Hydrogen atoms were treated implicitly, and no special handling was applied for isotopes or tautomer normalization. The CF-MF generation procedure is deterministic, for a given dataset, the collision-free length and substructure → bit mapping are uniquely determined by the molecular structures and remain consistent across runs. Importantly, CF-MF and CA-MF share the same bit-to-substructure mapping procedure through RDKit’s bitInfo parameter, ensuring consistent fragment attribution between the two representations. An illustrative example of this process is provided in the accompanying code.

Definition of bit collision

In this study, "bit collision" refers to post-hash folding collisions, where distinct substructural features are mapped to the same bit position due to the modulo operation used in fixed-length fingerprint generation. This is distinct from pre-hash collisions, which may occur during the initial encoding of substructures into hash values. Our iterative length-determination procedure specifically addresses post-hash folding collisions by increasing the fingerprint length until all unique substructure identifiers occupy distinct bit positions.

Information-theoretic analysis

To quantitatively assess the information content and loss within molecular representations, we employed a rigorous information-theoretic framework. The information loss ΔI specifically attributable to bit collisions was quantified by comparing the entropy of the collision-free representation with that of the traditional, collision-affected representation [58]. This was calculated as: ΔI=HCF-MF-HCA-MF, where H(CF-MF) denotes the entropy of the Collision-Free Morgan Fingerprint and H(CA-MF) represents the entropy of the standard collision-affected Morgan fingerprint. A positive ΔI indicates information gain from eliminating collisions.

To evaluate the relevance of the preserved information to target properties, we computed the mutual information I(X;Y) between fingerprint features (X) and the corresponding target properties (Y). Mutual information measures the reduction in uncertainty about a variable Y given knowledge of another variable X. This analysis was performed using sklearn.feature_selection.mutual_info_regression for continuous targets and sklearn.feature_selection.mutual_info_classif for categorical targets. Higher mutual information values indicate that the fingerprint features are more informative and relevant for predicting the target property.

Machine learning model development

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of CF-MF, we employed three distinct machine learning algorithms, each representing a different paradigm of model learning. This selection allowed for a robust assessment of CF-MF's benefits across diverse algorithmic complexities and assumptions.

We selected Ridge Regression as a linear model incorporating L2 regularization to represent parameter-efficient and interpretable approaches [59]. This algorithm is particularly sensitive to feature quality and collinearity, making it an ideal candidate to reveal the direct impact of collision elimination. Support Vector Machines (SVM) were chosen as kernel-based nonlinear models to represent flexible nonlinear approaches capable of capturing complex relationships [60]. For regression tasks, Support Vector Regression (SVR) was utilized, while Support Vector Classification (SVC) was used for classification tasks. Finally, CatBoost, a state-of-the-art gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble, was selected to represent highly performant and robust ensemble methods known for their ability to handle various data types and feature interactions [61].

Each dataset was split into training and testing sets using an 80:20 ratio. For classification datasets, stratified splitting was employed to ensure proportional representation of classes in both training and testing sets [62]. To eliminate the influence of random variation, this entire process was repeated five times with different random seeds, generating five distinct train-test pairs for each dataset. Models were built independently on each train-test pair, and the reported results represent the average performance across all five iterations. To guarantee fair and robust comparisons between CF-MF and CA-MF evaluations, identical train/test splits were meticulously maintained across all experiments. Optimal hyperparameters for each model were determined using Bayesian optimization [63], implemented via hyperopt, coupled with 5-fold cross-validation performed exclusively on the training data. For deep learning and kernel baselines reported in the comparative benchmark (GCN/GIN/DMPNN/WL-SVR), we used the same Bayesian optimization workflow and matched tuning budget, to minimize performance variance arising from arbitrary hyperparameter choices. Comparison with alternative representations

To comprehensively contextualize the performance of CF-MF, we conducted a comparative benchmark against representative molecular representation paradigms spanning graph neural networks, message-passing architectures, graph kernels, and pretrained Transformers. This comparison was performed on representative subsets of 10 datasets selected to span a broad range of physicochemical and environmental endpoints, including solubility, bioconcentration, partition coefficients, reaction kinetics, and biotransformation, while maintaining high internal structural diversity. This selection strategy ensures that the comparison with alternative molecular representations is not biased toward a specific property type or narrowly clustered chemical structures, but instead reflects performance across diverse tasks and structurally heterogeneous datasets.

We evaluated three widely used graph learning architectures: GCN [64], GIN [65], and DMPNN6. For these models, molecules were represented as graphs with atoms as nodes and covalent bonds as edges. We used standard atom- and bond-level descriptors as input features (e.g., atom type/atomic number, hybridization, formal charge, aromaticity, and bond type/conjugation/ring membership). GCN and GIN were implemented using the PyTorch Geometric framework, whereas DMPNN followed directed message passing along bonds, a commonly adopted paradigm for molecular property prediction.

In addition to neural message passing, we included a classical graph-kernel method, the Weisfeiler–Lehman (WL) subtree kernel, combined with support vector regression (WL-SVR) [66]. The WL kernel compares molecular graphs through iterative neighborhood label refinement and computes a precomputed kernel matrix, on which SVR is trained. This baseline provides a principled non-neural reference for graph-structured molecular learning.

We also included ChemBERTa, a Transformer-based molecular language model. ChemBERTa leverages large-scale self-supervised pretraining on external unlabeled SMILES corpora and is subsequently fine-tuned on each target dataset [38, 39, 67]. In this work, we used publicly available pretrained weights and performed downstream fine-tuning using SMILES sequences with the standard tokenization provided by the HuggingFace transformers library. Because ChemBERTa benefits from external pretraining, strict pretraining parity is not enforced between ChemBERTa and the from-scratch baselines; our intent is to benchmark CF-MF against widely used off-the-shelf representation paradigms as they are typically applied in practice. To avoid over-interpretation, all related statements in the manuscript are formulated in terms of downstream fine-tuning performance rather than total pretraining cost.

For all non-Transformer baselines, hyperparameter optimization and evaluation followed the same protocol described in the “Machine Learning Model Development” section, using identical data splits and a matched tuning budget to reduce variance arising from arbitrary parameter choices and ensure fair model selection.

Model interpretation and applicability domain analysis

To gain deeper insights into the learned structure–property relationships and to quantify the reliability of predictions, we conducted comprehensive model interpretation and applicability domain analyses.

SHAP values were computed to interpret the contributions of individual features (fingerprint bits) to model predictions [68]. For tree-based models (CatBoost), TreeExplainer was utilized, while for linear models (Ridge Regression), LinearExplainer was employed. For visualization purposes, SHAP values were meticulously mapped back to the corresponding molecular structures using RDKit's visualization capabilities. This allowed for the clear highlighting of atomic contributions through intuitive color-coding, facilitating chemical interpretation.

We validated SHAP reliability through stability and faithfulness analyses [69, 70]. Stability was assessed using Jaccard similarity of top-10 features across 5-fold cross-validation (10 pairwise comparisons per compound; Jaccard > 0.65 indicates reasonable stability). Faithfulness was evaluated via removal tests (masking top-10 features, measuring ΔPred) and sufficiency tests (using only top-10 features, measuring prediction similarity). All analyses were performed independently for CF-MF, CA-MF-1024, and CA-MF-512.

Consistent with our prior research [17], the AD was defined by comparing the similarity between a query compound and the training dataset compounds, rather than conformal prediction frameworks, measured using the Tanimoto index (Eq. 5). For CF-MF-based model, the similarity is calculated with the CF-MF, while for CA-MF-based model, the similarity is calculated with the CA-MF. A predefined similarity threshold was established to determine whether a query compound lies within the AD. Predictions for query compounds exhibiting similarity above this threshold are considered reliable and within the AD; conversely, predictions for compounds below this threshold are considered unreliable and outside the AD. A lower threshold value corresponds to a broader AD. Detailed procedures for threshold determination can be found in our previous publication [17].

Statistical analysis

To ensure the robustness and reliability of our findings, a comprehensive statistical analysis was performed on all experimental results. Performance differences between models utilizing CF-MF and CA-MF were rigorously assessed using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. This non-parametric test was chosen for its robustness to non-normal data distributions, which are often encountered in chemical datasets. Each comparison was conducted across the 5 independent data splits to ensure consistency. Beyond statistical significance, the practical importance of observed improvements was quantified using Cohen’s d as the effect size measure. This provided a standardized metric for the magnitude of the difference between CF-MF and traditional fingerprints.

Statistical significance was assessed using a two-stage testing framework following the recent research [37]. First, a binomial test was applied across all 20 independent datasets (n = 20) to evaluate whether the proportion of datasets favoring CF-MF exceeded the random expectation (null hypothesis: p = 0.5). Second, a one-sample t-test was conducted to determine whether the mean performance improvement across datasets was significantly greater than zero. This design treats datasets as independent experimental units, accounts for between-dataset variability, and provides an adequate sample size for robust inference at the dataset level.

Within each dataset, performance metrics were computed across five independent train–test splits, and paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied. This non-parametric test evaluates within-dataset consistency without assuming normality. Given n = 5, the minimum achievable p-value for this test is 0.0625, which we report transparently.

Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s d, with conventional thresholds for small (d ≥ 0.2), medium (d ≥ 0.5), and large (d ≥ 0.8) effects. We further performed post-hoc power analyses to assess whether the available sample size was sufficient to detect the observed effects. For comparisons among the three main algorithms, Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple testing (α_adjusted = 0.0167), whereas Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was used for exploratory, dataset-level analyses.

Recent research [37] recommend drawing 20–25 samples from repeated cross-validation (e.g., 5 × 5 CV) to achieve adequate statistical power. In this study, we adopt a complementary two-level strategy: cross-dataset testing (n = 20 datasets) serves as the primary basis for statistical inference, while within-dataset analyses (n = 5 splits) provide supporting evidence. This approach balances statistical rigor with the computational demands of large-scale evaluation (20 datasets × 3 algorithms × multiple configurations). Post-hoc power analysis confirms sufficient power to detect the observed large effects (Cohen's d = 1.52–4.07; power = 0.46–0.67), and the cross-dataset tests yield highly significant results (all p < 0.001).

Software implementation and code availability

The core algorithm for generating CF-MF has been developed into an open-source Python package, bit-collision-free-MF. This package is readily available for installation via PyPI (https://pypi.org/project/bit-collision-free-mf/ and https://github.com/Shifa-Zhong/CF-MF-project.git), facilitating easy adoption by the broader cheminformatics community: pip install bit-collision-free-MF. A web application based on CF-MF has been deployed at www.ai4env.world, enabling free prediction of 25 properties and activities for any given compound. The codebase further provides complete vocabulary files for all datasets at each of the three radius settings, as well as the associated training and statistical testing code, allowing full and accurate reproduction of all fingerprint representations and model results.
