Scaffold vaccination for prevention of orthopedic device infection
Authors: Alexander M. Tatara, Shanda Lightbown, Shawn Kang, Wei-Hung Jung, Hamza Ijaz, Jean C. Lee, Sandra B. Nelson, Michael Super, David J. Mooney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceInfections of orthopedic devices are difficult to treat and require additional surgery and long antimicrobial treatment courses. Preventative strategies, such as vaccination, are attractive given that most infections are caused by staphylococci. Conventional vaccine technologies have failed in preclinical studies and clinical trials to prevent surgical site infection by Staphylococcus aureus. We developed an alternative strategy using a biomaterial-based vaccine consisting of a biodegradable scaffold system to increase vaccine efficacy. This injectable scaffold system improved cell-mediated immunity and decreased bacterial burden in a murine model of orthopedic device infection against different clinically relevant strains of S. aureus.

Staphylococcus aureus is the leading bacterial cause of mortality and is associated with more than one million deaths per year globally (1). Implanted medical devices increase the risk of S. aureus infection (2) and infection of orthopedic devices is particularly devastating due to need for subsequent revision surgeries and extended courses of antibiotic treatment. Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) and fracture-related hardware infection occur in 2 to 4% of patients (3, 4) and the annual cost of managing PJI alone is estimated to exceed $1.8 billion in the United States (5). Once the hardware is infected, antibiotics are unable to achieve cure due to biofilm- current experts and guidelines recommend at least one additional revision surgery (6, 7). As the majority of these infections are caused by staphylococci, including S. aureus (2), vaccination is an attractive strategy for patients that require device implantation and could be incorporated into existing clinical workflow (8). Modeling demonstrates that even a partially effective S. aureus vaccine would be very cost-effective for the preoperative orthopedic population given the current infection impact on quality-adjusted life years (9).

However, multiple large vaccine trials to prevent S. aureus infection following surgery have failed (10–12). These trials used conventional vaccine technology in which antigens were injected in a liquid suspension and allowed to dissipate in the subcutaneous or muscular tissues. In a post hoc analysis of one of these failed vaccine clinical trials, it was discovered that patients who were unable to mount a Th1-driven cytokine response to vaccination had poor outcomes following subsequent S. aureus infection (11). An alternative type of vaccine platform that is more effective in generating cell-mediated immunity and capable of mitigating S. aureus in one of the most challenging biofilm-associated infections (PJI) would represent a major immunologic advance and have important implications for other vaccine targets where effective cell-mediated immunity has been difficult to achieve.

Given the failure of conventional vaccination in preclinical and clinical studies, we have developed an alternative vaccine technology leveraging an injectable and biodegradable scaffold composed of mesoporous silica rods (MSRs) (13, 14). MSRs contain aligned nanopores that can be exploited for controlled delivery of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) to recruit dendritic cells via chemotaxis. To activate dendritic cells that have infiltrated the scaffold, demethylated cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG), a toll-like receptor 9 agonist, is added to the system as a vaccine adjuvant. This system has been demonstrated to recruit and activate dendritic cells with high efficiency (6). In murine cancer models, this scaffold vaccine platform generates superior immunity and survival benefit compared to conventional liquid “bolus” vaccines (15). When loading the MSR vaccine with pathogen-associated molecular pattern molecules (PAMPs) using an engineered mannose-binding lectin motif, vaccinated mice had increased anti-S. aureus antibodies and reduced abscess formation following subcutaneous S. aureus injection (16). Scaffold vaccines with PAMPs from gram-negative Escherichia coli also provided mortality benefit to subsequent gram-negative Enterobacter cloacae infection, suggesting that PAMPs may generate broad immunity against related pathogens (16).

Our goal was to determine whether scaffold vaccination results in mitigation and/or prevention of staphylococcal orthopedic device infection (Fig. 1A). In this study, mice were vaccinated and evaluated to determine how scaffold vaccination affected host adaptive immunity. The animals were then challenged with infection of a femoral implant by a bioluminescent S. aureus to determine the protective effect of scaffold vaccination. The generalizability of PAMPs from one methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) isolate to provide protection against a different methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) was evaluated. Finally, the scaffold vaccine strategy was evaluated by comparing PAMPs to a monovalent recombinant protein as antigen source.

Results

Scaffold Synthesis Resulted in a Biomaterial with High Aspect Ratio.

One of the advantages of the MSR is its high length to width (aspect) ratio, with the high ratio used here increasing dendritic cell recruitment (13). By scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image analysis, MSRs had a mean length of 83 µm, mean diameter of 9 µm, and mean aspect ratio of 10.3 (Fig. 1 B and C and SI Appendix, Table S1). MSRs had a relatively high surface area of 532.5 m2/g (SI Appendix, Table S1) with a pore size (~7 nm) and pore volume similar to prior experiments (13).

Scaffold Vaccination was well Tolerated and Activated Innate Immune Populations.

Four mice per group were euthanized according to the procedure approved by the Harvard University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) on Day 7 with harvest of their spleens and tissue at the injection site. The Vaccine Scaffold group spleens were nearly three times heavier than naïve spleens (301 vs 108 mg, P < 0.0001) and nearly twice as heavy as the Scaffold No Antigen spleens (301 vs 173 mg, P = 0.0005) (Fig. 2C). Flow cytometry with staining for CD11c and MHCII was performed on tissue harvested from the injection sites and spleens (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–C). There was a trend toward greater numbers of CD11c+ cells, typically representative of dendritic cells in mice, at the injection site of the Scaffold Vaccine group and significantly more CD11c+ cells in the spleens of the Scaffold Vaccine group (P = 0.0142) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Greater than 72% of these CD11c+ cells coexpressed MHCII (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C), a marker of dendritic cell activation (13).

Scaffold Vaccination Resulted in Increased Antigen-Specific Adaptive Responses.

We hypothesized that scaffold vaccination would boost the anti-staphylococcal responses of both cell-mediated and humoral immunity. We first examined serum cytokine profiles to better understand the immune phenotype elicited by the different vaccine systems. Serum cytokines associated with a Th1 immune phenotype, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-2, IL-1beta (IL-1β), IL-7, and IL-12p70, were evaluated 48 h after vaccination (n = 4 per group). The Scaffold Vaccine group had significantly increased total Th1 serum cytokines (1,400 pg/mL) compared to the Naïve group (150 pg/mL) (P = 0.0328) (Fig. 2D). Both the Scaffold Vaccine and Bolus Vaccine groups also had greater total Th17-associated cytokines (1,700 and 1,400 pg/mL, respectively) compared to naïve mice (27 pg/mL) (P = 0.0028 and P = 0.0102, respectively) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A)

Next, we probed cell-mediated immunity by re-exposing vaccinated splenocytes to S. aureus antigen and measuring response compared to naïve splenocytes. IFN-γ expression (ELISpot) in splenocytes from mice euthanized on Day 7 and exposed to heat-killed S. aureus Xen29 as a stimulating antigen (Fig. 2 E and F) was analyzed as a surrogate marker of antigen-specific Th1 cell-mediated response (17). Only groups whose vaccines included antigen (Bolus Vaccine and Scaffold Vaccine) had significantly increased IFN-γ expression, and Scaffold Vaccine was significantly greater than Bolus Vaccine (62 vs 36 spots per well, P = 0.0044) (Fig. 2F).

Finally, we measured anti-staphylococcal antibody titers as a marker of humoral immunity. On Day 14, the remaining mice (n = 5 for Bolus Vaccine; n = 8 for all other groups) received a booster vaccine via subcutaneous contralateral flank injection. On Day 28 sera, a sandwich ELISA was performed using killed S. aureus Xen29 as binding substrate to assess mouse antibody levels to measure humoral responses (16). For total IgG, the Scaffold Vaccine group had greater concentrations of anti-S. aureus antibodies than Naïve and Scaffold No Antigen groups by Day 28 (Fig. 2G). To evaluate whether binding was due to staphylococcal Protein A Fc sequestration rather than antibody recognition, a S. aureus mutant lacking Protein A (Δspa) was also used as ELISA substrate and demonstrated similar antibody binding to Day 28 sera (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). IgG subclasses IgG1 and IgG2b were measured as they have previously been found to be relevant in murine models of S. aureus orthopedic device infection (18). There was a greater increase of anti-S. aureus IgG2b compared to IgG1 antibodies after vaccination (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C).

Scaffold Vaccination Mitigated S. aureus Xen29 Orthopedic Device Infection.

After assessing the immune response to vaccination, we next challenged vaccinated animals with S. aureus device infection to better understand host response and vaccine efficacy. On Day 35 (Fig. 3A), all groups underwent device implantation (n = 5 for Bolus Vaccine; n = 8 per all other groups). Following implant insertion into the distal femur, radiographs were taken to confirm placement (Fig. 3B). Surgery was well tolerated with minimal lameness after the first 24 h of recovery. Surgery without infection in unvaccinated animals resulted in loss of approximately 4% of body mass by three days, followed by gradual recovery (Fig. 3C). Two weeks following surgery, only the unvaccinated infected group had significantly persistent weight loss compared to the uninfected control (99 vs 93% of presurgical weight; P = 0.0013). All infected groups had increased anti-S. aureus total IgG by Day 49 (Fig. 3D). Scaffold vaccination resulted in higher total anti-S. aureus Xen29 titers compared to Uninfected, Untreated, and Scaffold No Antigen groups. By subclass, infection drove greater IgG2b than IgG1 response (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

To measure infection dynamics in real time, mice underwent bioluminescent imaging following surgery until euthanasia per Harvard IACUC-approved procedures (Fig. 3 E–G), starting 72 h after infection, the peak bioluminescent timepoint in this infection model (19). Prior to infection, mice had bioluminescence similar to the uninfected group (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). All infected groups had significantly greater bioluminescence compared to uninfected mice until Day 45, when the Scaffold Vaccine group was no longer significantly different from uninfected controls (P = 0.152). By Day 49, the Scaffold Vaccine group had significantly less bioluminescence than the other three infected groups (Fig. 3H).

At euthanasia on Day 49, implants were harvested under sterile conditions, and biofilm-embedded organisms were collected by sonication and vortex (19), consistent with clinical protocols to optimize culture yield in orthopedic device infection (20). No bacteria were recovered from the implants of uninfected animals (Fig. 3H), suggesting that there was not contamination during surgery. The mean bacterial burdens for infected groups were 4.75 × 105 CFU/implant (Untreated), 3.66 × 105 CFU/implant (Scaffold No Antigen), 4.14 × 105 CFU/implant (Bolus Vaccine), and 5.12 × 103 CFU/implant (Scaffold Vaccine). The Scaffold Vaccine group had significantly reduced burden compared to the other three infected groups. One of eight animals in the Scaffold Vaccine group had no detectable bacteria. There was a weak negative correlation between antibody titers and final bacterial burden (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

Scaffold Vaccination was Effective Against Other S. aureus Strains Including MRSA.

Our next set of experiments was designed to explore whether scaffold vaccination with PAMPs derived from one strain of S. aureus was broadly protective against other S. aureus strains, including clinically relevant isolates. We hypothesized that PAMPs would be broadly effective against different S. aureus strains as a large antigen pool. Given that we previously established that the scaffold vaccine was superior to bolus vaccination with PAMPs as an antigen (Fig. 3), we reduced the number of animals required by eliminating the bolus and scaffold without antigen groups from this set of experiments. First, Day 28 sera from mice that underwent scaffold vaccination with Xen29 PAMPs were tested against other MSSA and MRSA strains (SI Appendix, Table S2). Evaluated strains included Xen29 (bioluminescent MSSA derived from NCTC 8532), RN4220 (MSSA derived from NCTC 8325-4), NRS699lux (bioluminescent MRSA of USA100 lineage), UAMS-1 (MSSA of USA200 lineage), and JE2 (MRSA of USA300 lineage). Sera from mice vaccinated with PAMPs from Xen29 had total IgG with significant binding (at least more than 1.5 log increase) against all tested strains (Fig. 4A).

As USA100/CC5 S. aureus are the most common lineage associated with periprosthetic joint infection in US-based data (21), NRS699lux was chosen as a representative USA100 MRSA strain for subsequent studies. ELISpot was performed on splenocytes from mice vaccinated with scaffold containing Xen29 PAMPs with and without stimulation using heat-killed MRSA NRS699lux rather than Xen29 (Fig. 4B). Stimulation with heat-killed MRSA NRS699lux resulted in significantly increased IFN-γ secretion compared to unvaccinated splenocytes (43 vs 16 spots per well, P = 0.0008).

Next, a challenge experiment was performed in unvaccinated mice and mice vaccinated with scaffold vaccine containing Xen29 PAMPs (n = 8 per group). Mice were boosted on Day 14 and then underwent surgery on Day 35. Instead of inoculation with Xen29, implants were inoculated with 1,000 CFU of NRS699lux. Real-time imaging was performed to track the dynamics of infection (Fig. 4C); one of eight Scaffold Vaccine mice had higher bioluminescence than the rest in the group (Fig. 4D). Due to this outlier, there were not significant differences between the two groups over time (Fig. 4E). However, at euthanasia, according to procedures approved by the Havard IACUC, the implants of mice that received scaffold vaccination with Xen29 PAMPs had a significantly reduced MRSA NRS699lux burden (1.25 × 106 vs 7.9 × 103 CFU/implant; P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4F).

Scaffold vaccination provided protection with a single antigen.

In an effort to better understand the composition of protein-based antigens present in PAMPs, we further examined liquid chromatography/mass spectroscopy (LC/MS) data obtained from S. aureus ATCC 12598 PAMPs (16). After removal of ribosomal proteins, we developed a list of the top five proteins present in PAMPs by percentage protein mass in the composition (SI Appendix, Table S3). The most prevalent protein, Protein A (encoded by spa), is toxic when used as vaccine antigen in humans (22). Therefore, we chose the second-most prevalent protein, iron-regulated surface determinant B (IsdB), as our representative protein antigen. We confirmed that IsdB was present in Xen29 PAMPs and immunogenic by measuring anti-IsdB titers in the sera of mice vaccinated with Xen29 PAMPs in the scaffold vaccine at Day 28 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A).

We then evaluated recombinant IsdB as a vaccine antigen using 20 µg per immunization, an established dose in prior mouse orthopedic device infection studies (23). A bolus group was reintroduced as we had not yet demonstrated the efficacy of scaffold vaccination with non-PAMPs-based antigen. Mice were vaccinated with GM-CSF, CpG, and IsdB in saline (“Bolus IsdB”); GM-CSF, CpG, and IsdB in MSR scaffold (“Scaffold IsdB”); or vaccinated with GM-CSF, CpG, and Xen29 PAMPs in MSR scaffold (“Scaffold PAMPs,” previously labeled “Scaffold Vaccine” in prior studies) (n = 5 per group). Mice were boosted on Day 14 and challenged with Xen29 infection on Day 35 with bioluminescent imaging as before. There was no statistical significance between the three groups regarding bioluminescence although Scaffold PAMPs trended toward lower bioluminescence from Day 45 onward (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B and C). However, at euthanasia on Day 49, both groups with scaffold delivery of antigen had a lower implant bacterial burden than Bolus IsdB (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the ability of a biomaterial-based injectable scaffold system to elicit an adaptive immune response and mitigate S. aureus orthopedic device infection in an established murine model. Scaffold vaccination was well tolerated and was successful in generating Th1-associated immunity with increased antigen-specific cell-mediated and humoral responses. Compared to conventional bolus vaccination, scaffold vaccination decreased the bacterial burden in a murine model of orthopedic device infection. The protective effect of scaffold vaccination was generalizable against a different strain of S. aureus. The scaffold vaccine platform also mitigated infection with a conventional monovalent protein-based antigen.

Scaffold vaccination elicited a more robust immune response in mice compared to bolus vaccination. Scaffold vaccination generated a local immune response, resulting in a nodule at the site of scaffold injection that was larger than scaffold without antigen (Fig. 2B). Systemically, scaffold vaccination also resulted in increased spleen size (Fig. 2C), number of dendritic cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), and serum Th1- and Th17-associated cytokines (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). In terms of antigen-specific immunity, scaffold vaccination resulted in greater IFN-γ expression upon splenocyte rechallenge with S. aureus (Fig. 2 E–F), and both antigen-containing vaccines resulted in significant anti-S. aureus IgG production (Fig. 2G). The antigen-free scaffold control group, which contained demethylated CpG sequences as adjuvant, also resulted in increased production of anti-S. aureus IgG compared to naïve mice but to a lesser extent than antigen-containing groups (Fig. 2G). In mice, CpG alone increases anti-IgG against S. aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Streptococcus pyogenes prior to infection, and others have speculated that this effect is due to stimulation of polyreactive natural antibodies (24). In the context of what we know about S. aureus infection, post hoc analysis of a large prior S. aureus vaccine trial showed that host inability to generate strong Th1 and Th17 responses correlated with vaccine failure (11). It is promising that scaffold vaccination more robustly induced Th1- and Th17-associated cytokines and resulted in an antigen-specific IFN-γ response upon challenge.

Scaffold vaccination was effective in mitigating S. aureus infection in a well-established murine model of orthopedic device infection (19). By Day 45 (ten days after infection), there was significantly less bioluminescence in the leg in the Scaffold Vaccine group compared to infected controls (Fig. 3 E–G). On implant retrieval, the Scaffold Vaccination group had significantly fewer bacteria (Fig. 3H). Prior preclinical vaccine studies that demonstrated success against more superficial S. aureus infection and/or bacteremia failed to show protection against orthopedic device infection (12, 25). Therefore, we chose to study scaffold vaccination in a robust well-established model in which 1,000 CFU are directly inoculated on the hardware at the time of insertion (19). In other murine studies of active and passive immunization against S. aureus orthopedic device infection, results have been limited. Bacterial burden changes compared to unimmunized animals have ranged from approximately +0.5 log (vaccination worsening infection) to −0.5 log (mild reduction) (23, 26, 27). In this study, we demonstrated a 2.4 log reduction, a significant improvement compared to prior conventional approaches (SI Appendix, Table S4).

The mechanism underlying the improved effect of scaffold vaccination, as compared to bolus vaccination, will be an area of future work. Human clinical trials have demonstrated that bolus vaccines resulting in increased anti-staphylococcal antibody titers did not correlate with improved outcomes (10, 12), similar to our results (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Others have shown in mice that vaccine-elicited humoral immunity without cell-mediated immunity is insufficient to protect against some types of S. aureus disease such as abscess formation (28). Scaffold vaccination and bolus vaccination resulted in similar antibody titers in this study (Figs. 2G and 3D); however, scaffold vaccination resulted in greater Th1/Th17 cytokine production, cell-mediated immunity (Fig. 2 E–G and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), and final efficacy (Fig. 3H), pointing to the importance of these aspects of vaccine response. While this study was not designed to separate humoral and cell-mediated vaccine responses, it is possible that the strong cell-mediated response produced by scaffold vaccination is related to its efficacy in the context of S. aureus orthopedic hardware infection. The differences in bacterial bioluminescence between scaffold and bolus vaccine groups became more pronounced over time (Fig. 3G) which may suggest that time is needed for the T cell–driven cell-mediated response to coordinate bacterial clearance from the implant.

Scaffold vaccination with PAMPs as antigen created an immune response that was effective against another strain of S. aureus. One of the strengths of using PAMPs is the inclusion of a large heterogenous pool of antigens rather than a monovalent antigen or small cocktail of several antigens (16). Antibodies generated from scaffold vaccination with Xen29 PAMPs bound other strains of S. aureus, including a variety of clinically relevant methicillin-susceptible and methicillin-resistant isolates (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Table S1). This effect was not an artifact from protein A antibody sequestration (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Scaffold vaccination with Xen29 (MSSA) PAMPs also resulted in a cell-mediated response upon subsequent exposure to a MRSA strain (Fig. 4B) and increased protection from MRSA infection (Fig. 4F). Given that orthopedic device infection can be caused by a variety of S. aureus clonal complexes (21, 29), it is important that a vaccine approach leveraging PAMPs isolated from one strain offers protection against others. These results are consistent with our prior findings that PAMPs derived from E. coli delivered via scaffold vaccination elicited broad protection against gram-negative bacteremia, including against other species such as E. cloacae (16).

Scaffold vaccination as a platform may be useful for antigens other than PAMPs. Using a protein that is found in S. aureus PAMPs (SI Appendix, Table S3 and Fig. S6A) as a monovalent antigen, scaffold vaccination improved efficacy compared to conventional bolus vaccine delivery (Fig. 5B). There were not significant differences in bioluminescence although the study may have been underpowered for imaging purposes (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B and C). Recombinant IsdB was originally thought to be promising as a vaccine target but eventually failed in mouse orthopedic device infection models (23) and a clinical trial for surgical patients (10). While this study was not designed to mechanistically determine why scaffold delivery was more effective than bolus delivery of IsdB as an antigen, these results are consistent with prior scaffold vaccine data in the cancer field (13, 15). The heterogeneity of PAMPs is both a strength and a weakness- it provides a wide variety of epitopes but it is unclear which of these pathogen molecules provide a protective immunogenic effect and which may be detrimental. In these experiments, several vaccinated mice had not only reduced bacterial burden, but bacterial load lower than the LOD (potential implant sterility). A better mechanistic understanding of which PAMP molecules drive a protective response and how presentation in a scaffold system facilitates immunogenicity may allow more rational design to generate consistent sterilizing immunity.

S. aureus is one of our most challenging pathogens in orthopedic device infection and the field has failed to date to produce an effective vaccine despite much effort and several large clinical trials (10, 12). Scaffold vaccination offers a new modality of immunotherapy that has shown promise, especially in the field of cancer (30). Early preclinical work has suggested that scaffold vaccination can improve outcomes in S. aureus superficial soft tissue infection (16). In this study, we have demonstrated efficacy in a challenging orthopedic implant model and demonstrated that this strategy may be more broadly applicable against different strains and using different antigens. This study is unique and significant in the context of prior lack of efficacy in S. aureus hardware infection immunotherapy (SI Appendix, Table S4), the direct comparison of scaffold vaccination against current state-of-art (bolus vaccination) (Figs. 2, 3, and 5), and the robustness and clinical relevancy of the murine model compared to prior work including soft tissue infection (16) and implantation of precolonized hardware (23). When summing together all challenged groups with scaffold vaccination compared to all other groups studied in these experiments, 4/26 (15.4%) of mice receiving scaffold vaccines compared to 0/34 other mice had no viable growth of bacteria from their implants, which suggests sterilizing immunity and further highlights the potential of scaffold vaccination.

In the context of current state-of-the-art immunotherapies for S. aureus in murine orthopedic device infection (SI Appendix, Table S4), scaffold vaccination with PAMPs decreased bacterial burden by 2.4 log or ~250-fold (Fig. 3H) compared to 0.5 log or ~3-fold in the next-most efficacious attempt in the literature (27). Compared to bolus vaccination, scaffold vaccination with a single defined antigen (rIsdB) reduced burden by ~1 log or 10-fold (Fig. 5B). While this is still a large improvement compared to prior art (3-versus 10-fold reduction in burden), it suggests that there may be specific antigens within the pool of PAMPs that provide protection and others (like IsdB) that may not be as effective. Deconvolution of which PAMP factors are most impactful is the subject of ongoing studies. An effective vaccine against S. aureus would be lifesaving and improve health outcomes globally. Outside of infection, directing cell-mediated immune responses with scaffold vaccine technology may have broad implications for immunotherapy against cancer, autoimmune disease, and other health conditions. While much work remains before clinical translation, these data may stimulate more interest in leveraging new vaccine platforms to tackle targets where conventional vaccination has failed.

Materials and Methods

Antigen Generation.

To generate lysate and isolate PAMPs, a single colony of S. aureus Xen29 (PerkinElmer) was grown in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) at 37 °C at 100 RPM until mid-log, cooled on ice for 10 min, and then pelleted. Concentration was measured via McFarland standard and resuspended in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI) to a concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/mL. Oxacillin (0.5 mg/mL) was added, and cells were reincubated at 37 °C at 100 RPM. After 24 h, the resulting suspension was passed through a 70 µm filter, and 0.5 mg/mL oxacillin was added. Following 24 additional hours of incubation at 37 °C, the resulting lysate was plated [tryptic soy agar plates with 5% sheep’s blood (TSA)] in triplicate to confirm sterility. PAMPs were also generated from other MSSA and MRSA strains as shown in SI Appendix, Table S2. For MRSA strains used to evaluate antibody binding, a combination of vancomycin (0.5 mg/mL) and daptomycin (1 mg/mL) was used for killing rather than oxacillin.

Engineered mannose-binding lectin with Fc domain (FcMBL) was synthesized as previously described (16, 31). The amount of PAMPs, defined as molecules that bound to FcMBL for this study, was characterized via ELISA using mannose as a control ladder (16). To load PAMPs from lysate onto FcMBL, 0.125 mg of FcMBL bound to magnetic microbeads was added per 1 mL of lysate and incubated at room temperature at 20 RPM for 30 min (16). Beads were then magnetized and washed with a buffer of 50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween-20, and 5 mM CaCl2 to remove any unbound lysate. The amount of attached PAMPs was quantified using an ELISA with mannose as a control as previously described (16).

Heat-killed S. aureus were generated by inoculating 5 mL of TSB with a single colony of S. aureus Xen29 (MSSA) or S. aureus NRS699lux (MRSA) and growing overnight. Bacteria were then cooled for at least 10 min at 4 °C to arrest growth and diluted to 1 × 108 CFU/mL in RPMI and washed once. This solution was incubated at 95 °C for one hour. The resulting lysate was plated in triplicate to ensure nonviability and then frozen at −80 °C until use.

Scaffold Synthesis and Characterization.

MSRs were synthesized and characterized as described previously (13, 14, 16) via P123 surfactant (Sigma-Aldrich) template using tetraethyl orthosilicate with ethanol extraction to produce rods. For characterization, MSRs were incubated in 10% formalin for 24 h at room temperature and then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). MSRs then underwent dehydration via sequential soaks in ethanol with decreasing water content (50 to 70% to 80 to 90% ×2 to 100% ×2) for 15 to 20 min of incubation at room temperature per step. MSRs then underwent 15-min soaks in 2:1 to 1:2 to 0:1 ethanol:hexamethyldisilazane solutions. Finally, MSRs were transferred to a fresh 100% hexamethyldisilazane solution and allowed to dry overnight at room temperature. Subsequent MSR was sputter-coated with 80:20 platinum/palladium and then imaged with a Gemini Ultra55 Field Emission SEM (Zeiss). The width and length of three hundred particles were measured in ImageJ (NIH). Fragments, defined as pieces with an aspect ratio less than 3, were ~2% of total particles and were excluded. The aspect ratio was calculated as rod length divided by diameter. Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) nitrogen gas adsorption was performed to calculate surface area. Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) adsorption modeling was used to calculate average pore size and cumulative pore volume.

Vaccine Assembly.

Per vaccine, 10 mg of MSR, 3 µg of recombinant murine GM-CSF (Peprotech, Inc.), 100 µg of ODN 1826 CpG (InvivoGen), and FcMBL beads with 7.5 U of bound PAMPs were mixed at room temperature at 45 RPM for 12 to 16 h to promote adsorption of antigen, adjuvant, and chemokine to MSRs. For the “Scaffold No Antigen” group, the equivalent amount of FcMBL beads without any PAMPs was added. For the “Scaffold IsdB” group, 20 µg of recombinant IsdB (Integrated BioTherapeutics, Inc.) was substituted as antigen in place of FcMBL beads with PAMPs. The resulting mixture was then frozen at –80 °C and lyophilized. The resulting vaccine was stored at 4 °C until use. For the Bolus Vaccine and Bolus IsdB groups, reagents (including GM-CSF and CpG) were thawed and immediately mixed together prior to injection (no adsorption/incubation phase).

Vaccination.

Briefly, 8- to 10-wk male C57BL/6 mice (Jackson Laboratories) were vaccinated on day 0 of the study. Lyophilized scaffold vaccines were rehydrated in 250 µL of deionized water (diH2O) per vaccine. In all but four mice per group, blood was collected and sera was isolated at day 0 prior to vaccination. All mice were anesthetized with 3 to 4% isoflurane, and anesthesia was maintained with 1 to 2% isoflurane. The right flank was shaved, and 250 µL of vaccine was injected subcutaneously in the right flank. Forty-eight hours after vaccination, blood was collected and sera was isolated from the remaining four mice per group. Sera was stored at –80 °C until undergoing analysis for cytokines via Luminex (Eve Technologies Inc) or antibody titers. The size of the injection site nodule was measured by calipers one week after vaccination. Four mice in each group were euthanized on Day 7. On Day 14, the remaining mice in each group underwent booster vaccination with the same vaccine in the contralateral flank. On Days 28 and 49, blood was collected, and sera were isolated for measurement of antibody concentrations.

Surgery and Periprosthetic Joint Infection.

On Day 35, a subculture of S. aureus was made fresh from an overnight culture that had been inoculated from a single colony. The subculture was incubated at 37 °C at 100 RPM until reaching log-growth phase. Bacteria were then placed at 4 °C, washed 3x in saline, diluted to 5 × 105 CFU/mL, and kept at 4 °C until inoculation.

Mice were anesthetized via isoflurane and given 0.5 mL of saline subcutaneously prior to surgery. The left leg was shaved and sterilized with ethanol and chlorhexidine washes. The knee was accessed via scalpel under sterile conditions. The patella was laterally dislocated to access the distal femur. A 25G needle was used to ream the intramedullary canal. A 23G needle was used to widen the canal. A stainless-streel surgical Kirschner wire (Sklar) that was ~7 mm in length and 0.7 mm in diameter was placed into the distal femur such that 1 mm protruded into the knee joint. Depending on the animal group, the exposed implant was inoculated with 2 µL of sterile saline or saline containing 103 CFU of S. aureus. The patella was relocated and the incision closed with 5-0 Prolene™ suture (VWR). Pain was managed with subcutaneous buprenorphine.

Bioluminescent Imaging.

Following surgery and inoculation, mice underwent bioluminescent imaging twice weekly by an in vivo imaging system (Caliper Life Sciences). Animals were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane (induction at 3 to 4%, maintenance at 1 to 2%) and imaged. A region of interest (ROI) over the left knee was created and used for all mice. The bioluminescence in the ROI, measured in photons per second, was measured, and the background bioluminescence captured in the same image was subtracted for the final bioluminescence output.

Euthanasia and Tissue Isolation.

On Day 7, four mice from each group were euthanized according to procedures approved by the Harvard IACUC to study the early response to vaccination. Necropsy was performed under sterile conditions to collect the spleen and tissues at the injection site. Tissues were placed in PBS and kept at 4 °C during processing. On Day 49, the remaining mice were euthanized. Using a sterile set of instruments, the knee was accessed and the implant was removed. If needed, the distal femur was fractured via rongeurs to remove the implant. The implant was kept at 4 °C. Instruments underwent bead sterilization between mice.

Tissue Processing and Flow Cytometry.

In mice euthanized on Day 7, tissues from the injection site were incubated in 4 mL of RPMI with 10% FBS containing 2.1 mg of type IV collagenase (Gibco) and 0.4 mg DNAse (Sigma). Tissues were mechanically dissociated using a gentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) and then incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. This dissociation cycle was performed twice. The digestion was quenched with an additional 4 mL of PBS and the resulting tissue slurry was filtered through a 30 µm strainer. After an additional 10 mL PBS wash, cells were resuspended and counted. Spleens weighed before manual disruption. The subsequent cell suspension was passed through a 70 µm filter while being washed with 10 mL of PBS. Red blood cells were then lysed using RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend) followed by quenching with additional PBS. Cells were diluted to 1 to 5 × 106 cells/mL followed by immunohistochemical staining with PE anti-mouse CD11c antibody (BioLegend) and PerCP anti-mouse MHCII antibody (BioLegend). Flow cytometry was performed with a CytoFLEX LX (Beckman Coulter) with analysis performed using FlowJo v10.9 (BD Life Sciences).

Antigen-Specific Interferon-Gamma.

Splenocytes were resuspended in RPMI with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and plated in IFN-γ ELI-Spot wells (R and D Systems) at 5 × 104 cells per 100 µL. Splenocytes were either unstimulated (media alone) or stimulated (5 × 105 heat-killed S. aureus Xen29 or S. aureus NRS699lux in media). The cells were incubated for 20 h at 37 °C followed by staining for IFN-γ per manufacturer instructions. The subsequent number of spots per plate was quantified via bright field microscopy.

Antibody Titers.

Antibody titers were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). A standard curve of murine total IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch) was prepared by making serial dilutions in PBS from 40 to 0.625 ng/mL and 100 µL per well was plated in duplicate in high-bind ELISA plates (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). 100 µL per well of 30 U/mL of S. aureus PAMPs in PBS [concentration verified via FcMBL binding using a mannose control ladder (16)] was plated in subsequent experimental wells as a binding substrate. Plates were incubated at 4 °C on a tilting table overnight for substrate and ladder adherence. Plates were then washed ×3 with working buffer and blocked by incubating with 1:20 pig sera (VWR) for 90 min at room temperature on an orbital shaker at 300 RPM. Plates were washed ×1 and 100 µL of serum dilutions (typically 1:100, 1:1,000, and 1:10,000 in ELISA working buffer, 1% bovine serum albumin in tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 with calcium chloride) was added to wells and incubated at room temperature for 120 min at 300 RPM for antibody binding onto substrate. After washing wells ×3, 100 µL of a 1:20,000 dilution of goat anti-mouse IgG with conjugated horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Jackson ImmunoResearch) in working buffer was added to each well. Plates were incubated for 60 min at room temperature at 300 RPM for secondary antibody binding. Wells were washed ×3 and 100 µL of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB)-detecting HRP buffer was added to each well (ThermoScientific). The plates were incubated for 15 min, and then, 50 µL of 2 M sulfuric acid was added to each well. A plate reader (BioTek) was used to read wells at 450 nm with readings at 570 nm subtracted for normalization.

For IgG subclasses, ladders of the same concentration as above were created by using murine IgG1 and IgG2b (BioLegend). The same steps as above were performed until secondary antibody binding. A 1:4,000 and 1:10,000 dilution of goat anti-mouse IgG1 and IgG2b, respectively, with conjugated HRP (Cell Signaling Technology) were added to respective subclass wells rather than goat anti-mouse total IgG secondary antibody.

Bacterial Burden Analysis.

Harvested implants were placed in 1 mL of TSB with 0.3% Tween-80 and sonicated for 10 min followed by vortexing for 5 min to disperse biofilm-embedded organisms (19). The resulting suspensions were plated on TSA and incubated overnight at 37 °C for colony counting.

Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectroscopy (LC–MS).

LC/MS was previously performed to characterize proteins captured by FcMBL beads (9). We used this previously established database of S. aureus PAMP proteins captured by FcMBL and sorted by weight percent of the total composition to elucidate which proteins were present in the highest amounts.

Statistics.

Statistics were performed in R (v4.3.1, The R Foundation). For comparing means across two groups at a single timepoint, the two-tailed Student’s t test was used (α = 0.05). For comparing means across more than two groups at a single timepoint, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s honestly significant difference post hoc testing was performed (α = 0.05).
