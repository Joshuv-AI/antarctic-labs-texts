Thermoradiotherapy reverses proliferation, metastasis, and anoikis resistance in glioblastoma through ITGA5-PI3K/AKT axis
Authors: Ying Shi, Hongda An, Wenshuang Wang, Dong Nan, Yingzhe Cui, Mingyuan He, Jianxiu Lian, Xiaoxiao Zhang, Jing Peng, Xiuhua Yang, Pengfei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Reversing anoikis resistance via thermoradiotherapy to improve therapeutic outcomes.•Identifying ITGA5 as a prospective therapeutic target for thermoradiotherapy of GBM.•ITGA5 promoted the proliferation, metastasis, and anoikis resistance of GBM by regulating the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM), the most aggressive primary brain malignancy [1], accounts for 45.2 % of central nervous system tumors and exhibits a bleak median survival of 14.6 months despite multiple therapies [2]. Current standards strategies, including maximally surgical resection followed by radiotherapy (RT) and adjuvant temozolomide [3], fail to address the inherent resistance mechanisms driving recurrence, including diffuse infiltration, angiogenesis, and evasion of apoptosis [4]. Among these, anoikis resistance, the ability of detached cells to evade apoptosis, enables GBM cells to survive in anchorage-independent microenvironments [5], facilitating tumor progression and therapeutic escape [6].

Anoikis resistance in GBM is orchestrated by dysregulated molecular networks. Integrin, E-cadherin, transforming growth factor β, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and hypoxia enabling detached cells to bypass apoptotic checkpoints [7]. Bioinformatics analysis have identified 27 anoikis-related genes overexpressed in GBM, most of which are overexpressed in GBM tissues compared with those in normal tissues, contributing to anoikis resistance and presenting potential therapeutic targets [8]. While targeting these pathways holds therapeutic promise, clinical translation remains limited by incomplete mechanistic understanding and lack of strategies to disrupt anchorage-independent survival.

Hyperthermia therapy (HT), which elevates tumor temperatures to 43–48 °C, synergizes with RT by enhancing DNA damage, improving oxygenation, and sensitizing radiation-resistant cells [[9], [10], [11]]. Preclinical studies demonstrate combinations of HT and RT (thermoradiotherapy) overcome the resistance to RT in cervical and head/neck cancers, yet their impact on anoikis resistance of GBM remains unexplored [12]. This gap underscores the need to investigate whether thermoradiotherapy disrupts survival pathways in GBM.

Here, we propose that thermoradiotherapy reverses anoikis resistance in GBM by targeting the ITGA5-PI3K/AKT axis. Using in vitro and in vivo models, we evaluate effects of thermoradiotherapy on proliferation, migration, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and anoikis resistance, along with the underlying mechanism. Our findings may help identify the potential therapeutic target for GBM.

Materials and methods

Cell lines, animals, and patient samples

Human GBM cell lines (U251 and LN229), definitively sourced from the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, were cultured in a high-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco) with 10 % foetal bovine serum (FBS, Wisent, China) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Seven, Beijing, China) at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 humidified atmosphere, with regular monitoring of cell morphology and timely passaging. BALB/c nude mice (4–5 weeks old) were procured from Huachuang Sino (Jiangsu, China) and kept in a pathogen-free setting. The animal study protocol was approved by the Committee on the Use of Live Animals in Teaching and Research the of First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (No. 2023,007, Harbin, Heilongjiang, China). The experiments related to GBM tissues from clinical patients were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (No. 2024JS86, Harbin, Heilongjiang, China), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants, ensuring ethical compliance and reliability of the collected data.

In vitro and in vivo HT and/or RT

GBM cells were incubated overnight in 96-well (2 × 103/well), 24-well (5 × 103/well), or 6-well (2 × 104/well) plates. Cells were heated at 45 °C in a water bath for 30 min and/or with 4 Gy of ionizing radiation using an irradiation apparatus. Assays were performed 48 h post-treatment. For in vivo model, localized HT was generated by intratumoral injection of indocyanine green (ICG) followed by 808 nm near-infrared (NIR) light irradiation. The tumors were heated at 45 °C for 10 min and/or 4 Gy of ionizing radiation using an irradiation apparatus.

CCK-8 assay

CCK-8 assays were conducted following the kit instructions (Seven). U251 and LN229 cells were seeded at 5000 cells/well in 96-well plates, followed by the addition of 10 μL of CCK-8 solution and incubation for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm to ensure accurate viability assessment.

Colony formation assay

GBM cells were cultured in 6-well plates (5 × 102/well) for 2 weeks. Colonies were fixed with 4 % polyformaldehyde for 15 min at 25 °C, stained with 0.1 % crystal violet (Biosharp, China) for 15 min at 25 °C, and visible colonies were then pictured and counted. The experiments were conducted thrice to ensure reproducibility.

Transwell assays

Transwell chambers (JET BIOFIL) were used to assess the invasive and migratory capabilities of GBM cells. The lower chamber contained DMEM with 20 % FBS, whereas the upper chamber was seeded with GBM cells in FBS-free medium. For the invasion assay, the upper surface of the membrane was coated with Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA). Following incubation for 24–48 h at 37 °C and 5 % CO2, non-migrated/invasive cells were removed, whereas migratory/invasive cells were fixed, stained, and analyzed using an inverted light microscope (Leica). The experiments were conducted thrice to ensure reproducibility.

Anoikis assay

Experiments were conducted in 10-cm, 6-cm, 6-well, and 96-well plates coated with poly-Hema (Aladdin). Cells were subjected to PTT, RT, or PTT combined with RT and cultured in poly Hema-coated plates for 24 h. After harvesting with pancreatic enzymes and washed thrice with PBS, apoptosis was analysed using the cell viability/cytotoxicity assay kit (Beyotime) and Annexin V-APC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Vazyme, Jiangsu, China).

Cell viability/cytotoxicity assay

Harvested cells were stained with a calcein-AM/PI kit (Beyotime, China) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica), with excitation/emission wavelengths of 494/517 nm for green fluorescence and 535/617 nm for red fluorescence.

Annexin V-APC/PI apoptosis detection

Harvested GBM cells were stained with Annexin V-APC and PI in the dark for 10 min, and spontaneous apoptosis was analysed using a flow cytometer (NovoCyte, Agilent, USA). This procedure was repeated thrice.

Astral-data-independent acquisition (DIA) quantitative proteomic analysis and data processing

GBM samples (n = 3) were suspended in protein lysis buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors on ice and then processed with a high-flux tissue grinder for three 40-s cycles. After the mixture was incubated on ice (30 min), vortexed, centrifugated (16,000 × g, 4 °C, 30 min), the protein was collected from the supernatant, determined the concentration by BCA method, and performed SDS-PAGE electrophoresis.

Next, 100 μg protein was re-suspended with triethylammonium bicarbonate buffer (TEAB) to a final concentration (100 mM), reduced with Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (10 mM) at 37 °C for 60 min, and alkylated with iodoacetamide (40 mM) at 25 °C for 40 min in the dark. After centrifugation (10,000 × g, 4 °C, 20 min), the pellet was collected and re-suspended with 100 μL TEAB (100 mM). Trypsin was then added at a 1:50 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Following trypsin digestion, the peptides were dried, resolubilised in 0.1 % trifluoroacetic acid, desalted using HLB, dried again, and quantified using a Nano Drop One (Thermo Fischer Scientific) via UV absorption.

Using the peptide quantification results, the peptides were analyzed on a VanquishNeo coupled with an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). A uPAC High (75 μm×5.5 cm, Thermo) column was used with solvent A (water with 2 % ACN and 0.1 % formic acid) and solvent B (water with 80 % ACN and 0.1 % formic acid), and peptides were eluted using the 180 SPD.

DIA data were acquired on an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer operated in DIA mode, covering a mass range of 70–1050 m/z (MS1), and 150–2000 m/z (MS2). Raw DIA data were processed with Spectronaut software (Version 18), selecting six peptides per protein and three daughter ions per peptide for quantification, with parameters: protein false discovery rate ≤0.01, peptide false discovery rate ≤0.01, peptide confidence ≥99 %, and XIC width ≤75 ppm. Shared peptides and modified peptides were excluded, with peak areas were calculated and summed for quantification. Protein identifications included only those with at least one unique peptide.

Bioinformatics analysis was conducted on the Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). The R package ‘t-test’ was used to calculate P-values and fold change (FC) for proteins between the two groups. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified with thresholds of FC (>0.6 or <−0.6) and P-value of <0.05. Functional annotation of all identified proteins was performed via using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) and DEPs were further subjected to KEGG enrichment analysis.

Gene expression, protein expression, survival analysis, and protein interaction analysis

Differential expression analysis on mRNA and protein levels between human GBM samples and nontumor samples and patient survival analysis were conducted on GEPIA2 (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index) and UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/), respectively. Single-cell transcriptomic sequencing analysis of GBM tissues was carried out on CDCP (https://db.cngb.org/cdcp/). Protein-protein interactions were predicted using STRING (https://string-db.org).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total cellular RNA was isolated using a total RNA extraction kit (Vazyme) and transcribed into cDNA with HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme). The primer sequences are detailed in Table S1. qRT-PCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a QuantStudio 3 RT-PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Grand Island, NY, USA).

Western blotting (WB)

Tissues or GBM cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing PMSF and phosphatase inhibitor on ice for 30 min, centrifuged (13,500 rpm, 20 min), and protein concentrations were determined using a Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) kit. Equal amounts of protein from each sample were separated using 10 % sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE), transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with rapid blocking buffer (Seven). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, followed by thrice TBST washes after each incubation. The protein signals were visualised using a chemiluminescence imaging system (SYNGENE, USA). Primary antibodies, PI3K, pPI3K, N-cadherin, Vimentin, E-cadherin, MMP2, Bax, and Bcl-2 were obtained from Cell Signalling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA), whereas ITGA5, MMP9, AKT, p-AKT, p53, Cyclin D1, PCNA, β-actin, and secondary antibodies were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, Hubei, China).

Lentivirus construction and transfection

Lentiviruses designed to overexpress or knock down ITGA5 in GBM cells were synthesized by General Biol (Anhui, China). The sequences of the three short hairpin RNAs (shRNA#1, 2, 3) used to construc lentivirus for inhibiting the expression of ITGA5 are presented in Table S2. For lentiviral infection, the obtained lentivirus was added to target cells with polybrene (8 μg/mL). After 48 h, infected cells were selected using puromycin (5 μg/mL).

5-ethynyl2′-deoxyuridinelabeling (EdU) assay

The EdU assay was performed in 24-well plates (104 cells/well). After seeding and culturing overnight, cells were incubated with EdU reagent for 2 h, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, and stained using an EdU kit (Beyotime). Fluorescent images were photoed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica).

Intracranial tumour model

Female BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old, median weight 20 g) were intraperitoneally injected with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) to establish an intracranial GBM model. Briefly, 4 μL PBS containing 2 × 105 LN229 cells was stereotactically injected into the right striatum (1 mm posterior to the coronal suture, 2 mm lateral to the sagittal suture, and 4 mm deep) of the mice (n = 6 per group). Tumour formation and growth were confirmed using MRI and pathology. The weight and time of death of mice were recorded, and the cerebral tissue was fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde.

Subcutaneous GBM experiments

Following intraperitoneal injection with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg), BALB/c nude mice were randomised into the control (ITGA5-Ctrl), ITGA5 OE, sh-RNA, and sh-ITGA5 groups (n = 5 per group). The corresponding LN229 cells were injected subcutaneously into the left flank of each mouse. Tumour formation and growth were monitored at 3–4 weeks post-treatment through pathological examination. The tumour volume was calculated using the following formula: V = length × width2/2.

MRI of intracranial tumour models

Tumour size in intracranial tumour models (female BALB/c mice, n = 6) was detected using a 3.0 T MR scanner (Ingenia Elition; Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) with an 8-channel wrist coil. T1WI TSE (TRA) and T1WI mDIXON (COR) sequences were utilized to identify the tumour boundary. Imaging parameters are listed in Table S3.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

GBM cells were lysed in ice-cold immunoprecipitation buffer, centrifuged (13,500 rpm, 20 min), and incubated with the specified antibody overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the lysates were incubated with protein A + G magnetic beads at 4 °C for 2 h. The magnetic beads were adsorbed with a magnetic rack and washed thrice with PBS to remove proteins not bound to the magnetic beads. Finally, WB was performed using the indicated primary antibodies and the corresponding secondary antibodies.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent experiments, and SPSS version 25.0 was used for statistical analysis. Normal-distribution data were analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test (two groups) or one-way ANOVA (multiple comparisons), followed by Tukey’s HSD analysis after concluding statistical significance in the overall difference from the ANOVA test. We also checked the homogeneity of variance from the residuals. Skewed-distribution data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U (two groups) or the Kruskal-Wallis (multiple comparisons) test. Dunn's test was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons, following the determination of the statistical significance. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier and log-rank tests. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Thermoradiotherapy inhibits GBM proliferation and metastasis in vitro

Clinical trials have confirmed hyperthermic therapy, which involves heating tumour cells to 40–45 °C, as a viable treatment for recurrent or progressive glioma, enhancing the efficacy of RT and chemotherapy [13,14]. Hence, the HT experiments in this study utilised a therapeutic dose of 45 °C for 30 min. To explore the effect of thermoradiotherapy on GBM, U251 and LN229 cells were divided into four groups: control (Ctrl), HT, RT, and thermoradiotherapy (HT+RT). CCK-8 and colony formation assays were performed to investigate the effects of thermoradiotherapy on the viability and proliferation of U251 and LN229 cells. Result of CCK-8 assay indicated a slight inhibition of GBM cell proliferation following HT or RT, whereas thermoradiotherapy significantly inhibited GBM cell viability (Fig. 1A). Moreover, the colony formation assay showed that compared with the HT or RT groups, which slightly suppressed the clonogenicity of GBM cells, the clonogenicity in the thermoradiotherapy groups was dramatically reduced compared to the Ctrl groups (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, the expression of proliferation-related factors, including proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) and cyclin D1, was downregulated, whereas that of the cell cycle inhibitor, P53, was upregulated in the thermoradiotherapy groups, compared with that in the Ctrl groups, in U251 and LN229 cells (Fig. 1C).Fig. 1Thermoradiotherapy inhibits proliferation and metastasis and reverses anoikis resistance of GBM. A Transwell assay performed to further assess the influence of thermoradiotherapy on cell migration and invasion, indicated that thermoradiotherapy significantly restrained the migratory and invasive capacities of U251 and LN229 cells (Fig. 1D,E). Additionally, the expression of migration-, invasion-, and EMT-associated markers, including matrix metalloprotein (MMP)2, MMP9, N-cadherin, and Vimentin was decreased in the PTT + RT group, whereas that of E-cadherin was increased (Fig. 1F).

Thermoradiotherapy reverses anoikis resistance of GBM in vitro

An anoikis assay was conducted using GBM cells inoculated in poly HEMA-coated dishes to inhibit adhesion. Except for the Ctrl group, the other cell groups were treated with HT, RT, and thermoradiotherapy, respectively. A cell viability/cytotoxicity assay was performed to distinguish between living and dead cells (Fig. 1G). Strong green fluorescence signals detected in the living cells of the Ctrl group suggested that GBM cells were resistant to anoikis. Meanwhile, slight red fluorescence signals observed in the HT and RT groups indicated that monotherapy could partially reverse anoikis resistance. Conversely, thermoradiotherapy induced red fluorescent signals representing anoikis, demonstrating effective reversal of anoikis resistance in GBM cells. Subsequently, flow cytometry and Annexin V-APC/PI fluorochrome staining were used to evaluate anoikis in GBM cells (Fig. 1H). In the thermoradiotherapy group, the anoikis resistance of GBM cells was remarkably reversed, whereas HT or RT only mildly relieved anoikis resistance. Furthermore, WB analysis detected the anoikis-related factors Bcl-2 and Bax, indicating that thermoradiotherapy downregulated Bcl-2 and upregulated Bax expression (Fig. 1I).

ITGA5 is a potential target for thermoradiotherapy and correlates with anoikis-resistance

To comprehensively examine the effects of thermoradiotherapy on proteomic changes in GBM, tissues from the thermoradiotherapy and Ctrl groups were analysed using Astral DIA quantitative proteomics analysis, wherein DEPs were identified. A previous study identified 27 genes related to anoikis in GBM [8]. After analysing the differential expression of these genes in the proteomic data, the anoikis-related protein ITGA5 was found to be most significantly inhibited in GBM tissues following thermoradiotherapy (Fig. 2A, Fig. S1A). GBM could be divided into four molecular subtypes in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) network: classical, mesenchymal, neural, and proneural [15]. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that ITGA5 mRNA and protein expression was markedly elevated in human GBM tissues across all subtypes compared with that in adjacent non-tumour tissues (Fig. S1B,C). Meanwhile, elevated ITGA5 expression was strongly correlated with unfavourable clinical outcomes in patients with GBM (Fig. S1D). Furthermore, among GBM patients receiving RT, those with high ITGA5 expression exhibited more favorable survival outcomes compared to those with low expression (Fig. S1E). This strongly suggests ITGA5 as a potential therapeutic target for RT. Unfortunately, as hyperthermia is not a standard therapy for GBM, no relevant data exists within the TCGA database to investigate whether ITGA5 also represents a potential target for HT. To validate these findings, paired human GBM and adjacent non-tumor tissue samples were analyzed using qRT-PCR and WB, confirming significant up-regulation of ITGA5 on both mRNA and protein levels in GBM specimens (Fig. 2B,C). To delineate the effects of RT on the GBM immune microenvironment, we analyzed a public single-cell RNA-sequencing dataset. Following rigorous quality control (Fig. S2), which confirmed high-quality transcriptomes based on strong gene-count/sequencing-depth correlation and low mitochondrial gene content, we performed unsupervised clustering (Fig. S3). An optimal resolution was selected to resolve distinct cellular states, yielding 17 transcriptionally discrete clusters (Fig. 2D). Both control (CON) and RT group cells populated all clusters, indicating preservation of core niche architecture, yet with discernible density shifts suggestive of RT-induced remodeling (Fig. 2E). Annotation using canonical markers identified expected populations, including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), microglia, T-cell subsets (exhausted T cells, Tregs), B cells, plasma cells, NK cells, dendritic cells, astrocytes, endothelial cells, and neurons, thus comprehensively mapping the GBM immune landscape (Fig. 2F). Marker expression (P2ry12, Ccl22, Cldn5) validated cluster identities (Fig. S4).

We next interrogated the expression and potential role of ITGA5 within this framework. ITGA5 expression was spatially restricted, localizing predominantly to endothelial cells and TAMs (Fig. 2G), implying a niche-specific function. RT altered cellular composition, increasing TAM proportions while reducing endothelial abundance (Fig. 2H). ITGA5 expression was highest in these same populations (Fig. 2I). Stratification into ITGA5-high and -low fractions revealed distinct cellular distributions, with enrichment of endothelial and TAM lineages in the ITGA5-high cohort (Fig. 2J), positioning ITGA5 as a potential mediator of RT-responsive subsets. Violin plots confirmed significant ITGA5 enrichment in endothelial and TAM populations (Fig. S5). Notably, overall ITGA5 expression was significantly lower following RT (p = 0.043, Fig. 2K), suggesting RT may suppress ITGA5 in the GBM microenvironment.

After knocking down ITGA5, KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted to delineate the associated signalling mechanisms. Notably, PI3K–AKT signalling pathway were the most significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 2L). PI3K-AKT signaling pathway was selected for further investigation. In thermoradiotherapy groups, ITGA5, p-PI3K, and p-AKT were inhibited in both U251 and LN229 cell lines compared with Ctrl groups (Fig. 2M), which verified what was found in proteomic analysis. The expression level of PI3K and AKT was similar among Ctrl, HT, RT, and thermoradiotherapy groups. It revealed that ITGA5 suppressed the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT by regulating PI3K-AKT signaling pathway.

ITGA5 promotes the proliferation and metastasis of GBM in vitro and in vivo

ITGA5 was overexpressed using a lentivirus system in U251 and LN229 cells to analyse its biological function in GBM. WB and qRT-PCR confirmed ITGA5 overexpression (ITGA5-OE) at the mRNA and protein levels in both cell lines (Fig. 3A, B). Phenotypic changes, including alterations in cell viability and proliferation efficiency induced by ITGA5, were further examined using colony formation assays and EdU labelling. The colony formation assay demonstrated enhanced clonogenicity in the ITGA5-OE groups compared with that in the ITGA5-Ctrl groups of both U251 and LN229 cells (Fig. 3C; Fig. S6A). The EdU assay revealed increased proliferation rates in the ITGA5-OE group (Fig. 3D). Moreover, WB analysis demonstrated that PCNA and cyclin D1 were elevated, whereas P53 was reduced in ITGA5-OE groups compared to ITGA5-Ctrl groups (Fig. 3B). The impact of ITGA5 on proliferation in vivo was further explored using an intracranial tumor model. MRI showed larger volumes in ITGA5-OE group compared to ITGA5-Ctrl group (Fig. 3E). The Kaplan-Meier survivorship curve demonstrated significantly worse overall survival in the ITGA5-OE group than the ITGA5-Ctrl group (Fig. 3G). Meanwhile, the subcutaneous tumor model also identified that the volumes and weights of tumors were more prominent in ITGA5-OE group (Fig. 3F, H, I). The expression of proteins related to proliferation in tissues demonstrated a similar trend (Fig. 3J). On the other hand, shRNA-mediated gene silencing was employed to achieve ITGA5 knockdown in U251 and LN229 cell lines. The optimal knockdown cell line (sh-ITGA5#2) and control cell line (sh-RNA) were chosen for further analysis (Fig. 3K, L). The colony formation and EdU assay revealed that a marked reduction in cell viability and proliferation in sh-ITGA5 groups relative to sh-RNA groups (Fig. 3M, N, S6B). The sh-ITGA5 groups treated with thermoradiotherapy exhibited significantly reduced GBM proliferation (Fig. 3N, S6B). Specifically, the expression of PCNA and cyclin D1 decreased, whereas that of P53 increased in the knockdown groups (Fig. 3L). Furthermore, re-overexpressed ITGA5 was able to rescue the cell proliferation and proliferation-associated protein expression levels in the sh-ITGA5 group (Fig. 3L,N). Smaller tumour volumes, lighter tumour weights, and improved overall survival were observed in the sh-ITGA5 group in the intracranial and subcutaneous tumour models (Fig. 3O–S). Discussion

In this study, HT was used to increase the efficiency of RT for GBM, and the results showed that thermoradiotherapy could effectively inhibit the proliferation and metastasis of GBM cells and reverse anoikis resistance of GBM. Our proteomic analysis revealed significant downregulation of ITGA5 in GBM subjected to thermoradiotherapy, accompanied by statistically discernible alterations in PI3K/AKT signaling pathway enrichment. Single-cell RNA sequencing of GBM revealed that RT suppress the expression of ITGA5 and induces an immunosuppressive microenvironment characterized by TAM expansion and endothelial reduction. Subsequent Co-IP assays confirmed direct molecular interaction between ITGA5 and PI3K. Functional validation through ITGA5 overexpression and knockdown models demonstrated its pivotal role in modulating GBM proliferation, metastasis, and anoikis resistance via PI3K/AKT pathway regulation, both in vitro and in vivo.

HT has emerged as a promising oncological intervention, achievable through pharmacological agents or specialized medical devices [16,17]. When precisely controlled to exceed the critical threshold of 42 °C, this thermal modality induces irreversible protein denaturation and then triggers programmed cell death through apoptotic pathways. Furthermore, significant chemosensitization effects are observed under hyperthermal conditions (40–45 °C), where increased vascular perfusion and plasma membrane fluidity synergistically enhance chemotherapeutic biodistribution while reducing requisite dosages without compromising tumor response rates [18]. While traditional approaches to achieve HT rely on electromagnetic energy deposition through radiofrequency, ultrasound, or microwave region [18,19], contemporary advances has established another two thermal delivery systems including magnetic fields and near-infrared (NIR) radiation [12].

NIR-mediated HT encompasses two distinct energy transduction modalities. The first modality, laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT), employs percutaneous optical fibers to deliver focused NIR irradiation (980–1064 nm wavelength range) directly to lesions, inducing spatially confined photothermal ablation through controlled light-tissue interactions. This approach integrates real-time magnetic resonance thermometry for thermal dose optimization, leveraging the linear temperature dependence of water proton resonance frequency. Neuro-oncological applications demonstrate particular therapeutic utility, with evidence supporting LITT efficacy in managing surgically inaccessible GBM, radioresistant cerebral metastases, and radiation-induced necrosis [20]. The second approach employs nanotechnology-mediated photothermal therapy, where engineered nanomaterials with photothermal conversion ability serve as localized energy transducers. These nanoagents accumulate preferentially in tumor tissue through enhanced permeability and retention effects, converting absorbed NIR energy into cytotoxic hyperthermia for targeted thermal ablation. This emerging modality offers spatial precision exceeding conventional hyperthermia techniques while minimizing off-target thermal damage [18].

The present study investigated the therapeutic potential of HT in GBM management, elucidating multimodal mechanisms underlying its anti-tumor activity. By establishing an in vitro thermal exposure model, we demonstrated that HT or RT monotherapy could slightly inhibit the proliferation of GBM cells, but the thermoradiotherapy group could significantly inhibit the proliferation of GBM cells. Notably, mild HT (40–44 °C) demonstrated multimodal therapeutic potentiation, including radiosensitization, chemosensitization, and immunomodulation. Meta-analytical evidence quantified substantial improvements in locoregional control for recurrent malignancies following thermoradiotherapy versus radiotherapy alone, with obvious increases of 22.3 % (breast carcinoma), 22.1 % (cervical cancer), and 25.5 % (head/neck tumors) [21]. In addition, thermo-chemoradiotherapy protocols reduced treatment failure rates and mortality of chemoradiotherapy by 10.1 % and 5.6 %, respectively, in advanced cervical cancer cohorts [21]. Studies have also shown that preconditioning with HT prior to RT enhanced therapeutic responses of adenocarcinoma [22], osteosarcoma [23] and glioma stem cells [24] by inhibiting the proliferation of tumor cells, suggesting that thermoradiotherapy can be a potential therapy for GBM.

Resistance to anoikis in both physiological events and malignancies is recognised as a critical feature of oncogenic EMT, a process involving carcinogenesis and metastasis by promoting migration, invasion, and resistance to anoikis [25,26]. EMT has been recognized as one of the mechanisms that confer the invasive and metastatic properties of GBM cells [27,28]. It is a complex biological process in which cells are converted from an epithelial phenotype to a mesenchymal phenotype, exhibiting reduced cell adhesion, enhanced migratory and invasive capabilities, and resistance to anoikis [29]. Further, several studies support the role of EMT in morphological alterations, immune evasion, chemotherapy/radiotherapy resistance, anoikis, and stemness [30]. Therefore, exploring the effect of thermoradiotherapy on EMT may reveal its potential in reversing resistance to RT and anoikis. In this study, we evaluated the migratory and invasive capacities of GBM cells using Transwell assays, which demonstrated that thermoradiotherapy elicited marked suppression of both migratory and invasive behavior in GBM cellular models compared to monotherapy groups. These findings were further substantiated by molecular analyses revealing significant attenuation of EMT progression, as evidenced by downregulation of N-cadherin and Vimentin coupled with upregulation of E-cadherin. The coordinated inhibition of both physical motility and fundamental EMT reprogramming mechanisms provides compelling evidence of the anti-GBM efficacy in thermoradiotherapy.

Anoikis is initiated and executed through multiple pathways converging at caspase activation, ultimately leading to endonuclease activation, DNA fragmentation, and cell death. Anoikis is regulated by two apoptotic pathways: the intrinsic (mitochondrial) and extrinsic (death receptor) [31]. The proteins of the BCL-2 family play a central role in these processes and can be classified into three groups: (i) anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bcl-2); (ii) multidomain pro-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bax); and (iii) BH3-only pro-apoptotic proteins [32]. The present findings demonstrate that thermoradiotherapy effectively counteracts anoikis resistance in GBM by downregulation of the anti-anoikis marker Bcl-2 and upregulation of the pro-anoikis effector Bax. This regulatory shift in anoikis signaling pathways underscores the therapeutic capacity of thermoradiotherapy to restore programmed cell death sensitivity in GBM cells, thereby overcoming anoikis resistance.

Integrins, as transmembrane receptors, mediate cell adhesion to extracellular matrices and adjacent cells. ITGA5 is a fibronectin/fibrinogen receptor subunit, implicated in oncogenesis across diverse malignancies, including glioma [33], head/neck squamous carcinoma [34], non-small cell lung cancer [35], breast carcinoma [36], hepatocellular carcinoma[37], colorectal cancer[38], and clear cell renal carcinoma [39]. Consistently, ITGA5 overexpression correlates with tumor progression, metastasis, and poor prognosis [40,41]. In this study, multi-model validated the oncogenic role of ITGA5 in GBM. Proteomic analysis of anoikis-related proteins identified ITGA5 as a key downregulated target in thermoradiotherapy of GBM. This suppression underlies the observed anti-GBM efficacy, as ITGA5 inhibition restores anoikis sensitivity. Single-cell transcriptomic profiling delineated the complex remodeling of the GBM immune microenvironment following RT, positioning ITGA5 as a potential nexus between radiation-induced alterations and a pro-survival, anoikis-resistant phenotype [42]. The observed RT-driven expansion of TAMs, concomitant with a reduction in endothelial cells, suggests a shift toward a more immunosuppressive and pro-invasive niche. Notably, ITGA5 expression was found to be predominantly localized to two key cellular compartments: endothelial cells and TAMs [43]. Given the well-established role of ITGA5, in partnership with integrin β1 (ITGB1), in mediating fibronectin binding and activating pro-survival FAK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathways [43], its specific expression pattern strongly implies a functional role in conferring resistance to anoikis within the GBM tumor microenvironment.

Anoikis resistance in cancer cells is driven by integrin alterations, pro-survival and oncogenic signalling pathways, and overexpression or mutation of growth factor receptors [44]. Furthermore, the tumor microenvironment also plays a critical role on anoikis resistance through ECM stiffness regulation, pro-survival factors production, oxidative stress, EMT induction, and metabolic changes [45]. These processes allow cancer cells to evade anoikis machinery and survive after detachment. Fujita et al. linked GBM aggressiveness with ITGB1 activation via upregulation of tenascin-C-derived peptide TNIIIA2 and FNIII14. Notably, FNIII14 reversed TNIIIA2-mediated anoikis resistance, indicating that targeting ITGB1 could counteract anoikis resistance in GBM [46]. Integrins, including ITGA5 and ITGB1, regulate cell adhesion, signaling, and biological functions, highlighting the critical role of ITGA5 in mediating anoikis resistance. Integrins, particularly ITGA5 and ITGB1, play key roles in cell adhesion and signaling, emphasizing their importance in anoikis resistance mechanisms [47,48].

HT and RT intrinsically induce an oxidative stress response in GBM cells, leading to the generation of substantial reactive oxygen species (ROS). These ROS regulate the expression of ITGA5 by activating TGFβ/SMAD signaling pathway [49] or transcription factors such as NF-κB and AP-1 [50]. Subsequently, ITGA5 activates the FAK and Src signaling pathways, which in turn stimulates NADPH oxidase activity, resulting in increased ROS production [49]. This establishes a positive feedback loop. The present study primarily focuses on the impact of combined thermoradiotherapy on anoikis. Therefore, our investigation centers on ITGA5, as identified by proteomics sequencing data, rather than focusing on alterations in ROS levels and their regulatory effects on ITGA5. This latter aspect warrants further in-depth investigation in subsequent studies.

Accumulating evidence underscores the critical involvement of the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway in GBM carcinogenesis and maintenance [51]. Dysregulation of ITGA5 affects the expression of downstream components of the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway, which are integral for various biological processes, including cell differentiation, proliferation, migration, and invasion [52]. A study of human gastric cancer reported that ITGA5 regulates PI3K and AKT expression [53]. In addition, as a kinase, PI3K/AKT is activated by phosphorylation and subsequently regulates essential cellular functions, including proliferation, migration, invasion, and cell death [5]. Phosphorylation-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling pathway activation is also closely associated with cancer development [54]. The current study provides evidence that ITGA5 overexpression contributes to the proliferation and metastasis of GBM cells by activating PI3K/AKT signalling. Conversely, ITGA5 knockdown remarkably suppresses the activation of PI3K/AKT signalling, thereby reducing proliferation and metastasis of GBM and reversing anoikis resistance. These findings imply that ITGA5 plays a crucial role in GBM progression, metastasis, and anoikis by activating the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway.

In conclusion, we highlighted ITGA5 as a therapeutic target for the thermoradiotherapy of GBM. Further investigation revealed that ITGA5 promotes proliferation, metastasis, and anoikis resistance in GBM by regulating the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway. Consequently, targeting ITGA5 holds significant promise for improving GBM outcomes through biomarker-driven therapies.
