Econometric analysis of the long-run relationship between preventive care spending and mortality: evidence from OECD countries, 1970–2019
Authors: Mehdi Ammi, Farzaneh Davarzani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
While the share of health spending from public sources dedicated to preventive care has increased, the extent to which this preventive care spending can reduce mortality is still uncertain, mainly since effects may occur only in the long run. This paper takes advantage of a recent econometric method to empirically examine the long-run relationship between mortality and public preventive care spending in 37 OECD countries from 1970 to 2019. We construct an unbalanced longitudinal dataset on all-cause mortality and public preventive spending from publicly available OECD datasets. We detect cointegration and cross-sectional dependence in our data. This leads us to use the dynamic common correlated effects (DCCE) panel error correction model from Chudik and Pesaran (2015) to address these issues and account for heterogeneity across OECD countries. Our results indicate a long-run preventive care spending elasticity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}-0.10 in the OECD, and Granger non-causality tests suggest this may be a causal effect of spending on mortality. We also find that the long-run preventive care spending elasticity is of +0.04 for life expectancy at age 65. To better understand mechanisms, we explore the subcategories of preventive care spending and find that early disease detection programs and immunization programs drive the mortality reduction. To compare with other government health expenditures, we run our models using inpatient and outpatient healthcare expenditures as predictors and find the long-run association with mortality is less consistent. Overall, our findings indicate that higher preventive care spending may help reduce mortality in the long run in OECD countries, but this relationship is likely small.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00181-025-02820-2.

Introduction

Health expenditure is a major source of spending for governments around the world. For the richest economies, government health spending has significantly increased from 3% to 8% of the GDP between 1970 and 2021.1 An essential goal of health expenditures is to improve the health of the population, which in turn has been the object of much research in health economics (Tanaka et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2022; Gallet and Doucouliagos 2017; Hall and Jones 2007; Piérard 2016). As such, assessing the value of these expenditures is a concern for governments, and the present paper will contribute to this endeavor by investigating the relationship between public preventive care spending and health outcomes in the long run in OECD countries.

A growing body of empirical evidence studies the relationship between healthcare spending and health outcomes, as summarized in a recent meta-regression analysis (Gallet and Doucouliagos 2017). In their meta-regression, the authors find that health expenditures can affect health outcomes, with the expenditure elasticity for mortality being \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}-0.13. High-income country-specific analysis tends to find that higher government expenditures are associated with better health outcomes (Crémieux et al. 1999), even if it is not always the case (Piérard 2016). For example, using an instrumental variable approach, Edney et al. (2018) find that a 1% increase in government health spending is associated with a 2.2% decrease in the number of standardized years of life lost. An important consideration is that not all government health spending is the same, whether in physicians, hospitals, or preventive care.

Preventive care is a sub-component of government health spending, which has been a source of increasing attention with the COVID-19 pandemic. Spending on preventive care includes programs and services such as early disease detection, immunization, health promotion, and surveillance and control of outbreaks. Government spending on preventive care doubled from 1970 to 2021 in the OECD countries, from 3% to 6% of the share of total health expenditures.2

Understanding whether government expenditures on preventive care in OECD countries improve health outcomes has important policy implications. The sustainability of government health expenditures is a concern in OECD countries (Fineberg 2012; Berman 1995; Di Matteo 2010), and preventive care is often referred to as a way to reduce future healthcare costs (Cohen et al. 2008), with several preventive interventions deemed cost-effective (Masters et al. 2017). Our study therefore provides evidence on whether public preventive care spending could support better health outcomes in the long run.

While a proxy measure for health outcomes, mortality-based indicators are commonly used. In the health economics literature, most studies use mortality rate or life expectancy as health outcomes measures to analyze the effects of health expenditures: for example, out of 65 studies included in a systematic review, 48 studies estimate spending elasticities for mortality and 11 studies estimate spending elasticities for both mortality and life expectancy (Gallet and Doucouliagos 2017). In the health system performance literature, there is a recognition that mortality-based indicators such as mortality rates and potential years of life lost are valid metrics of health system performance (Allin and Grignon 2014; Liang et al. 2023). Given its preponderant use in the literature, our primary focus is on mortality rates, although we will also explore other health outcomes. During 1970–2021, all-cause mortality rates decreased in the OECD countries from 1550/100,000 inhabitants to 1180/100,000.

Furthermore, there are theoretical reasons for investigating public preventive care expenditure. The theoretical literature in the economics of prevention is primarily motivated by Grossman’s health capital model (Grossman 1972). The demand for preventive care derives from the investment motive in the demand for health (Kenkel 1994). Broadly speaking, spending on preventive care is an input in the production of health. Market failures such as externalities, imperfect information, and ex ante moral hazard provide theoretical justifications for government investment in prevention policies (Kenkel 2000).

Investing in prevention policies, that is, health surveillance, health promotion, and disease prevention, is expected to contribute to improving population health outcomes (Gmeinder et al. 2017; OECD 2015; Mergl et al. 2015). The WHO (2014) indicates that a wide range of preventive measures are cost-effective or even cost-savings, such as interventions to promote healthy behaviors, disease prevention through vaccination and screening, and interventions addressing social and environmental health determinants. In England, Owen et al. (2012) conclude that most of these public health interventions are highly cost-effective. Even if these interventions do not lead to cost-savings, the current evidence suggests that many interventions lead to better health outcomes at relatively low costs compared to the status quo or alternative interventions. Furthermore, investing in properly designed prevention policies can result in substantial economic benefits through a healthier and more active workforce leading to higher pension and welfare benefits (Gmeinder et al. 2017). That said, not all preventive interventions will be efficient in all OECD countries: in England, between one-quarter and one-third of preventive interventions are not cost-effective (Owen and Fischer 2019). Furthermore, preventive government spending tends to be subject to broad cuts rather than case-by-case claw-backs based on the inefficiency of the interventions (Jacques and Noël 2022). Hence, understanding how government preventive spending affects health outcomes broadly rather than on some specific interventions is an important endeavor, complementary to specific case-by-case cost-effectiveness studies.

Although previous studies examined the relationship between government health spending and health outcomes, we contribute to the literature by (1) our focus on the specific category of public preventive expenditures, which has high policy relevance, and (2) taking into account that preventive expenditures are expected to have effects mainly in the long run. This sectoral focus is rare in the literature, with a few exceptions, including a focus on pharmaceutical spending (Crémieux et al. 1999), and the literature on health expenditures is predominantly short run (Gallet and Doucouliagos 2017). Some studies explore the short-run relationship between preventive spending and mortality: Martin et al. (2020) do so using cross-sectional data from the UK and Mays and Smith (2011) by using longitudinal data from the USA, and both find that higher preventive spending reduces mortality. The closest to our study uses county-level public health expenditure data from the state of California and an augmented distributed lag model and finds that these expenditures decrease all-cause mortality (Brown 2013). We improve on this previous work by expanding the analysis to a larger number of countries and a longer period and by using an empirical strategy that accounts for the specificity of preventive spending and addresses additional econometric challenges. Specifically, we use longitudinal data on 37 OECD countries from 1970 to 2019 and a panel error correction model (ECM) called the dynamic common correlated estimator (DCCE) introduced by Chudik and Pesaran (2015). The ECM was first used in the health economics literature by Roberts (1999) to study the determinants of health expenditures. The DCCE extends the ECM and allows to overcome some important time-series data challenges by employing a panel cointegration approach while addressing cross-sectional dependence, parameter heterogeneity, and data stationarity. Since a defining characteristic of preventive care is for outcomes to take time to materialize (Gmeinder et al. 2017), the DCCE approach allows us to obtain long-run estimates for this relation. To the best of our knowledge, our paper is the first to study the relationship between public preventive care spending and mortality across OECD countries taking advantage of recent econometric advances, with the DCCE method.

Our findings suggest that a 10% increase in the long-run (i.e., from 1970 to 2019) public preventive care spending is associated with a 1% reduction in all-cause mortality. We also examine the relationship between all-cause mortality and sub-components of preventive care spending, called functions in the OECD System of Health Accounts, to better understand how public preventive spending can influence mortality. We find the long-run negative association of public preventive spending with all-cause mortality is primarily driven by spending on early disease detection programs and immunization programs with a spending elasticity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}-0.43 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}-0.14, respectively. Since all-cause mortality includes causes of death that may not be preventable, we also explore the long-run association with other mortality outcomes (e.g., infant and cancer mortality), potential behavioral drivers of preventable mortality such as smoking and alcohol consumption, as well as life expectancy. The most noticeable finding is a long-run elasticity of life expectancy at age 65 of +0.04, suggesting a cumulative effect of public preventive care spending benefiting later in people’s life. Furthermore, we assess how public preventive spending long-run elasticity is compared to government health spending in outpatient and inpatient care, and only find non-statistically significant results for these categories. Lastly, we assess the plausibility of a causal interpretation of our main findings with Granger non-causality tests, and test results suggest that public preventive spending may cause mortality reduction in the long-run. While this long-run investigation of preventive spending and health outcomes in the OECD is new, our results are broadly consistent with the existing literature that examines the positive role of government expenditures on health outcomes (McCullough and Leider 2016; Gallet and Doucouliagos 2017).

Empirical strategy and data

Data

We collected data on 37 OECD member countries from 1970 to 2019, primarily from the OECD iLibrary Health Statistics (OECD 2021a) and complemented by data from the World Bank.3 We voluntarily excluded 2020 due to it being the highly unusual COVID-19 pandemic first year. Public preventive care spending is measured in per capita current USD purchasing power parity (PPP) units to account for the differences in health prices across countries. All-cause mortality is measured in death per 100,000 population (standardized rate). We selected all-cause mortality as our main indicator due to its high prevalence in the health expenditures literature (Gallet and Doucouliagos 2017), but also because it has the most comprehensive data series compared to other mortality-based indicators. Nevertheless, we consider alternative mortality-based indicators in additional analysis (section 3.3). The data series are unbalanced and have an annual frequency; hence, we have a dataset of 927 country-years for our two key variables of interest. The choice of countries was first based on having data on all-cause mortality and public preventive spending and then the availability of data on the other explanatory variables. In the online Appendix, Table A1 displays the countries included in our data. We drop any countries with less than eight observations in preventive care spending, which are listed in Table A2.

As a first look at the relationship between public preventive care spending and all-cause mortality, we regress the average preventive care spending, over 1970 to 2019, per country on the average all-cause mortality and report results in Figure A1 in Appendix. It appears that there is a moderately weak relationship between public preventive spending and mortality in the pooled data, which, while it ignores the time dimension that is the focus of our study, encourages further investigation. We also note that some countries (Israel and Japan) are potential outliers whose influence will be assessed in robustness checks.

We then move on to check the statistical properties of the data to use appropriate econometric modeling, given the specific challenges of the OECD health care spending data (Roberts 1999). Recall our focus is on long-run relationship, and three main sets of tests are used in the applied macroeconomic literature. The first set of methods checks stationarity, the second explores cointegration, and the third explores cross-sectional dependence. We run Karavias and Tzavalis (2014)’s unit-root test to assess the data stationarity, use Pedroni (2004, 1999)’s cointegration test for panel dataset, and assess cross-sectional dependence using the CD test developed by Chudik and Pesaran (2015). We conduct Karavias and Tzavalis (2014)’s unit-root test to check for a possible unit-root problem in the panel data. Table A7 in Appendix shows the result using this unit-root test under different specifications. Overall, the test results indicate that the panel is non-stationary, leading us to apply the first difference method. Figure 1 shows all-cause mortality per 100,000 population in levels, while Figure 2 displays it in the first differences. Similarly, Figure 3 shows preventive care spending in levels, and Figure 4 displays it in the first differences. As further shown in these figures, the first-differenced data appear stationary.

The cointegration tests developed by Pedroni (2004, 1999) share a common null hypothesis of no cointegration and allow for panel-specific autoregressive parameters.4 Table A8 in Appendix displays the cointegration test results, showing overwhelming evidence of cointegration in the data.

We finally test for cross-sectional dependence using the CD test of Chudik and Pesaran (2015) in the levels of the variables. We also conducted the same tests for the first differences in the variables. The results from both tests are similar, reported in Table A9 in Appendix, and indicate that all variables are subject to cross-sectional dependence.5

Overall, the investigation of the data properties indicates the presence of cointegration, non-stationarity, and cross-sectional dependence. All of this makes the standard two-way fixed effects inappropriate. Next, we describe the methodology dealing with these specific econometric challenges.

Empirical strategy

This paper uses the dynamic common correlated effects (DCCE) approach to investigate the long-run relationship between public preventive care spending and all-cause mortality, an estimation procedure developed by Chudik and Pesaran (2015) to improve on previous error correction models (ECM) (Pesaran 2006; Pesaran and Smith 1995; Pesaran et al. 1999). The dynamic common correlated effects overcome many issues in time-series panel data, present in OECD health care spending data (Roberts 1999). It deals with cointegration and non-stationarity and allows fitting models with heterogeneous coefficients in a panel with many observations over cross-sectional units and periods. Allowing for such heterogeneity is important in models relying on cross-country analysis where a variety of cross-country differences remain unobserved. But most importantly, the DCCE addresses issues of cross-sectional dependence, which are likely to happen at least across a subset of OECD countries (e.g., countries of the European Union) or more broadly in cases of common shocks (e.g., a global financial crisis). The DCCE controls for cross-sectional dependence by adding cross-sectional averages and lags among the regressors, as proposed by Pesaran (2006) and Chudik and Pesaran (2015). In health-related studies, this model has been applied by Rodriguez Llorian and Mann (2022) to investigate the relationship between medical technology and healthcare expenditures. To our knowledge, our paper is the first to use this model to investigate the relationship between health expenditures and health outcomes.6

This DCCE is essentially an error correction model expanded by including cross-sectional averages and lagged cross-sectional averages (F) to the right-hand side of the equation in Eq. (1) and estimated using a mean group estimator (Pesaran and Smith 1995). The vector of control variables, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{it}$$\end{document}Xit, contains the GDP per capita in USD PPP (gdp), the proportion of the population over 65 years of age (pop65), the unemployment rate (unem), the sex ratio which is the male-to-female sex ratio calculated by dividing the number of males by the number of females and multiplying by 100 (sexratio), the number of live births (birth), and the fertility rate (fertility).

Our choice of control variables is guided by the literature, summarized in Gallet and Doucouliagos (2017), and data availability across our long series. We aim to control for confounding in the public preventive spending and mortality’s relationship to the best the data allows. Specifically, gross domestic product is a well-known determinant of mortality. Hall and Jones (2007) argue that, in theory, as people get wealthier, their marginal utility of consumption reduces rapidly. As a result, rational consumers want to spend more on health to improve their well-being and lifespan. Many studies indicate a statistical correlation between income and mortality, values of the correlation having increased over the past forty years (Feldman et al. 1989; Pappas et al. 1993; Preston and Elo 1995; Deaton and Paxson 1998; Marmot 2006). Thus, our model includes GDP per capita as an indicator of countries’ income levels. A second strand of the literature emphasizes population aging as one critical driver of mortality and expenditures (Economos 1982; Howdon and Rice 2018). Other empirical papers discuss the correlation between aging and mortality (Mathers et al. 2015; Smith and Kington 1997; Preston et al. 1996; Carey et al. 1992). Therefore, we also include a population over 65 as a control variable. There is a large literature on the relationship between unemployment and mortality (Ruhm 2000, 2015, 2016); therefore, we include the unemployment rate in our controls. Additionally, there is evidence of sex differences in the risk of death. For instance, George et al. (2003) and Schoevers et al. (2000) found an association between sex and mortality among blunt trauma or depressed patients in old age. We control for this with our sex ratio variable. The literature also discusses the relation between fertility and mortality rates which have been in approximate balance (Fehr et al. 2008; Bell 1997; Krause 1958; Galloway 1988). For instance, Frederiksen (1966) indicate that high birth rates approximately matched high death rates with rising levels of living. Hence, we also control for fertility rates.

The model presented in Eqs. (1) and (2) is our baseline model. As will be later developed, acknowledging that all-cause mortality is a broad health measure, we will investigate the relationship of public preventive spending with other health measures. Similarly, to better understand how public preventive spending compares to other government health spending, such as inpatient expenditures, when it comes to mortality reduction, we will run different versions of the DCCE. We will provide more details on these alternative DCCE models in the results section for ease of exposition.

This section explores alternative health outcomes to supplement the rather crude all-cause mortality outcome. We start our exploration of alternative health outcomes with mortality-based indicators. In terms of mortality-based indicators, infant mortality and cancer mortality are important additional health outcomes to investigate. Owusu et al. (2021) indicate that early intervention can promote children’s and mothers’ health. Similarly, cancer deaths are preventable to an extent with appropriate health promotion strategies and screening, and we found that early disease detection program had the highest spending elasticity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}-0.42, yet in a regression with additional control variables). We therefore consider these different outcomes in Table 5. We only find negative but not significant long-run association between preventive spending and infant mortality, and between preventive spending and cancer mortality. However, GDP appears to be the long-run driver of reducing infant mortality. DALYs measure the number of years of healthy life lost due to a disease, by combining premature mortality and years of healthy life lost due to disability, and therefore capture both mortality and morbidity (Ferrari et al. 2024). Thus, even if a debate exists regarding the appropriate weighting of disabilities in DALYs (e.g., Arnesen and Nord (1999); Salomon et al. (2012)), this metric can help assess whether preventive spending affects morbidity. Unfortunately, DALY data are only available from 1990 to 2019 and for a subset of the countries studied. With these limits in mind, and while the sign is negative consistently with other outcomes, we do not find a statistically significant long-run association between preventive spending and DALYs.

The last set of outcomes explores the role of behavioral factors in mortality reduction. We assess if there is a long-run relationship between public preventive spending and alcohol consumption or daily smoking. Results are reported in Table A11 in Appendix. There is no significant long-run association with preventive spending for any of those outcomes, but it must be noted that daily smokers regressions have a much smaller sample.

Overall, the exploration of these alternative health outcomes indicates that public preventive spending is primarily associated with health improvement by extending the life of older adults, as evidenced by increases in life expectancy at age 65. Additionally, the qualitative similarity between the spending elasticity for potential years of life lost (PYLL), which captures avoidable deaths, and that for all-cause mortality provides reassurance. Taken together with the main finding of a reduction of all-cause mortality, the increases in life expectancy at age 65 may reflect the cumulative influence of preventive spending over the life cycle.

Granger causality and robustness checks

Granger causality

All our findings so far are correlational: indeed, our method does not aim to demonstrate causal relationships but focuses on the long-run association while dealing with the data properties with an appropriate DCCE. That said, the key policy question is whether increasing public preventive spending would improve health in the long-run. To get a sense of whether the long-run association of public preventive spending with mortality captures a causal relationship, we test for Granger causality, which is standard in the applied macroeconomics literature. Precisely, we run a test to detect Granger non-causality from public preventive care spending to all-cause mortality in heterogeneous panels using the procedure developed by Dumitrescu and Hurlin (2012). Table 7 displays this Granger causality test results, showing that increased public preventive care spending does Granger-cause reduction in all-cause mortality for at least one of the panels. Robustness checks

This section run a last series of robustness checks, described below. We aim to assess the robustness of our findings to the influence of outliers, to the difference in level of development of the countries and across sexes, and to the inclusion of a research and development (R&D) expenditures among regressors. Overall, the main findings appear quite robust.

Influence of outliers: We check if the long-run relationship we detected between public preventive spending and mortality is maintained while excluding the outliers in our sample. These outliers are Israel and Japan, primarily identified with Figure A1 in the Appendix. Figure A2 in the Appendix displays the relationship in country time-averages excluding these outliers. Table A13 in the appendix shows the baseline results with outliers excluded. The long-run elasticity of public preventive spending with mortality is unaffected (elasticity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}-0.10). Hence, our results are robust to the inclusion of outliers.

Level of development: We assess the robustness of our findings on the long-run association between public preventive care spending and mortality if we divide countries into high-income (22 countries) and middle-income (15 countries) ones (see Table A14 for country list). This categorization is based on the World Development Index 2015.7 Table A15 in Appendix shows the results of this estimation. The long-run elasticity of public preventive care spending on mortality in the high-income group is lower (but not significant) than in the middle-income group, which may speak to preventive programs being more effective in middle-income groups sitting relatively more on the left of the Preston curve (Bloom and Canning 2007) linking health and economic development. Indeed, the marginal health benefit of spending is higher at lower levels of income, that is middle-income countries in this case.

Sex-specific mortality analysis: There are differences in the utilization of preventive care by sex, so preventive spending may have a different influence on male and female mortality. Table A16 in Appendix shows the results of this estimation. The size of the long-run elasticity is qualitatively similar between male and female mortality, the largest difference being that the estimate for male is statistically insignificant, while it is significant for female (about \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}-0.10). Thus, it is possible that long-term mortality reduction from public preventive spending is mainly driven by females.

Total gross expenditure on research and development (R&D): Finally, we incorporate a measure of R&D expenditure as a short-term variable in our baseline model. R&D, a proxy for technology, can act as a confounding variable, as technology measured in different ways is a determinant of health spending (Okunade et al. 2018) (Rodriguez Llorian and Mann 2022). R&D can also influence preventable mortality through advancements in preventive technologies, such as those used in cancer detection. To obtain this R&D measure, we gathered data on the overall gross spending on R&D from OECD iLibrary. However, it is important to note that this R&D indicator comes with heavy data constraints leading us to lose more than half of the sample, the reason why we only use this variable in a robustness check. Furthermore, the R&D encompasses various sources, both public and private, and does not specifically focus on the health sector; hence, it is rather crude. Nevertheless, results from this last set of analysis are in Table A17 in the Appendix. The long-run elasticity of public preventive spending is qualitatively similar, but not statistically significant, probably owing to a much smaller sample.

Conclusion

Using a long panel of about fifty years of observations, we have investigated the long-run relationships between public preventive care expenditures and mortality in OECD countries. We have decomposed public preventive care spending in different functions. Additionally, we have explored alternative health outcome measures to better understand the long-run association of public preventive care expenditures. We finally have assessed how the long-run association of public preventive care expenditures with mortality compares to other government health expenditures. Panel time-series data present several challenges in terms of stationarity, cointegration, and cross-sectional dependence that we have addressed using a dynamic common correlated effects (DCCE) approach. This approach further allowed us to account for the heterogeneity in our long-run relationship of interest across countries. To the best of our knowledge, the use of a DCCE approach and the focus on public preventive spending in OECD countries are novel in the literature.

Our key finding is a long-run public preventive spending elasticity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}-0.10. While such elasticity does not appear particularly big, it is in line with the (short-run) health spending elasticity recovered in the most recent meta-regression available (at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}-0.13, (Gallet and Doucouliagos 2017)). Importantly, the long-run spending elasticity of public preventive spending is higher than its short-run one. Furthermore, preventive spending on immunization and early disease detection programs appear to drive health benefits. In the long run, public preventive spending is also positively associated with life expectancy at 65, pointing toward cumulative effects of spending on preventive care. However, the elasticity is once again modest (+0.04). Overall, while modest, our findings suggest that preventive spending is an important sector of government health spending to promote population health.

The central limitation of this paper is that the preventive spending variable is endogenous. Precisely, the biggest concern is reverse causality. Indeed, while public preventive spending may decrease mortality, it is also partially determined by prior health outcomes. Using lagged value as in our DCCE only partly assuages this concern since contemporaneous and lagged preventive spending is also correlated. In canonical dynamic panel models (Arellano and Bond 1991), endogeneity is addressed by using lagged values as instruments. Still, these methods come with strong assumptions, and we are unaware of econometric approaches combining lagged values instruments and error correction framework. Fortunately, the empirical health economics literature suggests that the endogeneity bias pushes government health spending elasticity estimates downward (Edney et al. 2018), so our main estimate may likely be conservative. We further find evidence of Granger causality in our data, assuaging endogeneity concerns, at least through a sequential exogeneity but not a strict exogeneity perspective. Our dataset is unbalanced as focusing only on a balanced dataset of countries would have resulted in a too small sample of countries. Structural break tests are to be considered with caution in unbalanced panel, but future research exploring broader health expenditures, which have more comprehensive data, could estimate the extensions of the CCE model allowing for structural breaks on balanced data (Baltagi et al. 2016). Another limitation is that we analyze preventive care spending at the aggregate and national level, rather by specific preventative programs (e.g., influenza vaccinations, mammography screenings). However, the medical literature is full of cost-effectiveness studies summarized in systematic reviews (for example, Loong et al. (2022) and Tahir et al. (2021)) which are focused on the short run; hence, our study offers complementary evidence by exploring the long-run effect. We aimed to minimize this aggregation bias by analyzing the five available subcategories of public preventive care spending. Since others have shown that preventive care spending, as an aggregate, tends to be cut in austerity periods in the OECD (Jacques and Noël 2022), our finding that this aggregate public preventive spending can reduce mortality in the long run is important. Last, our focus on public preventive spending means that we do not capture the potential effect of private preventative activities funded by out-of-pocket or private insurance payments. Data quality issues prevented us from analyzing this category of spending as well, and it should be recognized that the vast majority of preventive care is publicly funded (Gmeinder et al. 2017). Perhaps the most puzzling is that we do not find strong evidence that more specific health outcomes (e.g., potential years of life lost, cancer deaths) are affected by public preventive spending. This may be due to data limitations and future research can aim to further investigate drivers of mortality reduction in specific countries.
