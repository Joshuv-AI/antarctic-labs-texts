Home Blood Pressure Phenotypes and Cardiovascular Remodeling in Hemodialysis
Authors: Meng-Kan Chen, Li-Pei Dai, Ru-Yin Hsu, Mu-Yang Hsieh, Hao-Min Cheng, Chih-Cheng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDialysis-unit blood pressure (BP) incompletely reflects the true BP burden in hemodialysis (HD), yet the clinical significance of BP phenotypes, particularly white-coat and masked patterns, remains uncertain. We examined associations between BP phenotypes defined by dialysis-unit and home BP and cardiac and vascular remodeling in maintenance HD.MethodsIn 217 adults receiving thrice-weekly HD, BP phenotypes were classified using predialysis BP (≥ 140/90 mm Hg) and standardized 7-day home BP (≥ 135/85 mm Hg) as sustained normotension (SNT), white-coat phenotype (WCH), masked hypertension (MHT), and sustained hypertension (SHT). The primary outcomes were left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and arterial stiffness (brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity [PWV, baPWV] ≥ 1800 cm/s). Multivariable logistic regression was adjusted for demographic and dialysis-specific covariates, with sensitivity analyses using alternative BP thresholds.ResultsPhenotype distribution was 29.5% SNT, 24.0% WCH, 6.9% MHT, and 39.6% SHT; nearly one-third of patients were misclassified by dialysis-unit BP alone. Compared with SNT, SHT was independently associated with LVH and arterial stiffness, whereas MHT showed the strongest association with arterial stiffness. WCH was not associated with LVH overall, but was associated with higher arterial stiffness. In stratified analyses, arterial stiffness increased across phenotypes regardless of treatment status, whereas excess LVH in WCH was observed only among untreated patients.ConclusionHome BP–based phenotyping reveals substantial misclassification by dialysis-unit BP and distinct patterns of cardiac and vascular remodeling in HD. Although SHT and MHT confer the greatest structural burden, WCH, particularly when untreated, is not entirely benign, highlighting the value of standardized home BP monitoring in dialysis care.Keywords: arterial stiffness, hemodialysis, home blood pressure, masked hypertension, white-coat hypertension

Hypertension is highly prevalent among patients receiving maintenance HD and contributes to cardiovascular remodeling and arterial stiffening; however, accurately characterizing BP burden in this population remains challenging. Dialysis-unit BP readings are affected by ultrafiltration, acute volume shifts, and sympathetic activation, and may not reflect interdialytic BP exposure. Consequently, reliance solely on peridialytic measurements risks substantial diagnostic misclassification and may obscure hemodynamic patterns most relevant to cardiovascular risk.1

Out-of-office BP monitoring provides a more reliable estimate of interdialytic BP load. Although ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM) offers valuable diagnostic and prognostic insights, it is impractical for routine HD care. Home BP monitoring, now recommended by international BP guidelines, represents a feasible alternative, although its ability to characterize BP phenotypes and their structural correlates in HD has been insufficiently studied.2, 3, 4 In the general population, discrepancies between office and out-of-office BP are often attributed to stress-related sympathetic activation. In patients on HD, substantial interdialytic volume fluctuations, accelerated arterial aging, vascular calcification, and autonomic dysfunction may influence BP dynamics and alter the association between BP phenotypes and target-organ remodeling, potentially differing markedly from observations in the general population.5, 6, 7, 8, 9

To address these gaps, we evaluated BP phenotypes using a standardized 7-day home BP protocol with dialysis-unit measurements. We aimed to determine the extent of misclassification when relying on dialysis-unit BP alone to characterize cardiac and vascular correlations of WCH and MHT phenotypes, and to assess the feasibility and clinical utility of home BP monitoring for phenotype-guided risk stratification in patients on HD.

Methods

Study Participants

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis within the ongoing Hsinchu Vascular Study, a prospective cohort initiated on January 1, 2022, which enrolls adults receiving thrice-weekly maintenance HD in 5 HD units in the Hsinchu District, Taiwan. The inclusion criteria were patients aged 18 to 99 years and on thrice-weekly dialysis for ≥ 3 months, with written informed consent obtained from all participants. We excluded patients dialyzing at a frequency > or < 3 sessions/wk, those with symptomatic heart failure (New York Heart Association functional class III or IV symptoms, supported by chest radiographic evidence of cardiomegaly and/or pulmonary congestion), poorly controlled fluid volume status (postdialysis cardiothoracic ratio > 60%), chronic atrial fibrillation, a recent change in dry weight or antihypertensive therapy (within 2 weeks), or hospitalization within the previous month. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of National Taiwan University Hospital.

BP Measurement

Dialysis-Unit Measurements

Dialysis-unit BP was recorded by study-participating dialysis nurses who had completed standardized BP measurement training and were certified by the principal investigator. The training covered patient positioning, rest period, arm positioning, and cuff placement. BP was recorded using the oscillometric module integrated into the HD machine. Measurements were obtained at every session over 1 week with the patient seated (back supported and legs uncrossed) after ≥ 5 minutes of rest. Two readings, separated by 1 minute, were averaged at each time point. For each participant, 3 predialysis and 3 postdialysis BP values were collected and averaged separately over the 1-week sampling period.

Home BP Monitoring

Home BP monitoring followed the contemporary international home-BP protocols.4,10 Participants or caregivers received standardized instructions from research nurses and used a validated oscillometric device (HEM-705 CP, Omron Healthcare, Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) provided by the study team. Measurements were obtained twice daily (morning and evening) for 7 consecutive days; at each sitting, 2 readings, 1 minute apart were averaged. If measurements were missing during the initial week, monitoring continued into the second week to obtain a complete dataset; the first 7 consecutive days with complete measurements were used for analysis.

BP Phenotypes

We use the term BP phenotypes to denote classification based on dialysis-unit and home BP measurements, independent of antihypertensive treatment status. WCH refers to elevated dialysis-unit BP with normal home BP; in treated patients, this corresponds to a white-coat effect or white-coat uncontrolled hypertension. For the primary analysis, 4 BP phenotypes were defined using a predialysis BP threshold of 140/90 mm Hg and a home BP threshold of 135/85 mm Hg. The dialysis-unit BP threshold was selected based on the 2005 Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative clinical practice guidelines. The home BP threshold of 135/85 mm Hg was adopted from established out-of-office BP definitions in general-population hypertension guidelines and a previous network meta-analysis,11 consistent with equivalence-based standards and previous dialysis studies, while acknowledging this limitation.12, 13, 14, 15 Accordingly, phenotypes were defined as follows: (i) SNT, predialysis BP mm Hg < 140/90 and home BP <135/85 mm Hg; (ii) WCH, predialysis BP ≥ 140/90 mm Hg and home BP < 135/85 mm Hg; (iii) MHT, predialysis BP < 140/90 mm Hg and home BP ≥ 135/85 mm Hg; and (iv) SHT, predialysis BP ≥ 140/90 mm Hg and home BP ≥ 135/85 mm Hg.

To assess the robustness of phenotype classification, 3 alternative combinations of dialysis-unit and home-BP thresholds were examined in sensitivity analyses.8,10,12,16 These include the following: (i) predialysis and home BP thresholds ≥ 140/90 and ≥ 130/80 mm Hg, respectively; (ii) postdialysis and home BP thresholds ≥ 130/80 and ≥ 135/85 mm Hg, respectively; and (iii) postdialysis and home BP thresholds ≥ 130/80 and ≥ 130/80 mm Hg, respectively.

Covariates

Baseline demographics, comorbidities, dialysis parameters, and medications were abstracted from electronic medical records using a standardized form. Comorbidities included coronary artery disease, heart failure, previous stroke, diabetes mellitus, and physician-diagnosed hypertension. Body mass index was calculated in kg/m2. Laboratory data and dialysis indices (e.g., interdialytic weight gain and urea clearance) were obtained using routine predialysis testing.

Cardiac and Vascular Assessment

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed on a midweek nondialysis day using a EPIQ 7 ultrasound system (Philips Medical Systems, Bothell, WA) with a 3.3-MHz phased-array transducer. A single experienced cardiologist (R-YH), blinded to all clinical data, performed and analyzed the studies. Left ventricular mass (LVM) was calculated using the Penn convention; LVM was indexed to the body surface area (LVM index).17 Echocardiographic measurements followed the American Society of Echocardiography recommendations; LVH was defined as LVM index > 115 g/m2 (in men) or > 95 g/m2 (in women).18

baPWV was measured on a midweek nondialysis day, in a dedicated vascular laboratory, by a trained technician using a validated waveform analyzer (form/ABI; Omron Colin Co., Ltd., Komaki, Aichi, Japan). After ≥ 5 minutes of supine rest, oscillometric cuffs were applied to the arm contralateral to the vascular access and to both ankles. Bilateral pressures and pulse volume waveforms were recorded simultaneously; the higher baPWV value was used for analysis. Arterial stiffness was defined a priori as baPWV ≥ 1800 cm/s, a threshold supported in East Asian cohorts.19,20

Statistical Analysis

The primary exposure was the 4-level BP phenotype (SNT, WCH, MHT, and SHT). The primary outcomes were LVH (yes/no) and arterial stiffness, defined as baPWV ≥ 1800 cm/s. LVM index and baPWV were additionally analyzed as complementary continuous outcomes to characterize the extent of cardiac and vascular remodeling across BP phenotypes. For group comparisons, we used analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. For binary outcomes (LVH and arterial stiffness), we used multivariable logistic regression with phenotype as the exposure and reported adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Covariates were prespecified based on established determinants of myocardial and vascular remodeling in HD and guided by the number of outcome events. For LVH, models were adjusted for age, sex, diabetes mellitus, dialysis vintage, body mass index, hemoglobin, interdialytic weight gain, serum albumin, and antihypertensive treatment status. For arterial stiffness, models were adjusted for age, sex, diabetes mellitus, dialysis vintage, body mass index, serum calcium and phosphate product, antihypertensive treatment status, and mean BP measured at the time of baPWV assessment to account for the pressure dependence of PWV.

Sensitivity analyses included the following: (i) redefining home hypertension at ≥130/80 mm Hg, aligned with the international guidelines, to reclassify phenotypes10,16 and (ii) alternative clinic definitions using predialysis only (≥ 140/90 mm Hg) or postdialysis only (≥ 130/80 mm Hg).12 Positive and negative predictive values were calculated from a 2×2 contingency table, with SHT representing true positives; WCH, false positives; MHT, false negatives; and SNT, true negatives. Two-sided P values < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Participant Characteristics

Among the patients enrolled in the Hsinchu Vascular Study during the 2022 study period, 355 patients consented to participate. Of these, 233 patients completed both transthoracic echocardiography and PWV assessments. After exclusion of 16 patients with incomplete home BP data, 217 patients were included in the final analysis, representing approximately 20% of patients treated in the participating HD centers. The adherence rate to home BP monitoring protocol was 93%. Age, gender, dialysis duration, and comorbidities did not differ significantly between adherent and nonadherent patients. Among the 217 participants with complete dialysis-unit and home BP data, the primary definition of BP phenotype distribution was: 64 SNT (29.5%), 52 WCH (24.0%), 15 MHT (6.9%), and 86 SHT (39.6%). SNT participants were older, whereas masked and sustained hypertensives tended to be younger (P = 0.009). Diabetes mellitus was more common in WCH and SHT (51%–65%) than normotensive participants (42%, P = 0.043). Calcium-channel-blocker use was the highest in SHT (51%, P = 0.01). As expected, both clinic and home BP increased progressively across categories from SNT, WCH, MHT, and SHT (all P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Risk of LVH and Arterial Stiffness

In multivariable logistic models using SNT as the reference (Table 3), SHT was independently associated with higher odds of LVH (adjusted OR: 2.84, 95% CI: 1.25–6.44) and arterial stiffness (adjusted OR: 3.52, 95% CI: 1.38–8.97). WCH was not associated with LVH but showed a significant association with arterial stiffness (adjusted OR: 2.84, 95% CI: 1.10–7.32). MHT demonstrated the strongest association with arterial stiffness (adjusted OR: 4.96, 95% CI: 1.06–23.15) and a borderline association with LVH.

Sensitivity Analyses

Results were consistent across alternative BP definitions (Supplementary Tables S1–S3). Lowering the home BP threshold to ≥ 130/80 mm Hg strengthened the associations for MHT and SHT, particularly for arterial stiffness. Using postdialysis BP as the clinic criterion modestly attenuated effect sizes but preserved overall risk gradients. Across all sensitivity analyses, SHT and MHT remained the phenotypes with the highest cardiac and vascular burden, whereas WCH consistently demonstrated intermediate vascular risk without overt cardiac remodeling.

Discussion

Main Findings

This study provides new insights into BP phenotypes in patients on maintenance HD using standardized home BP monitoring. Among 217 patients, SHT and WCH were the predominant patterns, whereas MHT was relatively uncommon. Nearly one-third of patients would have been misclassified if the diagnosis were based solely on dialysis-unit readings, underscoring the limitations of peridialytic BP assessment. Importantly, SHT and MHT were associated with the greatest cardiac and vascular burden, consistent with sustained interdialytic pressure overload. In contrast, WCH demonstrated a dissociation between vascular and cardiac correlations, with intermediate arterial stiffness but no corresponding excess in LVH.

Distribution of Phenotypes in the Context of Literature and Significance

The overall distribution of BP phenotypes in our cohort was broadly consistent with previous interdialytic ABPM studies, although the exact proportions varied with threshold and methodology.5,13 This pattern, predominance of SHT (39.6%) and WCH (24.0) with relatively few MHT (6.9%) cases, closely mirrors the findings of Agarwal et al.,5 who reported 35%, 15%, 15%, and 35% in corresponding categories using 44-hour ABPM. Modest differences likely reflect both methodological factors and dialysis-specific physiology. Home BP monitoring may underdetect MHT by capturing only daytime measurements and missing nocturnal or interdialytic surges detected by ABPM, whereas rapid volume shifts, heightened sympathetic activity, and stress before dialysis may transiently elevate dialysis-unit BP, increasing apparent WCH and reducing observed MHT.21

This mechanistic context helps explain why WCH was more prevalent and MHT less common in our HD cohort than in the general population.22,23 In community studies and meta-analyses, WCH is reported in approximately 15% to 30% of individuals with elevated clinic BP, whereas MHT affects nearly one-third of those with apparently normal office readings.22,23 The dialysis milieu therefore appears to shift balance toward WCH predominance as a characteristic hemodynamic phenotype in end-stage renal disease. Sensitivity analyses applying lower home BP cut-off points (≥ 130/80 mm Hg) or postdialysis BP definitions yielded only modest reclassification without changing the overall phenotype ranking, underscoring the robustness of these patterns.10,12, 13, 14, 15, 16

Clinically, this distribution is of direct relevance; nearly one-third of patients would have been misclassified if hypertension were defined solely using dialysis-unit BP. Although dialysis-unit and home SBP were moderately correlated, indicating that in-unit BP captures a substantial component of chronic BP burden, this population-level concordance does not preclude meaningful individual discordance. Standardized home BP monitoring therefore provides complementary value by identifying patients whose interdialytic BP exposure may be over- or underestimated by in-unit measurements alone.

Importantly, the interpretation of these phenotypes in HD requires caution, because optimal BP thresholds and their pathophysiological implications may differ from those in the general population. Dialysis-unit BP thresholds were informed by the 2005 Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative recommendations, which were largely extrapolated from nondialysis populations, whereas large observational studies in HD demonstrate a U- or J-shaped association between predialysis SBP and mortality, with the lowest risk often observed at higher SBP levels.24, 25, 26 Thus, dialysis-unit BP reflects a complex interplay of volume status, cardiac function, and illness severity rather than chronic hypertensive burden alone. Accordingly, WCH and MHT in HD should be viewed as context-specific hemodynamic phenotypes rather than direct analogs of those in the general population.

WCH and Arterial Remodeling

Emerging evidence in the general population indicates that WCH is associated with increased large-artery stiffness and should not be regarded as entirely benign.27 A recent meta-analysis of untreated individuals demonstrated that patients with WCH had significantly higher carotid-femoral PWV than normotensive controls, albeit lower than those with SHT.28,29 These observations suggest that transient clinic BP elevations may reflect underlying vascular aging or early arterial remodeling rather than purely situational stress response. In contrast, evidence of dialysis populations remains scarce. Although previous studies and reviews have consistently shown that arterial stiffness is markedly elevated in patients on HD and closely related to interdialytic BP burden, the specific contribution of white-coat BP patterns has not been examined.30,31

In the HD setting, elevated predialysis BP may reflect volume sensitivity, because measurements are obtained at the time of maximal interdialytic volume accumulation. However, in a related analysis of this cohort, predialysis SBP in the dialysis unit exceeded contemporaneous home SBP by approximately 13 mm Hg, whereas the true interdialytic SBP increase from the postdialysis nadir to the subsequent predialysis home peak was only 7.5 mm Hg, suggesting that environmental or situational factors within the dialysis unit contribute substantially to the observed BP elevation beyond volume accumulation alone.32

Our findings extend the literature by demonstrating that, even in the dialysis setting, WCH is associated with a higher prevalence of arterial stiffness than normotension, despite the absence of a parallel increase in LVH. Importantly, baPWV in our study was measured on a nondialysis day in a dedicated vascular laboratory, and multivariable analyses were adjusted for mean arterial pressure at the time of measurement, minimizing confounding from acute BP-related inflation of PWV.33 This design strengthens the interpretation that the observed association reflects increased cumulative vascular load or vascular aging rather than a transient sympathetic surge at measurement. Taken together, these observations indicate that WCH in HD likely represents a composite hemodynamic state integrating volume sensitivity, autonomic activation, and vascular stiffness.

This dissociation suggests that white-coat BP elevations in HD may primarily signal heightened vascular vulnerability rather than established cardiac remodeling. Clinically, these results challenge the traditional perception of white-coat BP elevations as harmless in dialysis care. Although aggressive antihypertensive intensification may be unwarranted in the absence of sustained out-of-unit hypertension, the associated increase in arterial stiffness indicates that WCH patients warrant closer longitudinal surveillance. In the context of the dialysis milieu, characterized by volume fluctuations, vascular calcification, uremic toxins, and accelerated arterial aging, WCH may represent an intermediate vascular risk phenotype with potential implications for future progression to SHT or cardiovascular risk.

WCH and Cardiac Remodeling

Our finding that the incidence of LVH was similar between WCH and SNT in the overall cohort is consistent with community-based cohorts showing comparable LV mass in white-coat hypertension and normotension.34,35 Notably, only approximately 60% of patients classified as SNT exhibited LVH, suggesting that LVH is not inevitable in HD and that refined BP phenotyping may help identify patients with lower structural cardiac burden. The absence of excess LVH in WCH may reflect that the transient BP elevations of this phenotype may be insufficient to generate the sustained pressure overload required for ventricular remodeling. In the patients on HD, this interpretation is biologically plausible, because LVH is strongly influenced by chronic volume overload, anemia, and vascular calcification; and is more closely related to out-of-unit BP exposure than to transient peridialytic elevations.1 Stratified analyses by treatment status provide additional insights: LVH prevalence was comparable between WCH and normotension among treated patients, whereas untreated patients with WCH showed a higher LVH prevalence than untreated normotensive patients, suggesting early or cumulative hypertensive exposure rather than a purely situational response. Notably, no previous dialysis study has directly compared LVH between WCH and SNT using formal home BP-based phenotyping. Our study therefore indicates that the cardiac implications of the WCH in HD depend on treatment status and cumulative BP burden, underscoring that BP-organ remodeling relationships in dialysis populations may differ fundamentally from those in the general population.

Treatment Status, WCH, and Target-Organ Remodeling

Stratified analyses by antihypertensive treatment status highlight important heterogeneity within the WCH. (Figure 3) In both treated and untreated patients, arterial stiffness increased stepwise across BP phenotypes, with WCH consistently exceeding normotension, suggesting a vascular burden independent of treatment status. In contrast, LVH showed treatment-dependent patterns: patients with untreated WCH exhibited higher LVH prevalence than untreated normotensive patients, whereas LVH prevalence was comparable between these groups among treated patients. These observations are concordant with the meta-analysis by Cohen et al.,27 which demonstrated that untreated white-coat hypertension is associated with increased cardiovascular events and mortality compared with normotension, while excess risk is attenuated in treated individuals. Together, these findings suggest that white-coat BP elevations, particularly when untreated, may reflect underlying cardiovascular vulnerability rather than a purely benign phenomenon.

Patients receiving antihypertensive therapy showed a higher prevalence of LVH and arterial stiffness across BP phenotypes than untreated patients. This should not be interpreted as a harmful effect of treatment but rather reflects confounding by indication, because antihypertensive therapy in HD is typically initiated in patients with more severe hypertension, greater interdialytic BP burden, or higher cardiovascular risk. Importantly, the relative patterns across BP phenotypes were preserved within treated and untreated groups, indicating that treatment status primarily reflects disease severity rather than a causal determinant of remodeling.

Volume management and ultrafiltration are central to BP control in HD. In our cohort, SHT was associated with greater interdialytic weight gain, supporting a role for chronic volume overload in hypertension, LVH, and vascular stiffness. “Treated” and “untreated” statuses were defined by antihypertensive medication use; some untreated patients may have achieved BP control through optimized volume management alone. Although interdialytic weight gain was adjusted for, detailed ultrafiltration metrics were unavailable, precluding further stratification. Notably, BP phenotype–related remodeling persisted after adjustment, suggesting risk beyond volume burden alone.

MHT

MHT in our HD cohort was associated with greater arterial stiffness and a higher incidence of LVH, findings that parallel those reported in nondialysis populations. In community studies, MHT consistently correlates with increased carotid-femoral PWV, and higher LVM compared with true SNT, reflecting the adverse impact of unrecognized out-of-office BP elevation on vascular and myocardial remodeling.36 Our results extend this pattern to the dialysis setting, suggesting that persistent interdialytic hypertension, rather than transient in-unit surges, may drive both arterial stiffening and ventricular hypertrophy. However, interpretation should remain cautious because of the small number of patients in the MHT group.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several methodological strengths. It applied a standardized, 7-day home BP protocol that adhered to international recommendations, capturing both dialysis and nondialysis days and minimizing random variability.4,37 The approach ensured reproducibility while maintaining feasibility for real-world clinical use. Cardiac and vascular structure were quantified using validated and blinded echocardiographic and hemodynamic techniques. Multivariable analyses were internally consistent across 3 sensitivity definitions, demonstrating the robustness of the associations. Nonetheless, certain limitations deserve mention. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and prospective follow-up is needed to confirm whether these phenotypes predict outcomes. Only patients who completed cardiovascular assessments during the early phase of an ongoing cohort were enrolled, which may limit generalizability to the broader HD population. The small number of MHT cases led to wide confidence intervals despite consistent directionality. ABPM was not performed; however, ABPM is logistically difficult in patients on HD and is poorly tolerated. Finally, our cohort was regionally derived, which may limit generalizability to other ethnicities and treatment protocols. Although measurement procedures were standardized relative to routine practice, formal quality control measures were not implemented, and some residual variability inherent to dialysis-unit BP measurement may remain.

Clinical Implications

Cardiac structure in WCH was comparable to that in normotension, suggesting that antihypertensive intensification should therefore be approached cautiously to avoid overtreatment and intradialytic hypotension. However, the consistently higher arterial stiffness observed in this group indicates greater baseline vascular vulnerability and a potential risk of progression to SHT, warranting continued surveillance rather than therapeutic inertia. Finally, structured home BP monitoring proved feasible in patients on HD and yielded phenotype distributions comparable to that in ABPM, with advantages including lower cost, greater patient acceptance, and suitability for longitudinal assessment.

Conclusion

In patients on maintenance HD, home-based BP phenotyping revealed substantial misclassification when relying solely on dialysis-unit measurements. SHT and MHT were associated with the greatest combined cardiac and vascular burden, consistent with sustained interdialytic BP exposure. In contrast, the WCH BP demonstrated a dissociation between cardiac and vascular correlates, with no clear excess in LVH but a higher prevalence of arterial stiffness, indicating that this phenotype may not be entirely benign. These findings support the incorporation of structured home BP monitoring into routine dialysis care to enhance diagnostic precision, refine risk stratification, and guide individualized BP management.
