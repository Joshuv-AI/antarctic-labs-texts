The role of the ischiofemoral ligament in hip dislocation resistance: a cadaveric study
Authors: Yoshiaki Ito, Daisuke Suzuki, Fumiya Kizawa, Arata Kanaizumi, Toshihito Hiraiwa, Yasuhito Yahara, Yoshiharu Kawaguchi, Satoshi Nagoya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ischiofemoral ligament (ISFL) contributes to posterior hip stability. However, its independent role and mechanical limitations remain unclear. This study aimed to quantify the stabilizing effect of the ISFL on posterior dislocation under progressive hip flexion.MethodsNine fresh-frozen cadaveric hips were tested using a custom jig under three conditions: (1) capsular incision distal to the ISFL with the ISFL preserved, (2) capsular repair with the ISFL preserved, and (3) capsular incision with the ISFL transected. Condition (1) represented a posteroinferior capsular incision performed distal to the ISFL, thereby preserving the ligament. Dislocation occurrence and internal rotation angle under 2 N·m torque were measured to evaluate passive capsuloligamentous restraint at flexion angles from 0°─105° in 15° increments.ResultsIn condition (1), dislocation occurred in four hips at 60°, eight at 75°, and all nine at 90°. Under condition (2), no dislocations occurred at any flexion angle. Under condition (3), dislocation began at 30° and became universal at 60°. Following capsular repair under condition (2), the internal rotation angle under constant torque increased by 10.8% relative to the pre-capsular incision state, indicating reduced resistance to internal rotation. At flexion angles ≥ 60°, the increase was 15.2%, which was statistically significant.ConclusionThe ISFL provides measurable resistance to posterior hip dislocation in shallow flexion; however, its stabilizing capacity diminishes in mid-to-deep flexion. Repair of the inferior capsule located distal to the ISFL sufficiently re-establishes mechanical restraint to prevent dislocation. These findings clarify the biomechanical limitations of the ISFL and emphasize the importance of inferior structures in maintaining posterior hip stability, with implications for capsular management strategies in posterior approach THA.Keywords: Ischiofemoral ligament, Hip dislocation, Capsule, Capsular repair, Capsular biomechanics, Posterior stability

Background

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is widely recognized as a well-established surgical procedure that offers substantial pain relief and functional improvement in patients with hip osteoarthritis [1, 2]. However, despite these advantages, postoperative dislocation remains a major unresolved complication, with reported incidence rates ranging from 0.4─4.5% [3–5] and is one of the leading indications for revision surgery following THA [6, 7]. As dislocation greatly affects patients’ quality of life, the need for strategies to reduce its incidence is strongly emphasized.

A posterior surgical approach has been widely adopted for THA. However, this approach has been reported to be associated with a higher dislocation rate than the anterior approach [3, 8–10]. Among the multiple etiological factors associated with dislocation after THA, impaired soft tissue involving joint stabilization is considered critical. Disruption of the posterior soft tissue structures of the hip contributes to postoperative instability [11], potentially accounting for the increased risk of dislocation observed with the posterior approach. However, evidence suggests that the meticulous reconstruction of disrupted posterior structures can effectively reduce the incidence of dislocation [3, 12–14]. Furthermore, modified posterior approaches aimed at enhancing dislocation resistance have been developed, which are less invasive to the short external rotator muscles and joint capsule [15–19]. In addition, a meta-analysis focusing on soft tissue repair in the posterior approach reported that repair of short external rotators alone offers limited effectiveness in preventing dislocation, whereas repair of the posterior joint capsule provides a greater preventive effect [13, 14]. These findings suggest that the joint capsule plays a beneficial role in preventing dislocations.

The hip joint capsule contains capsular ligaments that contribute substantially to joint mobility and stability. These ligaments comprise the iliofemoral, pubofemoral, and ischiofemoral ligament (ISFL) [20–25]. Through their resistance to rotational hip movements, the hip capsular ligaments function to stabilize the joint [25–29] and are therefore considered critical determinants for dislocation prevention. Appropriate management of soft tissue structures is critical in THA, with increasing attention being directed toward capsular management techniques and the functional significance of capsular ligaments in maintaining postoperative joint stability [30–33].

Among the hip capsular ligaments, the ISFL is not an isolated structure but represents a thickened portion of the posterior joint capsule. It extends from the posteroinferior acetabular rim to the inner surface of the greater trochanter [20–25]. This capsular thickening covers the posterior aspect of the hip and functions as an important restraint to internal rotation [25–29]. Cadaveric research has shown that the ISFL helps prevent femoral head dislocation during hip flexion, adduction, and internal rotation [27]. These biomechanical insights suggest that ISFL preservation may confer substantial benefits for postoperative joint function and reduce posterior dislocation risk following THA. In fact, modified posterior surgical approaches that incorporate ISFL preservation by avoiding detachment of the conjoined tendon have been developed and reported. However, biomechanical studies have demonstrated that strain on the ISFL peaks at approximately 30° of hip flexion and decreases with increasing flexion [34]. This biomechanical profile raises important questions about whether ISFL preservation provides sufficient protection against posterior dislocation during deep hip flexion, which is a known risk position for dislocation.

This study aimed to clarify the extent to which the ISFL contributes to posterior hip stability and to determine its mechanical limitations across different flexion angles using a cadaveric model. To address this question, a cadaveric study was conducted using fresh-frozen human hips to evaluate (1) the comparative effect of ISFL preservation versus transection on the occurrence of femoral head dislocation when the capsule is opened at varying degrees of hip flexion, (2) the influence of capsular repair on dislocation risk even when the ISFL is preserved, and (3) the quantitative changes in internal rotation restraint achieved through capsular repair. These findings have substantial implications for optimizing surgical techniques and providing evidence-based capsular management strategies for THA.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation

Nine fresh-frozen cadavers (three men and six women) frozen within 24 h post-mortem were used. One hip joint per cadaver was selected, yielding nine intact hips (four right, five left). The mean age at death was 83.5 years (range, 70–96 years). The average height was 156.7 ± 7.0 cm, the average BMI was 21.6 ± 3.2, and the mean femoral head diameter was 45.0 ± 2.4 mm. This study was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of Sapporo Medical University (No.2-1-63).

The specimens were transected at the third lumbar vertebra and the proximal one-third of the femur. The pelvis and proximal femur were thawed at room temperature and immersed in 0.9% saline solution. All soft tissues, except for the capsular structures and short external rotator muscles, were removed.

Anatomical observations revealed that the ISFL represents a thickened portion of the posterior capsule rather than a separate structure. It extends from the posteroinferior acetabular rim to the inner surface of the greater trochanter, forming a distinct thickening along the superior and inferior borders of the conjoined tendon (comprising the obturator internus and the superior and inferior gemelli). The trajectory of the conjoined tendon closely coincided with that of the ISFL, enabling its identification as capsular thickening.

Accordingly, when making a capsular incision with the ISFL preserved, the incision line was defined along the inferior border of the inferior gemellus muscle, corresponding to the inferior margin of the ISFL. This incision location corresponded to the capsular incision used in modified posterior approaches that preserve the conjoined tendon [17–19]. In contrast, when the ISFL was transected, an incision line was made along the inferior border of the piriformis muscle (Fig. 1). This location reflects the capsular incision commonly used in conventional posterior approaches in which the piriformis muscle is preserved. After marking the distal ends of the conjoined tendon and piriformis muscle on the capsule, all the short external rotators were excised, leaving the intact capsule on the pelvis and femur. All short external rotator muscles were removed before testing to eliminate their contribution to internal rotation resistance and allow isolated assessment of capsular function. All the hip joints exhibited a normal range of motion. All femoral heads appeared grossly spherical, with no obvious cartilage defects or macroscopic degenerative changes. The acetabular labrum was left intact throughout the experiment and was not involved in capsular incisions or repairs.

Measurement setup

A custom-made jig was employed to assess the contribution of the ISFL to hip dislocation resistance across various flexion angles (Fig. 2). This jig allowed lateral fixation of the pelvis such that the anterior pelvic plane was parallel to the femoral shaft, defining 0° of hip flexion. The jig also enabled the positioning of the hip joint at flexion angles in 15° increments, ranging from 15° of extension to 120° of flexion.

Custom-made jig. a The pelvis was fixed horizontally onto the base of a custom-made jig, with the line connecting both anterior superior iliac spines (ASISs) positioned vertically. b The hip joint center was aligned with the rotational center of the jig to approximate physiological hip rotation. Although the true rotational axis of the hip may vary slightly depending on anatomy and flexion angle, alignment was standardized across specimens to ensure consistent comparative measurements. The reference position, defined by the femoral axis being parallel to the anterior pelvic plane (APP), was set as 0° of flexion. Flexion was applied in 15° increments from this position. c A digital inclinometer was attached to measure rotation angles

A rigid rod was inserted and firmly anchored into the distal femoral canal, transmitting torque via a torque meter (RZ-T-100, AIKOH Engineering, Osaka, Japan; resolution 0.001 N·m, accuracy ± 1%). Rotating the rod induced internal or external rotation of the hip around the rotational axis passing through the centers of the femoral head and the bearing of the custom-made jig. Although the physiological rotational axis of the hip may vary slightly depending on joint morphology and flexion angle, alignment was standardized across specimens by matching the hip joint center with the jig’s rotational center to allow reproducible comparative measurements. The jig used in this study has been described in detail in earlier publications [35]. A digital inclinometer was attached to a metal wire inserted anteroposteriorly through the femur to measure the rotation angles. All measurements were performed with the femoral axis maintained in the horizontal plane, and the specimens were kept moist with saline throughout testing. The laboratory temperature was maintained at 20 °C.

Measurement protocol

The hips were tested under three sequential conditions designed to evaluate the biomechanical role of the ISFL as a thickened region within the posterior capsule:

Condition 1: Capsular incision distal to the ISFL with the ISFL preserved.An L-shaped posteroinferior capsular incision was made distal to the ISFL, preserving it (Fig. 1c–d, red dashed line; Fig. 3a). This condition represents the ISFL in isolation, with the inferior aspect of the hip joint exposed.

Condition 1: Capsular incision distal to the ISFL with the ISFL preserved.

An L-shaped posteroinferior capsular incision was made distal to the ISFL, preserving it (Fig. 1c–d, red dashed line; Fig. 3a). This condition represents the ISFL in isolation, with the inferior aspect of the hip joint exposed.

Condition 2: Capsular repair with the ISFL preserved.The distal incision made under condition (1) was subsequently repaired side-to-side without overlap using No. 2 Ethibond sutures at four points spaced 5 mm apart (Fig. 3b–c), restoring the continuity of the capsule inferior to the ISFL.

Condition 2: Capsular repair with the ISFL preserved.

The distal incision made under condition (1) was subsequently repaired side-to-side without overlap using No. 2 Ethibond sutures at four points spaced 5 mm apart (Fig. 3b–c), restoring the continuity of the capsule inferior to the ISFL.

Condition 3: Capsular incision with the ISFL transected.The incision was extended proximally along the distal edge of the piriformis muscle, including and transecting the ISFL itself, thereby fully opening the posterior capsule (Fig. 1c–d, blue solid line; Fig. 3d).

Condition 3: Capsular incision with the ISFL transected.

The incision was extended proximally along the distal edge of the piriformis muscle, including and transecting the ISFL itself, thereby fully opening the posterior capsule (Fig. 1c–d, blue solid line; Fig. 3d).

The capsular repair technique was designed to provide a standardized and reproducible side-to-side closure for biomechanical comparison rather than to replicate a specific clinical repair strategy. The number and spacing of sutures were selected to achieve uniform capsular apposition without excessive tension, thereby minimizing inter-specimen variability and isolating the mechanical contribution of the inferior capsular continuity.

A 2 N·m internal rotation torque was applied in each condition, and the occurrence of dislocation was examined. Dislocation was defined as the displacement of the femoral head from the acetabular cavity during the application of internal rotation torque. Specifically, dislocation was considered to have occurred when the femoral head escaped from the acetabulum either before the applied torque reached 2 N·m, owing to loss of capsular resistance, or when the femoral head was displaced from the acetabulum after reaching 2 N·m, resulting in an inability to maintain the applied torque.

Subsequently, under the pre-capsular incision and post-capsular repair conditions, the internal rotation angle reached when applying the same 2 N·m torque in 15° increments from 0° to 105° flexion was measured. Each measurement was performed twice, and the average value was used. The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) for two repeated measurements of the internal rotation angle was 0.99, indicating excellent intraobserver reliability.

Statistical analysis

To compare the internal rotation angles between the pre-capsular incision and post-capsular repair conditions across various hip flexion angles (0°–105°), paired t-tests were performed for each flexion angle. Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons, with the significance threshold set at P < 0.00625 (0.05/8). For each flexion angle, the gain ratio was calculated as the internal rotation angle after capsular repair relative to that before capsular incision (post/pre) and expressed as a fold change. The mean gain ratio and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were computed assuming an approximate normality of the gain ratio distribution. All analyses were performed using JMP Pro V.18 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

As this was a within-specimen repeated-measures cadaveric study, each hip served as its own control, thereby reducing inter-specimen variability. Due to the availability constraints of cadaveric specimens, a formal a priori power calculation was not performed. Instead, exact statistical tests and effect sizes with the corresponding CIs were used to ensure a robust interpretation of the results.

Results

Under condition (1) (inferior aspect of the hip joint exposed with ISFL preservation), no dislocations occurred between 0° and 45° of hip flexion. Dislocation was observed in four hips at 60° of flexion. At 75°, four additional hips were dislocated, resulting in eight hips altogether. By 90°, all nine hips were dislocated, and this pattern persisted at 105°, with all hips remaining dislocated.

Under condition (2), in which the capsule was repaired while preserving the ISFL, no dislocations were observed at any flexion angle between 0° and 105°.

Under condition (3) (posterior capsule opened with ISFL transection), one hip was dislocated as early as 30° of flexion. At 45°, six additional dislocations occurred, followed by two more at 60°, resulting in the dislocation of all nine hips at 60° of flexion. This complete dislocation pattern persisted at 75° and 90°. At 105° of hip flexion in condition 3, one hip that had dislocated at a lower flexion angle did not exhibit dislocation. This phenomenon occurred because, during internal rotation in deep hip flexion, the femoral head translated anteroinferiorly, opposite to the capsular incision site. Consequently, the femoral head was incidentally caught by the residual tension at the base of the capsular flap, preventing dislocation at this flexion angle. However, this phenomenon was observed in only one hip; at 105° flexion, the remaining eight hips were all dislocated.

When averaged across all flexion angles, the internal rotation angle increased by 10.8% (1.108-fold). Notably, when the analysis was limited to flexion angles ≥ 60°, the increase was more pronounced, reaching 15.2% (1.152-fold), with 95% CIs consistently exceeding unity, indicating a statistically significant reduction in resistance to internal rotation.

Discussion

This study evaluated the biomechanical role of the ISFL in preventing posterior hip dislocation. The extent to which the ISFL contributes to posterior hip stability when isolated from the surrounding capsular structures, and how its stabilizing effect changes across flexion angles, was assessed. Our cadaveric experiments revealed that while the ISFL prevents posterior hip dislocation in shallow flexion, it is insufficient to do so in mid-to-deep flexion. In contrast, when the inferior capsule, which was incised distal to the ISFL, was repaired while preserving the ISFL, dislocation was completely prevented across all tested flexion angles. Conventionally, the ISFL has been regarded as a major restraint of internal rotation during hip flexion; however, our results refine this view by demonstrating that its stabilizing effect diminishes with increasing flexion. These findings indicate that the ISFL alone is insufficient to resist dislocation in deep flexion, where the posterior dislocation risk is the highest, and underscore the complementary stabilizing role of the capsule inferior to the ISFL.

The onset of dislocation differed between the conditions. When the posterior capsule, including the ISFL, was incised (condition 3), dislocation occurred as early as 30° and became universal by 60°. When the ISFL was preserved and the capsule was incised distal to it (condition 1), the dislocation was delayed until 60° but became universal by 90°. These results demonstrate that the ISFL in isolation provides only partial restraint, particularly at lower flexion angles. However, typical posterior hip dislocation occurs during activities involving hip flexion > 90°, corresponding to approximately 70° of pelvic-femoral flexion after accounting for the pelvic tilt [36]. In high-risk positions, ISFL preservation alone did not provide sufficient resistance.

The limited efficacy of ISFL in mid-to-deep flexion likely reflects its anatomical coverage. The ISFL covers the posterior but not the inferior acetabulum. As hip flexion and internal rotation increase, the femoral head tends to dislocate more inferiorly from the acetabulum beyond ISFL coverage. If the capsule below the ISFL is incised, the inferior exposure allows dislocation through the uncovered region (Fig. 6). Therefore, repair of the inferior capsule located distal to the ISFL is critical for restoring the mechanical restraint in this area.

Capsular repair may restore joint constraints and rotational control to those in the native hip [37, 38]. In this study, when the capsule was incised at the inferior edge of the ISFL and repaired side to side, a sling-like structure formed at the inferior aspect of the joint (Fig. 3c), successfully restoring the dislocation resistance. However, compared with the pre-capsular incision state, the internal rotation angle after capsular repair increased by an average of 6.5% up to 60° of flexion, but rose to 15% at flexion angles ≥ 60°. This finding indicates a marked reduction in internal rotation resistance at flexion angles ≥ 60°, suggesting that the capsule below the ISFL is particularly susceptible to loading in the mid-to-deep flexion range. Accordingly, a more robust repair of this region may be required to achieve sufficient resistance to dislocations during deep hip flexion. These findings highlight the critical role of the inferior capsule in maintaining posterior hip stability. It should be noted that the suture material and configuration used in this study were selected to ensure experimental reproducibility and should not be interpreted as a recommendation for a specific clinical repair technique.

A constant internal rotation torque of 2 N·m was applied to evaluate passive capsuloligamentous restraint under controlled conditions. Although this magnitude was lower than the torques likely generated during dynamic activities or traumatic events [39], it was sufficient to reproducibly induce dislocations and detect differences among the experimental conditions. Previous biomechanical studies evaluating passive capsuloligamentous restraint of the hip have commonly applied torques in the range of approximately 1.5–5 N·m to assess rotational stability under controlled conditions [27, 29, 34, 40]. Therefore, the use of a 2 N·m torque in the present study is consistent with established experimental protocols designed to evaluate passive soft-tissue restraint rather than physiologic peak loading. The use of a relatively low torque minimized the viscoelastic creep and progressive capsular laxity during repeated testing, allowing for a sensitive assessment of the relative stabilizing contributions of the ISFL and inferior capsule. Given that physiological dislocation-related forces are likely substantially higher, the observed loss of stability under a 2 N·m torque suggests that the ISFL alone may be insufficient to resist posterior dislocation under clinically relevant loading conditions.

From a kinematic perspective, in the present experimental setup, hip rotation was generated around a mechanically defined axis passing through the femoral head center and the bearing of the custom-made jig. Although the distal passage point of the rotational axis was mechanically constrained, this configuration was intentionally adopted to ensure reproducible rotational motion across specimens and experimental conditions. In vivo, the instantaneous axis of hip rotation may vary depending on joint alignment, anatomy, and flexion angle. Therefore, the present model represents a simplified rotational condition designed to allow controlled comparison of capsular restraint rather than replication of physiologic joint kinematics. The potential influence of this constraint on joint mechanics should be considered when interpreting the results.

Posterior hip dislocation typically occurs under combined flexion, adduction, and internal rotation. In the present experimental model, internal rotation was applied under controlled flexion without adduction to isolate the passive stabilizing contribution of the capsuloligamentous structures, particularly the ISFL. The absence of adduction may have underestimated the overall instability risk, as adduction is known to increase posterior dislocation tendency by directing the femoral head toward the posterior acetabular rim. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting the isolated rotational restraint of the posterior capsule rather than fully reproducing clinical dislocation mechanics. Future studies incorporating multi-planar loading conditions, including combined adduction and internal rotation, are warranted to further clarify the role of capsular structures under more physiologic conditions.

From a clinical translation perspective, a direct extrapolation of the present findings to THA should be performed with caution. One limitation of this study was the use of native femoral heads rather than prosthetic components. Native femoral heads are substantially larger than the standard prosthetic heads used in THA, resulting in a greater jump distance and inherently higher resistance to dislocation. Furthermore, reduced head and neck size in THA has been reported to decrease ligament tension and diminish capsular function [30, 31]. In addition, preservation of the acetabular labrum likely contributes to dislocation resistance in this native hip model, further indicating that THA stability may have been overestimated by the present results. Despite these biomechanical advantages, posterior dislocation occurred at mid-to-deep flexion angles in our native hip model even when the ISFL was preserved.

Therefore, the present findings likely underestimate the degree of instability that would occur in a THA setting, where a reduced head size and altered head–neck geometry further decrease the jump distance and capsular tension. In prosthetic hips, the stabilizing contribution of the ISFL is expected to diminish even at shallower flexion angles compared to native hips. Consequently, the importance of robust capsular repair, particularly of the capsule inferior to the ISFL, may be greater in THA than in the present study.

Accordingly, although caution is warranted when directly applying these findings to THA, the observed limitations of the ISFL-mediated stability in native hips provide a conservative biomechanical framework for understanding posterior instability mechanisms in prosthetic hips.

Maximum hip flexion and extension angles after capsular repair were not directly assessed in this study. However, within the tested range of motion (0°–105° of hip flexion), no apparent increase in joint stiffness was observed, and the internal rotation angles increased after repair, indicating reduced rotational restraint rather than overtightening. The potential effects of capsular repair on obligate pelvic motion have not yet been evaluated and warrant further investigation.

Additionally, the donor population comprised older Japanese individuals with normal joint morphology, limiting generalizability to patients with THA. Capsular properties vary with age and location [41, 42], potentially affecting repair outcomes. In addition, although fresh-frozen cadaveric specimens were used, post-mortem tissue relaxation and changes in viscoelastic properties could not be completely excluded. Such factors may influence the torque–rotation response and represent an inherent limitation of cadaveric biomechanical studies.

Conclusions

The ISFL contributes to posterior hip stability primarily in shallow flexion; however, its mechanical restraint decreases in mid-to-deep flexion. Capsular repair, particularly of the portion inferior to the ISFL, sufficiently re-established mechanical restraint to prevent dislocation in our model. This study elucidated the biomechanical limits of the ISFL and highlighted the critical role of the inferior capsule in maintaining the posterior stability of the hip. These results enhance the understanding of capsular function and provide a biomechanical basis for capsular management strategies, particularly in posterior approach total hip arthroplasty.
