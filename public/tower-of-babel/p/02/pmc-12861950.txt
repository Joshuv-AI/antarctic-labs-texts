Rotational arc treatments with static ports and dynamic collimation reduces cardiopulmonary dose for esophageal cancer patients and diminishes model-based predicted benefit of proton therapy
Authors: Pieter Populaire, Gilles Defraene, Karel Aerts, Truus Reynders, Wout Piot, Bianca Vanstraelen, Karin Haustermans, Wouter Crijns
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Radiotherapy is a cornerstone in the multimodal treatment of esophageal cancer (EC), particularly in the context of trimodality treatment, which combines neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) followed by surgery. This approach has been shown to significantly improve overall survival compared to surgery alone [1]. While perioperative chemotherapy recently became the standard-of-care for esophageal adenocarcinoma, nCRT still provides an alternative in this population and remains the standard for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [2,3]. However, nCRT risks increases the risk of treatment-related toxicities [[4], [5], [6]]. Among these, postoperative pulmonary complications are particularly critical, as these can constitute to prolonged hospital stay, intensive care stay, long-term morbidity and mortality. The risk of pulmonary complications has previously been linked to dose to the lungs. Thomas et al. developed a normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) model that predicts the likelihood of postoperative pulmonary complications based on mean lung dose (MLD), age, body mass index and tumor histology [5]. Similarly, Berbée et al. introduced a model predicting 2-year mortality using mean heart dose (MHD) as a key parameter alongside gross tumor volume [7]. These predictive tools have emphasized the need for continued reduction of dose to organs-at-risk (OARs), such as the lungs and heart.

Technological advancements in radiotherapy can aid in reducing dose to OAR, resulting in reduced MLD and MHD. Compared to older techniques like 3D-conformal radiotherapy, intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) has demonstrated reductions in MLD and MHD [8]. More recently, proton therapy (PT) has emerged as a promising alternative, showing even greater reductions in OAR dose [9]. This technique has already demonstrated a reduction of total toxicity burden in a phase IIb randomised trial [10]. Ongoing phase III trials aim to confirm these findings [11,12]. However, PT is costly and has limited availability, necessitating selective use based on thresholds of predicted clinical benefit [13,14]. Aiming for refinement of photon-based radiotherapy (XT) retains the advantageous cost and availability of XT while lowering dose to OAR, subsequently reducing toxicity.

Current state-of-the-art XT for EC is either planned using rotational or volumetric modulated arc radiotherapy or IMRT [9,15]. Yet, each comes with limitations − for example, arcs can produce a widespread low-dose bath, potentially violating constraints such as the lung volume receiving at least 5 Gy (LungV5Gy) while IMRT typically has less conformity. Recently, a novel technique combining the advantages of rotational arcs with static beam angles (pausing gantry motion at predefined positions), while also allowing for dynamic collimator rotation (RAD) has been introduced [16]. These features hold potential to optimize the dose distribution, minimizing exposure to OAR.

Given its novelty, only few reports have identified clinically relevant indications for deployment of this technique. In this study, we evaluated the application of said technique in the setting of nCRT for EC. Specifically, we assessed whether it reduces OAR-dose and subsequent predicted toxicity risks compared to standard IMRT. Furthermore, we also assessed whether it lowers the number of patients meeting referral thresholds for PT.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient cohort

We conducted a comparative treatment planning analysis of IMRT, RAD, and PT. The primary objective was to reduce MLD and thereby lower the predicted probability of postoperative pulmonary complications, as estimated by the NTCP-model of Thomas et al. [5]. We selected patients enrolled in the phase III PROTECT trial (NCT050555648 [12]) at our institution between 2022 and February 2025 (ethical committee approval S65789). This approach ensured both clinical relevance and plan quality as depicted by pre- and on-trial quality assurance (QA) protocols [15].

From the pool of eligible patients, we selected ten patients with high MLD (>10 Gy) in the clinical (IMRT-based) XT-plan for this analysis. These patients represented the subgroup with highest relevance to reduce MLD and subsequent toxicity risk. Patient characteristics are displayed in Table 1.

To assess clinical relevance in patients with lower MLD, we also included reference cases representing the 10th and 25th percentiles (P10 and P25 respectively). No additional patient characteristics of these individual patients are provided to avoid personal identification.

Imaging procedures, segmentation and planning objectives

All patients were treated according to the PROTECT clinical trial protocol, including its radiotherapy-QA framework [12,15]. Each patient underwent a 4D-CT simulation in free breathing, capturing ten respiratory phases at a 2 mm slice thickness. Scans were acquired using Siemens SOMATOM Definition Edge or SOMATOM Drive scanners, in combination with Varian Respiratory Gating for Scanners systems. Patients were immobilized in supine position with arms above the head.

Target volume delineation was performed according to the guidelines by Thomas et al. [17]. The total clinical target volume was delineated on all individual respiratory phases and summed to an internal target volume that was projected on the average respiratory CT image. A 7 mm isotropic margin was added to create the planning target volume for XT-planning. PT-planning incorporated robust optimization using 7 mm setup uncertainty and 3 % range uncertainty. All plans were prescribed a total dose of 50.4 Gy delivered in 28 fractions. The planning focused on achieving low MLD, in accordance with the objectives of the PROTECT trial [12,15]. OAR delineation was performed following existing guidelines as referred to in the trial radiotherapy-QA protocol [15]. For spinal cord, a five millimeter PRV margin was used. Planning was performed on the average respiratory CT image using the objectives defined in the trial radiotherapy-QA protocol [15]. All XT and PT-plan evaluations included nominal dose distributions and robust assessments accounting for 7 mm shifts, as well as 4D-robust analysis across all respiratory phases (Supplementary Table S1). While accounting for setup uncertainty, these robust evaluations also ensured target coverage during shifts of the in diaphragm position, a frequent cause for replanning both XT and PT-plans [18,19].

IMRT and experimental RAD-plans were generated in Eclipse (RapidArc Dynamic, Varian Medical Systems – a Siemens Healthineers company, Palo Alto, United States) using the ACUROS photon optimizer (v16.1 for IMRT; v18.1 for RAD) with a 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm3 dose grid. IMRT plans were created for Halcyon (6MV FFF) or TrueBeam (10MV), depending on patient scheduling, using RapidPlan class-solution for knowledge-based planning with fixed beam angles (Halcyon: 220°, 300°, 330°, 0°, 30°, 60°, 155°; TrueBeam: 220°, 300°, 0°, 60°, 155°). RAD-plans were developed for TrueBeam using 6MV FFF, applying the same planning goals as IMRT. The RAD-plans consisted of one full 360° arc plus five static ports (205°, 330°, 0°, 30°, 155°) with balanced weighting between arc and static fields (26 control points per port). For the arc, collimator angles were optimized by the treatment planning system. At the static ports, gantry rotation was paused while beam delivery was modulated by multileaf collimator motion. When creating new RAD-plans versus existing IMRT-plans, MHD-increases were avoided to minimize bias and enhance comparability. PT-plans were generated in RayStation (RaySearch Laboratories AB, Stockholm, Sweden) versions 11A and 12A, using the IonMonteCarlo dose calculation algorithm (versions 5.2 and 5.4), dose calculation grid size 3x3x3 mm3. Plans were designed for ProteusONE (Ion Beam Applications, Louvain-La-Neuve, Belgium), with pencil beam delivery (intensity modulated PT). A three-beams setup was used with beams at 180° posterior and 140° left and right posterior oblique.

Plan comparison, toxicity prediction and potential referral for PT

Comparison between IMRT, RAD and PT was performed on the nominal plan, in alignment with the heart and lung constraints (Supplementary Table S1). For the ten high-MLD patients, statistical differences were assessed using either paired t-tests (after confirming normality using Shapiro-Wilk test and data visualization) or Wilcoxon signed-rank exact tests (normal distribution not assumed) (α = 0.05), with analyses performed via pvalue.io (Medistica) [20].

We assessed clinical relevance in line with the PROTECT trial hypothesis [12], by employing the NTCP-model to predict postoperative pulmonary complications [5]. Additionally, we explored the impact using the 2-year mortality prediction model as a secondary clinical evaluation [7], although it was not the primary focus of plan optimization. We adhered to the thresholds recommended by The Netherlands Model-Based Approach for selecting patients for PT: a 10 % NTCP-reduction for postoperative pulmonary complications and a 5 % NTCP-reduction for 2-year mortality [13,21,22].

Results

Plan comparison

In the high-MLD population, the median MLD decreased from 12.0 Gy (range 10.2–16.3 Gy) with IMRT to 10.2 Gy (range 8.3–12.5 Gy) with RAD (p = 0.002, Table 2). At the individual patient level, RAD consistently reduced MLD compared to IMRT for all patients (Fig. 1a). Similarly, LungV5Gy and LungV20Gy were also statistically significantly reduced when comparing IMRT versus RAD. Five IMRT-plans exceeded the primary LungV5Gy constraint (<70 %), whereas all RAD-plans remained within this limit. Importantly, the MLD-reduction with RAD did not come at the expense of higher heart doses (MHD, HeartV25Gy, and HeartV40Gy). Instead, they were all statistically significantly lower with RAD versus IMRT (Table 2, Fig. 1b).

Although RAD improved MLD compared to IMRT, PT further reduced this to a median of 5.1 Gy (range 2.8–7.1 Gy), representing a reduction of 6.9 Gy (p = 0.002) and 5.1 Gy (p = 0.006) versus IMRT and RAD respectively (Table 2, Fig. 1a). Similarly, PT achieved lower heart doses with a median MHD of 9.8 Gy (range 6.4–13.6 Gy), a reduction of 15.9 Gy compared to IMRT (p < 0.001) and 14.3 Gy (p = 0.002) compared to RAD (Table 2, Fig. 1b).

For the P25-patient, MLD decreased from 7.3 Gy using IMRT, to 5.2 Gy using RAD, to 1.6 Gy using PT. Meanwhile, for MHD this was respectively 25.8 Gy versus 23.8 Gy versus 8.8 Gy (Supplementary Fig. S1). For the P10-patient, MLD was respectively 6.5 Gy versus 5.8 Gy versus 2.4 Gy while MHD was respectively 20.8 Gy versus 19.4 Gy versus 9.7 Gy (Supplementary Fig. S1).

In all nominal plans, primary dose constraints were met using the three techniques, except the previously mentioned violations of LungV5Gy < 70 % in five IMRT-plans. Apart from LungV5Gy < 60 %, which was exceeded in eight IMRT-plans and seven RAD-plans, all secondary dose constraints were met. All plans passed robust and 4D-evaluations in agreement with the radiotherapy-QA protocol. Toxicity prediction and potential referral for PT

Using the NTCP-model for pulmonary complications, the predicted risk in the clinical IMRT-plans averaged 34 % (standard deviation 17 %). The MLD-reduction using RAD decreased the predicted risk on average 7 % (p = 0.002) (Table 2, Fig. 3). The largest ΔNTCP was 19 % while the smallest was 2 %. Six patients achieved a NTCP-reduction of at least 5 % and three patients achieved a reduction of at least 10 %. PT led to a further NTCP-reduction, with an additional average decrease of 11 % compared to RAD (p < 0.001) (Table 2, Fig. 3). The largest and smallest reductions were respectively 19 % and 5 %. Compared to IMRT, the ΔNTCP with PT was >10 % in nine out of ten patients, suggesting almost all patients would be eligible for referral to PT assuming the recommended threshold. Comparing PT with RAD, only five patients surpassed the 10 % ΔNTCP-reduction threshold, suggesting fewer would be eligible for PT-referral (Fig. 4a). For the P25-patient predicted risk for postoperative pulmonary complications dropped from 25 % to 20 % to 13 % for respectively IMRT, RAD and PT (Supplementary Fig. S1). For the P10-patient, this was respectively 30 %, 28 % and 19 % (Supplementary Fig. S1). Thus, using RAD neither of these patients would have been referred to PT whereas both would have been referred based on the IMRT-plan. In all patients who no longer passed the referral threshold after switching from IMRT to RAD, the ΔNTCP between IMRT and PT was below 15 %.

The predicted 2-year mortality risk in the clinical IMRT-plans averaged 47 % (range: 37–53 %), which decreased on average 2 % with RAD (p = 0.002) (Table 2, Fig. 3). PT offered a more substantial reduction, with an average ΔNTCP of 17 % compared to RAD (p < 0.001) (Table 2, Fig. 3). All patients exceeded the 5 % ΔNTCP-threshold for referral to PT with either XT-technique (Fig. 4b). For the P25-patient the 2-year mortality risk dropped from 40 % to 38 % to 20 % for respectively IMRT, RAD and PT (Supplementary Fig. S1). For the P10-patient this was respectively 27 %, 25 % and 16 % (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Discussion

Reducing OAR-dose could achieve toxicity reduction as hypothesized by both retrospective and prospective research. The currently ongoing phase III PROTECT trial aims to reduce the risk of postoperative pulmonary complications by reducing the MLD, based on an existing NTCP-model for pulmonary complications. Utilizing the same model, we showed that the XT-based RAD-technique significantly reduced the predicted toxicity risk versus standard of care IMRT. Furthermore, we showed the potential impact of RAD on referral patterns for PT. Herein, we used the recommended thresholds used in the model-based approach. Similarly, we evaluated the impact on the predicted 2-year mortality risk.

The statistically significant MLD-reduction we demonstrated, appeared to be partially attributable to an increased posterior dose attribution, which was avoided in IMRT-plans. The MLD-reduction was translated to a clinically meaningful 7 % reduction in predicted risk for postoperative pulmonary complications using the model of Thomas et al. [5]. Based on these findings, RAD-planning has been introduced into our clinical practice for EC-patients. PT allowed to achieve a further MLD decrease translating to an average toxicity reduction of 18 % versus the IMRT-plan and a reduction of 11 % versus the RAD-plan. The latter is still above the 10 % calculated benefit of PT versus XT in the PROTECT trial [12]. While in all ten patients PT performed better than either XT-technique, on the individual patient level, we saw that four patients that surpassed the set 10 % threshold for model-based referral to PT based on the IMRT-plan, no longer met this requirement when PT was compared with RAD. Even though RAD-planning was optimised towards reducing MLD [9,15], we found that heart doses also decreased statistically significantly using RAD versus IMRT. This translated into a reduction in 2-year mortality risk of approximately 2 % using the model of Berbée et al. While a difference of 5 % predicted 2-year mortality risk is currently used in The Netherlands to refer patients to PT, the initial recommendations defined a clinically relevant risk reduction at this 2 % level. This was later changed to 5 % as otherwise the model lacked discriminative ability and would select nearly all patients for PT [21,22]. In our cohort, PT achieved predicted 2-year mortality reductions of on average 19 % versus the IMRT-plan and 17 % versus the RAD-plan, suggesting PT-referral in all patients with either XT-technique.

This study offers several notable strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to investigate the use of RAD in the treatment of EC, presenting novel and clinically relevant dosimetric findings. By focusing on a high-risk population with MLD > 10 Gy, we targeted patients with a clearly defined clinical need for toxicity reduction. Furthermore our analysis addressed the question whether comparative studies between XT and PT remain relevant when using new and improved XT-based techniques. Nonetheless, this study has limitations. First, the retrospective design may have introduced bias, particularly in comparing clinically implemented IMRT and PT-plans to generated RAD-plans. Although the use of trial data with rigorous QA procedures of these patients included in a trial helped mitigate this, the potential for bias remains [12,15]. Furthermore, the experienced clinical planning team that developed the clinical IMRT-plans, also developed the RAD-plans. Second, our patient cohort contained a relatively small number of patients that was deliberately selected for high MLD, which may limit generalizability and could overestimate the benefit of both PT and RAD in a broader EC population. However, this selection aligned with our goal to identify the patients with the most pressing clinical need. We also evaluated patients at the MLD P25 and P10 as an illustration which confirmed the findings in our high-MLD population. Third, our definitions of clinical benefit relied on predictive models that, while validated, have varying degrees of accuracy [5,7,23,24]. Still, models like these are currently used in clinical routine, for example in the model-based approach [13]. Note that while our selection was done based on MLD, the toxicity prediction model of Thomas et al. also include age, BMI and histology that can influence the relation between MLD and predicted toxicity risk. For example, squamous cell carcinoma has a steeper dose–response curve, which is the main population of interest given the ESOPEC-trial advocates the use of perioperative chemotherapy instead of nCRT for adenocarcinoma [3]. Fourth and finally, our planning objectives reflected that of the PROTECT-trial [15]. We thus emphasized reducing MLD to reduce the predicted risk for postoperative pulmonary complications. As a result, the lacking discriminatory performance of the model of Berbée et al. that refers all patients to PT, with either XT-based technique, should be interpreted carefully: when optimizing XT-plans to lower MLD, inevitably this results in higher MHD and vice versa, while no such trade-off has to be made using PT. We therefore must limit our conclusion to highlight that RAD results in lower MHD than IMRT and therefore lower predicted 2-year mortality risks, even when this is not the primary (planning) objective.

Our findings underline the benefits of PT for EC by reducing dose to both heart and lungs and lowering subsequent predicted toxicities. Nonetheless, in the absence of phase III trials, the role of PT in treatment of EC remains disputed, with results of currently running trials being awaited [11,12]. In the meantime, RAD showed a clinically relevant improvement over IMRT and could become the current standard-of-care XT. Even if phase III trials demonstrate reduced toxicity of PT, the added cost and limited availability would require selecting patients that benefit most from this technique [25]. At time of writing, the Dutch model-based approach compares the XT versus PT-plan with proposed thresholds for referring patients to PT [13]. Here we showed that RAD lowered dose to OAR and therefore subsequent toxicity risks. Other approaches in XT have been proposed and shown promising in achieving the same goal, including partial arcs or non-coplanar treatment plans, though are not widely used, indicating the need for an integrated solution [26,27]. These advances in XT-based techniques potentially lower the demand and financial stress of PT by reducing the amount of patients surpassing the set threshold for referring patients to PT [25].

In conclusion, our findings showed that RAD results in lower doses to both heart and lungs versus current standard-of-care IMRT. PT retained its relevance due to lower doses on both heart and lungs when compared to either XT-technique. Reducing OAR doses could translate into reduced risk of postoperative pulmonary complications and/or 2-year mortality. By lowering this predicted risk, RAD holds potential to reduce the demand of PT, reducing the amount of patients surpassing the referral threshold.
