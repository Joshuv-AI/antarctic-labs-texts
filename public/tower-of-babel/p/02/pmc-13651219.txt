Correlation of P53 expression and TP53 mutation in stage I lung adenocarcinoma and the predictive value for postoperative recurrence within 5 years
Authors: Jiting Di, Kang Qi, Gang Lin, Dong Li, Xin Li, Yan Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study seeks to explore the correlation between P53 expression and TP53 mutations in stage I lung adenocarcinoma, while also assessing their predictive value for postoperative recurrence within 5 years.Methods and resultsNext-generation sequencing (NGS) of the TP53 gene and immunohistochemistry (IHC) for P53 protein were performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples from 191 patients diagnosed with stage I lung adenocarcinoma. Two thresholds for P53 overexpression were established: 40% for predicting TP53 mutations, determined through ROC curve analysis, and 60% for predicting recurrence, utilizing X-tile software. We defined P53 mutant expression as either overexpression (>40%) or null-expression (0%) and P53 aberrant expression as overexpression (>60%). A significant association was found between P53 mutant expression and TP53 mutations (p < 0.001), demonstrating 87.9% sensitivity and 98.4% specificity, with a perfect concordance (κ = 0.882). No significant differences in survival outcomes were observed among the distinct P53 mutant expression patterns and TP53 mutation types. In survival analysis, pTNM stage, histopathology grade, P53 aberrant expression and TP53 missense mutation were recognized as independent prognostic factors in stage I lung adenocarcinoma.ConclusionsBased on our study’s criteria, P53 mutant expression may serve as a surrogate marker for TP53 mutations, while P53 aberrant expression, along with TP53 missense mutation could potentially predict postoperative recurrence within five years for patients with stage I lung adenocarcinoma. The biomarkers of TP53 mutation, P53 mutant expression and P53 aberrant expression present opportunities for enhanced risk stratification and the formulation of personalized treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13000-026-01802-x.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, TP53 gene, P53 protein, prognosis

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed malignant tumor and the most common cause of death caused by cancer worldwide, accounting for approximately 12.4% of all cancer diagnoses and 18.7% of cancer deaths [1]. Among its subtypes, adenocarcinoma is the most prevalent, representing 50% of lung cancer diagnoses, with its incidence steadily on the rise [2]. The introduction of low-dose computed tomography screening has transformed lung cancer detection, resulting in a notable shift in the epidemiological landscape, characterized by an increasing proportion of cases identified at earlier stages [2, 3].

Stage I lung adenocarcinoma presents the most favorable prognosis, with surgical resection often offering a potential cure. Nevertheless, around 10–38% of patients experience disease recurrence despite receiving optimal treatment [4, 5], highlighting the urgent need for reliable prognostic biomarkers to enhance risk stratification and tailor personalized therapeutic strategies for early-stage patients.

The TP53 gene, often referred to as the “guardian of the genome”, is crucial for maintaining genomic stability by regulating apoptosis, cell cycle progression, and DNA repair [6]. TP53 mutations are highly prevalent in cancer, occurring in up to 51% of lung adenocarcinomas, and are significantly associated with tumor progression and poor postoperative outcomes [7–9]. Given the close relationship between TP53 mutations and P53 protein expression, IHC is commonly employed as a surrogate method for detecting TP53 mutations in pathological practice [10–12]. While the use of P53 IHC for identifying TP53 mutations has been validated in ovarian and endometrial cancers [11, 12], its application in lung adenocarcinoma remains relatively underexplored. Additionally, the predictive value of TP53 mutations and P53 expression for postoperative recurrence in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma continues to be a topic of debate.

This study aims to address these gaps by analyzing P53 expression, TP53 mutations, and survival data in 191 patients with stage I lung adenocarcinoma.

Materials and methods

Population and follow up

Between January 2015 and December 2016, a total of 599 patients with lung adenocarcinoma were registered in the institutional database at The Peking University First Hospital. Of these, 408 patients were excluded for the following reasons: (a) non-stage I disease (n = 313), (b) lack of adequate tissue for both p53 immunohistochemical and next-generation sequencing analyses (n = 60), and (c) incomplete clinical follow-up data (n = 35). The remaining 191 patients with pathologically confirmed stage I lung adenocarcinoma constituted the final study cohort (Fig. 1). These individuals underwent curative-intent surgery at The Peking University First Hospital, with staging classified according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) [13]. None of the patients received neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy, and all had complete follow-up data.

Flowchart of patient selection from the institutional lung adenocarcinoma database

The follow-up period extended until May 2021. Disease-free survival (DFS) was evaluated from the date of surgery to the date of the first recurrence or tumor-associated death. Tumor recurrence was identified clinically and corroborated by radiographic evidence or laboratory abnormalities, categorized into two types: regional and distant recurrence. Regional recurrence was characterized by the presence of a tumor in a second ipsilateral lobe, ipsilateral hilar lymph nodes, or ipsilateral mediastinal lymph nodes. In contrast, distant recurrence was identified by the presence of a tumor in the contralateral lung, contralateral mediastinal or ipsilateral supraclavicular lymph nodes, or in any location outside the hemithorax [14].

Other significant factors influencing prognosis include sex (male, female), age (in years), smoking status (yes, no), pTNM stage (IA, IB), and histopathology grade (G1, G2, G3), all of which were gathered through a thorough review of medical records. The usage of all patient samples and clinical data was approved by the Peking University First Hospital Ethics Committee (ethical approval no.: (2024) Research No. 203).

Next-generation sequencing for TP53 mutation

NGS was performed on DNA extracted from FFPE tissue specimens. Prior to sequencing, all tissue sections were evaluated by an experienced pathologist to confirm adequate tumor cell content (≥ 20%; range: 20%–80%; mean: 56%). DNA was quantified using the Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with a minimum concentration threshold of ≥ 20 ng/µL (range: 20–216 ng/µL; mean: 58.5 ng/µL). NGS was performed using the OncoAim® lung cancer multigene assay kit (Singlera Genomics, Inc., Shanghai, China), which is optimized for FFPE-derived DNA. TP53 mutation assessment was performed on the entire exome of the TP53 gene. Mutations and amplifications of EGFR, KRAS, BRAF, HER2, MET, STK11, as well as fusions of ALK, RET, and ROS1, were also reported.

IHC for P53 expression

Staining and scoring methods

Representative areas from FFPE tissues underwent IHC on 4-µm-thick sections, utilizing the Thermo Autostainer 480 S and the DAKO EnVision detection kit (DAKO, Carpinteria, California), in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The primary antibody used was P53 (clone DO-7, Origene, Maryland, USA, 1:100, Mouse monoclonal). In each staining run, known P53-overexpressing high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma specimens were included as external positive controls. For negative controls, the primary antibody was replaced with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Non-neoplastic cells within the tissue sections served as additional internal controls, demonstrating scattered nuclear staining of variable intensity. P53 immunostaining was assessed by counting at least 1000 neoplastic cells across the entire section, with evaluations conducted by two pathologists. The percentage of tumor cells displaying strong P53 immunoreactivity was recorded. P53 null-expression was characterized by tumor cells that were completely negative, contrasted against positively stained stromal cells.

Establishing the threshold for P53 overexpression

Two thresholds for P53 overexpression were identified. The threshold for predicting TP53 mutations was determined using ROC curve analysis, with TP53 gene status serving as the gold standard. In contrast, the threshold for predicting recurrence was established using X-tile software (Version 3.6.1, Yale University School of Medicine).

P53 mutant expression serves as a surrogate marker for TP53 mutation and is defined as either P53 overexpression or null-expression. In contrast, P53 aberrant expression, which is utilized to predict recurrence, is defined solely by P53 overexpression. Figure 2 presents representative IHC images illustrating P53 expression.

Statistical analysis

The relationship between TP53 mutations and P53 mutant expression was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic. The κ values were interpreted as follows: poor agreement (0.01–0.20), slight agreement (0.00–0.20), fair agreement (0.21–0.40), moderate agreement (0.41–0.60), substantial agreement (0.61–0.80), and perfect agreement (0.81–1.00). Additionally, the sensitivity and specificity of P53 mutant expression for predicting TP53 mutations were calculated.

The relationships among P53 mutant expression, P53 aberrant expression, TP53 mutations, and clinicopathological characteristics were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test along with Fisher’s exact test.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were employed to assess the predictive significance of TP53 mutations, P53 mutant expression, and P53 aberrant expression.

Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27.0 and GraphPad Prism version 10 software. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant for all tests performed.

Results

Patients information

This study examined 191 cases of primary stage I lung adenocarcinoma. The cohort included 107 females (60.8%) and 58 males (39.2%), with a median age of 63 years (ranging from 41 to 85). Among the patients, 114 (59.7%) were aged 60 years or older, and 52 (27.2%) had a history of smoking. According to the pTNM classification, 149 cases (78.0%) were classified as stage IA, while 42 cases (22.0%) were classified as stage IB. Histopathological grading indicated that 21 cases (11.0%) were G1, 154 cases (80.6%) were G 2, and 16 cases (8.4%) were G3.

The follow-up period ranged from 4 to 60 months, with a mean of 54.5 months and a median of 60 months. Among the 191 patients, 27 (14.1%) experienced disease recurrence between 4 and 60 months postoperatively, comprising 16 cases (59.3%) of regional recurrence and 11 cases (40.7%) of distant metastasis. Of these, 8 patients (29.6%) subsequently died of the disease, with the interval from documented recurrence to disease-specific death ranging from 1 to 20 months.

TP53 mutation

Among the 191 cases, 66 were identified as TP53 mutant-type (Supplementary data). A total of 73 mutations were recorded, comprising 50 (68.5%) missense mutations, 9 (12.7%) splicing mutations, 8 (11.3%) frameshift mutations, 5 (7.0%) nonsense mutations, and 1 (1.4%) insertion mutation. The mutations were most frequently observed in exon 5 (26 mutations, 35.6%), followed by exon 8 (17 mutations, 23.3%) (Fig. 3). Within the functional domains of TP53, variations were most commonly found in the DNA binding domain (DBD). The predominant single nucleotide variant (SNV) was G > T. Other mutations included A159V, G154V, R158L, and R273L, each found in 2 cases, while the remaining mutations were exclusive to individual cases.

Thresholds for P53 overexpression

In all instances, the percentage of P53 positive cells varied between 0% and 100%. The median and mean values were 8.0% and 23.7%, respectively. The threshold for P53 overexpression indicative of mutant expression was established at 40%, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.914 (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the threshold for P53 overexpression associated with aberrant expression was determined to be 62% using X-tile software (Fig. 4B-C). We found that there were no patients with P53 expression between 60% and 62% and to facilitate clinical application, 60% was selected as the practical threshold.

A ROC curve of P53 in predicting TP53 mutation of stage I lung adenocarcinoma; (B-C) X-Tile software was used to analyze the threshold of P53 IHC expression for the DFS of stage I lung adenocarcinoma

P53 mutant expression and aberrant expression

Out of the 191 cases analyzed, 60 exhibited P53 mutant expression, comprising 50 instances of P53 overexpression and 10 instances of P53 null-expression. Additionally, there were 31 cases of P53 aberrant expression.

Relationship between P53 mutant expression and TP53 mutation

In our study, we found that all instances of P53 null-expression corresponded to TP53 mutations, achieving a detection rate of 100%. This included 4 nonsense mutations (40.0%), 3 frame-shift mutations (30.0%), and 3 splice-site mutations (30.0%). Among the 50 cases of P53 mutant overexpression (>40%), 48 cases (96.0%) exhibited mutant TP53, while 2 cases (4.0%) were identified as wild type TP53. The correlation between P53 mutant expression and TP53 mutation was exceptionally strong, with a kappa value of 0.882. The sensitivity and specificity of P53 mutant expression for identifying TP53 mutations were 87.9% and 98.4%, respectively.

Predictive value of distinct TP53 mutation types and P53 mutant expression patterns

We investigated the prognostic relevance of the different TP53 mutation types and P53 mutant expression patterns within the stage I lung adenocarcinoma. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated that individuals with TP53 missense mutations exhibited the worst outcomes compared to those with either wild-type or non-missense mutations, whose prognoses were comparable (Fig. 5A). Accordingly, for subsequent survival analyses, the TP53 status of patients were stratified into two categories: TP53 missense mutations and others. Importantly, patients with P53 null-expression had the best prognosis, while those with P53 mutant overexpression(>40%), exhibited the worst outcomes. Patients with normal P53 expression fell in between these two groups (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

TP53 gene, located on chromosome 17p13.1, is a crucial tumor suppressor gene and the most frequently mutated gene in humans, playing a central role in maintaining genomic stability and preventing tumorigenesis [15]. Mutations in TP53 disrupt this function, leading to increased genomic instability and fostering cancer progression. Most TP53 mutations are missense mutations, resulting in single amino acid substitutions, with the DBD being the most commonly affected region [4, 16, 17]. The P53 protein, consisting of 393 amino acids, regulates diverse biological processes, including cell cycle control, apoptosis, autophagy, and genomic stability [18].

Under physiological conditions, P53 expression is low and manifests as scattered nuclear positivity on IHC. However, TP53 mutations can alter P53 expression, producing abnormal patterns such as null-expression (0%), overexpression (≥ threshold), and cytoplasmic localization [10]. Typically, P53 overexpression is strongly associated with TP53 missense mutations, while P53 null-expression or cytoplasmic localization often results from frame-shift, nonsense, or splice-site mutations [19].

In routine clinical practice, P53 IHC is the most commonly employed method for determining TP53 mutation status, primarily due to its cost-effectiveness, quicker turnaround time, and greater accessibility compared to NGS. A notable success story is found in ovarian carcinoma, where the criteria for P53 mutant expression have been well-defined: overexpression (> 80%), null-expression (0%) and cytoplasmic localization. This method demonstrates a specificity of 100% and a sensitivity of 96% for predicting TP53 mutations [11]. In endometrial carcinoma, applying the same criteria as in ovarian carcinoma yields a specificity of 94% and a sensitivity of 91% for predicting TP53 mutations [12]. While many studies suggest that P53 overexpression can reliably indicate TP53 mutations in various tumor types, the optimal threshold and predictive efficiency remain subjects of ongoing debate [20–22]. In lung cancer, research is notably sparse, however, a study from Japan indicated that using a 75% overexpression threshold resulted in substantial agreement between P53 overexpression and TP53 mutations (κ = 0.634) [23].

In our study, we established a 40% threshold for P53 overexpression and found that majority of patients exhibiting this overexpression (41 out of 50) had TP53 missense mutations. Among the remaining nine cases, there were two frameshift mutations, three splice site mutations, one nonsense mutation, one insertion mutation, and two cases with undetectable mutations. Our findings indicate that most instances of P53 overexpression in lung adenocarcinoma are attributable to TP53 missense mutations, while a minority arise from non-missense mutations. Additionally, the rare cases without detectable TP53 mutations may be due to other factors. Furthermore, our results confirm that P53 null-expression serves as a reliable indicator of TP53 non-missense mutations, aligning with existing research on ovarian cancer as noted in previous publications [24].

In our study, we found that using a 40% threshold for overexpression, the P53 mutant expression showed a significant association with TP53 mutations (p < 0.001), demonstrating perfect concordance (κ = 0.882). This concordance was notably higher than that reported in Japanese research, which had a κ value of 0.634 [23].

In addition, our research first investigated the prognostic relevance of distinct TP53 mutation subtypes and different P53 mutant expression patterns within lung cancer. In breast cancer literature inconsistent results have been reported regarding the impact of TP53 mutation types on clinical outcome [25, 26]. A study investigating 404 endometrial cancers reported there were no significant differences in 5-year recurrence rates between the P53 overexpression and null-expression patterns [27]. Our findings indicate that patients with TP53 non-missense mutations demonstrate survival outcomes comparable to individuals with wild-type TP53. Additionally, patients with P53 null-expression demonstrated the most favorable prognosis compared to those with normal or P53 mutant overexpressed. The data indicate a potential gradation in prognostic risk associated with specific molecular alterations. However, due to the limited number of cases with TP53 non-missense mutations and P53 null-expression in this study, these preliminary findings warrant further validation in larger, well-characterized cohorts.

In published studies of P53 overexpression and its prognostic implications in lung cancer, the thresholds defining overexpression varied significantly, ranging from 10% to 75% [23, 28, 29]. Notably, these thresholds were generally not established through outcome-based optimization, instead, they were primarily based on the quartile-based categorization or referenced from ovarian carcinoma studies. To establish a clinically relevant threshold, we employed X-tile software [30], a bioinformatics tool specifically designed for outcome-based threshold optimization, to establish the optimal threshold for P53 overexpression in stage I lung adenocarcinoma using DFS data. Unlike conventional approaches, X-tile systematically evaluates all possible thresholds and identifies the one that maximizes the survival difference between subgroups, with Monte Carlo cross-validation to guard against overfitting, and X-tile identified an optimal threshold of 60%. To our knowledge, this is the first study to define an optimal threshold for P53 overexpression in stage I lung adenocarcinoma using X-tile, although the software has previously been applied to optimize prognostic thresholds for lymph node parameters in lung cancer [31–33]. Using this 60% threshold, patients exhibiting P53 aberrant expression experienced significantly poorer DFS compared to those with normal expression. Moreover, P53 aberrant expression demonstrated greater specificity than TP53 gene mutations in predicting postoperative recurrence of stage I lung adenocarcinoma. This discovery underscores the potential of P53 aberrant expression as a practical and cost-effective biomarker for routine clinical application, providing valuable insights for personalized treatment strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study underscores the clinical utility of P53 IHC in lung cancer. By establishing a threshold of 40% for overexpression, we found that P53 mutant expression, categorized as either overexpression or null expression, serves as a reliable surrogate for TP53 mutations. Furthermore, both TP53 missense mutations and P53 aberrant expression, defined as overexpression with a threshold of 60%, effectively predict postoperative recurrence within five years for patients with stage I lung adenocarcinoma. Additional research is needed to investigate the prognostic implications of various P53 expression types and specific types of TP53 mutations in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma.
