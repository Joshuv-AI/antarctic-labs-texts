Outcomes and Clinical Practices in Follow-Up of Nonmalignant Pulmonary Nodules After Bronchoscopic Biopsy
Authors: Camilo A Avendano, Luis O Gerena-Montano, Jason A Beattie, Mihir S Parikh, Kai E Swenson, Adnan Majid, Chenchen Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundManagement of nonmalignant nodules after bronchoscopic biopsy lacks thorough investigation, leading to varied clinical approaches.Research QuestionWhat are the true-negative and false-negative rates of nonmalignant nodules by bronchoscopic biopsy, and what is the clinical practice pattern for follow-up?Study Design and MethodsA retrospective review was conducted for pulmonary nodules biopsied via bronchoscopy between 2019 and 2020 revealing no malignancy. True-negative and false-negative rates were calculated. Clinical practice patterns for repeat biopsy and follow-up imaging studies were analyzed using time-to-event analysis. Covariates, including sex, age, smoking history, nodule size, maximum standardized uptake values, active cancer diagnosis, bronchoscopy type, and nodule pathologic findings, were evaluated for their impact on clinical decision-making.ResultsAmong 136 patients with 139 nodules analyzed, only 39.6% of nodules were classified definitively as benign (true-negative rate), whereas 23.7% of nodules were deemed malignant (false-negative rate). Repeat biopsy was performed for 35.3% of nodules, and 66.9% of patients underwent follow-up CT imaging. Nodules initially diagnosed with suspicious or atypical pathologic results reached a final diagnosis (hazard ratio, 3.65; 95% CI, 1.33-9.99) earlier than those with nondiagnostic pathologic findings. Patients with such nodules underwent repeat biopsy sooner (χ2 = 15.85; P = .000) and exhibited a trend toward earlier repeat CT scans (χ2 = 5.93; P = .052).InterpretationAfter bronchoscopic biopsy, a 2-year follow-up revealed a notable lack of high true-negative rates for nonmalignant nodules, alongside significant false-negative rates. Diverse clinical practices, including repeat biopsy and follow-up CT scans, were observed in managing nonmalignant nodules.Key Words: biopsy, imaging, pathology, pulmonary nodule

Take-home Points

Study Question: What are the true-negative and false-negative rates of nonmalignant nodules by bronchoscopic biopsy, and what is the clinical practice pattern for follow-up?

Results: Among 136 patients with 139 nodules analyzed, 39.6% of nodules were classified definitively as benign (true-negative rate), whereas 23.7% of nodules were deemed malignant (false-negative rate), with 35.3% of nodules undergoing repeat biopsy and 66.9% undergoing follow-up CT imaging.

Interpretation: After bronchoscopic biopsy, a 2-year follow-up revealed significant false-negative rates and diverse clinical practices, including repeat biopsy and follow-up CT scans, in managing nonmalignant nodules.

A pulmonary nodule or mass represents a focal, discernible radiographic density within the lung parenchyma, typically identified on CT imaging. The identification of pulmonary nodules has surged after the widespread implementation of nationwide lung cancer screening scans.1 Despite the benign nature of most detected nodules,2 it is critical to distinguish malignant cases from benign ones because of the associated morbidity and mortality of lung cancer.3

Bronchoscopy, a common method for pulmonary nodule biopsy, has advanced with the introduction of electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy and robotic-assisted bronchoscopy, despite inherent imperfections.4, 5, 6 Meta-analyses on robotic-assisted bronchoscopy and electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy showed a pooled diagnostic yield of 84.3% and 64.9%, respectively.6,7 Interestingly, in those studies, the definition of benign nodules lacks clarity compared with that of malignant nodules, primarily because of interpretation challenges involving nonspecific or unexpected findings, necessitating further investigation.8, 9, 10, 11 This complexity extends to the optimal follow-up plan for pulmonary nodules, given the lack of consensus on repeat biopsy and the optimal frequency and duration for follow-up with imaging studies.

The current study endeavored to explore the true-negative rate of biopsy-proven nonmalignant nodules via bronchoscopy and to assess clinical diagnostic rates for benign nodules. It also examined decision-making timelines and factors influencing repeat biopsy, follow-up CT scans, and final decisions in this context.

Study Design and Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

A retrospective analysis evaluated patients undergoing diagnostic bronchoscopy with lung biopsy between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2020. This study obtained ethics approval from the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center review board (Identifier: 2023P000013). Patients undergoing bronchoscopy fine-needle aspiration, transbronchial biopsy, or both for lung nodule or mass were included in the study. Exclusion criteria applied if the biopsy lacked specific targeting by radiographic methods such as radial endobronchial ultrasound, fluoroscopy, cone-beam CT imaging, or a combination thereof. Patients with any form of malignancy or benign tumor from the nodule biopsy or malignancy identified from concurrent mediastinal lymph node biopsy were excluded from the study.

Data Collection

Demographic data, bronchoscopy reports, imaging studies, repeat biopsies, and clinic notes underwent examination. Chart review extended up to 2 years after the index biopsy. Three bronchoscopy techniques were used: ion robotic-assisted bronchoscopy (IntuitiveSurgical, Inc.), nonrobotic navigational bronchoscopy using an electromagnetic navigation system (superDimension navigation system, Medtronic), and nonnavigational bronchoscopy when regular bronchoscopy was performed without a navigational system.

Pathologic reports underwent recategorization retrospectively at the time of data collection. Patients showing suspicion of malignancy or atypical cells were classified in the suspicious or atypical group. The category of specific benign pathology was used to classify findings like granulomatous inflammation, organizing pneumonia, and inflammation with bacterial, mycobacterial, or fungal culture showing positive results that could explain the radiologic findings.9 Instances with pathology reports revealing alveolated lung parenchyma, bronchial cells, blood, clots, acute or chronic inflammation, or any nonspecific findings were categorized as nondiagnostic.

Definitions of Benign and Malignant Nodules

For the final diagnosis of a true benign nodule or a true-negative result, the assessment followed the following criteria: (1) malignancy was ruled out through confirmation on the repeat surgical biopsy or (2) nodule size remained stable on CT scans throughout a 2-year period or decreased in size, either with a subsequent nonsurgical biopsy revealing no malignancy or without a repeat biopsy. The nodule’s final diagnosis as malignant, or a false-negative result, relied on malignancy detection in repeat biopsy or the initiation of empirical cancer-directed therapy without a confirmed pathologic diagnosis.

A nodule received an inconclusive final diagnosis if one of the following criteria was met: (1) a nodule remained stable in size, but was tracked for < 2 years; (2) no imaging studies were conducted for follow-up assessment either without a repeat biopsy or after a nonmalignant diagnosis was reaffirmed after a repeat nonsurgical biopsy; (3) an increase in nodule size was observed by the end of the 2-year follow-up period; (4) the patient was lost to follow-up, passed away, or transitioned to hospice before conclusive clinical conclusions.

Outcome Measures

The primary end points assessed true-negative and false-negative rates in nonmalignant pulmonary nodules after bronchoscopy biopsy, with the rates of clinical diagnoses for benign and malignant nodules. Secondary end points included time to final diagnosis, time to repeat biopsy, and time to the first repeat CT scan.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]), whereas categorical variables are expressed as number (percentage). The comparison of variables was conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Time-to-event outcomes were assessed through Kaplan-Meier curves with the log-rank test used for comparison. Cox proportional hazards regression with the Breslow method for ties was used to examine the influence of covariances on the aforementioned time-to-event outcomes. Goodness of fit of the statistical models was assessed through multiple criteria: (1) the likelihood ratio χ2 test assessed overall model significance, (2) discriminatory power was measured using the concordance index, (3) the assumption of proportional hazards was verified using the Grambsch-Therneau test, and (4) deviance residuals were examined to assess model fit, identifying potential outliers that could influence results. Additionally, the model’s complexity and goodness of fit were assessed using the Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion. All statistical analyses were conducted using StataBE version 18.0 software (StataCorp).

Nodule Characteristics

Table 2 outlines features of the 139 biopsied nodules. Most nodules (88.5%) were discovered incidentally during chest CT scans, often prompted by concerns like shortness of breath. Nonrobotic navigational bronchoscopy was the most frequently used biopsy method. Nodules were predominantly in the right upper lobe (38.9%), concentrated in the outer one-third of the lung field (43.2%), as defined by Casal et al.12 Most nodules surpassed 10 mm, demonstrating a median short axis measurement of 23 mm. These nodules demonstrated low maximum standardized uptake values with a median value of 3.6. Additionally, 65.5% of them appeared to be solid, and a notable 98.6% displayed a positive bronchus sign. Most (62.6%) were categorized as having nondiagnostic pathologic findings. Complications from the index procedure were limited, with few instances of bleeding and pneumothorax reported.

Nodule Follow-Up

The follow-up management of the nodules, treatments offered, and overall outcomes are detailed in Table 3. Among the 139 nodules, 35.3% underwent repeat biopsy, with 57.1% of nodules (28/49) opting for CT imaging-guided percutaneous biopsy. Among the nodules under follow-up CT imaging surveillance (93 nodules [66.9%]), 76.3% of nodules (71/93) remained stable or decreased in size. Notably, 21.6% of nodules (30 nodules) did not undergo repeat biopsy or CT imaging examination, whereas 23.7% of nodules (33 nodules) underwent both repeat biopsy and follow-up CT imaging. Across the entire cohort, 41.9% of patients (57/136) received medical treatments, including cancer therapy, steroids, and antibiotics or antifungals. Among the 18 patients undergoing surgery, four patients did so following CT scan-guided repeat biopsy. Primary Outcome

The final diagnosis confirmed 39.6% of nodules as benign, 23.7% as malignant, and 36.7% as having inconclusive results. Table 4 compares management and outcomes based on initial pathologic diagnoses, revealing significant differences in clinical management patterns (P = .001). For nodules with suspicious or atypical findings, 69.2% of nodules (9/13) underwent repeat biopsy, with five nodules subsequently confirmed as malignant. In contrast, only 39.1% of nodules with nondiagnostic results underwent repeat biopsy, decreasing further to 15.4% for specific benign pathologic findings. Decision-making on no follow-up imaging was higher for nodules with specific benign pathologic results at 15.4%, compared with 4.6% for nondiagnostic results and 0% for suspicious or atypical findings.

Time-to-Event Analysis

The median time to reach the final diagnosis was 228 days (IQR, 63-730 days), as illustrated in the Kaplan-Meier curve depicted in Figure 2A. Covariates, including sex, age, smoking history, nodule size, maximum standardized uptake value, active cancer diagnosis, type of bronchoscopy, and nodule pathologic findings were incorporated into the Cox proportional hazards model (Table 5). The model exhibited insignificant predictive capability (χ2 = 13.33; P = .21), but acceptable discriminatory power (Concordance index, 0.65). Proportional hazards assumption testing showed no evidence of deviation from proportionality (P = .89). Deviance residuals confirmed adequate model fit with no significant outliers identified (e-Fig 1). The model’s Akaike information criterion was 348.70 and Bayesian information criterion was 371.6. Suspicious or atypical pathologic results demonstrated a significantly higher hazard than nondiagnostic pathologic results (hazard ratio, 3.65; 95% CI, 1.33-9.99), whereas specific benign pathologic features did not exhibit a similar characteristic (hazard ratio, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.35-2.50; χ2 = 1.60; P = .450) (Fig 2B).

In the time-to-repeat biopsy analysis, a total of 49 of the 139 nodules underwent repeat biopsy, with a median interval of 52 days (IQR, 30-111 days) (Fig 2C). Patients with suspicious or atypical findings underwent repeat biopsy much earlier in the course, followed by those with nondiagnostic findings (χ2 = 15.85; P = .000) (Fig 2D). Despite examining all the previously mentioned covariates, none exhibited a statistically significant higher hazard in the Cox proportional hazards model (e-Table 1, where the goodness-of-fit indices also can be found).

Time-to-event analysis also was conducted for the first follow-up CT scan. Ninety-three nodules underwent repeat CT scans after the initial biopsy, with a median interval of 57 days (IQR, 31-117 days) (Fig 2E). Patients with suspicious or atypical findings on pathologic examination tended to undergo the first repeat CT scans earlier than those with other pathologic results types (χ2 = 5.93; P = .052) (Fig 2F). The Cox model incorporating the mentioned covariates revealed that nodules biopsied nonrobotically underwent a slower rate of repeat CT scans compared with those biopsied robotically (hazard ratio, 0.41; 95% CI, 0.10-0.88) (e-Table 2, under which the goodness-of-fit indices are listed).

Discussion

This study focused on the diagnostic rates, follow-up strategies, and clinical decision-making for nonmalignant pulmonary nodules biopsied via bronchoscopy at a tertiary center. Only 39.6% of the nodules ultimately were classified as benign (true-negative rate), and 23.7% were classified as malignant (false-negative rate). Nodules exhibiting suspicious or atypical findings reached conclusions sooner than those with nondiagnostic pathologic findings, often involving prompt repeat biopsy or repeat imaging studies. A notable portion of nodules received empirical treatment without pathologic confirmation, whereas others lacked follow-up biopsy or imaging, highlighting practice standardization gaps.

Defining true benign nodules remains challenging because of discrepancies in the literature. Common criteria included benign cause confirmed with subsequent biopsy; stability or resolution of nodule on radiographic follow-up; and specific benign characteristics, positive microbiological results, or a combination thereof.5,6,13,14 Several problems exist with these criteria. First, a benign diagnosis made by a second nonsurgical biopsy does not completely exclude the possibility of malignancy.15 Second, certain specific benign diagnoses, such as organizing pneumonia and granulomatous inflammation, can be adjacent or coexist with lung cancer.10,11 Third, the optimal duration for imaging follow-up when a nonmalignant diagnosis is made is unknown. A 12-month interval is reported frequently in the literature.5,6,13 Other studies proposed that nodules demonstrating stability for at least 2 years were indicative of a benign nature.16, 17, 18 Our study adhered to a very strict definition for benign nodules, as stated in the Methods, consistent with the approach used by Gelbman et al.9 Berman et al19 studied patients treated with radiation therapy to the pulmonary nodules with or without a pathologic diagnosis, and no difference was found in local control or progression-free survival, suggesting that those patients without pathologic diagnoses truly might have had lung cancer. Therefore, we included nodules from patients who received empirical cancer therapy in the malignant category.

The false-negative rate of 23.7% found in our study aligns with findings reported in prior studies.7,20 Interestingly, 36.7% of nodules fell into the inconclusive category based on the definition. This is likely attributable to several factors, including nodules not undergoing repeat biopsy or follow-up CT scans or cases where nodules, although stable, were monitored for a duration of ≤ 2 years. In some instances, clinicians deemed no follow-up necessary, whereas in other instances, patients were lost to follow-up.

The rate of repeating biopsy in our study was 35.3%, influenced significantly by the initial biopsy results. The median interval for a repeat biopsy was 52 days. Patient selection criteria for a second biopsy remain unclear. One study suggested repeating transthoracic biopsy for all nonmalignant nodules found via bronchoscopy, achieving 83% accuracy from the first biopsy, increasing to 100% by the third biopsy.15 Repeat biopsy rates varied from 4.1% to 14.4% in other studies, with inconclusive or unexpected pathologic results being common reasons.21, 22, 23 The British Thoracic Society guidelines recommend repeating biopsy if the suspicion of malignancy is high (grade D).24 However, malignancy risk calculation falls beyond the scope of our study.

Following up with serial CT scans after a nonmalignant diagnosis is common. However, the optimal interval and length of follow-up are unknown, because the literature lacks consistent reporting. Our study found that the median time to first follow-up CT scan was 57 days. One study suggested repeating scans at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months.15 Interestingly, clinicians in our study often skipped follow-up imaging when specific benign pathologic findings were identified, contrary to existing literature and guideline.25, 26, 27 This discrepancy may be the result of patients receiving care outside our network, resulting in missing data.

Notably, our study demonstrated that factors conventionally correlated with lung cancer incidence, such as advanced age, smoking history, increased nodule size on CT scans, and heightened fluorodeoxyglucose avidity on PET scans,28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33 did not influence clinical decision-making regarding the timing of biopsies or repeat CT scans. Moreover, these factors did not impact the speed at which the final diagnosis was reached. This highlights the need for future studies to delve deeper into these aspects. In our study, the only factor associated with clinical decision-making on repeat CT scans, repeat biopsy, or both was the initial pathologic results, specifically when atypical or suspicious cells were found. This is likely because of the increased clinical suspicion of malignancy.

A recent study on the use of Percepta Genomic Sequencing Classifier (Veracyte, Inc.) to restratify pulmonary nodules after nondiagnostic bronchoscopy has concluded that the classifier successfully recategorized 38% of patients in the intermediate risk group and can help to guide decision-making.34 Unfortunately, the genomic classifier was not used widely during the study period, and therefore was not analyzed in this study. Study Limitations

This retrospective study explored the ambiguity surrounding the definition of true benign nodules, leading to varied clinical practices. However, being a single-center study, the generalizability of our findings to broader patient populations may be limited. Single-center studies often reflect specific patient demographics and local practice patterns, which may not be representative of other settings. Further multicenter studies are needed to identify factors that can predict the final diagnosis and can determine the necessity for repeat CT imaging, biopsy, or both in broader patient populations. Additionally, tools like the 1.1-mm cryoprobe, cone-beam CT imaging, and genomic classifier were not addressed in this study because of their limited use.

Interpretation

Our study revealed a low true-negative rate for biopsy-proven nonmalignant nodules, accompanied by notable false-negative rates. Diverse clinical practices in follow-up, including repeat biopsy, CT scans, or both, indicate complexity and heterogeneity in management. Standardized guidelines are imperative to improve consistency and reliability in clinical decision-making for this specific patient cohort.
