A complex case of moyamoya disease mimicking vasculitis: diagnostic challenges and systemic complications in a young adult
Authors: Majd A AbuAlrob, Abdullah Al Qazakzeh, Najla Al-Eshaq, Ali Aqel, Salma Faisal, Wanis H Ibrahim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMoyamoya disease (MMD) is a rare, chronic cerebrovascular disorder that can mimic vasculitis, making diagnosis challenging. We present a 35-year-old Filipino woman with occipital headache, left-sided facial weakness, and transient left arm numbness. Initial CT was unremarkable, but MRI showed multifocal ischemic infarcts; MRA later revealed bilateral MCA and ACA occlusions with collateral vessels, confirming MMD. Laboratory testing was negative for inflammatory markers, and PET-CT excluded vasculitis. The patient received antiplatelet therapy and blood pressure control, but her course was complicated by myocardial infarction and acute kidney injury, precluding surgical intervention. This case underscores the importance of early advanced imaging and comprehensive evaluation in young patients with recurrent strokes to distinguish MMD from vasculitis, enabling timely and appropriate management.Keywords: Moyamoya disease, Vasculitis, Stroke, Diagnostic challenge

Introduction

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a rare, chronic cerebrovascular condition characterized by progressive narrowing of the internal carotid arteries and their branches, particularly the middle and anterior cerebral arteries [1]. This narrowing leads to the development of compensatory collateral vessels, which appear like a "puff of smoke" on angiography, a hallmark of MMD [2]. Although first identified in children, MMD also affects adults, often presenting with recurrent ischemic strokes, transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), and sometimes hemorrhagic strokes or cognitive decline [3].

The underlying cause of MMD remains uncertain. However, mutations in the RNF213 gene have been strongly associated with the disease, particularly in Asian populations [4]. Environmental and immune factors may also play a role, though their contribution is less clearly defined compared to other vascular conditions like vasculitis [5]. Diagnosing MMD can be difficult, especially in adults, as the clinical presentation often overlaps with other conditions, notably vasculitis. This overlap frequently leads to delayed diagnosis, as clinicians may initially suspect an inflammatory cause [6].

Imaging is crucial in differentiating MMD from other cerebrovascular diseases. Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) is particularly effective in detecting stenosis and the characteristic collateral vessels associated with MMD, making it the preferred diagnostic tool [7]. Additional imaging methods like positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) can help assess metabolic activity and rule out vasculitis, while computed tomography angiography (CTA) provides detailed images of the affected vessels (Estmann, Toftedal, & Schou, 2011).

Laboratory tests also play an important role in the diagnostic process. Common tests, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and autoimmune panels, are useful in excluding vasculitis and other inflammatory conditions. Negative autoimmune findings, combined with characteristic imaging, strongly support an MMD diagnosis [6].

This case report presents a young adult who experienced recurrent ischemic strokes initially suspected to be due to vasculitis but later diagnosed with MMD. It emphasizes the importance of early advanced imaging and thorough laboratory evaluation to avoid diagnostic delays and ensure proper treatment. The case highlights the need for prompt and accurate diagnostic strategies in young patients with recurrent strokes and absent inflammatory markers.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old woman was admitted to Hamad General Hospital, Qatar, with a gradual onset of occipital, pulsating headache and lower left facial weakness, accompanied by transient left arm paresthesia lasting 1 hour. She had no prior medical or surgical history, and there was no known family history of cerebrovascular or autoimmune disease. Her symptoms began 1 day before presenting to the Emergency Department. Initial noncontrast CT of the head was unremarkable. MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging revealed multifocal patchy areas of diffusion restriction and T2 hyperintense signals in the bilateral cortical/subcortical frontal lobes, centrum semiovale, lentiform nuclei, and right insula, consistent with recent ischemic infarcts. Postcontrast MRI showed no abnormal parenchymal enhancement, supporting a noninflammatory etiology.

MR angiography revealed occlusion of the right MCA M1 segment just distal to its origin, with faint proximal flow, and occlusion of the left MCA proximal M1 segment over 5.8 mm, with patent distal flow. A hypoplastic left ACA was also noted (Fig. 1). The patient's National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score was 2, precluding thrombolysis or thrombectomy. She was treated with aspirin and statin. Initial echocardiography showed mild left atrial enlargement and grade 1 diastolic dysfunction, while Holter monitoring was unremarkable.

Supraclinoid ICA Occlusion with NonVisualization of Both MCA and ACA. This figure demonstrates occlusion of the supraclinoid internal carotid artery (ICA) with nonvisualization of both the middle cerebral artery (MCA) and anterior cerebral artery (ACA), suggesting significant flow disruption in both major territories.

Three days after admission, the patient’s condition worsened, with a sudden drop in her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score to 10/15 and new left-sided weakness (3/5 strength) with facial droop. Urgent head CT showed infarcts in the right frontal and temporal cortical-subcortical regions and right basal ganglia. CT angiography revealed persistent occlusion of both MCA M1 segments, more severe on the left (Fig. 2). A CT perfusion study showed perfusion defects in both frontal lobes and the right parieto-temporal region, raising suspicion for vasculitis. Other mimics—such as reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome and embolic disease—were considered but deemed less likely given the absence of thunderclap headache, the lack of a cardioembolic source on echocardiography and Holter monitoring, and the characteristic angiographic pattern. Repeat MRI confirmed interval development of new infarcts.

Right and left-sided views of supraclinoid ICA occlusion. Bilateral views showing the occlusion of the supraclinoid segment of the ICA. The images highlight the absence of flow in both MCA and ACA territories on both sides, with collateral vessel formation indicative of chronic vascular pathology.

By day 4, the patient's GCS had dropped to 8/10, leading to elective intubation and transfer to the intensive care unit. Lumbar puncture ruled out CNS infection, and methylprednisolone was initiated, followed by oral prednisolone. The rheumatology team was consulted for suspected vasculitis, but autoimmune and vasculitis workups, including inflammatory markers, were negative.

On day 5, repeat MR angiography showed progressive steno-occlusion of the bilateral internal carotid arteries above the clinoid segment, as well as the MCA M1 segments, more pronounced on the left (Fig. 3). These findings, coupled with the hypoplastic left ACA, suggested moyamoya disease, which was confirmed by digital subtraction angiography. The neurosurgical team opted against intervention due to the patient's poor clinical condition. These systemic complications, together with neurologic decline, precluded surgical revascularization and necessitated conservative medical management.

Right MCA occlusion, distal MCA preserved flow, and faint ACA visualization. Axial images demonstrating occlusion of the right MCA at the M1 segment with preservation of distal MCA flow. Faint visualization of the ACA suggests compromised but residual flow, potentially contributing to ischemic vulnerability in the ACA territory.

The patient’s clinical course was further complicated by ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) with ischemic cardiomyopathy, treated medically, and acute kidney injury requiring hemodialysis. She also developed septic shock, necessitating broad-spectrum antibiotics and inotropic support. A CT abdomen performed to rule out an abdominal collection revealed vascular abnormalities suggestive of vasculitis. Rheumatology recommended an MRA aortogram, which showed short-segment stenosis at the origin of the right renal artery and mild narrowing of the infrarenal aorta, raising suspicion for systemic vasculitis. However, a PET scan did not show active vascular inflammation, making the findings likely secondary to shock or inotropic support. She remained in the intensive care unit until her condition stabilized, after which she gradually improved. At present, she continues to have residual left-sided weakness but is otherwise clinically stable. No further serial MRI or CT perfusion studies were performed, as the patient’s unstable clinical status and systemic complications made additional imaging impractical and potentially fatiguing

Discussion

The delayed diagnosis of moyamoya disease (MMD) in this case highlights the difficulty in distinguishing it from vasculitis, particularly in young adults with recurrent ischemic strokes. Early use of advanced imaging techniques, such as magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) or digital subtraction angiography (DSA), could have expedited the diagnosis. The combination of multifocal infarcts, normal inflammatory markers, and negative autoimmune tests should have prompted earlier MRA to identify the characteristic stenosis and collateral vessels of MMD [8]. Although vasculitis can also show multifocal vascular involvement, the absence of inflammatory markers increases the likelihood of MMD, emphasizing the need for advanced imaging when lab results do not strongly indicate vasculitis [6]. Timely angiography could have revealed the "puff of smoke" appearance and mitigated diagnostic delays. Additionally, the risk factors for hemorrhagic presentation in MMD have been described by Lv, who highlighted vascular structural vulnerabilities contributing to bleeding in affected patients [9].

This case is notable for its combination of diagnostic complexity and severe systemic complications. To our knowledge, few reports describe MMD initially suspected to be vasculitis, investigated with PET-CT to exclude active vascular inflammation, and complicated by both myocardial infarction and acute kidney injury. The integration of multimodal neurovascular and systemic imaging in the diagnostic process, alongside management decisions influenced by non-neurological complications, underscores the innovative aspect of this report.

The management of the patient’s systemic complications, including myocardial infarction (MI) and acute kidney injury (AKI), also required earlier intervention. Preexisting hypertension increased the risk for both cerebrovascular and systemic events, but more proactive blood pressure control and early antiplatelet therapy might have reduced these complications. Research indicates that antiplatelet therapy can reduce recurrent strokes in MMD, especially in the absence of surgical intervention [3]. Implementing these measures sooner could have stabilized the patient before the onset of MI and AKI.

While surgery is the conventional treatment for MMD, nonsurgical options require further exploration, especially in complex cases. Emerging therapies that target endothelial dysfunction, such as statins and endothelin receptor antagonists, show promise in enhancing vascular reactivity and reducing stroke recurrence in MMD patients [4]. Given the patient's MI and AKI, which heightened surgical risks, nonsurgical management needed to be optimized.

Improved communication among the multidisciplinary team could have enhanced outcomes. Early interdisciplinary consultations between neurology, cardiology, and rheumatology might have expedited diagnostic and treatment decisions. A standardized protocol for young patients with atypical strokes, involving early imaging and consultations, would help ensure MMD is promptly considered. Regular interdisciplinary evaluations could have helped prevent delays in managing complex cases like this [1].

In conclusion, timely advanced imaging and proactive management of both neurological and systemic complications are critical in cases of recurrent ischemic strokes with negative inflammatory markers. Early MRA or DSA and improved multidisciplinary coordination can significantly improve patient outcomes.
