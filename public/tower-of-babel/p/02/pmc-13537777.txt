A novel approach for wound treatment using Mitragyna speciosa-encapsulated nanostructured lipid carriers in hydrogel film
Authors: Anan Khadijah Imani Zulkifli, Siti Efliza Ashari, Syahida Ahmad, Masriana Hassan, Razana Mohd Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdequate wound healing is important for re-establishing the normal skin function. Determining the most suitable therapeutic strategy for skin repair involves multiple considerations, including wound type and condition, injury severity, lesion extent, and exudate level. Mitragyna speciosa (M. speciosa) is prominent for its therapeutic benefits and wound-healing properties. Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) have proven beneficial for the M. speciosa extract, due to the restricted biocompatibility of active compounds in M. speciosa leaves. This study is the first to showcase the potential of M. speciosa leaf extract encapsulated in NLCs in a hydrogel for wound healing in rat models. M. speciosa leaf extract–encapsulated NLCs (MS-NLC) in hydrogel film can enhance wound recovery in rat models by facilitating faster wound contraction and promoting tissue repair in a shorter period as new growth tissue was visible at the peripheries of the abrasion observed from Day 3, hydroxyproline levels on day 21 was significantly higher than day 6 and day 15 (P > 0.05), and the reading of tumor growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) through quantitative-polymerase chain reaction results on day 6 further revealed upregulation particularly in the 1000 mg/mL hydrogel group, reflecting active fibroblast function and tissue repair. TGF-β1 signaling is involved in key wound healing processes and could facilitate wound recovery observed with the MS-NLC hydrogel film, supporting its potential as a novel wound healing approach. However, the absence of a vehicle control limits the ability to attribute the observed effects solely to the active extract.

INTRODUCTION

The skin, which resides at the outermost layer of a living organism, serves as a barrier against pathogens and supports thermoregulation.[1] Its function can be impaired by trauma, burns, infections, or chronic conditions, with wound healing outcomes depending on factors such as wound type, severity, exudate, and tissue damage.[234] Current research is focused on developing more effective wound treatments that will reduce hospital expenses while offering long-term comfort, as wound management remains a concern.[3]

Southeast Asia has long used the medicinal plant Mitragyna speciosa (MS) or also known as kratom, for conditions such as fever, diarrhea, diabetes, wounds, and fatigue.[56] Although the plant has been traditionally used for generations, experimental evidence validating its pharmacological activity is still insufficient.[7] Furthermore, the active constituents present in M. speciosa leaves exhibit limited biocompatibility and restricted penetration into the skin tissues, potentially reducing their therapeutic effectiveness.[8] To address this challenges, nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) have attracted attention as a promising approach to enhance M. speciosa bioavailability due to their ability to reduce particle size and improve dermal absorption. Hydrogel-based formulations additionally offer a favorable moist environment and structural support resembling the extracellular matrix, thereby facilitating tissue regeneration during wound repair.[4] This study is the first to explore MS leaf extract–encapsulated NLC (MS-NLC) in a hydrogel film for wound healing, with potential for in vivo evaluation.

Sprague Dawley rats were chosen as the animal model due to ease of handling, cost-effectiveness, and availability of research reagents.[9] They also provide a wound environment that closely resembles human biological processes, making them more suitable than mice.[10] This study, therefore, assesses the effectiveness of MS-loaded NLC in hydrogel films for therapeutic intervention for wounds.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

The Mitragyna speciosa-encapsulated NLCs (MS-NLC) were prepared using the method described by Syed Azhar et al.[8] The hydrogel film was prepared using sodium alginate (Marugo Corporation Co., Ltd., Saitama, Japan), glycerol (Kenei Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan), water (Milli-Q, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), and calcium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, United States of America). Xylazine (Macklin), meloxicam (Macklin), neutral buffered formalin 10% (Chemiz), and hydroxyproline detection kit BC0255 (Solarbio) were purchased from Scienfield Expertise PLT, Shah Alam, Selangor. Ketamine was purchased from Unit Perubatan Komparatif dan Teknologi (COMeT) UPM.

Plant material preparation

The MS plant was authenticated (voucher specimen MF 0121/19, UPM Herbarium), washed, cut, freeze-dried, and ground into a fine powder for extraction.[8]

Extraction

The standardized M. speciosa extract (50 g) was prepared by maceration in methanol (1:100) for 72 h, filtered, concentrated at 40°C, and stored refrigerated until use.

Nanostructured lipid carrier hydrogel film loaded with Mitragyna speciosa extract

The MS-NLC formulation was prepared using a high-energy method and optimized through response surface methodology from a previous study by Syed Azhar et al.[8] that is suitable for drug delivery systems (TS2022112902).

Experimental animals

Male Sprague Dawley (n = 36) rats (weight 180 g to 220 g) aged 8–10 weeks old were divided into six groups (n = 6) and housed individually in the standard polypropylene cages. The animals were kept under controlled conditions in a laboratory, within the animal facility at the Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences, UPM. They were provided unrestricted access to a standard commercial diet, clean water (ad libitum), and proper ventilation. All experiments were performed in accordance with the ethical standards and approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee UPM (UPM/IACUC/AUP-R042/2023) and complied with the Animal Research: Reporting of In vivo Experiments and Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development principles.

Wound creation

Animals were weighed and anesthetized before creating full-thickness dorsal wounds (1 cm × 1 cm) with a 10 mm biopsy punch. Rats were assigned to six groups (n = 6) (control, MS-crude extract, MS-NLC, MS-NLC hydrogel 250 mg/mL, MS-NLC hydrogel 500 mg/mL, and MS-NLC hydrogel 1000 mg/mL), with wounds cleaned daily and treatments applied evenly. A vehicle control group (blank hydrogel or blank NLC hydrogel without active extract) was not included in the present study. Therefore, the independent effects of the delivery system on wound healing outcomes could not be evaluated. Analgesic drug, buprenorphine 0.05 mg/kg, were given subcutaneously after wound excision. Wound healing and skin irritation were monitored and photographed, whereas body weight was measured on day 6, day 15, and day 21, with epithelialization defined as complete eschar detachment. Wound tissues with surrounding skin were collected and photographed for biochemical analysis (stored at −80°C) and histopathology (fixed in 10% formalin).

Histology evaluation

After euthanasia, wound samples were collected, preserved in 10% buffered formalin, and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 3 μm thickness collected on days 6, 15, and 21 were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome (MT) staining for wound healing assessment at ×100.

Hydroxyproline assay

Tissue hydroxyproline levels on day 6, day 15, and day 21 were determined using a commercial kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were hydrolyzed and processed with the kit reagents, and a microplate reader was used to measure absorbance at 560 nm.

Ribonucleic acid extraction and quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis

Ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted from wound tissues on day 6 and day 15 using Trizol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and quantified by NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was then generated from 1 μg of RNA using the HiScript ΙΙΙ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, China, Cat: R312). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using Taq Pro Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, China, Cat: Q712) with primers specific for tumor growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1), and GAPDH was used as the internal control. Custom primers for TGF-β1 (F5’-CTGAACCAAGGAG ACGGAATAC-3’, R5’-AACCCAGGTC CTTCCTAAAGTC-3’) and GAPDH (F5’-CAAAGCCAGAG TCCTTCAGA-3’, R5’ R5’-GATGGTCTTG GTCCTTAGCC-3’) were designed based on previous studies[1112] and obtained from Apical Scientific Sdn. Bhd, Selangor. Amplification was conducted for 40 cycles (95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 30 s) using the Vazyme FMR3 real-time PCR system and was validated by melting curve analysis followed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and the gene expression levels were determined using the standard-curve method.

Statistical analysis

Prior to statistical analysis, the dataset was examined for normal distribution. The wound healing profile was then analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by multiple comparison test (Tukey’s post hoc test for hydroxyproline assay content and Sidak’s post hoc for qPCR expression). Differences were considered statistically significant when P ≤ 0.05 compared with the control group.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Phase of wound contraction and skin cell regeneration

This research demonstrated that M. speciosa formulations accelerated wound contraction compared to the untreated control. Treatment wounds remained clean without exudate, and new tissue growth was observed from day 3, whereas it was delayed in the control group. By day 9 to day 15, MS-NLC hydrogel-treated wounds displayed proper epithelial renewal and dermal fibrosis [Figure 1].

The animal received a daily treatment including MS crude extract, MS-NLC formulation, and MS-NLC hydrogels. The control rats were left untreated. Previous in vitro studies by Zakaria et al.[13] showed that M. speciosa promotes cell proliferation, migration, and development of new blood vessels in 3T3 fibroblasts and human umbilical vein endothelial cells, indicating its potential for wound management. The antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties[1415] and hydrogel[16] further support healing by mitigating harmful factors such as free radicals, cytokines, and chemokines.

A limitation of this study is the absence of a vehicle control, as previous unpublished observations suggested no distinct effect compared to the control, making it difficult to distinguish the effects of the extract from the formulation, as hydrogels and NLCs may also facilitate wound healing. Overall, MS-based formulations enhanced tissue repair across all concentrations, leading to rapid skin maturation, remodeling, and contraction compared to control [Figure 1].

Histology evaluation

Microscopic wound healing examines the cellular and molecular processes that occur beneath the skin during tissue repair, including cell migration, proliferation, collagen formation, and angiogenesis. By observing how fibroblasts, keratinocytes, and immune cells interact to rebuild tissue structure, researchers gain valuable insight into how various treatments can enhance the speed and quality of wound healing.[17]

Through HE stain and MT, structured and efficient wound repair mechanisms were noted after administering the M. speciosa formulations. MS-NLC and MS-NLC hydrogel enhanced wound repair, showing moderate granulation tissue, fibroblasts, and more organized collagen compared to controls. Higher inflammatory infiltrates were noted in the 500 mg/mL and 1000 mg/mL hydrogel groups, while the control and MS crude extract groups had minimal granulation and disorganized collagen [Figure 2].

In addition to supporting MS healing properties, research conducted by Syaik Mossadeq et al.[18] demonstrated a decrease in carrageenan-induced paw swelling and a notable decline in the formation of granulomatous tissue in rats, indicating that the anti-inflammatory effects of MS could stem from a blend of reduced release of pro-inflammatory mediators and vascular permeability, along with bolstered immunity, encouragement of tissue regeneration, and healing mechanisms.

The histological findings affirm that MS-NLC hydrogels enhanced the rate and quality of wound healing compared to the control and crude extract treatments. The combined benefits of the hydrogel and nanostructured lipid systems created an optimal wound environment that facilitated rapid granulation, balanced inflammation, and efficient collagen remodeling. As a result, well-organized and mature tissue formed with minimal scarring by day 15.

Hydroxyproline assay content

Hydroxyproline, an indicator of collagen synthesized by fibroblasts in granulation tissue,[19] was measured to assess wound healing. Tissue samples were collected on days 6, 15, and 21 postexcisions for hydroxyproline assay. From day 6 to day 15, hydroxyproline levels increased slightly in the control, MS crude extract, and MS-NLC groups, while a larger difference was observed in the 250–1000 mg/mL MS-NLC hydrogel groups [Figure 3]. Tukey’s post hoc analysis revealed that hydroxyproline levels increased significantly over time. Day 15 was significantly higher than day 6 (P = 0.0227), day 21 was significantly higher than day 6 (P = 0.0001), and day 21 was also significantly higher than day 15 (P = 0.0117).

The tissues were collected on days 6, 15, and 21 postexcisions for hydroxyproline assay content. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (n = 6) analyzed by two-way analysis of variance using Tukey’s post hoc test

Hydroxyproline is a key biochemical marker for collagen content and, by extension, a reliable indicator of wound healing progression, as collagen synthesis is essential for tissue strength and structural integrity during repair. Additionally, the greater amount of hydroxyproline, the quicker healing process of the wound as biochemical assessments revealed elevated hydroxyproline levels, reflecting enhanced cellular growth and consequently greater collagen production.[20] Overall, these findings demonstrate that MS-NLC hydrogel treatments enhanced collagen synthesis and deposition compared to both the nontreated wound and nonhydrogel formulations. The results are consistent with the histological and macroscopic findings, collectively confirming the potential of MS-NLC hydrogels as an advanced wound healing system that accelerates tissue repair through enhanced collagen metabolism.

Tumor growth factor-beta 1 gene expression

QPCR analysis was conducted to quantify the messenger RNA (mRNA) profiles of TGF-β1 [Figure 4]. A two-way ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between row and column factors, F (3, 14) = 4.739, P = 0.0175, with no significant main effects of row factor, F (1, 14) = 1.058, P = 0.3212, or column factor, F (3, 14) = 2.907, P = 0.0717. Post hoc multiple comparisons using the Šídák correction for group treatment comparisons indicated that at the day 6 point, the 1000 mg/mL MS-NLC hydrogel group differed significantly from both the crude extract group (mean difference = −2.014, 95% confidence interval [CI] [−3.537, −0.4901], P < 0.05) and the MS-NLC group (mean difference = −2.086, 95% CI [−3.609, −0.5622], P < 0.05). No other pairwise comparisons were statistically significant (all P > 0.05).

Representative quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis of TGF-β1 messenger ribonucleic acid in rats’ wound tissue for the untreated group (control) and treated groups (MS crude extract, MS-NLC, and 1000 mg/mL MS-NLC hydrogel) on days 6 and 15. GAPDH was co-amplified as an internal standard, and the values were normalized to GAPDH expression. Values are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (n = 6) and analyzed by two-way analysis of variance using Sidak’s post hoc test

The analysis showed that TGF-β1 mRNA expression was upregulated earlier in the 1000 mg/mL MS-NLC hydrogel group by day 6 postwounding with a ΔΔCt = −6.54, corresponding to approximately 92.8 times higher expression compared with the untreated control group. However, the expression level decreased by day 15 (ΔΔCt = −1.16), corresponding to approximately a 2.25-fold increase relative to the control. This indicates that the 1000 mg/mL MS-NLC hydrogel induced an early but transient upregulation of TGF-β1, which subsided as the wound healing process progressed compared to the control, MS crude extract, and MS-NLC groups, which showed delayed increases by day 15.

Epidermally, TGF-β1 is mainly localized in the stratum granulosum and corneum, whereas TGF-β2 and TGF-β3 are found in suprabasal layers, indicating distinct roles.[2122] The early rise in TGF-β1 reflects its central role in wound repair, including inflammatory responses, granulation tissue development, fibroblast differentiation, development of new blood vessels, and keratinocyte migration.[2123]

To illustrate, the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial activities of M. speciosa are key to promoting wound healing.[13] NLCs enhance its efficacy by delivering M. speciosa compounds as nanosized particles to the wound site, while hydrogels provide a moist, bacteria-free environment that supports tissue repair.[24]

CONCLUSION

In this study, MS-NLC functioned as an efficient topical delivery system with notable wound healing activity in a rat model. Application of MS-NLC hydrogel treatment improved wound contraction, promoted collagen-related hydroxyproline accumulation, and facilitated tissue regeneration. These effects may be partly mediated through TGF-β1 modulation, suggesting that MS-NLC hydrogel could serve as a potential novel agent for wound healing. However, the lack of vehicle control limits the ability to attribute these effects specifically to the active extract.

Therefore, while the formulations show promise, further well-controlled studies incorporating appropriate vehicle controls are necessary to confirm the specific role and efficacy of the active compound in wound healing suggesting its potential as a wound healing agent, although further validation is required.
