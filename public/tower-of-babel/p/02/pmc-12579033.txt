Evidence-Based Guidelines for Intravitreal Anti-VEGF Therapy for Diabetic Retinopathy in Chronic Kidney Disease
Authors: Sourabh Sharma, Pradeep Venkatesh, Sanjay Kalra, Himanshu Verma, Nitin Kapoor, Lakshmi Nagendra, A G Unnikrishnan, Sudeep Prakash, Manisha Sahay, Maya Hada, Devesh Kumawat, Sree Bhushan Raju, Radhika Tandon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntravitreal therapy using anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) has transformed the approach to treating diabetic retinopathy (DR), but its application in individuals with diabetes and chronic kidney disease, the majority of whom have diabetic kidney disease, involves a complex evaluation of risks and benefits. Emerging evidence indicates that the systemic absorption of anti-VEGF agents administered intravitreally may lower circulating VEGF levels and negatively impact the renal endothelium, especially in individuals with pre-existing kidney issues. In this article, we showcase an evidence-based framework for the safe administration of anti-VEGF therapy in patients with DR accompanied by chronic kidney disease. We introduce a novel renoretinal syndrome, which we refer to as anti-VEGF-associated nephropathy (AVAN), and outline diagnostic criteria, severity classifications, biopsy indications, and treatment recommendations to guide clinical practice.Keywords: Anti-VEGF therapy, Chronic kidney disease, Diabetic retinopathy, Diabetic kidney disease, Aflibercept, Bevacizumab, Brolucizumab, Ranibizumab

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), diabetic kidney disease (DKD), and diabetic neuropathy are common microvascular complications of diabetes, often occurring together. The inhibition of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is fundamental in the management of DR, particularly in cases of diabetic macular oedema (DME) and proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) [1, 2]. High and/or dysregulated levels of VEGF are harmful when present in the retina; thus VEGF inhibition is needed for managing DR. However, VEGF plays an essential role in maintaining the integrity of glomerular endothelial cells via podocyte-endothelium crosstalk [3, 4], with VEGF isoforms VEGF121, VEGF165, VEGF189 and VEGF206 playing significant roles in kidney tissue survival and microvascular integrity. VEGF165, the most abundant and bioactive form of VEGF, maintains glomerular endothelial fenestration, supporting ultrafiltration. Podocytes and tubular epithelial cells constitutively produce VEGF, which acts in a paracrine fashion to preserve endothelial health, particularly during hypoxia or injury. Differential isoform binding to extracellular matrix components ensures spatially regulated VEGF activity that is crucial for both glomerular and peritubular capillary maintenance [3]. Studies have shown that systemic absorption of intravitreal anti-VEGF drugs—especially bevacizumab and aflibercept-occurs in both animal models and humans. This raises valid concerns regarding possible nephrotoxicity, particularly among vulnerable patient group with DKD [4–6].

Recent reports have documented instances of accelerated hypertension, increased proteinuria, a decline in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and biopsy-confirmed glomerular damage following anti-VEGF injections [4, 5]. This situation calls for the creation of a standardized clinical term and management approach. We suggest using the term AVAN (anti-VEGF associated nephropathy) for this renoretinal syndrome, and offer a detailed framework to assist clinicians in recognizing, categorizing and treating AVAN. Our aim in this article is to outline the diagnostic criteria, severity classifications, biopsy indications and treatment recommendations for AVAN to guide clinical practice.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Pharmacokinetics and Systemic Exposure

Intravitreal administration of anti-VEGF agents is the treatment of choice for DR [1]. Anti-VEGF medications, such as ranibizumab, aflibercept and bevacizumab, demonstrate considerable differences in their molecular structure, systemic exposure level and duration of VEGF suppression. Of these, ranibizumab, which is a 48-kDa Fab (fragment antigen-binding region) fragment, has the shortest systemic half-life (approx. 6 days) and results in minimal systemic VEGF suppression. In comparison, bevacizumab (149 kDa) and aflibercept (115 kDa) have longer systemic half-lives (around 18 days and 11 days, respectively) and have demonstrated the ability to suppress systemic VEGF for more than 4 weeks following injection [1, 4, 7, 8]. Systemic absorption of intravitreal anti-VEGF drugs was demonstrated by Avery et al. to be higher than that reported in the original US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) data [4, 7]. In these studies, after intravitreal injection, the systemic level of aflibercept remained higher than the 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) for up to 4 weeks, and that of bevacizumab closely matched the IC50, taking 2 weeks before starting to decrease [4, 7]. However, for ranibizumab, the systemic drug levels decreased quickly below IC50 within days, and this drug was eliminated more quickly than the other anti-VEGF agents [4, 7].

Renal Risks: Observational and Biopsy-Based Evidence

Evidence from real-world studies increasingly indicates that anti-VEGF treatment may not be harmless in CKD. In a retrospective analysis conducted by Rivero et al. [5], around 20% of patients undergoing intravitreal anti-VEGF treatment experienced > 25% drop in estimated GFR (eGFR) within 1 year, with 6.7% facing > 50% decline. Furthermore, 17.2% progressed to requiring dialysis within a 2-year timeframe [5]. These results are concerning and align with many case reports documenting new-onset nephrotic syndrome (minimal change disease [MCD], focal segmental glomerulosclerosis [FSGS] and membranous glomerulonephritis [MGN]) and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) [6, 9–20]. Bollee et al. [15] and Eremina et al. [16] recognized a direct link between anti-VEGF therapy and TMA. A meta-analysis by Wu et al. [14] highlighted a significantly increased risk of high-grade proteinuria and nephrotic syndrome among patients with cancer treated with bevacizumab. Several case-series [9, 12] have reported worsening proteinuria, increased blood pressure in patients with pre-existing CKD and even acute kidney injury (AKI) in some patients, particularly those with pre-existing CKD. De novo or relapsing glomerulonephritis has also been attributed to anti-VEGF therapy, which includes the relapse of MCD [10, 11] and membranous nephropathy [19]. Graft dysfunction, new or worsening proteinuria, and antibody-mediated rejection have been attributed to anti-VEGF therapy one case-series [13]. Studies by Haruhara et al. [17] and Izzedine H et al. [18] have further emphasized the histopathologic variety of AVAN ranging from podocytopathies to TMA.

Proteinuria is generally dose dependent. The underlying pathophysiological process is probably characterized by a reduction in systemic VEGF, resulting in injury to the glomerular endothelium, impairment of podocyte function and an increased risk of thrombotic microangiopathy; these are similar to the effects observed in patients with cancer receiving systemic anti-VEGF treatment [14, 15]. Furthermore, in patients with end-stage kidney disease undergoing dialysis, the fluid overload, along with existing hypertension, may increase patient morbidity. Studies have depicted age, proteinuria and pre-existing CKD as risk factors for AVAN [5], and animal research supports these observations. For example, Tschulakow et al. showed that aflibercept attaches to the glomerular endothelium and decreases endothelial fenestration [21]; in contrast, ranibizumab displayed minimal binding to the glomeruli in the same experimental set-up. Taken together, these results highlight the feasibility and biological foundation of AVAN.

Defining AVAN and Its Diagnostic Criteria

We characterize AVAN as the onset or worsening of kidney dysfunction that occurs between 2 and 8 weeks following the initiation of intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy, with no identifiable alternative causes. As outlined in Table 2, the diagnostic criteria of this novel reno-retinal syndrome necessitates fulfilment of all five criterion, namely: a clear temporal link; a measurable change in kidney function (either a drop in eGFR or an increase in proteinuria); rise in blood pressure (systolic and/or diastolic); elimination of other possible causes of AKI; and clinical improvement upon drug withdrawal or worsening upon rechallenge. By applying this systematic approach, we seek to facilitate consistent identification of AVAN in clinical practice [4–6]. An increase in blood pressure is so common with anti-VEGF therapy that it is often regarded as a surrogate marker of efficacy in certain cancers [22–26].

The pathology of AVAN is diverse, with different histological variants (see Table 3) TMA is the most prevalent variant and is usually associated with bevacizumab and aflibercept therapy. Cases of MCD, FSGS (including collapsing FSGS) and membranous nephropathy have also been documented [6, 9–20]. These patterns highlight the importance of VEGF in maintaining endothelial and podocyte stability, which is disturbed by extended systemic inhibition [3, 4]. As observed in various studies, bevacizumab has more harmful effect than ranibizumab, with larger randomized controlled trials recommended to provide evidence that supports this trend [3, 5]. Many authors believe that switching patients from bevacizumab and aflibercept to ranibizumab can decrease absorption and the risk of systemic side effects, including renal dysfunction [7, 21, 27, 28].

Mild AVAN can be managed through ongoing treatment and increased monitoring. For moderate AVAN, it is necessary to transition to an alternative therapy (e.g. ranibizumab), and incorporate renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) blockade. The management of severe AVAN necessitates stopping anti-VEGF therapy, conducting a biopsy, referral to a nephrologist and initiation of renal replacement therapy (RRT) in selected cases. Maintaining euvolemia is essential both for prevention and management. It is important to document the timing and dose of injection each time the agent is administered to determine the temporal relation of the therapy with renal dysfunction [4, 5]. Also, bilateral injections within a shorter period and repeated injections could be additional risk factors, and should be avoided at lower eGFR.

A renal biopsy is not always necessary, but it is critical in cases that are uncertain or severe. Biopsy is essential in cases of a swift decline in eGFR (eGFR drop > 30% in 1 month) to rule out TMA, the emergence of nephrotic-range proteinuria, microscopic hematuria and AKI with no clear alternative explanation. The biopsy facilitates determining the diagnosis and directing either immunosuppressive or supportive treatment [4–6].

Guideline for Anti-VEGF Use in DKD

Clinical decision-making must consider renal function as a guiding principle. Table 5 provides an overview of preferred medications, monitoring intervals and dosage approaches. Ranibizumab is deemed less harmful in the early stages of CKD [5, 7, 21, 27, 28], while bevacizumab and aflibercept are advised for the treatment of advanced CKD stage. The recommendation for patients with eGFR < 30 ml/min is to administer single-eye injections at extended intervals alongside close renal monitoring. In patients on dialysis, any anti-VEGF agent can be administered as there is no residual renal function left to lose. It is also essential to ensure cross-talk between the ophthalmologist, nephrologist, endocrinologist and other members of the diabetes care team [4]. In addition, treatment should be individualized depending on affordability, accessibility, efficacy and safety assessment after discussion with patient.

Certain conditions warrant caution or temporary deferral of treatment. If severe hypertension is present, administration of treatment should be delayed until blood pressure is checked. In pregnancy, it is preferable to avoid anti-VEGF therapy. In patients with a history of recent stroke and those with coronary artery disease (CAD), ranibizumab should be the preferred agent at the lowest possible dosage, after obtaining informed consent. In patients with histological diagnosis of TMA, in cases with active TMA, anti-VEGF therapy should be stopped and further therapy be deferred. In patients with a history of TMA, after informed consent has been obtained, ranibizumab can be administered though intravitreal route with ongoing serial monitoring.

Conclusion

Intravitreal anti-VEGF agents play a crucial role in the management of DR, but their use comes with certain systemic risks. A significant but often overlooked nephrotoxic potential exists, particularly with aflibercept and bevacizumab in the context of CKD. It is vital to identify potential issues early using validated criteria, along with risk stratification and an interdisciplinary approach. For patients who demonstrate renal vulnerability, ranibizumab is the preferred therapeutic option. Additional prospective studies are necessary to enhance our understanding of the epidemiology and pathology of AVAN and the ways to mitigate it.
