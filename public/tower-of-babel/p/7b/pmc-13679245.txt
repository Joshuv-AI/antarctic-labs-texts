Hospital length of stay and cost associated with routine-care documentation of probable or suspected fungal infection among patients hospitalized with acute myeloid leukemia: a propensity score analysis
Authors: Chengming He, Xufeng Liu, Biqing Wang, Le Hu, Jingdong Zhang, Xianying Zeng, Bolin Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFungal infection remains a recurring concern during inpatient care for acute myeloid leukemia (AML), but many hospitals lack local estimates of the bed-day and cost burden associated with fungal-infection documentation. We estimated excess length of stay (LOS) and hospitalization cost associated with routine-care documented probable or suspected fungal infection (PFI) among AML inpatient encounters.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective encounter-level study using routinely collected 2020–2025 AML inpatient data from the electronic medical record database of Ganzhou People’s Hospital. PFI was identified from prespecified fungal-infection terms in diagnosis text and microbiology fields (excluding records with an explicitly negative fungal result) to define routine-care documented PFI. Outcomes were LOS and total hospitalization cost in Chinese yuan (Renminbi; RMB, also CNY). The primary estimand was the average treatment effect among the treated (ATT). We fitted crude, propensity score matching, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), and doubly robust models. Sensitivity analyses evaluated alternative weighting and matching choices, robust uncertainty, PFI documentation source, patient-level clustering, cost missingness, and the feasibility of an early-PFI definition.ResultsThe analytic cohort comprised 2,686 AML inpatient encounters; 661 (24.6%) were classified as PFI and 2,025 as non-PFI. Median LOS was 18.97 days in the PFI group and 7.06 days in the non-PFI group. Among encounters with observed cost, median hospitalization cost was 24,888.24 RMB in the PFI group and 7,681.44 RMB in the non-PFI group. In the primary IPTW-ATT analysis, PFI was associated with 10.17 excess hospital days (95% CI, 8.49 to 11.85) and 20,542.01 RMB excess hospitalization cost (95% CI, 16,361.90 to 24,722.11). Doubly robust ATT estimates were similar: 10.21 days for LOS and 20,613.70 RMB for cost. Heteroskedasticity-robust (non-clustered) standard errors yielded directionally consistent intervals for IPTW-ATT: LOS 10.17 days (95% CI, 8.81 to 11.53) and cost 20,542 RMB (95% CI, 17,205 to 23,879). Cost was missing in 506 encounters (18.8%); cost-missing encounters differed modestly from cost-observed encounters.ConclusionsIn this single-hospital AML inpatient dataset, documented PFI was associated with a higher-burden hospital course, including longer LOS and higher observed cost.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14059-y.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is an aggressive hematologic malignancy in which diagnosis, supportive care, and treatment decisions often proceed rapidly and in parallel [1, 2]. Infection is a major reason why AML admissions become prolonged or clinically unstable, particularly during intensive therapy, prolonged neutropenia, mucosal injury, broad-spectrum antibacterial exposure, and repeated hospitalizations [3–5]. Fungal infection is especially challenging because early manifestations are often nonspecific, microbiological confirmation may lag behind treatment decisions, and antifungal therapy adds costs, toxicity monitoring, and drug-interaction concerns.

Invasive fungal disease (IFD) and probable fungal infection are well-recognized complications of hematologic malignancy. Registry and AML-specific studies suggest that AML accounts for a substantial share of proven or probable invasive fungal infections in hematology populations and that fungal infections remain clinically relevant despite prophylaxis and evolving treatment patterns [6–10].

Case definition is central to interpreting this burden. European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer/Mycoses Study Group (EORTC/MSG) and the later EORTC/Mycoses Study Group Education and Research Consortium (EORTC/MSGERC) criteria were developed to standardize research classification of proven, probable, and possible IFD [11–14]. Routine electronic health records, however, rarely contain all components of these criteria in directly analyzable fields. Diagnosis text, microbiology entries, medication orders, and billing variables are often more accessible than imaging interpretation, host-factor adjudication, or formal expert review. Hospital-record studies therefore need to state clearly what the exposure represents. Documentation- and coding-based case ascertainment can also have limited accuracy: administrative or coding data have shown positive predictive values of approximately 30% for invasive aspergillosis [15], 60% for invasive fungal infection in paediatric cancer [16], and 20% for healthcare-associated infection more broadly [17]. In this study, PFI denotes probable or suspected fungal infection documented during routine care, not proven IFD.

The clinical and economic burden of fungal disease is substantial, with hospitalization being a major driver of direct medical costs [18–21]. Local health-care systems also differ in diagnostic pathways, antifungal prescribing, reimbursement structures, bed management, and discharge practices. For that reason, burden estimates from one country, institution, or payer system may not translate directly to another setting.

For hospital teams, the practical question is not only whether fungal infection is severe, but also how much inpatient burden is associated with a fungal-infection care episode once it appears in the record. Such estimates are relevant to infection prevention, diagnostic stewardship, antifungal stewardship, bed planning, and future cost-effectiveness work. A crude comparison of PFI and non-PFI admissions would be difficult to interpret because encounters with PFI may already be clinically more complex, include more diagnoses or baseline infection, involve more intensive therapy, or simply provide more time for testing and documentation. Propensity score methods can address measured imbalance, although they do not remove unmeasured confounding or exposure misclassification [22–26].

We therefore examined AML inpatient encounters to estimate the LOS and cost burden associated with documented PFI. The primary estimand was the ATT because the main clinical question concerned encounters in which PFI had been recorded. Propensity score matching, IPTW, doubly robust modeling, and sensitivity analyses were used, with reporting guided by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) principles [27, 28].

Methods

Study design, data source, and participants

This retrospective hospitalization-level study used routinely collected 2020–2025 AML inpatient data from the electronic medical record database of Ganzhou People’s Hospital. The encounter, rather than the patient, was the unit of analysis. Eligible records were inpatient encounters among patients with AML during the study period. After eligibility criteria were applied and non-inpatient encounters were excluded, the analytic dataset included 2,686 encounters from 702 unique patients; 381 patients contributed more than one encounter. PFI status was available for all included encounters; length of stay was available for 2,684 encounters and observed cost for 2,180 encounters. Cohort construction and analytic exclusions are summarized in Fig. 1A.

The study aimed to quantify the hospitalization burden associated with PFI. Because the data came from routine care, the estimates represent observational associations rather than trial effects or registry-adjudicated fungal-disease outcomes. Analyses were performed in R. MatchIt was used for propensity score matching, cobalt for covariate balance assessment, tidyverse for data management, and ggplot2 for figure generation. Weighted regression and robust variance estimation were implemented within the R modeling framework [29–33].

Exposure definition

PFI was defined as a binary exposure based on prespecified fungal-infection terms in microbiology records and diagnosis text. The term set included fungal infection expressions and common fungal taxa in relevant Chinese and English fields. Encounters with a relevant term in either diagnosis text or microbiology records were classified as PFI; all other encounters were classified as non-PFI. Microbiology records expressing an explicitly negative fungal result (for example, “no fungal growth”, “no fungus detected”, or a fungal result reported as negative) were not treated as PFI-supporting evidence; accordingly, encounters whose only fungal-related record was such an explicitly negative result were classified as non-PFI (262 encounters).

Operationally, this definition captured probable or suspected fungal infection as documented during routine hospital care. It was not equivalent to a strict EORTC/MSGERC proven or probable IFD definition because the available structured data did not include complete adjudicated host, imaging, and mycological criteria. Throughout the manuscript, “PFI” is used as shorthand for this routine-care documented phenotype rather than as evidence of adjudicated invasive fungal disease [11–14].

PFI documentation could occur at different points during an admission and was not linked to a validated infection-onset date. The analyses therefore estimate the association between PFI documentation during hospitalization and LOS or observed cost, not the time-dependent effect of newly acquired fungal infection after admission.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were LOS and total hospitalization cost. LOS was calculated from hospitalization start and end timestamps. By prespecified rule, LOS was set to missing where it was negative or exceeded 365 days; no encounter met either condition in this cohort, and LOS was non-computable in 2 encounters that lacked a discharge timestamp. Total hospitalization cost was extracted from billing and hospital-discharge record fields and summarized at the encounter level; it was available for 2,180 of 2,686 encounters (506 [18.8%] missing), so cost analyses are complete-case on observed cost. Costs are reported in original Renminbi (RMB, also CNY) values without currency conversion.

Because cost data were incomplete and related to measured encounter characteristics, all cost analyses were interpreted as observed-cost complete-case associations. These models were not intended to estimate an unbiased full-cohort cost burden for encounters without recorded cost.

Covariates

Covariates considered for adjustment included age, sex, calendar year, a diagnosis-count proxy for comorbidity burden, a baseline documented-diagnosis indicator, AML risk category, intensive care unit (ICU) indicator, neutropenia indicator, treatment intensity, and baseline non-fungal infection status. Age was extracted from encounter-level age when available. Sex was standardized as male, female, or unknown. Calendar year was derived from the inpatient start time. The comorbidity proxy was the number of distinct diagnosis names recorded in the structured diagnosis field on the admission day, excluding fungal-infection diagnoses so that the count could not encode the exposure itself (a baseline, pre-exposure count); it should not be interpreted as a validated Charlson Comorbidity Index. The baseline documented-diagnosis indicator recorded whether any diagnosis was present in the structured field at admission. Baseline non-fungal infection was defined from admission-day diagnosis text containing infection, pneumonia, or sepsis terms, excluding fungal-infection terms so that this covariate could not encode the exposure. Neutropenia was defined as an absolute neutrophil count below 0.5 × 109/L (500/µL). Treatment intensity was identified from medication names indicating intensive or antileukemic therapy (the medication-name term list is provided in Supplementary Table S10). ICU care was identified from admission or discharge department text indicating ICU or critical-care service.

The primary propensity score model included variables that could plausibly be considered baseline or pre-exposure in the available dataset: age, sex, calendar year, the diagnosis-count proxy, a baseline documented-diagnosis indicator, and baseline non-fungal infection status. ICU care, neutropenia, treatment intensity, and AML risk were not included in the primary propensity score model because they either showed insufficient variation in the analytic dataset or could lie on the pathway between AML course, fungal infection recognition, and inpatient burden.

Propensity score and outcome analyses

The primary estimand was the ATT. This estimand was selected because the clinically relevant question was the excess burden among encounters classified as PFI, rather than the average effect of a hypothetical exposure change across all AML hospitalizations. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression. We performed 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching without replacement using an ATT estimand and a logit-propensity caliper. IPTW analyses were also performed for ATT and ATE, with weights trimmed at the 1st and 99th percentiles.

Outcome models estimated absolute differences in LOS and cost. We fitted crude models, PSM-based ATT models, IPTW-ATT models, IPTW-ATE models for LOS, and doubly robust ATT models. The doubly robust ATT model combined IPTW-ATT weighting with adjustment for the propensity score covariates. Because hospitalization cost was right-skewed, cost was also evaluated using a Gamma model with a log link as a relative-scale sensitivity analysis.

Balance was assessed using standardized mean differences before and after propensity score adjustment. Sensitivity analyses evaluated alternative matching and weighting choices, extreme-value handling, log-transformed LOS, bootstrap uncertainty, heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, patient-level clustering, and a first-admission-only cohort [34, 35].

Supplementary analyses

To characterize the impact of missing cost data, we compared cost-observed and cost-missing encounters across measured demographic and clinical variables. We also attempted a cost-observation model, but separation limited its interpretability.

We also assessed whether PFI documentation could be restricted to early hospitalization. Diagnosis start dates and microbiology check times were compared with admission start time. A fungal-related record within 48 h after admission was considered an exploratory early-PFI marker. This analysis was treated as feasibility evidence because routine text-based event times may not reliably represent infection onset.

To examine whether the findings depended on the record source used to define PFI, we constructed diagnosis-source and microbiology-source PFI indicators and repeated IPTW-ATT analyses using the same measured covariate set. These analyses were considered supportive because diagnosis and microbiology fields capture different aspects of clinical documentation and testing practice.

Results

Cohort characteristics

The analytic cohort included 2,686 AML inpatient encounters from 702 unique patients (Fig. 1A; Table 1). Overall, 661 encounters (24.6%) were classified as PFI and 2,025 as non-PFI. PFI documentation sources are summarized in Fig. 1B, and the observed and missing cost structure is shown in Fig. 1C. Median age was 55 years (IQR, 42 to 66), and 1,394 encounters (51.9%) were male. Baseline differences between PFI and non-PFI encounters were substantial for several clinical variables: the PFI group had higher baseline non-fungal infection prevalence, higher neutropenia prevalence, and a higher frequency of intensive or antileukemic treatment. These imbalances supported the need for adjusted analyses.

Baseline imbalance was most pronounced for calendar year and baseline non-fungal infection status. Propensity score overlap between PFI and non-PFI encounters was acceptable for the primary weighted analysis (Fig. 2A). IPTW-ATT improved balance across the included covariates, with adjusted standardized differences below conventional thresholds (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S1). Primary LOS analysis

In the crude analysis, PFI was associated with a 10.43-day longer LOS (95% CI, 8.73 to 12.13; Table 2). After propensity score matching, the estimated ATT was 9.61 days (95% CI, 7.84 to 11.37). In the primary IPTW-ATT analysis, PFI was associated with 10.17 excess hospital days (95% CI, 8.49 to 11.85). The IPTW-ATE estimate was 10.92 days (95% CI, 9.21 to 12.63; Supplementary Table S2), but the ATE analysis was considered supplementary because balance was less favorable for some covariates and because ATT better matched the study question.

The doubly robust ATT model estimated 10.21 excess days (95% CI, 8.67 to 11.75), closely aligning with the primary IPTW-ATT estimate (Table 2). The heteroskedasticity-robust standard error analysis produced a consistent IPTW-ATT interval: 10.17 days (95% CI, 8.81 to 11.53). Alternative caliper matching estimates ranged from 9.54 to 9.61 days, stabilized ATT weighting estimated 10.19 days, and exclusion of LOS values above the 99th percentile yielded an estimate of 8.83 days. The log(LOS + 1) model also showed a positive association. The consistency of LOS estimates across primary and sensitivity analyses is shown in Fig. 3C and Supplementary Table S2.

Primary cost analysis

Cost was observed in 2,180 encounters and missing in 506 encounters (18.8%). In complete-case crude analysis, PFI was associated with 21,497.46 RMB higher hospitalization cost (95% CI, 17,316.79 to 25,678.13; Table 2). The PSM-ATT estimate was 20,362.03 RMB (95% CI, 16,101.41 to 24,622.66). In the primary IPTW-ATT analysis, PFI was associated with 20,542.01 RMB excess hospitalization cost (95% CI, 16,361.90 to 24,722.11). The doubly robust ATT estimate was similar at 20,613.70 RMB (95% CI, 16,704.11 to 24,523.28).

The heteroskedasticity-robust standard error analysis supported the same direction: IPTW-ATT 20,542 RMB (95% CI, 17,205 to 23,879). Alternative caliper matching estimates ranged from 20,119 to 20,362 RMB. Stabilized ATT weighting estimated 20,578 RMB. Excluding cost values above the 99th percentile reduced the estimate to 17,061 RMB but did not eliminate the positive association. The Gamma log model yielded a positive relative-scale association, consistent with higher cost among PFI encounters (Table 2). The corresponding method-consistency plot for observed cost is shown in Fig. 3D.

Cost missingness and early-PFI feasibility

Cost-missing encounters differed from cost-observed encounters (Supplementary Table S3). The PFI proportion was 20.8% among cost-missing encounters and 25.5% among cost-observed encounters. Cost-missing encounters occurred earlier in calendar time, had a slightly higher median LOS, and had a lower median diagnosis-count proxy. Neutropenia was also more frequent among cost-missing encounters. A logistic cost-observation model showed near-separation, indicating that cost observation was strongly related to calendar year and other measured features. For this reason, cost estimates are best interpreted as observed-cost complete-case associations rather than full-cohort cost-burden estimates. Across the 2020 to 2025 study period, cohort-wide median LOS declined from 13.4 days in 2020 to 7.1 days in 2025, and median observed cost (recorded from 2022 onward) declined from 12,277 RMB in 2022 to 8,479 RMB in 2025 (Supplementary Table S7). Year-specific IPTW-ATT estimates were positive in every year but markedly smaller in 2025 (Supplementary Figure S3).

The early-PFI feasibility analysis showed that all 661 primary PFI encounters had documentation consistent with the PFI definition, but only 481 had time-stamped fungal-related records (Supplementary Table S4; Supplementary Figure S2). Among PFI encounters, 180 had no time-stamped fungal-related record. Only 48 encounters met the exploratory definition of fungal-related documentation within 48 h after admission. Adjusted early-PFI models were therefore underpowered and were not clinically interpretable as substitutes for the primary PFI definition. We retained timing uncertainty as a limitation and regard validated infection-onset timestamps as a priority for future datasets.

Source-specific PFI analyses yielded estimates in the same direction as the primary analysis (Supplementary Table S5; Supplementary Figure S1). Diagnosis-source PFI was associated with 10.45 excess LOS days (95% CI, 8.72 to 12.17) and 21,150 RMB higher observed cost (95% CI, 16,786 to 25,513). Microbiology-source PFI was associated with 10.57 excess LOS days (95% CI, 6.92 to 14.22) and 24,495 RMB higher observed cost (95% CI, 15,068 to 33,921). The two sources were consistent in direction with substantially overlapping confidence intervals; because the microbiology-only stratum was small (27 encounters), differences between the source-specific LOS and cost estimates reflect statistical variability rather than a substantive divergence.

Patient-level sensitivity analyses addressed the repeated-admission structure (Supplementary Table S6; Supplementary Figure S2). Cluster-robust standard errors by patient widened the intervals but did not change the primary interpretation: IPTW-ATT LOS 10.17 days (95% CI, 8.49 to 11.85) and observed cost 20,542.01 RMB (95% CI, 16,361.90 to 24,722.11). A first-admission-only sensitivity analysis also showed positive associations, although it had a different target population and a smaller sample.

The association was robust across alternative PFI phenotype definitions (Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

In this AML inpatient cohort from Ganzhou People’s Hospital, encounters with documented PFI had longer admissions and higher observed hospitalization costs than encounters without such documentation. In the primary ATT-weighted analysis, the adjusted differences were approximately 10.2 hospital days and 20,542 RMB. Similar estimates were observed across planned sensitivity analyses. The most direct interpretation is that documented PFI marks a high-burden hospitalization phenotype in AML care. It does not establish the causal effect of newly acquired invasive fungal disease.

The association is clinically plausible. AML admissions in which clinicians suspect fungal infection often become more complex before microbiological proof is available. Marrow failure, intensive therapy, neutropenia, antibacterial exposure, and infection-prevention measures all shape infection risk in AML care [2–5]. Once fungal disease enters the differential diagnosis, the admission may involve repeated cultures or fungal biomarkers, chest or sinus imaging, empirical or targeted antifungal therapy, liver and renal toxicity monitoring, drug-interaction review, and coordination between hematology and infectious diseases teams. Guidance from the European Conference on Infections in Leukaemia (ECIL) and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) reflects this diagnostic and therapeutic complexity for high-risk hematology patients with invasive aspergillosis, candidiasis, and related syndromes [36–40]. Mucormycosis guidance illustrates the same issue in suspected mold disease, where delays and uncertainty can quickly increase care intensity [41]. In routine practice, these steps can extend observation and increase cost even when fungal disease is never formally adjudicated. The exposure is therefore clinically meaningful as a recorded fungal-infection care episode.

These findings are broadly consistent with the existing literature. Sorveglianza Epidemiologica Infezioni nelle Emopatie (SEIFEM) registry studies identified AML as a major contributor to invasive fungal infections among hematologic malignancies and placed invasive aspergillosis in recognizable high-risk phases, including post-induction aplasia [6, 7]. AML-specific reports have also described fungal infections despite primary prophylaxis and during newer treatment contexts such as venetoclax-based or FLT3-targeted therapy [8–10]. Economic studies in hematology populations and broader population-level analyses have linked fungal disease and IFD with substantial direct cost and inpatient resource use [18–21, 42–44]. Our analysis adds a local, encounter-level estimate based on the type of hospital data available to clinical teams and administrators. Direct numerical comparisons across studies are limited by differences in case definitions, currencies, reimbursement rules, case mix, and cost availability, but the direction of the finding is consistent with prior work.

For hospital practice, the main implication is resource planning. An adjusted LOS difference of approximately 10 days is large enough to matter for hematology bed availability, isolation-room demand, diagnostic scheduling, and discharge coordination. An observed-cost difference of about 20,500 RMB may also inform local budget-impact discussions. The adjusted cost difference works out to roughly 2,020 RMB per additional hospital day, somewhat above the cohort’s median daily hospitalization cost of about 1,433 RMB. Longer admissions thus account for much of the added cost, although the higher per-day figure suggests that length of stay does not fully explain it. These estimates do not, by themselves, indicate whether broader antifungal use would reduce resource use. Prophylaxis and treatment studies support antifungal strategies in selected high-risk settings, but they also involve drug cost, monitoring, toxicity, and stewardship tradeoffs [45–48]. Expert discussions of empirical versus preemptive antifungal therapy and mucormycosis management similarly favor structured pathways over indiscriminate escalation [49, 50]. In practical terms, documented PFI can identify a subgroup in which targeted diagnostics, standardized imaging triggers, earlier infectious diseases consultation, and structured antifungal review deserve evaluation.

The analytic approach followed the clinical question and the data structure. We used the ATT estimand because the relevant burden question concerned encounters in which PFI was documented. The crude LOS and cost differences were larger than the adjusted estimates, consistent with baseline clinical differences between PFI and non-PFI encounters. Propensity score matching, weighting, and balance diagnostics can reduce measured imbalance, although unmeasured clinical factors may still affect exchangeability [22–25]. IPTW, marginal weighting, and doubly robust models therefore provide complementary views of the association rather than definitive causal evidence [26, 51, 52]. Source-specific and patient-level analyses supported the direction of the primary findings while clarifying the limits imposed by documentation source and repeated admissions.

Several limitations should be considered. PFI was identified from routine diagnosis and microbiology fields rather than adjudicated according to EORTC/MSGERC criteria, so exposure misclassification is possible [11–14]. The timing of fungal documentation was also incomplete. Longer admissions may create more opportunities for fungal testing and coding, while suspected fungal infection may itself delay discharge; the early-PFI analysis was too sparse to clarify this relationship. Cost was missing in 506 encounters, and the missing-cost group differed from encounters with recorded cost, so cost results should be read as observed-cost complete-case associations. Residual confounding may remain because detailed AML disease status, chemotherapy phase, neutropenia duration, antifungal prophylaxis, organism type, imaging findings, mortality, readmission, and drug-level costs were unavailable or incompletely structured. Finally, this was a single-system study, and absolute burden estimates may differ in hospitals with different AML pathways, diagnostic capacity, reimbursement rules, or discharge practices.

The main strengths of this study are the size of the AML inpatient dataset from Ganzhou People’s Hospital and the use of clear cohort and variable definitions. The analysis separated crude and adjusted estimates, used an ATT estimand aligned with the clinical burden question, and tested robustness through alternative propensity-score approaches, uncertainty analyses, source-specific PFI definitions, and patient-level sensitivity analyses. These features support its use as a local burden estimate. Prospective multicenter studies with adjudicated fungal definitions, validated onset timing, medication detail, and time-aware designs would provide a stronger basis for causal interpretation.

For clinical programs, these estimates may help guide measurement and pathway evaluation. Hospitals could use similar analyses to review fungal diagnostic workflows, estimate the bed-day impact of delayed documentation or delayed discharge, and design prospective studies that separate prevention, early diagnosis, treatment, and discharge planning. The present analysis identifies a burden signal; future interventional studies will be needed to determine which strategies can reduce it.

Conclusions

In this retrospective AML inpatient cohort, documented PFI was associated with longer LOS and higher observed hospitalization cost. The primary IPTW-ATT analysis estimated approximately 10.2 excess hospital days and 20,542 RMB higher observed cost among encounters classified as PFI. Consistent results across propensity score, doubly robust, source-specific, patient-level, and uncertainty analyses support documented PFI as a marker of inpatient resource burden in AML care. Multicenter studies with adjudicated fungal definitions, validated onset timing, detailed antifungal exposure, AML disease-risk variables, and longer-term outcomes are needed to test whether earlier diagnosis or targeted prevention can reduce avoidable burden.
