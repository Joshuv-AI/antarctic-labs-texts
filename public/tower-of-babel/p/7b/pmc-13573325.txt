Perceived reasons for violence toward family caregivers by relatives with schizophrenia in Eastern Turkey: a qualitative study
Authors: Sakine Aktas, Mehmet Cihad Aktas, Cemile Hurrem Ayhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious research has found that violence towards family caregivers caring for schizophrenia patients is common due to patient-family caregiver interactions. This study aimed to investigate family caregivers’ experiences of patient-initiated violence in schizophrenia and the perceived reasons for such violence.MethodsThe study was conducted using the phenomenological method, one of the qualitative research methods. The data was collected through individual in-depth interviews. The data collection ended with 12 participants. The data was analyzed using inductive qualitative content analysis to create themes and categories within the research. The confirmability, transferability, credibility and consistency of the study were checked.ResultsThe findings revealed that family caregivers’ experiences of patient-initiated violence were organized into four main themes: (1) limited illness insight and misattributions, (2) clinical symptoms and treatment non-adherence, (3) psychosocial vulnerability of the patient, and (4) family dynamics and interpersonal conflict. Caregivers often reported difficulty in identifying the causes of violence and, in some cases, attributed it to metaphysical beliefs such as the influence of jinn. Clinical factors, particularly non-adherence to treatment, hallucinations, and delusions, were perceived as major contributors to violent behavior. In addition, psychosocial vulnerabilities, including feelings of being misunderstood, loneliness, and imbalance in social life, were identified as triggers of aggression. Family-related factors, such as forcing treatment and interpersonal conflicts, were also reported to precipitate violent incidents. Overall, the findings indicate that caregivers interpret violence through a combination of clinical, cultural, and relational frameworks.ConclusionThis study provides comprehensive multidimensional insights into the causes, consequences and prevention of patient-related violence experienced by psychiatric nurses. The descriptions of nurses’ experiences of violence illustrate the severity of violence and its negative impact on nursing care. Patient-related violence can be minimized if psychiatric nurses receive psychological support to cope with the emotions caused by the violence, focus on and address the facility’s deficiencies and are trained in therapeutic interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05049-5.Keywords: Patient related violence, Psychiatric nursing, Psychiatric settings, Qualitative study, Workplace violence

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a mental disorder characterized by emotional, behavioral, and cognitive impairment, which significantly affects occupational, social, family and academic functioning. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 21 million people worldwide are affected by schizophrenia at any given time, and the lifetime risk of schizophrenia is estimated to be between 0.3% and 2.7% [1].

Schizophrenia is a complex and multifactorial psychiatric disorder influenced by genetic, neurobiological, and environmental factors. Recent research has demonstrated a rapid expansion in schizophrenia-related studies, particularly in the field of genetics, highlighting its polygenic nature and biological complexity [2]. These developments emphasize that schizophrenia cannot be explained by a single cause, but rather emerges from the interaction of multiple biological and psychosocial factors. Despite these advances, translating this growing body of knowledge into clinical practice and everyday caregiving contexts remains a significant challenge. In parallel with these developments, emerging approaches such as artificial intelligence have been proposed to enhance early detection, personalized treatment, and prevention of symptom exacerbation in mental disorders. These innovations offer promising opportunities to monitor symptoms and predict clinical deterioration [3]. However, despite technological advancements, many individuals with schizophrenia continue to experience delayed diagnosis, inadequate treatment adherence, and recurrent symptom exacerbations, which may contribute to negative outcomes for both patients and their families.

The violence experienced by patients diagnosed with schizophrenia, as reported by their family caregivers, is a multifaceted issue that stems from various psychological, social, and environmental factors. Understanding these reasons is crucial for developing effective interventions and support systems for both patients and their caregivers.

One of the primary reasons for violence in individuals with schizophrenia is the presence of comorbid conditions, particularly substance use disorders. Individuals with schizophrenia are not only more likely to engage in violent behavior compared to community controls but also exhibit higher rates of family violence incidents that come to police attention [4]. This suggests that the dynamics of violence often occur within familial settings, where caregivers may be directly impacted by the aggressive behaviors of their loved ones. Furthermore, cognitive deficits and symptom severity particularly paranoia are significant predictors of violent behavior, indicating that the cognitive impairments associated with schizophrenia can exacerbate the risk of violence, thereby affecting the quality of life for both patients and their caregivers [5]. Research indicates that caregivers often perceive aggressive behaviors in their care recipients as a direct consequence of persistent paranoia, which can lead to hostile actions [6]. Furthermore, cognitive impairments associated with schizophrenia can exacerbate the risk of violent behavior, as these deficits hinder the patient’s ability to process social cues and manage emotions effectively [5, 7]. The interplay of these symptoms creates a volatile environment where caregivers may feel threatened, leading to a cycle of fear and aggression.

Furthermore, neurobiological aspects of schizophrenia also play a significant role in violent behavior. Studies have shown that structural brain abnormalities, such as reduced hippocampal volumes, are associated with violent tendencies in individuals with schizophrenia [8]. These findings suggest that caregivers must navigate not only the behavioral manifestations of the disorder but also the underlying biological factors that contribute to violence. Understanding these neurobiological underpinnings can help caregivers develop more empathetic and informed approaches to managing violent behaviors.

The stigma surrounding mental illness plays a critical role in the dynamics of caregiver-patient relationships. Caregivers often experience societal stigma, which can heighten their stress and feelings of isolation [9, 10]. This stigma not only affects their mental health but also influences their perceptions of the patient’s behavior, potentially leading to increased tension and conflict within the household. As caregivers struggle with their own mental health issues, such as anxiety and depression, the likelihood of violent encounters may increase [11, 12]. The burden of caregiving, compounded by societal stigma, creates a challenging environment that can trigger violent incidents. Moreover, the historical context of violence in families with schizophrenia cannot be overlooked. Studies have shown that a significant percentage of individuals with schizophrenia have a history of violent behavior, which can perpetuate a cycle of violence within families [13]. This history often leads to heightened vigilance and fear among caregivers, who may anticipate aggressive outbursts based on past experiences. The fear of violence can create a self-fulfilling prophecy, where caregivers’ anxieties contribute to the escalation of conflicts [14].

In addition to these psychological and social factors, environmental influences also play a crucial role. Caregivers often report feeling overwhelmed by the demands of caregiving, particularly when patients exhibit severe symptoms that require constant supervision and intervention [15, 16]. This overwhelming burden can lead to frustration and, in some cases, reactive violence from caregivers who may feel powerless in managing their loved one’s condition. Furthermore, the lack of adequate support systems and resources for caregivers exacerbates their stress and can contribute to violent situations [17, 18].

The qualitative experiences of caregivers reveal a complex emotional landscape characterized by fear, anxiety, and a sense of responsibility for the safety of both the patient and themselves. It was found that even when patients are in a stable condition, caregivers often express concerns about potential violence, reflecting an ongoing fear of unpredictable behaviors [12]. This fear is compounded by societal perceptions of schizophrenia, which often paint individuals with the disorder as inherently dangerous. Social isolation and limited support systems can exacerbate the vulnerability of both patients and caregivers, potentially leading to violent situations [19]. In conclusion, the violence experienced by patients with schizophrenia, as reported by their family caregivers, is influenced by a complex interplay of symptom severity, stigma, historical patterns of violence, and environmental stressors. Addressing these factors through targeted interventions and support programs is essential for reducing the incidence of violence and improving the quality of life for both patients and caregivers. Moreover, the relationship between medication adherence and violence risk cannot be overlooked. Nonadherence to antipsychotic medication can significantly increase the risk of violent behavior in patients with schizophrenia [20]. This finding underscores the importance of ensuring that patients receive appropriate treatment and support, as caregivers often play a pivotal role in managing medication regimens. Caregiver burden in schizophrenia is widely conceptualized as a multidimensional construct comprising two core components: objective and subjective burden. Objective burden refers to the tangible demands of caregiving, including daily assistance, supervision, disruption of routines, financial strain, and reduced participation in social activities. In contrast, subjective burden reflects caregivers’ internal responses to these demands, encompassing emotional experiences such as stress, anxiety, worry, guilt, shame, frustration, and self-blame [21, 22].

Existing literature consistently identifies several key domains of caregiver burden, including emotional distress, financial difficulties, physical fatigue or health problems, social isolation and stigma, and strained family relationships. In addition, some studies highlight violence, discrimination, and the persistence of symptoms or relapse in individuals with schizophrenia as critical factors that further intensify caregiver burden [23]. Evidence from meta-analytic studies indicates that higher levels of burden are associated with greater symptom severity, functional impairment, and longer illness duration [24]. Overall, caregiving in schizophrenia involves not only the practical responsibilities of care but also the psychological consequences of living under continuous strain. This dual burden may contribute to feelings of helplessness among family members, particularly when they perceive that their relatives are not receiving adequate or effective treatment. This study aimed to explore the experiences of family caregivers of individuals with schizophrenia regarding violence perpetrated by their relatives and their views on the underlying causes of such violence.

Methods

Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) were used for reporting the current study [25].

Study design

The qualitative study was conducted using the phenomenological method, one of the research methods. The purpose of the phenomenological method, which was chosen because it is suitable for the nature of the research, is to understand the essence of the experiences of people who experience a certain phenomenon in depth [26, 27].

Participants

The sample consisted of 12 family members providing care for individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling, and data were collected through individual in-depth interviews. In qualitative research, data collection is typically guided by the principle of data saturation, defined as the point at which no new themes or insights relevant to the phenomenon under investigation emerge [28].

In this study, data saturation for female participants (FP) was reached at the fifth interview and confirmed in the sixth interview. Similarly, for male participants (MP), saturation was reached at the fifth interview and confirmed in the sixth interview. As participants’ responses began to repeat and no new themes were identified, data collection was concluded with a total of 12 participants.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) being aged 18 years or older; (b) being a family member responsible for the care of an individual with schizophrenia (including meeting daily needs, monitoring treatment, and organizing hospital appointments); (c) cohabiting with the patient for at least one year; (d) having experienced any form of violence perpetrated by the individual with schizophrenia; and (e) being willing to participate and able to communicate effectively.

Exclusion criteria included: (a) the presence of a neurocognitive disorder; (b) intellectual disability; and (c) any diagnosed psychiatric disorder.

Data generation

Data were collected using semi-structured, in-depth interviews. The semi-structured interview guide was developed by the second author (MCA) in consultation with academic scholars and clinicians specializing in psychiatric nursing, in line with the aims of the study. The final interview guide is presented in Appendix 1.

Data collection was conducted between March and July 2021 in Van, Turkey. Participants were identified from an existing database of family caregivers who had previously participated in studies conducted by the research team and had consented to be contacted for future research. Eligible individuals were contacted and those who agreed to participate were included in the study.

Prior to the main data collection, two pilot interviews were conducted with individuals who met the inclusion criteria but were not part of the final sample. These interviews informed minor revisions to the interview questions and interviewing approach.

All interviews were conducted by the second author (M.C.A) who has experience in qualitative research and psychiatric nursing. Interviews were conducted face-to-face in a private interview room within the hospital setting to ensure confidentiality and comfort. No one other than the participant and the researcher was present during the interviews.

Interviews were conducted face-to-face using a one-on-one format. Before each interview, participants were informed about the aim and procedures of the study, and written and verbal informed consent was obtained. Each interview lasted between 18 and 33 min.

With participants’ permission, all interviews were audio-recorded by the second author (MCA) using a digital voice recorder, and field notes were taken to capture non-verbal expressions and contextual observations. The recordings were transcribed verbatim. To enhance credibility, transcripts were returned to participants for member checking, allowing them to review, comment on, or revise their statements.

Research team and reflexivity

The research team consisted of three researchers with professional backgrounds in psychiatry and psychiatric nursing. One researcher is a faculty member and medical doctor working in the Department of Psychiatry at a Faculty of Medicine. The other two researchers are faculty members (PhD, Assistant Professors) in the Department of Mental Health and Psychiatric Nursing at a Faculty of Health Sciences. All researchers have prior clinical experience as nurses in hospital settings and have received formal training in qualitative research methods.

The researchers were aged between 37 and 40 years; two were female and one was male. The second author (MCA), who conducted the interviews, has extensive experience in psychiatric nursing and qualitative research, which facilitated effective communication with participants and in-depth exploration of their experiences.

The researchers had prior familiarity with some of the participants due to previous studies conducted in the same setting. This familiarity facilitated trust and openness during the interviews; however, the researchers remained aware of the potential for bias. To minimize this, reflexive practices were adopted throughout the research process, including maintaining awareness of preconceptions, engaging in regular discussions among the research team, and critically reflecting on how their professional backgrounds and prior relationships might influence data collection and interpretation.

Ethical issues

Ethics approval was granted from the Van Yuzuncu Yil University Non-invasive Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Protocol:2021/02–06 Date:12/02/2021). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment in the study. This research was conducted ethically in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

The principles of research trustworthiness were utilized [26]. Ensuring credibility involved cross-referencing the transcripts with the recordings, emailing the transcripts to participants for verification, and comparing themes across interviews. The transferability was improved by implementing well-defined procedures that allow for the replication of the approaches in different contexts and environments. The researcher exhibited dependability by engaging in reflective journaling to contemplate the intersubjectivity of their own self-awareness, situational awareness, motivation, and experience. The research demonstrates confirmability through the application of a qualitative, descriptive, inquiry technique, which is appropriate for studying lived experiences [30]. The research employs reflexive theme analysis and derives its results directly from the data (inductive) [29].

Results

Themes Emerged from Study

Theme 1: limited illness insight and misattributions

This theme encompasses caregivers’ limited understanding of schizophrenia and their tendency to attribute patient-initiated violence to non-medical explanations. It includes the sub-themes “not knowing the cause” and “metaphysical beliefs.”

Within the sub-theme of metaphysical beliefs, some participants attributed the violent behaviors of their relatives to supernatural causes, particularly the influence of jinn. Caregivers reported that patients acted violently under the control or direction of these entities. In contrast, under the sub-theme of not knowing the cause, some caregivers expressed uncertainty about the reasons for the violence and did not associate these behaviors with the illness itself. These accounts reflect a lack of illness insight and the presence of alternative explanatory models.

The statements of the participants are as follows:

“…I don’t know, to be honest, I don’t know why. He gets angry and attacks for no reason, and I don’t believe it has anything to do with the disease. Every person can be violent, and sometimes we use violence against others. So it’s not because of the illness…” (Code: MP-1).

“…. It’s definitely because of the jinn, I think the jinn made him do this. The hodja we went to said so anyway…” (Code: FP-3).

Theme 2: clinical symptoms and treatment non-adherence

This theme reflects caregivers’ recognition of the role of clinical symptoms and treatment-related factors in the emergence of patient-initiated violence. It comprises three sub-themes: “non-compliance with treatment,” “hallucinations,” and “delusions.”

Under the sub-theme of non-compliance with treatment, participants emphasized that discontinuation of medication and irregular attendance at follow-up appointments were associated with worsening symptoms and increased aggression. Caregivers reported that patients became more prone to violent behavior particularly during periods of treatment neglect.

The sub-theme of hallucinations includes accounts in which caregivers linked violent behavior to patients’ perceptual disturbances. Participants described situations in which patients acted violently in response to voices or visual experiences that they perceived as real.

Similarly, within the sub-theme of delusions, caregivers reported that paranoid and grandiose beliefs contributed to anger and subsequent violent behavior. Patients’ mistrust of others and distorted beliefs about being harmed or possessing special status were identified as triggers for aggression.

The statements of the participants are as follows:

“…When he doesn’t take his medication, his illness gets worse and he becomes more prone to violence. … Sometimes it’s time for a check-up, but he doesn’t go. He becomes very aggressive during these times. …” (Code: MP-2).

“Sometimes we take him to check-ups by force, we see very clearly that when he does not go to check-ups and does not use his medicine, the symptoms of the disease increase. He attacks us especially when he neglects his treatment. That is why we pay attention to his treatment as much as possible.” (Code: MP-5).

“… When we asked him why he behaved like this when he used violence before, one person told me to beat the person in front of me, the girl I saw kept saying ‘beat me’. So I think he did it because of his illness.” (Code: MP-3).

“… He had the idea that ‘I am a prophet’ and when we said the opposite, that it was not true, he said ‘you do not believe me, you do not trust me’ and turned to violence.” (Code: FP-2).

“… He does not trust anyone in eating, drinking, bathing or any other matter. He sees everyone as his enemy because of his illness. He thinks that they will poison him and that they are constantly following him. That is why he gets angry and turns to violence.” (Code: FP-6).

Theme 3: psychosocial vulnerability of the patient

This theme reflects caregivers’ perceptions of psychosocial factors that increase patients’ vulnerability to engaging in violent behavior. It includes three sub-themes: “feeling misunderstood,” “being alone,” and “imbalance in the patient’s social life.”

Under the sub-theme of feeling misunderstood, caregivers reported that patients often perceived others as not understanding them, even in situations where communication appeared clear. This perceived misunderstanding was described as a trigger for anger and subsequent verbal or physical aggression.

The sub-theme of being alone highlights the negative impact of social isolation. Participants emphasized that prolonged periods of loneliness were associated with increased irritability and a higher likelihood of violent behavior, indicating the importance of continuous social presence.

Similarly, the sub-theme of imbalance in the patient’s social life reflects the need for a stable and balanced level of social engagement. Caregivers noted that both excessive isolation and overwhelming social environments could exacerbate symptoms, and disruptions in this balance were linked to increased aggression.

The statements of the participants are as follows:

“… Sometimes he says something, I understand it completely but he claims that ı don’t understand and attacks us… He curses and insults us.” (Code: MP-4).

“ Sir, he shouldn’t be alone. He doesn’t like loneliness at all. He gets really angry when he’s alone. He should always have someone with him, otherwise there will be hitting, breaking, and swearing.” (Code: FP-1).

“….His social life needs to be balanced. He doesn’t like crowds, but if he is alone for too long, it gets worse. That’s why he should do social activities and get involved in life, but also spend time at home from time to time. When this balance is disrupted, it gets worse.” (Code: MP-6).

Theme 4: family dynamics and interpersonal conflict

This theme reflects the role of family interactions in triggering patient-initiated violence. It comprises two sub-themes: “forcing the patient to undergo treatment” and “conflicting with the patient.”

Under the sub-theme of forcing the patient to undergo treatment, caregivers reported that attempts to ensure treatment adherence—such as reminding patients to take medication or attend follow-up appointments—often led to resistance and subsequent aggression. Although these actions were intended to support the patient’s well-being, they were sometimes perceived by patients as coercive, resulting in violent reactions.

The sub-theme of conflicting with the patient highlights the impact of interpersonal disagreement. Participants indicated that when they expressed opinions that contradicted the patient’s beliefs or wishes, this often led to anger and escalation into violence. Caregivers described a tendency to avoid confrontation in order to prevent aggressive episodes.

The statements of the participants are as follows:

“……When he doesn’t take his medication, when we tell him to take his medication, when he doesn’t want to go to the doctor and we tell him to go to the doctor, he becomes violent” (Code: FP-4).

“Sometimes he doesn’t take his medication, we remind him to take his medication to protect him. He doesn’t like this, he says he doesn’t want to take his medication, when we force him he gets aggressive.” (Code: FP-5).

“When we say something that is against his ideas and what he says, he gets angry and turns to violence. It has to be what he says. The fact that he doesn’t have what he wants affects him badly, so we try not to be confrontational.” (Code: MP-6).

Discussion

The results of this study, which addressed the experiences of violence among family caregivers of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia, provide the basis for a holistic approach that includes effective treatment strategies, support systems for caregivers, and a societal shift in perceptions of mental illness. Schizophrenia is a complex and multifactorial disorder shaped by genetic, neurobiological, and environmental factors, and recent research highlights the rapid expansion of genetic studies emphasizing its polygenic nature and biological complexity [2]. Despite these advances, translating this growing body of knowledge into clinical practice and everyday caregiving contexts remains a significant challenge.

According to the results obtained from the current study, family members who care for individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia do not know why they are exposed to violence from the patient. Some caregivers do not see the illness as the cause of violence, see violence as a part of life and believe that it is not related to schizophrenia. Violence has a nature that can be encountered in all types of environments and people. The fact that family members generally encounter violence in their lives may have caused them not to distinguish this situation when they encounter violence related to the patient. It is known that individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia may exhibit more violent behavior than the normal population [13, 31, 32].

The fact that family members do not have any conclusions about the cause of violence may be due to the families’ lack of information or it may be due to their normalization of violence. In this study, it was determined that some family members who care for individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia stated that one of the reasons for the violence they were exposed to was jinn. Participants attributed the violence to a metaphysical cause and believed that jinn haunting their patients and leading them to violence was the main reason. The existence of jinn is reported in Abrahamic religions and holy books [33–35]. It can be said that cultural evaluations underlie this thought of the participants. In culturally embedded contexts, mental health experiences are often interpreted through religious and social meaning systems, which shape how symptoms and behaviors are understood [36].

In the region where the study was conducted, it is believed that jinn can have relations with people, appear to them and cause problems [37]. In particular, the fact that the hallucinations described by the patient were perceived as jinn by those around them may have caused the participants to accept jinn as one of the causes of violence.

In the current study, it was determined that treatment adherence is a factor that prevents violence, and that patients’ behaviors that are not compliant with treatment, such as delaying medication use and not going to regular check-ups, are effective in the emergence of violent behavior. In schizophrenia, non-compliance with treatment causes symptoms to increase and patients to have attacks. Studies have reported that patients are more prone to violence during periods when they do not comply with treatment [38, 39]. Participants are aware that one of the causes of patient-related violence is non-compliance with treatment.

In this study, participants reported that their patients were violent due to hallucinations and delusions. Command hallucinations and paranoid thoughts are effective in causing family members who care for individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia to become victims of violent behavior. Patients suspect family members because they have insecurity, and a conflict environment occurs. When some family members deny the patient’s grandiose delusions, they are subjected to violence. Family members are aware that the symptoms are due to the nature of the disease and that compliance with treatment is protective to prevent or reduce the symptoms. Studies have shown that command hallucinations and delusions are effective in the emergence of violent behavior, and that family members are often the victims of violence [40–42].

According to the family members, one of the reasons for patient-related violence is the patient’s thoughts that they are not understood. The participants reported that the belief and perception of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia that they are not understood makes them aggressive and leads them to violence. Thinking that they are not understood causes aggressive reactions for most people. Family members are aware of this situation and pay attention to this when communicating with the patient.

According to the current study, loneliness is a risk factor for schizophrenia patients to exhibit violent behavior. It has been stated that patients who are alone become aggressive and show aggression. In this study, family members think that the disruption of the balance in social life leads to violence, that the balance in the individual’s social life should be preserved, and that imbalances in social life negatively affect the individual diagnosed with schizophrenia and make them aggressive. Sariah et al. (2014) highlight that high levels of criticism from family members are associated with poorer outcomes for patients, leading to increased stress and interpersonal conflicts within the household [43]. This criticism can exacerbate the symptoms of schizophrenia, creating a vicious cycle that heightens the potential for violent outbursts.

Participants reported that they were subjected to violence from patients when they warned individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia to use their medications or go for check-ups. On the other hand, it was determined that conflict with the patient was a factor that caused violence. It can be said that there may be reasons for violence caused by family members. Family members forcing the patient to receive treatment and conflicting with the patient are seen as a reason for violence. The ambivalent relationships between patients and their family members, particularly mothers, have been identified as a significant factor in the occurrence of violence. A case report illustrates how emotional conflicts within families can act as triggers for violent behavior, underscoring the need for family-level interventions to address these dynamics [44]. The caregiving role often leads to significant stress and conflict, particularly when parents are tasked with managing the complexities of their child’s illness [45]. The burden of caregiving can lead to frustration and resentment, which may manifest as aggression or violence directed towards family members.

Finally, although emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence offer promising opportunities for early detection, monitoring, and personalized management of mental disorders, their integration into real-life caregiving contexts remains limited [3]. Therefore, understanding caregivers’ lived experiences and perceptions of violence remains essential for developing effective, culturally sensitive, and clinically applicable interventions.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strength of the research is the in-depth content of the individual in-depth interviews. Each participant had sufficient time to share their experiences, and they expressed their views in a relaxed manner.

The limitation of the study is that it is restricted to a single center and at the time of data collection. Both constraints make generalization difficult. For these reasons, additional research is needed to broaden the sampling and expand its scope. The study’s results are based on the opinions of the individuals who participated in this study. Therefore, they cannot be generalized.

Conclusion

In line with the results of the current study, it may be recommended to conduct studies in which the causes of violence inflicted by individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia on family members are examined in depth to plan experimental studies, including interventions to help families with individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia cope with patient-related violence and to create evidence on effective intervention programs.

Addressing these issues requires a holistic approach that encompasses effective treatment strategies, support systems for caregivers, and a societal shift in the perception of mental illness. Future research should continue to explore the lived experiences of caregivers, aiming to develop targeted interventions that can alleviate the burden of care while promoting the safety and well-being of both patients and their families.
