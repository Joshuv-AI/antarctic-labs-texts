Improvement in Motor Consistency and Stability with Foslevodopa/Foscarbidopa in Advanced Parkinson’s Disease: Post Hoc Analysis of Two Phase 3 Clinical Trials
Authors: Rajesh Pahwa, Jason Aldred, Michael J. Soileau, David G. Standaert, Victor S. C. Fung, Thomas Kimber, Irene A. Malaty, Diego Santos-García, Camille Carroll, Tove Henriksen, Ashwini Parab, Connie H. Yan, Maurizio F. Facheris, Amy Spiegel, Linda Harmer, Jorge Zamudio, K. Ray Chaudhuri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPeople with advanced Parkinson’s disease (aPD) frequently experience unpredictable “Off” time and debilitating motor fluctuations. Foslevodopa/foscarbidopa (LDp/CDp), a levodopa/carbidopa (LD/CD) prodrug, is delivered as a 24-h continuous subcutaneous infusion. This post hoc analysis evaluated the efficacy of LDp/CDp in achieving consistent motor symptom control and stable motor states in people with aPD.MethodsDiaries of people with aPD treated with LDp/CDp participating in a 12-week, phase 3, randomized controlled trial (RCT) were evaluated versus oral LD/CD, and in a 52-week open-label, single-arm trial (OLT) with LDp/CDp alone. Motor symptom control was assessed by frequency (30-min intervals) and duration (4-h blocks) of motor states (good “On” time [without dyskinesia or troublesome dyskinesia] or “Off” time) over a 16-h waking day. Motor state stability was evaluated by changes in daily motor fluctuations and extreme fluctuations (defined as transition from “Off” to “On” with troublesome dyskinesia, or vice versa). Outcomes were analyzed using adjusted regression models.ResultsAnalysis included 47 (RCT) and 55 (OLT) people with aPD on LDp/CDp. In the RCT, LDp/CDp had an approximately 1-h gain in good “On” time in the mornings versus a quarter-hour gain for those on LD/CD (P = 0.001), with > 80% of participants on LDp/CDp waking up in good “On.” At week 12, fewer motor fluctuations/day occurred with LDp/CDp versus LD/CD (3.2 vs 5.3; nominal P = 0.001), and twice as many participants on LDp/CDp had ≤ 3 fluctuations/day (53.2% vs 25.8%). In the OLT, the results seen at 12 weeks were sustained through week 52, with fewer mean fluctuations/day (3.1) than baseline (7.4) and more participants reporting ≤ 3 fluctuations/day at 52 weeks (66.6%) versus baseline (12.9%).ConclusionLDp/CDp provided consistent motor symptom control throughout the day, enhanced motor state stability, and reduced motor fluctuation, highlighting LDp/CDp’s potential to significantly improve the management of unpredictable motor states and overall quality of life for people with aPD.Trial informationClinicaltrials.gov ID: NCT04380142, NCT03781167.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-025-00827-6.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurological disorder with one of the highest increases in prevalence in the world among neurodegenerative disorders, increasing from 2.5 million in 1990 to 6.1 million in 2016 [1]. People with PD (PwP) frequently face multiple challenges, such as limited mobility, impaired daily activities, diminished quality of life (QoL), increased medical costs due to advancing disabilities, and heightened caregiver burden [2–4]. They also have a higher risk of falls and are more likely to develop nonmotor symptoms including cognitive, behavioral, psychiatric, and sleep issues [5, 6].

The “gold standard” treatment for PwP includes immediate release (IR) oral levodopa (LD) combined with carbidopa (CD; a peripheral dopa decarboxylase inhibitor) [7]. However, in advanced PD (aPD), oral IR LD/CD may fail to maintain stable motor symptom control throughout the day [7]. This instability is often due to a narrowing therapeutic window and fluctuating blood LD levels, which can be caused by the short half-life of LD and erratic drug absorption from unpredictable gastric emptying [8]. The resultant unpredictability can greatly impair the daily activities, mental health, and QoL of PwP, and can increase the risk of injury from falls due to freezing of gait or balance difficulty [2–4]. Consequently, PwP may benefit from continuous dopaminergic stimulation treatments that bypass the gastric absorption to attain more stable LD levels and therefore more consistent motor symptom control [9].

One approach to ensure continuous delivery of LD-based treatment is the use of infusion therapies, such as intrajejunal LD [10]. The efficacy of CD/LD enteral suspension (CLES) delivered via a pump through a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy-jejunostomy tube has been previously reported, with people with aPD experiencing significantly less “Off” time and more “On” time without troublesome dyskinesia over a 16-h day [11]. In a subsequent post hoc analysis, the stability of the effect was also demonstrated by fewer motor fluctuations, fewer motor-state transitions, and more predictability of time spent in “On” states with CLES versus oral IR LD/CD [12]. Another treatment option is foslevodopa/foscarbidopa (LDp/CDp), a soluble prodrug formulation of LD/CD, approved for the management of motor fluctuations in people with aPD [13, 14]. LDp/CDp is delivered as a 24-h continuous subcutaneous infusion (CSCI) via an infusion set connected to a portable pump that ensures stable plasma LD concentrations over a 24-h day [15, 16]. Results from two separate phase 3 studies demonstrated significantly improved “On” time without troublesome dyskinesia and “Off” time with LDp/CDp versus oral IR LD/CD in people with aPD over 12 weeks [17] and long-term improvements in people with aPD receiving open-label LDp/CDp over 52 weeks [18]. In both studies, LDp/CDp was well tolerated.

This post hoc analysis of the two phase 3 trials evaluated the efficacy of LDp/CDp for achieving consistent motor symptom control (i.e., temporal patterns of “Off” and good “On” time across the day) and improving motor state stability (i.e., motor fluctuations and extreme fluctuations) throughout the waking day in people with aPD versus oral IR LD/CD at 12 weeks. The impact of LDp/CDp on these outcomes was further evaluated for sustainment over a period of up to 52 weeks.

Methods

Study Design

Data from a 12-week randomized controlled trial (RCT) of LDp/CDp delivered as a 24-h CSCI versus oral IR LD/CD (Clinicaltrials.gov ID: NCT04380142) and from a separate 52-week single-arm, open-label LDp/CDp 24-h CSCI trial (OLT; Clinicaltrials.gov ID: NCT03781167) were included in this post hoc analysis. In both trials, the infusion set and the infusion site could remain unchanged for up to 3 days when LDp/CDp was infused continuously. Details of these two phase 3 clinical trial study designs, and their efficacy and safety findings, have been previously published [17, 18]. Safety was not evaluated in this post hoc analysis, but LDp/CDp was well tolerated, as discussed previously [17, 18].

Population

Participants aged ≥ 30 years with a diagnosis of LD-responsive PD and an average of ≥ 2.5 h of “Off” time/day were included in the RCT and OLT. Participants were required to have normal cognitive function (Mini Mental State Examination score ≥ 24; those with scores of 19–23 could enroll in the OLT at the discretion of the investigator). People with aPD were excluded from both studies if they had a history of significant skin conditions that could interfere with infusion of the study drug or prevent assessors from examining the infusion sites. People with aPD were excluded from the RCT if they had undergone a procedure for deep brain stimulation (DBS), received CLES, or were prescribed any PD therapy administered as a continuous daily infusion. In the OLT, people with aPD who previously received DBS were eligible for the study and could continue to receive DBS treatment if they were in stable condition and were LD responsive.

For inclusion in this post hoc analysis, people with aPD were required to have both baseline and week 12 data (RCT) and/or week 52 data (OLT) recorded in their PD diaries.

Assessment Measures

The PD diary was a validated clinical tool that could be used at home and allowed participants to record their motor state throughout the day [19, 20]. In both trials, participants recorded their motor states (“Off,” “On” without dyskinesia, “On” with nontroublesome dyskinesia, “On” with troublesome dyskinesia, and time asleep) in an electronic PD diary at 30-min intervals over 3 days before each study visit. The definition of “On” time without troublesome dyskinesia (good “On” time) was the combined number of hours of “On” time without dyskinesia and “On” time with nontroublesome dyskinesia. Daily “On” and “Off” times were normalized to a 16-h waking day. Wake-up time in the RCT was defined as the first nonsleep diary entry after at least 2 consecutive hours of asleep entries after 3:00 a.m. Wake-up time in the OLT was defined as the first nonsleep diary entry after the start of the diary day (6:00 a.m.). Diary days were excluded if < 12 h were recorded as awake. To ensure wake-up time and full 24-h days were calculable for all diary days, participants with ≥ 2 invalid diary days (≥ 4 entries missing from the daily 48 entries or < 24 entries that were not missing or asleep) were excluded. In this analysis, PD diary data were evaluated at baseline, week 12 (RCT), and at weeks 13 and 52 (OLT).

Outcomes

Consistency of motor symptom control was evaluated by the proportion of participants reporting good “On” time and “Off” time in 30-min intervals throughout the 16-h waking day; and based on the mean change from baseline in the duration of good “On” time or “Off” time throughout the day in 4-h intervals [0–4 h (morning), 4–8 h (midday), 8–12 h (afternoon), and 12–16 h (evening)].

Motor state stability was assessed by a reduction from baseline in the average number of daily motor fluctuations between three motor states (hereafter referred to “3-state transitions” and included transitions between “Off” time, good “On” time [i.e., “On” time without troublesome dyskinesia], and “On” time with troublesome dyskinesia) throughout the day, and the proportion of participants with ≥ 0 to ≤ 3, > 3 to ≤ 6, > 6 to ≤ 9 or > 9 daily “3-state state fluctuations” (with thresholds based on the distribution of the data and on similar previous analyses [12]). Participants with fewer fluctuations were considered to have improved motor state stability [12]. The number of transitions or fluctuations between any five states was evaluated as recorded in the Hauser diary (referred to as “5-state transitions” or “5-state fluctuations” in this report and included transitions from “Off,” “On” without dyskinesia, “On” with troublesome dyskinesia, “On” with nontroublesome dyskinesia, and asleep) throughout the day. Notably, the classification of “asleep” occurred during a typical waking day and thus was interpreted as falling asleep or napping and not as overnight sleep. In addition, the number of daily extreme fluctuations (defined as transitions from “Off” to “On” with troublesome dyskinesia or vice versa) was also assessed.

Statistical Analysis

As a post hoc study, sample sizes were based on the enrollment of the primary clinical trials, which were powered to examine different primary and secondary outcomes than the outcomes presented here [17, 18]. Therefore, these post hoc analyses were not powered to detect the endpoints of interest.

Descriptive statistics were calculated as the mean and standard deviation for continuous measures (consistency of motor symptom control, time and duration of motor state stability, number of extreme fluctuations) and count and frequency for categorical (proportion of participants in different subgroups defined by number of motor fluctuations) and binary measures (presence/absence of extreme fluctuations). Outcomes at week 12 in the RCT and at week 52 in the OLT were evaluated using linear regression adjusted for the following covariates: age, sex, race, PD duration, country, and baseline values of outcomes. Marginal predictions were generated from the fitted regression results. In the RCT, arithmetic differences in predictive margins were calculated between trial arms at each visit and were compared for equality across visits using Wald tests. Significance level was set at a P value < 0.05. All analyses were performed using a standard software package (STATA; StataCorp, LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethical Approval

These studies were conducted in accordance with their respective protocols, International Conference on Harmonisation guidelines, and guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. These studies were approved by independent ethics committees or institutional review boards at each study site (independent ethics committee/institutional review board details have been previously published for the OLT [18], and are provided in the supplementary appendix for the RCT). Participants and their caregivers (if applicable) provided written informed consent before screening. These studies were registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04380142 and NCT03781167). Permissions were obtained to use the scales employed in this study (e.g., PD Hauser diary, Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale).

Consistency of Motor Symptom Control

12-Week RCT

At week 12, participants receiving LDp/CDp had the greatest gains from baseline (approx. 1 h) in time in the good “On” state in the morning (within the first 4 h of waking; Fig. 1). The gain in good “On” time in the morning with LDp/CDp was greater than that achieved by participants receiving oral IR LD/CD (58.6 vs 15.8 min; between-group nominal P = 0.001). The gains in good “On” time with LDp/CDp treatment were sustained throughout the day and were greater than those receiving oral IR LD/CD. 52-Week OLT

Trends in improvements in time in the good “On” state and “Off” state, especially in the morning and throughout the waking day, were seen at week 13 and maintained through 52 weeks of treatment with LDp/CDp (Figs. 1, 2).

Motor State Stability

12-Week RCT

The mean number of daily 3-state fluctuations was similar at baseline between the LDp/CDp and oral IR LD/CD groups (7.3 vs 7.2, respectively; Fig. 3a). At week 12, participants receiving LDp/CDp versus oral IR LD/CD experienced fewer mean daily motor fluctuations (3.2 vs 5.3, respectively; between-group nominal P = 0.001). 52-Week OLT

Mean daily 3-state fluctuations decreased from baseline to week 13 and week 52 in people with aPD receiving LDp/CDp (7.4 to 3.2; within-group nominal P < 0.001 vs baseline) and 3.1 (within-group nominal P < 0.001), respectively Fig. 3b). Furthermore, the change from baseline in number of 3-state transitions was relatively stable across 4-h blocks of the 16-h waking day at week 13 (mean change from baseline counts of 0.7 in the morning to 1.0 in the afternoon) and week 52 (0.8 in the morning to 0.7 in evening; Fig. 4b). The proportion of participants receiving LDp/CDp with 0–3 daily 3-state fluctuations increased by nearly fivefold from baseline to week 13 and week 52 (14.3 to 66.7% and 71.4%, respectively; Fig. 5). In addition, the proportion of participants receiving LDp/CDp with > 9 daily 3-state fluctuations decreased by nearly fivefold from baseline to week 13 and week 52 (23.8% to 4.8% and 4.8%, respectively; Fig. 5). These proportions of participants with ≤ 3 or > 9 daily 3-state fluctuations were comparable when estimated using a regression model adjusting for covariates (ESM Table 1).

The mean number of 5-state transitions decreased from baseline to weeks 13 and 52 (10.6 to 5.3 and 5.4, respectively; ESM Fig. 1b). The number of 5-state transitions was relatively consistent throughout the 4-h blocks of the waking day at week 13 (mean count of 1.0 morning 5-state transition to 1.4 in the afternoon and evening) and week 52 (mean count 1.1 in the morning to 1.4 in the midday and evening periods; ESM Fig. 2B). The proportion of participants with > 9 daily 5-state fluctuations, as assessed using a regression model adjusting for covariates, decreased to approximately one-fifth from baseline (58.3%) to week 13 (11.0%) and week 52 (12.9%; ESM Table 1). In contrast, the proportion of participants with ≤ 3 daily 5-state fluctuations increased over 13-fold from baseline (2.3%) to week 13 (32.8%) and week 52 (30.8%) (ESM 1).

The proportion of participants receiving LDp/CDp who experienced ≥ 1 daily extreme fluctuations decreased from baseline to week 13 and week 52 [31.8% to 3.8% (adjusted; within-group nominal P ≤ 0.05) and 5.4% (adjusted; within-group nominal P ≤ 0.05), respectively]. The mean number of daily extreme fluctuations reduced from baseline to week 13 and week 52 [0.5–0.1 (within-group nominal P = 0.061) and 0.1 (within-group nominal P ≤ 0.05), respectively].

Discussion

This post hoc analysis of PD diary data from two phase 3 trials in people with aPD is the first to assess the effect of LDp/CDp on temporal motor state patterns throughout the day. Participants treated with LDp/CDp experienced greater consistency of motor symptom control with sustained greater increases in good “On” time and reductions in “Off” time compared across 4-h intervals of the day versus those participants receiving oral IR LD/CD. This finding is particularly noteworthy considering that oral IR LD/CD has been associated with unpredictable and inconsistent motor symptom control, which can greatly impact PwP in planning daily activities [4, 21].

The short half-life of oral LD (approx. 90 min) [22] causes the effects of conventional oral LD medications to lose benefit overnight, often leaving PwP in an “Off” state upon waking. Early morning “Off” (EMO), often referred to as early morning akinesia, is a challenge in PwP receiving oral PD medications [10]. When comparing CLES (a 16-h continuous therapy during waking hours) with oral IR LD/CD therapy, CLES-treated people with aPD achieved “On” time without troublesome dyskinesia faster after waking, with 49% of them experiencing onset of “On” time without troublesome dyskinesia within 1 h of waking compared with 2 h after waking in those being treated with oral IR LD/CD therapy [12]. LDp/CDp can further mitigate this issue by providing continuous 24-h infusion, including during overnight sleeping hours, which may effectively manage EMO periods. Previous research has demonstrated clinically meaningful lower rates of morning akinesia with LDp/CDp versus oral IR LD/CD therapy at 12 weeks (17% vs 63%) [17], and this incidence was also low with LDp/CDp at 52 weeks (28%) [18]. Notably, the majority of people with aPD receiving LDp/CDp awoke in an “On” state without dyskinesia (62.2%) at 52 weeks of therapy, highlighting the long-term benefit of this 24-h therapy [18]. Our analysis showed that the greatest improvements in 3-state motor stability with LDp/CDp occurred in the morning. This resulted in the greatest gains in good “On” time compared with baseline and the greatest reductions in “Off” time. Similar results were found when evaluating 5-state fluctuations, including four motor states and falling asleep during normal waking hours. More participants receiving LDp/CDp woke with good “On” time compared with those who received oral IR LD/CD; the latter therapy required approximately 2 h longer for a similar proportion of participants to achieve good “On” time. Not surprisingly, shorter time to a morning “On” state can significantly improve the QoL of PwP by enabling greater independence in morning routines [23].

In this study, participants receiving LDp/CDp had greater 3-state and 5-state stability with fewer fluctuations and half the number of extreme fluctuations compared with those receiving oral IR LD/CD in the 12-week RCT. All improvements in consistency of motor symptom control and stability of motor state with reduced fluctuations were also observed at 13 through 52 weeks of open-label LDp/CDp treatment in the OLT. The larger proportions of participants with ≥ 0 to ≤ 3 daily fluctuations and a lower percentage of those with > 6 daily motor fluctuations suggest that consistent motor symptoms may be explained by more stable plasma LD concentrations [15]. With conventional oral LD tablets, unpredictable absorption due to erratic gastric emptying, in combination with a short half-life, leads to variability in peak-to-trough LD plasma concentrations throughout a waking day [22]. Since CSCI LDp/CDp bypasses the gastrointestinal tract and provides dopaminergic therapy directly into the bloodstream, people with aPD receiving LDp/CDp have a higher chance of achieving more consistent plasma levels of medication and, therefore, more stable symptom control across the entire 24-h waking day.

In summary, findings from this post hoc analysis highlight the significant benefits of LDp/CDp therapy for people with aPD, which provides consistent motor symptom control throughout the day and reduces motor fluctuations. Compared with traditional oral IR LD/CD treatment, LDp/CDp demonstrates greater improvements in motor state stability and reduced "Off" time, especially in the morning, which can enhance the QoL for people with aPD. These improvements are attributed to the stable plasma LD concentrations achieved through 24-h CSCI, offering a noninvasive alternative that maintains consistent therapeutic effects. As PD progresses, oral IR LD/CD may become insufficient for motor symptom management, making LDp/CDp an important therapeutic option for achieving reliable and individualized treatment.

Limitations

As this was a post hoc study, the sample size was not powered to detect differences in the endpoints of interest between studies, particularly in the subgroup analyses. Only participants with baseline and end-of-study data were included, which may have impacted the precision of estimation of the treatment effect due to the reduced sample size from participant discontinuation. Inclusion of participants with data only at baseline and end of study could have resulted in an overestimation of motor stability, as participants who discontinued before the end of the study due to poor symptom control or treatment dissatisfaction would not be included in this analysis. The findings do reflect the benefits for patients who continue treatment. Primary reasons for discontinuation were previously reported for the RCT and OLT [17, 18]. However, there is insufficient information to determine if any given discontinuation was related to motor control. Secondly, infusions of LDp/CDp could be administered continuously for up to 3 days per single infusion site in these studies. The clinical effect of possible change in insertion site on motor state has not been examined in detail and the specific naming of this element of treatment should be acknowledged. In addition, the generalizability of these findings to real-world PD populations may be limited, given the clinical trial setting. PD diaries were based on participant self-report and may be susceptible to recall bias. To minimize this, participants were encouraged to record their symptoms every 30 min to the best of their ability. Additionally, participants were required to have normal cognitive function for inclusion in the parent studies; therefore, impaired cognition should not have influenced reporting. Certain methodological assumptions, such as dividing the day into 4-h blocks and categorizing fluctuations into 3-state or 5-state blocks, may oversimplify the complexity of motor symptom patterns. Nevertheless, trend analysis using motor state measurements collected at 30-min intervals provided additional granularity. Multiple time-point comparisons may, however, increase the risk of type I errors. Another potential limitation is that the analysis includes all motor state transitions, even those representing improvement, which are clinically desirable. However, this approach was intended to capture the continuous fluctuations that can undermine the ability to perform daily activities consistently and predictably. This aspect of instability is often under-recognized but is meaningful from a clinical and PwP perspective. Despite these limitations, clear treatment effects were observed in these post hoc analyses.

Conclusions

Continuous subcutaneous infusion LDp/CDp provides greater consistency in motor symptom control and motor state stability compared with oral IR LD/CD in people with aPD. These findings underscore the benefit of 24-h continuous therapy relative to oral dosing therapies that result in pulsatile plasma LD concentrations. Healthcare providers and people with aPD can discuss whether LDp/CDp is a good treatment option, especially if these people are experiencing fluctuations with oral therapies, including frequently transitioning between “Off” and “On” states or awakening in an “Off” state. LDp/CDp has demonstrated the ability to significantly impact two debilitating aspects of PD: unpredictability concerning motor state and motor fluctuations. Future research should focus on how improvement in consistency of motor symptom stability and decreased early morning “Off” time with LDp/CDp may contribute to improvements in QoL for people with aPD.
