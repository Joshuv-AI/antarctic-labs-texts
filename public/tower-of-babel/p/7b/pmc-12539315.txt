Sense of coherence and its relation to self perceived dental appearance and normative orthodontic treatment need among 12-year-old children
Authors: Turaga Sai Susmitha, Jagadeeswara Rao Sukhabhogi, Dolar Doshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSense of Coherence (SOC) is a psychological aspect that could have a significant effect on an individual's perception of aesthetics.AimTo assess the influence of Sense of Coherence on self-perceived dental appearance against normative orthodontic treatment need among 12 year old children.MethodsAn observational study was conducted among 268 school children aged 12 years old from in and around Hyderabad city to assess individuals’ Sense of Coherence (SOC-13), self-perceived aesthetics (Oral Aesthetic Subjective Impact Scale – 5 item) and malocclusion using Dental Aesthetic Index. Frequency distributions were used to describe the responses item wise for SOC and OASIS questionnaire. The difference between mean SOC scores between the groups was assessed using ANOVA. Multiple linear regression was performed to assess the affect of gender and OASIS on SOC.ResultsNearly half of the sample (48.13 %) demonstrated higher Sense of Coherence and 58.96 % of individuals had positive perception of aesthetics. Remarkable percentage (78.73 %) of children had normative orthodontic treatment need. Highest mean SOC score (60.70 ± 9.78) was noted for those without orthodontic treatment need and negative aesthetic perception (p = 0.40). Self perceived dental aesthetics revealed a negative correlation with SOC (r = −0.02; p = 0.72). Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that SOC could be predicted only by gender (p = 0.01) but not self perceived aesthetics (p = 0.27)ConclusionIn this study, Sense of Coherence did not influence the self perceived aesthetics irrespective of normative need.Keywords: Sense of coherence, Self perceived aesthetics, Malocclusion, School children

Introduction

Health is defined as “a state of complete physical, mental and social wellbeing and not merely the absence of disease and infirmity”.1 The current concepts of health assessment emphasize a holistic approach by taking into account the psychological, social and biological factors. Also, health is a sequel of complex interaction between the individual and the environment.2 Consequently, social determinants play a significant role in determining health.

Sense of Coherence (SOC) is one such important construct receiving considerable attention in promotion of health. Aaron Antonovsky is the pioneer behind the conceptualization of Salutogenic theory, with Sense of Coherence being its central domain. It reflects how a person views life, his capacity to respond to stressful situations, his ability to view life as structured, manageable, and meaningful. It is a personal way of thinking, being and acting, with an inner trust, which leads people to identify, benefit, use, and re-use the resources at their disposal.3 Higher the SOC, greater could be the one's ability to define events as less stressful.4

Individual's perception of health is invariably linked to oral health. A multitude of factors such as culture, age, gender, societal norms, peer pressure, attitude, self-perceived aesthetic standard and other psychosocial factors could bear an impact over the subjective aesthetic perceptions and orthodontic treatment seeking behavior.5,6 Growing evidence suggests that social and emotional well-being of a person is affected by malocclusion.7 Individuals with mal aligned teeth often tend to avoid social interactions and fall prey to nickname calling, teasing and harassment.8 Children with inaccurate occlusal self-perception tend to be less satisfied with their dental appearance and may have greater perceived treatment need.9 Therefore, an individual's SOC might be associated with the perception of aesthetics.

Moreover, subjects with higher aesthetic perceptions might feel the need for treatment even in the absence of normative need.10 Therefore, apart from the subjective perception of aesthetics, assessment of normative need plays a significant role in planning and evaluation of oral health of an individual.

Also, as demonstrated by Wilson-Cleary model, individual factors (like SOC), biological clinical variables (like Normative orthodontic treatment need) and health perceptions (like self-perceived dental aesthetics) are inter-related.11

Recognizing this importance, the present study aimed to determine the influence of Sense of Coherence on self-perceived dental appearance against normative orthodontic treatment need among 12 year old school children.

Materials and method

A cross sectional study was conducted to assess the influence of Sense of Coherence on self-perceived dental appearance against normative orthodontic treatment need among the 12-year-old school children. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC/OMC/2022/M.No.(10)/Acad-98). Informed consent/assent was obtained from the school authorities. Reporting of the study was done according to the STROBE guidelines.

With an expected prevalence of Sense of Coherence (p) of 50.1, margin of error (d) of 6 %, at a confidence interval of 95 %, the estimated sample size was a minimum of 241 subjects.

Using the method of convenient sampling, schools that were willing to permit data collection were approached. In total, five government English medium schools, in and around Hyderabad, were chosen for the study, mounting to an average of fifty students from each school. All individuals aged 12 years and were able to read and understand English were included in the study from each of the five schools. Those undergoing or have undergone orthodontic treatment, presence of underlying psychomotor impairment, not showing willingness towards oral examination and lack of informed consent or assent were excluded from the study.

The study was conducted in five government schools, over a period of two months, ranging from July 4th, 2022, to August 24th, 2022. The study tool was a questionnaire that collected the demographic details (gender) and assessed the Sense of Coherence (SOC-13)12 and subjective dental aesthetic perception through Oral Aesthetic Subjective Impact Scale (OASIS). The SOC-13 developed by Aron Antonovsky13 measures an individual's Sense of Coherence based on thirteen questions, rated on a seven point Likert scale ranging from 1- ‘very seldom or never’ to 7- ‘very often’. The questions 1,2,3,7 and 10 were negatively worded and hence the values were reversed. The total SOC score ranged from 13 to 91 (https://scales.arabpsychology.com/s/sense-of-coherence-scale-soc/#google_vignette).

Individual's perception of their dental aesthetics was assessed using five item OASIS scale developed by Mandall et al.14 Each question was rated on a seven point Likert scale with score 1 being ‘not at all’ to score 7 referring to ‘all the time’. The minimum and maximum scores that could be obtained through this questionnaire were 5 and 35, respectively.(https://www.mona.uwi.edu/fms/wimj/system/files/article_pdfs/dr_bourne_wimj.qxd_.pdf). Higher scores indicated a more negative perception of dental aesthetics.

Oral examination was carried out post questionnaire survey, for the included students, by a single pre-trained and pre-calibrated Dentist. (intra-examiner kappa value = 0.87). It included the assessment of malocclusion as per the norms mentioned in the Dental Aesthetic Index (DAI)15 section of the World Health Organization 1997 criteria. (https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/41905/9241544937.pdf). Based on the final DAI scores, an individual's normative orthodontic treatment need was evaluated.

The completed questionnaires were collected, data was entered and analyzed using SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) software 22.0 (IBM, New York). Descriptive statistics were presented as means and standard deviations for numerical variables, or as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The mean value of SOC was considered as cut off and individuals with scores equal to or higher than the mean were considered to have higher Sense of Coherence and vice versa. Similarly, the mean OASIS value of the sample was the cut off and values equal to or above the mean suggest negative perception of dental aesthetics. Frequency distributions were used to describe the responses item wise for SOC and OASIS questionnaire. Chi square test was used to determine the significant difference in proportions among the groups. The difference between mean SOC scores between the groups was assessed using ANOVA. Correlation analysis was done between OASIS and SOC. Multiple linear regression was performed to assess the affect of gender and OASIS on SOC. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic details of the participants showed that the sample constituted a higher percentage of females (57.54 %; n = 154) compared to males (42.54 %; n = 114).

Nearly half of the sample (48.13 %) demonstrated higher Sense of Coherence and about 58.96 % of individuals had positive self perception pertaining to dental aesthetics. However, a remarkable percentage (78.73 %) of children had normative orthodontic treatment need.

On assessing the normative orthodontic treatment need against self perceived dental aesthetics, a majority of them (n = 121; 45.15 %) perceived their dental aesthetics positively irrespective of normative need. On the contrary, 7.46 % of the individuals perceived their dental aesthetics negatively despite no orthodontic treatment need (Table 1 & Graph 1).

Discussion

Antonovsky,16, 17, 18, 19 through empirical work, demonstrated that individuals with a higher Sense of Coherence (SOC) tend to maintain better overall well-being despite exposure to stressors. In the field of oral health, individuals with higher SOC report fewer oral health-related issues,2 including lower rates of dental caries20,21 and reduced prevalence of dental pain.22 Additional studies have identified that lower SOC scores are linked to suboptimal oral hygiene behaviors such as infrequent tooth brushing,23 greater plaque accumulation,24 and poorer self-perceived oral health.25

Building on these associations, the present study examined whether SOC influences adolescents’ perception of dental aesthetics independently of normative orthodontic treatment need. However, the findings did not support this hypothesis. SOC showed no significant association with self-perceived dental aesthetics or orthodontic treatment need. The only significant association observed was between SOC and gender, with female participants exhibiting higher mean SOC scores.

This gender difference in SOC may reflect multiple interrelated factors. Studies have shown that while adolescent girls often report higher stress levels, SOC may serve as a stronger psychological coping resource for females, helping them manage emotional demands more effectively.26 Additionally, gender differences in emotional intelligence and social cognition may contribute to higher SOC in girls. Females generally tend to outperform males in tasks involving empathy and emotional regulation, which could support stronger perceptions of comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness—the three components of SOC. Notably, the direction of gender-related SOC differences varies across populations, suggesting that cultural, developmental, and contextual factors also play a role.27

Despite 80 % of participants presenting with a normative need for orthodontic intervention, approximately half reported a positive perception of their dental aesthetics. Within this subgroup, the high prevalence of malocclusion may have contributed to its perception as a typical or non-deviant condition, reducing the likelihood of negative aesthetic self-assessment. When a condition is widespread within a peer group, adolescents may regard it as part of the norm, thereby minimizing its perceived impact on appearance and self-esteem.

In contrast, among participants without orthodontic treatment needs, those who reported negative aesthetic self-perception exhibited the highest mean SOC scores. This finding suggests that even in the absence of objective clinical concerns, adolescents may experience dissatisfaction with their appearance due to psychosocial influences. In such cases, broader appearance-related pressures—such as media exposure, family expectations, or highly appearance-conscious peer groups—may outweigh both the absence of treatment need and the internal resilience typically associated with higher SOC. These results differ from those of Costa et al.,28 who found a positive perception of dental aesthetics among individuals with high SOC despite clinical treatment need. The discrepancy may reflect contextual differences in the strength and nature of aesthetic norms influencing adolescents’ self-evaluation.

Strengths and limitations

The present study possesses several methodological strengths. Clinical examination was conducted using standardized protocols by a calibrated examiner, thereby ensuring consistency and reliability in assessment. The use of validated questionnaires for measuring both Sense of Coherence and self-perceived dental aesthetics strengthened the internal validity of the data collected. Furthermore, the sample size was increased from the initially estimated 241 to 268 participants to compensate for potential non-responses or incomplete data. The choice of 12-year-olds as the study population allowed for a comprehensive assessment of permanent dentition, as eruption is typically complete by this age (excluding third molars), and facilitated school-based access to the target group.

Nevertheless, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to establish causal or temporal relationships between SOC, orthodontic treatment need, and self-perceived dental aesthetics. The use of convenience sampling and a single age group, restricts the generalizability of the findings.

Future research should consider adopting randomized sampling techniques—such as stratified or cluster sampling—to enhance representativeness. Longitudinal study designs are recommended to examine the progression and causal pathways between SOC and self-perception over time. Inclusion of additional psychosocial variables such as peer influence, media exposure, parental expectations, and socioeconomic status would offer a more comprehensive understanding of the contextual factors influencing adolescent perceptions of dental aesthetics. Comparative studies across different age groups, cultures, and socioeconomic backgrounds may further elucidate the developmental and environmental determinants of SOC and aesthetic self-assessment.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that resilience, as reflected by Sense of Coherence, may not consistently influence how children perceive their dental appearance, indicating its limited role in certain health-related perceptions. This highlights the importance of considering individual aesthetic concerns separately from clinical assessments and coping abilities. The mismatch between clinical need and self-perception points to the need for more personalized approaches in orthodontic evaluation. Given that many children may view common dental irregularities as normal, awareness programs should aim to improve understanding of when treatment is truly necessary. Overall, the study supports a more holistic approach to oral health that includes psychological and social influences.
