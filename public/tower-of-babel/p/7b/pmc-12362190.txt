Airtight compartmentalization of the anterior skull base reconstructed using a pedicled pericranial periosteal flap: illustrative case
Authors: Shunya Kashiwagi, Motoaki Fujimoto, Shuya Otsuki, Hideki Ogata, Takahiko Kamata, Naohito Seki, Shoichi Tani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDAnterior skull base surgery to address tumor, trauma, or infection causing extensive bony destruction is associated with rhinorrhea and infection as potentially life-threatening complications. When performing anterior skull base reconstruction, watertight dural closure and airtight closure of the nasal cavity mucosa are essential for preventing these complications.OBSERVATIONSThe authors describe the case of a patient with a mixed olfactory neuroblastoma and adenocarcinoma who underwent resection. The procedure involved a multilayer reconstruction of the skull base, combining fascia lata from the thigh, a pedicled pericranial periosteal flap, and a pedicled nasoseptal flap. Specifically, the authors achieved airtight compartmentalization by carefully suturing the pedicled pericranial periosteal flap to the normal dura mater.LESSONSThis suture technique for placing a pedicled pericranial periosteal flap from the frontal bone in the epidural space might support the goal of achieving a watertight and airtight closure. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25422 Keywords: skull base reconstruction, tumor invasion, pedicled pericranial periosteal flap, rhinorrhea, intracranial contaminationABBREVIATIONS: PPP = pedicled pericranial periosteal

Any coalescence of the intracranial and nasal cavities caused by an extensive defect in the anterior skull base requires surgical reconstruction.1,2 After resecting a tumor that involves both cavities, use of a pedicled pericranial periosteal (PPP) flap has shown promise in compartmentalizing this unified space.3 The PPP flap is usually placed in the space between the dura mater and the anterior skull base and assists in achieving both a watertight dural closure and an airtight closure of the nasal mucosa, which is also covered from below with a pedicled nasoseptal flap. In addition, the PPP flap is well vascularized, which promotes postoperative healing and helps prevent infection.4 We present the case of a patient with a mixed olfactory neuroblastoma and adenocarcinoma who underwent resection and skull base reconstruction using a PPP flap specifically designed for airtight compartmentalization (Video 1). Our method emphasizes not only the placement of the PPP flap, but also its careful suturing to the normal dura mater to adequately cover the dural defect and the lateral edges of the PPP flap. Suturing the anterior part of the planum sphenoidale is particularly challenging and requires the use of several techniques.

Illustrative Case

A 53-year-old woman with a history of temporal lobe epilepsy and Crohn's disease presented with decreased motivation, irritability, olfactory dysfunction, and headaches. Neurological examination revealed olfactory dysfunction, higher brain dysfunction, and mild left leg motor impairment. CT and MRI showed a large neoplastic lesion extending from the anterior skull base to the right ethmoid sinus and nasal cavity, with edema in both frontal lobes (Fig. 1A–C). Bone defects were seen in the frontal skull base and the sinus cavities, but the cellular structure of the right ethmoid sinus was preserved. There was no evidence of intraorbital extension. Cerebral angiography showed no significant feeder supply to the tumor. CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis showed no evidence of primary malignancy or enlarged lymph nodes. A transnasal biopsy specimen was suspicious for papilloma based on histopathological detection of papillary growth of high-cylindrical epithelial-like tumor cells and prominent pseudostratification of nuclei. The tumor had invaded the anterior skull floor and extended laterally. Because lateral progression beyond the orbital periosteum and adhesion to the brain parenchyma were observed, a simultaneous transnasal endoscopic and transcranial approach was performed instead of a transnasal endoscopic approach alone.

Operative Technique

The patient was positioned supine in a Mayfield head holder, padded, and secured to the bed. Neurophysiological monitoring and image-guided surgical navigation were used. A bicoronal skin incision was made, and a large symmetrical bifrontal craniotomy extending to the top of the frontal sinus was performed. Marginal tenting was performed by drilling a small hole in the rim of the frontal bone and suturing it to the dura. The dura, which had been invaded and destroyed by the tumor around the cribriform plate, was incised extensively to enable tumor manipulation and removal. Under the surgical microscope, the border between the tumor and the surrounding brain, dura mater, and skull was identified and divided, while the separated tumor was removed piece by piece (Fig. 2A). Simultaneously, the transnasal team approached the tumor endoscopically from below (Fig. 2B) and performed piecemeal resection of the tumor toward the skull base. Intraoperatively, the nasal endoscope was visible through the intracranial space (Fig. 2C) because the cribriform plate, posterior wall of the frontal sinuses, and the anterior part of the planum sphenoidale had been destroyed by the tumor (Fig. 2D). After the tumor had been completely resected, the dural defect was reconstructed using fascia lata harvested from the thigh; a watertight suture closure was achieved (Fig. 2E). After the dura was closed, when a wide space for tumor manipulation and removal was no longer needed, marginal tenting was performed on each side at the most caudal position of the bone edge, which was important for proper positioning of the PPP flap (Fig. 3A). Once the dura had been peeled away from the bone to expose where suturing is needed, interrupted sutures were placed to attach the PPP flap to the dura around the planum sphenoidale to cover the dural closure (Figs. 2F and 3B–C). Then, from the most laterally placed interrupted suture toward the bone at the most caudal position for marginal tenting, the PPP flap was fixed to the dura with a running suture (Fig. 3D). Next, the PPP flap was fixed to the edge of the bone and dura using several sutures with the hole for marginal tenting, so as not to leave any pathway for air between the PPP flap and the skull (Fig. 2G). Fibrin glue was then applied to the sutured portions of the dura and the PPP flap (Fig. 2H). A piece of the craniotomy flap’s inner table was removed using a high-speed drill and placed to reconstruct the skull base defect and prevent encephalocele (Fig. 1D). The craniotomy flap was then replaced, taking care not to compress the PPP flap and compromise its blood flow. After closing the scalp, the transnasal endoscope confirmed that the PPP flap was positioned over the skull defect (Fig. 2I). A pedicled nasoseptal flap was then elevated and placed to seal the skull base defect, supplemented by fibrin glue. Because the flap failed to cover the entire defect, especially around the frontal sinus, it was supplemented by another free flap of nasal mucosa. Postoperative imaging confirmed a complete tumor resection (Fig. 1D–F). Histopathological examination of the intracranial surgical specimen was consistent with olfactory neuroblastoma (modified Kadish type C, Hyams grade III) with prominent epithelial-like differentiation; the intranasal lesion was non–intestinal-type sinonasal adenocarcinoma. Eight months have passed since the patient was discharged from the hospital, and there has been no evidence of rhinorrhea, encephalocele, or nasal air infiltration into the epidural space.

Discussion

Large tumors extending into both intracranial and nasal cavities are challenging to resect completely because of the intricate structure of the frontal skull base. The potential complications of CSF leakage and infection can be life-threatening and should be avoided as much as possible.5 Adequate and rigorous reconstruction of the skull base defect is important and requires both watertight closure of the dura to prevent rhinorrhea and airtight reconstruction of the nasal cavity mucosa to prevent intracranial infection. Compartmentalization of the sterile subarachnoid space from the nasal cavity is key and can be achieved with tight dural closure and double-layering of pedicled flaps of periosteum and nasal mucosa.6,7 These flaps have enough vascularity to rapidly form the necessary barrier and heal solidly enough to withstand the changes in pressure between the subarachnoid space and nasal cavity related to the respiratory cycle, sneezing, and coughing. In particular, the pedicled nasal septum mucosal flap is not as expandable as the PPP flap; however, if there is extensive tumor destruction of the skull base involving the frontal and ethmoidal sinuses, as in our patient, the nasal mucosal flap might not cover the entire defect and air could enter the intracranial space.8 The PPP flap is therefore a method of last resort to preserve the sterility of the epidural space and prevent infection of the devascularized bone autograft and dural patch. In addition to preventing rhinorrhea with a watertight closure, the PPP flap should be installed to be as airtight as possible.

In general, anterior skull base reconstruction is based on the principles of repairing the frontal sinus defect associated with bifrontal craniotomy.9,10 Suture closure of the frontal sinus mucosa is reliable for achieving an airtight compartmentalization between the nasal cavity and brain.11 Although dense suture closure is ideal, transnasal suturing or suture closure between the mucosa and skull is technically difficult when tumor invasion or traumatic injury has extensively destroyed the anterior skull base. Alternatively, suture closure between the PPP flap and the dura can be performed through the bifrontal craniotomy.12 A PPP flap that is tightly sutured over the dural closure and extends to the edge of the bone should be able to maintain aseptic conditions within the cranium and subarachnoid space.

Observations

The PPP flap must be securely and perfectly sutured to the dura and bone, creating an effective shield against rhinorrhea and bacterial contamination. Several pitfalls that result in failure to achieve an airtight closure during PPP flap fixation must be avoided. The dural defect must be tightly sutured to the fascia lata after the normal dural transection has been fully dissected from the sphenoidal plane. Therefore, the surgical field is narrower and deeper because the PPP flap must be sutured to the dura covering the dural suture (red dotted circle in Fig. 3B). Occasionally, the skull must be resected between the left and right anterior clinoid processes. If the PPP flap does not cover the dural patch material, some of the dural sutures will be pushed outside the sterile epidural space, causing rhinorrhea and contamination. In some cases, a suturing technique may be required. For example, all threads should be passed through both the PPP flap and the dura and secured with forceps (Fig. 3B). The PPP flap is then dragged and applied to this suture point. This allows for more space to work around the prechiasmatic area and prevents needles from getting caught or dislodged in this small space. If a wide manipulating space was necessary, the final marginal tenting on each side at the most caudal position of the bone edge (red threads in Fig. 3A) could be performed after the work around the prechiasmatic area.

In other cases, the PPP flap might cover almost the entire area of the skull base and dural defects, but the lateral edges of the PPP flap (red dotted circle in Fig. 3C) create a large pathway through which air can pass. Suture closure of these edges with the dura toward the marginal tenting can close off this pathway. Finally, marginal tenting is extremely important to completely seal the space between the PPP flap and the dura, especially at the point closest to the skull base (red threads in Fig. 3A). Insufficient fixation of marginal tenting causes the dura to separate from the inner surface of the cranium, leaving a pathway for contaminated air to enter. Tying the PPP flap to both the outside of the cranium and the dura (red knots in Fig. 3D) should seal the thick edge of the cranial bone more firmly. The pedicle of the PPP flap at the most basal position might not be sutured anywhere; however, it is closely adhered to the outer surface of the cranium on the basal edge of the cranial opening, which is vascularized and entirely compressed by the skin flap, which cannot be peeled off after the flipped scalp is closed. This technique provides a hermetic and sterilized epidural space, where the PPP flap behaves as an airtight isolated shell cradling the free bone flap, which is particularly susceptible to infection. The main function of the bone autograft is prevention of encephalocele. It should be fixed with several sutures to the dura rather than the cranial base or PPP flap.

Grafts other than PPP flaps, such as the free rectus abdominis myocutaneous flap, can also compartmentalize the nasal and intracranial cavities; however, their application requires a high degree of skill and they are at risk of atrophic deformation because of muscle denervation.13 This could result in an air loophole at an inaccessible internal part and consequent infection of the bone autograft. Nevertheless, efforts to achieve airtight compartmentalization via the nasal space should be pursued as much as possible.8 In contrast, doing so via the epidural space is simpler and more familiar for neurosurgeons and can be achieved using PPP flaps. We recommend routinely using these flaps while sparing no effort to form multiple ramparts against the ingress of air into the intracranial space.

Lessons

Our technique has several limitations. The PPP flap is so thin and vulnerable that it might be impaired or shrink during a long operation. The periosteum should be divided as close as possible to the galea of the skin flap and stretched and secured with several sutures to the undersurface of the skin flap to prevent shrinkage until application. In practice, the PPP flap is for single use only. In case it is not intact enough to use, a galeal flap can be used as a substitute. However, such a flap must be so long and large that it could cause scarring and sensory disorders.

Our technique might also be applicable for patients with severe anterior skull base destruction caused by severe trauma or infection. However, it would be difficult to apply in cases in which olfactory nerve function must be preserved because the anchoring between the olfactory nerves and the cribriform plate interferes with placement of the PPP flap to cover the anterior skull base. In such cases, this technique should be arranged and limited so that it is applied only to the area required to achieve compartmentalization while preserving the cribriform plate. The exact degree and location of bony destruction might not be identified clearly until the craniotomy has been performed. However, individualized preoperative planning should be indispensable, considering that the PPP flap is intended for single use only.

For extensive anterior skull base defects, suturing the PPP flap to the dura and the bony edge while covering the dural suture line prevents air from entering the intracranial space from the nasal cavity and CSF from leaking in the opposite direction. Because use of a PPP flap cannot be repeated, the procedure must be performed correctly during the initial surgery to complete the anterior skull base reconstruction. The suturing technique we have described does not require expensive materials or special techniques.
