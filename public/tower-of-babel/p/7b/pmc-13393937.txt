Lipoprotein(a), insulin resistance, and cardiovascular outcomes in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease
Authors: Bingtian Dong, Yuping Chen, Fang He, Biao Li, Biao Wu, Zhengdong Chen, Enfa Zhao, Yongjian Chen, Chaoxue Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is associated with increased cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk. Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] and insulin resistance (IR) are established cardiovascular risk factors, yet their joint associations with cardiovascular outcomes in MASLD remain poorly understood.MethodsWe analyzed data from the UK Biobank and included 101,348 adults with MASLD for CVD mortality analyses and 94,089 individuals without baseline CVD for incident CVD analyses. IR was assessed using the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index. Participants were categorized according to Lp(a) levels (< 125 vs. ≥ 125 nmol/L) and TyG index (low vs. high, defined by the 75th percentile) and further classified into four joint Lp(a)/IR groups, with low Lp(a)/low IR serving as the reference group. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate associations of Lp(a), TyG, and their combined categories with incident CVD and CVD mortality.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 15.7 years, elevated Lp(a) and higher TyG levels were each independently associated with increased risks of incident CVD and CVD mortality, regardless of each other’s status. In the fully adjusted model, each SD increase in Lp(a) was associated with a 14% higher risk of CVD mortality (HR, 1.14; 95% CI 1.10–1.19; P < 0.001) and a 9% higher risk of incident CVD (HR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.07–1.11; P < 0.001). Similarly, each SD increase in TyG was associated with a 27% higher risk of CVD mortality (HR, 1.27; 95% CI 1.21–1.34; P < 0.001) and a 9% higher risk of incident CVD (HR, 1.09; 95% CI 1.07–1.12; P < 0.001). Compared with participants in the reference group with Lp(a) < 125 nmol/L and low IR, those with concomitantly elevated Lp(a) and high IR exhibited the highest cardiovascular risk, with adjusted HRs of 2.04 (95% CI 1.63–2.55) for CVD mortality and 1.49 (95% CI 1.35–1.64) for incident CVD (both P < 0.001), with a significant dose-response trend across groups (P for trend < 0.001). These associations remained consistent across subgroups stratified by age, sex, obesity, diabetes, and hypertension.ConclusionsElevated Lp(a) and IR were independently associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in MASLD, with the highest risk observed among individuals with concomitant elevations in both markers.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03294-x.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Cardiovascular disease, Lipoprotein(a), Insulin resistance, Triglyceride-glucose index

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide, affecting approximately one-third of adults and reflecting the systemic burden of metabolic dysfunction [1, 2]. The transition from nonalcoholic fatty liver disease to MASLD highlights its close links with insulin resistance (IR), obesity, and cardiometabolic disorders [3–5]. Importantly, cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of mortality in this population, underscoring the urgent need for improved risk stratification to better identify individuals at highest cardiovascular risk [6, 7].

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], a genetically determined and highly atherogenic lipoprotein, has emerged as a largely independent risk factor for CVD, contributing to residual risk beyond conventional lipid measures [8, 9]. Its association with cardiovascular risk has been consistently observed in individuals with and without diabetes [10]. In addition, both elevated and very low Lp(a) levels have been linked to impaired liver health in MASLD [11]. A recent study reported a paradoxical inverse relationship between Lp(a) levels and insulin sensitivity [12]. However, the clinical implications of this relationship in MASLD remain unclear.

IR is a central driver of cardiometabolic disease and contributes to both liver disease progression and increased cardiovascular risk [13]; its close association with MASLD is well established [14]. Accurate assessment of IR is therefore critical for risk stratification in MASLD. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, derived from fasting triglyceride and glucose levels, has emerged as a simple, cost-effective, and noninsulin-based surrogate marker of IR suitable for large-scale studies [15, 16]. Compared with the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp and the homeostasis model assessment of IR (HOMA-IR), TyG is more accessible, facilitating investigation of interactions between IR and lipid-related risk factors such as Lp(a).

The 2026 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association multisociety guidelines recommend measuring Lp(a) at least once in every adult for cardiovascular risk assessment [17]. The availability of the TyG index enables evaluation of the joint association of Lp(a) and IR with cardiovascular risk. We hypothesized that: (1) Lp(a) levels and IR, as measured by the TyG index, independently contribute to cardiovascular risk in adults with MASLD; and (2) the combined assessment of Lp(a) and IR improves risk stratification for cardiovascular outcomes. To test these hypotheses, we analyzed data from the UK Biobank, a large, contemporary population-based cohort in the United Kingdom.

Methods

Study design and population

The UK Biobank is a large prospective cohort study that enrolled over 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years between 2006 and 2010. Participants attended one of 22 assessment centers across England, Wales, and Scotland, where standardized physical examinations were conducted and biological samples were collected [18]. Baseline data were obtained through questionnaire interviews, anthropometric assessments, clinical examinations, and laboratory testing performed by trained personnel.

This study included participants with prevalent MASLD at baseline (application number: 217054). Participants with cancer, hepatitis B or C, alcohol-associated liver disease, or heavy alcohol consumption at enrollment were excluded. A total of 101,348 participants with MASLD were included in the analysis of CVD mortality. For analyses of incident CVD, participants with prevalent CVD at baseline were further excluded.

According to the latest consensus criteria [3], MASLD was defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis along with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, including overweight/obesity or waist circumference > 95th percentile, hypertension, prediabetes or type 2 diabetes, elevated triglycerides, or low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, in the absence of excessive alcohol consumption (> 30 g/day for men and > 20 g/day for women). Hepatic steatosis was identified using a fatty liver index (FLI) ≥ 60. The FLI was calculated based on body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, triglycerides, and gamma-glutamyltransferase levels and has been extensively validated as a reliable surrogate for imaging-based assessments, including ultrasonography and vibration-controlled transient elastography, with good diagnostic performance [19, 20]. Advanced liver fibrosis was defined as a Fibrosis-4 index > 2.67 [4], calculated using the following formula: age × aspartate aminotransferase/platelet count × (alanine aminotransferase)1/2].

Lp(a) measurement

Baseline serum Lp(a) concentrations (nmol/L) were measured using an immunoturbidimetric assay (Randox Laboratories, Crumlin, UK) on the Beckman Coulter AU5800 platform, based on the Denka Seiken method. The assay is traceable to the World Health Organization/International Federation of Clinical Chemistry reference material (SRM-2B) and demonstrates good agreement with this standard, with minimal bias even in individuals with large Lp(a) particles [8, 21]. A cutoff of ≥ 125 nmol/L was used to define elevated Lp(a) levels associated with increased cardiovascular risk [8, 22].

Assessment of IR

IR was assessed using the TyG index, calculated as ln [triglyceride (mg/dL) × plasma glucose (mg/dL)/2] [13]. At baseline, peripheral venous blood samples were collected from all participants using standardized procedures [23].

Assessment of main outcomes

The primary outcomes were incident CVD and CVD mortality. Incident CVD included stroke, ischemic heart disease, myocardial infarction, and heart failure. CVD mortality was defined as death primarily attributable to cardiovascular causes. In the UK Biobank, outcome events were ascertained through linkage to hospital inpatient records, primary care databases, and national death registries using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes (Supplementary Table S1). For CVD mortality, follow-up was censored at death or the end of follow-up. For incident CVD, follow-up was censored at the date of CVD diagnosis, death, or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. The censoring date for the UK Biobank was September 30, 2023.

Covariates

Based on prior epidemiological evidence and data availability, baseline covariates were selected as potential confounders, including age, sex, type 2 diabetes, and baseline CVD status. Type 2 diabetes was defined by any of the following criteria: ICD-10 code E11 identified through linked electronic health records, fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, self-reported physician-diagnosed diabetes, or use of antidiabetic medications. Hypertension at baseline was defined as self-reported hypertension, use of antihypertensive medications, a prior hospital diagnosis of hypertension, systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg at recruitment. Obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to assess the cross-sectional association between Lp(a) and TyG levels. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident outcomes in MASLD. Three models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3 was further adjusted for BMI, waist circumference, platelet count, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, albumin, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed by including interaction terms between exposure variables and follow-up time, and no violation was observed (both P > 0.05).

To examine potential non-linear associations between Lp(a) and TyG index with cardiovascular risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses based on Cox models were performed. Models with three to five knots were tested, and the optimal number of knots was selected according to the lowest Akaike information criterion. Log-rank tests were used to compare Kaplan–Meier Curves.

Participants were stratified by Lp(a) using a cutoff of 125 nmol/L, corresponding to a clinically relevant threshold for high cardiovascular risk. As no established clinical cutoffs exist for the TyG index, IR was defined using the 75th percentile (9.41) as the threshold in this cohort [8]. Participants were subsequently categorized into four groups according to Lp(a) and TyG status: Group 1, Lp(a) < 125 nmol/L with low IR; Group 2, Lp(a) ≥ 125 nmol/L with low IR; Group 3, Lp(a) < 125 nmol/L with high IR; and Group 4, Lp(a) ≥ 125 nmol/L with high IR. Baseline characteristics were summarized across the four groups. Subgroup analyses were performed according to age (< 60 or ≥ 60 years), sex (male or female), obesity status (yes or no), diabetes status (yes or no), hypertension status (yes or no), and advanced liver fibrosis (yes or no). All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1, R Foundation) and IBM SPSS (version 26.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 101,348 participants with prevalent MASLD were included in the analysis of CVD mortality, and 94,089 participants without baseline CVD were included in the incident CVD analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1). The mean age was 57.3 years, and 60.0% were male. At baseline, 52.4% had hypertension, 20.3% had diabetes, and 58.8% had obesity. During a median follow-up of 15.7 years, 2,442 (2.4%) participants died from CVD. A total of 15,811 (16.8%) incident CVD events were recorded.

Participants with concomitantly Lp(a) ≥ 125 nmol/L and high IR (Group 4) showed the highest prevalence of CVD mortality among the four groups (Table 1). Across Groups 1 to 4, the prevalence of CVD mortality increased progressively from 2.1% (1,409/67,304) to 3.7% (90/2,408), respectively. A similar increasing trend was observed for incident CVD across Groups 1 to 4, from 15.8 to 21.4%, respectively. Pairwise comparisons among the four groups are provided in Supplementary Table S2, with corresponding P values provided. Detailed baseline characteristics stratified by Lp(a) and IR categories are presented in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4. Spearman correlation analysis showed a weak inverse correlation between Lp(a) and TyG levels (r = − 0.08, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Independent associations of Lp(a) and IR with cardiovascular outcomes

The dose–response relationships between Lp(a), TyG index, and risks of CVD mortality and incident CVD are shown in Fig. 1. RCS analyses demonstrated significant linear associations between Lp(a) levels and both outcomes across all models (both P for overall < 0.001; both P for nonlinearity > 0.05). In contrast, TyG index showed significant non-linear associations with both outcomes (both P for overall < 0.001; both P for nonlinearity < 0.001), with cardiovascular risk increasing more steeply at higher TyG levels.

RCS analyses showing the associations of Lp(a) and TyG index with risks of CVD mortality and incident CVD across three models in MASLD. Panels A–C: Lp(a) and CVD mortality; D–F: TyG and CVD mortality; G–I: Lp(a) and incident CVD; and J–L: TyG and incident CVD (Models 1–3)

In both unadjusted and multivariable-adjusted Cox regression analyses, elevated Lp(a) and higher TyG levels were each independently associated with increased cardiovascular risk (Table 2). For each 1-SD increase in Lp(a), the fully adjusted HRs were 1.14 (95% CI 1.10–1.19; P < 0.001) for CVD mortality and 1.09 (95% CI 1.07–1.11; P < 0.001) for incident CVD. Similarly, each 1-SD increase in TyG index was associated with a 27% higher risk of CVD mortality (HR, 1.27; 95% CI 1.21–1.34; P < 0.001) and a 9% higher risk of incident CVD (HR, 1.09; 95% CI 1.07–1.12; P < 0.001). When analyzed categorically, Lp(a) ≥ 125 nmol/L and high IR were both associated with higher risks of CVD mortality and incident CVD compared with their respective reference groups across all models (both P < 0.001).

Associations of Lp(a) and IR with cardiovascular outcomes stratified by each other’s status

In analyses stratified by Lp(a), higher IR was consistently associated with increased risks of both CVD mortality and incident CVD, regardless of Lp(a) status (Table 3). In the Lp(a) < 125 nmol/L subgroup, high IR was associated with higher risks of CVD mortality (HR, 1.46; 95% CI 1.31–1.64; P < 0.001) and incident CVD (HR, 1.19; 95% CI 1.14–1.25; P < 0.001) in Model 3. Comparable associations were observed in the Lp(a) ≥ 125 nmol/L subgroup for CVD mortality (HR, 1.45; 95% CI 1.07–1.95; P = 0.015) and incident CVD (HR, 1.19; 95% CI 1.14–1.25; P = 0.004).

In analyses stratified by IR, the associations of elevated Lp(a) with cardiovascular outcomes varied across IR levels. In the low IR group, elevated Lp(a) was significantly associated with increased risks of both CVD mortality and incident CVD (both P < 0.001). In the high IR group, elevated Lp(a) remained significantly associated with CVD mortality (HR, 1.37; 95% CI 1.10–1.71; P = 0.005), whereas its association with incident CVD was no longer statistically significant after multivariable adjustment (HR, 1.14; 95% CI 0.88–1.48; P = 0.331). No significant interaction was observed between Lp(a) and IR for either outcome.

Cardiovascular outcomes across joint Lp(a) and IR categories

We further evaluated the joint associations of Lp(a) and IR with risks of CVD mortality and incident CVD in participants with MASLD (Table 4). Overall, participants with concomitantly elevated Lp(a) (≥ 125 nmol/L) and high IR exhibited the highest cumulative incidence of both outcomes, whereas those with low Lp(a) and low IR had the lowest incidence rates (Fig. 2, Panels E–F).

Discussion

Using a large population-based cohort, this study is the first to systematically evaluate the joint associations of Lp(a) and IR, assessed by the TyG index, with cardiovascular outcomes in adults with MASLD. We found that both elevated Lp(a) and higher IR were independently associated with increased risks of incident CVD and CVD mortality, irrespective of each other’s status. Notably, individuals with concomitantly elevated Lp(a) and IR had the highest cardiovascular risk. These associations remained largely consistent across multiple subgroups, including participants with obesity, diabetes, or hypertension.

CVD is the leading cause of death in MASLD, underscoring the importance of improved cardiovascular risk stratification [6, 7]. Although conventional risk assessment focuses mainly on traditional lipid markers, substantial residual cardiovascular risk persists beyond low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [24]. Lp(a) has emerged as an important genetically determined cardiovascular risk factor, and current guidelines recommend at least one lifetime measurement, with several Lp(a)-lowering therapies currently under clinical investigation [17, 25]. Elevated Lp(a) has been consistently associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in both primary and secondary prevention settings [8, 10, 26–28]. Recent evidence further supports Lp(a) as an important contributor to residual cardiovascular risk, showing a high burden of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and suboptimal low-density lipoprotein cholesterol target attainment despite intensive lipid-lowering therapy [29].

Previous studies have reported an inverse association between Lp(a) levels and liver fibrosis in MASLD. Pelusi et al. [30] demonstrated that lower Lp(a) levels were associated with increased liver stiffness measured by vibration-controlled transient elastography, and similar findings were observed in a biopsy-confirmed MASLD cohort [31]. Other studies have also reported reduced Lp(a) levels in individuals with advanced liver fibrosis, potentially reflecting impaired hepatic synthetic function as liver disease progresses [11, 30, 32]. Consistently, we observed lower Lp(a) levels in patients with MASLD and advanced liver fibrosis compared with those without advanced liver fibrosis, in line with previous evidence [31–33]. This phenomenon may be explained by progressive impairment of hepatic function in advanced liver disease. As Lp(a) is primarily synthesized and secreted by the liver and is largely genetically determined by the apolipoprotein(a) gene, advanced liver fibrosis may reduce hepatic synthetic capacity, leading to decreased Lp(a) concentrations. Accordingly, lower Lp(a) levels in advanced MASLD are more likely to reflect a consequence of disease progression rather than a causal factor [30].

Our findings demonstrated that Lp(a) ≥ 125 nmol/L was independently associated with increased risks of both CVD mortality and incident CVD compared with Lp(a) < 125 nmol/L, regardless of IR status. These results suggest that incorporating Lp(a) measurement into routine evaluation of MASLD may improve cardiovascular risk stratification and help identify individuals who may benefit from more intensive preventive strategies [32].

MASLD is characterized by profound metabolic dysfunction. IR is a central driver of atherosclerosis through multiple mechanisms, including hyperglycemia, chronic low-grade inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and atherogenic dyslipidemia [34]. The TyG index provides a simple, cost-effective, and widely applicable surrogate marker of insulin resistance that can be readily implemented in routine clinical practice [35]. In the present study, individuals with concomitantly elevated Lp(a) and TyG levels exhibited the highest risk of cardiovascular events. IR promotes atherosclerosis through multiple mechanisms, including inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and atherogenic dyslipidemia, while Lp(a) exerts pro-atherogenic, pro-inflammatory, and pro-thrombotic effects via its low-density lipoprotein-like structure and impaired fibrinolysis [8, 34]. Their coexistence may represent a convergence of metabolic and genetic risk pathways, leading to synergistic amplification of cardiovascular risk. These associations remained consistent across multiple subgroup analyses, underscoring the potential value of integrating lipid-related and metabolic biomarkers to improve cardiovascular risk stratification in MASLD. Interestingly, a Korean study reported that individuals with low Lp(a) levels and high IR had a greater risk of fatty liver disease compared with those with high Lp(a) levels and low IR, suggesting potentially opposite associations of Lp(a) and IR with fatty liver disease [36]. However, this discrepancy may be explained by important differences in study outcomes and assessment methods. That study focused on the risk of fatty liver disease rather than cardiovascular outcomes and assessed IR using the HOMA-IR, whereas our study evaluated cardiovascular outcomes in individuals with established MASLD using the TyG index as a surrogate marker of IR. It should be noted that Lp(a) plays a complex role in cardiometabolic health [37].

Notably, the association between concomitantly elevated Lp(a) and IR and cardiovascular risk differed according to advanced liver fibrosis status (defined as Fibrosis-4 index > 2.67). A significant association was observed in MASLD patients without advanced liver fibrosis, whereas no statistically significant association was found in those with advanced liver fibrosis. This heterogeneity may reflect differences in metabolic profiles and hepatic functional reserve across disease stages, as well as attenuation of Lp(a) levels in advanced liver dysfunction [30, 32]. However, fibrosis classification based on non-invasive blood-based scores may introduce potential misclassification, warranting further validation using histological confirmation or advanced imaging-based assessment. Despite relatively lower Lp(a) levels in patients with advanced liver fibrosis, cardiovascular risk remained substantially elevated. Across the overall MASLD population, individuals with concomitant elevations in Lp(a) and TyG consistently exhibited the highest cardiovascular risk. Collectively, these findings highlight the clinical value of combined Lp(a) and IR assessment in identifying high-risk MASLD patients who may benefit from more intensive cardiovascular prevention strategies.

Although not a primary objective of this study, we observed a weak inverse correlation between Lp(a) levels and IR in individuals with MASLD. Evidence regarding the relationship between Lp(a) and IR in MASLD remains limited; however, a previous study conducted in a broader general population also reported a similar inverse association between Lp(a) and IR [8]. Given prior evidence suggesting that Lp(a) levels and IR may have opposing associations with fatty liver disease [36], further studies are needed to clarify their relationship in MASLD [37]. Importantly, we found that elevated Lp(a) levels in individuals with MASLD and high IR were associated with a substantially higher risk of CVD.

A major strength of this study is the use of a large population-based cohort comprising more than 100,000 individuals with MASLD and a follow-up duration exceeding 15 years. This large sample size and long-term follow-up enabled us to systematically evaluate the associations of Lp(a) and IR with cardiovascular outcomes in MASLD. However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, despite demonstrating independent associations of Lp(a) and IR with cardiovascular risk, the observational nature of this study precludes causal inference. Second, both Lp(a) and the TyG index were assessed only once at baseline, preventing evaluation of longitudinal changes and their potential dynamic effects on cardiovascular outcomes in MASLD. Emerging evidence suggests that variability in IR may also influence cardiovascular risk [38]. Third, advanced liver fibrosis was identified using blood-based biomarkers/scores rather than vibration-controlled transient elastography or liver biopsy, which may have introduced misclassification bias. Fourth, detailed information on lipid-lowering and cardiometabolic therapies was not considered in the multivariable models, which may have introduced residual confounding. Future studies with more comprehensive treatment data are warranted to further validate our findings. Fifth, the UK Biobank predominantly includes individuals of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic populations. This is particularly relevant given the well-established racial and ethnic differences in Lp(a) concentrations and distribution patterns [39]. Future prospective studies in more diverse populations are needed to validate our findings and further clarify the causal relationships between Lp(a), IR, and cardiovascular outcomes in MASLD. Sixth, we lacked detailed measures of visceral adiposity, which may be more relevant than BMI or waist circumference in MASLD, potentially limiting adjustment for body fat distribution. Future studies incorporating direct assessments of visceral fat are warranted. Finally, we did not explicitly account for competing non-cardiovascular mortality during long-term follow-up, which may have influenced the observed associations. Future studies using competing risk models are warranted to address this issue.

Conclusion

Elevated Lp(a) and IR were independently associated with increased cardiovascular risk in individuals with MASLD, with the highest risk observed among those with concomitant elevations in both markers. These findings support integrated lipid-metabolic risk stratification in MASLD and warrant external validation in diverse prospective cohorts.
