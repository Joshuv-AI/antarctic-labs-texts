A Practical Surgical Technique for Removing a Transcatheter Pulmonary Valve
Authors: Hideto Ozawa, Kanta Araki, Kanji Ishizu, Koji Kagisaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a 22-year-old man with pulmonary atresia and an intact ventricular septum who presented with infective endocarditis after transcatheter pulmonary valve implantation with a Medtronic Harmony valve. Methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus was identified. Imaging revealed vegetations, severe valve dysfunction, multiple pulmonary emboli, and expansion with embedding of the distal stent flare at the pulmonary artery bifurcation. Despite antibiotic therapy, infection persisted, necessitating urgent surgery. A tourniquet-assisted purse-string technique with a protective outer tube was used to constrain the stent flare and enable safe explantation. Right ventricular outflow tract reconstruction was completed using a bioprosthetic valve and graft. The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful.

Since the first description of transcatheter pulmonary valve implantation (TPVI) by Bonhoeffer and colleagues1 in 2000, the technique has become a reliable alternative to open heart surgery for selected congenital heart disease patients with right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) dysfunction. The Medtronic Harmony valve is a self-expanding nitinol device featuring flared stent ends designed to anchor the valve from the RVOT to the pulmonary artery.2 Although TPVI reduces the need for repeat sternotomy, infective endocarditis remains a potentially life-threatening complication that may require surgical removal of the device.3 Here, we describe a patient with infective endocarditis after TPVI with the Harmony valve, who was successfully treated with surgical valve replacement using a tourniquet-assisted removal technique.

A 22-year-old man with pulmonary atresia and an intact ventricular septum underwent RVOT reconstruction with autologous pericardium in infancy. At age 22, the patient was determined to be a candidate for treatment for severe RVOT dysfunction. Additionally, the patient had severe atopic dermatitis. Therefore, transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement was chosen instead of open heart surgery. He received a heart valve replacement (TPVI) with a 25-mm Medtronic Harmony valve for severe RVOT regurgitation after 2 months of antibiotic treatment.

He presented 3 months later with fever and malaise. Blood cultures yielded methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus. Echocardiography revealed vegetations attached to the stent frame and the Harmony valve (Figures 1A, 1B). Chest computed tomography showed the distal end of the valve stent had expanded distally within the pulmonary artery bifurcation and appeared fixed to the pulmonary artery wall (Figures 1C, 1D), along with multiple pulmonary emboli (Figure 1E). Despite 2 weeks of antibiotic therapy, infection control was not achieved (Figure 1F), and surgery was indicated.

(C, D) Chest computed tomography demonstrates expansion and embedding of the distal stent flare at the pulmonary artery bifurcation. (E) Multiple pulmonary emboli were detected at presentation and (F) progressed despite 2 weeks of antibiotic therapy.

Before surgery, the tourniquet-assisted explantation technique for the Harmony transcatheter pulmonary valve was simulated. A purse-string suture was placed circumferentially around the proximal stent hooks (Figure 2A) and gradually tightened to constrain the flared proximal stent skirt (Figure 2B). An outer protective sheath was then advanced over the purse-string suture from the proximal side (Figure 2C). This maneuver enabled stepwise reduction of the device profile (Figures 2D, 2E), ultimately allowing the distal stent flare to be safely enclosed within the sheath (Figure 2F).

Simulation of the Harmony valve explantation. (A) First, a purse-string suture was placed around the proximal stent hooks. (B) Then, the tourniquet was tightened to constrain the flared proximal stent skirt. (C) An outer protective tube was advanced over the purse-string suture. (D, E) Controlled reduction of the distal stent flare's radial expansion improved exposure to the distal right ventricular outflow tract and pulmonary artery. (F) Finally, the distal stent flare was completely collapsed within the protective outer tube, allowing for controlled removal of the valve.

After redo median sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass was established. The pulmonary artery was incised longitudinally between the proximal and distal stent skirts. Purulent discharge was encountered, confirming infection. The incision was extended proximally to expose the RVOT, where the proximal stent skirt was densely adherent to the myocardium. A 2-0 polypropylene purse-string ligature with a tourniquet tube was applied around the proximal stent hooks, allowing controlled constriction of the flared proximal skirt and gradual retrieval of the valve into the protective outer tube. This maneuver provided adequate exposure and minimized tissue injury during explantation (Figures 3A, 3B).

(A, B) Intraoperative view shows tourniquet tube constraining the flared stent skirt of the Harmony valve. (C, D) The protective outer tube for retrieving the Harmony valve enabled effective visualization during dissection.

The outflow-side (distal) flare of the valve had become wedged and embedded within the thin and fragile pulmonary artery wall. Controlled retrieval using the tourniquet-assisted technique and protective outer sheath allowed safe visualization and careful dissection around the pulmonary artery bifurcation (Figures 3C, 3D). Vegetations were observed on both the inflow and outflow portions of the valve. Reconstruction was achieved using a 25-mm Carpentier-Edwards PERIMOUNT Magna Ease valve (Edwards Lifesciences) and a 26-mm Triplex woven vascular graft (Terumo Corp).

Postoperative targeted antibiotic therapy was continued for 8 weeks according to standard guidelines. The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful, and he remained free of infection at the 1-year follow-up.

Comment

This case highlights important considerations in the management of infective endocarditis after TPVI. Although surgical explantation of transcatheter pulmonary valves is often feasible using standard techniques, the unique design of the Harmony valve—with its self-expanding nitinol frame and flared stent skirts—can pose specific challenges in infected and friable tissues.

In particular, the distal stent flare may become partially embedded within the pulmonary artery wall, limiting visualization and increasing the risk of vascular injury during explantation. In such situations, uncontrolled radial expansion of the device may further compromise surgical exposure.

Our tourniquet-assisted technique allows controlled constraining of the proximal stent flare, which represents the widest portion of the device. By retrieving the valve into a protective outer sheath from the proximal side, the distal flare can subsequently be safely enclosed within the sheath, enabling controlled dissection and minimizing trauma to surrounding structures. This maneuver is not mandatory in all cases but may serve as a valuable adjunct in selected situations involving infected and fragile tissues.

Although previous reports have described general techniques for removing self-expanding valves,4 detailed guidance specific to the Harmony valve in the pulmonary position remains limited. The present technique offers a simple and reproducible approach that enhances surgical safety during explantation in complex cases.
