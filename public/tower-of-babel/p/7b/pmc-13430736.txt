Integration of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq profiling reveals an epigenetic regulator of anthocyanin synthesis in Prunus mume
Authors: Yi Tang, Man Wang, Ping Li, Baohui Li, Qin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe flower color is the key ornamental trait of Prunus mume (Mei) with a cultivation history of over 3,000 years in East Asia. Although the genetic regulation of pigment biosynthesis of P. mume has been partially characterized, the impact of chromatin accessibility on color development remains elusive.ResultsTo explore epigenetic mechanisms underlying anthocyanin synthesis, we utilized naturally color-varying cultivar P. mume 'Fuban Tiaozhi' as experimental material and conducted assays for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing, RNA sequencing, and functional validation of candidate genes. Transcriptome analysis identified that 17 structural genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis and constructed this pathway map in P. mume. ATAC-seq analysis further revealed the number of genes with chromatin accessibility was significantly higher in TZH than in TZB. We identified 93 differentially accessible motifs between TZB and TZH, which include bHLH, bZIP, C2H2, CDF, and MYB. Integrated RNA-seq and ATAC-seq analysis revealed one structural gene (PmLDOX1) and five differentially expressed TFs (one bHLH and four MYB), including PmMYB308.1. Notably, the chromatin openness of PmMYB308.1 in TZH increased compared to TZB, while the expression level of the gene decreased. Overexpression of PmMYB308.1 in tobacco significantly suppressed anthocyanin accumulation. In addition, based on the above analysis, we propose a potential correlation between PmMYB308.1 and the PmLDOX1 promoter.ConclusionsBased on ATAC-seq and motif analysis, increased chromatin accessibility of genes involved in the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis may promote pigment accumulation in TZH. Moreover, the downregulation of the negative regulatory gene PmMYB308.1 may also be an important contributing factor to anthocyanin accumulation in TZH petals. The results provide novel insights into the epigenetic regulation of anthocyanin synthesis in P. mume and establish a theoretical foundation and screening targets for investigating color traits in Rosaceae and other woody ornamental plants via chromatin accessibility analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09084-7.

Introduction

Flower color, a key ornamental trait in plants, primarily determined by the accumulation of pigments, including flavonoids, carotenoids, and betacyanin [5]. Among them, anthocyanins are crucial flavonoids contributing to coloration [15]. There are six most common anthocyanins commonly found in plants, which are cyanidin (Cy), peonidin (Pn), delphinidin (Dp), petunidin (Pt), malvidin (Mv), and pelargonidin (Pg) [47]. In addition, anthocyanins, as a natural edible pigment, are widely found in purple or blue fruits, vegetables, and flowers (Câmara et al., 2022). As a conserved branch of the phenylalanine biosynthetic pathway, the biosynthesis of anthocyanins involves numerous structural genes, including Chalcone synthase (CHS), Chalcone isomerase (CHI), Dihydroflavonol reductase (DFR), Flavanone-3-hydroxylase (F3H), Leucoanthocyanidin dioxygenase (LDOX), and UDP glucose flavonoid 3-glucosyltransferase (UFGT), etc. [27]. In the peach variety 'Sahong Tao', the differences in DNA methylation in the promoter region of the LDOX gene directly affect the synthesis and accumulation of anthocyanins by regulating the expression level of this gene, thereby determining the chimeric flower color phenotype with red and white mottling on the petals [51]. Using the BenthMap platform for localization, it was found that the premature stop codon and the G131R missense mutation in the LDOX gene of the Nicotiana benthamiana LAB strain are the fundamental reasons for its failure to accumulate anthocyanins (Ahmed et al., 2026). The expression of these structural genes is regulated by TFs. Many studies have demonstrated that MYB, bHLH, and WD40 are important TFs for anthocyanin biosynthesis, as well as the MBW ternary complex formed by these three TFs [18], [20]. The regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis by TFs has also been reported in other plants. Overexpression of MdMYB10 can induce the accumulation of anthocyanins in homologous and heterologous plants. Meanwhile, the co-expression of MdMYB10 with MdbHLH3 and MdbHLH33 promoted the biosynthesis of anthocyanins [14]. Additionally, anthocyanins can be transported in plants through glutathione S-transferase (GST) and smoothly transported to vacuoles by glutathione pumps [26].

The whole-genome sequencing of the P. mume has laid the foundation for the study of its flower color formation mechanism [56, 57, 59]. Previous studies on flower colors of P. mume have mostly focused on phenotype and anthocyanin content [9, 61]. In addition, there are only a few reports on the gene regulation mechanism of flower color synthesis regulation. It has been screened and identified the first TF PmMYBa1 that positively regulates anthocyanin synthesis from the genome of the P. mume [58]. Moreover, PmGSTF2 has been identified as crucial for anthocyanin accumulation, and PmMYBa1 can directly bind to the promoter of PmGSTF2 and activate its expression [39]. However, the epigenetic mechanisms controlling flower color synthesis in P. mume are still poorly understood. In order to better understand the mechanism of flower color formation, it is essential to conduct in-depth research in this area.

With the development of high-throughput sequencing, ATAC-seq has emerged as a powerful epigenetic technique. In ATAC-seq, sequencing adapters can be inserted into chromatin accessibility regions through the transposase Tn5 to explore chromatin accessibility. The obtained sequencing reads can be used to infer the regions with increased accessibility and to map the regions where TFs bind and nucleosome positions are located [6]. In recent years, due to its sensitivity and specificity, ATAC-seq has been widely used for identifying open chromatin in mammals, plants, and microorganisms [8, 23, 48]. For instance, the combined analysis of ATAC-Seq and RNA-Seq identified 9 cold-response TF genes, including VaRAV1 [41]. The chromatin accessibility related to apple gene expression under drought conditions has been analysed. It is found that a total of 9 TFs are differentially expressed at different chromatin accessibility motif binding sites during the response to drought, and participate in the response of apples to drought by regulating downstream genes [43]. However, ATAC-seq has not been applied to investigate the epigenetic mechanisms of flower color regulation. In this study, we have selected P. mume 'Fuban Tiaozhi' which can produce both white and pink flowers on the same branch as the experimental material and conducted a comprehensive analysis of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq to elucidate the differential regulatory mechanisms in anthocyanin biosynthesis. Through the identification of anthocyanin and flavonoid contents, the substances that affect the differences in flower color are initially determined. Moreover, combined with RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data, the changes in gene expression levels and chromatin openness are systematically studied. A PmMYB308.1 gene negatively correlated with anthocyanin synthesis was identified, suggesting a potential association with flower color variation in P. mume 'Fuban Tiaozhi'. Our findings provide novel epigenetic evidence insights into flower color regulation in P. mume at the chromatin level, paving the way for future breeding applications.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The fresh red petals (TZH) and white petals (TZB) were selected for sampling from the same branch of P. mume 'Fuban Tiaozhi' in Beijing Jiufeng National Forest Park (39°54′N, 116°28′E). The buds about to open and flowers in full bloom were selected for collection. Select flower buds that are about to bloom (TZH selects buds that are entirely pink and TZB buds are predominantly white, with some pink dots distributed on top.) and flowers during their peak flowering stage (select petals that were fully unfolded and could clearly observe the color of the petals) for collection (Fig. 1a, b). Determination of anthocyanin and flavonoid content

Firstly, prepare a 1% hydrochloric acid methanol solution: the ratio of hydrochloric acid to methanol was 1:99. Take 0.05 g of TZH and TZB, respectively, for grinding and place them in a centrifuge tube containing 5 mL of hydrochloric acid methanol mixture. Shake with ultrasound for 30 min, and then extract them in the dark at 4 ℃ for 24 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The absorbance values of each sample at 530 nm and 657 nm were determined by a G10S UV–VIS spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA) with 1% hydrochloric methanol solution as a blank control. Three biological replicates were set. The anthocyanin contents of TZH and TZB were calculated according to the formula (A530-0.25 × A657)/FW (FW is the fresh weight of the sample). Refer to Sawano for this measurement method [42].

The flavonoid content of TZB and TZH was determined by using the Plant Flavonoid Content Assay Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The samples were weighed and dried to constant weight, respectively, and then extracted by the ultrasonic extraction method at 60 ℃ for 30 min by adding 1 mL of 60% ethanol, and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Absorb the supernatant and volume it with 60% ethanol to 1 mL. We used the standard curve method of Rutin to calculate the total flavonoid content in the sample, prepared a standard solution of 10 mg/mL of rutin, and then diluted the solution to various concentrations required by the kit to establish a standard curve, and the obtained standard curve was y = 0.4421x-0.03567(R2 = 0.9704). We measured the absorbance value at 470 nm using a UV–VIS spectrophotometer and performed three repeated operations to obtain the average value, which was used to calculate the flavonoid content in the sample.

RNA extraction and library preparation

Sample total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer, and RNA degradation and contamination were detected on a 1% agarose gel. Measurements of RNA purity and integrity were performed by NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Science, Wilmington, DE) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). The obtained RNAs were used for the preparation of sequencing libraries, and these libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform to obtain 150 bp paired-end sequences. This sequencing included three independent biological replicates.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Raw data in fastq format were processed using fastp v.0.23.2 software (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) to remove sequences containing junctions [10], sequences containing poly-N, and low-quality sequences to obtain clean reads (clean data). Q20, Q30, GC content, and sequence repeat level were also calculated. The clean reads were mapped to the NCBI Rfam database to remove the rRNA sequences by using Bowtie2 v2.4.5 software (https://github.com/BenLangmead/bowtie2) [30]. After quality control, valid sequences were aligned to the P. mume reference genome (https://www.rosaceae.org/Analysis/13114608) using Hisat2 software [25], and known transcripts and predicted novel transcripts were identified from the alignment results using StringTie software [38]. Normalized by FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million fragments mapped) as a measure of transcript or gene expression level [46]. FPKM is calculated as follows: FPKM = cDNA Fragments/(Mapped Fragments (Millions)*Transcript Length(kb)). The FPKM value was only used for visualizing the gene expression level (such as heatmaps) to facilitate the intuitive comparison of expression trends among different samples. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 software [33], and Fold Change ≥ 1.5 and FDR < 0.05 were established as DEGs, while DEGs were annotated and enriched using the Genome-wide Gene Ontology (GO) [1] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases [24].

ATAC-seq library preparation

After extracting nuclei using density gradient centrifugation, library construction was performed using the Novizan TruePrep® DNA Library Prep Kit V2: tagmentation was carried out in the transposase system at 37 °C and 700 rpm in a metal bath for 30 min, followed by DNA purification using the Qiagen (28,006) kit, and PCR amplification according to the Vazyme TD202 kit (cycling conditions: 72 °C for 3 min, 98 °C for 30 s, and 10 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min). The amplified products were purified using VAHTSTM DNA Clean Beads magnetic beads, quality controlled and quantified with the Agilent 2100 and Qubit. This sequencing included three independent biological replicates. Libraries that passed quality control were subjected to paired-end sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (using the Novaseq × Series 25B Rgt Kit).

ATAC sequencing and analysis

The raw reads were filtered to remove adapters and low-quality reads using Cut adapt v1.8.3 to get high-quality clean reads in FASTQ format [36], and the high-quality reads were compared with the reference genome by Bowtie2 v2.2.4 to get information about the comparison efficiency of the sample reads and the position of the reads on the genome [29]. DeepTools v3.2.0 was used to map the distribution of the sequenced Read density in the upstream and downstream 3 kb intervals of the TSS for each gene, and the results were presented as heatmaps [40]. MACS2 v2.1.1 was used for peak extraction [60]. Functional annotation of genome-wide peaks was completed using ChIPseeker v1.2.6 [55]. The average number of peaks in the TSS region was calculated. Annotate the peak regions based on the distance relationship between the peak regions and the functional elements of each gene on the genome to determine whether the peak regions were located in the TSS, exons, 5'UTR, 3'UTR, introns, or intergenic regions. The proportions of Reads in different gene functional elements were calculated to demonstrate the distribution characteristics and patterns of reads.

We used MEME-ChIP 4.11.2 to identify and annotate Motifs enriched in the Peak region [35]. Motif identification was performed using MEME and DREME, and then the identified Motif sequences were compared and annotated with JASPAR (http://jaspar.genereg.net/) using Tomtom. Finally, the recognized Motifs were annotated to Peak by FIMO (find individual motif occurrences) (p < 0.0001).

We converted the number of reads in the gene promoter region into the reads Per Kilobase Per Million (RPKM) value to obtain the open value of each gene in the sample and understand the correlation between chromatin open level and gene expression level. Based on the previous methods [19, 32, 37], we classified chromatin openness into four levels: high (RPKM > 25), medium (5 < RPKM ≤ 25), low (1 < RPKM ≤ 5), and closed (RPKM ≤ 1). Finally, the gene expression levels and standard deviation of each sample in each of the four levels were calculated. The differential peaks between the two groups of samples were identified by DIFFBIND software [17, 22], normalized using the DESeq2 method, and an FDR < 0.05 was set as the significance threshold. The genomic features assigned to the peaks were annotated using ChIPseeker software [55]. Based on the published studies on plant ATAC-seq [32], the proximal peaks are defined as those located within 2 kb upstream of the transcription start site (TSS), to cover the promoter region,the distal peaks were defined as those ranging from 2 kb upstream of the TSS to 100 kb upstream, to cover possible distal regulatory elements. During gene assignment, the proximal peaks were directly annotated to the corresponding genes,the distal peaks were assigned based on the principle of proximity to the nearest gene. When multiple peaks were adjacent to the same gene, they were all included and corrected in the subsequent analysis. Then, we analysed and compared the influence of each peak at the proximal or distal end on the expression levels of downstream genes.

RT-qPCR validation of DEGs

Real-Time quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis was performed on 9 key DEGs related to anthocyanin synthesis based on transcriptome sequencing. TZB and TZH petals from the S1 period were extracted using OminiPlant RNA Kit (CwBio, Jiangsu, China) and HiScript®III 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription of the extracted RNA and then reacted with the 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix kit (US Everbright, Suzhou, China) on the QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR instrument. PmPP2A as an internal reference gene, using NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) to design primers (Table S6). The RT-qPCR procedure is as follows: 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles (95 °C 5 s, 60 °C 30 s). The relative expression level of each gene was analysed by the 2−∆ΔCT method. Three biological replicates were analysed for each sample.

Gene cloning and identification of transgenic Nicotiana tabacum

Based on the coding sequence (CDS) information of PmMYB308.1, the restriction endonuclease Nco I/BstE II was selected as the digestion site, and amplification primers were designed (Table S6). PCR amplification of PmMYB308.1 was performed using cDNA (cloned from TZH) as the template. The pCAMBIA1304 (stored in our laboratory) vector was subjected to a double enzyme digestion reaction using the above-mentioned enzymes, and the PmMYB308.1 fragment and the pCAMBIA1304 linearized vector were obtained through gel recovery. The obtained fragments are linked through a seamless clone (UE, Suzhou, China). The overexpression vector plasmid pCAMBIA1304-35S-PmMYB308.1 was transferred into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 by the freeze–thaw method. N. tabacum genetic transformation was carried out through the leaf plate method mediated by A. tumefaciens. Screening was performed on MS medium containing 50 mg/mL hygromycin. After flowering, the DNA of overexpressed transgenic N. tabacum corollas was extracted, and the positive seedlings were identified by PCR amplification and 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis detection.

Results

Analysis of the content of TZB and TZH pigments

We divided the obtained samples into two stages (Fig. 1b): S1 (bud stage) and S2 (flowers full opening stage). The anthocyanin content and flavonoid content of four samples from two stages were determined, respectively. The anthocyanin content in TZH S1 was 3.078 (Units g/FW) higher than that in white petal TZB of the same period, 0.405 (Units g/FW). In the S2 period, the anthocyanin content of TZH was 1.642 (Units g/FW), which was 6.49 times higher than that of TZB. The results of flavonoid content determination were consistent with the trend of anthocyanin content. During the S1 period, the flavonoid content was 43.431 (mg/g) in TZH and 31.367 (mg/g) in TZB. During the S2 period, the flavonoid content of TZH was 25.889 (mg/g), which was 2.18 times that of TZB (Fig. 1c, d). In TZH, the anthocyanin content in the S1 period was 1.76 times that in the S2 period, and TZB, it was 1.60 times, slightly less than that in TZH. In TZH, the content of flavonoids in the S1 period was 1.68 times that in the S2 period, and TZB, it was 2.66 times. We speculated that the reason for this phenomenon might be that more proanthocyanidins were synthesized during the formation of TZB (Fig. 1e, f). The above results indicated that the difference in pigment content was the main reason for the color difference between TZB and TZH. Moreover, the S1 phase was a critical stage for pigment accumulation. Therefore, we chose the S1 period for in-depth research.

Differentially expressed genes affecting flower color formation

To elucidate the gene regulatory mechanism of flower color variation in the P. mume 'Fuban Tiaozhi', we performed transcriptome sequencing of TZH and TZB petals from the S1 period. The six samples selected for analysis were compared against the P. mume genome. After quality control measures and raw data filtering, a total of 43.86 GB of clean data was obtained, with each sample achieving a clean data set of 6.81 GB. The percentage of Q30 bases was found to be 91.57% and above, while the GC content was determined to be between 45.85% and 46.26% (Table S1). According to the principal component analysis (PCA), the data set was divided into six distinct dimensions, with the PC 1 dimension accounting for 56.07% of the total variance, and the PC 2 dimension contributing 15.71% (Fig. S1a). The results of the Pearson correlation coefficients showed that the within-group coefficient was greater than 0.98, and the between-group coefficient was greater than 0.86. The parallelism observed among the samples conformed to the established criteria. There were significant differences between samples from the various groups (Fig. S1b).

As indicated by the RNA-seq data, a total of 4,035 DEGs were identified in TZH and TZB, encompassing 2542 up-regulated genes and 1493 down-regulated genes (Fig. S2, Table S2). Here, the number of up-regulated genes was approximately 1.7 times that of down-regulated genes. A total of 2,993 TFs were identified for DEGs, with a significant number belonging to the NAC, MYB, bHLH, AP2, and WRKY TF families (Fig. 2a). The MYB, bHLH, and WRKY TF families were recognized as playing pivotal roles in the regulation of the biosynthesis and of plant anthocyanins.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs were significantly enriched in several key pathways, including starch and sucrose metabolism, photosynthesis-antenna proteins, ABC transporters, and the biosynthetic pathways of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan (Fig. S3). Starch and sucrose metabolism as well as Photosynthesis-antenna proteins were closely related to the growth and development of P. mume ABC transporters could be involved in the transport of flavonoids, facilitating the accumulation of anthocyanins in plant vacuoles. Phenylalanine, serving as a precursor in the flavonoid synthesis pathway, was pivotal for downstream anthocyanin synthesis and was a significant factor in determining various flower colors. To facilitate a more profound investigation into the factors that gave rise to the emergence of TZB and TZH, a KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted on pathways that are closely associated with floral coloration (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, an in-depth analysis was undertaken on the DEGs obtained from this analysis to examine the structural genes that were implicated in the synthesis of anthocyanin pathways (Fig. 2c). These results indicated that 17 DEGs related to anthocyanin biosynthesis pathways had been identified, including two PmPAL, one PmC4H, one Pm4CL, one PmCHS, two PmCHI, one PmF3H, one PmF3’H, one PmDFR, two PmLDOX, two PmUFGT, three PmGST. Then, we constructed the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway heatmap based on the FPKM values (Fig. 2d).

This study results revealed significantly lower expression levels of the key upstream anthocyanin biosynthesis genes, including Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), Cinnamate 4-hydroxylase(C4H), 4-Coumarate: CoA ligase(4CL), and Chalcone synthase (CHS), in TZH compared to TZB. We speculated that these genes might be affected by feedback inhibition of the flavonoid pathway in plants during P. mume development [54]. The Chalcone isomerase (CHI) was a key enzyme in the biosynthesis pathway of anthocyanins and flavonoids, which induced Chalcone to Naringenin, and the formation of Chalcone and Anthocyanin was directly influenced by CHI. In our study, two CHI genes were identified through functional enrichment analysis. One gene had a high expression level and was believed to play an important role in anthocyanin formation, while the other gene had a low expression level and had little impact on anthocyanin synthesis. An increase in the level of flavonoid 3'-hydroxylase (F3'H), which catalyzed the conversion of dihydrokaempferol to dihydroquercetin, was observed. This reaction provided the essential substrate for downstream anthocyanin synthesis. LDOX, a key enzyme in the anthocyanin synthesis pathway, was responsible for converting colorless anthocyanins into colored anthocyanins. In this study, two LDOXs were found to be expressed at significantly higher levels in TZH than in TZB. At the same time, the LAR and ANR gene expressions in TZH were reduced, which made fewer substances flow to the proanthocyanidin synthesis pathway, and promoted the accumulation of anthocyanidins in TZH. Additionally, anthocyanins were usually transported by transporters to vacuoles for storage or separation. GST played a crucial role in the transportation of anthocyanins. In this study, the upregulation of GST gene expression facilitated the smooth accumulation of anthocyanins in TZH. The above findings suggested that the variation in flower color between TZH and TZB was primarily attributed to differences in the expression levels of genes downstream of the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway.

Within the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway, the expression of structural genes was regulated by TFs, mainly including MYB, bHLH, and WRKY transcription factors [49]. In this study, we analysed the TFs of metabolic pathways related to flavonoid biosynthesis and correlated the above key structural genes of the anthocyanin synthesis pathway with the three families of TFs, MYB, bHLH, and WRKY. Based on the STRING database, we selected those TFs and structural genes with r > 0.9 and p < 0.01 to construct a correlation network diagram. The result of this process was the identification of a total of 20 MYB, 18 bHLH, and 8 WRKY TFs (Fig. 2e, Tables S3, S4). Concurrently, an analysis of the protein interaction networks of the aforementioned TFs was conducted, revealing the presence of interaction relationships among them (Table S5). These relationships might potentially regulate the synthesis of anthocyanins.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

To verify the credibility of transcriptome sequencing data, we selected 9 genes related to the anthocyanins biosynthesis pathway for validation and analysed their expression levels using RT-qPCR (Fig. 3, Table S6). Among them, the RT-qPCR results showed that the expression trends of the 9 selected genes were consistent with those obtained by the RNA-Seq method, indicating that using RNA-Seq for counting reads was reliable and could be used for further analysis.

The identification of peaks within the ATAC-seq libraries was conducted, with a total of 67,335 to 68,516 peaks being identified across six libraries. The relative positions of the peaks indicated that they were mainly distributed in the high-incidence area of chromatin opening (0–2 kb in the promoter region), accounting for 54.71%−55.49% of their peak distribution. Approximately 15% of the peaks were distributed at 2–3 kb in the promoter region, around 13% of the THS were distributed in the exon and intron regions, and 17% of the peaks were distributed in the distal intergenic region (Fig. 4b). As the distance increases, the signal gradually weakens, which was similar to the results reported in other studies. We used fold change ≥ 1.5 and p-value < 0.05 as differential screening criteria to identify a total of 2817 DARs, including 1496 increased DARs and 1321 decreased DARs (Fig. S8, Table S8). To further analyze the impact of DARs on the appearance of TZH, we mapped a total of 1788 annotated differential peak-associated genes. We performed KEGG functional enrichment analysis of the samples and observed the presence of both up-regulated and down-regulated genes in phenylalanine biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and anthocyanin biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 4c). The results of the enrichment analysis indicated that the differences in floral coloration between TZH and TZB may be related to the changes in chromatin accessibility.

Motif enriched in regions of differential chromatin accessibility

The representative motifs enriched in the peak region identified in the ATAC library were used to predict potential TFs in this region. There were peaks at specific locations with different signal values. A comprehensive analysis of all peak motifs of TZB and TZH revealed 1200 motifs among a total of 17,301 position-specific peaks, identifying a total of 93 differentially expressed motifs. The analysis revealed that the bHLH family was the most prevalent TF-binding motif, with a total of 42 instances identified. This was followed by the bZIP, C2H2, CDF, and MYB families (Fig. 4d, Table S9). Among these motifs with significant differences in chromatin openness, based on previous related studies, we had focused on the TFs that potentially regulate anthocyanin synthesis (Fig. 4e, Fig. S9). The protein interaction network of PIF3 was analysed through the String database. The results indicated that PIF3 could interact with other TFs (such as HY5) (Fig. 3f, Table S10). HY5 could directly bind to the promoters of genes related to anthocyanin synthesis [53], such as CHS, CHI, and DFR, promoting the transcription of these genes. This further promoted the synthesis of anthocyanins. This synergistic effect might indirectly regulate the expression of anthocyanin synthesis genes. These results suggested that the above-mentioned TFs might play important roles in the synthesis process of anthocyanin.

To enhance comprehension of the correlation between RNA-seq and ATAC-seq, we converted the reads in the promoter region of the genes to RPKM values to obtain the gene activity levels of the samples and categorized the gene activity levels into four grades. There was a positive correlation between chromatin openness and gene expression levels (Fig. 5b). The number of genes with chromatin openness were significantly higher in TZH than in TZB. Functional validation of the effect of PmMYB308.1 on anthocyanin synthesis

To deeply explore the influence of PmMYB308.1 on flower color formation, we isolated it from TZH petals and performed heterologous overexpression in N. tabacum. Subsequently, we examined transgenic plants at three flower developmental stages: bud stage, semi-open stage, and full stage. Phenotypic analysis showed that compared to wild-type, overexpression of PmMYB308.1 resulted in the corolla of transgenic plants transition from red to white, starting from the flower bud stage until full opening. Consistent with this phenotype, the anthocyanin content was extremely low during these stages (Figs. 6b, c, d). Meanwhile, RT-qPCR was used to analyse the expression of anthocyanin synthesis-related genes in transgenic positive plants and wild-type with empty vector corolla. Compared with wild-type, overexpression of PmMYB308.1 could inhibit the expression of anthocyanin synthesis-related structural genes (NtCHS, NtCHI, NtDFR, NtANS, NtUFGT) and anthocyanin synthesis regulatory genes (NtAN1a, NtAN1b) in the corolla of transgenic tobacco to varying degrees (Fig. 6e). Among them, the down-regulation amplitudes of NtDFR and NtANS were significant. These results indicated that the overexpression of this gene had an inhibitory effect on the expression of anthocyanin biosynthesis-related genes, causing the corolla of transgenic tobacco to fade to white.

PmMYB308.1 affects the synthesis of anthocyanins

Overexpression of PmMYB308.1 could reduce the anthocyanin content in N. tabacum and inhibit the expression of related structural genes in the anthocyanin pathway. Since the flower color difference between TZH and TZB was mainly influenced by the anthocyanin content, we hypothesize that PmMYB308.1 might affect the structural genes involved in the anthocyanin biosynthesis process of P. mume, thereby influencing the synthesis of anthocyanins and leading to the emergence of phenotypic differences (Fig. 7). Discussion

Anthocyanins belong to flavonoid compounds and are water-soluble natural pigments. As an important class of secondary metabolites in plants, they can endow plants with color and play a crucial role in plant pollination and dissemination [28, 62]. P. mume 'Fuban Tiaozhi' has high ornamental value due to its unique "versicolor branch" phenomenon, and its color differences are mainly determined by the different anthocyanin contents. Given that anthocyanins are the key pigments affecting flower color, studying their biosynthesis and regulatory mechanisms was of great significance for revealing the formation mechanism of P. mume color traits and promoting the development of molecular breeding techniques based on color improvement.

The anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway is a highly conserved metabolic pathway in the plant kingdom. The structural genes involved in anthocyanin synthesis are mainly divided into early anthocyanin synthesis genes (EBG) and late anthocyanin synthesis genes (LBG) [12, 45]. Among them, CHI, F3H, and F3'H in EBG are mainly responsible for the synthesis of flavonoids, while DFR, LDOX, and UFGT in LBG mainly undertake the synthesis and modification of anthocyanins. In this study, the expression level of PmCHI in TZH was found to be higher than that in TZB, which is consistent with the research results in Ophiorrhiza japonica [44], indicating that PmCHI might promote the synthesis of anthocyanins in P. mume. Studies have shown that CmF3'H in Chrysanthemum plays an important role in anthocyanin accumulation, and CmF3'H is the most important enzyme required for cyanidin biosynthesis [21]. The high expression of F3'H in TZH might promote the synthesis of anthocyanins in P. mume.

In recent years, the application and development of ATAC-seq technology in plants have promoted research on chromatin openness in the entire plant genome [7, 34]. For example, under red light treatment conditions, Fatip1 can respond to red light induction and promote the ripening process of strawberry fruits [11]. In this study, we identified 2817 DARs from TZH and TZB, indicating that the color differences between TZH and TZB might be due to differences in chromatin openness. However, the distribution of chromatin accessibility peaks between TZH and TZB was similar, with most peaks located in the promoter region upstream of TSS. Based on ATAC-seq data, we identified differential members of the bHLH, bZIP, C2H2, and MYB TF families, providing direction for further exploration of key regulatory factors affecting flower color formation. PhASR1 has been demonstrated to regulate the expression of genes associated with anthocyanin synthesis, thereby affecting the process of anthocyanin synthesis [16].

In ATAC-seq, we annotated a total of 1788 differential peak-associated genes. In RNA-seq, we identified 4035 DEGs, which is 2.26 times higher than the differential peak-associated genes identified in ATAC-seq. This might be because the expression levels of some genes changed without a corresponding alteration in their chromatin accessibility. The joint analysis of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data could better clarify the relationship between chromatin openness and gene expression levels. We had identified a gene, PmLDOX1, that was highly expressed in the anthocyanin synthesis pathway in TZH. LDOX, also known as ANS, plays an important role in coloring plant flowers and fruits [2, 50, 52]. The absence of anthocyanin pigmentation in the white accession of Punica granatum is due to fails PgLDOX transcript accumulation, thereby blocking the downstream anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway. [4]. Through gene expression and predictive analysis, we further speculated that PmLDOX1 may play an important role in the process of TZH flower color synthesis.

Numerous studies have shown that the R2R3-MYB transcription factor plays a critical role in anthocyanin biosynthesis [13, 43]. Currently, MYB TFs that can promote the biosynthesis of anthocyanins in P. mume have been identified. For example, PmMYB1 can promote the accumulation of anthocyanin content in N. tabacum by activating the anthocyanin synthesis structural genes CHS, CHI, F3H, F3'H, DFR, ANS, and UFGT [57, 59]. However, there have been no reported studies on MYB inhibitory genes related to anthocyanin synthesis in P. mume. In this study, we successfully cloned and isolated PmMYB308.1 from P. mume petals. Amino acid sequence alignment showed that PmMYB308.1 was an R2R3-MYB TF, clustered with anthocyanin synthesis inhibitors from other species, such as peach, apple, strawberry, etc. The grouping of Arabidopsis thaliana R2R3-MYB gene family SG4 family is mainly based on their functional and conserved motif differences and is usually divided into FaMYB1 type and AtMYB4 type [3]. FaMYB1 type can regulate anthocyanin synthesis in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, while AtMYB4 type participates in lignin biosynthesis metabolism to regulate phenylalanine synthesis. Phylogenetic analysis showed that PmMYB308.1 was a FaMYB1-type TF. Through the stable transformation of N. tabacum, we found that overexpression of PmMYB308.1 could cause the N. tabacum corolla to turn white, indicating that PmMYB308.1 could negatively regulate anthocyanin synthesis. The results of RT-qPCR showed that the expression levels of anthocyanin-related synthesis genes in transgenic N. tabacum were significantly reduced. This result indicated that it might be involved in the regulation of the synthesis of anthocyanin in P. mume, but its exact role needs to be further validated through homologous systems or direct interaction experiments.

This study observed that the distal enhancer region of the PmMYB308.1 locus shows increased chromatin accessibility in red petals, yet its transcript abundance decreased, a seemingly contradictory phenomenon that warrants further investigation. This phenomenon might result from the asynchrony between chromatin opening of the distal enhancer and transcriptional activation: although the region became nucleosome-depleted and chromatin-open under the action of pioneer transcription factors, the enhancer could not effectively recruit the Mediator complex and co-activators due to the absence of specific transcriptional activators recognizing the enhancer in the cell, or because the relevant signaling pathways were not activated. Consequently, it could not establish functional interactions with the target gene promoter through chromatin looping, ultimately preventing RNA polymerase II from forming a stable transcription initiation complex at the promoter, leading to decreased gene expression. In addition, other possibilities cannot be ruled out, such as the open region corresponding to a silencer or repressor binding site, or the existence of miRNA-mediated degradation. This phenomenon suggested that in follow-up studies, it will be necessary to combine histone modification profiling (such as H3K27ac and H3K27me3) and chromatin conformation capture techniques to further dissect the regulatory properties of this region.

Based on the above findings, we speculated that PmLDOX1 and PmMYB308.1 might be candidate genes related to the occurrence of TZH and were involved in flower color formation. They also provided target genes for the molecular design and breeding of P. mume, offering a new idea for the improvement of flower color. However, the specific mechanism of action between these two genes still needs to be further investigated.

Conclusions

Overall, by integrating transcriptome and chromatin accessibility analyses, we identified one structural gene (PmLDOX1) and five differentially expressed TFs (one bHLH and four MYB). The chromatin openness of distal intergenic of PmMYB308.1 in TZH increased, while the expression level of the gene decreased. Furthermore, transgenic experiments had demonstrated that PmMYB308.1 was associated with reduced anthocyanin accumulation, suggesting a potential negative role in anthocyanin biosynthesis. Its downregulation might promote anthocyanin accumulation in TZH petals. In addition, based on the current sequencing data and functional validation results, we had established a working hypothesis: there might be an interaction between PmMYB308.1 and the PmLDOX1 promoter.
