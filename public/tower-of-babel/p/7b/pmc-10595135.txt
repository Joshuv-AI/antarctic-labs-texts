Tandem cranial and spinal cerebrospinal fluid leaks presenting with otogenic tension pneumocephalus: illustrative case
Authors: Dominic Chau, Zachary R Barnard, Thomas J Muelleman, Adam M Olszewski, Anna K D’Agostino, Marcel M Maya, Peyton L Nisson, Kevin A Peng, Wouter I Schievink, Gregory P Lekovic

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks are a heterogenous group of disorders that result in ectopic drainage of CSF. CSF draining through tegmen defects will commonly present as either CSF otorrhea or rhinorrhea, whereas spinal CSF leaks commonly form fistulae directly into paraspinal soft tissue or epidural veins.1 Importantly, cranial and spinal CSF leaks are associated with opposite CSF fluid dynamics. Cranial CSF leaks, especially CSF otorrhea, are associated with intracranial hypertension. Sustained or transient intracranial hypertension, most commonly idiopathic intracranial hypertension or obstructive sleep apnea, is found in a high proportion of patients with CSF otorrhea. Patients with CSF otorrhea also commonly have imaging findings characteristic of elevated intracranial pressure, such as an empty sella and venous sinus stenosis or other venous outflow obstruction. In contrast, spinal CSF fistulae are associated with spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH),2 which has an annual incidence of 5 cases in 100,000 individuals.3 The primary clinical symptom of SIH is orthostatic headaches. In one study of 90 patients with SIH, orthostatic headache was present in 67 patients (75%).4 Other clinical manifestations include pain or stiffness of the neck, nausea, emesis, horizontal diplopia, dizziness, change in hearing, visual blurring or visual field cuts, photophobia, interscapular pain, and occasionally face numbness or weakness or radicular upper-limb symptoms.5 Imaging findings characteristic of SIH, such as “brain sag,” are commonly identified in these patients.

Although intracranial hypertension is thought to be the cause of CSF leaks via tegmen defects, the cause of spinal CSF leaks has not been thought to be due to sustained elevated intracranial pressure and is most commonly idiopathic or associated with an underlying connective tissue disorder. The differing pathophysiologies between spontaneous cranial and spinal CSF leaks are diametrically opposed and, therefore, mutually exclusive in theory. Here, we report a case of spontaneous type 3 spinal CSF leak with clinical presentation of tension pneumocephalus. Pneumocephalus typically occurs secondary to trauma, infection, or tumors. We sought to illustrate the clinical and radiographic presentation including CSF venous fistula appearance on a variant of conventional myelography.

Illustrative Case

History and Physical Examination

A 66-year-old female presented to an outside institution with a 1-week history of left-sided sensation of crackling in her ear and headache worse when lying down. She denied symptoms of otorrhea, rhinorrhea, or hearing loss. Her only prior surgery was a small right-sided burr hole craniotomy for evacuation of a hematoma after being hit in the head many years prior. She underwent computed tomography (CT) scanning of her head, which revealed diffuse pneumocephalus as well as left-sided pneumocephalus with a large volume of air directly above a bony defect of the tegmen tympani and mastoideum. There was no fluid in the middle ear or mastoid. Repeat imaging at the outside facility showed progression of air, and the patient was transferred to our institution with the diagnosis of tension pneumocephalus (Fig. 1A and B).

Axial CT showing subdural diffuse pneumocephalus with a left temporal region of pneumocephalus with mass effect (A). Coronal image demonstrating left temporal pneumocephalus with mass effect and continuity to the mastoid through a defect in the tegmen mastoideum (B).

Surgical Procedure and Postoperative Follow-Up

The patient underwent a left middle fossa craniotomy for repair of the tegmen tympani CSF leak. General anesthesia was induced, and the patient’s head was rotated to the right in three-point pin fixation. The area was shaved, facial nerve monitoring electrodes were placed in the frontalis, orbicularis oculi, and orbicularis oris muscles, and a small reverse question mark incision was outlined. Scalp and temporalis muscle flaps were dissected separately and reflected anteriorly and were held in place with a self-retaining cerebellar retractor. Prior to reflecting the temporalis flap, a small 3 × 3 cm temporalis fascia graft was harvested. Using a high-speed drill and cutting burr, we performed a 4 × 4 cm middle fossa craniotomy.

The dura was elevated off the middle fossa floor to expose two bony tegmen defects. The dura was then opened with a no. 11 blade and tacked up with a 4–0 Nurolon suture. Intradural inspection revealed two small holes, which correlated with the two bony tegmen defects. We placed a dural graft substitute intradurally to cover the middle cranial fossa. The temporalis fascia graft was placed extradurally over the tegmen defects. Postoperatively, interval CT scans showed a progressive decrease in the volume of her pneumocephalus.

In the week after discharge, the patient developed a recurrence of positional headaches and underwent head magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which demonstrated bilateral subdural hematomas (SDHs) with no residual pneumocephalus (Fig. 2A and B). This constellation of imaging findings and clinical history raised the suspicion for intracranial hypotension and a spinal CSF leak. Further MRI of the brain and thoracic spine showed bilateral SDHs and multiple meningeal diverticula (Fig. 3A).

Coronal gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted MRI (A) and axial T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) MRI (B) demonstrating bilateral, left greater than right, SDHs with mass effect and left to right midline shift.

Magnetic resonance myelogram showing multiple meningeal diverticula (A). Digital subtraction myelogram showing thoracic CSF-venous fistula at T11–12 on the left (B).

The patient then underwent digital subtraction myelography, which identified multiple meningeal diverticula and a thoracic CSF venous fistula. The thoracic CSF venous fistula arose from the left T11–12 foramen and extended superiorly to the left T10–11 foramen (Fig. 3B). The patient was taken to the operating room for a left-sided T11–12 foraminotomy for ligation of the CSF venous fistula. After placing her prone and making a small 2- to 3-cm midline incision, we performed a subperiosteal muscle dissection to expose the T11 lamina. Using the AM8 drill, a hemilaminectomy and foraminotomy were performed to expose the exiting T11 nerve root. A Yasagril straight 7-mm aneurysm clip was then placed along its most proximal portion near the origin of the spinal cord. Special care was taken to avoid exposing too laterally and disrupting the facet joint during the foraminotomy. Throughout the procedure, neuromonitoring remained stable with no changes in somatosensory evoked potential or motor evoked potential signals. By the following day, she already had significant improvement in her orthostatic headaches. By postoperative day 3, the patient was discharged home neurologically intact.

At the 3-, 6-, and 12-month postoperative visits, there was no recurrence of symptoms, and MRI of the brain showed significant reduction of her SDHs (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Observations

This is the first case report of a “tandem” CSF leak involving a spontaneous spinal CSF leak and simultaneous tegmen defect. The patient presented with otogenic tension pneumocephalus instead of CSF otorrhea, the significance of which we initially did not appreciate and only understood after repair of her tegmen defect was followed by clear evidence of SIH including bilateral SDHs and brain sag. This case reinforces our prior observation that cranial CSF leaks in general do not cause SDH.6 Spontaneous otogenic pneumocephalus has been reported less than 40 times in the literature7,8 and is thought to occur secondary to thinning of the skull base including the tegmen tympani and mastoideum. It is therefore possible that the coexistence of cranial and spinal CSF leaks, while still very rare, is underappreciated.

In any event, our patient developed SIH secondary to her spinal CSF leak, and this led to tension pneumocephalus. Whether or for how long she had a preexisting but unnoticed tegmen defect is unknowable, but she had no imaging or clinical stigmata of underlying intracranial hypertension, nor did she provide a history of chronic serous otitis media. Our case raises interesting questions about the apparent dichotomous etiology of cranial and spinal CSF leaks, such that at least in this case the two phenomena appear to share a common mechanism (perhaps an undiagnosed connective tissue disorder). Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for spinal CSF leak whenever pneumocephalus is found, and we recommend close postoperative follow-up and clinical monitoring for symptoms of intracranial hypotension in any patients who undergo repair of a tegmen defect for otogenic pneumocephalus.

Lessons

Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for spinal CSF leak whenever pneumocephalus is found. Close postoperative follow-up and clinical monitoring for symptoms of intracranial hypotension in any patients who undergo repair of a tegmen defect for otogenic pneumocephalus is recommended.
