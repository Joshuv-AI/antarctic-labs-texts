Innovation breakthrough in the Alzheimer’s disease pharmaceutical industry
Authors: Hua Cheng, Leyi Kang, Yulong Xu, Rudolph E. Tanzi, Can Zhang, Changning Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) drug development has undergone cycles, driven initially by amyloid-β–based hypotheses and later setbacks by clinical failures. Recent advances in biotechnology, improved understanding of AD pathogenesis, and unmet medical needs have revitalized the field. This new wave is exemplified by lecanemab, the first fully FDA-approved disease-modifying therapy in two decades. We review approved and clinical-stage AD therapeutics using data from Cortellis and the FDA.

Over the past decades, the development of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) therapeutics has experienced intense progress and setbacks1,2. A surge of drug development has initially followed academic discovery of the amyloid-β precursor protein (AβPP) and associated hypotheses like the amyloid cascade, which has led to the development of targeted therapeutics reducing amyloid-β pathology, a hallmark in Alzheimer’s brains3. However, subsequent clinical failures have brought AD drug development into a prolonged trough. Recently, advances in biotechnology, in combination with new insights into improved understanding of disease pathogenesis, and the urgent medical need due to increasing aging populations, have reignited the momentum of drug discovery4–6. This led to another AD drug discovery wave, highlighted by Lecanemab, the first fully FDA-approved AD drug and an effective disease-modifying therapy identified in two decades7. Moreover, recent findings have prompted us to assess the current landscape and outcomes of AD drug development, which may help evaluate clinical drugs that are potentially on the verge of approval.

Particularly, we analyzed data on approved and clinical-stage innovative AD therapies listed on Cotellis datasets and the official FDA website up to August 2025. Approval information for some marketed drugs was obtained from public resources such as the FDA. Detailed data and analyses are provided in the Supplementary Information. All charts and graphs were generated using Microsoft Office Excel.

Launched drugs and clinical drugs

As shown in Fig.1b and Table S1, AD treatment drug development has progressed in distinct waves. The first AD drug approved by the FDA in 1993 initiated a decade of acetylcholinesterase (AChE)-targeted drugs, with five drugs (50%) approved between 1993 and 2003. However, these drugs did not alter the AD progression and temporarily slowed cognitive and functional decline. Drug approvals then entered a 20-year stagnation. GV-971 was launched but later withdrawn. Aducanumab (2021) was approved by the FDA, making it the first new drug for AD in two decades, but discontinued soon. A major milestone was reached in 2023 with the approval of Lecanemab, the first fully approved disease-modifying antibody targeting AβPP, followed soon after by Donanemab. Notably, 40% of all launched AD drugs were approved after 2020, marking the transition from a long dormant phase to rapid growth. Each surge in approvals has coincided with advances in therapeutic mechanisms and biotechnology. Interestingly, diagnostic drug development peaked during therapeutic stagnation (2010–2014), when three drugs were approved, representing 60% of all diagnostic drugs to date.

As shown in Fig.1c and Table S2, a total of 213 AD-related drugs were identified: 22 in Phase III, 86 in Phase II, 97 in Phase I, and 8 just as INDs. The majority of compounds are in early phases (I and II), consistent with the overall distribution pattern typically seen in drug development (Fig. 1c). Unlike the trend of approved drugs, the largest number emerged between 2010 and 2020, accounting for 53.68% of all clinical-stage drugs (Fig. 1d). Although numbers have declined slightly since 2020 (2025 has not yet fully compiled), they have remained stable. This is because while few AD drugs were approved between 2010 and 2020, there were significant breakthroughs in diagnostic drugs and biotechnological advances, particularly in antibody-based therapies. At the same time, growing market demand and a surge in overall investment in drug development contributed to maintaining a steady number of AD drugs in clinical development during that decade. This sustained momentum ultimately laid the foundation for the therapeutic breakthroughs observed after 2020.

Targets analysis of therapeutic and diagnostic drugs

To provide an integrated view of AD targets, we performed a combined statistical analysis of diagnostic and therapeutic targets. As shown in Fig. 2a, approved launched drugs mainly target AβPP and Tau aggregates, and acetylcholinesterase (AChE). However, clinical-stage drug targets show a different distribution, with the top three targets being Amyloid/AβPP, Tau, and inflammation (Fig. 2b and Table S3). Drugs targeting Amyloid/AβPP represent 23.47%, followed by Tau, which reflects continued emphasis on clearing pathological protein aggregates. Notably, inflammation and immune-related pathways appear at a similar proportion to Tau, emphasizing the rising importance of the inflammation hypothesis in next-generation AD therapies. Additionally, targets related to signaling pathways, cell therapy, and others also hold a notable share. Sponsoring organizations of approved AD drugs and AD drugs in clinical

Among approved AD drugs, all therapeutics except GV971 are owned by Top 500 companies (Fig. 3a and Table S1). By contrast, most diagnostic drugs (80%) are developed outside Top pharma, owing to lower investment needs. Analysis shows that Phase III drugs are most often supported by large firms, with 45% of funding from Top 100 (30%) and Top 500 (15%) companies. This share decreases from Phase III to Phase I, with no pre-registration drugs originating from Top 100 firms, because large companies typically join later stages to provide critical funding and trial expertise to advance promising candidates. In early phases, small and medium-sized enterprises play a major role, owing to their importance as sources of novel approaches and innovation in AD R&D. Summary

The findings highlighted in this analysis indicate that AD drug development is entering a breakthrough phase. Mechanistically, the recent discovery of FDA-approved antibodies as disease-modifying therapies has proven and validated APP/Aβ as the valid target of AD. The modest efficacy of such antibodies reflects disease stage and timing and warrants optimized antibodies going forward, rather than target invalidity. The current breakthroughs are indeed limited and not a complete cure for AD. Still, compared to the first decade of this century, there have been significant improvements in diagnosis and treatment, meanwhile, with a noticeable increase in capital investment and research pipelines. All FDA-approved drugs originate from large companies, while early-stage clinical programs are driven by smaller firms, indicating a balanced R&D pipeline. Large companies provide resources and validation, whereas small companies contribute innovation and novel approaches. Increasing emphasis on differentiated targets and advanced treatment strategies suggests a shift toward more innovative, high-end development, laying the foundation for future transformative breakthroughs in AD therapeutics.
