The Application of Nanomaterials in the Detection of Liver Cancer Biomarkers
Authors: Dianya Qiu, Weihua Cao, Yuanjiao Gao, Tianyu Ma, Wen Deng, Shiyu Wang, Yaqin Zhang, Xin Wei, Linmei Yao, Zixuan Gao, Shuojie Wang, Minghui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver cancer is one of the malignant tumors with high global incidence and mortality rates, with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounting for 90% of primary liver cancer cases. Due to the lack of specific symptoms in early-stage liver cancer, diagnosis is often delayed, leading to the majority of patients being identified at intermediate to advanced stages with poor prognoses. Early diagnosis is critical for improving patient survival; however, conventional diagnostic methods face limitations such as low sensitivity and insufficient specificity. Nanomaterials have demonstrated great potential in laboratory research to overcome the limitations of traditional detection technologies by virtue of their unique physicochemical properties. However, most of these relevant studies are still in the laboratory stage and require large-scale clinical validation. This review systematically summarizes the innovative applications of nanomaterials in liver cancer biomarker diagnostics, focusing on their mechanisms for improving detection performance, including signal amplification, targeted recognition, and system optimization. It highlights specific applications of nanomaterials in biomarker detection, discusses current challenges in nanodiagnostic technologies, and explores future development trends, aiming to provide novel perspectives for the diagnosis of liver cancer patients.

Introduction

The primary pathological types of primary liver cancer include hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), and combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma, with HCC accounting for 90% of cases.1 Liver cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Epidemiological data indicate approximately 865,000 new liver cancer cases annually globally, representing over 4% of all cancer diagnoses, with 758,000 deaths annually (7.8% of total cancer deaths). This positions liver cancer as the third most lethal malignancy after lung and colorectal cancers, and the second leading cause of cancer death among male patients.2 The high mortality rate of liver cancer is closely linked to challenges in early diagnosis. Early-stage liver cancer patients typically lack specific symptoms, which frequently leads to oversight. Most patients are diagnosed at intermediate to advanced stages, missing opportunities for curative treatment and resulting in poor prognoses. Consequently, early diagnosis is pivotal for improving survival rates.3

Breakthroughs in nanotechnology and novel nanomaterial development have created vast prospects for nanomedicine in precision diagnostics and therapeutics. Nanomaterials exhibit advantages such as high surface-to-volume ratio, low toxicity, enhanced sensitivity, and biocompatibility.4–6 These unique physicochemical properties endow nanomaterials with the potential to break through the limitations of traditional detection technologies and improve detection performance under laboratory conditions; however, their clinical value in the detection of hepatocellular carcinoma biomarkers still needs to be validated through rigorous large-scale clinical studies.

Conventional imaging techniques and biomarker detection methods remain limited. Imaging modalities face shortcomings such as low sensitivity and risks of contrast agent hypersensitivity.7–9 Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) is the most clinically critical serum biomarker, yet its clinical utility is constrained by negative results in 40% of early-stage patients.10 Beyond traditional markers, emerging biomarkers like des-γ-carboxy prothrombin (DCP/PIVKA-II) and Golgi protein 73 (GP73) have shown diagnostic potential.3,11 Additionally, liquid biopsy technologies, including circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), circulating tumor cells (CTCs), and extracellular vesicles (EVs), hold significant value for early detection.12,13 However, their clinical application still faces challenges. As illustrated in Figure 1, nanomaterials overcome these diagnostic limitations through signal amplification, targeted recognition, and detection system optimization, providing a comprehensive strategy for liver cancer biomarker detection. Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of nanomaterials in next-generation immune cell therapy and neuroprotective interventions.14,15 More broadly, nanomaterials have also been actively explored in cancer combination therapy, targeted drug delivery, tissue engineering, and organoid-based disease modeling.16–22 In contrast to these directions, comprehensive reviews focusing specifically on nanomaterial-based detection of liver cancer biomarkers remain insufficient. Literature Search Methodology

Searches were performed in the PubMed and Web of Science databases. The search strategy was developed based on the following core keyword combinations: (nanomaterials OR nanoparticles), (liver cancer OR hepatocellular carcinoma OR HCC), and (biomarkers OR early cancer detection OR diagnosis OR biosensors). Subject headings combined with free-text terms were adopted to broaden the search scope and ensure comprehensive coverage of relevant literature. The search covered the period from January 2009 to December 2025, with additional manual tracing of key early studies published before 2009. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) original research or review articles focusing on the application of nanomaterials in the detection of liver cancer biomarkers; (2) studies reporting analytical performance indicators such as limit of detection or linear range; (3) articles published in English. The exclusion criteria included: (1) studies irrelevant to liver cancer or nanomaterials; (2) conference abstracts, editorials, case reports, and other non-full-text publications; (3) studies with incomplete data or ambiguous methodological descriptions. After duplicate removal, all retrieved records were manually screened by researchers first based on titles and abstracts, followed by full-text evaluation to ensure relevance and quality. Two researchers independently performed qualitative methodological quality assessments on the literature that passed full-text evaluation and extracted the following information: sample size, sample source (authentic clinical samples or spiked samples), whether an independent validation set was established, and whether diagnostic performance indicators including sensitivity, specificity, and AUC were reported. Finally, all included literature was discussed and confirmed by all authors to support the arguments presented in this review. This review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and a systematic summary of the key limitations of the included studies has been conducted.

Protein Biomarkers of Liver Cancer

Protein biomarkers currently utilized for liver cancer diagnosis primarily include alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), des-γ-carboxy prothrombin (DCP/PIVKA-II), AFP-L3 isoform (AFP-L3), Golgi protein 73 (GP73), and glypican-3 (GPC3).11

Alpha-Fetoprotein (AFP)

AFP is a glycoprotein synthesized by fetal liver, yolk sac, and other tissues. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells, AFP localizes to subcellular regions such as the rough endoplasmic reticulum, perinuclear space, and the prominent Golgi apparatus surrounding the nucleus.11,12,23,24 Under normal physiological conditions, AFP levels in adults are extremely low, but its expression is significantly upregulated following dedifferentiation of HCC cells.11,12 AFP is now widely used for HCC monitoring and diagnosis.25,26 However, its utility is compromised by suboptimal specificity and sensitivity for early-stage HCC. Approximately 40% of early-stage HCC patients test AFP-negative, while elevated AFP levels may also occur in non-malignant conditions such as hepatitis, viral hepatitis, and cholangiocarcinoma, leading to false-positive results.10,11,27

Des-γ-Carboxy Prothrombin (DCP/PIVKA-II)

Des-γ-carboxy prothrombin (DCP/PIVKA-II) refers to a non-functional, abnormally carboxylated protein formed under vitamin K deficiency or antagonism, predominantly originating from HCC tissues.25,26 PIVKA-II is primarily detected in the cytoplasm of HCC cells.28 DCP has been widely employed in Japan for HCC diagnosis and surveillance.26 Notably, DCP serves as a diagnostic tool for AFP-negative HCC, with studies showing detectable DCP in approximately 90% of AFP-negative patients.29 Combined use of AFP and DCP enhances sensitivity and specificity to 91% and 74%, respectively.26 However, DCP false elevations can occur due to factors such as vitamin K antagonist use or hepatic injury, and its sensitivity for small HCCs (<3 cm) remains low.29–31

Alpha-Fetoprotein-L3 Isoform (AFP-L3)

AFP-L3, a fucosylated isoform of alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), is specifically secreted early by hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells and exhibits strong binding affinity to lens culinaris agglutinin (LCA), rendering it a specific biomarker for HCC.11,12 AFP-L3 aids in early HCC diagnosis and helps distinguish HCC from benign liver diseases.32–34 Despite its specificity, AFP-L3 demonstrates low sensitivity in detecting HCC, and its diagnostic performance is influenced by total AFP levels, showing limited efficacy at low AFP concentrations (unreliable when total AFP <20 ng/mL).32,35

Golgi Protein 73 (GP73)

Golgi protein 73 (GP73) is a transmembrane glycoprotein expressed in epithelial cells. During hepatocellular carcinogenesis, the secretion of GP73 increases, resulting in significantly elevated GP73 levels in HCC patients.11,33,36 As a potential biomarker for liver cancer diagnosis, GP73 complements AFP to enhance diagnostic accuracy.11,35 However, limitations persist in GP73-based detection. Slight elevation of GP73 levels may occur in benign liver diseases (eg, chronic hepatitis and cirrhosis), leading to false-positive results, while discrepancies in cut-off values across commercial assay kits further compromise result consistency.35

Glypican-3 (GPC3)

Glypican-3 (GPC3), a member of the membrane-anchored heparan sulfate proteoglycan family, is overexpressed in HCC tumors.11,25 This protein regulates tumor invasion, proliferation, and metastasis by modulating tyrosine kinase activity and Wnt signaling pathways.30 GPC3 serves as a supplementary diagnostic marker to AFP, and their combined application improves diagnostic sensitivity.11,33,37 Notably, GPC3 aids in differentiating HCC from AFP-negative nodules and demonstrates high specificity in virus-associated HCC. Nevertheless, its diagnostic value in non-viral HCC remains uncertain, and standardized assays with proven efficacy for early detection are still lacking.11

Nucleic Acid Biomarkers of Liver Cancer

Nucleic acid biomarkers for liver cancer diagnosis, including microRNAs (miRNAs) and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), exhibit high specificity and potential for early detection.

Circulating Tumor DNA (ctDNA)

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) refers to cell-free DNA fragments released during apoptosis or necrosis of tumor cells, with its proportion in total cell-free DNA (cfDNA) varying widely from <1% to >90%.11,38 ctDNA is released into the bloodstream via tumor cell apoptosis, necrosis, or active secretion (eg, via exosomes), enters the bloodstream, and is subsequently degraded by nucleases or cleared by organs.39 ctDNA carries cancer-specific genetic alterations such as aberrant gene methylation and mutations, with abnormal methylation playing a critical role in early hepatocarcinogenesis.12,40,41 The elevation of ctDNA levels in liver cancer patients underscores its utility as a biomarker for screening and liquid biopsy-assisted diagnosis.11,12,42 Detection of specific ctDNA mutations aids in diagnosis, though single-mutation assays show low sensitivity. However, elevated ctDNA levels are also observed in non-cancerous conditions, limiting its specificity when analyzed quantitatively. Additionally, high costs and the need for large-scale prospective validation hinder its clinical implementation.36,42

MicroRNA (miRNA)

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are endogenous small non-coding RNAs transcribed in the nucleus and maturated in the cytoplasm. They regulate gene expression in diverse biological processes, with deregulated miRNA expression significantly contributing to tumorigenesis.43 Circulating miRNAs have emerged as promising biomarkers for liver cancer diagnosis.25,33 For instance, miR-122 is markedly downregulated in HCC and inhibits tumor growth by suppressing angiogenesis in the tumor microenvironment, thereby depriving cancer cells of nutrients.44 A landmark three-phase multicenter retrospective longitudinal study developed a miRNA-based classifier (Cmi), which effectively identifies small-sized, early-stage, and AFP-negative HCC cases in high-risk populations.45 Collectively, these findings suggest that miRNAs have the potential to overcome limitations of traditional AFP testing, significantly improving diagnostic accuracy for early and AFP-negative HCC. However, most studies to date are small-scale, single-center observational analyses lacking large prospective validation or multicenter reproducibility. Furthermore, substantial heterogeneity in miRNA panels, assay methods, and cut-off values across studies impedes cross-comparison of results.12

Cellular and Vesicular Biomarkers of Liver Cancer

Cellular and vesicular biomarkers, such as circulating tumor cells (CTCs) and tumor-derived exosomes, reflect tumor metastatic potential and provide critical insights for disease evaluation.

Circulating Tumor Cells (CTCs)

Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) are tumor-derived cells that detach from primary or metastatic lesions and enter the bloodstream, carrying tumor-specific molecular signatures. Their quantity and phenotypic characteristics correlate with tumor progression.11,12 CTC analysis, an integral component of liquid biopsy, holds promise for early liver cancer detection. Studies demonstrate that combining CTCs with AFP enhances diagnostic accuracy. For instance, an optimized EpCAM mRNA-based CTC detection platform achieved an AUROC of 0.86, with 73.0% sensitivity and 93.4% specificity when combined with AFP.12,46 However, CTC detection in HCC exhibits significant heterogeneity. The scarcity of CTCs in early-stage tumors poses technical challenges, necessitating novel methodologies to address their rarity and heterogeneity.47

Exosomes

Exosomes, ubiquitous in bodily fluids, encapsulate diverse cellular components such as proteins, microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), and are secreted by nearly all cell types across species.48 As critical components of liquid biopsy, exosomes harbor genetic material that provides novel targets for liver cancer diagnosis, offering distinct advantages.49 Exosomal biomarkers demonstrate high sensitivity and specificity in HCC diagnosis, with certain markers showing diagnostic value even in AFP-negative cases. Detection of these biomarkers enables early diagnosis and prognostic assessment.11,48 Nevertheless, current exosome isolation techniques are time-consuming, technically complex, and lack standardized protocols, resulting in poor inter-study reproducibility. Additionally, unresolved challenges in standardization and scalable production hinder clinical translation, limiting their broad application.48,49

Metabolomic Biomarkers of Liver Cancer

Metabolomic biomarker analysis offers a novel approach for liver cancer diagnosis by characterizing dynamic changes in small-molecule metabolites within biological samples. This strategy identifies disease-specific markers through metabolic pathway alterations, phenotype-metabolome associations, and multi-omics integration.11 Metabolomics has been applied to analyze patient serum and urine, for example, a study by Chen et al identified 43 serum metabolites (eg, bile acids, histidine, inosine) and 31 urine metabolites in HCC patients. These metabolites are linked to critical pathways, including bile acid metabolism, free fatty acid metabolism, glycolysis, urea cycle, and methionine metabolism. Combined with AFP detection, selected metabolites achieved an AUC of 0.9275, with 100% accuracy in distinguishing HCC patients with AFP levels <20 ng/mL. Membrane phospholipid profiling via liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) further differentiated HCC from adjacent non-tumor tissues (AUC 0.963).50 These findings highlight the potential of metabolomic biomarkers to complement conventional diagnostics and enhance early detection rates. However, clinical implementation of metabolomic biomarkers requires further validation. Challenges include incomplete characterization of metabolic signatures due to HCC heterogeneity, insufficient cohort sizes in training/validation phases, poor cross-laboratory comparability owing to unstandardized workflows, and limited specificity, as certain metabolites are also dysregulated in cirrhosis and hepatitis.11,50

In summary, the various types of liver cancer biomarkers mentioned above exhibit limitations in clinical detection to varying degrees, including insufficient sensitivity, unsatisfactory specificity, and lack of standardization. Nanomaterials, with their unique physicochemical properties, offer novel technical approaches to overcome these limitations. The following section systematically introduces the types of nanomaterials used for the detection of liver cancer biomarkers and the core mechanisms by which they improve detection performance.

Nanomaterials for Liver Cancer Biomarker Detection

Recent advances in nanotechnology have propelled the application of diverse nanomaterials for sensitive detection of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) biomarkers (Table 2). Metal nanomaterials (eg, gold and silver nanoparticles) are widely employed due to their strong surface plasmon resonance (SPR) effects, which render their optical properties (eg, color) highly sensitive to environmental changes, while their large surface-to-volume ratios enable efficient conjugation with biorecognition elements (eg, antibodies, aptamers). Target biomarker binding induces measurable optical signal shifts for rapid detection.51 Quantum dots (QDs), with size-tunable fluorescence, narrow emission spectra, and superior photostability, serve as ideal fluorescent labels.52–54 Magnetic nanoparticles (eg, superparamagnetic iron oxide) facilitate rapid biomarker enrichment/separation and function as MRI contrast agents, enabling theranostic integration.55,56 Inorganic non-metallic nanomaterials (eg, silica-based nanostructures) exhibit high surface areas and stability for biomolecule immobilization, thereby significantly enhancing detection sensitivity, robustness, and multiplexing capabilities.57 Carbon-based nanomaterials (eg, graphene, carbon nanotubes) leverage exceptional electrical conductivity, high surface areas, and functionalizable surfaces to immobilize recognition elements (eg, aptamers) and amplify electrochemical signals, enabling label-free and ultrasensitive electrical sensing.58,59 Table 2Nanomaterial Types and Core Properties for Liver Cancer Biomarker DetectionNanomaterial ClassRepresentative ExamplesKey Analytical AdvantageBest-Suited Biomarker TypeMain LimitationsTranslational ImplicationRef.Metal nanomaterialsAuNPs, AgNPs, Au nanostars, Au nanorodsStrong plasmonic effects; In laboratory studies, nanomaterials can enhance the detection performance of hepatocellular carcinoma biomarkers through mechanisms such as signal amplification, targeted recognition, and optimization of detection systems, demonstrating potential to provide technical support for the early diagnosis and disease monitoring of hepatocellular carcinoma. Signal Amplification

Nanomaterials significantly enhance detection sensitivity via diversified signal amplification strategies. One primary strategy is cascade amplification. Metal nanoparticles (eg, Au, Ag nanomaterials) exploit surface plasmon resonance (SPR) to amplify optical signals in cancer diagnosis.51,62 For example, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), owing to their high extinction coefficients and facile surface functionalization, serve as excellent signal transducers and are widely integrated into biosensing platforms such as CRISPR-Cas systems.63 For instance, Xu et al demonstrated that AuNPs combined with catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA) enable femtomolar(fM)-level detection of miRNA-199a through cascade signal amplification.64 Nanozymes (eg, Van@BSA-MnO2) provide powerful signal amplification through efficient catalytic substrate conversion.65,66 Another strategy, loading capacity amplification, is achieved by increasing signal source loading. Quantum dots (QDs) significantly enhance optical signals via high quantum yields and exceptional photostability.67,68 Graphene increases antibody loading capacity and accelerates electron transfer via its large specific surface area and excellent conductivity, while functionalized carbon nanospheres (CNSs) achieve 7-fold signal amplification by leveraging synergistic enzyme cascades (eg, HRP-graphene hybrids) for ultrasensitive detection.69,70 These signal amplification strategies enable nanomaterial-based platforms to detect trace HCC biomarkers undetectable by conventional methods, opening avenues for early diagnosis.

Targeted Recognition

Nanomaterials achieve highly specific biomarker recognition through surface modification with targeting ligands, significantly improving detection performance.71 Antibody- or aptamer-conjugated nanoparticles, such as anti-AFP antibody-modified quantum dots, are employed to establish antigen-specific binding.56 Aptamer-mediated targeting leverages aptamer-functionalized nanomaterials (AFNs) combining aptamer affinity with nanomaterial versatility to precisely target HCC biomarkers like GPC3.72 This targeting strategy can be further enhanced by techniques like rolling circle amplification (RCA) to amplify the aptamer–target interactions.73 In biomimetic imprinting, nanomaterials based on molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) mimic natural receptor sites to selectively capture HCC biomarkers like AFP.74 In the field of structural engineering, AuNPs-loaded covalent organic frameworks (AuNPs@COF) enhance target recognition through high surface area and their ability to encasulate molecular recognition elements, efficiently immobilizing antibodies and enzymes.75

Detection System Optimization

Nanomaterials improve detection accuracy and practicality by enhancing anti-interference capabilities and system stability. Multiple anti-interference strategies leveraging nanomaterials have been developed to enhance detection reliability. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) coatings form hydration layers to reduce nonspecific adsorption of serum proteins, thereby improving signal-to-noise ratios.76 Graphene-metal hybrids suppress background noise via plasmonic coupling, enhancing SERS performance.62 Magnetic nanoparticles enable rapid target isolation under magnetic fields, minimizing matrix effects. Similarly, magnetic confinement chambers can trigger DNA cascade amplification for exosome detection. When coupled with fluorescent probes, these systems further enable integrated separation-detection, significantly reducing background interference.56,77,78 For platform innovation, Silver/polydimethylsiloxane (Ag/PDMS)-based optical pressure sensors convert biomarker binding into measurable pressure signals for AFP detection.79 Furthermore, silica-coated quantum dots (QDs) and upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) improve biocompatibility by combining high fluorescence intensity with minimal cytotoxicity, enabling reliable complex biofluid analysis.80,81 These advances streamline workflows and align nanomaterial-based platforms with clinical demands.

Corresponding nanomaterial strategies can be adopted to address the detection challenges of different types of liver cancer biomarkers: for protein biomarkers, gold nanoparticles can be used to construct immunosensors; for nucleic acid biomarkers, carbon-based nanomaterials such as graphene can be employed to achieve electrochemical signal amplification; for cellular and vesicular biomarkers, magnetic nanoparticles can be utilized for efficient enrichment and separation; and for metabolic biomarkers, silica nanomaterials can be applied to enhance the capability of multicomponent detection. The following section will elaborate with specific research cases.

Applications of Nanomaterials in Liver Cancer Biomarker Detection

With their unique structural and functional properties, nanomaterials have been extensively explored in laboratory research on the detection of liver cancer biomarkers. Preliminary studies have shown that they can achieve the detection of protein biomarkers, nucleic acid biomarkers, exosomes and other targets; however, these methods have not yet been applied in routine clinical practice. They further provide integrated platforms for simultaneous quantification of multiple biomarkers, significantly improving diagnostic accuracy and comprehensiveness.

Nanomaterials in Protein Biomarker Detection

Nanomaterials in AFP Detection

AFP detection faces challenges in terms of sensitivity and specificity for early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma. Owing to their optical properties and ease of functionalization, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) have been widely employed to construct highly sensitive and specific detection platforms for AFP.51 Various AuNP-based platforms have been developed for AFP detection, achieving ultra-low detection limits (LODs) and enhanced sensitivity. These include fluorescence ELISA (utilizing HAT-triggered fluorescence turn-on), electrochemical ELISA (where AuNPs act as nano-carriers for signal amplification), and MoS2/AuNP composite sensors.55,82 Yang et al developed a rapid immunoassay using gold nanostars (GNSs) labeled with rabbit anti-AFP antibodies as capture probes and AuNP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG as signal enhancers. This method achieved an LOD of 8.6 ng/mL (linear range: 20.0–110.0 ng/mL) and showed a strong correlation with clinical ECLIA results (correlation coefficient: 0.931), highlighting its potential for large-scale serum screening.83 Zhao et al fabricated sunflower-like Au nanoarrays via colloidal lithography and magnetron sputtering, serving as SERS-active substrates for AFP detection (LOD: 0.003–3 ng/mL).84 Another study designed a GDYO@AuNPs@PCN composite system, exploiting fluorescence quenching (GDYO/PCN) and AuNP-mediated signal amplification. Combined with a dual-aptamer “sandwich” structure and antifouling peptides, this platform achieved an LOD of 1.51 pg/mL for AFP, aligning with clinical serum analysis.85 The assay demonstrated strong consistency with clinical methods in serum sample detection, validating its potential for practical application.

Beyond AuNPs, other nanomaterials enhance AFP detection. Meng et al developed a 3D magnetic electroactive nanoprobe using multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MCNTs), Fe3O4 NPs, and AuNPs. After functionalized with hemin and PDDA, this system achieved an LOD of 0.04 ng/mL via electrochemical immunosensing.86 Wang et al engineered a label-free immunosensor based on TB-Au-Fe3O4-rGO nanocomposites. By leveraging graphene’s conductivity, Fe3O4’s electrocatalysis of toluidine blue (TB), and AuNP antibody immobilization, this sensor achieved an unprecedented LOD of 2.7 fg/mL (linear range: 1.0×10−5–10.0 ng/mL) and was clinically validated against ELISA (relative error: −4.9–4.0%).69 These studies significantly enhanced the sensitivity of AFP detection, highlighting the transformative potential of nanomaterials in early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) diagnosis.

It should be emphasized that although nanodiagnostic technologies can lower the detection limit of AFP to the fg/mL level, the “critical threshold” of concern in clinical diagnosis is not the technical detection limit, but the concentration cutoff that carries clinical decision‑making significance (currently generally accepted as 20 ng/mL).10,27 Future studies should re‑evaluate and optimize the diagnostic threshold of AFP in real‑world clinical cohorts and incorporate multi‑marker combined detection strategies to avoid false‑positive or overdiagnosis issues resulting from the blind pursuit of ultra‑low detection limits.5,87

Nanomaterials in Detection of Other Protein Biomarkers

In addition to AFP, other protein biomarkers such as DCP and GPC3 also exhibit deficiencies in sensitivity or specificity. Nanomaterials can effectively improve the detection performance of these biomarkers through strategies involving signal amplification and targeted recognition. For DCP detection, Ching et al designed a ZnO-NP-based screen-printed immunosensor with strong anti-interference capability, achieving an LOD of 0.078 ng/mL for DCP.88 For AFP-L3, Cheng et al constructed AgNP-modified unit arrays using magnetron sputtering for AFP-L3 quantification based on signal intensity modulation.89 With respect to GPC3, Li et al developed an H-rGO-Pd NP nanozyme-enhanced electrochemical sensor, achieving an LOD of 3.30 ng/mL with validated applicability in serum.66 Furthermore, for GP73, Liang et al created a fluorescent aptasensor using N-doped graphene quantum dots/MoS2@rGO nanosheets. This system demonstrated linear detection of GP73 (5–100 ng/mL; LOD: 4.54 ng/mL) and achieved 97.21–100.83% recovery in human serum.54

Nanomaterials in Nucleic Acid Biomarker Detection for Liver Cancer

Detection of nucleic acid biomarkers (eg, ctDNA, miRNA) is challenged by insufficient sensitivity and lack of standardization. Nanomaterials can significantly improve the detection sensitivity toward these biomarkers via signal amplification technologies.107 For ctDNA detection, Miao et al integrated rolling enzymatic DNA amplification with gold nanoparticle@silicon (Au NP@Si)-assisted surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS), achieving an ultralow limit of detection (LOD) of 9.1 fM in spiked blood samples. This approach provides a novel strategy for early diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring.108 Cai et al developed a dual-biomarker platform based on peptide nucleic acid-gold nanoparticle probes (PNA-AuNPs) and lead phosphate-encapsulated ferritin (LPA). This platform detected HCC-related ctDNA with an LOD of 10 fM and demonstrated its clinical utility in patient serum analysis.109 Additionally, for miRNA detection, Nanomaterial-enhanced electrochemical biosensors (eg, AuNPs, reduced graphene oxide, Prussian blue nanoparticles) significantly improved sensitivity for HCC-associated miRNAs like miR-122 and miR-21, achieving LODs as low as 1.5 fM to 0.135 fM.110 These platforms offer rapid, cost-effective tools for early HCC diagnosis. Collectively, these advancements underscore the pivotal role of nanomaterials in nucleic acid biomarker detection, bridging critical gaps in HCC diagnostics. Taken together, the comparative evidence summarized in Tables 3–5 reveals a consistent pattern across nanomaterial-based diagnostic platforms for liver cancer biomarkers. First, the major technical advances have occurred in analytical sensitivity, with many platforms achieving ultralow detection limits, especially for AFP, miRNAs, and ctDNA. Second, different nanomaterial classes appear to serve distinct functional roles: gold-based systems are prominent in optical protein assays, carbon-based materials frequently provide the strongest electrochemical signal amplification, and magnetic nanomaterials are particularly valuable for enrichment and isolation of exosomes or CTCs. Third, despite these analytical advantages, the most important barrier to clinical translation is not sensitivity itself, but insufficient validation, poor inter-study standardization, and the lack of clinically interpretable diagnostic performance metrics. Overall, current nanodiagnostic platforms are best viewed as highly promising but still largely preclinical tools that require more rigorous real-world evaluation before routine implementation.

Nanomaterials in Multiplexed Liver Cancer Biomarker Detection

The heterogeneity of liver cancer requires the simultaneous detection of multiple biomarker to overcome limitations of single-marker diagnostics. Nanomaterials enable simultaneous analysis of protein, nucleic acid, and exosomal biomarkers, significantly improving HCC diagnostic accuracy. In electrochemical multiplexing, graphene-based electrochemical sensors leverage high conductivity and large surface areas to detect diverse biomarkers (eg, AFP, GP73, exosomal proteins, miR-122) offering ultralow detection limits, operational simplicity, and robustness. These platforms demonstrate the potential for synchronous multi-marker quantification, which would significantly enhance diagnostic precision.44 In quantum dot-based platforms, Liu et al pioneered a potential-resolved electrochemiluminescence (ECL) immunosensor using quantum dots (QDs), achieving dual detection of AFP and AFP-L3 with LODs of 1.0 pg/mL and 3.24 pg/mL, respectively. Calculated AFP-L3% aligned with clinical data, validating this platform for multi-marker HCC diagnostics.125 In phosphorous-doped nanocomposites, Hu et al engineered phosphorus-doped carbon dot-functionalized dendritic mesoporous silica nanoparticles (DMSNs-BCDs). The derived lateral flow immunoassay (LFIA) detected AFP with an unprecedented LOD of 0.01 ng/mL (linear range: 0.01–1000 ng/mL) and enabled simultaneous AFP/CA199 quantification, offering a sensitive, cost-effective strategy for early HCC detection.57 These advances demonstrate the transformative potential of nanomaterial-driven multiplexed platforms in revolutionizing HCC diagnostics.

Comparative Analysis Between Conventional Diagnostic Methods and Nanomaterial-Based Biosensors

The clinical diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) still relies primarily on serological assays and imaging examinations, including enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA), and imaging techniques such as ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).126 Among these, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), as the most commonly used serum biomarker, exhibits certain specificity but shows low sensitivity in early-stage HCC, particularly in AFP‑negative patients.10 Although imaging methods can provide morphological information about tumors, they have limited capability for detecting micrometastases or small focal lesions and are prone to operator dependence.126 In contrast, nanomaterial‑based biosensors have recently demonstrated significant advantages in the detection of HCC biomarkers. By leveraging the excellent electrical, optical, and surface effects of nanomaterials, these sensors achieve efficient signal amplification, thereby markedly improving detection sensitivity.5 Studies have shown that the limit of detection for nanobiosensors can reach picomolar or even femtomolar levels, which is significantly superior to conventional ELISA methods.69,111,114 However, compared with well‑established and standardized traditional diagnostic methods, nanobiosensors still exhibit notable shortcomings in clinical translation, including insufficient reproducibility and stability of detection results, a lack of uniform testing standards and operational protocols, and a scarcity of large‑scale, multicenter clinical validation data. Moreover, the in vivo biosafety and long‑term toxicity of certain nanomaterials have not yet been systematically evaluated.5,87,127 Therefore, at the current stage, traditional diagnostic methods remain the primary means for HCC clinical diagnosis, whereas nanobiosensors are more likely to serve as complementary tools to enhance detection sensitivity and facilitate early diagnosis. In summary, the integration of conventional detection methods with nanobiosensing technologies holds promise for providing a more accurate and efficient diagnostic strategy for HCC.

In summary, selecting appropriate nanomaterial strategies according to the detection difficulties of different liver cancer biomarkers—such as gold nanoparticles for proteins, graphene for nucleic acids, and magnetic nanoparticles for cells/vesicles—represents an effective approach to improving detection performance. This integrated strategy of choosing nanomaterials based on biomarker characteristics provides technical support for the early diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma.

Challenges and Future Directions of Nanomaterials in Liver Cancer Biomarker Detection

Methodological Limitations of Included Studies

As stated in the methodology section, this review performed quality assessment on the included studies and identified the following common methodological limitations: (1) small sample sizes: most studies are single‑center exploratory investigations with limited sample sizes, lacking independent external validation cohorts;12,48 (2) insufficient standardization: substantial heterogeneity exists in the detection platforms, cutoff values, and biomarker combinations adopted across studies, making direct comparison of results difficult;12,35,49 (3) Insufficient preclinical validation: most nano-detection platforms have only undergone preliminary testing with a small number of clinical serum/plasma samples, and their detection performance has not been systematically evaluated under real-world conditions.5,87 Therefore, the current evidence needs to be further validated through high-quality studies. (4) Dependence on spiked samples: many studies used spiked samples in buffer or healthy serum to validate the analytical performance of the nanosensors, rather than authentic clinical specimens from patients with liver cancer. This validation approach cannot adequately reflect the matrix effects, non-specific interference, or true diagnostic accuracy of the detection methods in complex clinical backgrounds. Therefore, higher-quality studies are still required to validate the current evidence. (5) Diagnostic performance metrics: Consistent with the notes in Tables 3–5, only two studies94,104 provided partial or complete diagnostic performance data (sensitivity, specificity, AUC), while all other biosensors reviewed did not report these metrics. This limitation is acknowledged as a major barrier to clinical translation.

These methodological differences directly result in significant variability in detection performance across different studies. Taking AFP detection as an example, the limits of detection reported by different nanosensors vary considerably: the limit of detection for an immunoassay based on gold nanostars (GNSs) is 8.6 ng/mL,83 whereas that for a label-free electrochemical immunosensor based on multifunctional graphene nanocomposites is 2.7 fg/mL.69 The variability in limits of detection among studies can span more than six orders of magnitude. Regarding diagnostic performance indicators, the vast majority of studies did not report such indicators, and only a very small number of studies provided complete data on sensitivity, specificity, and AUC. This variability is related to differences in nanomaterial types, signal amplification strategies, and validation sample matrices.

Technical Challenges

Despite the significant promise of nanomaterials in HCC biomarker detection, their clinical translation faces critical challenges. Safety and biocompatibility concerns include insufficient long-term toxicity evaluations and systemic biocompatibility assessments, particularly regarding nanoparticle-induced hepatotoxicity.128,129 Quantitative toxicological studies have further revealed the dose–effect relationship of nanomaterial toxicity. Oberdörster et al found that 20 nm TiO2 nanoparticles induced significantly stronger pulmonary inflammation than 250 nm particles at the same mass dose, whereas their dose–response curves converged after normalization by surface area, indicating that surface area is a superior metric for toxicological dose. In addition, polytetrafluoroethylene fume nanoparticles can cause severe acute lung injury at a deposition dose of only 60 ng.130 Studies reveal that nanomaterials accumulate in the liver, causing oxidative stress, lipid droplet accumulation, elevated liver enzymes, steatosis, and fibrosis.127,131 These risks are compounded by impaired hepatic metabolism in HCC patients, which exacerbate nanomaterial toxicity and underscore the need for rigorous safety profiling.132 From a standardization perspective, a meta-analysis of nanotoxicity studies has provided further quantitative evidence. Among 1676 relevant publications, only a small number of studies simultaneously reported the standard deviation of particle size and the error range of the half-maximal effective concentration, thereby meeting the stringent inclusion criteria for meta-analysis. The analysis also indicated that for soluble nanoparticles (eg, silver, zinc oxide), expressing dose in terms of surface area/volume—rather than the conventional mass/volume—reduced the heterogeneity statistic H by up to 39.2%, highlighting the importance of using physicochemically relevant dose metrics instead of relying solely on traditional mass concentrations.133

Regulatory gaps, characterized by the absence of standardized protocols for nanomaterial production, recycling, and quality control, further hinder scalability. Additionally, tumor-induced vascular abnormalities can destabilize nanoparticles, impairing their targeting ability and controlled release.5,134 Technical bottlenecks also remain, such as the cost-effective mass production, batch-to-batch consistency, and standardization of these methods.5 Among these challenges, batch-to-batch consistency represents one of the core technical hurdles in large-scale production. Quantitative studies have shown that the diameter of silicon nanoparticles can vary from 32 to 160 nm across eight batches, and such significant variations directly compromise the performance and safety of nanoformulations.135 Controlling batch-to-batch variability and establishing standardized manufacturing processes remain urgent bottlenecks to be addressed.136 Furthermore, false positives and matrix interference in serum samples are also critical technical challenges. The abundant proteins and lipids in serum can cause non‑specific adsorption or signal interference of nanomaterials, leading to false‑positive results. Many of the included studies were validated only with spiked buffer solutions or diluted healthy serum, without verification using authentic serum samples from liver cancer patients, thus failing to reflect the actual clinical matrix effects. Future research should systematically assess the anti‑interference performance of nanodiagnostic platforms based on real clinical samples and compare with reference methods to establish reliable diagnostic thresholds. These technical bottlenecks not only hinder the translational progress of nanomaterials from basic research to clinical application, but also limit their widespread use in the detection of hepatocellular carcinoma biomarkers.

In addition, most nanobiosensor studies included in this review exhibit obvious deficiencies in analytical performance validation, especially the lack of systematic evaluation of sensor repeatability, reproducibility, and long-term stability. The few studies that reported these parameters only performed short-term (eg, days to weeks) or limited-batch tests, and standardized quality control protocols have not yet been established. In practical clinical applications, inter-batch consistency and long-term storage stability of sensors are key factors determining whether they can be translated into reliable diagnostic tools. Therefore, future studies should systematically evaluate the repeatability, reproducibility, and long-term stability of nanosensors with reference to performance validation guidelines for clinical laboratory testing (eg, CLSI guidelines) and report quantitative indicators such as the coefficient of variation (CV%).137

Major Barriers in Clinical Translation

In addition to the aforementioned technical challenges, the clinical translation of nanodetection technology also faces the following specific barriers. (1) Lack of large-scale, multicenter prospective clinical validation. At present, the vast majority of studies remain in the stage of proof-of-concept or small-sample exploration, and there is no independent, large-sample prospective cohort to systematically evaluate the diagnostic accuracy, reproducibility, and clinical benefits of nanodetection platforms. Most studies are confined to laboratory settings and lack rigorous validation in large-scale longitudinal clinical cohorts.12,48,87 (2) Difficulties in integration with the existing clinical diagnostic system. Nanotechnology-based detection methods have not yet been incorporated into domestic and international guidelines for hepatocellular carcinoma management, and clinicians lack a comprehensive understanding of their operational feasibility, cost-effectiveness ratio, and additional value compared to existing methods (eg, ultrasound, AFP detection). Currently, there is no consensus on their clinical application.138 The lack of head-to-head comparative studies with conventional detection methods also limits their clinical acceptance. (3) Industrialization and regulatory barriers are prominent. Nanodetection platforms face challenges such as batch consistency, long-term storage stability, and cost control during large-scale production. Meanwhile, the full-process regulatory standards and quality control systems from research and development to clinical application remain blank, and most nanosensors are still in the “proof-of-concept” stage, which is far from obtaining in vitro diagnostic reagent registration approval.5 Addressing these hurdles requires interdisciplinary collaboration among material scientists, clinicians, and regulatory bodies to bridge the gap between bench and bedside.

Future Directions of Nanomaterials in Liver Cancer Biomarker Detection

Future advancements will focus on three strategic pillars. The first pillar, safety and standardization, will prioritize systematic long-term safety assessments and harmonized regulatory frameworks.5,87,128,129 The second pillar, integrated theranostic platforms, aims to develop nanotheranostic systems that combine biomarker detection (eg, AFP, exosomes) with targeted drug delivery and treatment monitoring, enabling closed-loop “detect-treat-track” workflows.55 In terms of therapeutic functions, recent studies have developed polyoxometalate-nanozyme integrated light-driven nanomotors (POMotors). By virtue of their peroxidase-like activity and near-infrared light-responsive properties, these nanomotors enable self-propulsion-enhanced deep tumor penetration and synergistic photothermal-catalytic therapy, further expanding the application potential of nanomaterials in precise tumor treatment.139 The third pillar, precision oncology integration, will leverage multi-omics profiling and HCC molecular subtyping to design personalized nanosensors, enhancing diagnostic accuracy for tailored therapies.140 Large multicenter initiatives (eg, HCC Early Detection Consortium, Texas HCC Alliance) will provide robust clinical validation datasets to accelerate nano-diagnostic adoption.87 Concurrently, technological convergence will optimize scalable nanomanufacturing and AI-driven biomarker analysis to ensure cost-effectiveness and reproducibility. As these strategies mature, nanomaterials are poised to redefine HCC diagnostics, enabling early, precise, and actionable detection.

Convergence of Emerging Technologies: CRISPR, Microfluidics, and AI

In addition to the aforementioned developmental directions, nanomaterial-driven liver cancer diagnostic platforms are rapidly integrating with three cutting-edge technologies to enhance intelligence and automation.141 CRISPR-Cas biosensors leverage the high-specificity nucleic acid recognition capability of Cas proteins combined with signal transduction enabled by gold nanoparticles or graphene, allowing amplification-free, ultrasensitive detection of ctDNA/miRNA and effectively reducing background noise associated with traditional methods. Recent studies have demonstrated that CRISPR/Cas13a-based sensing systems combined with magnetic gold nanoparticles enable highly sensitive detection of miRNAs under amplification-free conditions via dark-field imaging; furthermore, the integration of CRISPR/Cas with plasmonic nanomaterials has further improved the sensitivity and specificity of disease biomarker detection.142–144 Microfluidic diagnostic systems, by integrating magnetic nanoparticles or functionalized nanosubstrates into chip-based platforms, enable efficient enrichment and multiplexed detection of trace markers such as exosomes and circulating tumor cells (CTCs), while markedly reducing sample consumption and shortening assay time.145,146 Furthermore, artificial intelligence (AI)-integrated platforms employ machine learning algorithms for noise reduction and feature extraction from complex optical, electrochemical, or Raman signals generated by nanosensors, thereby effectively minimizing serum matrix interference, improving recognition accuracy for low-abundance biomarkers, and supporting multi-marker combined analysis and intelligent diagnostic decision-making. Recent reviews have systematically summarized the advances of machine-learning-assisted electrochemical biosensors in disease biomarker detection, demonstrating that artificial intelligence significantly improves signal deconvolution and diagnostic accuracy.147 Another systematic review presents the integration strategy of AI with voltammetry and biosensors, whose methodology can be extended to the analysis of liver cancer biomarkers.148 Furthermore, latest studies highlight the great potential of AI-empowered electrochemical biosensors toward intelligent and adaptive detection.147–149 In the future, CRISPR, microfluidics, and artificial intelligence will be deeply integrated with nanoplatforms, giving rise to the next generation of intelligent nanodiagnostic systems. For instance, CRISPR-based point-of-care testing chips can readout AI-analyzed signals via smartphones, enabling real-time screening of populations at high risk for liver cancer in community clinics. AI-driven digital microfluidic systems can integrate multi-omics nanosensor networks to dynamically monitor protein, nucleic acid, and exosome biomarkers in blood, allowing risk prediction of tumorigenesis months before clinical onset. The development of fully automated “sample-in, result-out” early liver cancer diagnostic systems represents a key direction for advancing clinical translation.

Summary

Liver cancer, one of the leading global causes of cancer-related deaths, urgently demands more effective early diagnostic strategies. Conventional imaging techniques and serum biomarker assays remain limited by suboptimal sensitivity and specificity, failing to meet clinical needs. The advent of nanotechnology has opened transformative avenues in precision oncology. Nanomaterials, leveraging their high surface-to-volume ratios, low toxicity, enhanced sensitivity, and biocompatibility, overcome traditional diagnostic limitations through mechanisms such as signal amplification, targeted recognition, and detection system optimization. Although recent studies have demonstrated the potential of nanomaterials in immunotherapy and neuroprotection, comprehensive reviews that systematically integrate mechanisms, multi‑category biomarker coverage, and clinical translation—specifically for nanomaterial‑based detection of hepatocellular carcinoma biomarkers—remain scarce. Most laboratory studies have demonstrated that nanomaterial-based detection platforms possess favorable potential for the early screening of liver cancer; nevertheless, the existing evidence is limited to preclinical investigations, and large-scale clinical validation is still required before routine clinical application.

Despite persistent challenges in clinical translation (eg, long-term biocompatibility assessments, standardized protocols, large-scale validation), nanotechnology-driven platforms exhibit extraordinary potential for HCC detection. Future advancements in precision nano-diagnostics will likely resolve critical bottlenecks in early-stage detection, fundamentally improving patient prognoses. Prioritizing systematic safety evaluations, method standardization, and multicenter clinical validation is essential to accelerate real-world adoption. Interdisciplinary collaboration integrating nanotechnology with existing HCC management frameworks will be pivotal to achieving early diagnosis and timely intervention, ultimately reducing the global burden of this lethal disease. Nanomaterial-based HCC diagnostics epitomize the synergy between cutting-edge science and clinical medicine. Their evolution will not only redefine cancer diagnostics but also inspire precision detection paradigms for other diseases. As research progresses and technologies mature, nanomaterials are poised to assume an indispensable role in oncology and beyond, delivering transformative benefits to patients worldwide.
