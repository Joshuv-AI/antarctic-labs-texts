Longitudinal Echocardiographic Parameters of Diastolic Dysfunction Are Influenced by Kidney Function: A Study in Routine Clinical Practice
Authors: Cindy P. Porras, Elisa Dal Canto, M. L. Handoko, Saskia Haitjema, Mark. C.H. de Groot, Michiel L. Bots, Marianne C. Verhaar, Robin W.M. Vernooij
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft ventricular diastolic dysfunction is frequently observed in patients with chronic kidney disease. Nevertheless, the relationship between chronic kidney disease and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction over time is poorly understood. This study aims to assess whether the association between chronic kidney disease and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction varies over time.MethodsWe included outpatients from the Utrecht Patient‐Oriented Database, who underwent at least 2 echocardiographic assessments and had a measurement of kidney function. Linear mixed‐effects models were used to estimate the associations of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and echocardiographic parameters.ResultsA total of 1812 patients were included and stratified into 3 groups on the basis of eGFR: >90 (491), 60–90 (923), and <60 (398). The mean age was 66.7 years, with 62.3% being men. At baseline, patients with lower eGFR exhibited a higher prevalence of hypertension (62.3% versus 55.2%) and diabetes (19.6% versus 11.6%). Linear mixed‐effects models showed that declining eGFR was significantly associated with increased E/e′ ratio, left atrial volume index, tricuspid regurgitation velocity, and left ventricular mass index, with respective estimates of −0.03 (P<0.001), −0.04 (P=0.005), −0.00 (P<0.001), and −0.16 (P<0.001). The interaction between time and eGFR was not statistically significant (P>0.20).ConclusionsThis study shows that lower kidney function was consistently associated with a more adverse diastolic echocardiographic phenotype, characterized by higher filling pressures, greater structural remodeling, and increased hemodynamic burden. This phenotype was already established at initial clinical assessment and did not show measurable further divergence on serial routine echocardiograms.

Left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) characterized by impaired relaxation and stiffness of the left ventricle (LV), represents an early and often asymptomatic stage of heart failure (HF) with preserved ejection fraction. 1 LV diastolic function is commonly evaluated using a combination of echocardiographic parameters that reflect different, although related, physiologic domains, including ventricular relaxation, filling pressures, and structural adaptation. These include mitral annular early diastolic velocity (e') and the E/e' ratio, which provide estimates of LV filling pressures, as well as left atrial volume index (LAVI), tricuspid regurgitation (TR) velocity, and LV mass index (LVMI), which reflect chronic pressure burden, pulmonary pressures, and structural remodeling. 2 , 3 , 4 These parameters are integrated into diagnostic algorithms, which provide a standardized approach, supporting the assessment of diastolic function based on a combination of threshold values and clinical context. 3 In patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), the prevalence of LVDD is notably high, with studies reporting the presence of LVDD any grade in >75% of the patient with CKD. 5 , 6 This coexistence is partly due to the shared pathophysiological mechanisms between kidney and cardiac dysfunction, such as volume overload, systemic inflammation, and vascular calcification, 7 , 8 as well as shared risk factors including cardiovascular disease (CVD), hypertension, diabetes, higher age, and more common female sex. 9 With the growing prevalence of CKD and cardiovascular risk factors, 10 the global burden of cardiac and kidney dysfunction is expected to increase. 11

Previous studies, including our own analyses, have consistently shown an elevated risk of LVDD in patients with CKD. This elevated risk is already apparent in the early stages of kidney dysfunction, and the association strengthens with more advanced kidney dysfunction. 12 , 13 However, most existing studies rely on cross‐sectional data, which offer only a static snapshot and cannot determine whether these associations change over time. In particular, it remains unclear whether abnormalities in echocardiographic parameters indicative of LVDD in patients with CKD worsen with ongoing decline in kidney function or are established early and subsequently remain relatively stable. A few recent longitudinal studies have examined cardiac remodeling in CKD, but they often focus on specific subgroups (eg, older adults or patients with diabetes or hypertension), rely on single echocardiographic parameters, or use dichotomous outcomes. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18

To address this gap, we conducted a longitudinal study using real‐world data from a large and diverse outpatient cohort. We aimed to assess whether the association between kidney function and echocardiographic parameters indicative of LVDD changes over time. This study provides new insights into the timing and potential value of early cardiac monitoring in CKD and highlights the need to consider longitudinal patterns in risk stratification and clinical decision making.

Methods

The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines were used to ensure transparent reporting (Table S1).

Institutional Review Board Statement

Ethical review and approval was not applicable. This study did not involves humans or animals.

Informed Consent Statement

Informed consent was not required for this study as it involved the use of fully anonymized electronic health care records, where individuals were not identifiable.

Data Availability Statement

The data are based on information from confidential electronic health records. As such, data cannot be made publicly available. Requests for use of the data can be directed to the Utrecht Patient‐Oriented Database Project office (email: upod@umcutrecht.nl).

Cohort Selection

We conducted a cohort study using the Utrecht Patient‐Oriented Database. The Utrecht Patient Oriented Database comprises prospectively collected data of all patients treated at University Medical Center Utrecht, including data on clinical characteristics, medication, and laboratory measurements collected as part of routine care since 2000. 19 We identified adult patients who received outpatient care at the University Medical Center Utrecht between 2012 and 2022 and had undergone multiple echocardiographic assessments (ranging from 2 to 10). To minimize selection bias, we excluded patients with >10 echocardiograms, as these likely reflect more severe disease status. Eligible patients had at least 90 days and no more than 5 years between successive echocardiograms and serum creatinine measurements available within 31 days of each echocardiography. The date of the first echocardiogram was considered the index date (baseline). Patients were excluded if they had received renal replacement therapy (peritoneal dialysis, hemodialysis, or kidney transplantation) before enrollment or had a history of atrial fibrillation (n=41, as some parameters are not validated in this population 20 ), prosthetic heart valves, or significant valvular abnormalities, including stenosis or regurgitation of the aortic (n=280), mitral (n=117), tricuspid (n=0), or pulmonary valves (n=1).

Study Parameters

We collected data on demographics, history of hospitalization (based on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD‐10] and Diagnosis Treatment Combination codes), medication use (based on the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system), laboratory measurements, and repeated measurements of serum creatinine and echocardiographic data. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated for all patients using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration 2021 creatinine formula without ethnicity 21 and expressed in mL/min per 1.73 m2. Patients were categorized into 3 groups: ≥90 mL/min per 1.73 m2, ≥60 to <90 mL/min per 1.73 m2, and <60 mL/min per 1.73 m2. Table S2 contains baseline characteristics including individuals with eGFR <30 mL/min per 1.73 m2.

Medical history at baseline was defined as follows: hypertension as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, documented history of antihypertensive medication use, or diagnosis of hypertension recorded in medical records. Diabetes was defined as glycated hemoglobin ≥48 mmol/mol (≥6.5%), history of glucose‐lowering medication use, or diagnosis of diabetes recorded in medical records, without distinction between type 1 and type 2 diabetes. The prevalence of CVD was determined on the basis of a history of myocardial infarction, stroke or transient ischemic attack, peripheral artery disease, renal artery stenosis, or abdominal aortic aneurysm, identified using ICD‐10 codes recorded in medical records. History of malignancies, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and asthma were determined using ICD‐10 codes and medication records. HF was identified on the basis of ICD‐10 codes. Similarly, a history of cardiomyopathy was defined by the presence of any of the following ICD‐10 codes: I42.0 (dilated cardiomyopathy), I42.1 (obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy), I42.2 (other hypertrophic cardiomyopathy), I42.4 (endocardial fibroelastosis), I42.5 (other restrictive cardiomyopathy), I42.8 (other cardiomyopathies), or I42.9 (cardiomyopathy, unspecified). If no ICD codes, medication records, or relevant laboratory measurements were available for a given condition, the patient was assumed not to have the respective comorbidity. Details on the definitions of medical history can be found in Table S3. Additionally, Table S4 contains the prevalence of each cardiomyopathy category in our cohort.

Outcome of Interest

The primary outcomes were changes in echocardiographic parameters reflecting different physiologic domains related to diastolic function, including LV filling pressures (E/e'), chronic pressure burden (LAVI), pulmonary pressures (TR velocity), and structural remodeling (LVMI and left ventricular posterior wall thickness [LVPWT]). These parameters were selected based on guideline recommendations. 3 Longitudinal changes in these measures were used to evaluate the progression of LVDD and its association with kidney function across repeated echocardiographic assessments.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using mean±SD or median (interquartile range [IQR]) depending on the distribution, and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. To observe changes in echocardiographic parameters by eGFR groups, we calculated the change (Δ) in mean or median values of each parameter relative to the first echocardiographic assessment. This analysis was restricted to the first 5 echocardiographic time points to ensure sufficient sample size and comparability across follow‐up intervals.

To assess the longitudinal association between eGFR and echocardiographic parameters over time, we used linear mixed‐effects regression models incorporating repeated measurements of E/e′, LAVI, TR velocity, LVMI, and LVPWT from consecutive echocardiographic of the same individuals. We entered covariates assumed to have a potential prognostic impact into the model, including age, sex, history of hypertension, diabetes, CVD, and history of any cardiomyopathy. Separate models were constructed for each echocardiographic parameter.

Time was modeled as a continuous variable in all analyses. We compared models with random intercepts only to models including both random intercepts and random slopes for time. The random intercept–only model demonstrated best fit based on a lower Akaike information criterion and was therefore selected. Random intercepts were specified for each participant to account for within‐subject variability arising from repeated measurements. This approach accounts for correlation among repeated measurements, allowing observations from the same individual to share a common subject‐specific intercept.

To evaluate whether longitudinal trajectories of echocardiographic parameters differed by kidney function, we included main effects of eGFR and interaction terms between eGFR and time. Subgroup analyses stratified by sex were performed to explore potential differences in associations between male and female patients.

We did not perform imputation for missing echocardiographic outcomes, as linear mixed‐effects models are well suited for handling unbalanced data and can accommodate missing values. 22 Additionally, there is evidence that multiple imputation of missing data before linear mixed‐effect model analysis does not increase precision in the estimated rate of change. 23

Sensitivity Analyses

To assess the robustness and consistency of our results, we conducted sensitivity analyses using both linear and logistic regression models. We conducted linear regression analyses to examine the relationship between kidney function and each parameter at multiple time points. These models used eGFR as continuous variable, with adjustment for the same covariates as in the primary analysis. Additionally, we calculate the change (Δ) between estimates at each echocardiography with respect to the baseline (first echocardiogram) to estimate temporal changes. To further explore potential effect modification, we repeated the longitudinal regression analyses, estimating changes in echocardiograms across eGFR categories.

In addition, we conducted logistic regression analyses to evaluate the odds of clinically relevant echocardiographic abnormalities across eGFR categories. We applied the thresholds defined in the guidelines (E/e′ ratio >14, LAVI >34 mL/m2, TR velocity >2.8 m/s, LVMI >115 g/m2 for men and >95 g/m2 for women, and LVPWT >1.2 cm). 3 , 4 Odds ratios and 95% CIs were calculated for each eGFR category, with the >90 mL/min per 1.73 m2 group serving as the reference.

Finally, as an additional sensitivity analysis, we fitted linear mixed‐effects models with eGFR entered as a categorical variable (>90, 60–89, and <60 mL/min per 1.73 m2) to assess consistency with the primary mixed‐effects analyses in which eGFR was modeled as a continuous variable.

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (R Core Team; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 24

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The initial cohort consisted of 3129 patients. After excluding 1317 patients due to history of HF, atrial fibrillation, or valvular heart disease, or because they had only 1 or >10 echocardiography assessments (Figure 1), our final cohort comprised 1812 patients (62.3% males) with a total of 5653 echocardiograms.

Medication use at baseline also varied by kidney function. Antithrombotic agents were the most prescribed across all groups, used by approximately one third of patients. Diuretic use increased with declining kidney function, from 9.2% in the >90 group to 17.1% in the eGFR <60 group. Similarly, the use of renin–angiotensin system agents was slightly more frequent in patients with reduced kidney function (12.1% in the <60 group versus 10.8% in the >90 group). β Blockers and calcium channel blockers were used at similar rates across groups, while antihypertensive drugs were rarely prescribed overall. Echocardiographic Parameters Over Time

The median number of echocardiograms was 2 (IQR, 2–10). Echocardiographic parameters were not available for all examinations, reflecting routine clinical practice. Across all 5653 echocardiograms, the proportion of missing values varied by parameter, with missingness of 35.0% for E/e′ ratio, 63.2% for LAVI, 56.3% for TR velocity, 39.4% for LVMI, and 32.9% for LVPWT.

At baseline, echocardiograms were progressively worse with declining kidney function. The median E/e′ ratio increased from 8.81 (IQR, 7.04–11.38) in the >90 group to 10.99 (IQR, 8.53–15.43) in the <60 group. Similarly, LVMI rose from 87.04 (IQR, 73.55–106.94) g/m2 to 98.54 (IQR, 82.65–128.24) g/m2, and TR velocity increased from 2.36±0.41 m/s to 2.63±0.51 m/s.

Longitudinal changes in these parameters, across eGFR categories, were modest and variable. E/e′ ratio fluctuated slightly across follow‐up assessments, with no consistent trend. In the <60 eGFR group, E/e′ increased by 2.76 units at the fifth echocardiogram compared with baseline, but intermediate time points showed mixed changes. For LAVI, while patients with lower eGFR had higher baseline values, the most pronounced increase over time was observed in the >90 eGFR group at the third and fourth assessments. LVMI tended to decrease over time in the >90 and 60–89 groups, while remaining relatively stable or increasing slightly in the <60 group. TR velocity and LVPWT showed minimal changes across time points. Table 2 presents complete results of echocardiographic parameters at baseline and the changes in the subsequent measurements.

Linear Mixed‐Model Analysis

Linear mixed‐effect models showed that declining eGFR was significantly associated with increased E/e′ ratio, LAVI, TR velocity, and LVMI, with estimates of −0.03 (P<0.001), −0.04 (P=0.005), −0.00 (P<0.001), and −0.16 (P<0.001) respectively, meaning that as eGFR was lower, the parameter increased. These associations, although small, remained after adjusting for age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, and cardiomyopathy. Importantly, the interaction term between time and eGFR was not statistically significant for any of the echocardiographic parameters, indicating that the association of kidney function on LVDD markers remained consistent throughout the follow‐up period (Table 3).

Other covariates also showed independent associations. Age was consistently associated with worsening echocardiographic parameters. Each additional year of age was linked to higher E/e′ (0.09, P<0.001), LAVI (0.28, P<0.001), TR velocity (0.01, P<0.001), LVMI (0.45, P<0.001), and LVPWT (0.00, P<0.001). Male sex was associated with lower E/e′ ratio (−1.38, P<0.001) but higher LVMI (9.90, P<0.001) and LVPWT (0.06, P<0.001).

Furthermore, the presence of diabetes was significantly associated with higher E/e′ ratio (1.53, P<0.001) and LVPWT (0.04, P<0.001), but with lower LAVI (−2.39, P=0.019). Finally, cardiomyopathy showed the most pronounced associations, with significantly elevated E/e′ ratio (2.43, P<0.001), LAVI (3.86, P=0.010), LVMI (22.36, P<0.001), and LVPWT (0.11, P<0.001) (Table 3).

Sex‐stratified analyses showed consistent directions of association for both women and men (Tables S6 and S7).

Sensitivity Analyses

In fully adjusted linear regression, eGFR was significantly associated with higher E/e′ at baseline, but the association weakened over time, with no significant differences observed at later echocardiograms. For LAVI, a significant increase was observed at the third echocardiogram, but the direction and magnitude of association varied across time points. LVMI showed a significant inverse association with eGFR at baseline, but at the third follow‐up, the association became positive. When analyses were stratified by kidney function categories, the group with eGFR <60 exhibited more pronounced and persistent adverse changes in echocardiographic measures.

Logistic regression analyses further supported these findings. In fully adjusted models, participants with eGFR <60 had significantly higher odds of abnormal E/e' ratio, LAVI, and TR velocity. The likelihood of having an elevated LVMI was highest at baseline and the second echocardiogram in the eGFR <60. No significant associations were observed for increased LVPWT over time.

Mixed‐effects models using categorical eGFR yielded results consistent with the primary analyses, with worse echocardiographic parameters observed in lower eGFR categories and no significant interaction between time and eGFR category. See complete results in Tables S8 through S11.

Discussion

In this longitudinal retrospective study, including data over a 10‐year time span of 1812 patients, lower kidney function was consistently associated with higher values of echocardiographic parameters reflecting different, although related, physiologic domains of diastolic function. E/e' ratio primarily reflects LV filling pressures and is sensitive to loading conditions, LAVI reflects cumulative exposure to elevated filling pressures over time, and TR velocity provides insight into pulmonary and right‐sided hemodynamics, while LVMI reflects structural remodeling and hypertrophy. Although the magnitude of these associations was modest in absolute terms, the consistent results across multiple parameters suggest that the cardiac burden associated with CKD is already established at the time of the first echocardiographic assessment. This phenotype did not show evidence of further divergence over time across kidney function strata, suggesting that routine echocardiographic monitoring may have limited sensitivity to detect progressive diastolic changes in this population.

A key finding of our study is the absence of a statistically significant interaction between time and kidney function for any of the echocardiographic parameters studied. This indicates that, within this cohort, the adverse diastolic phenotype associated with lower kidney function was already present at the time of the first assessment and did not measurably worsen on serial imaging. This finding does not imply absence of myocardial progression but rather reflects the behavior of the specific parameters studied, which capture different physiologic domains and are influenced by loading conditions, clinical management, and measurement variability. Routine echocardiographic parameters reflect integrated hemodynamic and structural effects rather than isolated myocardial properties. Consequently, this observation highlights the limited sensitivity of routinely acquired echocardiographic indices to capture subtle longitudinal changes in intrinsic myocardial function outside protocol‐driven imaging studies.

Several pathophysiological mechanisms may explain these observations. Hemodynamic factors likely play a central role, including hypertension, chronic volume overload, and increased arterial stiffness, which are common in CKD and contribute to elevated filling pressures, left atrial enlargement, and pulmonary hypertension. 25 In addition, activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system may influence cardiac structure and function through both hemodynamic and nonhemodynamic pathways. Beyond hemodynamic stressors, metabolic and systemic factors characteristic of CKD, such as anemia, inflammation, oxidative stress, and accumulation of uremic toxins, may further modulate cardiac function.

Compensatory physiological mechanisms and the stabilizing effects of standard treatments, such as antihypertensive therapy, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors, glucose‐lowering agents, and lifestyle interventions may also contribute to the observed lack of differential progression. However, treatment effects were not explicitly accounted for in this study, and medication use and adherence may have influenced echocardiographic trajectories. Future studies incorporating time‐varying pharmacological data are needed to better understand how therapeutic strategies influence the trajectory of LVDD in CKD populations.

Our findings are consistent with previous follow‐up studies showing that reduced eGFR contributes to the risk of HF, kidney events, and cardiovascular and all‐cause death. 19 , 26 , 27 However, this study differs in several important aspects. First, the study population reflects real‐world clinical practice and consisted of patients drawn from routine outpatient care. Thus, we analyzed a more heterogeneous population than prior studies such as Buckley et al 26 and Hallan et al, 27 which focused on older adults or patients with diabetes and hypertension. Another key distinction of our study is the inclusion of multiple echocardiographic parameters as continuous measures rather than looking at dichotomous events. The use of hospital data, which provide access to detailed and accurate medical records, contrasts with community‐based studies that often rely on self‐reported data. This allows the external validity of our findings, making them applicable to everyday clinical settings. However, it also introduces challenges inherent to real‐world data, such as variability in measurement timing, missing data, and indication bias. 28 For example, patients undergoing repeated echocardiography may represent a higher‐risk subgroup with more severe disease or more intensive monitoring. We attempted to mitigate this by excluding patients with HF, patients with significant valvular disease, and those receiving renal replacement therapy, but residual bias may remain.

From a clinical perspective, these findings carry important implications. First, the consistent association between lower kidney function and worse values across multiple echocardiographic markers of diastolic dysfunction indicates that patients with CKD present with early cardiac remodeling that is already detectable at the time of the first clinical echocardiographic assessment. Clinicians should consider kidney function as a relevant factor when interpreting echocardiographic findings, as the observed diastolic abnormalities may reflect CKD‐associated hemodynamic and structural effects rather than primary myocardial disease. Second, the concordance of abnormalities across filling pressures, left atrial remodeling, and ventricular hypertrophy suggests that these echocardiographic findings may serve as baseline phenotype markers of cardiac involvement in CKD, helping to characterize the cardiovascular burden at presentation. While outcome data would be needed to establish their prognostic value, this phenotypic characterization may inform future risk stratification approaches in patients with CKD. Third, the absence of detectable longitudinal change on serial routine echocardiography raises the possibility that standard echocardiographic markers of diastolic dysfunction may not be sufficiently sensitive to capture progressive diastolic deterioration over time in clinical practice. This has implications for the design of monitoring strategies in patients with CKD and supports the need for prospective studies that incorporate more sensitive myocardial and atrial functional parameters to evaluate whether gradual diastolic progression occurs and whether it can be captured with contemporary imaging techniques.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the use of real‐world data allowed a long follow‐up period of ≈10 years and the inclusion of a large, clinically diverse patient population. This resulted in >5000 echocardiographic assessments, providing a rich data set for longitudinal assessment. Second, unlike previous studies that focused primarily on the E/e′, we examined multiple parameters, including LAVI, TR velocity, LVMI, and LVPWT, offering a better understanding of the structural and functional changes associated with declining kidney function. Third, although the diagnosis of LVDD typically relies on a combination of echocardiographic abnormalities, we deliberately analyzed each parameter individually. This approach allowed us to identify which specific aspects of diastolic structure and function were most sensitive to changes in kidney function over time.

Nonetheless, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, echocardiographic examinations in this study were performed as part of routine outpatient care according to clinical indication, without a standardized imaging protocol. Contemporary myocardial and atrial functional parameters, including left atrial strain, LV global longitudinal strain, and separate assessment of septal and lateral e' velocities, were not systematically available. Our analysis was therefore restricted to routinely acquired guideline‐recommended markers of diastolic dysfunction, which capture filling pressures, structural remodeling, and chronic hemodynamic burden but do not directly assess intrinsic myocardial relaxation properties.

Second, patients who undergo repeated echocardiography assessment may represent a subgroup at higher risk and may receive more frequent and detailed evaluation that could influence observed associations. At the same time, patients with more advanced CKD or more severe CVD may have been less likely to survive, which could lead to an underestimation of longitudinal progression in LVDD. We attempted to mitigate these sources of bias by excluding patients with established HF, those with valvular disease, and those with a history of renal replacement therapy. Future studies incorporating information on the indications for echocardiography and survival status could help to understand the impact of these biases on longitudinal echocardiographic assessment in CKD populations.

In addition, LAVI and TR velocity had a substantial proportion of missing values, likely reflecting indication‐driven acquisition rather than random variability. These measurements are preferentially obtained when elevated filling pressures, pulmonary hypertension, or HF are suspected, resulting in structural acquisition bias. Because contemporary diastolic grading algorithms require the simultaneous availability of several echocardiographic parameters, complete grading was not feasible in a large proportion of examinations. Importantly, despite this bias toward more complete measurement in sicker patients, we did not observe meaningful temporal changes in diastolic parameters among the subgroup with available measurements, suggesting that the observed stability over time is unlikely to be driven by differential acquisition patterns.

Third, the small number of patients with advanced CKD (eGFR <30 mL/min per 1.73 m2) limits our ability to draw conclusions about this group of patients. Third, the definition of cardiomyopathy in our study was based on ICD‐10 codes within category I42, except for subtypes I42.3 (endomyocardial disease, eosinophilic) and I42.7 (cardiomyopathy due to alcohol). Due to small sample size, we could not test the impact of each specific cardiomyopathy subtype in our outcomes. Thus, we group all patients with any ICD‐10 code I42 to adjust for the presence or absence of cardiomyopathy in our analyses. While this approach ensured consistency and statistical feasibility, it may have masked subtype‐specific effects.

Finally, defining an “index date” in observational data remains challenging, as disease progression cannot be precisely determined. For example, medication use was described at baseline, and we did not adjust for individual drug classes in the regression models. Medication exposure was incorporated into the definition of key comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and CVD. Additional adjustment for medication may therefore result in overadjustment or collinearity, particularly given the absence of longitudinal treatment data.

Conclusions

In this large longitudinal study from routine clinical practice, lower kidney function was consistently associated with a more adverse diastolic echocardiographic phenotype, characterized by higher filling pressures, greater structural remodeling, and increased hemodynamic burden. This phenotype was already established at initial clinical assessment and did not show measurable further divergence on serial routine echocardiographies. These findings support the presence of persistent differences in cardiac hemodynamic profile across kidney function strata and highlight the importance of interpreting echocardiographic parameters within the broader clinical context of kidney disease. Future prospective studies incorporating contemporary myocardial functional indices and clinical outcomes are needed to clarify the mechanistic and prognostic significance of these observations.
