Effect of second-hand smoking on the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting in children after adenoidectomy: a single-centre retrospective study
Authors: Michal Frelich, Filip Burša, Ondřej Jor, Vojtěch Vodička, Kristýna Křenková, Natálie Vítková, Adéla Kondé, Michal Parma, Denis Buršík, Patrik Šámaj, Nela Walachová, Peter Kantor, Viktoria Hranková, Simona Motalová, Peter Sklienka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) are among the most common postoperative complications affecting children. In adult patients, cigarette smoking significantly reduces the incidence of PONV, although the mechanism underlying this effect is unclear. Here we aimed to investigate whether environmental tobacco smoke exposure influenced the PONV incidence among children.MethodsThis monocentric retrospective study included 1348 children (1–15 years of age), who underwent endoscopic adenoidectomy under general anaesthesia, between 22 March 2017 and 26 June 2023. Data for this study were obtained from the patient’s medical records and from a telephone conversation with their parent.ResultsOur analysis revealed that the PONV incidence was significantly lower in children exposed to environmental tobacco smoke (ETS), compared to in children who were not passive smokers (13.9% vs 22.1%; p < 0.001). Children of non-smoking parents were at a higher risk of developing PONV (OR: 1.82; 95% CI: 1.31–2.58).ConclusionsAlthough exposure to ETS has many undesirable consequences, it is also associated with a lower incidence of PONV. Conversely, children without ETS exposure are at a higher risk of PONV development and should receive proactive pharmacological prophylaxis.Keywords: Postoperative nausea and vomiting, Second-hand smoking, Environmental tobacco smoke, Adenoidectomy

Background

Postoperativenausea and vomiting (PONV) is a significant cause of postoperative morbidity among children, particularly after ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgery [1]. Children who undergo these procedures exhibit a PONV incidence of up to 73% [2]. The adverse effects of PONV include dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, and aspiration [3]. Moreover, PONV can contribute to postoperative bleeding, which is a greatly feared and sometimes fatal complication following ENT surgery [4].

PONV pathogenesis is multifactorial, with some risk factors overlapping between adult patients and children [5]. In 1998, Apfel et al. identified non-smoking as a significant risk factor for PONV in adult patients, [6] and this finding was confirmed by subsequent studies [7, 8]. Although the exact mechanism through which smoking reduces PONV incidence is unclear, this protective effect is thought to mainly result from the fact that chronic exposure to cigarette smoke accelerates the metabolism of emetogenic anaesthetics [9].

Although children are usually not active smokers, they may regularly inhale tobacco combustion products through passive smoking. Second-hand smoke, also known as environmental tobacco smoke (ETS), has a number of harmful effects on children’s health. ETS exposure makes children more susceptible to respiratory infections, asthma, middle ear infections, and bacterial meningitis. Additionally, exposure to ETS doubles the risk of adverse respiratory events, particularly laryngospasm, during the perioperative period [10].

Although little is known about the effect of passive smoking on PONV in children, it can be assumed that exposure to ETS accelerates the metabolism of emetogenic anaesthetics in children as it does in adult smokers, given that same substances are inhaled. However, the only published clinical study found no association between ETS exposure and reduced postoperative vomiting in the recovery room [5]. Notably, this study only evaluated vomiting, not postoperative nausea, and only monitored patients for a short period of time. To further clarify this subject, here we conducted a retrospective study to determine whether ETS exposure is associated with reduced PONV in children within 24 h after surgery. The results of this analysis could enable more accurate prediction of PONV risk, thereby improving the effectiveness of antiemetic prophylaxis for children.

Methods

Study design

This single-centre retrospective study included a total of 1411 patients aged 1–15 years who underwent endoscopic adenoidectomy under general anaesthesia between 22 March 2017 and 26 June 2023. Exclusion criteria were ASA of III or greater, history of PONV, and chronic corticosteroid medication. The study flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the study. PONV, postoperative nausea and vomiting; ETS, environmental tobacco smoke

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital of Ostrava on 23 January 2025 (reference number: 24/2025) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, we were not required to obtain signed informed consent from the patients’ parents; however, all parents consented to the use of patient data for research purposes upon admission to the hospital.

Data collection

All study data were obtained from the patients’ medical records—including the children’s demographic data, duration of surgery, method of securing the airway, and administration of antiemetic prophylaxis. The study only included patients who underwent inhalational induction of general anaesthesia, which was then maintained with sevoflurane at a minimal alveolar concentration (MAC) of 1–1.2, in accordance with the practices of our facility. The parents’ smoking status was routinely recorded at the time of the child’s admission to the hospital. If at least one parent regularly smoked in the household where the child lived, the approximate number of cigarettes smoked per day was recorded. For this study, a child was considered exposed to ETS if a parent regularly smoked 10 or more cigarettes per day.

PONV was defined as the onset of nausea and/or vomiting within 24 h after surgery. Postoperative nausea was assessed using the Baxter Animated Retching Faces (BARF) score, which is a validated picture scale that comprises a series of pictures of faces expressing increasing nausea intensity, and is suitable for assessing nausea in children who are unable to verbally express their feelings. The child is instructed to select the face with the expression that corresponds to their level of nausea, and the BARF score ranges from 0 (no nausea) to 10 (most severe nausea) [11]. Following adenoidectomy, the BARF score was assessed at 2-h intervals and documented in the medical record. According to the clinical practice at our department, a BARF score of >4 defines the presence of postoperative nausea. The occurrence of postoperative vomiting in patients was identified from nursing records. As part of the long-term monitoring of postoperative outcomes among children at our institution, a parent was contacted 48 h after the child’s discharge to ask if the child had developed PDNV (child self-reported nausea or vomiting). Patients were excluded from the study if their records lacked data relevant to the study objective, or if it was not possible to contact their parents by telephone.

Statistical analysis

Numerical variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as absolute numbers and relative frequencies (%). Between-group differences were assessed using the Mann–Whitney test, or the chi-square test of independence for contingency tables. We performed a detailed analysis of risk factors for PONV using logistic regression, and the results are presented as odd ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A forest plot was generated to describe the multivariable logistic regression model. The significance level was set at 0.05, and statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.1, www.r-project.org).

A total of 368 patients (27.3%) had ETS exposure. Our analysis revealed that the PONV incidence was significantly lower among children with ETS exposure, compared to children who were not passive smokers (13.9% vs 22.1%; p < 0.001). The PDNV incidence did not differ between these groups (p = 0.517). The patients with versus without ETS exposure did not differ in baseline demographics, duration of surgery, or perioperative opioid dose. Antiemetic prophylaxis was given to only 14% of patients in the whole cohort, and this rate did not differ between the groups with versus without ETS exposure (p = 0.283; Table 2).

Discussion

Our present results showed that children exposed to ETS had a lower incidence of PONV within 24 h after surgery, compared to children not exposed to passive smoking. There are several possible explanations for this finding. Cigarette smoke contains approximately 5000 substances, some of which may theoretically have an antiemetic effect [9, 12]. However, it is unlikely that this would reduce PONV incidence, since this would require that the antiemetic substance have additional effects mediated by dopamine, 5-hydroxytryptamine, and other receptors involved in PONV development [9, 13]. An alternative explanation is that the reduction of PONV is a consequence of adaptation to long-term exposure to cigarette smoke. Such adaptation includes extensive changes in the function of nicotine receptors in the brain, which occur during chronic exposure to cigarette smoke [9]. Supporting this theory, Whalen et al. demonstrated that cigarette smoke does not have an immediate pharmacological effect towards preventing PONV. [14]

The most likely mechanism underlying PONV risk reduction is the induction of cytochrome P450, particularly through exposure to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons [9]. This system plays key roles in the metabolism and detoxification of various drugs, environmental chemicals, pollutants, natural plant products, and some anaesthetics [15]. In the setting of PONV, the most important isoenzyme is CYP2E1, which is responsible for the metabolism of inhalational anaesthetics [16, 17]. Notably, inhalation anaesthesia is one of the most important triggers of PONV within the first 6 h after surgery [18]. CYP2E1 induction by cigarette smoke leads to faster metabolism of inhalational anaesthetics, resulting in faster and smoother recovery from anaesthesia [9]. This process could potentially mitigate the emetic effect of these agents. This theory is consistent with our finding that the PDNV incidence did not differ between children with versus without ETS exposure, since inhalational anaesthetics only significantly contribute to PONV development within 24 h after surgery [18]. Cytochrome P450 induction by cigarette smoke can affect the metabolism of other anaesthetics, particularly opioid analgesics [19]. Faster analgesic metabolism leads to more intense postoperative pain among passive smokers, necessitating repeated opioid doses [20].

Most of the patients in our study were preschool children, who usually experience the highest exposure to ETS in the home, due to the large amount of time spent with their smoking parents. They are also exposed to tobacco smoke residue on their parents’ hair and clothing, as well as on furniture and in cars (‘third-hand smoking’). This significantly increased tobacco exposure likely results in a greater effect on PONV, compared to in older children. Furthermore, young children have faster metabolic and respiratory rates [10].

Our results are consistent with the findings reported by Chimbira and Sweeney, which demonstrated that adult smokers experience a lower PONV incidence than non-smokers [7]. Furthermore, their reported difference in PONV incidence was very similar to our present results. The protective effect of smoking against PONV development has also been demonstrated in adult patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy [8, 21].

To date, only one study has investigated the effect of passive smoking on the PONV incidence among children. Unlike our present study, the previous research did not demonstrate that PONV was less common in children exposed to passive smoking [5]. However, that study only evaluated postoperative vomiting within the first 2 h after surgery. Furthermore, the questionnaire used in the previous study defined exposure to cigarette smoke as spending time with smokers, rather than living with them, as in our study.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective and single-centre study. However, we found that the two groups did not significantly differ with regard to confounding factors known to influence PONV incidence, such as length of surgery and administration of antiemetics. Based on current knowledge, the method of securing the airway does not affect the incidence of PONV [1, 13]. Secondly, ETS exposure was determined solely by asking the child’s parents, with no use of objective measurements, such as determining cotinine levels in saliva, urine, or blood. However, asking parents about ETS exposure during the preoperative examination strikes a reasonable balance between acceptability and reliability [10]. Finally, this study revealed an association between exposure to ETS and a lower incidence of PONV, but was unable to establish a causal relationship.

Our present results provide two key insights for clinical practice and future research. Firstly, based on our findings, we suggest a more liberal approach towards administering antiemetic prophylaxis to children without ETS exposure. This is particularly important for patients at otherwise low risk of PONV, since recent recommendations suggest one of two options: either do not administer an antiemetic (‘wait and see’ strategy), or administer one antiemetic [22]. As children without ETS exposure were almost twice as likely to experience PONV, we suggest administering antiemetic prophylaxis to this group of patients, even if no other risk factors are present. This approach is consistent with Schlesinger’s recent publication, in which he highlighted that PONV management based only on scoring systems can result in underestimation of the actual risk to patients. This is partly because the risk factors used to create the POVOC and VPOP scoring systems for children are not clearly defined [13]. We also observed this problem in our study, as we found that motion sickness did not increase the risk of PONV, unlike in previous studies [23, 24]. A post hoc analysis revealed that many parents reported motion sickness in their children, even based on isolated cases of nausea or vomiting during car travel—especially when there was a short time period between eating and travelling. In other words, not all of the children considered to have motion sickness in our study actually experienced motion sickness. The same issue applies to other risk factors that lack a precise definition. Another implication of our present findings is that ETS exposure should be considered a significant confounding factor in all clinical studies investigating PONV prevention in children.

Conclusions

Our data suggests that children who are not exposed to ETS have a higher risk of experiencing PONV. Therefore, antiemetic prophylaxis should be considered for this group of patients, regardless of the presence of other PONV risk factors.
