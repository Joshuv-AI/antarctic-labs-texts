Association between electronic screen time and childhood asthma: a cross-sectional study using NHANES 1999–2014 data
Authors: Ruixue Xiao, Huiguo Zhang, Yuxiang Ye, Xiaomo Yang, Wei Ju
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood asthma prevalence has risen globally, paralleling increased electronic screen exposure. This study investigated the association between electronic screen time (TV, computer, and total device use) and asthma risk in U.S. children.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis of 15,526 children (aged < 20 years) from NHANES (1999–2014) was conducted. Screen time (hours/day) was assessed via questionnaires. Asthma status was self-reported. Multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic splines, and mediation analyses adjusted for demographics, obesity, income, and environmental factors.ResultsTotal screen time was positively associated with asthma risk (adjusted OR = 1.48 per hour increase, 95% CI:1.26–1.75, p < 0.001). Computer use ≥ 5 h/day conferred 30% higher asthma odds (OR = 1.30, 95% CI:1.06–1.60). Dose–response analysis showed elevated risk even at ≤ 2 h/day (OR = 1.18, 95% CI:1.04–1.34). Subgroup analyses (age, gender, body Mass Index) confirmed consistency (p < 0.05). No mediation by physical activity was observed.ConclusionsProlonged electronic screen time independently increased childhood asthma risk, emphasizing its role as a modifiable factor. Clinicians should integrate screen behavior assessments into asthma management. Future research should explore causal pathways, including circadian disruption and indoor pollutant interactions.

Introduction

Asthma, one of the most prevalent chronic respiratory disorders among children, represents a significant public health concern. In 2019, asthma affected approximately 358 million individuals globally, with an estimated prevalence of current asthma at 5.4% (95% CI: 3.23–8.97) among those aged 5 to 69 years [1]. Predictions suggest that by 2050, the prevalence of childhood asthma will rise to approximately 2,608.05 cases per 100,000 individuals (95% UI: 1,632.94–3,868.26) [2]. sthma not only compromises the quality of life in pediatric populations but also leads to irreversible pulmonary impairment and life-threatening complications, thereby constituting a critical public health challenge worldwide.

Recent epidemiological research on asthma has transitioned from a singular environmental exposure pathogenesis model to investigating multifactorial mechanisms involving complex interactions [3–5]. Beyond traditional risk factors—including genetic predisposition and ambient air pollution—researchers are increasingly investigating lifestyle influences on asthma pathogenesis [6]. For example, metabolic factors like high-sugar diets, sedentary behavior, and obesity have been demonstrated to elevate asthma risk through systemic inflammation or immune dysregulation [7, 8]. Indoor environmental triggers, such as passive smoking, dust mites, and mold exposure, are also recognized as critical contributors [9, 10]. Notably, the proliferation of electronic devices has led to a substantial rise in pediatric screen time (e.g., televisions, computers, smartphones) [11–13]. This trend may impact respiratory health through direct or indirect pathways. However, current evidence on the screen time-asthma association remains limited, and its underlying mechanisms and clinical implications require urgent exploration.

The proliferation of electronic screens and devices in the twenty-first century has established computers and televisions as predominant forms of entertainment for children. Extensive research indicates that such screen-based engagement significantly impacts child health [14–16]. Notably, the electronic screen time of minors continues to rise; Data reveal [17] that American children's daily television viewing increased substantially from 1997 to 2014, with the latter reporting an average of three hours per day. According to Common Sense Media (2019), children aged 8 to 12 spend approximately five hours daily on entertainment-based electronic devices, while adolescents aged 13 to 18 devote nearly seven hours per day. The adverse health effects of excessive electronic device use in children are well-established across multiple domains. In the context of metabolic disorders, prolonged sedentary behavior and increased screen time elevate the risk of obesity, a known independent predictor of asthma. Evidence indicates that children who engage in more than two hours of screen time per day face an 80% higher risk of obesity and increased odds of developing adolescent fatty liver disease (OR = 2.10, 95% CI: 1.34–3.29; p = 0.032) [18, 19]. In visual health, a dose–response relationship between screen exposure and myopia progression is widely acknowledged [20]. Furthermore, excessive device use is associated with neurobehavioral sequelae, including sleep disturbances and attention deficits, which may disrupt immune homeostasis through neuroendocrine mechanisms [21, 22]. In recent studies, we have discovered the potential association between electronic screens and asthma. Excessive screen time may further disrupt sleep patterns [16]. Inadequate or poor-quality sleep compromises immune homeostasis, elevating pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) while suppressing anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Consequently, this cascade exacerbates asthma-related airway inflammation [23, 24].

To further explore the connection between electronic screen usage time (computer and television use) and childhood asthma, we screened the data using the the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database in the United States and included eligible child participants to analyze the association between the two, providing certain clinical evidence references for children's health.

Methods

We performed a cross-sectional analysis to evaluate associations between computer, television, and total screen time with childhood asthma. Data were derived from the U.S. NHANES, with participants screened across cycles spanning 1999–2014. The initial cohort comprised 64,598 respondents. Individuals aged > 18 years (n = 38,025) were excluded. Subsequently, participants with incomplete asthma status data (n = 3,755) or missing screen time records (n = 7,292) were excluded, yielding a final analytical sample of 15,526 participants.

Confirmation of the ending

To identify participants with asthma, we evaluated their responses to the medical condition questionnaire asking,"Has a doctor or other health expert ever told you that you have asthma?"Those who answered"Yes"were classified as asthma patients, while those who answered"No"were classified as non-asthma patients.

Determination of the use of electronic screens

To obtain the data related to the average daily electronic screen usage time, we reviewed the physical activity questionnaires in the NHANES database through two questions [25, 26],"Hours watch TV or videos past 30 days"."Hours use computer past 30 days"assesses the time children spent using electronic screens each day in the past 30 days. On this basis, we add up the TV screen usage time and the computer screen usage time to obtain the total daily electronic screen usage time (ESTT) in the past 30 days.

Physical activities

By assessing whether children Have engaged in moderate or vigorous physical activities in the past 30 days, the assessment contents include [1] Moderate physical activities: Do you do any moderate-intensity sports, fitness, or recreational activities that cause a small increase in breathing or heart rate such as brisk walking, bicycling, swimming, or golf for at least 10 min continuously? (2) Vigorous physical activities: Do you do any vigorous-intensity sports, fitness, or recreational activities that cause large increases in breathing or heart rate like running or basketball for at least 10 min continuously?

Covariates

In our analysis, a series of potential confounding factors were considered to separate the effects of electronic screens and asthma. These include age 1–18 years old), race, and income-to-poverty ratio (PIR) (PIR is divided into three levels: Less than or equal to 1.3 indicates low, and between 1.3 and 3.5 indicates medium. Obesity in children and adolescents is defined as a body Mass Index(BMI) higher than the 95th gender-specific percentile of the age-specific BMI growth chart of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in the United States [27]; Second-hand smoke exposure, family history of asthma and allergy (whether close relatives have asthma). Extract data related to these covariates from demographic, dietary, examination and questionnaire data to ensure comprehensive adjustments for factors that may affect the research results.

Moral statement

This study used in human subjects were acquired from NHANES agreement, the agreement has obtained the national center for health statistics (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Nhanes/) institutions approved by the review committee. All participants or their legal guardians provided written informed consent forms.

Data analysis

Sample weights were applied as recommended to account for NHANES’s complex survey design. Baseline characteristics were summarized using weighted means ± standard errors (SE) for continuous variables and weighted frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. We followed NHANES analytic guidelines while conducting our analyses, taking into account complex sampling designs and sampling weights [28]. The sample weight for this study was determined using the “WTMECPRP” variable from the 1999–2014 cycles. Weighted chi-square tests and survey-weighted t-tests assessed differences in categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Survey-weighted logistic regression models evaluated associations between television, computer, and total screen time with childhood asthma. Three models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for sex, age, and race/ethnicity), and Model 3 (fully adjusted for all covariates). The risk of increased asthma risk due to screen use was expressed by odd ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). To assess nonlinearity, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with four knots (5th, 35th, 65th, 95th percentiles) were fitted, treating total screen time as the independent variable and asthma prevalence as the outcome. If nonlinearity was detected (p-nonlinear < 0.05), threshold effects were quantified using piecewise linear regression. Stratified analyses were performed by age, sex, race/ethnicity, secondhand smoke exposure, PIR, and family history of asthma/allergies (To determine whether different population characteristics would have different effects on the outcome, we conducted an interaction test analysis between subgroups). Sensitivity analyses evaluated the robustness of findings across alternative model specifications. All analyses employed two-tailed tests, with p < 0.05 deemed statistically significant.

Dose–response analysis and threshold analysis of electronic screen usage time and asthma prevalence

The nonlinear association between ESTT and asthma was detected using the RCS model. The results in Fig. 2 revealed that there was no nonlinear association between ESTT and asthma (p-nonlinear = 0.349), but it was found that with the increase of ESTT, the risk of asthma increased significantly (p-overall < 0.001). To further determine the protective effect of recreational activities on screen usage, we took moderate and vigorous recreational activities as intermediate variables for mediation analysis to explore the association between recreational activities in total screen usage time and the risk of asthma in Fig. 3. Mediation analysis showed that total screen time had a significant overall effect on the risk of asthma (coefficient = 0.00601, 95% CI: 0.00390–0.00808, P < 0.001) in Fig. 3A. Although moderate/vigorous recreational activities were included as potential mediating variables, their indirect effects did not reach statistical significance (coefficient of moderate recreational activities = −0.00004, 95% CI: −0.00022–0.00009, P = 0.480;) In Fig. 3B, the coefficient of intense recreational activities = −0.00007, 95% CI: −0.00026–0.00002, P = 0.340. After adjusting for this mediating variable, the direct effect of total screen time on asthma remained significant, indicating that the association between the two was mainly driven by the direct effect of screen time or other pathways not included in the analysis (such as sedentary behavior, exposure to indoor air pollutants, etc.). Discussion

This study, utilizing a large sample from the NHANES database, was the first to identify an independent positive association between children’s daily electronic screen time—specifically television and computer use—and asthma risk. The results demonstrated that, after adjusting for confounders including age, gender, race, family income, and obesity status, children with daily screen time exceeding 2 h (whether from computers, televisions, or total screen exposure) exhibited a significantly increased asthma risk, with the association persisting in non-obese children (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: [1.07–1.12]).

With the rapid advancement of information technology, screen usage has become an indispensable component of people's daily lives. Children are in formative stages of growth and development, and unhealthy lifestyle habits may adversely affect their physiological and psychological well-being. Empirical evidence demonstrates a direct correlation between prolonged screen exposure and elevated risks of pediatric myopia [20]. Furthermore, longitudinal studies indicate that excessive screen time elevates susceptibility to obesity and psychosocial disturbances, including emotional dysregulation and behavioral disorders [29, 30]. Consistent with our findings, a Brazilian cross-sectional study of adolescents demonstrated a positive correlation between screen time duration and asthma prevalence. This relationship exhibits a dose-dependent trend, with asthma risk incrementally rising alongside extended exposure (PR = 1.19; 95% CI: 1.01–1.42) [31]. Contrastingly, a 2022 cohort study investigating adolescent physical activity levels, lung function, and asthma outcomes revealed no significant association between screen time and asthma incidence, though it identified a negative correlation with forced vital capacity (FVC) [32].

Screen time may contribute to an elevated risk of pediatric asthma through multiple pathophysiological pathways. First, prolonged screen exposure compromises sleep quality in children, manifesting as reduced sleep duration and delayed sleep onset latency. Sleep deprivation alters circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL)−1β, IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10 [33, 34], potentially exacerbating allergic sensitization and asthma pathogenesis [35]. Second, screen time and physical activity represent competing behaviors. Extended screen engagement displaces opportunities for moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA). Our subgroup analysis demonstrated that children engaging in regular MVPA exhibited a modestly reduced asthma risk (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.79–0.98) compared to sedentary peers. Evidence confirms that structured physical activity enhances early-life pulmonary development, particularly alveolar maturation and airway elasticity. A large-scale meta-analysis revealed that children in the highest quartile of physical activity exhibited a 12% reduction in asthma incidence (RR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.82–0.95) [36]. Third, prolonged screen exposure is associated with heightened susceptibility to pediatric obesity. Obesity constitutes a critical determinant of compromised pulmonary function. This obesity-mediated pulmonary impairment may potentiate asthma susceptibility through chronic airway inflammation and mechanical ventilatory constraints.

Furthermore, this study also found that there were gender differences in the impact of screen time on asthma. The risk rate for girls was slightly higher than that for boys (OR = 1.11 vs. OR = 1.09), which might be related to the longer sedentary time of girls and the influence of hormone levels on immune regulation. However, further research is needed for verification. An interesting phenomenon is that children with a family history of asthma have a lower risk of developing the disease than those without a family history of asthma (OR = 1.04 vs. OR = 1.10), which may be attributed to the fact that family members with a family history of asthma have a higher prevention concept and pay more attention to the respiratory tract of children. Subgroup analyses on second-hand smoke exposure have not yet observed that second-hand smoke exposure increases the risk of childhood asthma caused by screen usage time. However, in many previous studies, smoke exposure (during pregnancy and early childhood) has been associated with an increased risk of childhood asthma. This still suggests that we should reduce household smoke exposure while reducing the use of electronic screens. So as to take adequate preventive measures for children's airway health.

Review of risk factors for childhood asthma

Concerns regarding children's health have progressively transitioned from treatment to prevention [3]. Childhood represents a critical phase for the development of the immune system, during which various environmental factors can adversely impact childhood asthma. The most significant of these factors is external material stimulation, which can compromise the airway mucosal barrier, thus elevating the risk of asthma in children. A recent meta-analysis on particulate matter (PM) and asthma indicates that black carbon, organic carbon, and ammonium nitrogen in PM are primary contributors to the heightened risk of asthma [37]. Furthermore, common pollutants, including but not limited to air pollutants such as ozone (O3) and perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), also adversely affect the immune system. They promote cytokine production, leading to an imbalance in T helper cell populations (Th1/Th2) and exacerbating the overactive immune response observed in asthma [38]. In addition, children's sleep problems may have a series of adverse effects on the onset and progression of childhood asthma. A cross-sectional study conducted by Professor Lu found that poorer sleep quality in children is associated with a higher risk of asthma (aOR = 1.45; 95% CI: 1.23–1.73) [39]. Similarly, multiple studies have demonstrated that insufficient sleep duration and sleep-disordered breathing increase the risk of new-onset asthma [40, 41]. Furthermore, a cohort study indicated that the accumulation of sleep problems is linked to a dose-dependent increase in the risk of incident asthma. Sleep disturbances can lead to elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-12) while decreasing levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-10) [42, 43]. The excessive release of inflammatory mediators heightens the risk of airway inflammation and hyperresponsiveness. Moreover, poor sleep quality can disrupt the immune system, resulting in a reduction in both the number and activity of natural killer cells and T cells, thereby increasing an individual's susceptibility to allergens [23, 44].

The connection between children's dietary conditions and asthma is also a significant area of focus. A primary concern is that an unbalanced diet during childhood can lead to obesity, which places children's bodies in a heightened inflammatory state, consequently increasing the risk of asthma [45, 46]. Moreover, ongoing research into children's diets has revealed that early dietary patterns and structures are equally crucial. Modifying the proportions of various food types can positively influence children's asthma. For example, several anti-inflammatory diets, including the Mediterranean diet [47], a high-plant protein diet [48], and a healthy diet index [49], share a common characteristic: a higher intake of plant-based foods. Current studies indicate that a moderate increase in the consumption of vegetables and fruits can positively affect health. These foods are abundant in essential vitamins and nutrients. Notably, flavonoids exhibit anti-inflammatory properties, inhibit NF-κB signal transduction, and reduce the expression of pro-inflammatory markers [50]. Additionally, carotenoids possess antioxidant capabilities and may help alleviate airway inflammation in asthma patients while supporting lung function [51].

Asthma has become an important health issue in childhood. To better prevent and control its symptoms, we can take a series of positive measures to prevent the occurrence of asthma. For example, even if screen time does not improve, can we use indoor air purifiers? Can more intake of anti-inflammatory diets and maintaining good sleep quality improve the asthma symptoms of children with asthma? This is an interesting question. It is hoped that future researchers can continue to explore the synergy among different favorable factors.

Advantages and limitations

The primary methodological strength of this study resides in its utilization of the NHANES dataset's rigorous multi-cycle design. The stratified, multistage probability sampling framework enhances national representativeness of the pediatric population. The integration of standardized questionnaires with objective laboratory measures mitigates recall bias and diagnostic variability. This study novelly differentiates the impacts of television versus computer exposure, quantifies a dose–response relationship between cumulative screen time and asthma risk, and proposes evidence-based lifestyle modifications for pediatric asthma prevention.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment: Cross-sectional design limitations preclude causal inference. While statistical adjustments addressed measured confounders, residual confounding (e.g., early antibiotic exposure) and reverse causation (e.g., activity-restricted asthmatic children adopting sedentary behaviors) remain plausible. Measurement validity concerns persist: The 24-h recall methodology, while standardized, may incur proxy-reporting inaccuracies, particularly for underreported smartphone and tablet computer usage—a ubiquitous yet unmeasured screen exposure source in modern cohorts. Our team urged that to better explore the usage of electronic screens, the roles of mobile phones and tablets should be fully considered. We emphasized the necessity of distinguishing electronic screen usage during school hours from leisure time at home to facilitate the development of more specific health policy measures. Similarly, this study incorporated major confounding factors and employed rigorous statistical methods to investigate the association between electronic screen use and childhood asthma. However, it is noteworthy that while we accounted for common confounding factors where possible, key variables such as air pollution, sleep problems, and daily diet were not included, which may have influenced the results. This omission did not alter the observed association. Previous studies have indicated that air pollution, sleep, and diet exhibit strong correlations with asthma, suggesting that it may be inappropriate to regard them solely as confounders. For future research, we highlighted the need for more rigorous designs to explore the potential synergistic or antagonistic effects among these factors. While mediation analysis elucidated potential protective effects of physical activity against screen-related asthma risk, unmeasured mediators (e.g., dietary patterns) may partially explain observed associations.

Conclusion

Increased electronic screen time was independently associated with higher childhood asthma risk, persisting after adjustment for confounders. Prolonged computer use (≥ 1 h/day) and cumulative screen exposure significantly elevated asthma odds, highlighting the need for screen time guidelines in asthma management. Future longitudinal studies should explore causal pathways, including circadian disruption and indoor pollutant exposure, to inform targeted interventions. Clinicians should integrate screen behavior assessments into asthma risk evaluations and advocate for balanced screen use alongside physical activity promotion.
