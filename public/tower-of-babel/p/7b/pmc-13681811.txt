COL10A1+ fibroblasts define rupture-prone abdominal aortic aneurysms and enable molecular risk stratification
Authors: Huawei Zhuo, Chengxin Ou, Meng Duan, Jiwu Yang, Dan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) rupture remains the leading cause of AAA-related mortality, while current clinical management relies primarily on aneurysm diameter, which inadequately predicts rupture risk. Increasing evidence suggests that stromal cell heterogeneity critically shapes vascular remodeling; however, the specific fibroblast states associated with AAA rupture predisposition remain poorly defined.MethodsWe integrated cross-species single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets spanning the full spectrum of AAA progression to systematically characterize fibroblast heterogeneity and state transitions. Pseudotime trajectory analysis, transcriptional regulatory network inference, and ligand–receptor interaction modeling were applied to delineate fibroblast-associated microenvironmental programs. Based on these findings, a fibroblast-derived molecular signature was constructed and validated in an independent human AAA cohort.ResultsSingle-cell analysis revealed a progressive transition from homeostatic IGFBP6+ fibroblasts to a disease-associated COL10A1+ fibroblast state during AAA progression. COL10A1+ fibroblasts were selectively enriched in rupture-prone lesions and exhibited transcriptional programs linked to extracellular matrix remodeling and osteogenic-like activation. Intercellular communication analysis indicated that COL10A1+ fibroblasts were predicted to engage macrophage populations through coordinated ligand–receptor signaling pathways, including MDK, CSF1, and CXCL12, collectively delineating a rupture-associated vascular microenvironment. Translationally, leveraging this fibroblast state enabled the development of a minimal two-gene risk score (COL10A1–IGFBP6), which stratified AAA patients by molecular rupture risk independently of aneurysm diameter in the validation cohort.ConclusionsThis study identifies a COL10A1+ fibroblast–associated transcriptional state that defines a rupture-prone vascular microenvironment in abdominal aortic aneurysm. The derived COL10A1–IGFBP6 signature establishes a biologically informed framework for molecular risk stratification, complementing anatomical size–based assessment and providing a foundation for future development of mechanism-guided strategies to refine AAA risk evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08689-8.

Introduction

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a major cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, particularly in the aging population. It is characterized by the progressive and irreversible dilation of the infrarenal aorta, which can culminate in the catastrophic event of rupture, associated with in-hospital mortality rates of 80–90% [1, 2]. The clinical challenge lies in its asymptomatic nature prior to rupture, making the preemptive identification of high-risk patients paramount.

Elective surgical intervention for AAA is predominantly guided by the maximum aortic diameter, with established size thresholds (e.g., 55 mm for men) stemming from landmark population-based studies. However, this size-based criterion is fraught with well-recognized limitations and fails to account for individual variation in rupture risk [3]. A significant proportion of small aneurysms rupture before reaching the intervention threshold, while many large aneurysms remain stable for years [4]. This “diameter paradox” underscores that aneurysm size is an imperfect surrogate for the complex biological processes determining wall integrity and rupture susceptibility and highlights the urgent need to identify molecular and cellular drivers of disease progression [5].

The pathobiology of AAA involves a complex interplay of chronic inflammation, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) depletion, and neovascularization [6]. Historically, research has focused on infiltrating immune cells, particularly macrophages and lymphocytes, as the principal mediators of this destructive microenvironment [7]. Vascular SMCs, the key cellular component of the media, have also been extensively studied for their phenotypic modulation and apoptosis in AAA [8]. In contrast, fibroblasts—the most abundant stromal cells in the vascular wall—have been largely overlooked and traditionally viewed as passive, matrix-producing support cells.

This view is being radically revised in other disease contexts, such as cancer and fibrosis, where fibroblasts are now recognized as dynamic, functionally heterogeneous regulators of immunity, fibrosis, and tissue remodeling [9, 10]. Of particular interest is Collagen type X alpha-1 (COL10A1), a short-chain fibrillar collagen originally identified in hypertrophic chondrocytes and classically associated with cartilage maturation and skeletal development [11]. Recent multi-omics studies have shown aberrant upregulation of COL10A1 in the tumor stroma of breast, pancreatic, and gastric cancers, where it correlates with lymph node metastasis, hematogenous spread, and poor prognosis [12, 13]. Functionally, COL10A1 contributes to ECM remodeling and interacts with proteins such as prolyl 4-hydroxylase subunit beta (P4HB) and integrin subunit beta 1 (ITGB1) to facilitate cell proliferation, migration, and tissue remodeling [11, 14]. However, the precise origin, cellular sources, and mechanistic role of COL10A1 within vascular disease contexts, such as AAA, remain largely unknown.

In the aorta, adventitial fibroblasts are uniquely positioned as biosensors and signaling hubs. They are among the first responders to hemodynamic stress and inflammatory signals from the lumen and vasa vasorum, orchestrating downstream response [15, 16]. We therefore hypothesize that within the AAA microenvironment, specialized, pathogenic fibroblast subpopulations exist that are not merely reactive but are active drivers of disease progression toward rupture.

The advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides an unprecedented opportunity to dissect this cellular heterogeneity and identify such culpable subsets [17]. Recent scRNA-seq studies in AAA have begun to catalog the diversity of immune cells and VSMCs [18–20]; however, a comprehensive, functional annotation of fibroblast states across the disease continuum—from early dilation to imminent rupture—remains elusive.

In this study, we propose and validate a “fibroblast-centric” paradigm for AAA pathogenesis. By integrating multi-species, multi-stage scRNA-seq datasets, we constructed a high-resolution atlas of aortic fibroblasts and aimed to answer three central questions: (i) Does functional heterogeneity exist among fibroblasts in AAA? (ii) Is there a specific fibroblast subpopulation associated with increased susceptibility to rupture? (iii) Can the molecular signature of this subpopulation be leveraged to build a molecular framework that complements and refines diameter-based risk assessment? Our work identifies a COL10A1+ fibroblast–associated state as a key component of a rupture-enriched vascular microenvironment and translates this insight into a biologically informed molecular risk-stratification framework with translational potential, providing a foundation for future efforts to incorporate biological features into AAA risk assessment alongside anatomical size.

Methods and materials

Human tissue samples and bulk RNA-Sequencing analysis

Publicly available bulk RNA-sequencing datasets of human abdominal aortic tissue were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; GSE183464 [21] and GSE202267 [22]). The integrated data included samples from 11 AAA patients and 11 normal aortic tissue controls. Corresponding clinical metadata were extracted from the original publications.

For GSE202267, raw counts were converted to FPKM using the edgeR package (v4.0.16) [23]. Gene lengths were defined as the sum of merged exon lengths based on the TxDb.Hsapiens.UCSC.hg38.knownGene annotation [24]. TMM (Trimmed Mean of M-values) normalization was applied [25], and FPKM values were calculated using the rpkm function with adjusted library sizes. For GSE183464, FPKM values were extracted directly from the repository. Batch effects were corrected using ComBat (sva package, v3.50.0) [26] with dataset origin as batch and disease status as biological covariate. Differential expression analysis was conducted using limma (v3.54.0) [27]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using stringent thresholds of an adjusted p-value (padj) <0.05 and an absolute log₂ fold change > 1. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to the variance-stabilized data to assess overall transcriptomic variance and detect batch effects. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs for Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.12.0) [28], with terms considered significant at padj < 0.05.

To estimate the relative abundance of major cell types (e.g., fibroblasts, immune cells, smooth muscle cells) from the bulk transcriptomes, we applied the MCP-counter deconvolution algorithm [29] with established cell-type-specific gene signatures. Fibroblast-associated gene expression was also evaluated using a set of canonical fibroblast markers (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, DCN, LUM, PDGFRA, and FAP).

Single-cell RNA-Sequencing data acquisition, quality control, and processing

Human scRNA-seq datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; GSE166676 [18] and GSE237230 [20]), comprising 8 AAA samples and 2 control samples from aortobifemoral bypass surgery. Additionally, a longitudinal mouse model dataset (GSE239620) [30] covering the full spectrum of AAA disease progression—spanning Normal, Angiotensin II-induced Aortic Dilation (AAD), established AAA, and three stages of Intramural Hematoma (IMH-I, IMH-II, IMH-III)—was acquired. Clinical and experimental metadata for all samples were compiled (Table S1).

All scRNA-seq datasets were processed separately for each species using Seurat (v5.1.0) in R [31]. Initial quality control was performed per sample. Cells were filtered based on: (1) number of detected genes (200–6,000), (2) total unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts < 40,000, and (3) mitochondrial gene percentage < 20% to exclude low-quality cells and potential doublets.

Filtered datasets were normalized using the “LogNormalize” method (scale factor = 10,000). Mouse and human samples were separately merged into Seurat objects without additional batch integration, as cells from different datasets showed broad overlap across major cell populations and no obvious dataset-driven segregation. Following normalization, the top 2,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were selected for principal component analysis (PCA). The first 30 principal components (PCs), capturing the majority of biological variance as determined by an elbow plot, were used for downstream nonlinear dimensionality reduction and clustering.

Cell clustering and annotation

Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was utilized for non-linear dimensionality reduction based on the selected PCs. Cell clustering was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions in Seurat. To determine the optimal clustering resolution, we performed cluster stability analysis using Clustree (v0.5.1) [32] across a range of resolutions (0.1–1.0). Based on this analysis, a resolution of 0.1 was applied to the human dataset and 0.5 to the mouse dataset. These major clusters were manually annotated based on the expression of well-established canonical marker genes: PECAM1 and VWF for endothelial cells (ECs); ACTA2, MYH11, and TAGLN for vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs); CD68, CD14, FCGR3A (CD16), LYZ for macrophages/monocytes; CD3D, CD3E for T cells; and COL1A1, COL1A2, PDGFRA, DCN for fibroblasts. For mouse samples, annotation followed the same principles using homologous marker genes as defined in prior studies.

Fibroblast identification and functional profiling

In the mouse scRNA-seq dataset, four fibroblast subclusters (Fibro_1 to Fibro_4) were identified directly from global clustering, without additional reclustering. Marker genes that defined each fibroblast subcluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, requiring min.pct = 0.25 and log₂ fold change > 0.25. The relative proportion of each fibroblast subcluster was calculated and compared across the six disease stages (Normal, AAD, AAA, IMH I, IMH II, IMH III).

To characterize the biological functions of each subcluster, marker genes were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with thresholds of adjusted p-value < 0.01 and log₂ fold change > 1. The top 150 most significant marker genes (ranked by adjusted p-value) were selected for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of Biological Processes (BP) using the clusterProfiler (v4.10.1) package [28]. Enrichment results with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significant. Additionally, to assess pathway activity at a systems level, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was conducted using the GSVA R package (v1.50.5) [33]. The analysis utilized the Hallmark gene sets (n = 50) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, v7.5.1) [34]. The resulting GSVA enrichment scores, representing the relative activity of each pathway per sample, were visualized in a heatmap using the ComplexHeatmap package (v2.18.0) [35], focusing on the top 30 pathways displaying the greatest variance across all disease stages.

Pseudotemporal trajectory inference of fibroblast states

To infer potential differentiation or activation trajectories and the dynamic transitions among fibroblast subclusters, pseudotemporal ordering analysis was performed using Monocle3 (v1.3.0) [36]. The fibroblast subset, along with its associated UMAP coordinates and cluster labels from Seurat, was imported into Monocle3. A trajectory graph was learned using the learn_graph function. The Fibro_2 subcluster, characterized by high expression of IGFBP6 and other homeostatic genes, was manually selected as the trajectory root (beginning state) based on its transcriptional profile and GO analysis. Cells were then ordered along the inferred trajectory branches, and a continuous pseudotime value was assigned to each cell, quantifying its inferred progression along the developmental or activation path.

Inference of cell-type-specific transcriptional regulatory networks

To uncover the master transcriptional regulators driving fibroblast heterogeneity, single-cell regulon activity was inferred using pySCENIC (v0.12.1) [37] in a Python (v3.9) environment. The analysis followed the standard three-step pipeline: GRN Inference: GRNBoost2 was used to construct co-expression networks and identify potential transcription factor (TF)-target gene regulatory interactions from the fibroblast expression matrix; Motif Enrichment & Regulon Pruning: RcisTarget was applied to perform motif enrichment analysis (using cisTarget databases for mouse [mm10__refseq-r80__500bp_up_and_100bp_down_tss.mc9nr.feather]) to prune the co-expression network, retaining only direct, high-confidence TF-target relationships (regulons); Regulon Activity Scoring: AUCell was used to quantify the activity of each identified regulon in every individual cell, resulting in an AUC (Area Under the Curve) score matrix.

Duplicate regulons and those with low activity were filtered out. The final binarized regulon activity matrix (AUC score > 0.1 for active) was imported into R. To identify TFs specifically active in each subcluster, Regulon Specificity Scores (RSS) were calculated for each regulon across all subclusters.

Analysis of intercellular communication networks

Cell–cell communication was analyzed using CellChat (v1.6.0) [38] following its standard pipeline. The analysis was performed separately for human and mouse scRNA-seq datasets to address distinct biological questions.

In the human dataset, CellChat was applied to all annotated cell types to quantify outgoing signaling strength for each cell type and identify the predominant sources of specific signaling pathways, such as CXCL.

In the mouse dataset, to investigate longitudinal changes in intercellular communication during disease progression, the mouse dataset was analyzed across six stages (Normal, AAD, AAA, IMH I, IMH II, IMH III). Neuronal and low‑abundance populations were excluded prior to analysis. Major annotated cell types included fibroblast subclusters (Fibro_1 – Fibro_4), VSMCs (expressing Acta2, Myh11), endothelial cells (expressing Pecam1, Cdh5), immune cells, and endothelial‑to‑mesenchymal transition (EndoMT) cells co‑expressing endothelial and mesenchymal/chondrogenic (Col1a1, Fn1, Sox9) markers. Communication networks were quantified to characterize intercellular interactions, with particular focus on fibroblast–macrophage crosstalk and stage‑specific ligand–receptor signaling changes.

Cross-species projection of Fibro_3 and ProtMac signatures onto human bulk RNA-seq

High-specificity marker genes for the Fibro_3 and ProtMac subpopulations were identified from mouse scRNA-seq data using stringent criteria (pct.1 > 0.5, pct.2 < 0.1, avg_log2FC > 1.0). All marker genes meeting these criteria were retained for downstream analyses and mapped to their human orthologs using Ensembl gene annotations. Two independent human AAA bulk RNA-seq datasets (GSE202267 and GSE183464) were integrated and batch-corrected using the ComBat algorithm. For each human sample, a weighted signature score was calculated as the sum of orthologous gene expression values weighted by their corresponding mouse log2 fold-change values.

Development and validation of a fibroblast-based molecular risk score

A fibroblast‑centric molecular risk score was defined as the standardized expression of COL10A1 minus the standardized expression of IGFBP6: Risk Score = Z(COL10A1) – Z(IGFBP6). The score was validated in an independent human cohort (GSE57691 [39]; n = 59 individuals, including 10 normal aortas, 20 small AAAs, and 29 large AAAs) using receiver‑operating‑characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated with 95% confidence intervals via 2,000 bootstrap replicates. The predictive performance of the risk score was statistically compared with that of the maximum aortic diameter using DeLong’s test for two correlated ROC curves [40].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.3.1). Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) as appropriate. For two-group comparisons, Welch’s two-tailed t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was used as appropriate. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test or the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was applied as appropriate. For transcriptomic analyses, including differential expression and gene set enrichment analyses, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) and 95% confidence intervals were reported where applicable. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Integrated transcriptomic profiling reveals fibroblasts as a central signaling source in the AAA Niche

To establish a robust, foundational molecular profile of AAA, we integrated and analyzed bulk RNA-sequencing data from two independent public cohorts (GSE183464 & GSE202267), forming a combined dataset of abdominal aortic tissues from 11 AAA patients and 11 non-aneurysmal controls. Principal component analysis (PCA) based on differentially expressed genes clearly segregated AAA tissues from control samples (Fig. 1A). To define the functional programs underlying AAA progression, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed on significantly upregulated DEGs. Strikingly, the AAA transcriptome was dominated by chemotaxis-associated inflammatory programs, including “chemotaxis”, “cell chemotaxis”, and “leukocyte chemotaxis” (Fig. 1B). Consistently, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis confirmed robust activation of the “Chemokine signaling pathway” and “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” (Fig. 1C). Having defined these dominant pathway alterations at the tissue level, we next sought to resolve their cellular origins. To this end, we integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from 10 human aortic samples (obtained from patients undergoing open AAA repair or aortobifemoral bypass; GSE166676 and GSE237230) Following stringent quality control, UMAP visualization showed no obvious batch-driven clustering across datasets (Supplementary Fig. S1A; Fig. 1D). We constructed a unified cellular atlas of eight major vascular cell types defined by established marker genes and distinct transcriptional signatures (Fig. 1E, F).

Systematic analysis of cell-cell communication (CellChat) revealed that fibroblasts are the dominant source of CXCL signals within AAA lesions, whereas no detectable CXCL signaling was observed in normal aortic tissues (Fig. 1G; Supplementary Fig. S1B–C), and they occupy a leading role as senders across the global intercellular communication network (Fig. 1H). This prominent signaling role coincides with an increased proportion of fibroblasts in AAA as observed in scRNA-seq data (Fig. 1I). To validate this finding in a larger cohort, we performed deconvolution analysis (MCP-counter method) on the aforementioned bulk RNA-seq data, which confirmed the elevated abundance of fibroblasts in AAA tissues (Fig. 1J). In addition, canonical fibroblast markers were more highly expressed in AAA tissues than in controls (Supplementary Fig. S1D).

Taken together, these findings indicate that in AAA, fibroblasts are not passive bystanders but active architects and drivers of the chronic inflammatory microenvironment, positioning them as a prominent signaling source within the AAA microenvironment.

Single‑Cell profiling reveals Stage‑Specific enrichment of a Rupture‑Associated fibroblast subpopulation

Given the dominant role of fibroblasts as a central signaling hub within the AAA pathological niche, we next sought to dissect their intrinsic functional heterogeneity and to evaluate whether specific fibroblast subpopulations are selectively linked to aneurysm rupture risk.

Although existing human AAA single‑cell RNA sequencing datasets have offered valuable insights into cellular composition under steady‑state conditions, their limited scale and general lack of pathological annotations for terminal events such as aneurysm rupture have hindered the systematic reconstruction of cellular dynamics driving rupture progression. To address these limitations, we integrated a single‑cell transcriptomic dataset from a mouse AAA model spanning the full disease spectrum (Fig. 2A, GSE239620). This model adopts the pathological classification system by Daugherty et al. [41, 42], covering a continuum from normal aorta and abdominal aortic dilatation without dissection (AAD), to a single aortic aneurysm without dissection (AAA), and further to increasingly complex stages of dissection/intramural hematoma: IMH‑I (aortic dissection/intramural hematoma without aneurysm), IMH‑II (single aortic aneurysm with dissection/intramural hematoma), and IMH‑III (multiple distinct aortic aneurysms with dissection/intramural hematoma), which corresponds to the most rupture-enriched terminal pathological state. Analysis of approximately 50,000 high-quality single-cell transcriptomes initially identified 31 transcriptionally stable clusters at the global level (Fig. 2B). Based on marker‑gene expression profiles, we refined the clustering further to annotate immune cells and four functionally distinct fibroblast subpopulations (Fig. 2C). Cluster stability across a range of resolutions was confirmed using Clustree analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Relative proportions of these subpopulations underwent marked dynamic shifts along the disease continuum, indicating that AAA progression is accompanied by active, stage‑specific remodeling of fibroblast states (Supplementary Fig. S2B-C).

To systematically characterize this functional remodeling at the pathway level, we performed gene‑set variation analysis (GSVA) on fibroblasts isolated from each pathological stage. The results revealed a clear trajectory of metabolic and functional reprogramming. Compared with the normal state, fibroblasts in early disease stages showed suppression of fundamental metabolic pathways together with robust activation of inflammatory signaling. As the disease progressed into late‑stage and rupture‑prone phases, programs associated with fibrotic remodeling—such as epithelial‑mesenchymal transition and TGF‑β signaling—were specifically upregulated (Fig. 2D, Table S2). Notably, in contrast to the inflammation‑dominant signature observed in whole‑tissue analyses, fibroblasts in late‑stage disease displayed a distinct shift toward a pro‑fibrotic phenotype.

Together, these results suggest that AAA progression is accompanied not by uniform fibroblast accumulation, but by stage-specific remodeling of fibroblast states, coinciding with transcriptomic features associated with rupture-enriched lesions. This observation lays the groundwork for subsequent molecular characterization and mechanistic investigation of fibroblast subpopulations associated with aneurysm rupture.

Functional profiling identifies a pro-rupture COL10A1+ fibroblast subpopulation

We characterized the four fibroblast subtypes through transcriptional and functional profiling. Each subtype exhibited distinct marker genes (Fig. 3A) and showed dynamic redistribution across disease stages in UMAP visualization (Fig. 3B). Quantitative analysis revealed pronounced and stage‑specific shifts in subtype proportions (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Fig. S3A): Fibro_1(ELN+, matrix-stabilizing) was present during early aneurysmal dilation (AAD, 6.2%) and became the most abundant subtype in rupture‑prone IMH‑III lesions (68.8%); Fibro_2 (IGFBP6+, homeostatic) was highly enriched in normal aorta (99.3%) and early non‑aneurysmal stages (AAD: 88.4%; IMH‑I: 89.6%) but nearly absent in IMH‑III (0.7%); Fibro_4 (COMP+, fibrotic/osteogenic) was most prominent in IMH‑II lesions (52.0%). Notably, although Fibro_1 constituted the predominant population in IMH‑III, its functional profile was primarily associated with structural maintenance and injury repair. In contrast, Fibro_3 (COL10A1+) was virtually undetectable in normal aorta and early, non‑rupture‑prone stages (AAD: 0.7%; IMH‑I: 0.3%), but expanded selectively in aneurysm‑containing stages, rising from 11.5% in AAA to 29.6% in rupture‑prone IMH‑III lesions. Gene Ontology analysis further delineated subtype‑specific functional programs (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Fig. S3B). Fibro_1 was associated with wound healing, extracellular matrix stabilization and tissue injury repair, reflecting a protective phenotype aimed at limiting damage and maintaining vascular integrity in rupture-prone lesions; Fibro_2 with extracellular matrix organization and immunomodulation; Fibro_4 with collagen fibril organization and osteogenic signaling. Critically, Fibro_3 was uniquely enriched for ameboidal‑type cell migration, together with processes related to active tissue remodeling, cell–matrix adhesion, cartilage development, and angiogenesis regulation—a functional signature that points directly to active vascular wall disruption and destabilization.

Collectively, these findings identify Fibro_3 as a distinct COL10A1+ fibroblast state characterized by enhanced migratory and tissue-remodeling activity. Despite being numerically less abundant than Fibro_1 in late-stage lesions, Fibro_3 exhibited a unique functional program closely associated with pathological remodeling and rupture-associated AAA progression.

Trajectory inference and regulatory network analysis reveal a pathogenic transition toward the COL10A1+ fibroblast state

To delineate the lineage relationships and progressive transitions among fibroblast subpopulations during abdominal aortic aneurysm progression, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle 3. Fibroblasts were ordered along a continuous developmental trajectory originating from the homeostatic and immunomodulatory Fibro_2 (IGFBP6+) cluster and extending toward the terminal Fibro_3 (COL10A1+) state (Fig. 4A). Correspondingly, pseudotime scores increased progressively across the four subpopulations, with the lowest values in Fibro_2 and the highest in Fibro_3 (Fig. 4B). The inferred trajectory strongly correlated with disease severity: cells at early pseudotime points were predominantly derived from normal aorta or early-stage aneurysmal dilation, whereas cells at the distal end were almost exclusively sourced from high-risk intramural hematoma type III (IMH-III) lesions. To investigate transcriptional programs underlying the establishment and maintenance of this pathogenic state, we applied the SCENIC framework to infer cell-type–specific regulons (see Methods). The top 5 regulons per fibroblast subtype were visualized based on regulon-specific scores (RSS, Fig. 4C) and as integrated heatmaps (Fig. 4D). Fibro_3 (COL10A1+) exhibited highly specific regulon activity, with Runx1, Nr2f2, Mafb, Churc1, and Meis2 among the top 5, defining the core regulatory network of this pathogenic state.

Analysis of downstream target genes of these Fibro_3 (COL10A1+)-specific transcription factors revealed a coordinated effector program associated with disruption of vascular wall integrity (Fig. 4E). Runx1 regulons were linked to pathological angiogenesis and aberrant extracellular matrix remodeling, exemplified by regulation of Vegfa and the cartilage/bone-related matrix gene Col11a1. Nr2f2 preferentially targeted genes involved in matrix stability and proteolytic balance, including Serpine2. Igfbp5 was identified as a shared target of both Mafb and Churc1 regulons, suggesting its role as an integrative node that consolidates multiple transcriptional inputs to sustain and amplify the pathogenic phenotype of Fibro_3.

Collectively, trajectory inference and regulatory network analyses indicate that the emergence of the Fibro_3 (COL10A1+) fibroblast state represents a progressive, disease-associated transition driven by coordinated activity of multiple transcription factors, converging on effector programs that promote angiogenic imbalance and pathological matrix remodeling, thereby aligning with molecular programs associated with AAA progression and rupture susceptibility.

Given the established role of macrophages in AAA pathogenesis, macrophages were further stratified based on their transcriptional programs into three major subpopulations: proteolytic macrophages (ProtMac), inflammatory macrophages (InfMac), and resident macrophages (ResMac). ProtMac were defined by elevated expression of genes associated with extracellular matrix degradation and active tissue remodeling, InfMac were characterized by enrichment of inflammatory response–related gene signatures, whereas ResMac exhibited transcriptional profiles consistent with tissue residency and homeostatic macrophage functions (Supplementary Fig. S4A).

Quantitative analysis of the global communication network revealed that Fibro_3 exhibited the highest outgoing signaling activity within the AAA microenvironment (Fig. 5B, C; Supplementary Fig. S4B-C). Focusing on fibroblast-specific interactions, Fibro_3 displayed highly selective communication, with its strongest outgoing signals directed toward proteolytic macrophages (ProtMac) (Fig. 5D). ProtMac were characterized by high expression of the extracellular matrix–degrading enzyme Mmp12 and lipid‑associated genes Trem2 (Fig. 5E). This molecular signature defines a macrophage subset with potent destructive remodeling capacity: MMP12 directly cleaves vascular elastic and collagen fibers, driving wall weakening [44, 45], while TREM2 [46, 47] marks disease-associated macrophages that orchestrate pathological tissue clearance and inflammation, contributing to plaque destabilization and vascular injury. Notably, ProtMac underwent dramatic expansion during disease progression, increasing from 25.5% of macrophages in AAA to 81.2% in IMH-III lesions (Fig. 5F), indicating strong co-evolution with advanced pathological states.

To dissect the dynamic evolution of the Fibro_3–ProtMac axis, we compared its communication strength across disease stages. This interaction was negligible in normal aorta, gradually strengthened from AAA to IMH-II, and peaked in IMH-III lesions (Fig. 5G). Ligand–receptor pair analysis further revealed stage-specific enhancement of signaling pathways converging on three functional categories: chemotactic and recruitment axes mediating monocyte/macrophage attraction and retention (e.g., Cxcl12–Cxcr4, Mif–(Cd74+Cxcr4)); survival and activation axes maintaining macrophage viability and pro-inflammatory phenotype (e.g., Csf1–Csf1r); and matrix remodeling cooperative axes centered on the midkine (Mdk) pathway (e.g., Mdk–Lrp1, Mdk–Sdc4), which simultaneously drive inflammation, angiogenesis, and matrix degradation. In contrast, stabilizing signals associated with endothelial barrier integrity (Jam3–F11r) were markedly attenuated in late-stage lesions (Fig. 5H). This reciprocal enhancement of pro-degradative signaling and reduction of stabilizing interactions exacerbates microenvironmental instability in advanced AAA. Furthermore, to assess whether Fibro_3 and ProtMac-associated transcriptional programs are detectable in human disease, we projected their marker-gene signatures onto independent human AAA bulk RNA-seq datasets (GSE202267 and GSE183464). Both signatures showed increased enrichment in AAA tissues compared with normal controls (Supplementary Fig. S4D-E).

In summary, COL10A1+ Fibro_3 cells act as an active signaling hub rather than passive participants, selectively recruiting, activating, and sustaining the expansion of proteolytic macrophages (ProtMac) via dynamically reinforced ligand–receptor axes, particularly those involving Mdk, Cxcl12, and Csf1. Together, these interactions create a self-amplifying loop of uncontrolled matrix degradation and inflammation, which is most prominently observed in rupture-enriched late-stage lesions.

Fibroblast-derived molecular programs stratify rupture risk beyond aneurysm diameter

To assess whether the pathogenic fibroblast-driven molecular programs identified at the single-cell level could be captured in human abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) tissues and associated with rupture risk, we analyzed an independent clinical validation cohort from the GEO database (GSE57691). This dataset contains genome-wide expression profiles of aortic specimens from 59 individuals, including normal donor aortas (n = 10), small AAAs (n = 20, mean maximum diameter 54.3 ± 2.3 mm), and large AAAs (n = 29, mean maximum diameter 68.4 ± 14.3 mm).

Unsupervised transcriptomic projection based on AAA-associated differentially expressed genes revealed clear separation between normal aorta samples and all AAA samples, whereas small and large AAAs highly overlapped in transcriptomic space and could not be distinguished from each other (Fig. 6A). Further concordance analysis indicated that, although both small and large AAAs exhibited extensive and significant transcriptional remodeling relative to normal tissues, there were no significantly differentially expressed genes between small and large AAAs (Fig. 6B). Based on these findings, we further explored whether fibroblast-dominated pathogenic signals could reveal molecular risk states hidden beneath the diameter phenotype. Unsupervised clustering of AAA samples stratified patients into two molecular subtypes: one with transcriptional profiles highly similar to normal aorta, defined as a low-risk subtype; the other markedly deviating from normal at the transcriptome level, defined as a molecularly high-risk subtype (Fig. 6C). Notably, this molecular stratification was independent of aneurysm diameter, indicating the existence of molecular risk states in AAA that are uncoupled from anatomical size.

To translate these findings into a clinically applicable metric, we developed a minimal fibroblast‑centric molecular risk index. This index integrates two fibroblast markers with opposing functional signatures: the pathogenic driver COL10A1 (Fibro‑3) and the homeostatic regulator IGFBP6 (Fibro‑2). The risk score is defined as the arithmetic difference between their standardized expression values (COL10A1 – IGFBP6; Fig. 6D), providing a simple yet biologically grounded measure of the shift from vascular homeostasis to a rupture‑prone state.

In an independent clinical validation cohort, the fibroblast-derived risk score stratified samples along a continuous spectrum from normal aorta, through low-risk AAA, to high-risk AAA. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated excellent accuracy in distinguishing AAA from normal aortic tissues, as well as significant discriminatory power in identifying the high-risk molecular subtype (Fig. 6E). Importantly, this risk score reflects the pathogenic biological state of the aneurysm rather than its anatomical size, highlighting its ability to capture molecular features associated with rupture that are not conveyed by diameter measurements alone (Fig. 6F).

In summary, our study demonstrates that pathogenic COL10A1+ fibroblast programs leave a detectable molecular imprint in human AAA tissues and can be distilled into a concise and biologically grounded risk scoring system. This fibroblast-centric molecular signature identifies a subset of “molecularly high-risk” aneurysms that cannot be distinguished by conventional diameter-based criteria, thereby providing a biologically informed framework for molecular risk stratification that complements, rather than replaces, conventional morphology-based assessment.

Discussion

The progression of aneurysmal disease ultimately culminates in aortic rupture, a catastrophic event whose underlying cellular mechanisms are insufficiently captured by current anatomy-based risk parameters. Here, we demonstrate that AAA progression is associated with an actively regulated pathological program involving specialized cellular states. Through integrated cross-species single-cell analyses, we identify a COL10A1+ fibroblast–associated state that emerges selectively during late-stage disease and is enriched within a vascular microenvironment prone to rupture. To our knowledge, this study represents the first to systematically map fibroblast heterogeneity across the full spectrum of AAA progression and to link a specific fibroblast state to biological features associated with rupture susceptibility.

The current clinical paradigm for assessing AAA rupture risk relies predominantly on maximum aortic diameter, which incompletely reflects the biological heterogeneity of the diseased aortic wall [4, 48]. Our findings indicate that disease-associated fibroblast states may provide additional biological information beyond anatomical measurements alone. Within the heterogeneous fibroblast compartment, we identified multiple transcriptionally distinct subtypes with stage‑specific roles in aneurysm pathogenesis: ELN, a major component of elastic fibers critical for vascular integrity [49]; IGFBP6 is associated with the maintenance of tissue homeostasis [50]; COMP drives collagen fibrillogenesis and is linked to fibrotic-osteogenic reprogramming [51]; and COL10A1 is a hallmark of hypertrophic differentiation and pathological tissue remodeling [12, 13].

Although ELN+ Fibro_1 constituted the numerically dominant fibroblast population in rupture-prone IMH‑III lesions (69%, Supplementary Fig. S2B), its functional signature—enriched for extracellular matrix stabilization, tissue injury repair, and wound-healing pathways—suggests a reactive, protective role in attempting to limit damage and maintain vascular integrity, rather than an active driver of destabilization (Fig. 3D). This interpretation is further supported by pseudotime trajectory analysis, which positioned Eln+ Fibro_1 at a relatively lower differentiation state compared with COL10A1+ Fibro_3, indicating a less specialized pathological phenotype (Fig. 4B).

In stark contrast, COL10A1+ Fibro_3 displayed defining characteristics of a pathogenic effector population: it emerged specifically during aneurysm progression, expanded dramatically in rupture-prone stages (increasing from 11% in AAA to 30% in IMH‑III), and exhibited a unique functional repertoire centered on ameboid-like migration, aggressive matrix remodeling, and dysregulated angiogenesis. The convergence of stage specificity and pro-remodeling activity positions this fibroblast state as a cellular link between molecular pathology and compromised aortic wall integrity.

The pathogenic influence of COL10A1+ Fibro_3 extends beyond autonomous cellular behavior to encompass microenvironmental reprogramming. Through enhanced expression of chemotactic and survival factors, this fibroblast subpopulation establishes a self-reinforcing circuit with proteolytic macrophages, forming a feed-forward signaling network associated with enhanced matrix degradation and inflammatory activity (Fig. 5D–H). This fibroblast–macrophage axis complements established macrophage-centric inflammatory models of AAA by highlighting the contribution of stromal–immune cooperation in sustaining tissue-destructive programs during late-stage disease.

Developmental trajectory analysis revealed that COL10A1+ Fibro_3 originates from homeostatic IGFBP6+ Fibro_2 fibroblasts through a process of pathological differentiation driven by chronic mechanical and inflammatory stress. The transcriptional regulators associated with this transition—including Runx1, Nr2f2, and Mafb—collectively govern programs related to angiogenic imbalance and pathological matrix remodeling, underscoring that AAA progression involves qualitative shifts in cellular identity in addition to quantitative changes in cell abundance. This highlights that AAA progression involves not merely changes in cell numbers, but fundamental alterations in cellular identity and function.

Translational implications of this work extend to both risk stratification and therapeutic targeting. A parsimonious dual-gene signature integrating COL10A1 and IGFBP6 expression provided improved discrimination of molecular risk states compared with diameter-based assessment alone, supporting its potential utility for molecular risk stratification beyond conventional anatomical assessment. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the integrated human scRNA-seq dataset contained substantially fewer control samples than AAA samples because of the limited availability of publicly accessible normal human abdominal aortic scRNA-seq datasets. To reduce the potential impact of this imbalance, we performed independent validation in a well-balanced bulk RNA-seq cohort, which yielded consistent fibroblast-associated findings. Importantly, the major conclusions of this study are centered on fibroblast heterogeneity, state transitions, and intercellular communication remodeling rather than differences in overall fibroblast abundance. Nevertheless, validation in larger human scRNA-seq cohorts will be necessary to further confirm these observations.

Second, although cross-species integration enhances biological inference, validation in well-annotated human AAA specimens across defined stages of rupture risk will be essential. Our functional predictions, though supported by pathway analysis and ligand‑receptor network modeling, require experimental validation through targeted perturbation of COL10A1+ Fibro_3 in preclinical models. Furthermore, the complex multicellular ecosystem of the aneurysmal aorta—encompassing endothelial cells, smooth muscle subpopulations, and diverse immune subsets—undoubtedly contributes additional layers of regulation that future spatial multi‑omics approaches will help to elucidate.

Third, although the COL10A1–IGFBP6 score identified biologically distinct AAA subtypes, the validation cohort did not include clinically confirmed ruptured AAA specimens. Therefore, the current findings support molecular risk stratification and the identification of rupture-associated biological states rather than direct prediction of clinical rupture events. Future studies incorporating longitudinal follow-up and clinically ruptured AAA samples will be required to determine the prognostic utility of this signature.

In summary, this study reconceptualizes AAA rupture not as an inevitable mechanical endpoint, but as a biologically driven process mediated by specialized fibroblast subpopulations. By identifying a COL10A1+ fibroblast–associated program enriched in advanced disease states associated with rupture susceptibility, we provide a mechanistic framework for understanding aortic wall destabilization and a molecular foundation for advancing biologically informed risk stratification and precision management of AAA.
