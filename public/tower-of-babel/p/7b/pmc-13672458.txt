Impairment of preoperative activities of daily living predicts poor prognosis after surgery for perihilar cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Takashi Ofuchi, Kosuke Mima, Hiromitsu Hayashi, Kosuke Kanemitsu, Yuki Adachi, Takuya Tajiri, Taichi Horino, Rumi Itoyama, Yuki Kitano, Hirohisa Okabe, Masaaki Iwatsuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposesPerihilar cholangiocarcinoma (PHC) is a rare malignancy with rising incidence and mortality. With global population aging, more patients present with impaired activities of daily living (ADL), potentially influencing surgical outcomes. This study aimed to assess the association between preoperative Barthel Index (BI) scores, a measure of ADL, and survival outcomes after PHC surgery.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 88 patients who underwent PHC resection from 2005 to 2020. Preoperative ADL was evaluated using the BI (range 0–100), with scores ≤ 95 defined as impaired. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to assess the impact of ADL on survival, adjusting for confounders.ResultsAmong the 88 patients, 29 (33%) had impaired preoperative ADL (BI ≤ 95). Multivariable analysis revealed that impaired ADL was independently associated with worse overall survival (HR: 1.90, 95% CI: 1.05–3.40, P = 0.031) and recurrence-free survival (HR: 2.35, 95% CI: 1.36–4.07, P = 0.002).ConclusionsImpaired preoperative ADL is linked to poor prognosis in PHC patients. Efforts to maintain or improve perioperative ADL may enhance long-term outcomes after surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00423-026-04142-y.

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma arises from the biliary system, and is anatomically classified as intrahepatic, perihilar, or distal cholangiocarcinoma [1]. Perihilar cholangiocarcinoma (PHC), which accounts for about 50% of all the cholangiocarcinoma, is confined to the larger bile ducts in the hepatic hilum [2]. Due to its complex anatomical location at the hepatic hilum and its propensity for early local invasion and distant spread, PHC poses a formidable challenge for curative treatment [3], as expressed by high postoperative morbidity (20%– 78%) and mortality (2%– 15%) are still high [4].

The global increase in average life expectancy has led to an increasing number of cancer patients with poor functional levels of activities of daily living (ADL) [5]. PHC typically affects elderly patients with a median age of over 70 years [6, 7]. The treatment of PHC in patients with decreased ADL is an ongoing clinical problem [8]. We have reported that patients with gastrointestinal cancers who are vulnerable or frail tend to have lower treatment tolerance, higher surgical risks, and a poorer prognosis [9, 10].

The Barthel Index (BI) is an objective tool that assesses 10 categories of ADL performance, including personal care, mobility, transfers, bathing and feeding [11]. The BI total score ranges from 0 (bedridden and completely dependent) to 100 (completely independent) [12]. Patients with lower perioperative BI have been shown to experience significantly poorer clinical outcomes [13–16]. However, the association between clinical outcomes of PHC the BI score remains unclear. We hypothesized that lower perioperative BI scores are associated with poorer clinical outcomes following surgery in patients with PHC. In this retrospective study of 88 patients who underwent surgery for PHC, we investigated the relationship between BI scores and both perioperative and survival outcomes.

Materials and methods

Patients

In this retrospective study, we included 88 patients with PHC who underwent surgical resection at the Department of Gastroenterological Surgery, Kumamoto University, between January 2005 and December 2020. The inclusion criteria were an age greater than 18 years and histologically confirmed PHC. The main exclusion criteria were the presence of distant metastases, other active malignancies, or insufficient data on ADL. Patients who underwent hepatopancreatoduodenectomy or R2 resection were also excluded. This study was approved by the Human Ethics Review Committee of Kumamoto University Hospital, Japan (Institutional Review Board No. 2220).

The preoperative BI was prospectively assessed at the time of admission for surgery by physicians or trained medical professionals, such as nurses. The postoperative BI was assessed at the time of hospital discharge using the same evaluation method. The BI evaluates 10 categories of ADL: feeding (0 = unable; 5 = needs help cutting or spreading; 10 = independent), grooming (0 = needs help with personal care; 5 = independent), bathing (0 = dependent; 5 = independent), toilet use (0 = dependent; 5 = needs some help but can perform part independently; 10 = independent), dressing (0 = dependent; 5 = needs help but can perform about half unaided; 10 = independent), mobility (0 = immobile; 5 = wheelchair independent, including corners; 10 = walks with assistance; 15 = independent), transfer (0 = unable, no sitting balance; 5 = major help, can sit; 10 = minor help; 15 = independent), stairs (0 = unable; 5 = needs help; 10 = independent), bowels (0 = incontinent; 5 = occasional accident; 10 = continent), and bladder (0 = incontinent or catheterized and unable to manage; 5 = occasional accident; 10 = continent) [11]. The BI is scored in 5-point increments from 0 to 100, with a score of 100 indicating complete independence. Patients were classified according to their preoperative and postoperative BI scores as either < 100 or 100. This cutoff was used based on a previous study evaluating the prognostic impact of perioperative activities of daily living in patients undergoing hepatic resection for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [16].

Preoperative management

At first, all patients first underwent multidetector row computed tomography (MDCT) to assess tumor staging, biliovascular anatomy, and liver volume [17]. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography was routinely conducted for various purposes, including biliary drainage, direct cholangiography, intraductal ultrasonography, forceps biopsy and intraductal ultrasonography [18]. As a first-line approach, a single endoscopic biliary drainage catheter was placed in the future liver remnant, with additional catheters or percutaneous biliary drainage implemented as necessary [19]. Portal vein embolization was conducted 4 to 6 weeks prior to resection in the case with extended right hemihepatectomy, trisectionectomy, or insufficient future remnant volume (less than ICG-K of liver remnant ≤ 0.05).

Surgical procedure

The surgical approach was decided during a meeting of attending hepatopancreatobiliary surgeons. In general, right hepatectomy was selected for Bismuth type 1 and 2 tumors [20], as well as for right-dominant type 3 and 4 tumors [21], while left hepatectomy was chosen for left-dominant type 3 and 4 tumors [22]. Extrahepatic bile duct resection alone was indicated only in cases of patients with impaired hepatic reserve or Bismuth type I or II tumors that did not extensively invade the hepatic hilum. Cases with diffuse infiltration of the entire extrahepatic bile duct, downward superficial tumor spread, or extensive nodal metastasis in the pancreatoduodenal region were excluded.

Postoperative management and follow-up

PHC were staged according to the 7th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) for International Cancer Control (UICC) staging manual [23]. Postoperative complications were documented using the Clavien–Dindo classification system [24].

The surveillance protocol included serum tumor marker testing every three months for three years and computed tomography scans every three or six months for three years. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time between surgery and recurrence or death. Overall survival (OS) was calculated as the time from surgery to death from any cause. Recurrence was assessed using imaging. Distant metastasis comprises any tumor recurrence in the peritoneum, distant lymph nodes, or distant organs such as the liver and lungs, with or without locoregional recurrence. Locoregional recurrence was defined as tumor recurrence in the surgical bed or regional lymph nodes without distant metastasis. This study adhered to the reporting guidelines outlined in Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [25]. Since 2005, for recurrent cases, first-line treatments included gemcitabine hydrochloride tegafur-gimeracil-oteracil potassium, and gemcitabine plus cisplatin [26]. In cases of locoregional recurrence, radiation therapy was sometimes administered at the discretion of the attending physician [27].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using JMP software version 12.2 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). All P values were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression models. To identify independent risk factors for OS and RFS, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed. Variables with a P value < 0.10 in the univariable analyses were included in the multivariable models. Multicollinearity among covariates included in the multivariable Cox regression models was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and a VIF value < 5 was considered indicative of the absence of significant multicollinearity. Categorical variables are expressed as proportions and were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Results

Clinicopathological features and perioperative outcomes according to preoperative Barthel Index scores

The preoperative BI scores of 88 patients who underwent surgery for PHC were analyzed. Of these, 29 patients (33%) had preoperative BI scores of 95 or lower. Among patients with impaired preoperative ADLs (BI ≤ 95), the median preoperative BI score was 90 (IQR, 85–95). The distribution of preoperative BI scores within the impaired BI group is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Tables 1 and 2 present the clinicopathological characteristics and perioperative outcomes according to the preoperative BI category. No significant differences were observed in clinicopathological factors between patients with BI ≤ 95 and those with BI > 95. Patients with BI ≤ 95 tended to receive preoperative or adjuvant chemotherapy less frequently, although this trend did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.06; Table 1).

There were no significant associations between preoperative BI scores and operative time, intraoperative blood loss, or the need for perioperative red blood cell transfusion (Table 2). Patients with BI ≤ 95 showed a higher, though not statistically significant, incidence of major postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥IIIa) (P = 0.11; Table 2). Notably, patients with BI ≤ 95 were significantly less likely to achieve R0 resection (P = 0.026; Table 2).

Associations of preoperative Barthel Index scores with OS and RFS

We examined the relationship between preoperative BI scores and both OS and RFS in 88 patients. The median follow-up duration was 21.6 months (interquartile range: 11–44 months). As shown in Fig. 1, patients with preoperative BI scores ≤ 95 (indicating impaired preoperative ADL) had significantly shorter OS (P = 0.001) and worse RFS (P < 0.001) compared with those who were fully independent (BI = 100), as determined by the log-rank test. In contrast, postoperative BI status at discharge was not significantly associated with either OS (P = 0.95) or RFS (P = 0.79) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

In univariate Cox regression analyses, preoperative ADL impairment, residual tumor status, and disease stage were significantly associated with OS (P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 1). Elevated preoperative CA19-9 levels also showed a trend toward poorer OS (P = 0.08; Supplementary Table 1). Similarly, univariate analyses identified preoperative ADL impairment, residual tumor status, and disease stage as significant prognostic factors for RFS (P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 2). None of the remaining factors were significantly correlated with RFS (P > 0.05). Multivariate analysis confirmed preoperative ADL impairment as an independent adverse prognostic factor for RFS (P = 0.002; Table 3).

During the median follow-up of 27.1 months, 27 patients (31%) developed locoregional recurrence and 23 (26%) developed distant metastases. Preoperative ADL impairment was significantly associated with locoregional recurrence after curative resection (P = 0.034; Supplementary Table 3). There were no significant differences between groups in the management of recurrence (P = 0.65; Supplementary Table 3).

Subgroup analysis excluding patients who underwent bile duct resection alone

To further clarify the prognostic value of preoperative ADLs, we conducted a subgroup analysis excluding 18 patients who underwent bile duct resection alone. Among the remaining 70 patients, 22 (31%) had impaired preoperative ADLs. Comparison by the log-rank test showed that with respect to preoperative BI scores = 100 (independence of preoperative ADL), preoperative BI score ≤ 95 (impairment of preoperative ADL) were significantly associated with shorter OS (P = 0.028; Fig. 2A) and worse RFS (P = 0.002; Fig. 2B). In univariable Cox regression analyses, ADL impairment was significantly associated with both OS (P = 0.003) and RFS (P < 0.001) (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). Multivariable Cox regression analyses after adjusting for potential confounders showing a univariate prognostic association (P < 0.10) confirmed that ADL impairment remained an independent predictor of both OS (P = 0.047) and RFS (P = 0.022) in this subgroup (Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the association between preoperative ADL impairment, as evaluated by the BI score, and clinical outcomes in patients who underwent surgery for PHC. Our results showed that patients with a preoperative BI score ≤ 95 had significantly poorer OS and RFS compared with those who achieved a score of 100. Moreover, multivariate Cox regression analyses identified preoperative ADL impairment as an independent prognostic factor for both OS and RFS.

The association between functional status and postoperative outcomes has been previously reported in various malignancies, including gastrointestinal cancers [28]. Frailty and impaired ADL have been linked to increased surgical risks, reduced treatment tolerance, and poorer survival outcomes [29]. Our study extends these findings to PHC, highlighting the importance of assessing preoperative functional status as a prognostic factor in this challenging malignancy. Notably, the impact of preoperative ADL impairment on OS and RFS persisted even after adjusting for potential confounders, reinforcing its clinical significance.

Interestingly, a key finding of our study was that patients with a preoperative BI score ≤ 95 had significantly fewer R0 resections. This suggests that preoperative functional impairment may be associated with a lower likelihood of achieving complete tumor removal. Several factors may contribute to this observation. Patients with impaired ADL often have compromised physiological reserves, which may limit the extent of surgical resection feasible without excessive morbidity. Additionally, these patients may be more susceptible to postoperative complications, leading to prolonged recovery times and increased recurrence risks. Although the difference in postoperative complication rates did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.09), there was a trend toward higher rates of CD ≥ IIIa complications in patients with ADL impairment. Generally, non-R0 resection is associated with a higher recurrence rate and poorer prognosis [30]. In our study, patients with a preoperative BI score ≤ 95 had a lower R0 resection rate, which may have contributed, at least in part, to their higher recurrence rates and poorer survival outcomes. Importantly, the association between impaired preoperative BI and worse OS and RFS persisted even among patients who achieved R0 resection (Supplementary Fig. 3), suggesting that the adverse prognostic impact of preoperative ADL impairment is not solely attributable to differences in resection margin status. This observation further supports the prognostic significance of preoperative functional status in patients undergoing resection for PHC. From a clinical perspective, we do not propose using BI to determine surgical eligibility. Rather, perioperative BI may serve as a simple and practical tool to identify patients at increased risk of poor long-term outcomes who may benefit from intensified prehabilitation, nutritional support, optimization of postoperative recovery, and closer postoperative surveillance.

The mechanism underlying the association between preoperative ADL impairment and poor survival outcomes remains unclear. One possible explanation is that patients with impaired ADL may have systemic frailty, sarcopenia, or underlying comorbidities that affect their ability to withstand the physiological stress of surgery. Additionally, impaired mobility and self-care may delay postoperative recovery, reduce adherence to adjuvant therapies, and increase the likelihood of tumor recurrence.

From a clinical perspective, our findings emphasize the importance of preoperative functional assessments in patients with PHC. The BI is a simple yet effective tool that can be used to identify high-risk patients who may benefit from prehabilitation strategies [12]. Optimizing nutritional status, physical activity, and perioperative care in these patients may improve surgical outcomes and overall prognosis. Future studies should explore interventions aimed at improving preoperative ADL and their impact on long-term survival in PHC patients.

In addition, subgroup analysis excluding patients who underwent bile duct resection alone confirmed the robustness of our findings. Even after excluding these less-invasive cases, preoperative ADL impairment remained independently associated with both poorer OS and RFS. This reinforces the notion that the prognostic impact of impaired functional status is not merely reflective of surgical extent or case selection bias. Rather, it likely reflects an intrinsic vulnerability in patients with impaired ADLs, such as reduced physiological reserve, systemic frailty, or subclinical comorbid conditions, which adversely affect both recovery from surgery and long-term tumor control.

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, while the BI provides an objective measure of ADL, it does not capture other important aspects of frailty, such as cognitive function, sarcopenia, or psychological well-being. Third, the lack of statistical significance in some associations (e.g., CA19-9 and residual tumor status with OS) may be due to the sample size, and larger prospective studies are needed to validate our findings. Furthermore, the relatively limited number of events may have increased the risk of model overfitting despite the use of clinically relevant variables in the multivariable analyses. In addition, treatment strategies and perioperative management may have evolved during the study period, potentially influencing patient outcomes. Although the BI cutoff used in this study was based on previous reports in biliary tract cancer, further validation is required to determine the optimal threshold for risk stratification. Finally, postoperative functional status was assessed only at discharge, and the number of patients who experienced substantial functional improvement or deterioration was limited. Consistent with this, postoperative BI status was not significantly associated with either OS or RFS in the present study. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect potentially meaningful associations. Therefore, larger prospective multicenter studies with external validation are warranted to confirm the prognostic significance of preoperative ADL impairment, clarify the impact of postoperative ADL status and perioperative changes in ADLs, and evaluate the potential benefits of prehabilitation interventions.

Our study highlights the prognostic significance of preoperative ADL impairment in patients undergoing surgery for PHC. Impaired preoperative ADLs, as assessed by the BI, were associated with poorer long-term oncological outcomes, including worse OS and RFS. Preoperative ADL assessment using the BI may serve as a simple and objective tool for risk stratification and perioperative management in patients with PHC. Further research is warranted to determine whether interventions aimed at improving preoperative functional status can translate into improved long-term outcomes in this patient population.

Conclusion

Preoperative ADL impairment, as assessed by the Barthel Index, is an independent predictor of poor oncological outcomes in patients undergoing surgery for perihilar cholangiocarcinoma. A BI score ≤ 95 was associated with lower R0 resection rates, higher recurrence, and worse overall and recurrence-free survival. These findings underscore the clinical importance of preoperative functional assessment and suggest that optimizing ADL before surgery may improve both surgical and long-term outcomes in this patient population.
