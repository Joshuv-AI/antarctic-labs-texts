Analysis of avian pathogenic Escherichia Coli (APEC) and its antimicrobial resistance risk characteristics and critical control points in laying hens
Authors: Tongxu Tian, Yuying Bao, Jiale Hui, Yubin Gao, Lin Wang, Jianmei Zhao, Xiaojing Hao, Cuiping Song, Junwei Wang, Ge Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFor precise prevention and control of Avian Pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC) and its antimicrobial resistance (AMR) transmission in egg-laying hen farming, this study analyzed the prevalence, resistance characteristics, and risk sources of APEC in 614 samples from layer farms using antimicrobial susceptibility testing and whole-genome sequencing(WGS). Key findings include:APEC prevalence exhibited significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity, with the isolation rate peaking during the egg-laying period in August. APEC was primarily enriched in high-organic matter environments like the cloaca, manure waste, and flies. Among resistance genes, tet(A) (tetracyclines) had the highest detection rate, followed by floR (florfenicol) and sul2 (sulfonamides); mcr-1.1 (colistin) was also detected. Resistance genes for six classes of antibiotics, including ampicillin, showed a significant positive correlation with their corresponding phenotypic resistance (P < 0.05). All strains carried mobile genetic elements, with the plasmid IncFIB(AP001918) present in 87.2 % of isolates. Elements such as the IS575-aph-sul2 complex, Tn2- blaTEM, and ISVsa3 drove multidrug resistance transmission, with water sources and eggshells serving as important resistance gene reservoirs. A total of 22 sequence types (STs) were identified, with ST69, ST162, and ST155 being the predominant types. Core genome MLST (cgMLST) and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) traceability analysis confirmed: ST69 (cgST115177) was highly homologous (≤ 2 allelic differences) between feed and fecal samples, indicating feed as a risk source; ST162 (cgST130769) was homologous (≤ 4 allelic differences) among eggshells, air, and wall samples, suggesting horizontal transmission within the farm; and strains of cgST87293 in water sources indicated cross-farm transmission. This study reveals the AMR risk characteristics of APEC in layer farms and identifies key control points, highlighting the urgent need to strengthen precise prevention and control guided by the "One Health" concept.Keywords: Avian Pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC), Antimicrobial resistance (AMR), Laying hen, Whole-genome sequencing (WGS), Critical control point

INTRODUCTION

Escherichia coli (E. coli) is a Gram-negative bacterium that commonly colonizes the intestines of humans and animals as a commensal organism (Leimbach et al., 2013). Under specific conditions, this bacterium can also cause intestinal or extraintestinal infections (Jang et al., 2017), leading to a range of clinical manifestations. For example, Avian Pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC) can induce respiratory, reproductive, or systemic infections in laying hens. Unlike most E. coli pathotypes, APEC is not defined by a single specific virulence gene but rather by combinations of diverse virulence genes (Watts and Wigley, 2024). Its diverse infection sites can reduce economic returns in poultry farms, impair chick performance, and sometimes cause flock mortality.

Poultry farms commonly use antibiotics to address challenges posed by APEC infections (Ghunaim et al., 2014). However, antibiotic use in food-producing animals carries multiple adverse consequences, including gut microbiota dysbiosis, antibiotic residues in products, impacts on the public environment, and the emergence and spread of AMR. Previous studies based on molecular phylogeny and genotypic similarities indicate that some APEC subpopulations share homology with human-derived Extraintestinal Pathogenic E. coli (ExPEC) isolates, such as uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) and neonatal meningitis E. coli (NMEC). Several APEC strains have been demonstrated to cause human-associated ExPEC infections (Mortensen et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2021), indicating a close genetic relationship between APEC and ExPEC. Therefore, analyzing the epidemiological distribution and AMR profile of APEC during laying hen production will help provide a scientific basis for preventing and controlling related human ExPEC infections.

Currently, bacterial AMR constitutes a global public health challenge (Miles et al., 2006; Tang et, al., 2023; World Health, Organization, 2025). AMR in APEC strains may enable other pathogenic E. coli strains to acquire resistance genes(Li et, al., 2025; Li et, al., 2024; Wang et, al., 2017). If these resistance genes are carried by mobile genetic elements, there is a risk of direct or indirect transmission between animals and humans (Li et al., 2020). AMR complicates treatment and has led to the emergence of certain APEC genotypes (e.g., ST23, ST117, ST131, ST355, and ST428) with heightened pathogenic potential (Johnson et al., 2022). Concurrently, the multidrug resistance (MDR) exhibited by APEC strains carrying multiple resistance genes poses a significant new threat to animal health and food safety (Wang et al., 2010). Currently, research on APEC isolated from laying hens and its antimicrobial resistance remains scarce (Atiq et al., 2025), with most studies focusing on antimicrobial resistance phenotypes and virulence genes in broiler-derived APEC (Johar et al., 2021; Subedi et, al., 2018). No published studies had yet investigated laying hen-derived APEC through genomic sequencing. This study aims to identify the sources of antibiotic resistance in APEC isolated from laying hens through isolation and identification, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and genomic sequencing of samples collected at different time points and from various sample categories. By analyzing spatiotemporal associations, we sought to clarify the origins of antibiotic resistance risk in laying hen-derived APEC. Through genomic data analysis, we aimed to validate the horizontal transmission pathways of resistance genes. Ultimately, this research will reveal the sources and transmission mechanisms of antibiotic resistance in laying hen-derived APEC, providing scientific guidance for the precise prevention and control of APEC and its resistance in laying hens.

Materials and methods

Major reagents

MacConkey agar, Tryptic Soy Agar, LB broth (Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., China); Antimicrobial susceptibility testing panel (Shanghai Fosun Diagnostics Technology Co., Ltd., China); Molecular biology reagents.

Sample collection and escherichia coli isolation

From July to October 2024, we conducted periodic sampling at a representative large-scale layer farm in Qingdao, China. Samples included direct contact surfaces: areas in direct contact with hosts or excretions, including cloacal swabs from laying hens, manure conveyor belt swabs, and egg surface swabs; indirect contact surfaces: equipment and environmental areas where transmission occurs via secondary contamination, including cage surface swabs, floor swabs, wall surface swabs, egg conveyorbelt swabs, feed samples, and drinking water samples; transmission routes: non-contact biological or physical carriers capable of transmitting pathogens, including air samples, fly specimens, and swabs from workers' protective clothing, totaling 614 samples. Standardized surface sampling of environmental and object specimens was performed using sterile cotton swabs. For homogeneous sample types (e.g., cage surfaces), triplicate swabs were collected from three distinct locations and pooled into a single composite sample.

Concurrently, we documented the farm's biosecurity protocols and medication records. All samples were transported to the laboratory on the day of collection for immediate Escherichia coli isolation and identification. The procedure was as follows: Swab samples (or appropriate volumes of liquid/solid specimens) were inoculated onto MacConkey agar for selective cultivation. A single pooled environmental sample consists of cotton swabs collected from three distinct locations placed in the same liquid culture medium. Similar to cloacal swabs, these were subjected to selective culture by inoculation onto MacConkey agar. Typical pink colonies were then selected, with final species confirmation achieved through MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry.

Multiplex PCR identification of APEC

Crude DNA extraction was performed using the boiling method. APEC isolates were diagnostically identified using the multiplex PCR assay established by Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2018). The specific multiplex primers required are listed in Table 1 and were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The reaction mixture comprised: 12.5 μL of 2 × Premix Taq, 7.5 μL of nuclease-free water, 3 μL of template DNA, primers (iss-F/R, cvaC-F/R, irp2-F/R, iucD-F/R, iroN-F/R, tsh-F/R; each at 10 pmol/μL) added in amounts of 0.5 μL, 0.7 μL, 0.4 μL, 0.6 μL, 0.3 μL, and 0.5 μL respectively, 2 μL of genomic DNA, and 8.5 μL of ddH₂O. The multiplex PCR cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 94°C for 5 min; followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 45 s, annealing at 55°C for 45 s, and extension at 72°C for 1 min; with a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. Isolates possessing three or more of the target virulence genes were classified as APEC.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Following the criterion of selecting at least one strain from different time points and stages, 96 bacterial isolates were randomly selected. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for 14 antimicrobial agents spanning 10 classes were determined using the microbroth dilution method with 96-well panels. The antimicrobials tested included: Penicillins (ampicillin), β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (amoxicillin-clavulanate), Cephalosporins (ceftiofur, ceftazidime), Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, spectinomycin), Tetracyclines (tetracycline), Amphenicols (florfenicol), Sulfonamides (sulfisoxazole, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole), Fluoroquinolones (enrofloxacin, ofloxacin), Carbapenems (meropenem), and Polymyxins (colistin). Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 served as the quality control strain. Each panel contained a series of doubling dilutions for each antimicrobial. Following inoculation with standardized bacterial suspensions diluted in broth, panels were incubated at 35°C for 16-20 hours. MIC endpoints were determined using either an automated microbial identification and susceptibility system or visual inspection. MIC values were interpreted as Susceptible (S), Intermediate (I), or Resistant (R) according to the relevant standards of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). Isolates exhibiting resistance to three or more antimicrobial classes were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR).

Ninety-six randomly selected isolates from various sampling points underwent antimicrobial susceptibility testing via the broth microdilution method using 96-well microdilution plates. Testing encompassed 14 antimicrobial agents across 10 classes: Penicillins (ampicillin), β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (amoxicillin-clavulanic acid), Cephalosporins (ceftiofur, ceftazidime), Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, spectinomycin), Tetracyclines (tetracycline), Amphenicols (florfenicol), Sulfonamides (sulfisoxazole, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole), Fluoroquinolones (enrofloxacin, ofloxacin), Carbapenems (meropenem), Polymyxins (colistin). Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 served as the quality control strain. Each microdilution plate contained wells with twofold serial antibiotic dilutions for every antimicrobial agent. Bacterial suspensions adjusted in broth medium were inoculated into wells, followed by incubation at 35°C for 16-20 hours. Post-incubation, results were interpreted using either an automated microbiology system or visual inspection. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined, with susceptibility categories (Susceptible [S], Intermediate [I], or Resistant [R]) assigned per Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) standards. Isolates resistant to ≥3 antimicrobial classes were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR).

WGS and phylogenetic analysis

The selection was ensured to include at least one APEC strain from different time points and stages, and then 39 isolates with a higher number of virulence genes and drug resistance phenotypes were prioritized for selection. Single colonies of 39 APEC isolates were subcultured onto MacConkey (MAC) agar plates. Following 4-6 successive purification steps, single colonies were inoculated into LB broth and incubated with shaking at 37°C for 12 hours. Bacterial suspensions were inactivated at 65°C for 20 minutes prior to shipment to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. for WGS and de novo assembly.

The assembled genomes were uploaded to the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (CGE) platform (https://www.genomicepidemiology.org/) (Zankari et al., 2012). Acquired antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) were detected using ResFinder v4.7.2 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/ResFinder/). Virulence genes were identified using VirulenceFinder 2.0 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/VirulenceFinder/). Plasmid replicon types were determined using PlasmidFinder v2.0.1 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/PlasmidFinder/). Serotypes were predicted using SerotypeFinder v2.0.1 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/SerotypeFinder/). Mobile genetic elements were identified using MobileElementFinder v1.0.3 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/MobileElementFinder/). Multilocus sequence typing (MLST), clonal complex (CC), and cgMLST were determined using the PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org/).

An MLST-based minimum spanning tree was constructed using BioNumerics software. Sequence alignment and SNP calling were performed using BacWGSTdb. A core genome phylogenetic tree was constructed using APEC—O18 (GenBank accession CP006830, ST95) as the reference genome. Phylogenetic trees were visualized and refined by importing Newick tree files into Chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online).

Results and analysis

Comparison of APEC isolation rates

A total of 291 Escherichia coli isolates were recovered from the 614 collected samples, among which 109 isolates (37.5 %) were identified as APEC. The isolation rates of E. coli and APEC exhibited significant dynamic variations throughout the production cycle (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). E. coli isolation rates remained high during the July brooding phase and the August laying phase (66.3 % and 61.5 %, respectively), rates that were significantly higher than those in the July depopulated layer house period and the October laying phase (P < 0.001). The APEC isolation rate peaked in August (27.2 %), significantly exceeding rates in the July brooding phase (9.2 %, P = 0.0017) and the July depopulated layer house period (4.4 %, P < 0.001). The maximum difference in isolation rates between E. coli and APEC occurred during the July brooding phase, while the minimum difference was observed in October.

Analysis of different sampling points (Fig. 1B) revealed that E. coli exhibited the highest isolation rates from cloacal swabs and manure samples (72.2 % and 75.9 %, respectively). Similarly, APEC isolation rates were highest from manure and cloacal swabs (32.8 % and 30.0 %, respectively), while lower rates were detected in air samples (3.3 %) and on farm worker surfaces (9.4 %).

Environmental samples collected throughout the production cycle were categorized based on transmission routes: direct contact surfaces, indirect contact surfaces, and vectors. Both E. coli and APEC isolation rates displayed considerable fluctuations across these categories (Fig. 1C-E). In July, during the brooding phase, direct and indirect contact surfaces exhibited the most severe E. coli contamination (74.4 % and 75.6 %, respectively). Conversely, during the same period in the depopulated layer house, bacterial isolation rates were consistently low across all contact routes. August represented the peak contamination period within the cycle. The E. coli isolation rate increased significantly (P < 0.001) during active layer house operation, particularly for APEC, reaching 30.0 % on direct contact surfaces and 27.0 % on indirect contact surfaces. Concurrently, the E. coli isolation rate approached levels observed during the brooding phase, and bacterial isolation from vectors peaked (E. coli: 57.6 %; APEC: 21.2 %). In October, positive detection rates on contact surfaces diverged: isolation rates on indirect contact surfaces declined sharply (E. coli: 15.0 %; APEC: 12.5 %), while rates on direct contact surfaces remained elevated (55 % to 57 %).

Analysis of escherichia coli antimicrobial resistance phenotypes

Analysis of AMR rates in E. coli isolates from four poultry farms across different periods (Fig. 2) revealed significant disparities between brooding houses (July) and depopulated layer houses (July). Brooding houses exhibited markedly higher resistance rates to 10 antimicrobials, including tetracycline (95.0 % vs. 40.0 %, P < 0.001), florfenicol (80.0 % vs. 20.0 %, P < 0.001), and sulfisoxazole (80.0 % vs. 15.0 %, P < 0.001), indicating intensified antimicrobial exposure or transmission risks in brooding environments. Temporally, the transition from depopulation to initial restocking constituted the most critical window for controlling resistance emergence, during which resistance rates for nine antimicrobials changed significantly (P < 0.0083). Notably, ampicillin resistance rose from 40.0 % to 80.0 %, tetracycline resistance surged from 40.0 % to 93.3 %, and ceftiofur resistance increased from 15.0 % to 43.3 %. Although overall resistance rates remained elevated in October layer flocks, only ceftazidime (from 16.7 % to 46.2 %) and meropenem (from 3.3 % to 23.1 %) showed further increases compared to August, suggesting stabilization of resistance development. Furthermore, the 12 antimicrobials demonstrating significant overall differences (P < 0.05) were categorized into three distinct resistance patterns: The first comprised nine core indicators (e.g., ampicillin, tetracycline, florfenicol) showing significant increases from depopulation to August within the layer farm timeline. The second involved emerging threats like meropenem, which increased from undetectable levels in July-depopulated houses to 23.1 % by October (P < 0.001). The third pattern featured fluoroquinolones (e.g., enrofloxacin), highly prevalent in brooding houses but decreasing over time in layer flocks (declining to 26.9 % by October). In contrast, no significant intergroup differences were observed for amoxicillin-clavulanate or colistin (P > 0.05).

Resistance rates of Escherichia coli isolates to different antibiotic classes.

APEC isolates exhibited significantly more pronounced antimicrobial resistance compared to Non-APEC isolates (Fig. 3A, B). Resistance rates for six antibiotics differed significantly between the two groups (P < 0.05), with APEC consistently demonstrating higher resistance than Non-APEC. These antibiotics included ampicillin, ceftiofur, tetracycline, florfenicol, sulfisoxazole, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. Multidrug resistance was observed in 74 % of E. coli isolates (Fig. 3C), with APEC isolates showing a more concentrated MDR profile (resistance to 6–7 drug classes) compared to the less frequent and more phenotypically dispersed MDR among Non-APEC isolates.

A: Antibiotic resistance profiles and resistance rates to various antibiotics for the 46 APEC isolates; B: Antibiotic resistance profiles and resistance rates to various antibiotics for the 50 Non-APEC isolates; C: Comparison of MDR phenotype proportions among E. coli, APEC, and Non-APEC isolates; D: Antibiotic resistance rates of APEC isolates across direct contact, indirect contact, and vehicle transmission routes; E: Antibiotic resistance rates of Non-APEC isolates across direct contact, indirect contact, and vehicle transmission routes.

Statistical analysis revealed that APEC isolates displayed the broadest resistance advantage on indirect contact surfaces, where resistance rates to all tested antibiotics were significantly higher than those of Non-APEC (P < 0.05). The most pronounced disparities were observed for penicillins (81.3 % vs. 30.0 %, P < 0.001) and tetracyclines (93.8 % vs. 50.0 %, P < 0.001). Among transmission vectors, APEC isolates achieved complete resistance to penicillins (100.0 % vs. 69.2 %, P = 0.031) and exhibited significantly higher resistance to β-lactamase inhibitors (10.0 % vs. 0.0 %, P = 0.019) and phenicols (70.0 % vs. 38.5 %, P = 0.003). On direct contact surfaces, APEC isolates showed significantly higher resistance to cephalosporins than Non-APEC isolates (52.5 % vs. 32.4 %, P = 0.040) but significantly lower resistance to β-lactamase inhibitors (0.0 % vs. 11.8 %, P = 0.034) and carbapenems (0.0 % vs. 17.6 %, P = 0.003). These findings underscore the robust environmental transmission-linked resistance profile of APEC, particularly highlighting elevated risks associated with β-lactam and tetracycline resistance.

Analysis of antimicrobial resistance genes based on genome sequencing

Overall detection of resistance genes in APEC isolates

ResFinder 4.7.2 identified 73 resistance genes from 39 APEC isolate genomes (Fig. 4). The data revealed the highest detection rate for tet(A) (89.7 %), followed by floR (69.2 %), sul2 (64.1 %), aph(3′')-Ib and aph(6)-Id (both 59.0 %), blaTEM-1B (53.8 %), and qnrS1 (53.8 %). Genes with moderate detection rates (30 %−50 %) included blaCTX-M-55 (43.6 %), dfrA14 (41.0 %), multiple blaTEM variants (e.g., blaTEM-141, blaTEM-206, etc.; 30.8 %), and mph(A) (30.8 %). Numerous genes exhibited low detection rates (<30 %), such as sul3 (23.1 %), cmlA1 (23.1 %), and rare genes including mcr-1.1 (5.1 %) and rmtB (5.1 %).

Correlation analysis of resistance genes and phenotypes

A phenotypic study was conducted on 28 of the 39 sequenced APEC isolates that had undergone antimicrobial susceptibility testing, correlating the phenotypic susceptibility to antibiotics with the absence or presence of resistance genes in the isolates (Table 2). First, in some instances, the presence of resistance genes was positively correlated with resistance to specific antibiotics. For example, the detection of resistance genes for ampicillin, ceftiofur, gentamicin, florfenicol, sulfisoxazole, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole showed significant positive correlations (P < 0.05) with phenotypic resistance. Among these, the phenotypic resistance rate reached 100 % (24/24) in genotype-positive samples for ampicillin, with resistance attributable to ESBL genes (e.g., blaCTX-M, blaTEM variants) and AmpC enzyme genes (e.g., blaCMY-2). In contrast, a significant negative correlation (P < 0.001) was observed for amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, where only one genotype-positive sample exhibited phenotypic resistance (3.6 %). Although the resistance gene was carried, the reason for the lack of resistance might be that the gene was not effectively induced for expression within E. coli and remained in a silenced state. Additionally, certain antagonistic factors might also have been present. The lack of significant association for fluoroquinolones (enrofloxacin, ofloxacin) was due to uncovered gyrA/parC gene mutations or efflux pumps (e.g., QepA). Tetracycline exhibited a 100 % phenotypic resistance rate with a genotype coverage rate of 96.4 % (27/28); this universal phenotypic resistance obscured genotype-phenotype correlation, with one genotype-negative isolate exhibiting phenotypic resistance, suggesting a possible phenotypic false positive or resistance gene false negative.

Comparison of resistance gene counts

The number of resistance genes exhibited significant variation across sampling sites, time points, and farms (Fig. 5). Cloacal samples consistently harbored higher gene counts (Broiler farm, July: 10 to 14; Layer farm, August: 7 to 25; Layer farm, October: 3 to 18), with the peak count (25 genes) detected in an August cloacal sample (ID191) from the layer farm. Eggshell samples carried substantial gene numbers in both August (6 to 15) and October (16 to 24). Feed samples showed the greatest variability (Broiler farm, July: 6 genes; Layer farm, August: 0 and 20 genes), with the minimum count (0 genes) observed in an August layer farm sample (ID240). Among vector samples, flies carried relatively high numbers (Depopulated house, July: 4 to 11; Layer farm, August: 12 to 15; October: 8 genes). Temporally: July broiler farm samples contained 6 to 16 genes, while depopulated house samples (floor, flies) ranged from 4 to 11; August layer farm samples demonstrated overall higher and broader counts (0 to 25); October layer farm samples showed elevated counts in eggshells and select environmental samples (e.g., walls, air), whereas cloacal and fecal samples displayed marked heterogeneity (1 to 18 genes). These data visually demonstrate the ubiquity and heterogeneity of resistance genes within layer farm environments and biological specimens. Overall, resistance gene abundance followed a distinct gradient: host-associated sources (cloaca) > contact sources (eggshell) > vector sources (flies) > environmental sources (feed, walls, air, floor, etc.).

Analysis of mobile genetic elements

All 39 APEC isolates harbored MGEs. PlasmidFinder predicted 25 plasmid replicon types, with IncFIB(AP001918) being the predominant replicon (34/39), followed by IncFIC(FII) (n = 22), IncFII (n = 11), and p0111 (n = 10). The MGE analysis additionally identified 58 insertion sequences (IS), 14 transposons, and 2 miniature inverted-repeat transposable elements (MITEs). Among these, 26 MGE types demonstrated associations with resistance genes (Fig. 6). Resistance genes underwent persistent dissemination via MGEs across diverse environmental samples from July to October: IS575 functioned as a core vector, exhibiting high detection frequencies in broiler farms, depopulated layer houses, and various environmental samples (including eggshells, walls, egg conveyor belts, and flies), primarily carrying aminoglycoside resistance genes aph(6)-Id/aph(3′')-Ib and sulfonamide resistance gene sul2. Beyond IS575, sul2 co-occurred with other MGEs such as ISAba1, ISVsa3, and ISEc59. ISVsa3 not only carried the aforementioned aph-sul2 combination but also frequently mediated enrichment and dissemination of tetracycline resistance gene tet(A) and florfenicol resistance gene floR within layer farm environments—the latter (floR) was additionally disseminated independently or co-occurring with erm(42) and sul2 via elements like IS3 across feces, floors, personnel, and flies. ISKpn19 primarily facilitated dissemination of quinolone resistance gene qnrS1 or tetracycline resistance gene tet(A), detected in layer farm cloacal swabs, feed, cages, walls, egg conveyor belts, and flies. Plasmids played equally crucial roles: IncFIA/FIC(FII) plasmids co-located with multiple β-lactamase genes (blaTEM-1B, blaTEM-135, blaTEM-20, blaTEM-32, blaTEM-1C), aminoglycoside resistance genes (aph(6)-Id, aph(3′')-Ib), and sul2, dfrA14, qnrS1 on the same contig; IncHI2A and IncI2 plasmids were associated with colistin resistance gene mcr-1.1. Furthermore, specific resistance genes exhibited strong MGE linkages: mph(A) co-distributed with IS61 across eggshells, feed, flies, walls, and air in layer farms; blaCTX-M-55 primarily co-occurred with ISEc9-like elements; aac(3)-IV and aph(4)-Ia predominantly associated with ISEc59; and aac(3)-IId was carried by ISCfr1.

Serotyping results

Serotype O9:H19 was the most environmentally widespread across the layer farm, detected at four distinct sites in October: the manure conveyor belt (ID553), eggshells (ID441), air (ID599), and walls (ID513). O15:H18 was concentrated in samples from the layer farm in August, specifically in feed (ID243), the manure belt (ID331), and cages (ID272). O76:H45 was identified in the breeder farm water source (ID47) in July and on the egg conveyor belt (ID577) at the layer farm in October. Other serotypes, such as O161:H4, O119:H27, O71:H16, O2:H10, O4:H4, O1:H34, O22:H4, O163:H23, O128ac:H34, O78:H25, and O8:H10, were predominantly sporadically distributed. Importantly, among the three major serotypes associated with APEC strains—O1, O2, and O78 (Wang et al., 2022; Guabiraba and Schouler, 2015)—one isolate each was detected in this study (ID279, ID452, and ID299, respectively).

Virulence gene detection results

Genes encoding the enterobactin system (entB/C/D/E/F/S, fepA/B/C/D/G, fes), key virulence factors (the efflux pump acrB, adhesion protein ompA, curli assembly protein csgB, and regulator rcsB), major type 1 fimbriae genes (e.g., adhesin fimH), and the ECP fimbriae operon (yagZ/ecpA, etc.) were universally present in all strains (39/39). Genes of the T2SS secretion system (gspC-K/M) were detected in 84.6 %–97.4 % of strains, T6SS effector proteins espL1/R1 in 84.6 %–92.3 %, while T5SS invasion factors (espX1/4/5, adhesin fdeC) were identified in 79.5 %–87.2 % of strains. Among auxiliary iron acquisition systems, aerobactin (iucA/B/D, iutA; 74.4 %) constituted the predominant pathway, whereas yersiniabactin (ybt operon, irp2, fyuA; 28.2 %) marked a subset of strains with enhanced invasive potential. Low-frequency virulence genes exhibited heterogeneous distribution: the heme transport system chuA-Y (7.7 %–20.0 %) and heme receptor shuA-Y (7.7 %–17.9 %), along with the enterotoxin astA (33.3 %), were sporadically present, while P fimbriae (the pap gene cluster; 5.1 %–20.5 %), capsular synthesis genes (kpsM/D; 23.1 %), and the neuroinvasive factor ibeA (5.1 %) occurred only sporadically.

MLST and Clonal Complexes

The 39 APEC strains exhibited significant genetic diversity, comprising 22 distinct STs, with 4 strains remaining non-assignable (NA) (Fig. 7). ST162 (12.8 %, n = 5), ST69 (10.3 %, n = 4), and ST155 (7.7 %, n = 3) emerged as the predominant types. All ST69 strains were exclusively assigned to the independent clonal complex CC69, while ST162 strains uniformly belonged to clonal complex CC469, each forming stable phylogenetic lineages. ST155 and its phylogenetically related variants (ST58 strains ID240 and ID401) constituted clonal complex CC155, demonstrating local microevolution of distinct ST strains within the farming environment.

Core Genome Multilocus Sequence Typing (cgMLST)

A phylogenetic tree of the 39 APEC strains was constructed based on cgMLST (Fig. 8), revealing allelic differences ranging from 1 to 2,429 loci between strains. Applying the homology threshold established in reference (Schürch et al., 2018) (strains differing by ≤ 10 loci classified as homologous), two homologous strains were identified within cgST115177 (ID243 from an August layer farm feed sample and ID331 from a fecal conveyor belt, differing by 1–2 loci). Similarly, three homologous strains were found in cgST130769 (ID599 from an eggshell, ID441 from air, and ID513 from a wall, differing by 1–4 loci). Furthermore, isolates collected at different times and sampling sites clustered within the same clade; for example, strain ID577 from an October layer farm egg conveyor belt in cgST87293 clustered with strain ID47 from a July depopulated house water source and strain ID246 from an August layer farm water source, differing by 41 and 112 loci, respectively.

Discussion

Poultry colibacillosis can be prevented and controlled through the rational therapeutic use of antimicrobials. However, the evolution of multidrug-resistant APEC strains and the widespread dissemination of resistant strains pose significant challenges for mitigating APEC infection risks (Subedi et al., 2018).

APEC isolation rate analysis

Analysis of the spatiotemporal distribution of 614 samples in this study revealed significant heterogeneity in the transmission of APEC. Temporally, August in the layer farm represented a peak period for the isolation of E. coli and its pathogenic variants. Notably, APEC exhibited enhanced proliferation characteristics during the layer period; for instance, the proportion of APEC on direct contact surfaces in August rose to 30.0 % (a 95 % increase compared to the brooding period), while total E. coli recovery only reached 74 % of the brooding period level. This dynamic change may be associated with host physiological stress (e.g., immunity fluctuations due to egg-laying) and increased environmental temperature and humidity. Spatially, APEC was enriched in areas with high organic matter content, such as the cloaca, manure, and flies, while its prevalence was lowest on abiotic contact surfaces like air and samples from personnel. Barbosa similarly observed enrichment of high-risk APEC clones in the fecal-cloacal pathway in Brazilian farms (Barbosa et al., 2023). Collectively, these findings indicate that baseline chick carriage and hygiene control during the laying period are critical interventions for APEC prevention on this farm.

APEC antimicrobial resistance (AMR) analysis

Inappropriate antibiotic use in intensive farming is a key driver for the emergence and persistence of resistant APEC strains, exacerbating the difficulty of clinical treatment for animal infections (Almeida et al., 2025;). In this study, 93.5 % of APEC isolates exhibited phenotypic resistance to tetracyclines, and genotypic detection revealed a high prevalence (89.7 %) of the tetracycline resistance gene tet(A). This directly correlates with the farm's long-term, intermittent use of doxycycline (a tetracycline-class antibiotic), demonstrating a strong association between antibiotic usage and the evolution of bacterial resistance. The high frequency of the florfenicol resistance gene floR (69.2 %) and the sulfonamide resistance gene sul2 (64.1 %) further confirms the selective pressure exerted by these drug classes within the farming system. Notably, the detection rate of the quinolone resistance gene qnrS1 exceeded 50 % (53.8 %), indicating its widespread dissemination against commonly used antimicrobials. Although the colistin resistance gene mcr-1.1 had a low detection rate (5.1 %), its presence constitutes a significant public health risk due to colistin's status as a last-resort antimicrobial (Liu et al., 2024). This finding aligned with studies on antibiotic resistance in chicken-derived E. coli from Zhejiang, Qinghai, and other regions in China, which had similarly exhibited high resistance to tetracycline, florfenicol, and sulfonamide drugs(Ma et, al., 2022;Tang et, al., 2022). Concurrently, China's 2023 clinical bacterial resistance surveillance revealed that over 50 % of E. coli isolates exhibited resistance to quinolones and sulfonamides. This underscored the urgent need to intensify antimicrobial resistance prevention and control efforts to avert public health crises.

WGS-based analysis of antimicrobial resistance gene (ARG) distribution revealed clear heterogeneity within the farm environment. Driven by high bacterial loads and selective pressure in the intestinal microenvironment, ARG abundance in the host cloaca (10-25 genes) was generally higher than on environmental or abiotic carriers. Contamination of eggshell samples (6-24 genes) may be linked to microbial surface contamination; ARG variation in feed samples suggests inconsistent quality control or unstable environmental input; the high ARG abundance and distribution in flies (vector samples) highlight their significant risk for horizontal transmission. Temporally, the peak in ARGs observed in August samples may relate to higher temperatures promoting bacterial proliferation and resistance gene expression, while differences in October samples underscore the impact of seasonal changes on microbial growth. This spatiotemporal heterogeneity reveals multiple driving factors, including antibiotic usage patterns, environmental temperature/humidity fluctuations, and microbial community succession. Furthermore, the detection of ARGs across diverse environmental samples confirms the potential for cross-compartmental transmission of resistance within the "One Health" framework.

All strains harbored MGEs, indicating a high risk for horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of resistance genes. The high frequency of the plasmid replicon IncFIB(AP001918) underscores the core dissemination role of IncF-family plasmids in APEC, a plasmid type also prevalent in human clinical E. coli isolates (Johnson et al., 2005). The co-localization of IS575 and the aph-sul2 fixed gene cluster on the same contig mediates co-resistance to aminoglycoside-sulfonamide combinations. Similarly, Tn2 transposons specifically carry blaTEM variants, acting as dedicated vectors for β-lactamase gene spread. IS61, the primary carrier of the mph(A) gene, was widely distributed in media like eggshells and aerosols, highlighting environmental exposure risks. Crucially, the co-occurrence of mcr-1.1 with ISApl1, IncHI2A, and IncI2 is particularly significant due to the public health risk of colistin resistance gene transmission to humans via plasmids, given colistin's last-resort status (Johnson et al., 2005). MGEs in environmental samples further revealed niche differences in resistance transmission. Water sources were dominated by the ISEc9-blaCTX-M-55 and ISEc59-sul2 combinations; isolates from eggshells were enriched with multidrug resistance elements (ISAba1, IS575, ISCfr1, IS61, ISVsa3), signifying the farm environment's risk as an ARG reservoir. Fly-derived strains carried diverse elements like IS575 and ISKpn19. ISVsa3 functioned as a multidrug resistance hub, carrying floR alone or co-existing with tet(A), sul2, and aph, making it a key target for controlling co-resistance to florfenicol-tetracycline-sulfonamide combinations. The dispersed distribution of qnrS1 across six element types (e.g., ISKpn19, IS575) reflects the complexity of transmission pathways, necessitating multi-pathway blocking strategies for quinolone resistance control. The temporal stability of MGE-ARG combinations in environmental samples (e.g., persistent IS575 from July to October 2024) further validates the role of the poultry farm environment as an ARG reservoir. This multi-compartmental diffusion pattern aligns with Brooks' findings under the "One Health" framework: animal feces, soil, and water share resistomes via MGEs (Brooks et al., 2023).

MLST, cgMLST, and SNP-Based Phylogenetic Analysis

Homology analysis of APEC is crucial for understanding its genetic diversity and evolutionary relationships. Initial correlation analysis employed the MLST method (Ranabhat et al., 2024). Among the 22 distinct MLST types identified in this study, ST162, ST69, and ST155 were predominant. ST69 was detected across multiple sampling areas (Feed, Fecal belt, Cages, Eggshell) in the August 2024 Layer Farm and showed a strong association with the O15:H18 serotype (3/4 strains). cgMLST further confirmed these isolates as cgST115177, their concordance evidencing clonal spread within the farm and suggesting a transmission chain potentially originating from a common source like feed or environmental contamination. Studies indicate that ST69 E. coli isolates represent an emerging high-risk clone, being one of the most common clinical isolates from urinary tract infections (Cuénod et al., 2023). Furthermore, a frequent lineage in avian and human ExPEC infections, associated with high virulence gene carriage and antibiotic resistance (Salaheen et al., 2023). In dairy and poultry studies, ST69 strains often carry multiple virulence factors (e.g., iutA, iroN) and causing systemic infections (Salaheen et al., 2023). We had isolated ST69 from laying hens, which carried the IncFIB plasmid, the tet(A) antibiotic resistance gene, and virulence factors including the heme transport system chuA-Y, the heme receptor shuA-Y, the enterotoxin astA, the P-pili (pap gene cluster), and the capsular synthesis genes (kpsM/D). Studies had indicated that the IncFIB plasmid carrying resistance and virulence genes exhibited high prevalence in human populations, typically exceeding 50 % (Johnson et al., 2010). Therefore, ST69 in layer farming posed significant public health risks. It could directly infect workers and indirectly spread to humans through environmental contamination and egg consumption. Furthermore, the presence of IncFIB carrying antibiotic resistance and virulence genes substantially increased the risk of transmitting these genes. Furthermore, ST162 was detected in October layer farm samples (Fecal belt, Eggshell, Air, Walls), and its consistency with cgST130769 and serotype O9:H19 indicates persistent transmission via environmental media. The persistence of ST155 in brooder farm and layer farm water sources suggests water may be a key contamination reservoir. Additionally, the co-occurrence of ST746 in personnel and cloacal samples reflects a risk for cross-transmission between humans and poultry.

Compared to MLST, cgMLST, by extending to hundreds or thousands of core genes, effectively discriminates minor genetic differentiations between strains sharing the same Sequence Type (ST), enabling precise differentiation of sub-lineages within Clonal Complexes (CC) (Yan et al., 2023). In cgMLST analysis, isolates differing by ≤ 10 loci are considered homologous, indicating direct transmission chains or a common source (Schürch et al., 2018). This study identified 33 cgMLST types among the 39 APEC isolates. The minimum spanning tree revealed clusters containing isolates from different times and sampling points, demonstrating horizontal transmission of APEC within the layer production chain. For example, cgST87293 was detected in ID47 (Jul 2024 Brooder Farm, Water), ID246 (Aug 2024 Layer Farm, Water, MLST NA), and ID577 (Oct 2024 Layer Farm, Egg belt). Although ID246 had serotype H25 (O-antigen missing) while ID47 and ID577 were both O76:H45, this supports the hypothesis of water as a transmission vector, though serotype variation warrants cautious interpretation. The two isolates of cgST115177 (ID243 and ID331), originating from feed and the fecal conveyor belt in the August layer farm, differed by only 1-2 loci, indicating high homology and suggesting cross-environmental transmission chains within the layer farm. Studies have also isolated APEC in hatcheries, emphasizing feed and manure belts as important vectors, especially during periods of high chick mortality when pathogens spread via equipment cross-contamination (Steffen et al., 2025). Minimal locus differences (≤ 2) further suggest recent common ancestry or direct transmission events (Cody et al., 2017). Additionally, the three isolates of cgST130769 (ID599, ID441, ID513), all from different sampling points (Eggshell, Air, Walls) in the October 2024 Layer Farm and differing by ≤ 4 loci, were also clearly homologous, indicating a risk of horizontal transmission.

In molecular epidemiology, SNPs serve as crucial genetic markers for strain typing, source tracking, and evolutionary studies (Rahman et al., 2022). Analyzing SNP differences between strains via WGS effectively traces pathogen transmission chains, identifying potential infection sources and pathways (Blankenship et al., 2023). This study found that SNP differences among ST69 isolates from different sites (Feed, Fecal belt, Cages) within the same layer farm (August Layer Farm) ranged from 39 to 283. The minimal difference (39 SNPs) reflects homology consistent with recent transmission from a common source (Steffen et al., 2025; Magray et al., 2019). For instance, feed or the fecal belt could serve as cross-contamination sources enabling rapid clonal spread within the flock (Ranabhat et al., 2024; Ievy et al., 2020). The maximum difference (283 SNPs) was below the common APEC sub-lineage differentiation threshold (typically <500 SNPs) (Papoušková et al., 2019; Dziva et al., 2013), indicating all isolates belonged to the same evolutionary lineage without long-term genetic divergence. Another group with relatively small SNP differences were ST162 isolates (ID441, ID513, ID599), originating from Eggshell, Air, and Walls in the October 2024 Layer Farm. Although all belonging to ST162 (a globally distributed high-risk clone), SNP differences ranging from 102 to 350 indicated high genetic diversity among the strains. According to core genome analysis standards, SNP differences ≥100 typically represent distinct sub-clonal lineages, signifying the presence of multiple independent APEC transmission chains within this farm.

Conclusion

The explosive proliferation of antimicrobial-resistant APEC in layer farms during the laying period, its enrichment in high-organic-matter media (cloaca, manure, flies), and the strong concordance between resistance gene profiles (e.g., tet(A), floR, sul2) and the farm's antibiotic usage history collectively reveal a dual driving mechanism of spatiotemporal exposure risk and antimicrobial selection pressure. Resistance genes form stable reservoirs across multiple environmental compartments (eggshells, water sources, flies) through modular combinations on MGEs, enabling resistance to breach species barriers and establish cross-host transmission chains. Concurrently, the clonal spread of lineages like ST69 and ST162 molecularly corroborates the objective risk of horizontal cross-transmission among poultry, eggs, the environment, and humans. This chain of evidence underscores the urgent need for APEC control strategies grounded in the "One Health" concept, employing integrated approaches including enhanced hygiene during the laying period, biosecurity management, strict antibiotic control, and decontamination of environmental resistance gene reservoirs.
