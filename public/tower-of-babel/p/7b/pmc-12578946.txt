An importance sampling vortex particle method for turbulence visualization simulation in maritime simulators
Authors: Tianhui Zhu, Hongxiang Ren, Rui Tao, Dejian Wei, Pinliang Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysically-based turbulence modeling significantly enhances the realism of maritime simulator scenarios. We propose a novel importance sampling vortex particle method to simulate turbulent details within the SPH framework. The core of our method is a Monte Carlo estimator that approximates the global vorticity of the fluid system, addressing the computational cost associated with the global iteration of vortex particles. By constructing a probability function for importance sampling of vortex particles, we effectively suppress high-frequency variance fluctuations and improve simulation accuracy. Experimental results demonstrate that our importance sampling vortex particle method effectively reduces vorticity field variance by about 27% compared to uniform sampling, thereby improving simulation accuracy and producing visually prominent turbulent motion in the fluids. This study provides a theoretical reference for realistic ocean scene simulation in maritime simulators.Keywords: Maritime simulator, Vortex particle method, Importance sampling, Computer graphics, Ocean scene visualizationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Realistic maritime scene simulation remains a challenge in the field of visual simulation for maritime simulators and is widely applied in ocean engineering and computer graphics. High-fidelity simulation is essential for crew training and operational immersion, particularly in complex turbulent scenarios where accurately reproducing fluid dynamics is critical for vessel control feedback, visual coherence, and user immersion. Current ocean wave modeling in maritime simulators primarily relies on spectrum-based methods1. Recent studies have synthesized physically accurate spectral waves using Fourier-based approaches2. Although these methods are computationally efficient and physically consistent, they fundamentally describe wave fields through global frequency superposition, lacking dynamic coupling and interaction with other physical entities under realistic sea conditions.

By solving the incompressible Navier–Stokes Equation (NSE)3, fluid motion can be accurately described while conserving momentum, resulting in precise simulation outcomes. The Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH) method4, a meshless Lagrangian approach, discretizes the NSE using particles that carry fluid properties. This method avoids the difficulties of Eulerian grid-based techniques in handling topological changes and is particularly effective for simulating complex fluid behavior involving free surfaces and large deformations, making it suitable for ocean scene simulation. While standard SPH methods maintain global momentum conservation at the macroscopic level, their performance at the microscopic scale is limited. The smoothing kernel used in the discretization process and the addition of artificial viscosity improve numerical stability and reduce density-volume oscillations. However, these measures also result in the loss of high-frequency fluid details and rapid vorticity dissipation, thereby weakening the representation of small-scale vortical structures essential for turbulence simulation5.

To overcome these limitations, recent studies have introduced Lagrangian vortex methods as a compensation mechanism for turbulence details in high Reynolds number fluid simulations. Vortex methods track the evolution, stretching, and diffusion of vorticity using the vorticity formulation of the NSE. In practice, the vorticity field can be discretized using vortex particles6,7, vortex filaments8,9, or vortex sheets10,11, and the velocity field is reconstructed using the Biot–Savart integration. These methods effectively capture localized vorticity and preserve its temporal evolution, thereby maintaining key turbulent features. Among them, the vortex particle method is particularly compatible with SPH due to its particle-based formulation, making it easier to integrate. Therefore, we adopt the vortex particle method in this work. However, treating all SPH fluid particles as vortex particles for vorticity computation would lead to significant computational overhead. Thus, a strategy is needed to reduce computational cost without compromising simulation fidelity.

Inspired by the work of Rioux-Lavoie et al.12, who incorporated Monte Carlo estimators—commonly used in computer graphics rendering—into fluid solvers, and Ye et al.13, who decoupled the vortex particle method from pressure projection and avoided global iteration by sampling a subset of SPH particles, we propose a Monte Carlo vortex particle method with importance sampling14 to simulate turbulent details in SPH fluids. Importance sampling is a variance reduction technique used in Monte Carlo methods. Its core idea is to adjust the sampling probability function according to the distribution of significant regions of the integrand, thereby improving integration accuracy. In SPH fluid domains, vortex structures are highly localized. Given the characteristics of Monte Carlo-based vortex particle sampling, the global vorticity is often dominated by particles with high vorticity. Therefore, by selecting an appropriate probability function to guide sampling toward these critical regions, our method effectively reduces variance and improves integration accuracy.

Related work

SPH-Based Incompressible Fluids

In the field of computer graphics, extensive research has been conducted on the SPH method. Müller et al.15 first introduced SPH for fluid simulation in computer graphics. At that time, limited hardware capabilities restricted scene scale, and the method was based on the ideal gas equation of state without modeling incompressible fluid. SPH pressure solvers can be broadly categorized into local and global approaches. Local pressure solvers estimate pressure using an equation of state, with representative methods including WCSPH16 and PCISPH17. Global pressure solvers compute pressure by solving the Pressure Poisson Equation (PPE), with examples such as IISPH18, DFSPH19, and PBF20. The DFSPH method proposed by Bender et al.19 simultaneously enforces volume incompressibility and divergence-free velocity fields by solving two PPEs within a single simulation step. This approach is widely adopted and enables stable simulation of large-scale fluid systems.

Due to the use of kernel functions for fluid property estimation and the discrete particle approximation, SPH methods inherently introduce numerical dissipation21. To address this, various grid-particle and particle–particle hybrid methods have been proposed, such as Hybrid Eulerian-DFSPH22, DFPBF23, FDSPH24, and SPH-MPS25. These hybrid methods are often designed for specific application scenarios and achieve good performance within those contexts. To reproduce high-fidelity turbulent behavior, additional techniques are required to capture small-scale turbulence details.

Turbulence

In the Lagrangian framework, existing strategies for restoring high-frequency details in SPH-based simulations can be broadly classified into three categories: vorticity confinement techniques26, micropolar fluid models27, and Lagrangian vortex methods28.

Vorticity confinement enhances pre-existing vortical structures by applying additional forces. Originally introduced by Fedkiw et al.29 for smoke simulation in computer graphics, this approach was later refined by Letine et al.26 to improve energy conservation. Jang et al.30 introduced a force-level classification strategy under varying conditions to achieve more stable visual outcomes. Macklin and Müller31 incorporated vorticity confinement into position-based dynamics to restore angular velocity. Despite its ease of implementation, this method only amplifies existing vortices without generating new flow features. Furthermore, its effect is highly parameter-dependent and introduces artificial energy into the system, which compromises physical consistency.

Micropolar fluid models32 describe micro-rotational motion via asymmetric stress tensors and have been used to simulate microscopic rotational effects in systems such as liquid crystals33, animal blood34, and ferrofluids35. Bender et al.36 applied this model to augment surface turbulence details in DFSPH. While effective in capturing fine-scale surface phenomena through modeling gyration, the method remains limited in large-scale flow simulations, as it lacks the ability to reconstruct intrinsic vorticity from the kinetic energy of fluid system.

Lagrangian vortex methods reconstruct turbulent details using filaments8,9, particles6,28, or sheets11. These methods solve the vorticity form of the NSE to track and evolve vorticity. A major challenge lies in reconstructing the velocity field from the vorticity field, which requires evaluating the Biot–Savart integral—a computationally expensive process. Research in this area has primarily focused on reducing the computational cost to improve simulation efficiency. Some studies simplify the problem by addressing boundary coupling through hybrid grid-based approaches. For example, Zhu et al.37 coupled SPH with FLIP, solving the vorticity equation on the grid near boundaries. Golas et al.38 proposed a hybrid domain decomposition method that accelerates large-scale simulations using compact vortex bases and a small Eulerian region. Wang et al.39 modeled viscous vortex dynamics using vortex particles based on the Rankine vortex model and simplified governing equations. Zhang et al.5 introduced a hybrid advection scheme combining vorticity and velocity to reduce numerical dissipation, which is more suitable for smoke simulation. Liu et al.40 replaced the Biot–Savart integration with a stream function approach to reconstruct SPH turbulence from the vorticity equation. However, its performance is highly sensitive to the selection of control parameters, which compromises simulation stability.

In addition to these approaches, several none Navier Stokes equation methods have been explored. For instance, Chern et al.41 introduced the Clebsch representation into fluid simulation in graphics. Feng et al.42 combined the Clebsch formulation with a gauge transformation to model coherent vortex structures. Xiong et al.43 combined the level set method with the Clebsch approach to address numerical instabilities at dynamic boundaries for free surface flow simulation. While effective, these methods are difficult to couple with NSE-based solvers and are not easily integrated with pressure solvers required for stable simulation.

Among Lagrangian vortex approaches, the vortex particle method is particularly well-suited for particle-based fluid simulation. A key challenge is accelerating the Biot–Savart summation. Rioux-Lavoie et al.12 proposed a recursive Monte Carlo estimator of the Biot–Savart law in the Eulerian framework to reduce computational cost. Ye et al.13 adapted this method to SPH using uniform sampling to define the vortex particle distribution, without considering the impact of sampling strategies on simulation accuracy.

In our approach, we integrate a Monte Carlo estimator into the SPH-based fluid particle system for turbulence simulation. We explicitly define a non-uniform sampling probability function using importance sampling and perform the Biot–Savart summation accordingly.

Results and discussion

To evaluate the improvements in simulation accuracy and turbulence representation achieved by the proposed importance sampling vortex particle method, we compare our turbulence model with the mainstream turbulence simulation approaches. To ensure consistency across experiments, all simulations use the DFSPH pressure solver and the boundary handling method proposed by Akinci et al.44The entire experimental framework is implemented in Python using the high-performance graphics programming language Taichi45, with development carried out in the PyCharm IDE. Conclusions and future work

We propose a Lagrangian vortex particle method based on importance sampling, which is integrated with SPH-based fluid solvers for turbulence simulation. In this approach, SPH particles are paired with randomly sampled vortex particles. A Monte Carlo estimator is used to approximate the global vorticity of the fluid system, reducing the computational cost associated with evaluating the Biot–Savart integral. A probability function is designed to enable importance sampling, which effectively reduces vorticity field variance and suppresses high-frequency fluctuations, thereby improving simulation accuracy. Experimental results demonstrate that our importance sampling vortex particle method can be stably coupled with SPH and produces visually realistic turbulent motion. It also adapts well to maritime scenes with complex boundary conditions.

While our method improves the representation of turbulence in SPH simulations, several challenges remain. First, the design of the probability function relies on local vorticity features of individual vortex particles. In multiphase fluids and multiscale vortex structures, this can lead to biased definitions of important regions, limiting coverage of the full fluid domain. Future work could incorporate machine learning or adaptive sampling to dynamically optimize the probability function. In addition, due to multiple rounds of neighbor searches, physically based fluid simulations remain computationally expensive. A reduced order model based on Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) and machine learning technique46,47 is proposed to improve the computational cost. Real-time simulation in large-scale maritime scenario is still difficult to achieve. Addressing this challenge will require not only advances in hardware but also the development of efficient algorithmic solutions in future research.
