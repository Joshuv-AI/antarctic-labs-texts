Pulmonary mucormycosis in a young adult as a complication of a bronchopulmonary fistula
Authors: Norman Alejandro Rendón Mejía, Anahí Carrasco Chávez, Niza Idaly Lozoya Carrasco, Adriana Torres Zagarnaga, Luis Fernando González Calzadillas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary mucormycosis (PM) is a life-threatening opportunistic infection caused by fungi of the Mucorales order, including genera such as Rhizopus, Absidia, and Mucor. This condition predominantly affects immunocompromised individuals, with mortality rates exceeding 50 % in high-risk populations. We present the case of a 33-year-old female with a medical history of type 1 diabetes mellitus (poorly controlled) and hypothyroidism, who presented to the hospital with a 7-day history of productive cough (mucoid expectoration), chills, and myalgia. An unenhanced chest computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a pneumonic process with left basal consolidation, predominantly in the lower lobe. Empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy was initiated; however, the patient exhibited clinical deterioration, prompting consultation with the thoracic surgery team. A left thoracotomy with wedge resection and histopathological biopsy was performed, confirming invasive pulmonary mucormycosis through visualization of broad, pauci-septate hyphae on microscopy. Following diagnosis, the infectious disease department initiated antifungal therapy with intravenous liposomal amphotericin B (5 mg/kg/day). The patient demonstrated marked clinical improvement over the subsequent two weeks, enabling discharge with plans for 6 weeks of continued antifungal treatment and glycemic optimization.

Introduction

Mucormycosis represents a life-threatening angioinvasive fungal infection caused by organisms belonging to the order Mucorales (formerly classified within the class Zygomycetes), constituting the third most prevalent invasive mycosis worldwide, superseded only by candidiasis and aspergillosis [[1], [2], [a], [b], [3]]. The taxonomic order Mucorales comprises 55 genera encompassing 260 species, among which approximately 40 have been identified as clinically significant human pathogens [4,5].

Epidemiological data demonstrate that India exhibits the highest global prevalence, with an estimated incidence of 140 cases per million population, whereas population-based surveillance studies conducted in the United States indicate an annual incidence of 1.7 cases per million, corresponding to approximately 500 cases annually [6]. A comprehensive retrospective analysis conducted in Mexico spanning three decades (1982–2016) documented 418 confirmed cases of mucormycosis, with a notable predominance (72 %) among patients with underlying type 2 diabetes mellitus, and Rhizopus species identified as the most frequently isolated pathogen [7]. Contemporary pediatric epidemiological data from 2011 revealed that among 100 documented cases of pulmonary fungal infections in the pediatric population, mucormycosis accounted for 6 % of cases, in contrast to aspergillosis (54 %), pneumocystosis (26 %), and histoplasmosis (7 %) [8].

The pathophysiology of Mucorales infection is characterized by their predilection for environments exhibiting iron overload, acidic pH, and immunological compromise, wherein these organisms demonstrate rapid proliferation through the production of resilient zygospores. The distinctive histopathological manifestation comprises broad, ribbon-like, pauciseptate hyphae exhibiting characteristic angioinvasive properties, culminating in extensive tissue necrosis [[9], [10], [11]]. The principal modes of transmission include inhalation of airborne spores (resulting in respiratory tract involvement), ingestion (affecting the gastrointestinal system), or direct traumatic inoculation (producing cutaneous manifestations). Immunocompromised populations demonstrate disproportionate susceptibility, with rhinocerebral involvement representing the most frequent clinical presentation (55 %), followed by pulmonary manifestations (30 %). Pulmonary mucormycosis frequently presents with clinical features mimicking chronic bacterial pneumonia; however, progression to severe complications, including bronchopleural fistula formation, pulmonary abscess development, or chronic cavitary disease, may occur [12].

Herein, we describe the clinical presentation and management of a 32-year-old female patient with inadequately controlled type I diabetes mellitus who presented to our tertiary care facility exhibiting progressive dyspnea, productive cough, and constitutional symptoms including rigors. Radiographic evaluation demonstrated left lower lobe consolidation with evidence of cavitation. Despite implementation of broad-spectrum empirical antimicrobial therapy, progressive clinical deterioration necessitated surgical intervention. Exploratory thoracotomy with wedge resection was performed, and subsequent histopathological examination definitively established the diagnosis of pulmonary mucormycosis, revealing characteristic invasive, nonseptate hyphae with extensive tissue invasion. The patient's postoperative management comprised systemic antifungal therapy with liposomal amphotericin B in conjunction with optimization of glycemic control, resulting in gradual clinical improvement. This case exemplifies the paramount importance of prompt recognition, definitive diagnosis, and implementation of comprehensive multidisciplinary therapeutic strategies in the management of invasive mucormycosis.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old female patient with inadequately controlled type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and concomitant hypothyroidism presented to our institution with a 10-day history of persistent fever, non-productive cough, pleuritic chest pain, and progressive exertional dyspnea. Initial ambulatory management with empirical oral antimicrobial therapy (specific regimen undocumented) failed to achieve symptomatic improvement. On the twelfth day following symptom onset, the patient required hospitalization at a rural healthcare facility, where she subsequently developed hemoptysis accompanied by progressive respiratory compromise. Laboratory investigations revealed diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), while radiographic imaging demonstrated findings consistent with community-acquired pneumonia. Given the patient's progressive clinical deterioration and absence of definitive diagnostic clarification, she was subsequently transferred to our tertiary care medical center for comprehensive evaluation and management.

Upon admission to our facility (day 12 of illness), physical examination revealed significant hypoxemia (peripheral oxygen saturation 89 % on ambient air), tachypnea, and markedly diminished breath sounds throughout the left hemithorax. High-resolution computed tomography of the thorax demonstrated left lower lobe consolidation with basilar predominance (Fig. 1). Arterial blood gas analysis revealed compensated respiratory alkalosis (pH 7.48, pCO2 28 mmHg). Therapeutic interventions comprised empirical broad-spectrum antimicrobial coverage with intravenous ceftriaxone and ciprofloxacin, aggressive fluid resuscitation for volume optimization, and supplemental oxygen therapy delivered at 2 L/min via nasal cannula. Comprehensive histopathological examination of the resected pulmonary tissue revealed distinctive pathological features: extensive areas of coagulative necrosis with prominent zones of ischemic tissue death accompanied by dense neutrophilic infiltration, consistent with pulmonary infarction secondary to angioinvasive disease. Characteristic fungal morphology was identified, demonstrating broad (10–20 μm diameter), ribbon-like, pauciseptate hyphae exhibiting irregular branching patterns at approximately 90-degree angles, with evident infiltration of vascular lumina and vessel walls (Fig. 5), findings pathognomonic for invasive Mucorales infection. Targeted antifungal therapy was promptly initiated on day 28, comprising combination treatment with liposomal amphotericin B (administered at 5 mg/kg/day intravenously) and isavuconazole (372 mg loading dose followed by 186 mg daily maintenance therapy). Following 14 days of intensive inpatient management, the patient achieved sufficient clinical stability to warrant transition to outpatient care with oral isavuconazole monotherapy. Throughout the subsequent six-month follow-up period, the patient experienced three separate readmissions for management of DKA exacerbations and recurrent respiratory symptomatology.

Discussion

Mucormycosis predominantly manifests in immunocompromised populations, with particular predilection for individuals with diabetes mellitus (DM)—most notably those experiencing diabetic ketoacidosis, chronic renal insufficiency, or diabetic foot complications—as well as patients receiving prolonged systemic corticosteroid therapy [13]. The present case exemplifies this association, documenting a 32-year-old female patient with refractory hyperglycemia despite intensive insulin therapy, thereby elucidating the critical interrelationship between inadequately controlled glycemic status and enhanced susceptibility to opportunistic fungal pathogens.

The pathogenesis of infection characteristically involves inhalation of ubiquitous Mucorales spores present in soil and decomposing organic substrates [14,15]. Occupational exposure through agricultural activities, as demonstrated in our patient's history of corn and pumpkin harvesting, constitutes a well-documented environmental risk factor for disease acquisition [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. The diabetic milieu confers multiple immunological deficits that facilitate fungal pathogenesis, including diminished production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (specifically interleukin-1 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha), impaired phagocytic function of neutrophils and macrophages, and elevated bioavailable iron within tissue microenvironments—factors that synergistically promote spore germination, hyphal proliferation, and subsequent angioinvasion [4,8]. It is noteworthy that diabetes mellitus and solid organ transplantation represent the predominant predisposing conditions for both pulmonary and gastrointestinal manifestations of mucormycosis [11].

Our patient developed severe pulmonary complications—specifically bronchopleural fistula (BPF) formation and parenchymal abscess—within ten days of tertiary care referral, a temporal progression consistent with previously documented median BPF onset intervals (8–12 days) [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. The initial clinical presentation, characterized by progressive dyspnea and pleuritic chest pain in conjunction with antimicrobial-refractory pneumonia, necessitated systematic exclusion of alternative bacterial and viral etiologies through comprehensive microbiological evaluation, including negative sputum cultures, pleural fluid analysis, and molecular testing for SARS-CoV-2 and influenza viruses via reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction.

Radiographic evaluation utilizing chest radiography demonstrated lobar consolidation with cavitary changes, findings concordant with previously published cohort studies documenting consolidation in 43 % and cavitation in 30 % of pulmonary mucormycosis cases [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the thorax, acknowledged as the gold standard imaging modality for assessment of pulmonary mucormycosis [5], revealed ground-glass opacifications, bronchial wall thickening, and radiographic features consistent with BPF formation (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Classical radiological manifestations, including the “halo sign” (a nodular lesion surrounded by ground-glass attenuation) and vascular thrombosis culminating in pulmonary infarction [[3], [4], [5], [6]], were notably absent in this presentation; however, disease extension into contiguous anatomical structures, particularly the pleural space, was radiographically evident.

Definitive diagnosis necessitates histopathological demonstration of characteristic broad, pauciseptate hyphae exhibiting vascular invasion, accompanied by coagulative necrosis and dense neutrophilic infiltration [[6], [7], [8]]. In the present case, tissue biopsy definitively confirmed the presence of morphologically consistent Mucorales hyphae infiltrating small-caliber pulmonary vessels and extending into pleural tissue (Fig. 4). Critical differentiation from invasive aspergillosis—characterized by narrow, septate hyphae demonstrating acute-angle branching patterns—was established through careful morphological analysis [6]. Molecular speciation utilizing reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction targeting the Mucorales-specific 28S ribosomal RNA gene sequence could not be performed due to institutional resource limitations.

In the context of progressive respiratory failure complicated by BPF formation, urgent surgical intervention comprising exploratory thoracotomy, abscess evacuation, and primary fistula repair was undertaken to achieve source control through fungal burden reduction and restoration of pleural anatomical integrity [10]. Adjuvant systemic antifungal therapy was initiated postoperatively, employing combination treatment with liposomal amphotericin B (administered at 5 mg/kg daily) and isavuconazole (372 mg loading dose followed by 186 mg daily maintenance therapy), a regimen consistent with contemporary evidence supporting combination therapy for severe invasive mucormycosis [15,16]. Despite achieving initial clinical stabilization, the patient experienced three subsequent readmissions for recurrent diabetic ketoacidosis and respiratory decompensation over the ensuing six-month period, underscoring the substantial challenges inherent in maintaining optimal glycemic control and ensuring consistent antifungal therapeutic adherence in this complex clinical scenario.

Conclusion

The present case underscores the considerable morbidity and mortality associated with invasive mucormycosis in the diabetic population, emphasizing the paramount importance of maintaining a high index of clinical suspicion when confronted with antimicrobial-refractory pneumonia in this vulnerable demographic. While contrast-enhanced computed tomography and comprehensive histopathological examination remain the fundamental diagnostic modalities for definitive diagnosis, resource constraints in many healthcare settings frequently result in diagnostic delays that may adversely impact clinical outcomes. The implementation of comprehensive multimodal therapeutic strategies—encompassing aggressive surgical debridement for source control, systemic amphotericin B-based antifungal therapy, and meticulous optimization of glycemic parameters—represents an essential approach for mitigating the substantial mortality burden, which exceeds 50 % in cases of pulmonary involvement despite optimal management. Future prospective investigations should prioritize the establishment of evidence-based guidelines regarding optimal duration of antifungal therapy, development of standardized therapeutic protocols for bronchopleural fistula management, and identification of prognostic biomarkers that may facilitate risk stratification and personalized therapeutic interventions in this challenging clinical entity.
