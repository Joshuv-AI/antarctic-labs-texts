ECMO-facilitated rapid and deep hypothermia reduces brain injury on MRI following prolonged cardiac arrest in a translational swine model
Authors: Joseph E. Tonna, Dustin Anderson-Bell, Miriam E. Peckham, Guillaume L. Hoareau, Stavros Drakos, Adam DeHavenon, Matthew D. Alexander, Austin M. Johnson, Jacob Steenblik, Scott T. Youngquist
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNeuroprotective interventions after cardiac arrest are essential but largely lack evidence of efficacy. Early therapeutic hypothermia (TH) is the only intervention that has shown promise in humans. However, despite a consistent signal for efficacy in animal models, conflicting clinical data hamper clinical acceptance. Two potential causes for the lack of translation from animal studies to humans are the time to achieve target temperature in humans and the inability to cool to deep hypothermic states due to the inherent detrimental cardiac effects accompanying deep hypothermia. Given the observed inconsistent impact of TH on human patients with cardiac arrest despite animal data, we developed a perfusion-controlled, translational swine model to quantify the effects of rapid deep TH on HIBI, quantifying severity using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) at a controlled time threshold.MethodsTen swine underwent cardiac arrest with 20 min of “no-flow” state, followed by resuscitation and controlled reperfusion using extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Animals were randomized to either control (normal temperature reperfusion) or rapid hypothermic reperfusion (RHR) (29 °C through ECMO-facilitated cooling). All swine underwent brain MRI with Diffusion Weighted Imaging (DWI) before cardiac arrest and then 2 h after ECMO reperfusion. Whole-brain gray and white matter apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values were compared pre- and post-ECMO cannulation and arrest in all animals.ResultsAt 45 min post-reperfusion, the mean temperature for RHR animals was 30.4 °C (95 % CI 29.6–31.1 °C), while for control animals it was 35.7 °C (95 % CI 34.9–36.5 °C, p < 0.0001). Whole brain ADC in RHR swine increased by a mean of 1.36 ± 4.09 %, while in control swine it decreased by a mean of 4.36 ± 4.50 % (Median difference of −5.91, 95 %CI −12.13 to −0.15; P value = 0.047).DiscussionSwine with induced cardiac arrest who underwent rapid ECMO-mediated cooling post-arrest had less cerebral hypoxic cellular injury, as quantified by changes on MRI DWI, than controls. These findings support the protective effect on neurologic injury of a rapid and brief period of induced deep hypothermia after cardiac arrest. Compared to prior translational models, our use of ECMO has the advantage of an ability to control important factors such as no-flow ischemic time and variability in post-arrest cardiac output as well as to mitigate complications of cardiac dysrhythmias that tend to arise from deep hypothermia. This portends a greater promise for translational success of ECMO-facilitated rapid cooling and potentially other ECMO-mediated models of cardiac arrest than experienced by previous attempts.

Introduction

Hypoxic ischemic brain injury (HIBI) results from inadequate cerebral oxygenation and perfusion during cardiac arrest. HIBI is the most common cause of death/disability in patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest who survive to admission.1 Multiple human trials sought to treat HIBI using various neuroprotection strategies in the setting of recent or ongoing ischemia. The only intervention that has demonstrated a well-replicated preclinical signal for neuroprotection is therapeutic hypothermia (TH),2, 3 with seminal work by Levy et al.4, 5, 6 However, the effectiveness of TH, beyond just avoidance of fever, has failed to translate into the expected benefit in the most extensive human clinical trials to date.7 Postulated reasons for the lack of difference in efficacy between hypothermia and normothermia in these studies include 1) excessive duration between the cardiac arrest event and achieved hypothermia, 2) clinical heterogeneity in other important factors, most notably post-arrest cardiac dysfunction,8 and 3) the observation that the most significant benefit in animals is with temperatures <32 °C or lower. Achieving temperatures <32 °C in humans often causes ventricular arrhythmias, leading to hemodynamic instability or death.9 We, therefore, sought to leverage the hemodynamic supporting effects of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) to investigate the degree of neuroprotection afforded by deep TH (<32 °C) if implemented rapidly after the cardiac arrest when the duration of no-flow time and the parameters of reperfusion are controlled.

Translational animal models have been used to test promising HIBI treatments, with multiple therapies specifically targeting reperfusion injury.10 However, most of these studies have used rodents and have, therefore, suffered from a high rate of translational failure due to substantial differences from human neuroanatomy and neurophysiology.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Swine are currently the most widely accepted animal model for cardiac arrest, as coronary anatomy and blood flow characteristics are similar to humans.16, 17 Additionally, swine have more similar neuroanatomy to humans compared to rodents18 and can undergo clinical neurobehavioral testing to assess functional sequelae of HIBI.19

MRI diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) has been substantiated as an informative and useful test for neuroprognostication in HIBI.20, 21, 22 Studies utilizing DWI to assess HIBI-associated brain changes in piglets have shown a global reduction in apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC)23, 24 and decreased mean diffusivity/increased mean kurtosis in injured brain areas with correlated histopathologic evidence of neuronal necrosis.25 Another study utilizing DWI outcomes in swine undergoing hypothermia after cardiac arrest showed a smaller decrease in ADC values with hypothermia, suggesting reduced cellular injury. The most significant restoration of ADC occurs within 24–72 h of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC).26 However, this study suffered from heterogeneity in cerebral perfusion because the duration of both the “no flow period” (during cardiac arrest) and the “low flow period” (the time from initiation of CPR to ROSC) were variable.

In this study, we hypothesized that rapid, deep, and brief post-arrest TH would decrease whole-brain cellular injury (as assessed by changes in ADC on DWI) in a swine model of cardiac arrest undergoing a fixed duration of no-flow. This was followed by controlled restoration of perfusion to pre-arrest levels using ECMO. This translational model was utilized to achieve greater control over the degree of ischemic insult experienced by each subject and to allow achievement of lower temperatures at a faster rate without inducing hemodynamic instability.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was a randomized, experimental investigation of the impact of a controlled period of ischemia in swine, followed by hypothermic reperfusion using an ECMO circuit, on the outcome of early MRI markers of ischemia/reperfusion injury.

Ethical statement

This experiment conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Animal Welfare Act Regulations and Standards. It was approved by the University of Utah’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Institutional Protocol 17-09009).

Housing and husbandry

Animals were purchased from a commercial breeder, housed in groups within cages with environmental enrichment, and given free access to standard chow and water. They were maintained in this environment and observed periodically for signs of illness for at least seven days before the experiment.

Experimental animals and procedures

Female mixed-breed Yorkshire swine (48 ± 4 kg [kg], N = 12) were fasted for 8 h before the experiment with free access to water. Swine were first sedated with a combination of intramuscular Tiletamine/Zolazepam (4.4 mg [mg]/kg]), Ketamine (2.2 mg/kg), and Xylazine (2.2 mg/kg). Swine were then endotracheally intubated and had vascular access obtained through peripheral intravenous (IV) cannulation in the auricular vein.

General anesthesia was induced and maintained with inhaled isoflurane of 1.5–3 % in 100 % oxygen. Adequate depth of anesthesia was ensured by assessing jaw rigidity every 15 min and, during MRI, the absence of spontaneous movement. Ventilator settings were initiated with tidal volumes of 10 mL/kg, 15 breaths per minute, and a Fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) of 0.25 with subsequent adjustments of minute ventilation to maintain an end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) of 35–45 mm of mercury (mmHg) as measured by sidestream capnography. Continuous plethysmographic oxygen saturation (SpO2) was monitored via a probe attached to the ear or tongue and intermittently confirmed using arterial blood gas analysis. SpO2 was maintained > 92 %. A heating pad and reflecting blanket was used to maintain the animal’s temperature at 37 ± 0.5 °C.

Baseline MRI sequences were obtained on a Siemens PRISMA 3 T scanner for 60 min. The animal was then placed in the supine position, and 5 mL of 2 % lidocaine or 0.5 % bupivacaine were injected into the subcutaneous tissue at planned vascular access sites. Fig. 1 illustrates the experimental setup. Using ultrasound guidance, five French vascular sidearm sheaths were inserted into the femoral artery and external jugular vein. Using fluoroscopy, micromanometer-tipped catheters (Milar Instruments, Houston, TX) were placed in the distal aorta (Ao) and the right atrium (RA) for pressure measurements. Continuous Ao and RA pressure measurements and lead II surface electrocardiogram were recorded on a laptop computer (PowerLab Pro, ADInstruments, Sydney, Australia). Unfractionated heparin (100 international units [IU]/kg) was administered and repeated every hour to prevent thrombus. The femoral artery and ipsilateral femoral vein were accessed using a modified Seldinger technique and, under fluoroscopic guidance, a 15 French (Fr) arterial ECMO cannula and 21–24 Fr venous ECMO cannula were advanced into each vessel, respectively, and maintained clamped. An arterial blood gas sample was analyzed (iSTAT CG8+, iSTAT Corp, Princeton, NJ) to provide baseline values.Fig. 1Experimental setup.

Experimental setup.

Animals were randomized to 20 min of untreated ventricular fibrillation (VF) with or without hypothermia using a prespecified, permuted block randomization scheme (https://www.randomizer.org). VF was induced using a bipolar pacing catheter placed via a sheath in the right external jugular vein in contact with the right ventricle (RV) endocardium. A 9-volt battery was connected to the bipolar pacing leads, and once VF was confirmed on the electrocardiogram, the pacing catheter was withdrawn, and the ventilator was turned off. Standard adhesive defibrillator pads were placed bilaterally on the shaved thorax and attached to a commercial monitor/defibrillator (Zoll R Series ALS Monitor/Defibrillator, Zoll Corp).

At the end of the ischemic period (20 min), full ECMO support was initiated (Rotaflow, Maquet Cardiovascular, Wayne, NJ), with the pump speed adjusted to maintain a flow rate of 2.8–3.5 L/min with 100 % oxygen sweep gas at 3 L/min. After 4 min of reperfusion, the animal was defibrillated with escalating biphasic energies (120 J, 150 J, 200 J) until a spontaneous heartbeat returned. Concomitantly, ventilator support was resumed at pre-arrest settings.

Animals assigned to the RHR arm were then cooled over the first 30–45 min of reperfusion to a target core body temperature of 29 °C, maintained at this temperature for 30 min, and gradually rewarmed to a target of 39 °C (swine resting body temperature) over 1 h (total cooling + rewarming time of 2 h). Cooling was achieved via conduction across the oxygenator membrane by utilizing a thermostatic water circulator (MHC1000, CardioQuip, College Station, TX) filled with ice water and connected to the male Hansen connectors of the oxygenator. Rewarming was achieved by changing the temperature of the water bath in the thermostatic water circulator and augmented using a warming blanket and a reflective blanket. This was necessary to reduce natural cooling due to the externalization of blood volume through the ECMO circuit.

After 1.5–2 h of circulatory support, the animals were weaned from ECMO support; flow rates were decreased by 500–1000 mL/min every 10–15 min, with echocardiographic evaluation of left ventricle (LV) function at each time point via transesophageal echocardiography (FUJIFILM Sonosite, Bothell, WA; Probe T8-3; Sonosite PX). ECMO was withdrawn if sustained mean aortic pressure remained >50 mm Hg, and echocardiography demonstrated an estimated ejection fraction >25 % at ECMO flow of 1.5 L/min. Periodic fluid boluses of normal saline and a continuous epinephrine drip (20–30 µg mcg/min) were utilized, as needed, to achieve these goals. At the end of the experiment, the ECMO pump was turned down to 1500 RPM, the cannulas clamped, the ECMO circuit turned off and the cannulas withdrawn. Hemostasis was achieved using Combat Gauze (QuikClot, Z-Medica, LLC, Willingford, CT) and direct pressure for 10 min.

After decannulation, a repeat brain MRI was performed. At the end of image acquisition, the animal was euthanized. Fig. 2 provides the experimental timeline.Fig. 2Experimental timeline. Sham procedures

To assess the effects of instrumentation, ECMO flow, and vasopressors on MRI findings, a sham group was included in the experiment. Animals randomized to the sham procedure underwent the same instrumentation procedures, including cannulation. However, cardiac arrest was not induced (no-flow period of 0 min). These animals were started on ECMO with a flow rate of 3 L/min and the same oxygenation and sweep as animals treated for cardiac arrest and remained on ECMO for the same period as treatment and control animals before decannulation. During the post arrest period, animals were also administered a continuous infusion of epinephrine at 20 mcg/min.

MRI parameters

The following MRI sequences were obtained of the brain: coronal b2000 DWI in 20 directions (TR = 7000 ms, TE = 86 ms, slice thickness = 3 mm, gap = 0, resolution = 256 × 256, flip angle = 90), and coronal FLAIR (TR = 9800 ms, TE = 116 ms, slice thickness = 3 mm, gap = 0, resolution = 320 × 256, flip angle = 170).

Voxel segmentation and co-registration

Whole brain b2000 ADC measurements of gray and white matter regions were performed as follows: extracranial soft tissues were excluded from the coronal FLAIR sequence on ITK-snap27 using the threshold function, then further segmentation of the brain into gray and white matter regions was performed using FSL (Oxford, United Kingdom).28 Gray and white matter images were then resampled at the resolution of the b2000 ADC image, and voxels for these regions were included in a restriction mask. These gray and white matter restriction masks were co-registered with the b2000 ADC sequence using SPM 12 (Wellcome Trust, London) for MATLAB (Natlick, MA). ADC values in the gray and white matter regions were calculated at the time of imaging using Siemens online reconstruction algorithm and reported in units of 1000 x ADC. Mean values of whole-brain gray and white matter regions were reported for each subject.

Statistical analysis and sample size

MRI interpretation was blindly assessed. Whole brain ADC values for each time point were first determined by averaging ADC values from gray and white matter within each subject. Percent change for each subject was then calculated by comparing their whole brain ADC values pre- vs post-intervention. Mean whole brain ADC values were then calculated by averaging percent change from all individuals within each group. Comparisons between groups were performed utilizing a pairwise two-sample Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test and 95 % confidence intervals (CI) were calculated with the Hodges-Lehmann estimator. Temperature curves were estimated using generalized linear mixed models (Stata’s xtmixed procedure) with group assignment entered into the model as a fixed effect and time as a random effect. We also included the squared value of time to improve model fit, allowing for curvature in the temperature response trajectory. An a priori power calculation could not be performed given the lack of prior literature to support the expected effect size in this current proof-of-concept study. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata Version 18 (College Park, IL, USA). P-values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant, and all tests were two-tailed.

Discussion

Clinical studies investigating neuroprotection after post-arrest therapeutic hypothermia have had mixed results, often suffering from limitations due to either the relevance of the chosen animal model, inadequate degrees of hypothermia, or differences in timing the implementation of hypothermia. In the present study, we sought to tighten the rigor of the methodology and improve the consistency of the HIBI outcome in different groups using an established animal model of HIBI relevant to human anatomy and physiology. We utilized a neurobehavioral and histopathological-validated imaging modality and analytic approach (e.g., changes in ADC values in DWI sequenced brain MRI)20, 21, 22, 23 to quantify HIBI-related changes in swine after a highly controlled and fixed duration cardiac arrest. We employed extracorporeal membrane oxygenation to achieve rapid and consistent cooling to temperatures lower than normally studied due to the risk of arrhythmias.

Our model included controlled induction of VF, fixed duration arrest, controlled reperfusion to normal hemodynamic targets, rapid cooling using the extracorporeal circuit, and controlled post-resuscitation physiology. Using this approach, there were significantly greater ischemic changes in control swine compared to rapidly cooled swine. These findings substantiate recent work that utilizing early and deep cooling using ECMO in swine after cardiac arrest limits hypoxia-induced neuronal damage.29

Despite robust laboratory evidence base for therapeutic hypothermia after cardiac arrest, clinical trials have demonstrated, at best, mixed results. 2, 7, 30, 31 Society guidelines note that the evidence at this time does not demonstrate a benefit to therapeutic hypothermia in the post arrest population.32

Animal studies demonstrate improved neurologic function when treated with therapeutic hypothermia following cardiac arrest. In two meta-analyses, the largest improvements in functional survival are observed when animals are briefly (30–300 min) and rapidly cooled to lower temperatures (31–32 °C) during CPR or very early following ROSC (within 0–5 min).33 We note that both the depth and speed of hypothermic cooling have been studied, with varying results depending on the model and context. Clinically, good survival has been seen in humans with accidental rapid deep hypothermia, as occurs with accidental ice water submersion.34 While a benefit of deep hypothermia has been demonstrated in animals after traumatic arrest,35 other models have demonstrated detrimental effects from deep, compared to moderate/mild hypothermia.36 Recent meta analyses from cardiac surgery37 and human trial data suggest the best outcomes come from moderate hypothermia when using extracorporeal support.4, 5, 6 These models involve pre-cooling prior to arrest,37 cooling during cardiogenic shock,4 and cooling soon after arrest.5

The type of deep brief, rapid, and early hypothermia treatment that informs preclinical studies remains an alluring, but logistically difficult opportunity for translational science due to the multiple transfers of clinical care (from prehospital to emergency department to ICU) and because of the need to stabilize hemodynamics before initiating TH. Currently, TH is largely implemented by surface or intravascular cooling within hours of hospital arrival. The average decrease in temperature using existing intravascular devices or a water-circulating blanket is approximately 0.4–2 °C per hour, 7, 38, 39, 40 which may not be fast enough to impart the potential benefits suggested by pre-clinical studies.

Limitations

The mild cooling effects of ECMO in control animals treated with room temperature reperfusion may have reduced the overall injury observed in these animals. However, such an effect would tend to bias results towards the null hypothesis of no difference between groups. Given our findings of significant differences, we feel this limitation did not impact the validity of the findings. Secondly, RHR animals received a statistically significant increase in defibrillations required to achieve ROSC. This is likely due to the pro-arrhythmic effects of hypothermia. It is unclear how this may have affected our results. We did not evaluate functional outcomes nor the possibility that cold reperfusion simply delayed MRI findings beyond the present observation window. Further, we note that the model was not designed to assess neurologic outcomes. Finally, we note that cooling via ECMO is an invasive method to achieve hypothermia. Less invasive methods of rapid cooling include esophageal cooling,41 nasal cooling,42 and also total liquid ventilation,43 though this later approach is also quite invasive. The use of ECMO to achieve rapid hypothermia is most appropriate in the context of refractory arrest or hemodynamic instability, but is only one of other plausible cooling approaches for patients who achieve ROSC and are hemodynamically stable.

Conclusions

We have successfully demonstrated a stable model of swine cardiac arrest using controlled induction of VF, fixed duration arrest, rapid controlled reperfusion to normal hemodynamic targets, rapid induction of hypothermia using the extracorporeal circuit, and controlled post-resuscitation physiology.

These findings suggest that rapid induction of hypothermia when controlling for hemodynamics using ECMO-facilitated cooling and reperfusion should be studied as a treatment worth investigating in human cardiac arrest. We note our study is limited by the lack of clinical outcomes data, relying instead on an early imaging biomarker with good predictive value for ultimate neurologic outcome, and while the sample size is too small to support definitive conclusions, these preliminary data support the study of ECMO-mediated hypothermia during cardiac arrest resuscitation as a promising experimental design for future studies.
