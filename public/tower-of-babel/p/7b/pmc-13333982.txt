Computational Fluid Dynamics Simulations to Inform Cancer Therapeutics
Authors: Emilie Roncali, Amirtahà Taebi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer therapies such as chemotherapy, radiopharmaceutical therapy, and transarterial embolization rely on effective drug or radiation delivery through the bloodstream. Understanding how various drugs and particles, which form and size span multiple scales, are transported through blood and tissue is essential for optimizing treatment. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) is a powerful tool to simulate blood flow and drug transport, solving flow governing equations under biologically realistic conditions. This review explores CFD applications in cancer therapy, focusing on transarterial embolization, tumor perfusion, and organ-on-a-chip systems. In radioembolization, CFD can predict microsphere transport and dose distribution to spare vital functions. Tumor perfusion modeling and organ-on-a-chip systems benefit from CFD by replicating vascular dynamics and drug dispersion. Despite its versatility and established mechanical principles, CFD faces challenges, including the need for patient-specific data, computational demands, and multiscale modeling. This review highlights opportunities for integrating CFD with imaging modalities and artificial intelligence tools to overcome these barriers and advance personalized cancer treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Background

Cancer therapeutics such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, radiopharmaceutical therapy, or embolization techniques inherently involve drug delivery via blood (1–5). Delivery can take many forms: venous, arterial, systemic, loco-regional, or intratumoral, thus involving different mechanisms of transport in blood and vectors (e.g., molecules, particles, vesicles, micelles) (6, 7). Delivery and transport in blood and tissue are essential determinants of therapeutic efficacy and toxicity, thus requiring thorough understanding of underlying mechanisms, careful treatment planning, and controlled environmental conditions. Studying and optimizing transport and delivery are of great interest to both academia and pharmaceutical companies, which need precise and accurate tools to do so. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) is one such discipline that can inform about drug delivery, hemodynamics, and drug transport (8).

CFD applied to biofluids simulates how fluids such as blood or lymph interact with biological structures by solving the fundamental Navier–Stokes equations incorporating biological constraints and boundary conditions. This review focuses mainly on blood and particle transport, thus discussing characteristics such as complex geometries derived from medical imaging, and interactions between fluids and solid structures relevant to cancer therapeutics. CFD can be applied at multiple scales, modeling diffusion of nanoparticles or transport of larger particles in the micrometer range such as those used in embolization techniques. Select applications discussed in this review include vascular embolization for cancer treatment, tumor perfusion, and organ-on-a-chip (OoC) systems built on microfluidic devices. We contrast CFD methods applied to these different applications.

CFD for Transarterial Embolization

Transarterial embolization involves injecting microspheres, particles, or emulsions into the bloodstream via catheter to block tumor blood flow or to deliver targeted therapies—either localized radiation (radioembolization) or chemotherapy (chemoembolization). Its effectiveness and safety depend on several factors: vascular anatomy, choice of particles, injection location, organ and tumor perfusion, and the presence of venous shunting in the case of liver radioembolization (1, 2, 5). Two types of radioactive microspheres are available: yttrium-90 (90Y) microspheres, which is approved for use in the United States, and holmium-166 (166Ho), which is approved for use in Europe (2, 9). In radioembolization, planning is typically tailored to each patient through imaging and estimations of the delivered radioactive dose. To improve treatment planning precision, several research groups have explored multiphysics modeling to predict the dose distribution by combining CFD simulations and radiation transport to model the microsphere transport in blood and radioactive processes (10–15).

While radioembolization is primarily used for liver tumors and represents more than 10,000 annual interventions in the United States, there has been an effort to expand its use to brain cancer embolization, which is currently evaluated through a clinical trial for recurrent glioblastoma (GBM) (16, 17). With the imperative need to avoid eloquent matter regions responsible for cognitive functions, brain radioembolization must be precisely planned on the basis of the predicted dose distribution in both these critical regions and the tumor. Imaging the vasculature in the brain can be done with contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), allowing researchers to create a representation of the vascular tree to carry out CFD simulations of the microsphere transport to optimize the microsphere injection location and dosage.

Other CFD applications for embolization include prostate artery embolization for prostate hyperplasia and splenic artery embolization (18–20). Ideally, treatment planning must be personalized for each patient. However, personalized and patient-specific simulations (e.g., informed by imaging) carry stringent requirements such as the need for patient-specific boundary conditions and anatomy extracted from patient images that remain challenging to implement, creating research opportunities for multiscale and multiphysics modeling, including digital twins (20, 21). Important challenges and opportunities are discussed in this review.

Tumor Perfusion and Organ-on-a-Chip Models

Tumor perfusion and vascularization play an important role in delivering the drug to the tumor vicinity and ultimately penetrating the tumor microenvironment and stroma to reach tumor cells. The tumor microenvironment is highly complex, heterogeneous, and dynamic, shaped by vascular architecture, angiogenic activity, interstitial pressure, and immune cell interactions. Itis also an important contributor to metastasis, promoting tumor cell migration and intravasation into systemic circulation (22). OoC models based on microfluidics allow researchers to study these processes in vitro by replicating the biomechanical and biochemical conditions of the tumor vasculature, including shear stresses and vessel permeability (23). Combined with CFD, these approaches allow for precise simulations of drug transport, particle dispersion, and fluid exchange within tumor tissues, building high-fidelity in silico models. CFD enables predictive modeling of drug delivery under various physiological conditions, helping to optimize injection protocols, particle size, and drug formulations for enhanced penetration and therapeutic efficacy in solid tumors. Some of these aspects can be studied, informed, and ultimately optimized with CFD (24–26).

Challenges and Opportunities in CFD for Cancer Therapy

CFD methods used to study these phenomena differ vastly and thus present different challenges. Three main aspects are identified and discussed in this review: (a) patient-specific CFD models and input data to personalize these models that are not always accessible in patient care, (b) the trade-off between high-fidelity CFD and computational burden (data volume and computation time), and (c) simulations across multiple scales (spatial and temporal) and how to bridge the gap between these scales (e.g., how to simulate nanoparticle transport in a complete human patient for digital twins, or phenomena spanning nanoseconds to days). This review article first introduces basic CFD principles (Section 2) before discussing several applications including drug delivery, cancer embolization treatments, tumor perfusion, image-guided CFD, and artificial intelligence (AI) solutions applied to CFD (Section 3). Finally, the discussion summarizes key challenges and future directions to address these challenges (Section 4).

BASIC PRINCIPLES OF BIOFLUID DYNAMICS AND CFD

CFD Simulation Pipeline

A patient-specific CFD workflow typically begins with defining the computational domain. The geometry of organs and tissues of interest, including the patient’s vascular anatomy, is reconstructed based on 3D medical images such as CT, MRI, angiography, and cone-beam CT (CBCT) scans. Particularly, vessel surfaces are often segmented using algorithms such as marching cubes and cross-sectional segmentation (14, 18). For example, segmentation can be done within a confined volume of interest in grayscale images via pixel intensity thresholding to extract isosurfaces at a specific gray level. To define vessels, continuous structures must be created by finding and retaining large-connected regions and removing smaller isolated regions in the segmented image. Finally, smoothing (e.g., with Taubin filters) is applied to create 3D vascular structures. Alternatively, some studies used segmented centerlines and radius measures to reconstruct tubular models to ensure a watertight model (no holes, gaps, or nonmanifold regions) suitable for meshing (14).

Obtaining unstructured tetrahedral meshes from segmented medical images is common for complex arterial trees and is often done with prism layers to capture steep near-wall gradients without incurring prohibitive element counts (11, 14, 18). For example, Taebi et al. (14) generated a tetrahedral mesh of the hepatic artery with three prism layers (thickness ratio of 0.8) to resolve shear layers. While structured hexahedral meshes can dramatically reduce solve time, offering up to 25× speedups, their generation in tortuous, branching vasculature remains largely impractical, driving exploration of hex-dominant meshes instead, where a small number of tetrahedral elements is allowed, or dividing the mesh domain into several simpler subdomains (27). Mesh convergence evaluations are essential, as prior studies found that coarsening the grid cuts central processing unit time by approximately 45% with less than a 1.5% error in tumor dose (11, 27).

Once a high-quality mesh is obtained, boundary and initial conditions are applied. Finally, governing equations such as the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations are solved to capture pulsatile hemodynamics in multiple cardiac cycles. Multiple cardiac cycles are simulated to eliminate transient start-up effects and ensure that the hemodynamic solution reaches a physiologically periodic state. After simulation, flow fields (velocity, pressure) and, crucially for drug delivery applications, Lagrangian particle trajectories and deposition patterns are analyzed. All steps, including segmentation, meshing, solving, and analysis, should be verified (e.g., mesh independence, solver validation) to ensure accuracy.

Patient-Specific Model Inputs

The fidelity of CFD simulations in cancer therapeutics hinges on the accurate incorporation of patient-specific data. This section outlines the essential components and considerations for developing individualized models that can inform and optimize treatment strategies.

High-resolution imaging modalities, such as CT angiography, CBCT, and MRI, are foundational for capturing patient-specific vascular structures and tumor boundaries. Contrast-enhanced imaging enhances the visualization of arterial networks, which is particularly beneficial in procedures such as radioembolization. Segmentation tools such as ITK-SNAP and Mimics facilitate the extraction of vascular geometries, while software such as MeVis and 3D Slicer assist in liver and tumor volumetry (28). For comprehensive vascular mapping, multiscale imaging approaches, combining macroscale MRI with micro-CT, can capture both large vessels and tumor microvasculature (29).

Accurate boundary conditions are critical for realistic CFD simulations (30). Inflow conditions are ideally derived from patient-specific measurements using Doppler ultrasound or MRI-based perfusion imaging (31). However, in the absence of direct measurements, literature-based wave-forms scaled to the patient’s cardiac output are utilized in most current studies. Outflow conditions often employ outlet pressure, flow rates, or lumped-parameter models, such as the three-element Windkessel model, also known as the RCR model, with parameters tuned to match patient-specific blood pressure and flow distribution layers (14). In hepatic models, dual inflow from the hepatic artery and portal vein may be considered, especially in advanced simulations. Transient, pulsatile flow conditions are particularly important when modeling catheter-based interventions, as they can perturb flow and significantly influence particle trajectories (32).

Modeling the transport and deposition of therapeutic agents requires detailed characterization of the drug carriers. For instance, in radioembolization, 90Y microspheres, typically 20–60 μm in diameter, are modeled as rigid spheres with known densities. Key injection parameters, including catheter tip location, orientation, injection rate, and duration, are critical inputs, as they significantly influence particle dispersion and targeting efficacy (19, 33–36). For nanoparticle-based chemotherapies (~100 nm), additional properties such as surface charge, shape, and ligand– receptor interactions may be incorporated into the models to simulate adhesion and uptake mechanisms.

Governing Equations

In CFD simulations pertinent to cancer therapeutics, the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations serve as the foundational framework for modeling blood flow within vascular networks. Given the laminar nature of blood flow in large arteries, such as the hepatic artery where Reynolds numbers typically remain below 900–1,000, blood is often approximated as a Newtonian fluid with standard properties of approximately 1,060 kg/m3 density and 0.004 Pa·s viscosity (10, 37). This simplification facilitates tractable numerical solutions using finite elements or finite volume methods. However, in smaller arterioles or regions (d < 0.3 mm) with recirculatory or disturbed flow, non-Newtonian models, such as Carreau, Casson, or Quemada, are employed to capture shear-thinning behavior more accurately, albeit with increased computational cost (37, 38). To enhance numerical stability and accuracy, especially in advection-dominated flows or when employing equal-order interpolation for velocity and pressure fields, stabilization techniques such as the streamline upwind Petrov–Galerkin method are implemented (14). Time integration within these simulations is typically handled using implicit schemes, such as the generalized-α method or backward differentiation formulas, which allow for larger time steps while maintaining stability and accuracy. These methods are particularly advantageous in capturing the transient behavior of pulsatile blood flow and the dynamic interactions between blood and therapeutic agents. Vessel walls are typically assumed to be rigid and impermeable with a no-slip condition for simplicity, although fluid–structure interaction models, which simultaneously solve the motion of the blood together with the deformation of the vessel walls, are available to account for wall compliance, which may be significant in certain vascular regions (38).

For modeling the transport of therapeutic agents, two primary approaches are employed: the Eulerian and Lagrangian frameworks (39, 40). The Eulerian approach treats the drug as a continuous scalar field, solving advection–diffusion–reaction equations to predict concentration distributions, which is suitable for simulating the dispersion of small molecules or nanoparticles (<100 nm) through the vasculature. In contrast, the Lagrangian approach tracks individual particles, such as microspheres used in radioembolization, by solving Newton’s second law to account for forces such as drag, gravity, and buoyancy (32, 38). This method provides detailed insights into particle trajectories and deposition patterns, essential for optimizing targeted delivery.

In simulations that extend beyond the vascular compartment to include drug extravasation into the tumor interstitium, Darcy’s law is often employed to model the porous media flow of interstitial fluid (41–43). This multiscale coupling becomes important when investigating how interstitial pressure gradients influence therapeutic agent transport, especially in poorly perfused tumors where convection dominates over diffusion (44, 45). However, such models place additional demands on the quality and resolution of anatomical and physiological input data and remain less common than their intravascular counterparts due to complexity and limited availability of patient-specific interstitial parameters. These parameters include interstitial porosity and hydraulic conductivity, which are necessary when modeling drug transport within tumor tissues (41). Simplified assumptions, such as uniform perfusion rates, are sometimes adopted for computational efficiency, with parameters tuned to align with observed drug uptake patterns (28).

Multiscale Modeling and Particle–Fluid Interactions

Intravascular drug delivery for cancer treatment inherently spans multiple spatial and temporal scales, necessitating multiscale modeling approaches to accurately capture the complex interplay between macroscopic blood flow and microscopic particle dynamics. At the macroscopic level, 3D CFD simulations are employed to resolve blood flow within large arterial networks, providing detailed insights into hemodynamic parameters such as velocity profiles and wall shear stresses. To account for the microcirculation, which cannot be fully resolved due to computational constraints, lumped parameter models, porous media representations, or perfusion models are integrated at the outlets of the 3D CFD domain (15, 46). This coupling allows for the incorporation of microvascular resistance and compliance, effectively bridging the gap between large-scale arterial flow and capillary-level perfusion.

A key component of these simulations is the modeling of therapeutic carriers, such as microspheres and nanoparticles, as discrete Lagrangian particles (38, 47). After solving the fluid velocity field, particles are injected and tracked by integrating Newton’s equations of motion. The accuracy of this approach depends on specifying accurate particle size, density, and drag coefficients. For example, 90Y glass microspheres (~30 μm) used in liver radioembolization are approximately 70–90 times denser than blood, following flow streamlines with slight settling due to gravity (15). The drag force is typically computed using Reynolds number–based correlations (e.g., Stokes drag for Reynolds number <1), while additional forces, such as buoyancy, Brownian motion, virtual mass, and Basset force history, are considered depending on particle size and flow regime. Brownian motion, for instance, becomes significant for nanoparticles approximately 100 nm in size and is modeled using random-walk formulations, whereas lift forces (e.g., Saffman lift) may be included to capture lateral migration of particles in shear flow. Some studies also introduce empirical diffusion terms to account for unresolved interactions with red blood cells or turbulent fluctuations (48).

Blood is not a simple fluid; its cellular components induce additional transport phenomena (28). For instance, red blood cells and platelets cause margination of particles toward vessel walls, enhancing the probability of adhesion and uptake (48, 49). While explicitly modeling red blood cell–particle interactions using immersed boundary CFD (where cell membranes are represented within a fluid grid to capture their deformation and motion) or dissipative particle dynamics (a particle-based method that simulates fluid and cell interaction at the mesoscale) can provide high-resolution insight, such methods are computationally prohibitive for large-scale, patient-specific simulations. As a result, most models avoid resolving individual cells and instead incorporate empirical models of margination, such as cross-stream drift velocities or shear-dependent diffusion coefficients. Emerging multiscale frameworks suggest that combining high-fidelity microscale simulations with macroscale CFD can improve boundary condition formulations and particle adhesion models, although fully two-way coupled simulations in realistic vascular geometries remain rare.

Some studies suggested the effectiveness of truncated and hybrid models to reduce the computational costs (35, 50), such as a 1D network model of an arterial tree (faster to solve) coupled to a key 3D region around the injection site or tumor. This preserves large-scale hemodynamics while focusing computational effort on the critical region. In multiscale models of tumors specifically, macrovascular blood flow supplies are linked to tumor capillary networks, which can be represented as porous media or 1D vascular networks (51, 52). Perfusion imaging and histopatho-logical data can also provide estimates of tumor vascular density and permeability to improve model fidelity.

Likewise, Lagrangian particle injection can be hybridized: for example, it can simulate only a representative injection sample, then statistically map it to the full set of injected particles. At the distal ends of the vasculature, particle fate must be defined by boundary interaction models. In liver radioembolization, for example, microspheres are assumed to microembolize and remain at the terminal branches of the hepatic artery (53); simulations often assign particles to outlet branches proportionally to tumor burden (35, 46) or local flow rates, particularly at higher drug injection rates (34, 54). This is partly because a higher injection velocity, provided it is not excessively greater than the blood velocity, increases the mixing of drug particles with blood, leading to a drug distribution at the arterial outlets that closely mirrors the blood flow distribution (33, 34). Some approaches deposit dose along the particle’s trajectory through the network, reflecting microsphere behavior in partially occlusive flows. In other contexts, boundary interactions are more complex. For targeted delivery, particles may adhere to the endothelium via receptor–ligand interactions, which can be modeled using kinetic binding schemes if molecular data are available (55). Magnetic nanoparticles can also be retained by applying external magnetic fields, and models may incorporate capture probabilities based on local field strength and vessel orientation (40, 56, 57).

Tumor microenvironments present additional complexities due to their heterogeneous and often aberrant vasculature. To model drug delivery in such settings, multiscale frameworks incorporate both macroscale perfusion data (from perfusion MRI/CT) and microvascular imaging (e.g., ex vivo confocal or micro-CT) to inform parameters such as porosity and hydraulic conductivity within porous tissue models (29, 41, 52, 58). Particle delivery into tumor interstitium might be captured by adding extravasation terms at the end of the vascular circuit.

At high particle concentrations, such as during embolic therapy, particle–particle interactions become significant. These include collisions, aggregation, and potential clogging, which are not captured in standard one-way coupled models. Some studies have used coupled CFD–discrete element methods to simulate collisions, although these approaches are still relatively rare. For most drug delivery applications involving dilute suspensions, particle–particle interactions are neglected, and flow is assumed to be unaffected by the particles. Overall, multiscale modeling provides a powerful framework for investigating and optimizing intravascular cancer therapies. By incorporating particle dynamics, complex flow environments, and tumor-specific features across scales, these models enable deeper insight into delivery efficiency and drug or particle distribution.

Validation of Multiscale Models

Standard practice is to validate the CFD flow solver separately using benchmark cases or in vitro phantoms. For patient-specific models, validation data are scarce. Some studies compare simulated flow splits or pressure drops to imaging data, for example, 4D flow MRI or Doppler ultrasound. For instance, a scaled bench model of a hepatic arterial tree was used to validate microsphere trajectory predictions (12). Invasive measurements of flow during catheter procedures are also sometimes used.

Particle transport validation is key for drug delivery models and may be done through ex vivo bench tests or in vivo imaging. Benchtop flow phantoms (with transparent casts of patient arteries) allow the injection of tracer particles and imaging of their paths; CFD can be run on the same geometry for comparison. For liver radioembolization, posttreatment 90Y positron emission tomography (PET) provides a 3D map of microsphere deposition. A proof-of-concept study by Roncali et al. (10) qualitatively compared patient-specific CFD predictions to 90Y PET/CT and found a consistent activity distribution between the simulated and measured results. Ultimately, models aim at predicting the delivered radiation dose, which means that verification requires clinical outcomes or imaging. After radioembolization, follow-up MRI or PET done at 3 months can show tumor response. Quantitative dose validation is challenging due to image resolution, registration errors, and motion during imaging.

On the other hand, model validation must account for uncertainties in inputs, such as geometry, boundary conditions, and patient physiology. Some groups have performed sensitivity studies, for example, varying inflow rate, viscosity, or catheter position to see the effect on outputs (11, 15). Bomberna et al. (11) showed that a “steady,” that is, nonpulsatile, assumption could cause up to a 20% error in predicted tumor dose, highlighting the importance of waveform details. These studies help assess confidence in model predictions.

When considering model validation, the main challenge is that true “ground truth” is hard to obtain. In vivo, only indirect or surrogate measurements are available, and patient-specific validation typically involves small cohorts. 3D-printed phantoms and animal studies can help bridge the gap, but translating to human-scale vasculature is nontrivial, due to scale and hemodynamics differences. The multiscale nature means that mismatches at one scale, for example, microvascular assumptions, propagate to outputs. A critical challenge is to jointly validate the entire chain: for instance, to verify that a model that accurately measures hemodynamics also correctly predicts particle delivery.

Evaluation Metrics

Standard CFD outputs such as velocity profiles, wall shear stress, and pressure drop are first-level metrics. In cancer therapy, we often track flow splits (e.g., percentage of hepatic arterial flow to each segment) as an indicator of how microspheres will distribute. However, the primary interest is how many particles (or what fraction of radiation dose) reach tumor tissue. Metrics include particle release maps (visualizations of the inlet plane, rendered in color-coded cross sections, depicting the pathways through which microspheres exit into their respective branches), tumor dose fraction (percent of injected microspheres entering tumor-feeding branches), and nontarget ratio (fraction going to healthy tissue or lung). Taebi et al. (14) reported 22% versus 82% tumor delivery for different injection strategies, illustrating these as key outcomes. Also important is the tumor-to-normal uptake ratio. For nanoparticles, metrics such as mean particle residence time or vascular transit time may be evaluated. Especially for radioembolization, one computes the 3D dose distribution from particle positions (convolving with a dose kernel for 90Y, for example). Metrics include the mean absorbed dose in tumor versus normal liver and coverage metrics (e.g., percentage of tumor volume receiving >X Gy). Comparisons to clinical outcomes are the ultimate goal, yet challenging to achieve.

CFD APPLICATIONS FOR CANCER THERAPY

In this review, we primarily focus on blood transport and particle delivery, although it is worth mentioning other drug delivery systems involving CFD through inhalation or implanted devices, such as the hepatic artery infusion pump chemotherapy system (59, 60). Nanoparticles, liposomes, or microspheres are typically injected or infused locally or systemically. Injections can be loco-regional (transarterial), systemic (intravenous), or intratumoral. In systemic injections, the key point to ensure delivery to tumors is to avoid drug dissociation, which relates to the drug’s binding affinity to the target and can result in free radioisotopes in the case of radiopharmaceuticals. This is better described with pharmacokinetic and biochemistry models of the compound rather than CFD and thus is not the focus of this review. Section 3 focuses on drug delivery, starting with transarterial embolization with microspheres for liver, brain, and prostate cancer (Section 3.1). Section 3.1.4 discusses the role of image guidance for precise delivery. Tumor vascularization and the critical role of tumor microenvironment in facilitating drug delivery are then discussed (Section 3.2.1), leading to the need for lab-on-a-chip (LoC) and OoC devices and the role of CFD in designing and employing these devices (Sections 3.2.3 and 3.2.4).

Transarterial Embolization Techniques

This section discusses transarterial embolization methods for liver, prostate, and brain cancer, focusing on applications of CFD principles.

Liver transarterial embolization.

Liver transarterial embolization is based on the premise that tumor blood flow primarily comes from the hepatic artery, contrary to the rest of the liver that receives 75–80% (61) of its blood supply from the portal vein, allowing the targeting of tumors (61). Transarterial embolization involves microspheres, particles, or emulsions of different sizes to create embolization and to deliver chemotherapy or radiation to the tumors. Transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) can be performed with emulsions or drug-eluting beads (DEB-TACE). DEB-TACE has a greater efficacy and safety profile than conventional TACE thanks to an advantageous slow release of the chemotherapeutic agent into the tumor vasculature and a lower peak plasma concentration (62). Transarterial embolization is performed in interventional radiology in two phases ~2 weeks apart: a day-long workup and a day-long treatment. The workup, useful to build the treatment plan, includes vascular mapping with CBCT. For transarterial radioembolization (TARE), it is accompanied by single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) imaging of 99mTc macroaggregated albumin, another type of particle that is biodegradable, to estimate the fraction of radioactive microspheres that may spill to the lungs through venous shunting and make the patient ineligible for treatment (63). However, limited improvement in overall survival has been reported (64, 65), mostly because transarterial embolization is used only on advanced cancers and is often underdosed, which limits its efficacy. The efficacy of transarterial embolization depends on the vascular anatomy, the particle type, the injection location within the hepatic artery, the liver and tumor blood flow, and the presence of venous shunting. Transarterial bland embolization and TACE employ emulsions or 150–700-μm microspheres to block the tumor blood flow and/or deliver in situ chemotherapy (66, 67). No quantification of venous shunting is done, and a fixed dose of chemotherapy is typically used for all patients. Posttreatment imaging at 2–12 weeks shows that ~70% of tumors treated with TACE have residual blood flow, indicative of incomplete occlusion of the tumor blood supply (68). TARE utilizes ~30-μm microspheres that have only a microembolic effect, so its efficacy is characterized by the radiation dose distribution rather than residual blood flow. Posttreatment dosimetry studies showed that a minimum of 50 Gy is required to shrink tumors and that the effective dose did not reach this threshold in 80% of patients (69, 70), highlighting that tumor targeting is difficult to plan and implement with current imaging techniques and is highly patient-dependent. Its unreliability creates a risk of toxicity and imposes treatment plans based on safety rather than efficacy. If the efficacy and safety profile of transarterial embolization were improved through personalized, robust, and precise planning, it could have a much higher impact on patient outcome (71–73), which motivates the development of new treatment planning approaches based on CFD. Important parameters that have been identified through CFD studies include the optimal distance between the injection location and a bifurcation between two vessels, the catheter distance from the central axis of the vessel, the injection timing with respect to the cardiac cycle (Figure 1), and the catheter angle and position (13, 33, 74, 75).

CFD studies can highlight the differences between embolic microspheres due to their density and size but must be coupled with a second model that describes the microsphere functionalization and their interaction with biological tissue, either radiation physics in the case of 90Y (10) or a diffusion model based on measured drug-release pharmacokinetics for TACE. For example, 90Y is a high-energy electron emitter with a maximum and mean electron range of 11 mm and 2.5 mm in water (76). Doxorubicin with a dosage of 50–75 mg/m2 (100–150 mg per session) is typically used in TACE, followed by an injection of embolic microspheres (77). Doxorubicin-eluding beads used in DEB-TACE are well characterized in vitro and in vivo through measured drug loading capacity (typically 50 mg/mL), diffusion and desorption kinetics, and tissue concentration (78–80), which allows for the beads to be numerically modeled. It can be assumed that the blood flow is close to zero when reaching stasis and that the total chemotherapy is given by the cumulative drug concentration over the eluting period of ~10 days. Diffusion and desorption are spatially limited to a few cells. These considerations are essential for accurately coupling CFD-based flow predictions with drug or radiation transport models, ultimately enabling a more realistic estimation of therapeutic dose distribution and treatment efficacy.

Prostate artery embolization for prostate cancer.

Prostate artery embolization has been widely used to treat benign prostate hyperplasia, related to aging. More recently, its use has been extended to prostate cancer with chemoembolization in the form of DEB-TACE or prostatic artery chemoembolization with a mixture of docetaxel and embolic microspheres (81–83). The prostate receives its blood supply from several feeding arteries down the iliac artery; identifying the correct ones to embolize, including a precise mapping of their tortuosity, anatomy, and size, is critical to plan prostatic artery embolization and the adequate catheter shape and size (18). Optimizing these parameters could be done with a combination of CFD and catheter modeling (geometry, injection profile, release mechanism).

Brain radioembolization.

GBM is often characterized by dense vascularity and poor therapeutic penetration. Its infiltrative nature prevents complete resection and motivates the need for effective secondary therapies (84). Drug passage through the blood–brain barrier also limits the number of options and makes in situ delivery of particles such as rhenium-186 (186Re) nanoliposomes or 90Y microspheres attractive. In both therapies, it is crucial to avoid eloquent matter areas executing critical cognitive functions, and therefore adequate placement of catheters and delivery techniques are required.

Using intracranial catheters, 186Re nanoliposomes are infused by convection-enhanced delivery (CED). Successful delivery of the target radiation dose through 186 Re beta particles that have a range of 2–4 mm relies on predicting the spatiotemporal distribution of the nanoliposomes and selecting the optimal catheter placement accordingly. Woodall et al. (17) have proposed a CFD model of CED for recurrent GBM, which accounts for particle mobility and uses patient-specific magnetic resonance (MR) images (T1- and T2-weighted MRI, diffusion-weighted MRI) acquired pretreatment to compute the apparent diffusion coefficient and other relevant parameters to predict diffusion and nanoliposome distribution. Briefly, the CFD model solves governing equations for the delivery and interstitial transport of 186Re nanoliposomes in a 2D brain computational domain, considering the fluid flow through the brain, pressure loss in capillaries, and transport of 186Re nanoliposomes in brain tissue. The fluid flow through the brain is modeled through Darcy’s law for porous media, and the transport of 186Re nanoliposomes is modeled by an advection–diffusion system through the skull. Importantly, the CFD model was calibrated using SPECT obtained the day of treatment, highlighting the importance of imaging. This proof-of-concept study demonstrated that the CFD model could predict the nanoliposome distribution middelivery, indicating the potential of such models to optimize catheter placements.

90Y brain embolization for recurrent GBM is currently evaluated in the FRONTIER trial (16), a prospective, single-arm, multicenter study. 90Y glass microspheres (TheraSphere) are directly injected into cerebral arteries to deliver high doses of radiation locally with a target of40 Gy, using up to two locations. While no model has been fully developed yet, CFD informed by imaging (e.g., CT or MR angiography) could be a powerful tool to determine the best injection location and injection rate to deliver the highest dose while avoiding eloquent regions (85).

Flow imaging to guide therapy and CFD.

Previous sections highlighted the critical role of imaging to guide therapy and inform CFD models. Beyond the need for anatomical information to define CFD computational domains by segmenting and blood vessels (86), which can be achieved with CT and MRI, imaging modalities that can provide blood flow data in addition to anatomical information are particularly important. Doppler ultrasound, perfusion CT, contrast-enhanced CT, and MRI are among these modalities and have been applied to develop cardiovascular CFD models (31, 87–89). Kissas et al. (87) proposed to use 4D flow MRI to inform a 1D CFD model based on a graph approach combined with a physics-informed network. Using the blood velocity from the 4D flow MR images, they demonstrated the ability to reconstruct the spatiotemporal distribution of the pressure wave in the arterial network, overcoming the inherent noise and limited resolution of 4D flow MRI. This study is an important step toward calibrating boundary conditions, a recurrent challenge in patient-specific CFD simulations, as discussed in previous sections. The presence of tumor also changes the pressure conditions through changes of interstitial pressure, potential occlusion of blood vessels, and leaky vasculature, making the need for patient-specific boundary conditions important for cancer therapeutics studies utilizing CFD (90, 91).

Drug Delivery Systems

This section describes several drug delivery systems, first introducing the importance of considering tumor vascularization when optimizing drug delivery, and then detailing LoC and OoC systems.

The importance of tumor vascularization and microenvironment.

Tumor perfusion and vascularization play an important role in enabling drugs to reach tumor cells by letting them penetrate the tumor microenvironment. Newly formed blood vessels in solid tumors differ from normal vasculature, often lacking basement membrane and pericyte coverage, which renders them structurally abnormal, highly heterogeneous, and leaky, leading to excessive interstitial fluid accumulation (22) (Figure 2). Without a functional lymphatic system, tumors exhibit elevated interstitial fluid pressure, ranging from 10 to 100 mm Hg, which is far higher than the 0–5 mm Hg typical of healthy tissue. As tumors grow, vascular density may decline, creating hypoxic regions that contribute to the development of necrotic cores. These cores also contribute to malignant processes such as angiogenesis and metastasis and are particularly difficult to treat.

Quantifying tumor dynamics remains a major challenge, limiting our understanding of the parameters that govern perfusion, hypoxia, and drug response. Addressing these complexities is crucial for improving therapeutic strategies and designing more effective treatments for solid tumors. As an example, Salavati et al. (7) developed a CFD model to investigate interstitial fluid flow and drug transport in tumors, emphasizing the spatial heterogeneity of biophysical properties such as hydraulic conductivity and vascular permeability. Their parametric approach allowed for systematic exploration of how tumor geometry, necrotic core characteristics, and lymphatic vessel presence influence drug delivery. The study revealed that tumor shape plays a critical role in small tumors: Spherical tumors showed minimal disruption in drug penetration when necrotic cores were present, whereas elliptical tumors experienced significant delay. The effect of necrotic cores was substantial only in small tumors, suggesting that tumor size modulates the impact of internal structural features. Additionally, the presence of functional lymphatic vessels slightly reduced interstitial fluid pressure but did not meaningfully alter drug transport, indicating that lymphatic drainage may not be a dominant factor in therapeutic delivery. Overall, the results underscore the importance of accounting for tumor heterogeneity in modeling efforts (7). The novel parametric CFD strategy introduced by Salavati et al. (7) could be applied to predicting tumor perfusion and optimizing drug delivery for personalized therapy planning. By integrating realistic tumor characteristics with advanced fluid dynamics, this approach could enhance the understanding of the interplay between tumor architecture and treatment efficacy. The next section describes an application of in silico computational modeling of chemotherapy.

In silico modeling of chemotherapeutic transport.

Understanding cancer progression and therapeutic delivery necessitates a system-level view of cellular behavior and its interaction with the host microenvironment, including immune components and tumor vasculature. Chemotherapy drug transport following systemic intravenous injection proceeds through intravascular, transvascular, and interstitial flows. The distribution and uptake of therapeutic agents at the tumor site are governed by pharmacokinetics determined by the host biophysical properties and by pharmacodynamics that characterize drug-induced physiological effects. These mechanisms span multiple scales from molecular dynamics at the atomic level (10−10 m) to the tissue and organ levels (10−3–10−1 m). For micro- and nanoscale drug design, modeling fluid transport and concentration dynamics within vascular networks while accounting for these different scales is critical. In silico frameworks representing solid tumors can incorporate these mechanisms either explicitly through vasculature-resolved models or more generally via growth-centric simulations. Mathematical frameworks simulating angiogenesis facilitate the generation of synthetic vascular networks, within which CFD methods can assess drug delivery efficiency. Computational models provide spatiotemporal multiscale capabilities and access to detailed information that complement or even surpass in vitro and in vivo experimental approaches. These models are mainly applied in cancer research, with clinical translation challenged by the lack of empirical parameters that should be measured in patients to personalize simulations rather than using population-based data. Using in silico frameworks is thus synergistic with experimental platforms such as LoC devices that can provide some of these numerical parameters. The next two sections describe basic concepts of LoC and OoC devices and their application to cancer.

CFD for lab-on-a-chip platforms.

LoC platforms are miniaturized devices integrating multiple laboratory functions at a microscale via precise, small fluid volume manipulations. Microfluidics is a core technology in LoC devices, paired with other sensors and actuators. LoCs are heavily applied to cancer research, for example, to model tumor environments. This may include creating synthetic tissue architectures and incorporating dynamic data such as cellular interactions and transport phenomena to study cancer cell migration, invasion, response to therapies, and oxygen concentration in controlled conditions close to in vivo environments, and to advance knowledge on tumor heterogeneity and metastasis (26). Microfluidic platforms are also applied to high-throughput drug screening and personalized medicine approaches, accelerating the development of targeted cancer treatments. As they involve microchannels in which fluid dynamics must be controlled (Figure 3), their design is often complex, with an inherently iterative process involving multiple costly and time-intensive prototyping cycles (92). To streamline development, accurate and rapid simulation tools are needed for assessing functionality prior to fabrication, which can be achieved with CFD. However, most LoC systems feature complex networks of inter-connected channels, necessitating sophisticated approaches not compatible with simple modeling of Newtonian flows such as the Hagen–Poiseuille law, which assumes a parabolic velocity profile and mainly applies to a single microchannel (for example, see Figure 3c showing sharp bifurcations). Several numerical approaches in CFD, such as the finite volume method, finite element method, and lattice Boltzmann method, are commonly employed to solve the Navier–Stokes equations governing microfluidic flows. Instead of solving continuous equations, the lattice Boltzmann method represents fluid flow as discrete particles moving and colliding on a lattice, which can be done with simplified numerical solving methods. Nonetheless, the computational burden of simulating flow within microfluidics devices with numerous components rapidly becomes prohibitive. Approximations from single straight microchannels may be used to accelerate simulations (93).

It should be added that microfluidic systems exhibit distinct fluid behavior due to the dominance of surface forces over body forces at microscale dimensions. In particular, two-phase flows with free surfaces, such as capillary wicking and droplet formation, are primarily governed by capillary forces, characterized by low Reynolds and Weber numbers (94). Accurately modeling these flows requires the incorporation of surface tension effects (a low Weber number indicates the dominance of surface tension over inertia), for which the volume of fluid method remains a popular method. Another defining feature of microfluidic environments is the greater role of diffusion in mixing phenomena, which cannot be easily simulated with simplified methods, especially in devices with intricate channel networks such as shown in Figure 3 (23).

The practical implementation of CFD for microfluidic applications depends heavily on the availability of robust software tools that can handle these specialized conditions. For example, software packages such as OpenFOAM or COMSOL provide capabilities for simulating these phenomena, with applications such as simulating cancer cell migration (95, 96). CFD platforms now incorporate refined solvers and geometry-aware meshing techniques, but simulating free surface evolution with precision remains computationally demanding. Therefore, new and adapted CFD tools still need to be developed for microfluidics design and integrated with multiphysics models to consider all components of LoCs and OoCs.

CFD for organ-on-a-chip systems.

Analog to LoCs, OoCs are bioengineered microdevices designed to mimic the structure of living organs and reproduce some key physiological and biological functions, with important applications in cancer research (97, 98). These platforms integrate 3D tissue cultures and microfluidic channels that simulate biomechanical forces such as stretch or shear stress, as well as fluid flows. OoCs have evolved into sophisticated systems ranging from single-organ models to multiorgan networks, with sizes ranging from 1 cm to that of a large well plate (99) (Figure 4). Their development has been supported by advances in stem cell technology, genome editing, 3D printing, and sensor engineering. Important features include 3D tissue architecture, incorporation of diverse cell types (e.g., vascular, immune), and real-time simulation of fluid flow, which can be achieved with microfluidics. The microfluidic channel system provides nutrients and mechanical stimuli such as shear forces to simulate vascular flow. Precise modeling of flow dynamics is critical, as channel geometry (including diameter, corners, and port layout) affects performance of the OoC. OoCs’ advanced features can also be designed with CFD, including fluid–structure interactions (100, 101).

Once OoCs are built and execute physiological functions, monitoring them is essential for advancing their physiological accuracy and expanding their biomedical applications. Real-time tracking of metabolic and pathophysiologic processes helps refine chip design to better mimic in vivo conditions, as well as inform about the OoC itself. Innovations such as on-chip PET imaging can enable functional visualization of cellular activity (102). Additionally, patient-derived OoCs are a powerful tool for personalized drug screening to identify the most effective treatment for individual patients based on their biological response (60).

Accelerating CFD with Artificial Intelligence

While traditional CFD can offer high accuracy to simulate particle transport in blood or microscale fluid flows such as in microfluidics platforms, its increasing computational intensity poses challenges for real-time clinical use, especially when rapid decision-making is required and needs to be personalized to the patient (hence involving a realistic and complex vascular anatomy).

To overcome these limitations, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) have emerged as powerful tools for accelerating simulations (103, 104). PINNs embed physical laws directly into neural network architectures, allowing them to learn solutions to complex fluid dynamics problems while preserving physical consistency (87, 105, 106). These AI models act as solvers of partial differential equations and dramatically reduce computation time and enable fast sampling across a wide range of flow conditions, making them promising for real-time clinical decision support. The key idea is to treat the problem as a function approximation task rather than a transport task, as is done in traditional CFD where the fluid flow is transported through a mesh. That allows PINNs to work with sparse data, which are often found in clinical applications. PINNs can be extended to generative networks including informed generative adversarial networks, diffusion models, and transformer-based surrogates (103, 104). Their ability to generalize across patient-specific geometries and boundary conditions makes them especially valuable for developing theranostic digital twins (107). Briefly, a digital twin is an operator described by mathematical or computational models (e.g., partial differential equations, Monte Carlo simulations, computational fluid dynamics) that are personalized via specific input data. In health care, digital twins are biomedical models informed with patient data. For an in-depth review of digital twins and associated AI solutions, we refer readers to a recently published perspective article by Kumar et al. (107) discussing specific applications of digital twins for radioembolization. Beyond cancer research, PINNs have broad applicability in biomedical CFD, including cardiovascular modeling, respiratory flow analysis, and tumor perfusion studies (108, 109).

DISCUSSION AND FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS

In this review, we discussed the application of CFD to several aspects of cancer therapeutics, highlighting the differences between phenomena to be modeled and difficulties specific to each application. LoC platforms primarily use CFD to design and optimize microfluidics, requiring high-fidelity multiscale models including fluid–structure interactions that engender a significant computational burden. In a different domain, CFD is increasingly used for cancer patient–specific pretreatment and treatment applications with the ultimate goal of clinical translation. With the emergence of theranostic digital twins (107), this is perhaps where the greatest challenges and opportunities can be identified. Despite increasingly automated pipelines, segmentation and meshing of complex anatomies as encountered in patients (often containing hundreds of outlets and submillimeter branches) remain labor-intensive, and trade-offs between mesh resolution and computational tractability continue to challenge rapid digital twin workflows. Balancing fidelity against run time while ensuring reproducibility and robustness across varied patient geometries defines the current frontier to advance CFD-informed cancer therapeutics.

Acquiring accurate patient-specific data required to personalize CFD simulations remains another significant issue. Noninvasive measurements of inflow and outflow conditions are often unavailable, necessitating reliance on generalized physiological data or indirect estimations through systemic 0D and 1D models for example. The heterogeneity of tumor vasculature introduces additional uncertainty and further reinforces the need for defining patient-specific boundary conditions. Modeling the conditions of catheter-based infusions is complex, as minor deviations in catheter positioning can substantially alter particle distribution, yet exact localization and optimal orientation are difficult to implement in clinical practice. Furthermore, interpatient variability in blood rheology, such as differences in hematocrit levels, is seldom accounted for but may influence particle transport dynamics. As discussed in Sections 2 and 3, the presence of tumors and their abnormal vasculature also modifies hemodynamics in an uncontrolled manner that is highly patient-specific and makes precise modeling for each patient harder to realize.

All these sources of uncertainties and growth of personalized CFD simulations create challenges in multiscale modeling, particularly concerning computational demands and the need for high-quality, patient-specific data. For example, the integration of fluid–structure interaction models that account for vessel wall compliance has shown promise in enhancing the accuracy of simulations, although at the cost of increased computational complexity (110). Simplification strategies, such as geometry truncation and steady-flow assumptions, have been explored to reduce computational load without significantly compromising accuracy (11). However, these simplifications must be carefully validated to ensure their applicability across different clinical scenarios. Therefore, developing CFD-based cancer therapies requires navigating the challenge of tailoring models to individual patients while keeping computational burden manageable.

A promising solution to address computational demands, presented in Section 3.3, is the use of physics-informed networks that combine the speed of AI with a lower need for training data than traditional neural networks, thanks to the physics constraints (106). These networks are of particular interest for CFD applications, since CFD simulations are too slow to generate large datasets with high fidelity. Finally, two important challenges in translating CFD to clinical use concern regulatory considerations, especially in the context of digital twins (112), and visualization tools (real-time display of distributions, potentially using augmented reality). In this review, we stressed the need for patient-specific simulations, which require high-fidelity CFD models informed by patient images and data, potentially collected across multiple institutions to train neural networks. Inevitably, the use of patient data poses a risk to patient confidentiality and privacy and faces barriers from regulatory institutions, which greatly differ between countries. As for visualization, clinical translation of patient-specific CFD applications for treatment planning will require making tools accessible to a broad community beyond engineers, such as physicians. This could be done by leveraging virtual and augmented reality, which are increasingly used in clinical environments from surgery training to real-time procedures. The ability to manipulate 3D flow distributions, optimizing treatment in real time (e.g., in the case of radioembolization moving the injection location within the arterial tree and dynamically computing and displaying the new microsphere distribution), will become a necessity.

In conclusion, CFD applications in cancer therapy are increasing, benefiting from computational advances while creating new opportunities for research, making CFD and AI-guided CFD an exciting field for researchers.
