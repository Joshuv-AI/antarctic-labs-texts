Heart rate and heart rate variability responses during three exercise tests and recovery in horses participating in the Fédération Équestre Internationale Eventing World Challenge
Authors: Thita Wonghanchao, Kanokpan Sanigavatee, Piyamaporn Pongnarudech, Temhuajai Suchairat, Supatchaya Jitsopin, Boonbaramee Wanichayanon, Dalad Rattanakarn, Pongkan Jantakanangkoon, Tharaporn Jaraswutiwong, Nattanit Kalanuson, Napat Simasurapoj, Wanwalee Srisujja, Salinthip Tippayaratsoontorn, Pannawat Kranpan, Ponlakrit Charoenchanikran, Chanoknun Poochipakorn, Metha Chanda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHorses competing in eventing competitions encounter significant challenges that increase their risk of severe injuries and metabolic disorders. Numerous studies have focused on the risk of such injuries and disorders during the cross-country segment. While one study has examined autonomic responses during this segment, research on these responses across all three phases of competition, particularly during one-day events, is still limited. The objective of this study was to analyse the autonomic responses of horses by measuring heart rate (HR) and heart rate variability (HRV) during three exercise tests and their recovery while competing in the Fédération Équestre Internationale Eventing World Challenge. HR and HRV were monitored in 10 horses during the competition; however, data were collected from only nine horses, as one was eliminated.ResultsBeat-to-beat (RR) interval decreased from control (1742 ± 202.3 ms) during dressage (D), jumping (J) and cross-country (CC) tests, lasting 60 min after cross-country (60-CC) (D: 594.6 ± 40.2 ms, J: 420.6 ± 94.5 ms, CC: 358.0 ± 66.9 ms and 60-CC: 1424.2 ± 98.3 ms vs. control: 1742.0 ± 202.3 ms, p < 0.05–0.001). HR increased during the exercise tests, extending to 60-CC (D: 101.4 ± 7.0 beats/min, J: 148.2 ± 30.5 beats/min, CC: 172.0 ± 27.0 beats/min and 60-CC: 42.2 ± 3.0 beats/min vs. control: 34.8 ± 3.7 beats/min, p < 0.05–0.001). Various HRV metrics were lower during J than D, including standard deviation of normal-to-normal RR interval (D: 25.4 ± 9.1 ms vs. J: 9.8 ± 6.2 ms, p < 0.01), total power band (D: 512.4 ± 261 ms2 vs. J: 126.8 ± 180.4 ms2, p < 0.05) and standard deviation 2 (D: 35.0 ± 12.5 ms vs. J: 13.4 ± 8.9 ms, p < 0.01). Changes in the parasympathetic and sympathetic nervous system indices corresponded with RR interval and HR modifications, respectively.ConclusionsThe study found that autonomic responses were significantly pronounced during three exercise tests, with notably stronger reactions observed during the jumping and cross-country segments of the Eventing World Challenge. This preliminary study provides valuable insight into the autonomic regulation of horses during these phases of one-day eventing competitions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05090-3.Keywords: Eventing, Heart rate variability, Horse, One-day event, Recovery, Welfare

Introduction

Eventing is a demanding equestrian discipline that includes three main phases, namely dressage, show jumping, and cross-country. The cross-country segment, in particular, involves varied terrains, obstacles, and footing, making it a key focus of evaluation. The objective is to complete the course during the competition [1, 2]. The sport’s high cardiovascular and metabolic demands can lead to several adverse effects, such as fatigue and musculoskeletal injuries. These injuries may cause high-level horses to withdraw from competition [3, 4]. Notably, eventing horses exhibit the highest reported rate of sudden death among competitive sport and riding horses, primarily due to cardiovascular failure, which often occurs during the cross-country phase [5]. As a result, eventing is considered one of the most dangerous modern equestrian sports, placing considerable stress on horses during competition. If horses with low fitness levels are entered into these events, their risk of injuries or fatalities could be increased, compromising their welfare.

According to the regulations set by the Fédération Équestre Internationale (FEI), eventing competitions are categorised based on format (short and long formats) and difficulty level. The difficulty level is defined using a progressive star system that ranges from one star, indicating the lowest level, to five stars, indicating the highest level [6, 7]. Recently, the FEI proposed the short format of the FEI Eventing World Challenge, aiming to provide opportunities for athletes who may be unable to participate in international competitions due to various factors, including travel costs and distances. This competition is regarded as a bridge between the national and international levels of competition, enabling athletes to compete internationally without leaving their own country (https://inside.fei.org/fei/disc/eventing). The one-day event format may cause horses to experience mental and physiological stress during the three competition phases. Unfortunately, the stress responses of horses participating in these three exercise tests during a one-day event have yet to be reported. This information may help manage and prepare horses for satisfactory performance while preserving their welfare during such events.

Stress in horses can be assessed through their hormonal and physiological responses [8, 9]. One key measure is heart rate variability (HRV), which refers to the fluctuations in the time intervals between heartbeats. HRV has been used to indicate stress responses in horses experiencing cardiac diseases [10, 11] and pain [12, 13], as well as during road transport [14, 15], training, and competitions [8, 16–20]. The modulation of HRV results from the interplay between the sympathetic and parasympathetic (vagal) impulses acting on the sinoatrial node of the heart [21, 22]. This mechanism enhances the flexibility and adaptability of biological systems, helping maintain homeostasis during external challenges [23, 24]. Various HRV metrics are used to represent sympathetic and vagal activities. Key indicators include the beat-to-beat (RR) interval, the standard deviation of normal-to-normal RR intervals (SDNN), the low-frequency (LF) band, and the standard deviation in the Poincaré plot along the line of identity (SD2), all of which reflect long-term variations influenced by both sympathetic and vagal activities [22, 25]. Conversely, the square root of the mean squared differences between successive RR intervals (RMSSD), the high-frequency (HF) band, and the standard deviation in the Poincaré plot perpendicular to the line of identity (SD1) correspond to high-frequency variations, which are indicative of vagal activity [21, 22]. The application of HRV to guide training regimens, assess fatigue, prevent overtraining, and manage emotional anxiety in human athletes has been reported [26]. Research has also shown that horses training for eventing competitions exhibit lower RMSSD, HF band, and SD1 values alongside an elevated LF/HF ratio, suggesting decreased vagal activity and increased sympathetic activity in these animals [20]. Additionally, a recent study reported HRV modulation during the eventing discipline. However, the modulation was mentioned only for the cross-country test [27].

This study aimed to investigate horses’ autonomic responses by monitoring heart rate (HR) and HRV during three exercise tests and their subsequent recovery periods. The participants were horses competing in the FEI Eventing World Challenge Category A. We hypothesised that autonomic responses and recovery times would vary across the three exercise tests, with an expectation that heightened autonomic responses would persist prior to the cross-country test during this one-day event.

Methods

Horses

The study was conducted on 10 healthy eventing horses (two stallions, two mares, and six geldings) aged 12.2 ± 3.0 years and weighing 444.9 ± 38.5 kg. These horses participated in the FEI Eventing World Challenge, held at the Thai Polo and Equestrian Club in Thailand (12°53’25.9"N 101°00’09.2"E) in December 2024. Four horses were sourced from the Royal Horse Guard Riding Club, three from the Thai Polo and Equestrian Club, and one each from the Navamintra Queen Mother Guard Equestrian Club, Chavallino Equestrian Center, and BB Horses Stable. All horses had prior experience in national and international eventing competitions. During the study, the horses were housed in single-stalled boxes measuring approximately 4 × 4 m, equipped with straw bedding and rubber pads. They were fed 2–3 kg of commercial pellets and 60 to 100 g of electrolyte supplements, divided into three meals per day. Additionally, the horses had free access to pergola hay and tap water within their stables. The training protocols varied among the horses, guided by questionnaires, and included flatwork, dressage skill tests, cross-country riding, and jumping two to four times a week. To be included in the study, horses had to meet the following criteria: (1) experience in at least one prior eventing competition, (2) no clinical illness, medical or surgical treatment during the 30 days leading up to the study, and (3) passing a horse inspection to be deemed fit to compete. Horses were excluded if they were eliminated from any exercise test or exhibited health problems at any time during the competition. Unfortunately, one horse was disqualified during the jumping test after accruing more than four obstacles down as outlined in the rules [7]. Therefore, data were collected from the nine horses that completed the event. The experimental protocol was approved by the Thailand Equestrian Federation (TEF) Board of Directors to conduct the research in the designated venue. This study was approved and supported by the FEI Veterinary Committee (Supplementary File 1).

Experimental protocols

The horses were scheduled to participate in three consecutive exercise tests—dressage, jumping, and cross-country—all in one day, in accordance with Category A of the Eventing World Challenge format. The relative humidity and air temperature, measured from 7:00 AM to 9:00 PM, were 60.5 ± 6.5% and 27.6 ± 1.8 °C, according to the data logged by temperature and humidity devices (TM-305U; Tenmars Electronics, Taipei, Taiwan). The competition commenced with the dressage test at the Concours International Combiné one-star (CIC1*) level, starting at 8:30 AM. Following this, the jumping test took place at approximately 11:00 AM. This jumping test featured 11 obstacles and required 13 attempts over a distance of 450 m. The horses jumped over fences with heights ranging from 95 cm to 100 cm at a maximum speed of 350 m/min. Finally, the horses participated in the cross-country test at 3:00 PM. This test involved jumping over 12 obstacles with a total of 14 efforts, covering an 1800 m course at a speed of approximately 500 m/min. The optimal completion time for the cross-country test was 216 s, with a maximum allowable time of 432 s. After completing the cross-country test, the horses were immediately showered with plenty of ice-cold water in the cooling area. They were released to their stables once their HRs dropped to between 60 and 70 bpm, which took about 29–30 min of cooling down. Throughout the three phases of the competition, the horses were fitted with a heart rate monitoring (HRM) device (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland) to record RR interval data for subsequent HRV analysis. The HRM device is an easy-to-use, non-invasive tool validated for HRV analysis in horses [28–31]. To prepare the Polar equine belt for riding, it was soaked in tap water, and ultrasound gel was applied to the electrode surface to improve signal transmission. The belt was then fastened around the horse’s chest, positioning the sensor pocket on the left side of the middle chest. The HR sensor was connected to the Polar sports watch (Vantage V3) to record RR interval data during both the competition and the recovery periods. The HRM device was installed on the horses starting at 6:00 AM (120 min before the dressage test) and remained on for 6 h after the cross-country test. Although watches are not permitted in any category according to the rules [7], the riders participating in this study were granted special permission by the event’s technical delegate to wear watches fully covered by elastic tape to conceal the information on the watch screen during the entire competition (Supplementary File 2).

Data collection and acquisition

The data from the Polar sports watch were subsequently synchronised with the Polar FlowSync program (https://flow.polar.com/), allowing for exportation as a CSV file. The data were then processed using Kubios Premium software (Kubios HRV Scientific, Kuopio, Finland;https://www.kubios.com/hrv-premium/) to derive specific HRV metrics. The resulting HRV data were saved as MATLAB MAT files. Any artefacts present in the RR interval time series, including missing beats, extra beats, misaligned beats, premature ventricular contractions, and other arrhythmias, were automatically corrected by the Kubios Premium software. Given the extensive duration of the RR recordings in this study, segments of noise could arise, potentially skewing consecutive beat detections and compromising the accuracy of the HRV analysis. To mitigate this issue, automatic noise detection was calibrated to a medium setting to exclude these segments from the analysis. Smoothness priors were implemented to eliminate non-stationarities in the RR interval time series, with a specified cutoff frequency of 0.035 Hz. HR and HRV variables were expressed using four analysis methods:

Time domain results: RR intervals, mean HR, peak HR, SDNN, RMSSD, and stress index.

Time domain results: RR intervals, mean HR, peak HR, SDNN, RMSSD, and stress index.

Frequency domain results: LF band, HF band, total power band, and LF/HF ratio.

Frequency domain results: LF band, HF band, total power band, and LF/HF ratio.

Nonlinear results: SD1, SD2, and the SD2/SD1 ratio.

Nonlinear results: SD1, SD2, and the SD2/SD1 ratio.

ANS index: parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) index and sympathetic nervous system (SNS) index.

ANS index: parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) index and sympathetic nervous system (SNS) index.

Data were collected at several key points: 30 min of resting before the warm-up for the dressage test, during the dressage test itself, at 30-min intervals between the dressage and jumping tests for 90 min, throughout the warm-up and jumping test, at 30-min intervals between the jumping and cross-country tests for 240 min, during the warm-up and the cross-country test, and finally, 240 min after the cross-country test.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Due to missing data during the experiment, a mixed-effects model analysis (restricted maximum likelihood) with Greenhouse–Geisser correction was employed to estimate the effect of exercise tests and recovery periods on HR and HRV metrics changes. The activity patterns (exercise tests and recoveries) were considered a fixed effect (type III), whereas horses and residuals were set as random effects. Tukey’s multiple comparison tests were subsequently used to investigate HR and HRV metrics differences across time points throughout the competition period. Data were expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Mean HR, peak HR, and RR interval

The effect of the exercise tests and recovery periods was observed based on modulation in mean HR (p < 0.0001, F (2.426, 13.29) = 78.26), peak HR (p < 0.0001, F (2.924, 16.96) = 34.05), and RR interval (p < 0.0001, F (2.085, 11.42) = 61.72).

In comparison to the control period, which had a baseline RR interval of 1742.0 ± 202.3 ms before the competition, the RR interval significantly decreased (p < 0.01) during the warm-up phase before the dressage test (W-D: 702.7 ± 70.6 ms) and during the dressage test itself (D: 594.6 ± 40.2 ms). The RR interval then returned to baseline levels 30 to 90 min after the dressage test (30-D to 90-D). During the warm-up before the jumping test, the RR interval also declined to 821.2 ± 199.5 ms (W-J, p < 0.05) and dropped further during the jumping test (J: 420.6 ± 94.5 ms, p < 0.05). Although the RR interval at 120 min after the jumping test was significantly lower than the control value (120-J: 1430 ± 169.2 ms, p < 0.05), it gradually returned to baseline levels over 30 to 240 min after the jumping test (30-J to 240-J). The RR interval decreased again during the warm-up before the cross-country test (W-CC: 761.6 ± 159.9 ms, p < 0.01), reaching its lowest value during the cross-country test itself (CC: 358.0 ± 66.9 ms, p < 0.001). Subsequently, the RR interval increased gradually, although it remained lower than baseline values 30 and 60 min after the cross-country test (30-CC: 870.7 ± 131.2 ms, p < 0.01; 60-CC: 1424.2 ± 98.3 ms, p < 0.05). It returned to baseline levels 90 to 240 min after the cross-country test (90-CC to 240-CC). Additionally, the RR interval during the cross-country test was significantly lower than that during the warm-up (W-CC: 761.6 ± 159.9 ms vs. CC: 358.8 ± 66.9 ms, p < 0.001); see Fig. 1A.

Compared to the control (34.8 ± 3.7 beats/min), mean HR increased (p < 0.001) during W-D (86.3 ± 9.0 beats/min) and D (101.4 ± 7.0 beats/min). Afterwards, the HR returned to the baseline levels at 30-D to 90-D. The mean HR was consistently elevated during W-J (76.7 ± 16.0 beats/min; p < 0.05) and J (148.2 ± 30.5 beats/min; p < 0.05). Although it gradually decreased after the jumping test, it remained higher than the control value at 30-J (49.5 ± 3.1 beats/min; p < 0.05). The mean HR returned to baseline levels between 60 and 240 min after the jumping test (60-J to 240-J). The mean HR rose again during W-CC (81.4 ± 15.3 beats/min, p < 0.05), peaking at CC (172.0 ± 27.0 beats/min, p < 0.001). Finally, the HR returned to baseline between 90-CC to 240-CC. SDNN, RMSSD, and stress index

Despite fluctuations during the competition, no statistically significant difference was observed in SDNN compared to the control. Nonetheless, the SDNN was lower in J than in D (D: 25.4 ± 9.1 ms vs. J: 9.8 ± 6.2 ms, p < 0.01). It was also lower in CC than in W-CC (W-CC: 30.8 ± 10.3 ms vs. CC: 9.2 ± 7.0 ms, p < 0.05); see Fig. 2A. In comparison to the control, the RMSSD showed no significant differences throughout the exercise tests and recovery periods. However, the RMSSD was lower in CC than in W-CC (W-CC: 18.8 ± 8.2 ms vs. CC: 4.0 ± 1.6 ms, p < 0.05); see Fig. 2B.

Discussion

The present study investigated the autonomic responses of horses by observing HR and HRV metrics in response to three exercise tests and their recovery periods during the FEI Eventing World Challenge. The key findings of the study are as follows: (1) The RR interval decreased, accompanied by an increase in the mean HR and peak HR, during the warm-up and activities of each exercise test; (2) A more prolonged duration was needed for the RR interval, mean HR, stress index, and PNS index to return to baseline values during the jumping phase compared to the dressage phase, with the most extended recovery period noted in the cross-country test; (3) The peak HR during the cross-country test was similar to that in the jumping test but higher than that in the dressage test; (4) The metrics SDNN, total power band, and SD2 were lower during the jumping test than during the dressage test; (5) Differences in HR and HRV metrics during warm-up and the corresponding activities were only observed in the cross-country test; and (6) The SNS index remained elevated compared to the control before the start of the cross-country phase. These results indicate variations in autonomic responses among horses engaged in different activities and exercise tests, as well as heightened autonomic responses observed before the cross-country test during the one-day eventing competition.

A decreased RR interval and an increased mean HR were expected during the warm-up and corresponding exercise tests, primarily due to enhanced sympathetic activity [8, 16, 17]. The differing duration of the RR interval and mean HR recovery after each exercise test reflected variations in vagal reactivation and sympathetic withdrawal during the recovery period [32]. Notably, the higher peak HR observed during the cross-country test compared to the dressage test, but not the jumping test, suggested that the exercise intensity was somewhat similar for both the jumping and cross-country tests. Although metrics such as SDNN, RMSSD, total power band, SD1, and SD2 displayed a trend towards decrease during the exercise tests, they did not show statistically significant differences compared to baseline levels. The lack of change in SDNN during the competition was consistent with previous reports in other equestrian disciplines [8]. Research indicates that horses exhibit considerable individual variation [33, 34], and their fitness levels may differ due to distinct training prior to the event [20]. These factors likely contributed to significant variations in HRV metrics among the horses, resulting in no statistically significant differences during the competition. In particular, SDNN, total power band, and SD2 values were lower during the jumping test than during the dressage test. Since these three HRV metrics indicate long-term variations in HR and are influenced by both vagal and sympathetic activity, a decrease in these metrics may indicate reduced vagal activity, increased sympathetic activity, or an interplay between the two systems [21–23]. Regarding RMSSD and SD1, which reflect short-term variations and are linked to vagal activity [21, 22], no differences were found between the dressage and jumping tests. This suggests that vagal activity was similar in both exercise tests. Thus, the decrease in SDNN, total power band, and SD2 during the jumping test is thought to result primarily from increased sympathetic activity in comparison to the dressage test. The higher stress index recorded during the jumping test than the dressage test further supports the idea that stress induced a decrease in HRV due to heightened sympathetic activity [21, 35].

In the present study, the LF and HF bands, expressed in normalised units, remained unchanged throughout the competition. The LF band is influenced by both the sympathetic and vagal components, while changes in the HF band primarily reflect vagal activity [21, 22]. These findings indicate that sympathetic and vagal contributions to total power band modulation were balanced during each exercise test and recovery period. The LF/HF ratio, which reflects the balance between sympathetic and vagal activity [21, 22, 36], showed a trend towards increasing during the exercise tests. However, individual differences among horses may lead to non-significant variations in this metric during the tests. A notable difference was observed only during the warm-up before the dressage test. This finding is partly consistent with the change in the SD2/SD1 parameter, which is correlated with the LF/HF ratio [36, 37], observed during the initial warm-up and the dressage test. Increases in these two metrics may indicate heightened sympathetic activity due to the horses’ anticipation of familiar activities [35, 38]. Consequently, this outcome highlights the marked variation of HRV modulation among the horses, leading to non-significant differences in these metrics during the competition.

As expected, a decrease in the PNS index was observed during the warm-up, exercise tests, and recovery after the cross-country test. This finding closely corresponded to changes in the RR interval, emphasising a reduced vagal role during these activities. However, the modification of the SNS index varied among the exercise tests and the recovery period. The SNS index increased in tandem with a rise in mean HR during the dressage and cross-country phases. This reflected a shift towards sympathetic activity during these two phases, highlighting the significant role of the sympathetic component in HR modulation during the dressage and cross-country phases. Notably, the SNS index also increased during the jumping test and remained elevated for 30 min after the test. Although a gradual decrease occurred, the SNS index remained higher than the control value during the recovery period after the jumping test, indicating a sustained high level of sympathetic activity before the start of the cross-country test. In horses, HR recovery is influenced by sympathetic withdrawal and vagal reactivation, with proper training potentially enhancing HR recovery by improving vagal regulation [32]. Even without changes in mean HR, fluctuations in the SNS index could reflect a delayed sympathetic withdrawal and vagal reactivation during the post-jumping recovery period. The typical movements, riders’ specific techniques, and horses’ exercise capacities may contribute to the distinct sympathetic and vagal activation observed during the jumping phase. The variation in HR and HRV metrics during warm-up and the respective exercise tests was detected only in the cross-country phase, indicating a disparity in autonomic responses between the warm-up and exercise test in this phase. While significant autonomic responses were identified in this study, it remains unclear whether these responses and their modulation would differ at higher levels of competition that unfold over multiple days, as opposed to the format used in this research. Further investigation is also necessary to examine the impact of training on autonomic responses during eventing competitions.

The present study encountered several limitations during the experiment. Firstly, the small sample size renders the findings preliminary; thus, further validation with a larger cohort is essential. Unlike participants in higher-level eventing disciplines, horses in the current Eventing World Challenge faced no restrictions on participation. Consequently, the horses involved may have exhibited varying levels of fitness, potentially contributing to significant disparities in HR and HRV modulation during the activities. These variations may explain the non-significant differences observed in HRV metrics, even though such differences are typically anticipated in exercise tests. Additionally, there was variability in the levels of physical activity and the duration of warm-ups prior to each exercise test, influenced by the rider’s intent and available resources. This inconsistency could result in substantial variability in HRV, leading to non-significant differences despite a higher mean value compared to the control group. Moreover, during the exercise tests, there were unavoidable temporary disconnections between the heart rate monitor (HRM) sensor and the Polar sports watch, resulting in some missing data in the HRV analysis. Lastly, while the gender of the animals may have influenced changes in HR and HRV, this could not be assessed in the current study due to the limited sample size. Therefore, the modulation of HR and HRV metrics in this study should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that HR and HRV change during dressage, jumping, and cross-country phases of one-day eventing, with the most pronounced responses observed in jumping and cross-country. These preliminary findings suggest that monitoring HR and HRV may help support horse welfare, although further research with larger numbers and additional stress indicators is needed to confirm these observations.
