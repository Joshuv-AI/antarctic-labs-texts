Exploring Interstitial Pneumonia With Autoimmune Features: Case Study Insights and Review of Current Literature
Authors: Mohammad Hadi Tajik Jalayeri, Somayeh Sadani, Narges Lashkarbolouk, Mahdi Mazandarani, Mehrnaz Razzazan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInterstitial Pneumonia with Autoimmune Features (IPAF) refers to a subgroup of interstitial lung diseases that show autoimmune‐like characteristics but do not align with established conditions such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or recognized connective tissue diseases (CTD). In this case report, we present a 43‐year‐old woman who had a history of dry cough, decreased appetite, weight loss, and progressive shortness of breath. Initial investigations, including spirometry, autoantibody testing, and CT scan, revealed evidence of nonspecific interstitial pneumonia and positive autoantibodies. Our patient did not fully meet the CTD criteria, and based on the clinical symptoms, imaging findings, and the presence of autoantibodies, an IPAF diagnosis was made. Our patient was treated with prednisolone and mycophenolate, significantly improving her symptoms, pulmonary function, and exercise capacity during the one‐year follow‐up period. Our report mentioned the importance of considering IPAF in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with interstitial lung disease and features of an autoimmune process. Delayed diagnosis contributes to poorer prognoses and increased disease severity among affected individuals.Keywords: autoimmune features, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, interstitial lung diseases, interstitial pneumonia

Summary

Being a subgroup of interstitial lung diseases, Interstitial Pneumonia with Autoimmune Features (IPAF) shows autoimmune‐like characteristics but does not align with established conditions, often resulting in delayed diagnosis and a subsequent lack of timely and appropriate treatment. This delay contributes to poorer prognoses and increased disease severity among affected individuals.

Introduction

Interstitial pulmonary fibrosis with autoimmune features (IPAF) is a progressive and usually fatal interstitial lung disease (ILD) that mainly affects older adults. This disease is characterized by the buildup of fibrotic tissue in the lungs, leading to decreased lung function and respiratory failure [1, 2]. The European Respiratory Society (ERS) and American Thoracic Society (ATS) introduced the concept of IPAF in 2015 [3, 4]. IPAF serves as a research classification aimed at identifying patients with idiopathic interstitial pneumonia (IIP) who exhibit clinical, serological, or morphological indications of an underlying autoimmune process but fail to meet the established connective tissue disease (CTD) criteria [5, 6]. IPAF is a disease with a complex condition often linked to autoimmune diseases like rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and scleroderma [7]. The prevalence of IPAF in ILDs ranges from 7% to 34%, largely influenced by the studied population and patient recruitment profile [5, 6, 7].

The etiology of IPAF is largely unknown, leading to its classification as “idiopathic.” Nonetheless, certain risk factors have been identified, including a history of smoking, older age, and environmental exposures like occupational dust and pollutants. Additionally, genetic predispositions have been suggested, as familial cases offer insights into potential heritable patterns of the disease [7, 8].

Diagnosing IPAF involves clinical, radiological, and histopathological criteria, with nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) being the most commonly observed radiological pattern [1, 2]. Usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) is defined by reticular opacities and honeycombing, primarily affecting the lower lung zones. These changes are typically bilateral and symmetrical, with a progressive decline in lung function corresponding to the severity of structural alterations. In contrast, NSIP exhibits a more uniform interstitial pattern without the distinct honeycombing seen in UIP, often leading to less fibrosis and a better overall prognosis [9, 10].

Treatment usually employs a multidisciplinary approach addressing both the lung condition and the underlying autoimmune disorder, including anti‐inflammatory medications, management of the autoimmune disease, and symptomatic treatments [8, 9]. Limited knowledge exists about the best management and treatment for patients meeting IPAF criteria. Some studies indicate that IPAF patients may have a better prognosis than those with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [7, 8].

This case study details the clinical and laboratory findings of a 43‐year‐old woman who developed gradual symptoms over three years, including worsening breathlessness, a persistent dry cough, reduced appetite, and weight loss. After a comprehensive evaluation, she was diagnosed with IPAF. Following treatment with prednisolone and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), her condition significantly improved.

Case Presentation

Case History/Examination

A 43‐year‐old married woman, who is a stay‐at‐home parent with one child, was referred to the respiratory clinic at Sayad Shirazi Hospital in Gorgan, Iran, due to experiencing shortness of breath. The patient presented with significant weakness, showing signs of illness and cachexia, along with moderate to severe symptoms of shortness of breath on exertion (The New York Heart Association [NYHA] functional class III).

The patient's shortness of breath has been progressively worsening, particularly during physical activities, to the extent that it has disrupted her usual daily activities. Notably, she did not exhibit any symptoms related to rheumatological conditions, such as arthritis, joint pain, joint inflammation, Raynaud's phenomenon, photosensitivity, skin changes, or rash. Furthermore, she did not report any oral ulcers, dryness in the morning, dry eyes, or dry mouth. The patient had no history of previous miscarriage or stillbirth, no occupational exposure to Tandoor (a traditional oven for baking bread), no history of tobacco smoking or drug consumption, and she was not exposed to passive smoke. Furthermore, her first‐degree relatives had no history of significant diseases.

In the patient's medical history, it was discovered that she had been experiencing shortness of breath while climbing stairs and heavy activities since 3 years ago. This was accompanied by a dry cough, gradually leading to a loss of appetite and weight. Three years ago, the patient was admitted to the hospital, where an investigation was done. A bronchoscopy was performed, revealing no abnormalities in the bronchial passages. A spiral lung computed tomography (CT) scan (Figure 1) ordered. Eventually, she was diagnosed with IPF and treated with pirfenidone.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigation and Treatment

Upon the patient's arrival at our clinic, pulmonary function tests with and without bronchodilator (PFT ± BD) and a spiral lung CT scan were conducted. The PFT results indicated a restrictive pattern (Table 1), while the CT scan exhibited ground‐glass opacities, interlobular septal thickening, and lung fibrosis, accompanied by traction bronchiectasis in both lungs. A cardiac evaluation was also requested for the patient. The echocardiogram reported an ejection fraction of 50% and a systolic pulmonary artery pressure of 30 mmHg.

Conclusion and Results

The imaging findings were most consistent with NSIP (Figure 2) and autoantibodies were positive. The patient presented with Idiopathic Interstitial Pneumonia (IIP) and autoimmune features, but did not fully meet criteria for CTD‐associated ILD. Therefore, based on the ERS/ATS new classification, a diagnosis of IPAF was considered for her. Following the IPAF diagnosis, the patient was treated with prednisolone (1 mg/kg daily) and MMF (1 g twice daily). The prednisolone dosage was tapered over 6 months to 7.5 mg daily.

(A–D). The lung computed tomography (CT) scan of the patient reveals the presence of ground‐glass opacities, thickening of the interlobular septa, and pulmonary fibrosis, with associated traction bronchiectasis observed in both lungs.

The patient's symptoms significantly improved during the one‐year follow‐up after diagnosis and treatment initiation. She demonstrated substantial clinical and functional improvement. In Table 1, we compared the patient's pulmonary function test results during her initial visit and the 6‐month and one‐year follow‐up appointments. The data revealed a significant improvement in the patient's pulmonary function over time. Additionally, the patient's SpO2 level increased from 89% to 94% after treatment, and the six‐minute walk test distance increased from 180 m before treatment to 260 m after 1 year of treatment. All these results demonstrate the efficacy of the treatment in managing the disease progression.

Discussion

IPAF highlights the overlap between IIPs and CTD‐ILD, offering a framework for the condition's designation and study. In most cases, respiratory symptoms may involve chronic cough, dyspnea, or fatigue, often linked to restrictive lung disease indicated by PFT. Radiological findings usually show ground‐glass opacities, reticular patterns, or honeycombing on high‐resolution CT (HRCT), signaling interstitial lung involvement. Histopathological examination, often through lung biopsy, can reveal patterns like NSIP or follicular bronchiolitis, which, when combined with clinical and radiological data, support an IPAF diagnosis [1, 2, 11].

Timely diagnosis of IPAF is crucial, as early recognition enables prompt intervention, significantly enhancing patient outcomes and quality of life. As IPF progresses, it can cause various complications, including acute exacerbations, pulmonary hypertension, respiratory failure, lung cancer, cardiovascular issues, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, emphysema, and pulmonary embolism. Due to the late diagnosis, our patient experienced multiple acute exacerbations during this period that did not respond to outpatient treatments.

According to the 2015 ERS/ATS statements on IPAF, diagnosis requires meeting three prior requirements and having at least one feature from at least two domains (Figure 3). Prior requirements include (1) evidence of interstitial pneumonia via HRCT imaging or lung biopsy, (2) exclusion of known causes of interstitial pneumonia following a comprehensive clinical evaluation, and (3) no fulfillment of criteria for a diagnosed CTD. Three domains include clinical presentation, serological tests, and morphological manifestations [12, 13, 14]. In our study, the patient presented with confirmed interstitial pneumonia of unknown etiology, inconsistent with CTD, but she had a positive ANA, NSIP pattern on HRCT, and restrictive pattern in PFT. In line with our findings, a 2020 study by Karampeli et al. found that the average age at diagnosis for IPAF was 63.2 (±11) years, with 62% of patients being female. The most common clinical features were arthritis (82%) and rash (54%, not included in the IPAF criteria). ANA positivity was observed in 59% of patients, and NSIP was the predominant radiological pattern (61.5%). PFTs showed stabilization or improvement in 79.5% of patients at 12 months follow‐up. Infections occurred in 23.1% of patients during the first semester and 12.8% during the second [15]. The study by Sambataro et al. (2019) reported that IPAF patients were predominantly female (62.1%) with a median age of 66 years. Key findings included NSIP (68.8%), ANA positivity (17.7%), and Raynaud's phenomenon (31.1%). Compared to IPF, IPAF patients were younger, performed better on PFTs, required less oxygen support, and had a higher prevalence of females and the NSIP pattern [16].

Understanding the patient's demographic and clinical background—such as age, occupation, and family history—is vital as these factors may indicate environmental exposures or genetic risks for ILDs [5, 6]. Fernandes et al.'s study (2019) found that patients meeting IPAF criteria often have a smoking history comparable to those with IPF. The most common clinical and serological autoimmune markers are Raynaud's phenomenon and positive antinuclear antibodies, respectively. While NSIP is the primary radiological and histopathological pattern, patients identifying with IPAF criteria may also exhibit a UIP pattern based on clinical and serological findings [9].

Managing is challenging due to its progressive nature and limited treatment options. In the treatment method, corticosteroids can reduce lung inflammation and may be used alone or alongside other treatments. Medications such as azathioprine, MMF, or methotrexate can help manage autoimmune activity and inflammation [4, 5, 6]. Antifibrotic agents like pirfenidone and nintedanib can slow disease progression. Lung transplantation is the only potential cure for eligible patients, but, it is limited by a shortage of donor organs and the need for lifelong immunosuppression. Supportive care, including pulmonary rehabilitation and supplemental oxygen, is essential for improving quality of life and relieving symptoms [12, 14, 17]. In our case, treatment with prednisolone and MMF resulted in improvement in her symptoms within a year, including enhanced pulmonary function and oxygen saturation.

This report emphasizes the need to consider IPAF in patients with IIP who exhibit autoimmune process characteristics yet do not fulfill the full criteria for CTD. Correct IPAF diagnosis is crucial for guiding treatment decisions and improving patient outcomes. While IPAF remains a research classification, it is expected to transition into a clinical diagnosis and may eventually be recognized as a distinct subset within the ILD classification framework. IPAF has played a vital role in standardizing nomenclature and establishing consistent classification criteria, fostering research on patients with idiopathic interstitial pneumonia exhibiting autoimmune features.

IPAF is a complex condition that poses diagnostic challenges due to shared symptoms with other autoimmune diseases and ILDs, requiring thorough investigation for accurate distinction. There are no universally accepted diagnostic IPAF criteria, resulting in variability among healthcare providers. The presence of autoantibodies can differ significantly, and not all IPAF patients will test positive, complicating reliance on serological tests. Additionally, imaging results can be nonspecific; HRCT scans might reveal patterns similar to other ILDs, increasing the risk of misdiagnosis [15, 16]. Similar to our case, which has been misdiagnosed for about 3 years and has been receiving treatment for symptoms but has not responded to treatment due to suboptimal management related to misdiagnosis.

Our study benefited from early diagnosis and prompt treatment, resulting in improved patient symptoms and disease progression. A challenge we faced was the need to send specialized test samples to a better‐equipped center.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our case study highlights the effective management of a middle‐aged woman with IPAF, who showed significant improvements in symptoms, lung function, and exercise capacity after treatment with prednisolone and mycophenolate. The findings emphasize the importance of early detection and intervention for IPAF patients. It is also vital to determine whether IPAF progression is influenced by specific clinical, serological, or morphological traits that could serve as prognostic indicators. Furthermore, investigating effective therapeutic strategies is crucial for enhancing the well‐being of those affected by this condition.
