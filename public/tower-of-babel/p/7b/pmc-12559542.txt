OsLEML2 Regulates Rice Thermotolerance Through Auxin and ROS Homeostasis
Authors: Xingzhuo Sun, Yuwei Wang, Yulin Chen, Peng Zhang, Yanting Li, Tongyue Ouyang, Lihua Li, Jianqing Zhu, Xiaomei Jia, Xiaoying Ye, Jun Zhu, Rongjun Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Escalating global warming threatens rice (Oryza sativa L.) growth and yield, making research into thermotolerance mechanisms imperative. OsLEML2, identified as a heat-responsive gene through microarray screening, was investigated in this study. It was found that overexpression of OsLEML2 reduces thermotolerance in transgenic plants. RT-qPCR analysis and physiological parameter measurements revealed that OsLEML2 disrupts reactive oxygen species homeostasis as well as suppresses HSF-HSP signaling. This gene affects the expression of auxin biosynthesis genes and catabolic genes, thereby influencing auxin homeostasis and regulating the heat tolerance of rice. Additionally, OsLEML2 potentially regulates thermotolerance by maintaining cellular auxin homeostasis through facilitating the efflux of excess auxin or its sequestration into the central vacuole, and by modulating pollen viability through the regulation of pollen reactive oxygen species levels. Collectively, our functional characterization has identified OsLEML2 as a negative regulatory factor in heat stress responses, providing a potential genetic target for enhancing thermotolerance in crops via molecular breeding strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-025-00856-7.

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.), one of the most critical cereal crops globally, serves as a staple food for over half of the world’s population and is predominantly cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions (Gross and Zhao 2014; Hussain et al. 2016). Escalating global warming and extreme climatic events have intensified plant exposure to extreme temperature fluctuations. Ambient temperature profoundly influences the entire plant life cycle, including the regulation of growth and development, geographical distribution, and phenological transitions (Ding et al. 2020). Throughout evolution, plants have evolved multilayered adaptive mechanisms to counteract heat stress, such as proteostasis (protein homeostasis) regulation, reactive oxygen species (ROS)scavenging, osmoregulation, and phytohormone signaling networks.

Heat-induced protein misfolding or denaturation triggers cytotoxic stress (Fragkostefanakis et al. 2016). Heat shock proteins (HSPs), functioning as molecular chaperones, maintain proteostasis by stabilizing aberrant protein conformations and facilitating their refolding (Rieu 2016; Samtani et al. 2022). Heat shock transcription factors (HSFs), central regulators of the heat stress response, orchestrate HSP expression under thermal stress (Andrási et al. 2020). Among these, class A1 HSFs (HSFA1s) act as master transcriptional regulators, operating upstream of multiple transcription factors (TFs) to coordinate the expression of hundreds of downstream heat-responsive genes (Andrási et al. 2020; Bingjie, et al. 2018). Under non-stress conditions, HSP70 and HSP90 sequester HSFA1s, suppressing their activity and nuclear translocation. Elevated temperatures induce the dissociation of HSFA1s from HSP70/90 complexes, enabling HSFA1 activation and subsequent upregulation of downstream targets (e.g., HSFA2s, HSFBs, and DREB2A), which amplify HSP production and ROS-scavenging enzyme activity to mitigate heat-induced damage (Ohama et al. 2017; Hahn et al. 2011).

ROS play dual roles in plant development and stress adaptation, with ROS signaling networks exhibiting high conservation across aerobic organisms (Mittler 2017). Moderate ROS levels act as signaling molecules to activate HSF-mediated thermotolerance pathways, whereas excessive ROS accumulation induces oxidative injury (Volkov et al. 2006; Foyer and Christine H 2018). Mechanistically, ROS potentiate the transcriptional activity of HSFA1s and other HSFs, driving the expression of heat-responsive genes (Liu et al. 2013; Dickinson et al. 2018). For instance, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) enhances the DNA-binding activity of HSFs and promotes HSP accumulation via nitric oxide (NO)-mediated signaling in Arabidopsis (Wang, et al. 2014). Conversely, ROS also regulate developmental processes, such as triggering programmed cell death (PCD) in tapetal cells during anther development to allocate nutrients and energy for pollen maturation (Shi-Xia and Qiang-Nan 2017; Hu et al. 2011).

Heat stress also induces excessive ROS accumulation in chloroplasts, mitochondria, and the plasma membrane, leading to protein denaturation, oxidative damage to membrane systems, and subsequent cell death (Alfonso and Puppo 2009). To counteract this, plants upregulate antioxidant enzyme biosynthesis under heat stress to mitigate ROS-driven oxidative damage (Baxter, et al. 2014).

Concurrently, heat stress triggers the synthesis and accumulation of osmoprotectants to regulate osmotic potential across cellular compartments, thereby enhancing thermotolerance. Proline (Pro), a canonical osmoprotectant, plays a pivotal role in plant adaptation to abiotic stress. Under thermal stress, proline safeguards protein integrity, enhances enzymatic activity, scavenges ROS, maintains cellular homeostasis, and acts as a signaling molecule to modulate mitochondrial function, cell proliferation/death dynamics, and stress-responsive gene expression-processes critical for post-stress recovery (Szabados and Savouré 2010).

Phytohormones are central mediators of abiotic stress responses. Research revealed that foliar IAA content increased following exposure to both high- and low-temperature stress. Foliar application of 10⁻5 M IAA and 1 mM spermidine mitigated the adverse effects of heat stress on rice (Lakshmi et al. 2022). In rice, most Aux/IAA family genes are induced by high temperature at 45 °C, with the transcript levels of OsIAA7 and OsIAA25 continuously increasing with prolonged exposure to high temperature, indicating that the auxin response is associated with the duration of heat stress (Qiu et al. 2025; Sharma, et al. 2018). Hormone response depends on stress intensity/duration, tissue, and developmental stage (Sharma, et al. 2018; Aryan et al. 2023). Recent studies reveal that elevated abscisic acid (ABA) in rice anthers under heat stress accelerates PCD and induces abnormal tapetum degradation via ROS overaccumulation (Cheng 2023).

Current understanding of LEM1 proteins in plants remains limited, with reports confined to barley (Hordeum vulgare) and rice (Oryza sativa L.). In barley, the anther-specific gene Lemma1 (LEM1) is predominantly expressed in the lemma and palea of developing spikes during elongation stages, potentially associating with membrane systems and participating in organ identity maintenance or non-morphogenic functions (Skadsen et al. 2002). Notably, the regulatory mechanism of LEM1 is evolutionarily conserved between barley and wheat (Triticum aestivum), as demonstrated by transgenic wheat harboring the barley LEM1 promoter. This promoter drives transient activity in the rachis, lemma, palea, and anthers-but not in vegetative tissues or seeds-enabling tissue-specific production of defense proteins without grain accumulation, a trait exploitable for resistance breeding against Fusarium head blight (Tundo et al. 2016; Somleva and Blechl 2005).

In rice, the LEM1 family comprises three members: OsLEML1 (LOC_Os01g70440; also designated ‘RTS’), OsLEML2 (LOC_Os02g32080), and OsLEML3 (LOC_Os04g32700) (Gollan and Bhave 2010). In situ hybridization revealed that OsLEML1 expression is restricted to the tapetum during the trinucleate stage of anther development, with no detectable transcripts in pollen grains (Kapoor 2011). Subsequent studies propose that OsLEML1, synthesized in tapetal cells, may be secreted into developing pollen to supply structural components for exine formation or nutrient allocation. Silencing OsLEML1 results in male sterility while preserving female fertility, underscoring its critical role in pollen maturation (Luo et al. 2006). QTL-seq-based rapid identification of quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with spikelet fertility under heat stress revealed OsLEML2 as a candidate gene linked to high-temperature-induced spikelet sterility, with peak expression detected in rice (Oryza sativa L.) anthers (Phakchana Nubankoh et al. 2020),indicating its potential importance for anther and pollen development under thermal stress.

Collectively, we demonstrate that OsLEML2 functions as a negative regulator of thermotolerance in rice. Overexpression of OsLEML2 exacerbates heat-induced oxidative damage by suppressing the HSF-HSP signaling cascade, reducing antioxidant enzyme activity, and elevating ROS accumulation, ultimately impairing pollen viability and seedling survival. Conversely, OsLEML2 knockdown or knockout enhances heat tolerance. These findings advance the mechanistic understanding of LEM1-family proteins in stress responses and highlight OsLEML2 as a critical genetic determinant for engineering thermotolerant crops.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Condition

Rice (Oryza sativa L. subsp. japonica cv. Nipponbare) was used as wild type (WT) and material for genetic transformation. In all experiments, the seeds were soaked with 2% (v/v) NaClO for 30 min, washed with sterile water and subsequently subjected to imbibition at 37 °C for 3 days in the dark. Then, the germinating seeds were grown in nutrient solution in a growth chamber with a cycle of 16-h light at 28 °C and 8-h dark at 25 °C.

Microarray and Bioinformatics Analysis

Affymetrix expression microarray experiments were performed following the GeneTech manual (Shanghai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), as described previously by Chen et al. (Chen et al. 2012). The Rice Genome Annotation Project (RGAP; http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/index.shtml) and NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) were used to retrieve basic information for the OsLEML2 gene. The PlantCare database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) was employed to analyze cis-acting elements in the 1500-bp promoter region upstream of the open reading frame.

Construction of Transgenic Plants

The full coding sequence of OsLEML2 was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Expression vectors D-163 + 1300 and D-163 + 1300 + GFP (derived from pCAMBIA1300 and pJIT163) were double-digested with HindⅢ and BamHⅠendonucleases, then ligated to construct OsLEML2 overexpression and GFP fusion vectors. Antisense expression vectors were generated by inserting the reverse-complementary coding sequence into the D-163 + 1300 vector. The 1500-bp promoter sequence upstream of OsLEML2 was retrieved from NCBI and amplified from rice genomic DNA, then cloned into the pCAMBIA3301 vector digested with HindⅢ and NocⅠ. Transgenic lines were generated via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of callus tissue, and positive lines were validated by RT-qPCR (Toki et al. 2010; Azizi-Dargahlou and pouresmaeil 2023).

RNA Extraction and RT-qPCR Analysis

According to the instructions, total RNA was isolated from the two-week-old rice seedlings using TRIzol (Vazyme, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using All In One 5X RT Master Mix (ABM, Rich­mond, BC, Canada). The RT-qPCR was conducted using a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time System coupled to a C1000 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA). The internal reference genes were UBQ5 and 18S (Dombrowski and Martin 2009). Normalized relative transcript levels were obtained by the 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Primers were listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Histochemical GUS Activity Assay and Protein Subcellular Localization

Following the previous method (Jefferson and R. A 1989), different parts of transgenic rice were placed in GUS staining solution at 37 °C for 12 h, then immersed in 70% (v/v) ethanol for 5 min, added with 95% (v/v) ethanol and boiled so as to remove chlorophyll, and finally visualized by using a Zeiss fully automated stereo microscope.

Following the previous method (Wang et al. 2022), the OsLEML2-GFP vector was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens EHA105 and then injected into the lower epidermis of tobacco leaves, which were incubated for 2 d under light protection. The CaMV 35S: GFP was used as control. GFP-derived and mCherry-derived fluorescence was.

analyzed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (Nikon A1 i90, LSCM, Japan). Excitation/emission wavelengths: GFP (488 nm/505–575 nm), mCherry (543 nm/560–615 nm).

Measurement of the Physiological Parameters

Fourteen-day-old seedlings of WT, OsLEML2 overexpression, and antisense-expressing transgenic rice lines were subjected to 42 °C treatment. Leaf tissues were collected at 0 h (pre-treatment) and 24 h (post-treatment) for physiological parameter measurements. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD) activities, malondialdehyde (MDA) content, and proline levels were quantified using previously described protocols (Arshad and Naegele 2023; Zhang and Huang 2013),with minor modifications.

Crude Enzyme Extraction

Fresh leaf tissue (0.2 g) was weighed and ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen using a pre-chilled mortar. The homogenate was suspended in 3 mL of ice-cold 100 mmol/L phosphate buffer solution (PBS; pH 7.8) and thoroughly homogenized. The slurry was transferred to a 5 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected in a fresh tube for subsequent analysis.

SOD Activity Assay

Reaction mixture preparation: A SOD reaction solution was prepared by mixing 30 mL of 100 mmol/L PBS (pH 7.8), 0.6 mL of 1 mmol/L EDTA-Na2, 2 mL of 130 mmol/L methionine (Met), 2 mL of 750 μmol/L nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT), and 2 mL of 20 μmol/L riboflavin (sufficient for 30 reactions) (Miao, et al. 2010).

Assay procedure: 50 μL of crude enzyme extract was added to 1 mL of the SOD reaction mixture. Control groups included: Control I, 50 μL of 100 mmol/L PBS (pH 7.8) incubated in complete darkness. Control II, 50 μL of 100 mmol/L PBS (pH 7.8) exposed to 4000 lx (lx) light intensity. Test samples and Control II were illuminated (4000 lx) for 10–15 min, while Control I remained unexposed. Absorbance at 560 nm was measured under low-light conditions using Control I as the blank reference.

POD Activity Assay

Reaction mixture preparation: 50 mL of 100 mmol/L PBS (pH 7.0) was supplemented with 28 μL of 0.2% (w/v) guaiacol (heated and stirred until fully dissolved). After cooling, 19 μL of 30% H2O2 was added (sufficient for 50 reactions) (Sekmen et al. 2014). Assay procedure: A reference cuvette was prepared with 50 μL of 100 mmol/L PBS (pH 7.8) and 1 mL of reaction mixture. For test samples, 50 μL of crude enzyme extract was added to the cuvette, followed by 1 mL of reaction mixture. Dynamic absorbance at 470 nm was recorded every 15 s for 1 min, and the average rate of change was calculated to determine POD activity.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) Content Assay

100 μL of crude enzyme extract was added to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube containing 1 mL of 0.25% (w/v) 2-thiobarbituric acid (TBA) solution. A control reaction was prepared by replacing the enzyme extract with 100 μL of 100 mmol/L PBS (pH 7.8). The mixtures were incubated in a boiling water bath for 15 min, cooled on ice for 5 min, and absorbance was measured at 532 nm and 600 nm (Bates et al. 1973).

Proline Content Determination

Fresh leaf tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in 5 mL of 3% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid and boiled for 10 min. After cooling, the homogenate was filtered into a 5 mL centrifuge tube. A 2 mL aliquot of the filtrate was mixed with 2 mL of glacial acetic acid and 2 mL of 2.5% (w/v) acidic ninhydrin solution, incubated in a boiling water bath for 30 min, and cooled. Subsequently, 5 mL of toluene was added, and the mixture was vortexed vigorously. After phase separation, the upper red layer was collected, and absorbance at 520 nm was measured. A control was prepared using 2 mL of ddH2O instead of the extract (Ma et al. 2025).

Hydrogen Peroxide (H2O2) Accumulation Assay

Leaves (1 cm segments) from 14-day-old seedlings treated at 42 °C for 24 h were immersed in 1 mg/mL 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution and incubated under light (28 °C) for 10 h. Chlorophyll was removed by boiling in absolute ethanol, and H2O2 accumulation (brown polymerization product) was visualized using a stereomicroscope. Untreated leaves served as the control (Nan 2011).

Expression Analysis of Heat Stress-Responsive Genes in OsLEML2 Transgenic Plants Under Thermal Stress

To investigate the signaling pathways mediated by OsLEML2 during heat stress, 14-day-old WT and OsLEML2 transgenic seedlings (grown under identical conditions) were exposed to 42 °C in a controlled thermal chamber. Leaf tissues were sampled at 0 h, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 16 h post-treatment for RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis (Zhang et al. 2025). RT-qPCR was performed using UBQ5 as the internal reference gene to quantify transcript levels of HSFs and HSPs family genes. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Pollen Viability and Oxidative Stress Analysis

Mature anthers from WT and OsLEML2 transgenic plants were dissected onto glass slides, crushed with forceps, and treated with 0.05 g/L 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) solution. After 20 min of incubation at 37 °C in darkness, pollen viability was examined under an light microscope.

For oxidative stress detection, 100 mg DAB and 50 mg NBT were dissolved separately in 100 mL phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) to prepare staining solutions. Spikelets from boot-stage WT and OsLEML2 plants (heat-treated at 42 °C for 1 d) were immersed in the solutions and stained in darkness at 25 °C for 12 h. Oxidative damage (DAB: brown polymerization; NBT: dark blue formazan deposits) was visualized and imaged using a light microscope.

Constructs and Recombinant Protein Expression

To achieve protein expression, an EGFP-tagged LEML2 expression construct was generated by cloning the PCR product encoding OsLEML2 into the pET-28a vector. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S3.His-EGFP-LEML2 was recombinantly expressed in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) cells. After 4 h of protein induction at 37 °C in auto-induction medium (AIM), expression continued at 25 °C for 16 h. Cell lysates were affinity-purified using Ni–NTA His-binding resin following sonication according to manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, Product P2226). Purified proteins were flash-frozen and stored at −80 °C (Guan and Xue 2022).

Phase Separation Assays

In vitro phase separation assays were performed in solution containing 5 μM His-EGFP-LEML2 protein (150 mmol/L NaCl, 50 mmol/L Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 1 mmol/L DTT). For fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) and time-lapse imaging experiments, 10 μl of solution was sealed between two coverslips. Images were acquired on an IXplore SpinSR (Olympus FV3000) with focal plane lock using an 80 × oil-immersion objective. Specific regions of LEML2 condensates were photobleached with 488 nm laser light at 100% intensity. Recovery was recorded at 5-s intervals in a single focal plane (for in vivo experiments, photobleaching was performed for 10 s). Photobleaching was performed using a 488 nm argon-ion laser at 100% intensity (Wang et al. 2024; Geng et al. 2025).

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed with SPSS 25.0 software. The statistical difference was clarified through analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Student’s t-test, with P < 0.05 (*) and P < 0.01 (**) to be significantly different. Three biological replicates were conducted for each of the samples examined. The data were presented as means ± SD.

Results

Bioinformatic Analysis of OsLEML2

Microarray data analysis revealed that OsLEML2 responds to multiple abiotic stresses, exhibiting a significant 1,797.5-fold upregulation in the panicles of rice (Oryza sativa L.) under high-temperature stress, indicating its transcriptional responsiveness to heat stress. (Supplementary Table S2). The gene encodes a LEM1-like protein 2 (LEML2), designated OsLEML2 (LOC_Os02g32080), belonging to the rice LEM1 protein family, though its biological function remains uncharacterized. Located on chromosome 2, OsLEML2 lacks introns, contains a 417-bp coding sequence (CDS) encoding 138 amino acids, and predicts a protein of 13.65 kDa with an isoelectric point (pI) of ~ 8.14. A canonical signal peptide sequence was identified at the N-terminus.

Homologous amino acid sequences of OsLEML2 were retrieved via NCBI Blastp and aligned using ClustalW in MEGA7. While OsLEML2 exhibits conserved motifs across species, no conserved domains were identified. In barley (Hordeum vulgare), the homolog HORVU.MOREX.r3.1HG0064840 encodes an anther-specific RTS-like protein sharing 43.14% amino acid identity with OsLEML2. Similarly, wheat (Triticum aestivum) homolog TraesCS1B02G267400 (RTS-like protein) shows 45.58% identity, suggesting OsLEML2 may also function as an anther-specific protein.

Given the high expression of OsLEML2 in anthers and enhanced GUS staining in rice roots under cold and heat stress, temporal changes in GUS staining intensity in anthers and pollen grains of OsLEML2pro: GUS plants were further analyzed following 4 °C and 42 °C treatments. Prolonged stress duration significantly intensified GUS staining signals (Fig. 1c, d), suggesting OsLEML2 involvement in thermotolerance and cold stress adaptation during anther/pollen development. Moreover, the expression level of OsLEML2 in anthers increased after high-temperature treatment (Fig. S3). To validate this hypothesis, subcellular localization of OsLEML2-GFP in anthers and pollen cells under temperature stress was examined. Under control conditions, GFP signals were restricted to the periphery of pollen sacs. Cold stress induced partial GFP signal distribution within pollen sacs, while heat stress triggered near-complete GFP signal coverage across the entire pollen sacs (imaged under identical parameters; Fig. 1e, f). This temperature-dependent redistribution indicates that OsLEML2’s subcellular localization is dynamically regulated by thermal cues, potentially modulating anther development. Notably, GFP fluorescence was exclusively detected in immature pollen grains and absent in mature pollen (Fig. 1e, f), implying stage-specific roles of OsLEML2 in pollen maturation.

Transmembrane domain prediction using the TMHMM-2.0 algorithm identified two transmembrane helices in OsLEML2 at amino acid residues 5–27 and 47–69 (Fig. 1g), suggesting its potential role as a membrane-integrated protein. Confocal microscopy of OsLEML2-GFP transgenic root cells revealed dynamic cytoplasmic localization of the fusion protein (Fig. 2a). OsVAMP727 (LOC_Os08g44430.2), a vesicle-associated membrane protein (VAMP) in rice, belongs to the SNARE superfamily and regulates vesicle-target membrane fusion, intracellular trafficking, and membrane dynamics (Zhang et al. 2019). To further investigate OsLEML2 subcellular localization, Agrobacterium-mediated co-transfection of OsLEML2-GFP and OsVAMP727-mCherry constructs into tobacco leaves was performed. Fluorescence signals of OsLEML2-GFP and OsVAMP727-mCherry exhibited strong colocalization (Fig. 2d), with dynamic aggregation and dissociation of fusion proteins over time (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Movie 1), indicating OsLEML2 localization to vesicular structures and potential involvement in endomembrane trafficking. Notably, spherical droplets of varying diameters were observed (Supplementary Movie 1), with smaller droplets fusing into larger ones (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Movie 1), a hallmark of liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS).

LLPS is mediated by multivalent weak interactions between intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) or modular domains (Guan and Xue 2022). In silico prediction using PONDR confirmed IDRs in both OsLEML2 and OsVAMP727 (Fig. S4). We observed spherical OsLEML2 droplets that were separable within coleoptile cells (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Movie 2). Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) analysis demonstrated rapid recovery of the OsLEML2-GFP fluorescence signal following in vivo photobleaching (Fig. 2i, l (left panel) and Supplementary Movie 3), confirming that OsLEML2 undergoes liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) in vivo, with molecular diffusion occurring between OsLEML2 condensates and/or the cytoplasmic pool. Discussion

OsLEML2 Negatively Regulates Rice Thermotolerance by Elevating Pollen ROS Levels and Impacts Seed Development

Previous QTL-seq studies have identified OsLEML2 as a key candidate gene associated with spikelet fertility under heat stress (Nubankoh et al. 2020). This study further reveals that after prolonged heat stress during the reproductive stage, OsLEML2 overexpression lines exhibit significantly lower pollen viability than WT, while antisense-expressing and knockout lines show significantly higher pollen viability compared to WT (Fig. 4a). Simultaneously, the expression level of OsLEML2 is positively correlated with ROS levels in pollen (Fig. 4b, c), indicating that OsLEML2 enhances ROS accumulation in pollen under heat stress, thereby reducing rice pollen thermotolerance.

In this study, seeds from OsLEML2 overexpression line oe-16 and antisense-expressing lines were observed to be longer than WT, with antisense-expressing line ax-5 showing significantly greater seed width compared to WT (Fig. 10). Reportedly, higher auxin content can increase rice grain length (Yamamoto et al. 2007). Through statistical analysis of panicle-stage ambient temperature, we found OsLEML2 transgenic lines experienced a high-temperature period during panicle development (Fig. S7). Since auxin biosynthesis genes were upregulated and catabolism genes downregulated in OsLEML2 antisense lines after heat stress (Fig. 7), it is speculated that OsLEML2 affects rice seed development, and its antisense lines may accumulate higher auxin levels in grains, thereby increasing grain length. It has been reported that excessive ROS accumulation in rice anthers under heat stress induces premature PCD and degradation of tapetal cells, leading to significant reductions in pollen viability and spikelet fertility (Zhao et al. 2018). Given that this gene is highly expressed in anthers and upregulates ROS levels and reduces pollen viability under heat stress, these findings imply that OsLEML2 may also influence seed development by regulating ROS homeostasis during anther development. Interestingly, the grain length of the OsLEML2 overexpression line oe-16 is significantly greater than that of the wild type, and the1000-grain weight of the OsLEML2 antisense-expressing lines is significantly lower than that of the wild type. It is speculated that OsLEML2 also affects grain filling, and the underlying mechanism merits further investigation in the future.

OsLEML2 Negatively Regulates the HSF-HSP Cascade via the MAPK Signaling Pathway and Disrupts ROS Homeostasis

HSFs are pivotal components of the complex transcriptional regulatory network governing plant heat stress responses. They orchestrate transcriptional cascades to activate downstream thermotolerance genes, including HSPs, ROS-scavenging enzymes, and stress-responsive transcription factors (Gong et al. 2020). Under heat stress, HSPs function as molecular chaperones to maintain proteostasis and mitigate oxidative damage, thereby enhancing plant survival (Scharf et al. 2012). In this study, OsLEML2 overexpression seedlings exhibited reduced thermotolerance compared to WT (Fig. 3a), with most HSF and HSP genes showing significantly lower expression in overexpression lines and partially higher expression in antisense-expressing lines relative to WT (Fig. 5). These findings demonstrate that OsLEML2 suppresses the HSF-HSP signaling cascade.

The activity of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD), levels of membrane lipid peroxidation products (MDA), and stress markers (proline, chlorophyll) reflect the severity of abiotic stress-induced cellular damage (Szabados and Savouré 2010; Zhang et al. 2022; Moore et al. 2021). Physiological assays confirmed that overexpression lines accumulated the highest MDA content and exhibited the lowest POD activity, proline, and chlorophyll levels post-heat stress, while antisense-expressing lines displayed the highest SOD activity (Fig. 3c–i).

Following high-temperature stress, thermotolerance in OsLEML2 antisense-expressing lines showed no significant difference compared to wild-type. We speculate that while antisense RNA technology can reduce gene expression levels, it cannot achieve complete loss-of-function. Although the suppression of OsLEML2 partially derepresses its inhibition of the HSF-HSP pathway, the overall enhancement of thermotolerance may still be constrained by other unidentified nodes within the network or physiological limiting factors. Conversely, overexpression of OsLEML2 likely exerts a more direct and potent suppression on the core HSF-HSP defensive cascade (Fig. 5) and disrupts ROS homeostasis (Fig. 3, 4), resulting in more severe phenotypic defects. Additionally, the basal expression of OsLEML2 in wild-type plants exhibits tissue specificity (Fig. 1a). Antisense suppression may reduce its expression in anthers, with an even weaker impact in vegetative tissues (e.g., seedling leaves). This is consistent with the non-significant differences observed between antisense lines and WT in seedling-stage survival rates, ROS levels, and other metrics (Fig. 3b, c).

Notably, crosstalk between HSP/HSF and MAPK signaling pathways has been reported under heat stress (Yu et al. 2019), For instance, MPK3 and MPK6 phosphorylate HSFA4A at three residues, with Ser-309 identified as the primary phosphorylation site. Activation of MPK3/MPK6 induces expression of the HSP17.6A gene, while mutation of Ser-309 to Ala drastically reduces HSFA4A phosphorylation and HSP17.6A transcription (Perez-Salamo et al. 2014). GSEA revealed significant suppression of the MAPK pathway in overexpression lines and activation in knockout lines (Fig. 8a, b). Collectively, we propose that OsLEML2 inhibits MAPK pathway activity, attenuating HSF transcriptional activation and subsequent HSP expression. This cascade suppression leads to unfolded protein accumulation, impaired ROS scavenging, disrupted ROS homeostasis, aggravated oxidative injury, and ultimately, cell death under heat stress.

OsLEML2 Regulates Seedling Thermotolerance by Modulating the Auxin Metabolic Pathway

Studies indicate that plants undergo morphological and structural adaptations to elevated temperatures, a process in which auxin plays a pivotal role (Küpers et al. 2020). Reduced endogenous auxin levels impair thermotolerance in rice (Chen et al. 2024). In this study, following high-temperature treatment, OsLEML2 overexpression lines exhibit downregulated auxin biosynthetic genes and upregulated catabolic genes (Fig. 7), contributing to reduced endogenous auxin levels. Consequently, the seedling survival rate of OsLEML2 overexpression plants is significantly lower than WT under high-temperature stress (Fig. 3a), and exogenous auxin application failed to rescue this phenotype (Fig. 6a, c). Conversely, antisense-expressing seedlings display marginally higher survival rates than WT after heat stress. This phenotype likely results from sustained higher expression of auxin biosynthetic genes in antisense-expressing OsLEML2 seedlings under thermal stress, while auxin catabolic gene expression remains statistically indistinguishable from WT (Fig. 7). Consequently, antisense lines potentially accumulate elevated auxin levels, thereby mitigating heat-induced damage. These collective data suggest that OsLEML2 affects the auxin metabolic pathway.

It is noteworthy that auxin regulation exhibits a concentration-dependent effect (Berkel et al. 2013). Moderate auxin levels facilitate plant coping with heat stress, whereas exogenous auxin application at excessively high concentrations compromises plant thermotolerance. According to the established literature, elevated background auxin concentrations attenuate the defensive response of cotton anthers to high temperature (Min et al. 2014). Furthermore, excessive auxin concentrations under heat stress induce excessive elongation of plant height in wheat, thereby leading to leaf tip desiccation, water loss, and increased leaf senescence area, which detrimentally affects normal wheat growth (He 2021). In the present study, exogenous auxin application under high-temperature conditions, due to excessive auxin concentrations, resulted in a significant decline in the seedling survival rate of antisense-expressing plants (Fig. 7).

The mechanism by which OsLEML2 regulates rice thermotolerance through auxin metabolism merits further investigation. Subsequent studies could include: (1) performing auxin inhibitor treatment assays to verify whether the phenotype of OsLEML2 antisense lines can be rescued; (2) applying exogenous auxin at varying concentrations under heat stress to observe the thermotolerant phenotypes of transgenic lines at both seedling and heading stages; and (3) examining the expression levels of related genes. These experiments would help elucidate how OsLEML2 modulates heat tolerance via auxin metabolism.

OsLEML2 Regulates Cellular Auxin Homeostasis via SNARE-Mediated Vesicular Trafficking to Modulate Thermotolerance in Rice

It has been reported that the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter LEM1, expressed in yeast, selectively modulates intracellular steroid hormone levels (Kralli et al. 1995). In accordance with the preceding discussion, we further hypothesize that OsLEML2 may be involved in maintaining intracellular auxin homeostasis. The OsVAMP727-incorporated SNARE complex mediates membrane fusion between dense vesicles (DVs) and protein storage vacuoles (PSVs) (Yulong et al. 2020). OsLEML2 co-localizes with OsVAMP727 in vesicular structures (Fig. 2c–e) and exhibits high-temperature-dependent and auxin-dependent protein condensation (Fig. 2f, g), This observation implies their potential functional synergy in SNARE-mediated membrane fusion through liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), thereby modulating intracellular auxin levels. Based on established reports, the R-SNAREs VAMP721 and VAMP722 facilitate post-Golgi trafficking of auxin transporters from trans-Golgi network (TGN) subdomains to the plasma membrane (PM) (Zhang et al. 2021). These findings suggest that OsLEML2 may promote the efflux of excess auxin or sequester it into the central vacuole, thereby maintaining intracellular auxin homeostasis and regulating rice seedling thermotolerance, by mediating vesicle formation and trafficking as well as modulating auxin homeostasis.
