Contouring and dose extraction of the maxilla, mandible and teeth on head and neck cancer radiotherapy planning scans
Authors: Emma C. Foster-Thomas, Koen B. Krommenhoek, Shermaine Pan, Craig W. Barclay, Angela Davey, Lucy S.C. Davies, Peter Sitch, SMILE Consortium, Thomas Melichar, Marinka L.F. Hol, Marianne Aznar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeDespite their vulnerability to radiation-induced damage, the maxilla and dentition are not routinely contoured in head and neck (HN) radiotherapy (RT), preventing accurate dose assessment and establishment of dose-effect relationships. This literature review aimed to: (1) identify published dentofacial contouring approaches, (2) synthesise current methods, and (3) where feasible, compare practices in paediatric and adult RT.Materials and MethodsMEDLINE and Embase were searched for studies that contoured the maxilla, mandible, or teeth in HN RT. Reference lists were manually screened.ResultsThirty-one (2003–2025) papers met inclusion criteria. Manual contouring predominated (77.4%), with substantial heterogeneity across all structures. Only seven studies (22.6%) contoured the maxilla. Tooth contouring varied from individual segmentation to variable groupings. Eight studies (25.8%) reported dental professional involvement.ConclusionDentofacial contouring lacks standardisation and predominantly relies on manual techniques. This variability impedes dose assessment and cross-study comparisons. Standardised international guidelines for the contouring of the maxilla, mandible and teeth are necessary to ensure uniformity and enable comparability between studies.

Introduction

Childhood head and neck (HN) cancer is rare, with a global frequency of 0.25–15% of all cancer cases in children [1]. Overall survival rates for childhood cancers exceeds 80% (5-year survival rate) in high-income countries [2]. Treatment often requires intensive multimodality therapy including radiotherapy (RT), which can result in adverse effects (AE) [3]. With an increasing population of childhood cancer survivors (CCS), there is a need for long-term monitoring and greater understanding of dose-effect relationships.

Selected anatomical structures in the HN region, referred to as ‘organs at risk’ (OAR), are routinely contoured (“delineated”) on computed tomography (CT) scans for RT planning. Consensus guidelines recommend contouring a minimum of 25 OAR in the HN [4]. Apart from the mandible, these guidelines do not include dentofacial structures. This is recognised as a demanding task that requires clinical expertise and time [5]. Planning and assessment of modern RT techniques which allow for better tissue sparing, rely on consistent tumour and OAR contouring [4].

Disturbances in tooth and jaw development following RT are well supported [6], [7]. To report on possible dose-effect relationships, one needs to contour these structures. However, consensus is lacking on how to contour the maxilla and the teeth, which hampers the comparison of published dose-volume effect relationships. This was identified as an area of unmet clinical need in a survey assessing current practice and future developments in dentofacial structure contouring [8]. This survey was distributed amongst paediatric radiation oncology centres in the UK, Europe and Oceania. Only 56% of the responders from 52 centres routinely contoured one or more dentofacial bone. Radiation dose to the maxilla and mandible was currently only assessed in 15% and 48% respectively, with most adopting an as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA) approach, due to the absence of published dose thresholds for such structures [8]. Furthermore, only 25% of centres assessed dose to the teeth (dentition type not specified), with 4 centres applying a < 20 Gy or < 10 Gy dose constraint and 8 centres aiming for ALARA doses. This applied dose constraint appears to be based on the findings of Kaste et al [9].

To support treatment planning and AE risk education, dentists, reconstructive surgeons, and oral and maxillofacial surgeons often need to request information on the radiation doses delivered to the jaws. Depending on the contouring approach adopted, this data can be limited in providing the actual dose delivered to a specific region of the maxilla and/or mandible [10]. Although contouring the entire structure may serve as a crude surrogate to assess dental dosimetry, teeth exist and develop as independent units and radiation dose to adjacent teeth can vary. Although the dentition (“tooth bearing area”) is recognised as an OAR necessitating careful management and preventive strategies, individual or groups of teeth are not routinely contoured. Thus, it is not possible to routinely extract tooth-level dose metrics and establish dose-effect relationships.

To further understand adopted practices, this narrative literature review will provide a comprehensive summary of approaches to contouring and dosimetric extraction for the maxilla, mandible and teeth in both paediatric and adult oncology. The objectives are as follows: to identify and summarise all published work on contouring approaches for these dentofacial structures and to identify if alternative approaches to their contouring are adopted in paediatric and adult HNRT planning.

Methods

The primary research question that this narrative literature review aims to address is as follows: ‘What approaches to contouring of the maxilla, mandible and teeth are being adopted to determine radiation dose in HN cancer patients?’. The population for the review was paediatric and adult HN cancer patients who have had any radical RT dose regimen. Publications describing permanent teeth were only included to ensure sufficient studies for comparison with mixed dentition publications. The intervention of interest was maxillary, mandibular and/or tooth contouring on RT planning CT scans. Although the primary focus was on CT scans, papers that contoured these dentofacial structures on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were considered. No restrictions on contouring approach were placed. The outcomes were focused on radiation dose estimation and extraction for the maxilla, mandible and teeth in primary, mixed and permanent dentitions. Articles published in English from any country or geographic region were included, with no restrictions placed on study setting or approach to extracting the radiation dose statistics. No restrictions on publication date or type were applied.

The literature search was performed by one author (EFT) in MEDLINE and Embase (via Ovid) from earliest publications in the database to November 2025. The search strategy targeted the disease: “cancer in the head and neck area”, population “children and adults”, intervention: “radiotherapy” and outcome “delineation” (search strategy outlined in supplementary material Table 1). Manual reference searching was conducted. Title and abstract screening was performed (EFT, MA), followed by data extraction (EFT, KK). The identified articles were published between 2003 and 2025; no earlier publications were deemed eligible. Sample sizes varied, with just 1 subject in both Foster-Thomas et al [11]. and Sijtsema et al [12]. to 244 subjects in the article by Tsai et al [13]. The articles originate from various regions: Northern America (n = 12), Europe (n = 7), South America (n = 4), East Asia (n = 3) and the United Kingdom (n = 5). Subjects were treated with a variety of RT techniques, including 3D conformal (3D-CRT), intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), proton therapy (PT), stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) and volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT). The type of RT delivered was specified in all but seven studies [10], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]. A manual approach to contouring was predominantly adopted (n = 24) in the articles (Supplemental Table 2), with two [12], [20] of these utilising auto-contouring methods for the mandible and a manual approach for the teeth. One article [19] used manually delineated CT scans to test the efficiency and accuracy of a developed automated method that adopted an encoder-decoder three-dimensional U-Net structure. The contouring of dentofacial structures, where specified (n = 24), was mainly undertaken by radiation oncologists. Of the 29 articles that delineated teeth, input from a dental professional was sought in eight [11], [19], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26].

Contouring of the mandible

The mandible is a routinely contoured OAR on HNRT planning scans. Brouwer et al [4]. defined a consensus guideline for delineating the mandible for RT planning in adult patients. However, there remains no consensus for contouring developing mandibles in paediatric patients [27]. Fourteen articles state that the mandibles of study participants were contoured. Technical clarification, for example, specification of anatomical landmarks or referring to previously published work or guidelines, was provided in nine articles [10], [11], [13], [14], [16], [17], [19], [28], [29]. Although there is some overlap of anatomical landmarks, a variety in the anatomical boundaries used is evident. Mandibular dose statistics were not extracted in three of these articles, which focused on development of a contouring atlas [10] and segmentation tools [14], [19]. Of the two articles focused on paediatric patients, the mandible was contoured in one [11].

With the aim of improving communication between dentists and radiation oncologists, Rouers et al [10]. proposed delineating the mandible by identifying easily identifiable landmarks. This article also described mandibular contouring in a patient without teeth, known as edentulous. This can be more challenging owing to the absence of teeth as a supporting landmark and loss of the alveolar ridge height. Melerowitz et al [19]. developed a deep-learning auto-segmentation model to overcome the inconsistency and time demands that manual segmentation can present. Within this, the mandible is divided into eight segments (left and right front, molar, ramus and condyles). Cranial, caudal, dorsal, lateral, medial and anterior boundaries are provided for the majority of these segments, with a graphical colour-coded model and axial cross-sectional CT images, which provide further clarity.

Contouring of the maxilla

Contouring of the maxilla was conducted in seven articles [10], [11], [14], [18], [19], [26], [30] Of these, the technical approach is detailed in all but two [18], [26]. Van Dijk et al [14]. refers to using “consensus-based delineation guidelines” [4], [10] to support the manual contouring of both the maxilla and mandible. Although Rouers et al [10]. states key landmarks to be identified in the maxilla, it is focused on delineating both the maxilla and mandible into sextants, for example the “central superior sextant”.

Foster-Thomas et al [11]. defines the cranial, caudal, posterior and lateral limits of the maxilla used for contouring purposes. Polce et al [30]. describes retrospectively contouring “each third of the maxilla” and from the provided figures, it could be interpreted that the anterior and lateral portions were demarcated in the canine region. Melerowitz et al [19]. segments the maxilla into four segments, specifically left and right front and molar regions. The authors detail and define the segment boundaries. For example, the cranial boundary is the base of the anterior nasal spine and base of the maxillary sinus in the molar region. Similar to Foster-Thomas et al [11]. and Polce et al [30], the anterior and lateral maxilla were demarcated in the canine region, i.e. along the distal margin of the maxillary canine tooth. Thariat et al [18]. describes the laborious efforts of contouring this structure amongst others, but does not provide contouring specifics. Although not expressly detailed, Parahyba et al [26]. present a single axial CT slice of the delineated maxilla segmented into three regions.

A response providing further explanation was received for two, namely Kovarik et al [22]. and Rouers et al [10]. The dentition contoured was specified in the majority of articles (n = 25). Predominantly the articles delineated subjects in the permanent dentition (n = 22), which is usually complete (excluding the third molars) by 16 years [34]. Fang et al [35]. developed and evaluated a dental contouring atlas, in which five radiation oncology clinicians individually contoured primary and permanent teeth on eight selected patients. The patient cohort was carefully selected to include a mix of primary and permanent teeth, present and absent third molars and some anatomical deviations. Thompson et al [36]. contoured teeth in a cohort with primary, mixed and permanent dentitions. Whereas, Foster-Thomas et al [11]. contoured teeth in sextants within the surrounding bone of a single patient in the mixed dentition. Of the 27 articles that reported dental dose statistics, all but one [22] reported a mean dose.

Teeth were contoured individually to some extent in 20 articles and into groups in 13 articles. Some articles describe contouring the teeth within bone, as evidenced in Figs. 3B and 3C [28] [11]. The crowns of individual teeth were contoured exclusively in three articles [21], [25], [37], then subsequently grouped to extract radiation dose. Bak et al [38]. appears to initially contour individual teeth, then group the premolars and anterior teeth (without midline separation) for dosimetry extraction. A similar approach was adopted by Yang et al [39]. in that teeth were individually contoured and then divided into sextants for dosimetry purposes.

Alberga et al [41]. translated RT plans to dental maps, which is a symbolic representation of the radiation dose on the permanent dental arch. Each number in the dental map represents two adjacent teeth and relates to a dose calculation for a cylindrical shape of 5 mm in diameter and 6 mm in height. This process doesn’t claim to precisely contour teeth, rather it is focused on anatomical areas of where two adjacent teeth would reside on an ‘ideal’ dental arch.

Okamoto et al [42]. explored the use of panoramic reconstructed CT images to present the RT dose delivered to tooth structures. Digital Imaging and Communication in Medicine (DICOM) RT datasets that included the CT image, structure, dose and plan data, were imported into an open-source platform called 3D Slicer. This programme allowed contouring of the ‘dental arch curve’. To verify the anticipated dose distributions, a radiation oncologist and medical physicist used this software to outline mandibular teeth (crowns and roots).

Discussion

Summary of findings

Manual OAR contouring was predominantly used, with some auto-contouring methods for the teeth and mandible which were trained and developed in adults. Current mandibular contouring methods lack standardisation. While many studies contour the mandible from alveolar crest to inferior cortex using teeth as anterior-posterior boundaries, only five [10], [11], [14], [16], [17] include posterior structures (rami, coronoid, condylar processes) and just three studies [10], [17], [19] provide detailed anatomical landmarks for the entire mandible. Only Rouers et al [10]. addresses contouring in edentulous cases.

The maxilla is not routinely contoured as an OAR in HN RT planning, with only 30% of surveyed centres including it in their clinical practice protocols [8]. While some guidelines [27] define hard palate boundaries, consensus on anatomical landmarks for complete maxillary contouring is still in development.

The exact methodological approach adopted to contouring individual or groups of teeth was predominantly unclear; specifically, the CT slice thicknesses and whether a standard window / Hounsfield unit was selected for all contours. Notably, Alberga et al [41]. acknowledged a significant limitation in their dosimetric analysis, whereby exact dose values for lateral incisors and first premolars were not directly measured but rather extrapolated from adjacent structures with the highest recorded radiation values. This assumption-based approach may introduce systematic errors in dose reporting and limit the clinical applicability. While Thariat et al [18]. provided detailed specifications for bone window settings used (800–2500 Hounsfield units), other studies failed to report these technical parameters. This could raise concerns about the reproducibility and comparability of results across different institutions and imaging protocols.

Only two studies [11], [36] contoured images of patients in the mixed dentitions. Identification of teeth on CT scans in children as young as one year old has been demonstrated [35]. The degree of enamel calcification, referring to mineral deposition in dental enamel, directly corresponds to the degree of radiopacity. Fang et al [35]. report a general increase in contouring accuracy as teeth mature with subject age. Despite this, it can be assumed that the number of dental artefacts from restorations, which add further complexity to contouring, is less in younger children compared to their older counterparts. Considering the small number of articles focused on paediatric patients, any comparisons between adult and paediatric RT are limited.

Teeth were individually contoured in 20 articles. Some authors alluded to the time demands of this. This was also confirmed in communications with authors, such as Kovarik et al [22]. Owing to time demands, Thariat et al [43]. investigated an automatic atlas-based segmentation framework to provide individual tooth dose estimates. The hope was to use this approach routinely in practice to improve communication between radiation oncologists and dentists, instead of conducting visual dose estimates, which can potentially provide inaccurate risk-level data. Aside from the physical time required to manually contour a tooth, one must also acknowledge the quantity of data that this produces and whether this is required to meet clinical and/or study objectives. Thus, the grouping of teeth for contouring and/or dosimetric extraction purposes, as depicted in Fig. 3D may be more appropriate. For example, if a dentist is planning dental implants in an irradiated mandible in the canine regions, the dose that these teeth and surrounding teeth received is required. Being provided the dose for the left and right anterior mandible and premolar regions should be appropriate to enable decision making. Whereas, when focused on dose-effect relationships for disturbances in tooth development, individual tooth contouring may be indicated, such as that shown in Fig. 3A.

Reliable contouring of dentofacial structures is essential for dose minimisation (where possible) during treatment planning and optimisation, dose reporting and patient information. However, contouring in current clinical practice is limited, mostly due to lack of time and lack of guidance [8]. This review confirms the need for contouring guidelines for these complex structures. In parallel, automatic solutions are emerging, but their usefulness for RT is challenging to evaluate without a consensus on structure definition. Several approaches have been described, ranging from in-house deep learning models to tools capable of individual tooth segmentation. TotalSegmentator2, an open-access deep-learning based tool, supports fully automated and reproducible contouring of individual teeth using FDI notation, trained on the ToothFairy2 CBCT dataset annotated by maxillofacial experts [44], [45]. Another tool, IMPACT (Image-based Mandibular and Maxillary Parcellation and Annotation using Computed Tomography) has been proposed as a framework specifically developed for radiotherapy planning CT, aligning tooth and jaw sub-volume definitions with the ClinRad osteoradionecrosis staging system [46]. However, both tools were developed in predominantly adult cohorts, and neither of them use RT-specific contouring guidelines, thus raising questions about their performance in the primary and mixed dentitions, which remain underrepresented in training datasets. The study by Okamoto et al [42]. although not yet fully automated, shows promise in advancing communication of dose distributions between oncology and dentistry.

Although MRI-based contouring was not excluded in the search, CT scans are routinely required for RT planning and are superior for tooth identification, particularly in children with mixed dentitions [36]. MRI has limitations in visualising calcified structures like teeth, due to their high mineral content. Therefore, the included papers predominantly focus on CT-based contouring.

As shown in Table 1, a variety of physicians contoured the CT scans assessed in the included articles. Contouring was performed primarily by radiation oncologists, with input from dental professionals in 8 articles. Dentists, “dental oncologists” and dental hygienists completed the contouring, either independently or alongside a radiation oncologist, medical physicist or dosimetrist. The title ‘dental oncologist’ is referenced in Fregnani et al [21]. and Morais-Faria et al [25]. This is becoming an increasingly recognised term internationally and refers to a dentist who specialises in the dental sequelae of cancer [47]. Whilst it is not feasible for a dental professional to routinely conduct or support contouring, it is sensible to propose that clear guidelines for dental structures should be developed to support routine treatment planning.

Strengths and limitations

This paper synthesises the limited research available at the intersection of paediatric oncology and dentistry. It addresses key shortcomings in the field, including the lack of standardisation, the scarcity of paediatric-specific contouring data, and the inherent complexity of craniofacial contouring, and provides concrete recommendations for future research and reporting. As with any literature review, however, the conclusions are bound by the available evidence, and several limitations should be acknowledged.

As this is a literature review, no formal quality assessment of the included studies was performed. Similarly, no statistical analyses were conducted, as the heterogeneity of the included studies precluded meaningful data pooling. Nevertheless, the methodological strengths and limitations of each study were carefully considered. The included studies varied considerably in study design, sample size, and follow-up duration, which limited cross-study comparability. Automated contouring tools were discussed descriptively rather than quantitatively, without review of performance metrics or clinical acceptance thresholds, largely due to the limited and underreported evidence currently available.

Implications for practice and research

A lack of universally accepted methods for contouring of the maxilla, mandible and dentition in the paediatric population specifically, suggests a necessity for standardised guidelines. This may promote consistency in contouring approaches, aiding dentofacial dose-effect research and future automated contouring tool development. Enhanced collaboration between dental professionals and radiation oncologists, as advocated by Rouers et al [10]. and others, is particularly critical for complex cases including paediatric and edentulous patients. While automated contouring shows promise for reducing variability, these methods require further validation before clinical implementation. Further evaluation of automated solutions for contouring of primary, mixed and permanent dentitions, may allow deep-learning models to be used in routine clinical practice to support dental dose avoidance. Development of approaches to present dose distributions on panoramic reconstructed CT images may offer an accessible visual representation of dose to the teeth and jaws on a single plane.

Contouring recommendations

Establishment of an international consensus for contouring the maxilla and teeth, with clear anatomical landmarks and boundaries will lead to more uniformity and enable comparability in clinical and research works. Given that international consensus guidelines exist for contouring the mandible in adult RT [4], it would be beneficial to extend this to paediatric RT. Considering the complexity of dental anatomy, standardised tooth contouring guidelines are needed to enable accurate contouring in routine practice. There remains insufficient evidence on the benefit of delineating teeth individually, in groups or as an entire ‘dentition’ structure. For comprehensive RT treatment planning, it is beneficial to delineate the entire tooth, from the crown to root apex in a fully developed tooth or root terminus (in a developing tooth). In select cases, for example, children in the mixed dentition or cases where complex dental interventions are planned, consultation with a dental professional may be beneficial.

Radiation oncologists are not expected to have knowledge of the primary or mixed dentitions, therefore, approaching teeth in groups in paediatric cases specifically appears sensible. For example, four groups in each jaw comprising of anterior/posterior teeth demarcated in the midline. Contouring the surrounding bone is important as the developing tooth buds will be exposed to RT.

Currently, there is insufficient evidence to support gold standard recommendations for contouring the teeth. It is, however, vital that published data sets conform to rigorous reporting standards, to allow result comparison and/or pooling. Conclusion

Standardised international contouring guidelines for the maxilla and teeth are necessary to ensure uniformity and enable comparability between studies. Automation and improved visualisation techniques may further optimise clinical application. Compared to permanent tooth contouring, there is still much to learn in the contouring of the primary and mixed dentitions. Since contouring in the mixed dentition is scarce in the literature and understandably more challenging than in a permanent dentition, clinicians should allocate additional time and be cognisant of the difficulties inherent in contouring developing teeth.

Establishment of a standardised approach to sharing dose distributions with clinicians such as dentists will streamline post-operative care and support treatment planning decisions in relation to these structures.
