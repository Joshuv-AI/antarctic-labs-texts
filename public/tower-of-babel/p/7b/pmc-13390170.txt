Prevalence and risk of cardiovascular events among people living with HIV: a meta-analysis
Authors: Shuqing Jin, Hongfei Liu, Xiyuan Song, Qian Yu, Jingyu Yue, Liran Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople living with HIV (PLWH) have increased cardiovascular risk, but the contribution of HIV-related factors to all-cause cardiovascular events remains unclear. We assessed the prevalence and determinants of cardiovascular events in PLWH, including subtype-specific risk profiles.MethodsPubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Web of Science and Scopus were searched from January 1, 2004 to October 31, 2024. Observational studies reporting cardiovascular event prevalence or adjusted risk estimates in PLWH were included. Two reviewers independently selected studies and extracted data. Pooled relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using fixed- or random-effects models, and heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q and I2 statistics.ResultsForty-three studies including 4,175,459 PLWH and 166,225 cardiovascular events were analyzed. The pooled prevalence of cardiovascular events was 6% (95% CI: 0.05–0.07). Higher risk was associated with older age, smoking, antiretroviral therapy, male sex, CD4+ T-cell count <200 cells/mm3, detectable HIV viral load, dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, syphilis coinfection, prior cardiovascular disease and elevated red cell distribution width. Higher CD4+ counts (per 100 cells/mm3) and Hispanic ethnicity were associated with lower risk. Subgroup analyses showed that smoking, antiretroviral therapy and dyslipidemia were linked to myocardial infarction; prior cardiovascular disease and CD4+ T-cell count <200 cells/mm3 to arrhythmia; and male sex, dyslipidemia, hypertension and syphilis coinfection to stroke.ConclusionPLWH have a substantial cardiovascular event burden driven by both traditional and HIV-related risk factors. Early cardiovascular risk assessment, viral suppression and immunological recovery should be prioritized.

Introduction

Acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS), caused by human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, is characterized by immune dysfunction and opportunistic infections. As a newly emerging, severe, complex, and highly fatal infectious disease over the past four decades, it has persisted as a major global public health concern, affecting approximately 39.9 million individuals worldwide [1]. Recent studies indicate that people living with HIV (PLWH) exhibit significantly elevated risks of cardiovascular disease (CVD) compared to the general population, encompassing both incident and recurrent events [2]. This heightened risk has been postulated to associate with antiretroviral therapy (ART), vascular inflammation, and other contributing factors including chronic immune activation and mitochondrial dysfunction [3,4]. In summary, the relationship between cardiovascular events and HIV infection constitutes a multifactorial phenomenon, and elucidating CVD risks in PLWH could provide critical insights for clinical management. A recent meta-analysis by San Zhu et al. revealed higher prevalence of traditional CVD risk factors, such as hypertension and dyslipidemia, among PLWH. However, this study did not comprehensively analyze risk factors for all-cause cardiovascular events in PLWH [5]. Do conventional CVD risk factors align with those observed in HIV-associated cardiovascular disease? Do HIV-specific factors influence the pathogenesis and progression of CVD in PLWH? To address these unresolved questions, we conducted a meta-analysis to determine the prevalence and risk factors of all-cause cardiovascular events in PLWH, with subgroup analyses stratified by CVD subtypes, thereby providing evidence-based strategies for clinical prevention and intervention.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses [6]. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (URL: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?ID=CRD42023444468).

Search strategy

The search period spanned from January 1, 2004, to October 31, 2024. We performed unrestricted searches across PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. The search strategy incorporated both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords. To minimize study omission, we manually screened references from included studies and existing meta-analyses. Detailed search strategies are provided in Appendix A. The complete search strings for each database, including MeSH terms (e.g. ‘HIV’ AND ‘Cardiovascular Diseases’) and free-text keywords (e.g. ‘stroke’ OR ‘myocardial infarction’), are listed in Appendix A. Only English-language publications were considered to ensure consistency in terminology interpretation. No additional filters (e.g. by study design) were applied to maximize sensitivity.

Eligibility criteria

Studies meeting the following criteria were included: (1) cross-sectional, cohort, or case–control designs; (2) exposure groups comprising PLWH with cardiovascular events, with control groups consisting of non-AIDS individuals with cardiovascular events; (3) reporting adjusted odds ratios (OR), relative risks (RR), or hazard ratios (HR) as risk estimates, as these adjusted measures mitigate confounding effects and observational biases.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they were: (1) conference abstracts; (2) duplicate publications from the same cohort were excluded to avoid double-counting of participants and potential bias in pooled estimates; (3) contained incomplete data or lacked outcomes of interest; (4) evaluated potential cardiovascular risk factors (e.g. biomarkers) without measuring actual cardiovascular events; (5) reported only crude estimates without adjusting for essential confounders such as age and sex.

Study selection

Study selection was conducted using NoteExpress 3.8.0 for reference management. Two reviewers (Yu Qian and Liu Hongfei) independently screened titles/abstracts, followed by full-text review of eligible studies. Conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer (Xu Liran). Exclusions at full-text stage were documented (e.g. duplicate cohorts, incomplete outcomes). The PRISMA flowchart (Figure 1) summarizes the process.

Studies screening process.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (Jin Shuqing and Song Xiyuan) independently conducted data extraction using predefined forms in accordance with systematic review and meta-analysis guidelines. The extracted data, informed by prior high-quality meta-analyses [7,8], included the following elements: first author, publication year, country, sample size, study design, mean follow-up duration, outcomes, covariates incorporated in models, and adjustments for confounding factors.

Risk-of-bias assessment

Methodological quality was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOS) for cohort and case–control studies, which assesses three domains: selection, comparability, and outcome. The NOS scoring system ranges from 0 to 9, with higher scores indicating superior quality: ≥7 (high quality), 4–6 (moderate quality), and ≤3 (low quality). Cross-sectional studies were appraised using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) scale (0–11 points: 0–3 = low quality, 4–7 = moderate quality, 8–11 = high quality). Two investigators independently performed quality assessments, with discrepancies resolved through consensus-based discussions. Different tools were selected due to distinct methodological risks: NOS focuses on longitudinal study design (e.g. follow-up adequacy), while AHRQ addresses cross-sectional-specific biases (e.g. sampling representativeness).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Stata 15.0. Results were expressed as risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), selecting the most fully adjusted estimates from each study. For studies reporting hazard ratios (HR) instead of RR, we converted HR to RR using the formula: (RR = (1–eHR * ln (1–r))/r; r: cardiovascular event incidence [9]. When odds ratios (OR) were reported instead of RR, OR were interpreted as RR if cardiovascular event risk was <20%; otherwise, generic inverse variance was applied to calculate pooled RR. As the original trials exhibited cardiovascular event incidences below 20%, no conversion was required. Heterogeneity was assessed via Chi-square test I2 statistics, with substantial heterogeneity defined as p < 0.1 or I2>50%, warranting random-effects models; otherwise, fixed-effects models were used. Sensitivity analyses evaluated result robustness and heterogeneity sources. Subgroup analyses were conducted for cardiovascular events. Publication bias was assessed through Egger’s regression test. Sensitivity analyses employed the leave-one-out method by iteratively excluding individual studies to assess their influence on pooled estimates. Publication bias was evaluated via Egger’s regression test (for factors with ≥5 studies); if bias was detected (p < 0.1), the trim-and-fill method imputed hypothetical studies to adjust asymmetry. Conversions from HR/OR to RR were validated by comparing derived RR with directly reported RR in studies providing both metrics. Geographical region was recorded as a study-level characteristic but not included as a covariate in meta-regression models due to heterogeneous reporting standards across studies and insufficient granularity of regional data. Conversions were validated by comparing derived RR with directly reported RR in 12 studies that provided both metrics (mean absolute difference: 0.08, range 0.01–0.15). Small subgroups (n < 5) were excluded from pooled RR calculations due to instability risks, as confirmed by sensitivity analyses.

Results

Literature search

The initial search identified 6,691 records, reduced to 4,268 after duplicate removal. Following title/abstract screening, 4,124 records were excluded due to evident ineligibility. Full-text review of the remaining 144 articles resulted in 43 studies meeting inclusion criteria. The selection flowchart is presented in Figure 1.

Study characteristics

The 43 included studies, published between 2004 and 2024, comprised research from 7 countries across six continents (excluding Antarctica), with North America contributing the majority. The pooled cohort encompassed 4,175,459 people living with HIV (PLWH), ranging from 1,559,351 participants[10] to 186 [11], with 166,225 documented cardiovascular events. HIV diagnoses adhered to WHO classification standards, while cardiovascular events were defined using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) criteria. Key confounders including age, sex, and ethnicity were rigorously controlled. Study characteristics are detailed in Table 1.

Among observational designs, 21 cohort/case–control studies achieved NOS scores ≥7 (high quality), while 6 scored 4–6 (moderate quality). Cross-sectional studies demonstrated 6 high-quality (AHRQ 8–11) and 10 moderate-quality (AHRQ 4–7) evaluations. Prevalence and risk of cardiovascular events in people living with HIV

All included studies [10–51]investigated cardiovascular event prevalence among people living with HIV (PLWH). The pooled prevalence of cardiovascular events was 6% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.05–0.07) as shown in Figure 4. Figure 4 presents the geographical distribution of cardiovascular event prevalence among PLWH, with regional variations ranging from 1% (South America) to 22% (Oceania). Confidence intervals for all regional estimates are shown; the figure can be enlarged for detailed viewing.

Prevalence of cardiovascular event among people living with HIV.

Geographical distribution analysis revealed substantial regional variation, with Oceania (22%) and Africa (21%) demonstrating the highest prevalence rates, while South America reported the lowest rate (1%). While our analysis revealed significant geographical variation in CVD prevalence among PLWH, these estimates should be interpreted in context of regional differences in: (1) healthcare systems and ART access, (2) diagnostic criteria for cardiovascular events, (3) prevalence of traditional risk factors, and (4) genetic/socioeconomic population characteristics. The limited number of studies from certain regions (e.g. only 3 from South America) precluded formal subgroup analysis by geography. Future multinational studies should standardize data collection to better disentangle true biological variation from healthcare system artifacts.

Pooled analysis identified multiple risk determinants for cardiovascular events in PLWH: age (per 5-year increment), middle age (40–54 years), smoking, antiretroviral therapy (ART), male sex, CD4+ T-cell count <200 cells/mm³, detectable HIV viral load, dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, syphilis coinfection, History of cardiovascular disease, and elevated red cell distribution width (RDW). Protective factors included higher CD4+ T-cell counts (per 100 cells/mm³ increase) and Hispanic ethnicity (Table 2). Although Hispanic ethnicity was associated with lower CVD risk (RR 0.56, 95% CI 0.38–0.81), this association likely reflects structural factors (e.g. healthcare utilization patterns, cultural dietary habits) rather than biological protection. Race and ethnicity are social constructs shaped by systemic inequities; future studies should explicitly adjust for socioeconomic confounders (e.g. insurance status, neighborhood deprivation index).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses stratified by cardiovascular event subtypes revealed distinct risk profiles: smoking, antiretroviral therapy (ART) use, and dyslipidemia were significantly associated with myocardial infarction in people living with HIV (PLWH). Prior cardiovascular disease and CD4+ T-cell counts <200 cells/mm³ emerged as key determinants for arrhythmia. For stroke events, male sex, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and syphilis coinfection demonstrated robust associations, while diabetes unexpectedly demonstrated protective effects (Tables 3–5).

Interactions between subgroups

As shown in Table 6, subgroup analysis revealed that smoking and antiretroviral therapy (ART) significantly increased the risk of myocardial infarction (MI) among people living with HIV (PLWH), with relative risks (RRs) of 2.03 (95% CI: 1.48–2.78) and 1.54 (95% CI: 1.38–1.71), respectively. Hyperlipidemia was also strongly associated with higher MI risk (RR = 1.52; 95% CI: 1.38–1.68). For arrhythmia, male sex and CD4+ T-cell counts <200 cells/mm³ were significant risk factors, showing RRs of 2.00 (95% CI: 1.30–3.08) and 2.20 (95% CI: 1.47–3.28). Regarding stroke, male sex had an RR of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.55–1.05), hypertension yielded an RR of 1.71 (95% CI: 1.58–1.84), while syphilis coinfection demonstrated a substantially elevated RR of 3.01 (95% CI: 1.40–6.47). Conversely, diabetes exhibited a protective association against stroke (RR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.71–0.86). These findings indicate distinct risk profiles across cardiovascular events (MI, arrhythmia, and stroke), underscoring multifaceted cardiovascular risk interactions in PLWH and necessitating phenotype-specific clinical management strategies.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out approach identified key sources of heterogeneity in all-cause cardiovascular events. Removal of Boettiger DC’s study (primary source for age-related heterogeneity) maintained substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 92.9%, p = 0.000). Exclusion of Drozd DR and Patel UK’s studies (smoking-related heterogeneity) showed persistent moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 50.6%, p = 0.059). Dorjee K and Worm SW’s contributions to antiretroviral therapy heterogeneity demonstrated minimal residual heterogeneity upon exclusion (I2 = 3.7%, p = 0.404). Notably, elimination of Patel UK’s dual studies (gender-related heterogeneity) eliminated heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.982).

For dyslipidemia-related heterogeneity, exclusion of Boettiger DC and Drozd DR’s studies retained significant heterogeneity (I2 = 69.1%, p = 0.003). Mushin AS’s hypertension-associated studies showed sustained heterogeneity post-exclusion (I2=78.8%, p < 0.001). Al-Kindi SG’s RDW data removal resolved all heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, p = 0.745). Subgroup analyses revealed: Drozd DR’s study drove smoking-related heterogeneity in myocardial infarction (post-exclusion I2 = 56.9%, p = 0.073). Dorjee K’s antiretroviral therapy analysis for myocardial infarction showed markedly reduced heterogeneity (I2 = 17.6%, p = 0.295). Conversely, exclusion of Zhang L and Patel UK’s studies eliminated diabetes-related heterogeneity in stroke analysis (I2 = 0%, p = 0.927) (Table 7).

Discussion

This meta-analysis comprehensively evaluates the prevalence and risk determinants of all-cause cardiovascular events among people living with HIV (PLWH) through integrated subgroup analyses. Our pooled analysis revealed a cardiovascular event prevalence of 6% (95% CI: 0.05–0.07) in this population. Risk factor analysis demonstrated that conventional cardiovascular risk determinants—including advanced age, male sex, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and prior cardiovascular disease—persisted as significant contributors in PLWH. Additionally, syphilis and red blood cell distribution width (RDW) were identified as risk factors for all-cause cardiovascular event prevalence in people living with HIV (PLWH). HIV-specific risk factors including antiretroviral therapy (ART), CD4+ T cell counts <200 cells/mm³, and detectable HIV viral load were significantly associated with increased cardiovascular risk in PLWH. Conversely, higher CD4+ T cell counts (per 100 cells/mm³ increment) demonstrated a protective effect against cardiovascular event prevalence. Subgroup analysis revealed an unexpected inverse association between diabetes and stroke risk, though this finding warrants further investigation.

In recent years, several meta-analyses have been published on this topic [52–55]. These studies consistently demonstrate a substantially elevated cardiovascular disease burden among PLWH. Witchakorn Ruamtawee’s systematic review specifically examined cardiovascular disease prevalence among PLWH in the Asia-Pacific region [54]. Our comprehensive global analysis revealed elevated cardiovascular event prevalence in Oceania (22%, particularly Australia) and Africa (21%), findings consistent with prior regional studies that attribute this increased risk to higher baseline cardiovascular risk factor prevalence in these populations [56–59]. In contrast, the lowest prevalence was observed in South America (1%), likely associated with the limited number of included studies (n = 3) from this region.

Min Du’s [53]systematic review identified conventional CVD risk factors as primary contributors to CVD incidence in PLWH, while demonstrating no significant associations with HIV-specific parameters including abacavir/efavirenz use or nadir CD4 counts. Our meta-analysis delineates distinct mechanisms: CD4+ T-cell counts <200 cells/mm³ emerged as independent risk factors for both all-cause cardiovascular events and myocardial infarction in PLWH. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) and detectable HIV viral load were significantly associated with elevated cardiovascular risks, whereas incremental CD4+ T-cell counts (per 100 cells/mm³) demonstrated protective effects. These findings potentially reflect our focus on cardiovascular event prevalence rather than incidence rates in PLWH. The robust inverse correlation between immune competence (quantified by CD4+ levels) and cardiovascular risk underscores the clinical imperative for intensified monitoring of immunocompromised individuals (CD4+ <200 cells/mm³) in cardiovascular management. ART-associated cardiovascular risks were principally driven by abacavir regimens in our analysis. PLWH receiving abacavir exhibit an 83% higher risk of cardiovascular disease compared to those not receiving abacavir, consistent with prior research findings [60,61]. This increased risk is likely attributed to the close structural similarity between abacavir and endogenous purines (ATP, ADP, and AMP), which are key paracrine signaling molecules known to trigger prothrombotic and proinflammatory vascular pathways [62]. Sustained HIV viremia correlates with chronic inflammation, where elevated viral loads amplify endothelial dysfunction and accelerate atherogenesis—pathophysiological mechanisms that substantially elevate risks of acute cardiovascular events including myocardial infarction and stroke [63].

Traditional cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors persist among people living with HIV (PLWH). Age remains a prominent CVD risk factor in PLWH. While studies indicate significant CVD risk elevation at 50 years for women and 60 years for men in the general population [64], our meta-analysis reveals that PLWH aged 40–54 exhibit 2.75 times higher CVD prevalence compared to those under 39. This accelerated disease onset may partially explain the elevated CVD incidence in PLWH, underscoring the necessity for earlier cardiovascular monitoring in this population. Male PLWH demonstrate heightened risk for all-cause cardiovascular events, attributable to social stress, stigma, and smoking behaviors [5]. While Hispanic ethnicity was associated with lower CVD risk (RR 0.56, 95% CI 0.38–0.81), this association may reflect structural factors such as healthcare access, cultural dietary patterns, or genetic admixture rather than innate biological protection. Future studies should explicitly model socio-economic confounders (e.g. insurance status, neighborhood deprivation) to disentangle these effects. Hypertension and HIV infection synergistically amplify all-cause cardiovascular risk through enhanced inflammatory responses, arterial stiffness, and potentiation of conventional CVD risk factors. Dyslipidemia and diabetes mellitus not only promote atherogenesis but also induce chronic inflammation and activate inflammatory cascades that exacerbate CVD pathogenesis in PLWH [3]. These multifactorial interactions collectively accelerate both the onset and progression of cardiovascular complications in this population.

Atherosclerosis in both large arteries and small vessels constitutes the predominant etiology of ischemic stroke among HIV-infected patients. Neurosyphilis infection triggers extensive neurovascular inflammation, inducing endarteritis and a prothrombotic state. The coexistence of arterial wall inflammation and thrombotic predisposition elevates atherosclerosis risk, consequently predisposing to ischemic stroke [65]. Our study demonstrated a 3.01-fold increased stroke risk in HIV patients with neurosyphilis compared to their syphilis-free counterparts. Red cell distribution width (RDW) serves as an immunological activation marker, correlating with elevated levels of C-reactive protein, TNF-α, and IL-6. Inflammatory cytokines impair erythrocyte survival, induce erythropoietin resistance, and stimulate hepcidin production, collectively disrupting erythropoiesis and augmenting RDW [37]. Elevated RDW persisted as an independent predictor of cardiovascular events in HIV patients after adjusting for age, sex, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension. Subgroup analysis paradoxically identified diabetes as a risk factor for overall cardiovascular events but a protective factor against stroke. Previous studies have established diabetes as a stroke risk factor in HIV populations, mediated through chronic inflammation, myocardial remodeling, atrial fibrillation, and macrovascular atherosclerosis [65]. Diabetes was unexpectedly protective against stroke (RR = 0.78, p < 0.001),this subgroup comprised hospitalized HIV patients where glycemic control, inflammatory modulation, and opportunistic infection management in diabetic subjects were optimized. The observed stroke reduction likely reflects therapeutic efficacy of diabetes-associated anti-inflammatory and anti-opportunistic infection regimens, rather than intrinsic protective properties of diabetes itself. The finding may reflect optimized glycemic management in PLWH with diabetes, which could mitigate vascular inflammation [66] or the ability of metformin to attenuate atherosclerosis by reducing Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission in an AMPK-dependent manner [67]. Further studies are needed to validate these hypotheses.

This meta-analysis demonstrates enhanced methodological rigor through strict adherence to PRISMA 2020 guidelines and PROSPERO protocol registration (CRD42022324973). As the inaugural systematic evaluation of all-cause cardiovascular event epidemiology in people living with HIV (PLWH), it synthesizes 46 studies encompassing 4,175,459 participants. By exclusively analyzing multivariate-adjusted risk estimates, we effectively circumvented publication bias inherent in single-variable analyses, thereby strengthening evidence-based prevention frameworks. Three key limitations warrant consideration: 1) The 20-year publication window may introduce temporal confounding, 2) Broad cardiovascular endpoint classifications limited granular risk stratification. Only myocardial infarction, stroke, and arrhythmia achieved sufficient study numbers for subgroup analyses, precluding comprehensive assessment of phenotype-specific risk profiles. 3) The current analysis focuses on traditional and HIV-specific risk factors, future studies should explicitly incorporate socioeconomic confounders (such as health insurance status and community poverty levels) to disentangle the structural factors behind the protective association in the Hispanic ethnic group (RR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.38–0.81). This association is more likely to reflect social determinants rather than a biological protective effect.

Conclusions

People living with HIV (PLWH) demonstrate increased risk of cardiovascular events compared to seronegative populations, with conventional risk factors (hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking) exhibiting amplified effects in this cohort. Notably, PLWH experience accelerated cardiovascular risk profiles, manifesting clinically 10–15 years earlier than age-matched controls. HIV-specific pathomechanisms—including protease inhibitor-based antiretroviral therapy (ART), persistent viremia (HIV RNA >50 copies/mL), and CD4+ T-cell depletion (<200 cells/mm³)—synergistically potentiate endothelial dysfunction through chronic immune activation. Conversely, each 100 cells/mm³ increment in CD4+ counts confers 14% risk reduction (95% CI: 0.80–0.92)。These pathophysiological mechanisms involve chronic immune activation and persistent low-grade inflammation associated with HIV persistence. Clinical management strategies should prioritize early cardiovascular risk assessment in this population, with particular emphasis on optimizing immunological recovery through ART adherence and viral suppression. Targeted interventions to mitigate systemic inflammation, such as statin therapy in high-risk individuals, may synergistically reduce both cardiovascular morbidity and HIV-related complications. A paradigm shift toward integrated care models addressing both viral control and cardiometabolic health is imperative for improving long-term outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Although we provided geographic subgroup analyses, the generalizability of our findings may be influenced by disparities in healthcare systems, ART regimens, and diagnostic standards. For instance, Developed countries in Europe, North America and the United Kingdom have standardized ART protocols with routine lipid monitoring, whereas regions such as South Africa and India rely on more limited resources, which could lead to underreporting of dyslipidemia-related events. These factors highlight the need for region-specific validation studies.
