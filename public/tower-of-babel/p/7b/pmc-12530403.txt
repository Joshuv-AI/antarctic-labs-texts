Clinical characteristics and prognostic factors in adult macrophage activation syndrome: an observational study in a Chinese cohort
Authors: Yuanyuan Pei, Xiao Han, Fengtao Yang, Xi Wang, Lingjie Cao, Ranran Yao, Renge Liang, Wenfeng Huang, Jihong Zhu, Yin Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMacrophage activation syndrome (MAS), a subtype of secondary haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), is a rare and life-threatening complication of rheumatoid diseases. However, the prognostic factors in adult MAS patients are still not fully elucidated, which need further investigation.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of adult MAS inpatients between January 2016 and January 2024. In accordance with HLH-2004 criteria, 116 patients were eventually enrolled in this study. Clinical manifestations, laboratory data, treatments and outcomes had been recorded. Influence factors associated with prognosis were analysed using logistic regression models.ResultsSeventeen of 116 (14.7%) patients died during hospitalisation. Further multivariate analysis suggested that admission ferritin <1350 ng/mL (OR=5.387, 95% CI 1.203 to 24.127, p=0.034), platelets (PLT) ≤30×109/L (OR=5.387, 95% CI 1.203 to 24.127, p=0.028), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) ≥275 U/L (OR=5.732, 95% CI 1.167 to 28.167, p=0.032), central nervous system (CNS) involvement (OR=11.268, 95% CI 1.353 to 93.851, p=0.025) and procalcitonin (PCT) >0.9 ng/mL (OR=11.224, 95% CI 2.019 to 62.381, p=0.006) were potential prognostic factors for mortality of adult MAS patients.ConclusionOur results indicated that adult MAS patients with lower ferritin levels at admission, lower PLT counts, elevated ALT, CNS involvement and more active infection were associated with an unfavourable prognosis.

Introduction

Haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a rare, hyperinflammatory syndrome mediated by an unbridled and persistent activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer cells. It is characterised by persistent fever, cytopenia, elevated ferritin levels, coagulopathy, transaminitis, hypertriglyceridaemia and haemophagocytosis, which lead to a cytokine storm, multiple organ failure and unfavourable prognosis.13 HLH is traditionally classified into primary or familial HLH (due to an underlying genetic defect in immune function) and secondary HLH (sHLH).4 5

Macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) is commonly recognised as a form of sHLH that develops due to rheumatic diseases,3 6 which could be a deterioration of the underlying diseases or a complication aroused by infection or drugs. In adults, MAS is most prevalent in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)69 and adult-onset Still’s disease (AOSD), the adult equivalent of systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA).9 10 Other rheumatic diseases associated with adult MAS include dermatomyositis (DM),10 primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS)11 and rheumatoid arthritis.12 Despite increasing awareness, the precise incidence of MAS remains unknown. Limited literatures indicate that MAS may occur in 10%–40% of sJIA patients, 10%–25% of AOSD patients, and 0.9% to 9.0% of SLE patients.1315 MAS can present as the initial clinical manifestation, directly develop during the course of the disease or be triggered by other factors, most commonly infection.

As a life-threatening complication, MAS can progress to multiorgan failure and result in a high fatality rate if not promptly recognised. Previous studies suggest that early diagnosis followed by aggressive treatment can significantly reduce mortality and improve prognosis.6 16 Nevertheless, the clinical symptoms of MAS resemble those of active rheumatic diseases or severe infections, such as cytopenia, fever and hyperferritinaemia, which makes early recognition highly challenging. The diagnostic criteria and treatment regimens for MAS are primarily based on the paediatric research data, including the HLH-2004 criteria, MAS/sJIA criteria in 2016 and the MAS/sJIA (MS) score in 2019,1719 none of which has been adequately validated in MAS patients, particularly in adults. Thus, a more thorough and deeper understanding of the clinical characteristics and prognostic factors of MAS is needed. However, only a few studies with relatively small sample sizes have been reported for the rarity of MAS in rheumatic diseases.

Therefore, we conducted a bidirectional observational cohort study on adult MAS by collecting demographic data, clinical features, laboratory findings and initial treatments to identify the potential prognostic factors associated with in-hospital mortality. This study aims to facilitate early detection, enhance clinical decision-making and provide prompt therapies for adult MAS patients.

Methods

Study population

This bidirectional observational cohort study was conducted in the Peking University People’s Hospital, Beijing, China and recruited 121 consecutive patients diagnosed with MAS from January 2016 to January 2024 through the electronic medical record system, with 116 patients enrolled ultimately (online supplemental figure 1). To be included in the study, patients had to fulfil at least 5 of the HLH-2004 criteria17: (1) fever; (2) splenomegaly; (3) bicytopenia or pancytopenia (haemoglobin, Hgb <90 g/L, platelets, PLT <100×109/L, neutrophil <1.0×109/L); (4) triglyceride (TG) >3.0 mmol/L and/or fibrinogen (FIB) <1.5 g/L; (5) haemophagocytosis in the bone marrow, spleen or lymph nodes; (6) low or absent activity of natural killer (NK) cells; (7) ferritin ≥500 µg/L; and (8) soluble CD25 (sCD25) ≥2400 IU/mL or sCD25 ≥6400 pg/mL. Additionally, the exclusion criteria included (1) patients under 18 years old (two cases); (2) patients whose clinical data were incomplete (one case); (3) patients who were not enough to diagnose MAS after the inquiry of clinical data (two cases).

Clinical data collection

The baseline characteristics and the following comprehensive data were collected from the patients’ electronic medical records: (1) demographic data (gender, age); (2) composition of primary rheumatic diseases; (3) clinical manifestations including maximum temperature, emaciation, rash, purpura, arthrodynia, myodynia, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly and lymphadenectasis; (4) complications including cytopenia, acute liver injury (ALI), acute kidney injury (AKI), pancreatic involvement, gastrointestinal involvement, central nervous system (CNS) involvement, acute interstitial pneumonia, diffuse alveolar haemorrhage and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA); (5) laboratory data: haematological parameters including white blood cell (WBC), Hgb and PLT counts; coagulation function indicators including FIB, fibrin degradation products, prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) and D-dimer; liver function indexes including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (T-bil), direct bilirubin (D-bil); kidney function indexes including blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine (SCr), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR); other biochemical parameters such as lactic dehydrogenase and TG; immunological parameters including complement component 3 (C3), complement component 4 (C4), sCD25 levels and NK cell activity; inflammatory parameters including C reactive protein (CRP); erythrocyte sedimentation rate; procalcitonin (PCT), ferritin and haemophagocytosis; and also infection-related indicators including cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), fungal infection and haemoculture; (6) therapies and outcomes.

Definition

Based on the prognosis, patients were categorised into the death group and remission group to analyse. The death group was defined as death during hospitalisation or within 1 month after discharge, with specific causes of death including disease activity of the underlying rheumatic disorders, uncontrolled MAS and complications such as TMA and post-treatment infections. In contrast, the remission group consisted of patients whose clinical conditions had improved sufficiently to allow discharge. In terms of complications, cytopenia was defined as either bicytopenia, which refers to a reduction in any two of the following: red cells, white cells or PLT; or pancytopenia, indicating a reduction in all three cell lineages; TMA was referred primarily to acquired TMA secondary to immune diseases, characterised by the triad of peripheral thrombocytopenia, mechanical haemolytic anaemia and organ injury, further substantiated by schistocytes on peripheral smear and adjunctive tests including ADAMTS13 (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 13) activity and C5b-9 levels20 21; ALI was defined by transaminase levels at least three times the normal upper limit; AKI was identified by an elevation in SCr of 26.4 µmol/L within 48 hours, an increase to 1.5 times the baseline level within the first week or a reduction in urine output to no more than 0.5 mL/kg/hour for a minimum of 6 hours22; pancreatic involvement encompassed acute pancreatitis and pancreatic enlargement; gastrointestinal involvement was defined as bleeding and/or perforation; CNS involvement was defined by neurological and/or psychiatric symptoms, including irritability, meningeal irritation, epilepsy, altered consciousness and convulsions as well as abnormalities in CNS imaging and cerebrospinal fluid analysis.23 When one or more of these manifestations arise, CNS-HLH should be considered.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis was conducted to compare the remission group with the death group by using SPSS software (V.25.0). All variables underwent the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to assess normal distribution initially. Independent sample t-tests were used to compare continuous variables with a normal distribution, while the Mann-Whitney U test was applied to continuous variables that were not normally distributed. Moreover, the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to analyse categorical data. Descriptive statistics were presented as the mean±SD or the median (25%–75%), with p<0.05 considered to be statistically significant. The Enter method was used to construct a logistic regression model, and association measures (adjusted ORs) were calculated with a 95% CI. Variables with p<0.05 and those of significant clinical value (serum ferritin) were selected and included in the final regression model. Continuous variables were categorised based on the median or clinical criteria in the development of the logistic equation. Subsequently, probit regression was applied to the variables identified by logistic regression to calculate their weights in the prediction score, using the probit coefficient for each variable. To evaluate the model’s fit, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was applied, with a p value exceeding 0.05 indicating a satisfactory fit. Finally, we employed the bootstrap resampling method to conduct a robust performance evaluation of the trained model. Specifically, we first set the sample size to the size of the original dataset (with a total of N samples). In each iteration, N samples were randomly drawn with replacement (replace=True) to form the bootstrap training set, while the samples not selected were used as the out-of-bag (OOB) test set. To ensure the repeatability of the results, the random seed was fixed at 42. This process was repeated 100 times (n_bootstrap=100), and the accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and area under the curve (AUC) were calculated on the corresponding OOB samples each time. All the iteration results were then aggregated to estimate the distribution and CI of the model’s performance, thereby objectively reflecting the model’s generalisation ability under limited samples.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the adult MAS patients

A total of 116 patients with MAS were finally enrolled in this study, with their detailed characteristics based on the HLH-2004 criteria listed in table 1. Among these patients, 97 (83.6%) were female and 19 (16.4%) were male, with a median age of 38 (29, 55) years old. Additionally, 17 patients died during hospitalisation, resulting in a mortality rate of 14.7%. The constitution of rheumatic diseases that induced MAS included 53 cases of SLE, 44 cases of AOSD/sJIA, 9 cases of DM, 5 cases of pSS, 3 cases of RA, 1 case each of antiphospholipid syndrome and Christian-Weber disease (online supplemental table 1). Among them, the mortality of MAS secondary to SLE, AOSD/sJIA and other rheumatic diseases was 18.9% (10/53), 4.5% (2/44) and 26.3% (5/19), respectively.

According to the HLH-2004 criteria, the clinical manifestations and relevant laboratory examinations were assessed in this population. In terms of clinical features, 102 (87.9%) had persistent fever, 81 patients (69.8%) developed cytopenia (biocytopenia or pancytopenia), 58 (50.0%) patients presented with splenomegaly and 11 (9.5%) had hepatomegaly. Bone marrow biopsy was further conducted in 99 patients, 43 (43.4%) of whom found haemophagocytosis (table 1). With regard to laboratory tests, 110 of 111 patients (99.1%) presented with hyperferritinaemia with a median of 8713 (2612 to 30346) ng/mL, while 53 of 106 patients (50.0%) exhibited increased TG and 56 of 115 patients (48.7%) with decreased FIB. Additionally, median serum TG was 3.00 (2.04 to 4.45) mmol/L, and the mean of FIB was 154 (117 to 231) mg/dL. Otherwise, the tests of sCD25 and NK cell activity were performed in 89 (76.7%) patients. Among them, 74.2% of cases had elevated sCD25 levels, with a median of 12 849 (6212, 22711) pg/mL. Meanwhile, decreased NK cell activity was further identified in 51.7% of patients, with a median of 14.59 (12.16 to 16.87)%.

The clinical and laboratory characteristics of the remission and death groups of adult MAS patients

As shown in table 2, although the remission group tended to have a higher proportion of female patients, there was no statistical difference between the two groups. Compared with the remission group, the death group tended to accompany more complications (table 3), including cytopenia (94.1% vs 65.7%, p=0.021), AKI (41.2% vs 11.1%, p=0.005), gastrointestinal involvement (29.4% vs 1.0%, p<0.001) and CNS involvement (29.4% vs 7.1%, p=0.016) as well as TMA (17.7% vs 2.0%, p=0.022). Furthermore, the death group exhibited more severe cytopenia, characterised by a greater reduction of WBC and PLT, along with more pronounced anaemia (table 4). They also showed marked liver dysfunction (increased AST, ALT, T-bil and D-bil, accompanied by decreased albumin levels) and kidney injury (higher SCr and lower eGFR) (all p<0.05). Additionally, the death group experienced a heightened state of infection, with increased PCT levels and a higher incidence of active CMV infection and fungal infection (all p<0.05). Moreover, a prolonged APTT was observed (p=0.003).

Comparison of treatment options between remission and death groups of adult MAS patients

As shown in table 5, immunoglobulin, methylprednisolone (MP) pulse and cyclosporine A were the most commonly used treatment options in MAS patients. In addition, among all MAS patients, 28.5% received etoposide (VP-16), 6.9% used Janus Kinase inhibitors (JAKi), 10.3% adopted rituximab and 8.6% accepted tocilizumab, respectively. Notably, anti-infection therapy (81.9%) was universally used in the MAS population. In comparison to the remission group, the death group had significantly higher rates of interleukin (IL)−2 therapy, plasma replacement and mechanical ventilation (all p<0.05).

Prognostic factors related to the hospitalised adult MAS

To clarify the influencing factors associated with the prognosis of MAS inpatients, on the basis of taking the primary aetiologies of MAS as covariates, a regression model was, therefore, created. The domain risk factors included admission ferritin <1350 ng/mL (OR=5.387, 95% CI 1.203 to 24.127, p=0.034), PLT ≤30×109/L (OR=5.387, 95% CI 1.203 to 24.127, p=0.028), ALT ≥275 U/L (OR=5.732, 95% CI 1.167 to 28.167, p=0.032), CNS involvement (OR=11.268, 95% CI 1.353 to 93.851, p=0.025) and PCT >0.9 ng/mL (OR=11.224, 95% CI 2.019 to 62.381, p=0.006) (table 6). The discrimination capacity was measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.869 (95% CI 0.753 to 0.984, p<0.001) (figure 1), with a good calibration indicated by the Hosmer-Lemeshow method (χ2=4.405, p=0.622). Additionally, the Youden Index of AUROC was calculated as 0.658, highlighting the test’s optimal balance between a sensitivity of 0.943 and a specificity of 0.667. This indicated the test’s reliability in accurately predicting the prognosis of MAS inpatients while minimising false positives. In this study, we employed the bootstrap approach to further conduct internal validation for the logistics model, and the results showed that the accuracy was 0.8838±0.0476 (95% CI 0.7762 to 0.9660), the precision was 0.7224±0.2640 (95% CI 0.0950 to 1.0000), the recall was 0.4201±0.1996 (95% CI 0.0475 to 0.8000), the F1-Score was 0.5016±0.1885 (95% CI 0.0731 to 0.8000), and the AUC was 0.8335±0.0819 (95% CI 0.6311 to 0.9675), indicating that this model had good predictive performance.

Discussion

MAS is a serious, rare and fatal complication of rheumatic diseases, arising out of a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, immunodeficiency, infectious triggers and dominant innate immune effector responses.24 It is characterised by a loss of control over the initial immune response, which progresses to excessive activation and expansion of T lymphocytes and macrophages. This leads to an exaggerated secretion of inflammatory cytokines, resulting in systemic inflammatory manifestations, commonly referred to as ‘cytokine storm’.1 4 25

According to recent studies, the median contribution of rheumatic diseases to adult MAS was 8%,26 with the mortality rate ranging from 17% to 66%.1316 2729 Given the generally relative poor prognosis of MAS, early diagnosis is crucial for enabling timely intervention, appropriate treatment and improving prognosis. As aforementioned, the heterogeneous clinical presentation complicates the establishment of a definitive diagnosis, and the absence of a gold diagnostic standard makes the early recognition of MAS challenging. Nevertheless, current studies are primarily retrospective and involve small sample sizes,133033 with limited exploration of the clinical characteristics and prognostic factors of MAS. In this study, we provided a comprehensive analysis of clinical data in 116 hospitalised adult MAS patients, aiming to observe their clinical features and identify potential risk factors linked to poor prognosis.

In addition to the common clinical manifestations (rash, arthrodynia, etc) of rheumatic diseases, the most frequent manifestations related to HLH-2004 criteria in MAS patients were sustained fever, hyperferritinaemia, elevated sCD25 and cytopenia. Meanwhile, the occurrence of the other relevant criteria, including hypertriglyceridaemia, hypofibrinogenaemia, splenomegaly, haemophagocytosis and decreased NK cell activity, remained approximately 50%. Within the aetiological spectrum of MAS, SLE and AOSD/sJIA emerged as the most frequent causes in our cohort, aligning with the results of previous studies. Moreover, the overall mortality of MAS patients in our study was 14.7%, which was lower than the average levels reported in the previous reports.1314 3034

Furthermore, a logistic regression equation is established to identify influencing factors associated with in-hospital mortality. The result indicated that the admission ferritin levels (<1350 ng/mL), PLT counts (≤30×109/L), CNS involvement, ALT levels (≥275 U/L) and PCT >0.9 ng/mL were the risk factors for in-hospital death in MAS patients. The discrimination performance of the logistics model is excellent, with an AUC of 0.876, which illustrates its reliability in identifying the severity of MAS.

The often-striking elevation of serum ferritin levels, an increased plasma concentration of the alpha chain of the sCD25, and decreased NK cell activity are considered the characteristic diagnostic parameters of MAS, which is consistent with the findings of this study. Of note, the extreme hyperferritinaemia has been identified not only as a potential prognostic factor for the development of MAS1327 3537 but also as a significant predictor of poor prognosis32 in previous studies. Conversely, our study revealed that lower ferritin level at admission (p=0.041) was related to unfavourable prognosis in adult MAS patients, while higher ferritin level at discharge was linked to adverse outcomes. Furthermore, Shaar et al38 identified dynamic changes in ferritin levels as the most significant prognostic factor of 30-day mortality of adult HLH patients. We speculated that the paradoxical ferritin may reflect a rapidly progressive disease course. Specifically, fulminant macrophage activation may deplete ferritin stores early in disease progression. In this study, the mortality rates of AOSD, SLE and other rheumatic diseases induced MAS were quite different. Considering the differences in clinical characteristics in different causes of MAS, although the underlying aetiologies were not included as a risk factor related to poor prognosis, the lower level of ferritin level should be considered in relation to the primary disease of MAS, because the elevation of ferritin in AOSD is higher than that in SLE and other autoimmune diseases. In addition, the result partly highlighted the importance of early identification of MAS, for the slight elevation of ferritin level may be difficult to draw the attention of clinicians in the first instance, which may lead to delayed diagnosis and poor outcome. Meanwhile, it was suggested that the death group experienced more rapid disease progression and was more difficult to alleviate.

Additionally, our findings suggested that PLT≤30×109 /L was a risk factor for poor prognosis, which was in consonance with preceding studies.39 40 As a frequent complication in patients with MAS and HLH, the potential mechanisms of thrombocytopenia include the activity of rheumatic diseases, cytokine storm, destruction of bone marrow regeneration, hypersplenism, excessive depletion and even disseminated intravascular coagulation.41 Severe thrombocytopenia is commonly accompanied by coagulation dysfunction and adverse bleeding complications, including gastrointestinal and cerebral bleeding. Consistently, both prolonged APTT and a higher incidence of gastrointestinal involvement were observed in our study.

Liver injury and dysfunction is another common complication of HLH, manifested as the increase in aminotransferase and bilirubin, hepatomegaly and even acute hepatic failure,42 43 which was substantially consistent with the MAS patients in our study. We are the first to discover that elevated ALT levels are associated with in-hospital death in MAS patients, which has been confirmed in many HLH-related clinical studies.43 44 Otherwise, Bris et al42 have reported that the hepatic haemophagocytosis was detected in 56% of liver biopsies in HLH patients with haematological cancers, which remains uncharted in patients with MAS. The liver is considered an immunological organ, playing a crucial role in stimulating the production of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-8 and IL-33.43 45 Consequently, both elevated serum levels of aminotransferase and bilirubin serve as indicators of liver injury and inflammation, which are associated with adverse outcomes.

Notably, infections are the most prevalent triggers in adult MAS patients, including viral infections (EBV and CMV, etc), bacterial infections, parasitic infections and fungal infections.46 47 Our study found that both fungal and CMV infections occurred more frequently in the death group as treatment-related complications. Among these, the elevation of PCT emerged as an independent risk factor for prognosis in MAS patients, consonant with our previous study in HLH.48 Zhang et al (n=272)49 reported that lower FIB and higher PCT levels were associated with increased mortality, both Song et al (n=96)50 and Apodaca et al (n=64)51 linked infections and neurological symptoms to reduced survival, consistent with our findings. The underlying denominator was the immunosuppression state induced by standardised therapy, coupled with an excessive inflammatory response, which contributes to an unfavourable prognosis.

We then focused on the incidence of CNS involvement and TMA in adult MAS patients. Considering CNS involvement could be the initial clinical manifestation of TMA, these two severe complications had an overlap to some extent. Further analysis confirmed that CNS involvement was linked to inferior prognosis, which was also clarified in a previous HLH-related study.40 48 The limitations of this study merit consideration. First, it was a single-centre, retrospective, observational study with relatively small sample size, which may introduce potential biases and may limit the generalisability of our findings. Second, MAS was diagnosed according to HLH-2004 criteria in our cohort, which may be too strict for adult MAS patients. Given the retrospective nature of our data, we opted the most classic and stringent HLH-2004 criteria to define MAS. Although this approach may have reduced diagnostic sensitivity to some extent, it helped ensure the diagnostic accuracy and consistency. This trade-off was considered appropriate to minimise misclassification and enhance the reliability of our findings. Otherwise, given that MAS often arises as a complication of rheumatic diseases, the activity of the underlying disease should also be taken into account, considering previous studies have demonstrated that underlying disease activity, such as SLE flare, serves as another common trigger.33 52 Future studies should address these limitations by employing a multicentre cohort design and incorporating factors related to the underlying disease.

In conclusion, this study has provided an elaborated analysis of the clinical characteristics of adult MAS patients, identified the predictive factors associated with poor prognosis and demonstrated that factors including the lower admission ferritin levels, thrombocytopenia, CNS involvement, elevated ALT and PCT levels could predict the risk of in-hospital mortality. Our findings will assist clinicians in promptly recognising MAS patients at substantial adverse prognostic risks and contribute to optimising treatment options.
