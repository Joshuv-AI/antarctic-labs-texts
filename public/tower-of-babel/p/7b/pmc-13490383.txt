Reassessing negative 24 h pH impedance tests for hidden gastroesophageal reflux disease using multi feature anomaly detection
Authors: Songho Lee, Junhyeong Lee, Donggeun Park, Sang Kil Lee, Jae Hee Cho, Kyoung G. Lee, Hee Man Kim, Seunghwa Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) diagnosis traditionally relies on acid exposure time (AET) obtained from 24-h multichannel intraluminal impedance-pH (MII-pH) monitoring, the gold standard for GERD diagnosis. However, a negative result (AET < 4%) does not always exclude GERD, as the limited 24-h monitoring window may fail to capture reflux events in patients with intermittent or low-frequency reflux. To address this limitation, we proposed a complementary machine learning-based framework targeting exclusively patients with negative MII-pH results (AET < 4%) to identify potential false-negative cases within this cohort, by integrating statistical and waveform-derived features from pH signals to enhance anomaly detection. Using one-class support vector machine and support vector data description models trained on real-world MII-pH datasets, the framework achieved an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{3}$$\end{document}F3 score of approximately 0.9 and identified potential anomalies undetected by the conventional AET criteria. Explainable AI techniques using Shapley additive explanations showed that features such as kurtosis and peak-to-peak amplitude contributed significantly to the identification of subtle reflux patterns within this cohort. These anomalies may indicate additional candidates for clinical reassessment within the AET-negative cohort. This complementary approach, operating downstream of the conventional MII-pH diagnostic system, could help identify potential false-negative cases among patients with negative MII-pH results, potentially assisting in their proper clinical management.

Introduction

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a condition in which stomach contents reflux into the esophagus or mouth, primarily occurring when the pressure of the lower esophageal sphincter decreases1. This refluxate can damage the esophageal mucosa and lead to typical symptoms of GERD, such as heartburn and acid reflux. Furthermore, it may cause chest pain, dysphagia, globus sensation, and chronic cough2. Epidemiological studies indicate that GERD is highly prevalent worldwide, with reported rates of approximately 23% in North America, 17% in Europe, 5% in East Asia, 21% in the Middle East, 12% in Australia, and 23% in South America3–5. Over the last decade, there has been a significant increase in the number of patients with GERD, attributable to multiple factors such as the use of analgesics, consumption of certain types of food and beverages, smoking, high body mass index, and limited physical activity6.

Accurate diagnosis of GERD is crucial given its high global prevalence and substantial clinical burden. The esophageal pH monitoring is a key diagnostic tool, providing an objective and quantitative assessment of esophageal acid exposure. Various tests, including catheter-based and wireless capsule-based pH monitoring, have been developed to measure gastric acid reflux, thereby improving the diagnostic accuracy. Recently, 24-h multichannel intraluminal impedance and pH (MII-pH) monitoring has been introduced to further enhance diagnostic accuracy and is considered the gold standard (GS)7. This device facilitates comprehensive detection of both acid and nonacid reflux by measuring intraluminal impedance and pH changes across multiple esophageal segments, thereby allowing more accurate evaluation of reflux symptoms. It also enables the collection of detailed physiological data, which can be used to calculate diagnostic indices and assist clinicians in making evidence-based decisions.

Alongside monitoring devices, some diagnostic indices have been proposed for the evaluation of GERD. One of the primary indices is acid exposure time (AET), which denotes the percentage of time during which the esophageal pH remains below 4. Based on AET values, GERD is classified as normal (<4%), inconclusive (4–6%), or conclusive (≥6%) according to Lyon consensus8. AET is highly reproducible and easily calculated, making it the most widely used parameter in MII-pH monitoring. Another major index is the DeMeester score (DMS), which is derived from 24-h esophageal pH monitoring and incorporates six parameters: A DMS greater than 14.72 is typically considered indicative of pathologic acid reflux. Calculating the DMS relies on the manual input of various patient activities, such as standing, lying down, and mealtimes. However, patients frequently omit these details, inevitably compromising diagnostic accuracy. Given these limitations, the Lyon Consensus currently prioritizes AET as the more reliable diagnostic metric, owing to its superior reproducibility and reduced dependence on patient-reported activity logs.

AET and DMS are well-established and clinically reliable metrics within the standard 24-h monitoring framework. However, because the monitoring window is inherently limited, patients with intermittent or low-frequency reflux may not exhibit sufficient acid exposure during the recorded period. As a result, a standard 24-h MII-pH study may yield false-negative (FN) findings, not because AET itself is inadequate, but because clinically relevant reflux events simply did not occur during the observation interval. In the present study, such missed yet potentially clinically meaningful cases were regarded as potential FNs8. To mitigate this limitation, clinicians may prolong the monitoring period beyond 24-h or incorporate auxiliary indices such as the symptom index, symptom association probability, mean nocturnal baseline impedance, and post-reflux swallow-induced peristaltic wave index. However, extending catheter-based monitoring exacerbates patient discomfort. Moreover, because these auxiliary indices require patients to manually record their symptoms and clinicians to match these records with reflux events, they remain partly dependent on subjective human input, which introduces variability and the risk of recording and interpretation errors9,10.

To date, advanced approaches, such as using machine learning (ML), have been proposed to address these diagnostic challenges. ML-based anomaly detection (AD) algorithms can detect subtle patterns in high-dimensional data that traditional techniques may overlook. Previous research has highlighted the effectiveness of ML across diverse medical applications, ranging from imaging to physiological signal analysis11. For instance, Dairi et al. employed a variational autoencoder to extract features from blood test samples for COVID-19 detection and diagnosed anomalies using a one-class support vector machine (OCSVM)12. Hojjati and Armanfard proposed a deep autoencoding support vector data description (SVDD) model that integrates a neural network with standard SVDD, demonstrating its effectiveness across multiple domains, including medical data13. Beyond these ML-based AD models, autoencoder architectures based on 1D and 2D CNNs have also been applied to medical time-series and their time-frequency representations, where anomalies are detected as regions with elevated reconstruction errors relative to normal patterns14. More recently, Hu et al. proposed FECT-OSA, a transformer-enhanced multimodal supervised framework for contactless monitoring of obstructive sleep apnea that leverages event labels to distinguish apnea-hypopnea events from normal segments15.

Despite these advances, ML and DL methods have rarely been applied to the re-evaluation of negative MII-pH results with the aim of identifying potential FN cases among patients with AET < 4%. Moreover, precise expert labeling of GERD MII-pH recordings is highly time-consuming and typically yields imbalanced datasets, in which abnormal patterns are both sparse and heterogeneous. Under these conditions, it is practically difficult to deploy supervised deep learning models that presuppose large, well-annotated, and balanced training datasets.

To address these constraints, we designed an unsupervised AD framework for reassessing AET-negative 24-h MII-pH recordings. Rather than relying on direct labels, the framework operates on AET together with statistical features extracted from 24-h MII-pH signals. The proposed AD framework combines the conventional clinical index AET with both statistical and waveform-derived features extracted from 24-h MII-pH signals. These features are used to train an ML-based AD model aimed at identifying potential FN cases. The framework specifically focuses on patients with AET values within the normal range (<4%) but near the diagnostic threshold, where subtle abnormalities may be overlooked. Finally, we assess the model’s detection capability and the feasibility of subtle patterns indicative of potential FNs using clinical data, thereby providing clinicians with additional, interpretable information to support further clinical reassessment.

To overcome this limitation, we introduce a multi-feature ML-based AD framework that integrates data-driven features with an unsupervised ML-based AD model. Initially, various data-driven features (e.g., mean, variance, etc.) were extracted from the raw pH signals. A correlation analysis was then conducted to identify a subset of informative features, including AET (Fig. 1D). These multidimensional features were used to train unsupervised ML-based AD models, specifically OCSVM and SVDD, with the intended use-case of identifying potential FN candidates within the AET-negative cohort (Fig. 1E). Finally, Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) were applied to quantify each feature’s contribution to the anomaly decision, thereby supporting clinical decision-making (Fig. 1F). Accordingly, the target of the proposed framework is not the direct diagnosis of GERD itself, but the identification of potential FN cases among patients with AET < 4%. The task is therefore to detect subtle patterns indicative of potential FNs that may support further clinical reassessment beyond the conventional AET criterion.

According to the Lyon Consensus, esophageal AET < 4% is considered physiologically normal, 4–6% inconclusive, and >6% conclusive (abnormal)10. For Asian populations, Recommendation 13 of the Seoul Consensus classifies AET ≥ 4% as abnormal, thereby covering both the 4–6% and ≥6% ranges1. However, some patients continue to report reflux-related symptoms yet are not identified as abnormal because their AET values fall below this diagnostic threshold. Specifically, currently available sensing systems may not be sensitive enough to detect prolonged acid exposure during the standard 24-h monitoring period. Such cases may therefore denote potential FNs that are not detected by conventional AET-based criteria.

To show the representative cases, four examples of 24-h pH monitoring signals are presented, including a true positive (TP) case, a true negative (TN) case, and two potential FN cases classified as normal by AET (Fig. 2). Figure 2A shows the TP patient with frequent and sustained acid reflux (AET: 19.98%, DMS: 82.5), whereas Fig. 2B displays the TN patient showing stable signal patterns and accurately classified (AET: 0.00%, DMS: 0.8). Conversely, Fig. 2C, D present potential FN cases that were classified as normal based on AET, but show atypical patterns that may warrant additional clinical reassessment. Figure 2C shows a low AET of 3.39% and a high DMS of 16.90 with SI < 50% and SAP < 95%, accompanied by a total of 103 reflux episodes (76 upright and 27 recumbent) and two recorded episodes of soreness, considered as a functional heartburn or potential FN case. Figure 2D shows a low AET of 0.58% and a low DMS of 2.40, with a total of 59 reflux episodes (37 upright and 22 recumbent). Nevertheless, symptom correlation analysis documented 695 belching events, including 284 episodes temporally associated with reflux (SI 40.9%, SAP 0.0%), indicating a considerable symptom burden despite normal AET/DMS values. Moreover, anomalies in clinical MII-pH data are inherently diverse, stemming from a range of sources beyond pathological acid reflux alone. These include sensor artifacts, signal distortions caused by inconsistent probe placement, and transient pH fluctuations influenced by dietary or behavioral factors. Given this heterogeneity and absence of detailed annotations distinguishing different types of anomalies, supervised learning approaches that require explicit labels are not feasible. Instead, our framework adopts an unsupervised approach by modeling the distribution of normal physiological patterns and identifying deviations as anomalies. To capture both true abnormal cases and potential FNs, we extract additional data-driven features from the pH signals, creating a multidimensional representation for AD using ML.

Feature extraction and selection

To enhance the detection of atypical reflux patterns that may be overlooked by conventional AET-based criteria, we augmented the feature set by extracting 11 features from the 24-h pH and impedance signals. The overall process is illustrated in Fig. 3, which outlines the workflow for feature extraction, correlation analysis, and final selection. As depicted in Fig. 3A, raw time-series data, including six-channel impedance (I1–I6) and pH signals, were collected over a 24-h monitoring period. From these signals, we derived 10 data-driven statistical and waveform features, including mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, peak to peak, and root mean square (RMS), along with the domain-specific feature AET (Fig. 3B, see Table 3 for details). These features were designed to capture the amplitude and distributional characteristics of the signal and to represent recording-level waveform variations associated with transient, irregular, or weakly acidic reflux-like patterns that may not be sufficiently reflected by AET alone. Pearson correlation analysis was then performed to identify and eliminate highly correlated features, thereby mitigating the risk of overfitting16. The outcomes of the correlation tests are visualized in Fig. 3C. As the pH signal inherently does not have negative values, the absolute mean and mean were identical. Similarly, the RMS showed a significant correlation with the mean, as it is calculated as the square root of the average of the squared value of the signal. The shape factor, calculated as the ratio of RMS to the geometric mean, also showed a high correlation with these metrics. Therefore, the absolute mean, RMS, and shape factor were excluded, while the mean was retained for further analysis. Based on the above analysis, eight variables, including mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, peak to peak, impulse factor, crest factor, and AET, were finalized as the major feature set for AD, as illustrated in Fig. 3D. The selected feature set could provide not only as a dimensionality reduction step, but as a clinically interpretable representation of acid waveform variability within the 24-h MII/pH signal. Representative signal patterns corresponding to the minimum and maximum values of each selected feature are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

Model training and hyperparameter tuning

To train unsupervised AD models, two different approaches, OCSVM and SVDD, were utilized, and their hyperparameters were optimized. These two models were selected for their ability to effectively detect abnormal patterns in high-dimensional feature spaces without relying on labeled datasets. This capability is particularly valuable in medical contexts, which are often characterized by imbalanced and incomplete labeling. Hyperparameter tuning is crucial for these models to balance detection accuracy with generalization and to prevent overfitting to the training dataset.

To further analyze the model’s decision-making process and clinical relevance, SHAP analysis was applied to the OCSVM model predictions across the full dataset (Fig. 5C). The SHAP beeswarm plot (Fig. 5C, left) illustrates how individual features contribute to AD, showing the distribution and impact of feature values on the model’s predictions. Negative SHAP values shift the prediction toward an anomaly, whereas positive values support a normal prediction. Feature values are color-coded from high (red) to low (blue), with deeper red tones indicating higher values of the corresponding feature and deeper blue tones representing lower values. The mean absolute SHAP values (Fig. 5C, right) further quantify each feature’s global contribution, highlighting the predominant influence of AET (0.029), followed by kurtosis (0.018) and peak-to-peak amplitude (0.011). In contrast, impulse factor and crest factor demonstrated minimal global contributions.

Consistent with clinical definitions, AET showed the most substantial negative SHAP contributions, particularly when feature values were high (red), indicating that overall acid burden remains the dominant feature driving anomaly classification. Similarly, elevated kurtosis also showed strong negative SHAP values, indicating sharper, more peaked signals that often reflect sudden drops in pH below 4. Moreover, these findings provide that sharp waveform deviations provide additional information beyond AET alone. In the case of peak to peak amplitude, both extremely high and low values were linked to negative SHAP contributions, indicating that abnormal classifications arise from either excessive or abnormally muted signal variations. These findings support interpreting the selected features as a recording-level representation that combines conventional acid burden information with higher-order waveform deviations. In this sense, the proposed model does not directly resolve episode-level temporal mechanisms, but it can identify atypical patterns that may not be sufficiently captured by AET alone.

To further quantify the contribution of each feature, an ablation study was performed on the OCSVM model, in which the model was re-optimized via Bayesian hyperparameter search after removing each feature in turn and re-evaluated on the same test set using the expert labels. This analysis confirmed that AET is the single most influential predictor, that kurtosis provides additional discriminative power in borderline weakly acidic cases, and that amplitude-based features (e.g., peak to peak, impulse factor, crest factor) mainly offer redundant or auxiliary information without materially altering overall performance. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Note 5.

Discussion

This study presents a data-driven clinical reassessment framework for patients with AET < 4%, rather than a direct diagnostic model for GERD itself. The target of the proposed framework is to identify potential false-negative candidates who may be overlooked by the conventional AET criterion, and the task is to detect subtle signal patterns that may support further clinical reassessment. To this end, we combined the conventional clinical index AET with statistical and waveform-derived features extracted from 24-h MII-pH signals and used them in an unsupervised AD framework. In this intended use-case, the proposed model is designed not to replace the conventional AET-based classification, but to provide additional quantitative information that may help clinicians prioritize reassessment in diagnostically ambiguous cases.

Model development was guided by four key considerations. First, the dataset showed a strong class imbalance, with much more normal than abnormal samples, rendering supervised learning approaches less effective. Second, in accordance with the Seoul Consensus Recommendation, all cases with AET ≥ 4% were labeled as abnormal, including both inconclusive and conclusive categories. Third, features such as kurtosis, variance, and peak-to-peak amplitude were incorporated to better capture recording-level waveform deviations associated with transient reflux-like patterns that may not be sufficiently reflected by AET alone. Finally, due to the heterogeneity of signal anomalies and the absence of detailed annotations, an unsupervised approach was chosen, allowing the model to learn the distribution of normal patterns and identify deviations without relying on labeled data.

Our model showed practical utility as a reassessment aid for identifying potential FN candidates, identifying nearly twice as many anomalies as the conventional AET-based criterion and achieving an F₃ score of approximately 0.9. Representative case analyses further showed that the proposed framework can identify borderline or weakly acidic patterns that are not sufficiently reflected by AET or DMS alone and may therefore warrant additional clinical review.

SHAP analysis provided additional interpretive support for the selected feature set at the recording level. High AET values consistently showed strong negative SHAP contributions, reaffirming their consistency with established clinical definitions of abnormal reflux. Additionally, data-driven statistical and waveform features, such as kurtosis and peak-to-peak amplitude, significantly influenced anomaly predictions. This multidimensional feature set enabled the effective detection of atypical reflux-like signal patterns that were not sufficiently reflected by AET alone. However, these selected features should be interpreted not as direct markers of reflux-specific pathophysiological mechanisms, but rather as a compact representation of acid burden and waveform variability that may help identify atypical cases for further clinical reassessment.

The proposed AD framework was evaluated under label noise, sensor artifacts, and inter-patient variability, without requiring pre-labeled anomaly subtypes. A comparative study with various unsupervised AD models (see Supplementary Note 8) indicated that the proposed multi-feature kernel-based models, OCSVM and SVDD, performed favorably for identifying potential false negatives within the specific context of our dataset and problem formulation. While deep learning-based approaches showed limited performance in this specific setting, likely due to data scale and complexity constraints, they may become more useful for modeling temporal dependencies in future studies. Specifically, if larger datasets or advanced feature extraction techniques (e.g., STFT, wavelet transform) become available, such models may provide a more useful basis for exploring temporal dependencies.

The present findings should be interpreted within the intended scope of the proposed framework. In this study, the anomaly score is best understood as a recording-level signal that may highlight additional cases for clinical reassessment beyond the conventional AET criterion, rather than as a direct indicator of reflux-specific pathophysiological mechanisms. In addition, the independent expert review provided a practical retrospective reference for evaluating the intended use-case of the model under real-world constraints, although further validation with repeated monitoring and prospective follow-up would strengthen the clinical interpretation of these findings. Because the current study was based on a retrospective single-center cohort with 24-h MII-pH signals, it was not designed to directly resolve episode-level temporal mechanisms or to distinguish all possible sources of anomaly signals. In addition, because routine esophageal biopsy is not standard practice in the workup of GERD in Korea, eosinophilic esophagitis could not be completely excluded in a subset of patients, although its relatively low prevalence in Korean populations makes a substantial impact on the present results less likely. A more detailed discussion of these limitations and future directions is provided in Supplementary Note 9.

In future work, this framework could be validated on longer (48–72 h) wireless MII-pH signals and multi-center datasets, enabling more detailed analyses of the relationships between esophageal acid exposure, symptom frequency, and the anomaly diagnosis by the proposed model, using approaches similar to those adopted in related works17,18. Further, deep learning-based models could be applied to raw multichannel MII signals combined with time–frequency preprocessing (e.g., STFT, wavelet), as in related works14,15 to complement the present statistical features, better exploit their spatiotemporal information, and improve clinical decision support. Such future studies may enable more direct modeling of episode-level temporal dynamics, including clustering, day–night variation, and other reflux-relevant temporal patterns that are not explicitly resolved in the present framework.

Methods

Medical records and ethical considerations

The data of MII-pH monitoring for this study were retrospectively obtained from Severance Hospital, Seoul, Korea, which is a tertiary referral hospital in Korea. The study population consisted of patients referred from primary or secondary care facilities to a tertiary referral center due to refractory or suboptimal response to standard GERD treatment, who subsequently underwent 24-h MII-pH monitoring between October 2018 and July 2022. A total of 716 consecutive patients were initially screened. Of these, 628 patients met the following inclusion criteria: (1) normal upper gastrointestinal endoscopy performed within 6–12 months prior to or after MII-pH monitoring, confirming the absence of structural abnormalities including esophageal or gastric malignancy; (2) normal esophageal manometry, which was performed routinely prior to MII-pH monitoring to exclude primary esophageal motility disorders including achalasia, and to precisely localize the lower esophageal sphincter for accurate catheter placement; and (3) MII-pH monitoring performed in the PPI-off state. The remaining 88 patients were excluded due to one or more of the following: abnormal manometry findings (including achalasia and other motility disorders), endoscopic abnormalities, or ongoing PPI use at the time of monitoring. After further exclusion of 16 patients with insufficient signal quality or incomplete recording data, the final analytic cohort consisted of 612 patients.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Gangnam Severance Hospital, Yonsei University College of Medicine (IRB No. 3-2022-0221). Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the IRB.

Dataset description

The wired MII-pH monitoring (ZepHr Impedence/pH, Diversatek Healthcare) dataset was collected from patients at Severance Hospital and includes six impedance signals and one pH signal. The impedance signals are used to determine the direction of liquid movement along the esophagus, while the pH signal measures acidity levels, allowing for the detection of both acid and nonacid reflux episodes. AET and DMS values, obtained from the Zvu® Advanced GI Diagnostic Software (Version 3.0.0, Diversatek Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA), served as reference diagnostic labels in this study. These values were automatically calculated by the commercial software based on the acquired raw signals. Because the internal procedures for noise handling and calculation are not fully disclosed, AET was recalculated from the raw pH signals and these recalculated values were used in our analyses.

Patients were categorized based on AET readings into three groups: Normal (0–4%, label 0), Inconclusive (4–6%, label 1), and Conclusive (≥6%, label 2). Concurrently, the DMS provides further classification into Normal, Mild, Moderate, and Severe categories, with a threshold of 14.72 indicating abnormal conditions19. A total of 612 patients, 250 males, 344 females, and 18 of unknown gender, were included in the study, revealing a significant class imbalance, with 80.6% (493/612) classified as Normal and 19.4% (119/612) as either Inconclusive or Conclusive. The total dataset (n = 612) was divided into a training set (n = 489; 80% of the total dataset) and a test set (n = 123; 20%). Features and formulas used for feature extraction

Model-based AD

In this study, we implemented model-based AD techniques using OCSVM23 and SVDD24 to classify recording-level feature patterns extracted from 24-h MII-pH signals as normal-like or anomalous for reassessment purposes. The SVM, from which these models are derived, is fundamentally a ML technique that identifies an optimal hyperplane capable of categorizing different data classes within a feature space.

OCSVM is an adaptation of the original SVM specifically designed for AD. It distinguishes between normal and anomalous data points by separating them from the origin in a transformed high-dimensional feature space. Unlike traditional SVM, which is a supervised method, OCSVM operates under unsupervised conditions and does not require labeled data. This characteristic is particularly beneficial in medical applications, where labeled data may be scarce or incomplete. The hyperparameters νν and ss play a crucial role in shaping the decision boundaries of the OCSVM. The hyperparameter νν, which ranges from 0 to 1, controls the proportion of support vectors. A larger νν allows for more anomalies. The parameter ss influences the width of the kernel function. Smaller values of ss, resulting in a narrow kernel, enable the model to create more complex and fine-grained decision boundaries, while larger values of s, corresponding to a wider kernel, lead to smoother and less complex decision boundaries.

SVDD offers an alternative approach by encapsulating data within a hypersphere instead of separating it with a hyperplane. Hyperparameter tuning

Both the OCSVM and SVDD models are inherently unsupervised and require careful tuning of their hyperparameters, particularly νν and ss, to optimize their AD performance. Since the available training dataset contains explicit labels differentiating normal from abnormal instances, these labels can effectively guide the selection of hyperparameters that facilitate accurate classification of anomalies.

To efficiently optimize these hyperparameters, BO is utilized—a technique renowned for its effectiveness in navigating complex parameter spaces25,26. BO systematically models the relationship between the hyperparameters and the corresponding model performance, allowing for the identification of an optimal hyperparameter set, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\boldsymbol{\theta }}}^{{\boldsymbol{* }}}$$\end{document}θ*, that maximizes the selected evaluation metric, specifically the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{3}$$\end{document}F3 score. This optimization process is critical for developing a robust model capable of identifying not only the known anomalies in the training dataset but also potential unseen anomalies, thereby enhancing the generalizability and clinical utility of the AD framework.

Feature importance using SHAP

In clinical applications, the reliability of ML models relies not only on their predictive accuracy but also on their transparency and interpretability. This critical need for clear insight in medical decision-making is met through explainable artificial intelligence (XAI). Among various XAI techniques, SHAP values are commonly employed to illustrate how different input variables impact a model’s predictions27.
