Green governance in action: Energy conservation and carbon reduction effects of ecological civilization pilot zones
Authors: Chuang Li, Jiayi Wu, Xiaofan Yang, Liping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study examines the energy-saving and carbon-reduction effects of China’s Ecological Civilization Pilot Demonstration Zones using panel data for 229 prefecture-level cities from 2004 to 2023 and a spatial difference-in-differences framework. The results show that the policy significantly reduces urban carbon intensity and energy intensity by 25.2% and 8.0%, respectively. Spatial effect decomposition further reveals strong positive spillovers, with indirect carbon-reduction effects exceeding direct effects. Mechanism analysis indicates that the policy operates by strengthening government environmental governance, promoting green technological innovation, upgrading industrial structure, and enhancing public environmental attention. Heterogeneity analysis shows stronger policy effects in large cities and resource-based cities. These findings provide empirical evidence for optimizing ecological civilization policies, promoting low-carbon urban transition, and strengthening regional collaborative governance.Keywords: Ecological Civilization Pilot Demonstration Zone, Carbon emission intensity, Energy intensity, Spatial difference-in-differences, Policy spillover

Introduction

Research background

Climate change is fundamentally reshaping patterns of human development, with carbon emissions emerging as one of the most critical global challenges.1 As population growth and economic expansion continue, global energy demand has risen sharply.2 At the same time, the fossil-fuel-dominated energy system not only intensifies air, water, and soil pollution but also generates large volumes of greenhouse gas emissions, thereby exacerbating global warming and posing significant threats to sustainable development.3 Against this backdrop, strengthening environmental governance and accelerating the transition to low-carbon development have become central policy priorities worldwide.4 As the world’s largest carbon emitter, China faces particularly acute challenges. On the one hand, its energy structure remains heavily dependent on coal, making rapid substitution toward clean energy difficult in the short term.5 On the other hand, sustained economic growth generates rigid energy demand, increasing the difficulty of achieving emissions reduction while maintaining economic expansion. Consequently, improving energy efficiency and promoting the green transformation of energy consumption have become essential pathways for reconciling economic development with environmental protection.6 In response, the Chinese government has elevated energy conservation, emissions reduction, and low-carbon development to the core of its national development strategy.7

To systematically advance this transition, China has introduced the concept of ecological civilization as a key institutional framework for sustainable development.8 Ecological civilization emphasizes the harmonious coexistence between humans and nature and seeks to achieve a dynamic balance between economic growth and environmental protection through the combined roles of institutional innovation, technological progress, and social governance.9 Importantly, this framework extends beyond traditional environmental regulation by integrating policy, technological, and cultural dimensions. At the policy level, it involves stricter environmental regulations and green development strategies; at the technological level, it promotes the development and diffusion of green technologies10; and at the societal level, it emphasizes raising public environmental awareness.11 Among these initiatives, ecological civilization pilot demonstration zones (ECPDZs) represent a central policy instrument for implementing ecological civilization reforms. In 2014 and 2015, two batches of pilot zones were established to explore scalable green development pathways through optimizing spatial development patterns, enhancing resource efficiency, and strengthening ecological protection.12 With the introduction of China’s dual carbon goals, these pilot zones have been further integrated into the national carbon reduction framework, reinforcing their role in promoting energy conservation, emissions reduction, energy structure optimization, and environmental quality improvement.13

From a policy perspective, ECPDZs are a typical form of place-based policy, which operates through geographically targeted institutional arrangements to guide the spatial allocation of capital, labor, and technology. Unlike conventional environmental regulations that focus primarily on emissions control, ECPDZs simultaneously target energy efficiency improvements and structural transformation of the development model. Their policy mechanisms are therefore embedded in both the energy system and the environmental governance system. From the perspective of the energy-environment nexus, energy consumption and carbon emissions are intrinsically linked14: the scale and structure of energy use determine emission levels, while improvements in energy efficiency represent a key pathway toward low-carbon development. Moreover, according to spatial economics and new economic geography, place-based policies often generate significant spatial spillover effects. ECPDZs may induce green transformation in surrounding regions through technology diffusion, institutional learning, and industrial upgrading, thereby generating positive externalities.15 Against this background, this study addresses three key questions: (1) Do ECPDZs simultaneously reduce urban carbon intensity (CI) and energy intensity (EI)? (2) Do these policy effects exhibit significant spatial spillovers? (3) Are the effects heterogeneous across cities with different sizes and resource endowments? Answering these questions contributes to a deeper understanding of the mechanisms through which place-based environmental policies operate and provides empirical evidence for promoting coordinated regional emissions reduction and low-carbon development.

The contributions of this paper are 3-fold. First, in terms of research content, this study extends the scope of low-carbon policy evaluation by jointly examining CI and EI, thereby providing a more comprehensive assessment of policy impacts on the energy-environment system. Second, in terms of analytical perspective, it embeds place-based policy within a spatial framework to identify spillover effects, revealing how regional interactions shape the overall effectiveness of energy conservation and carbon reduction. Third, in terms of research design, it integrates a multi-period difference-in-differences (DID) approach with a spatial Durbin model (SDM), allowing for the simultaneous identification of direct, indirect, and total effects, and thereby offering more structured empirical evidence on the spatial transmission of policy shocks.

Section 4 introduces the empirical strategy, variables, and data. Section 5 reports the main results. Section 6 provides additional analysis. Section 7 concludes. Overall framework

Literature review

Research on the effects of ECPDZs

As a vivid embodiment of ecological civilization and an important strategic initiative, ECPDZs have attracted substantial scholarly attention with respect to their policy impacts. As pioneer and model zones for China’s green and low-carbon transition, ECPDZs have achieved notable progress in mechanism innovation, institutional supply, and model exploration.16 Some studies find that the establishment of ECPDZs can promote green technological innovation4 and improve ecological total factor productivity,17 thereby contributing to better air quality and improved public health. Other studies focus on the city level and use DID models to evaluate the effectiveness of the pilot policy, showing that the establishment of ECPDZs has positively improved urban ecological efficiency and economic development,18 while significantly enhancing urban welfare associated with green development. In addition,19 find that the pilot policy for water ecological civilization cities significantly improves cities’ green innovation capacity. At the same time, the pilot policy for water ecological civilization cities has markedly reduced industrial wastewater discharges in pilot cities.20 Overall, pilot policies for national key ecological function zones not only improve environmental quality but also promote economic growth, thus achieving a win-win outcome between regional economic development and environmental protection.21

More specifically, the energy-saving and carbon-reduction effects of ECPDZs have gradually become an important topic in recent years. Existing studies mainly examine this issue from three perspectives: carbon-reduction effects, energy-saving effects, and underlying mechanisms. First, regarding the identification of carbon-reduction effects, the literature primarily evaluates policy outcomes using indicators such as total carbon emissions, CI, and carbon emission efficiency. Some studies use total carbon emissions and find that the construction of ECPDZs can significantly curb the growth of regional carbon emissions and reduce overall emission levels.9 Other scholars use CI and argue that the pilot policy lowers carbon emissions per unit of output by optimizing the mode of economic development, improving energy utilization, and strengthening environmental constraints.22 Compared with total carbon emissions and CI, carbon emission efficiency places greater emphasis on the relative relationship between economic output and carbon emissions under given input conditions, and therefore better reflects the quality of green development. Related studies show that the ECPDZ policy can significantly improve urban carbon emission efficiency, suggesting that the policy not only reduces emissions but also enhances efficiency.18 Overall, the existing literature has reached a broadly consistent conclusion that the construction of ECPDZs generally contributes to regional decarbonization.

Second, with respect to the identification of energy-saving effects, existing studies show that ecological-civilization-related pilot policies also play a positive role in reducing EI and promoting resource conservation. Research finds that ecological-civilization-related policies significantly advance regional energy conservation and emissions reduction, while to some extent reconciling economic growth with resource and environmental constraints.19 Compared with a sole focus on carbon emissions, EI better captures whether the mode of regional energy use has undergone a fundamental transformation; therefore, incorporating energy-saving effects into the analytical framework helps provide a more comprehensive assessment of policy performance.23 Accordingly, examining the effects of ECPDZs from the dual perspectives of energy conservation and carbon reduction offers stronger policy relevance and explanatory power.

Finally, regarding the underlying mechanisms, the literature generally holds that the energy-saving and carbon-reduction effects of ECPDZs do not arise from a single channel, but rather reflect the combined influence of multiple governance mechanisms. First, the pilot policy strengthens environmental regulation and performance assessment constraints, compresses the development space of high-energy-consuming and highly polluting industries, and forces the exit of obsolete capacity, thereby reducing energy consumption and pollution emissions.12 Second, policy implementation helps promote the optimization and upgrading of industrial structure by directing resources toward sectors with higher technological content, lower EI, and lower pollution levels, thus reducing regional EI and CI.24 Third, ECPDZs are often accompanied by incentives for green technological innovation and improvements in institutional supply; by encouraging the application of energy-saving technologies, cleaner production technologies, and green processes, they enhance energy efficiency and improve emissions-reduction performance.25 In essence, therefore, the construction of ECPDZs is a comprehensive policy that combines administrative regulation, structural adjustment, technological progress, and governance coordination, and its energy-saving and carbon-reduction effects exhibit clear systemic characteristics.

Research gaps and contributions

The existing literature has conducted extensive and in-depth research on ecological civilization and its carbon-reduction effects, providing an important theoretical foundation for this study. Nevertheless, two limitations remain. First, most existing studies focus on total carbon emissions, CI, or carbon emission efficiency, while paying insufficient attention to the energy-saving dimension, particularly EI. As a result, the overall policy effects have not yet been fully assessed. Second, current research mainly examines the local effects of pilot policies, with relatively limited attention to their potential spatial spillover effects, which may lead to either underestimation or overestimation of the true policy impact. Against this backdrop, this study treats the construction of ECPDZs as a quasi-natural experiment, evaluates its policy effects from the dual perspectives of energy conservation and carbon reduction, and further identifies its spatial spillover effects, with the aim of providing empirical evidence for deepening the reform of ECPDZs and advancing the realization of China’s “dual carbon” goals.

Theoretical analysis

The direct effect of ECPDZs on energy conservation and carbon reduction

Pilot zones serve as comprehensive platforms for national institutional reform and innovation in ecological civilization, primarily intended to encourage local governments to take the lead in testing major and challenging ecological civilization institutions. Through the construction of these pilot zones, local governments aim to organically integrate ecological value with economic development, achieve a “win-win” outcome between ecological protection and economic growth, promote the co-construction and sharing of ecological civilization, and foster a new pattern of harmonious coexistence between humans and nature.21 At the macro level, regions implementing ECPDZs actively promote profound changes in production and lifestyles, values, and institutional arrangements, thereby strengthening broad-based social recognition of and commitment to green and low-carbon development.26 At the same time, by building a more comprehensive environmental governance system, these pilot zones improve the ecological environment and human settlements, effectively enhance the efficiency of resource and energy use, and enable socio-economic development to enter a new paradigm and stage. In turn, this contributes to energy conservation, emissions reduction, and lower CI, thereby providing strong support for the achievement of carbon neutrality goals.12 At the micro level, the construction of ECPDZs, as a form of formal environmental regulation, inevitably affects firms’ production behavior and thus helps reduce both energy consumption and carbon emissions. On the one hand, under the cost hypothesis framework, as environmental policies within the pilot zones become increasingly stringent, firms are required to increase investment in environmental protection facilities and bear higher pollution-control costs, gradually internalizing the negative externalities of environmental pollution into production costs.4 Although this process may crowd out part of productive resource investment in the short run, in the long run it effectively compels firms to improve resource-use efficiency, reduce pollution emissions, and achieve a higher level of green development. Based on this reasoning, this study proposes the following hypothesis.

H1: The construction of ECPDZs can significantly reduce urban EI and CI.

Spatial spillover effects of ECPDZs on energy conservation and carbon reduction

According to the polarization-trickle-down effect theory, the influence of resource factors is not confined to their original geographic location but can diffuse to surrounding areas, thereby generating spillover effects.17 Existing studies show that urban governance policies can produce spillover effects over a broader geographic scope.27 With the establishment of national ECPDZs, both demonstration effects and comparative-advantage effects are likely to emerge within a certain spatial range. Neighboring non-pilot cities may emulate pilot cities in ecological development and environmental protection, industrial restructuring, and governance practices. At the same time, the construction of ECPDZs inevitably requires local governments to pursue low-carbon-oriented technological innovation. According to network economics theory, geographically proximate regions are more likely to experience the diffusion of technological innovation through the exchange of knowledge and skills.28 As a result, positive spillover effects enable neighboring regions to acquire new technologies and practical experience from pilot areas, thereby promoting their progress in energy conservation and carbon reduction.29 Overall, ECPDZs can transmit their internal green innovation capacity and development experience to surrounding areas through multiple channels, including knowledge spillovers, resource reallocation, and technology diffusion. In doing so, they may help reduce energy consumption and carbon emissions in neighboring regions while further strengthening regional environmental resilience and sustainable development capacity. Based on this reasoning, we propose the following hypothesis.

H2: The construction of ECPDZs generates spatial spillover effects and can reduce energy consumption and carbon emissions in neighboring cities.

Mechanisms through which ECPDZs affect energy conservation and carbon reduction

Ecological civilization is a complex systemic undertaking that requires the joint participation and coordinated efforts of multiple actors, including governments, firms, and the public.30 As advances in technologies for defining environmental property rights and growing resource scarcity have made it increasingly feasible to let the market play a decisive role in resource allocation while allowing the government to function more effectively, the mechanisms through which ECPDZs affect energy conservation and carbon reduction can be understood from the following perspectives.

(1)Government environmental governance. Government plays a pivotal role in environmental governance. Within China’s environmental governance system, government acts as the helmsman of ecological civilization, the driver of green institutional reform, and the guardian of market order for green products.31 The establishment of ECPDZs strengthens local governments’ environmental governance responsibilities, requiring them to assume greater responsibility for energy conservation and carbon reduction while also deepening green institutional reform and improving the policy framework for ecological civilization. The pilot policy explicitly requires local governments to incorporate indicators such as resource consumption, environmental damage, and ecological benefits into the assessment system for economic and social development, and to strengthen accountability mechanisms for ecological civilization, thereby enhancing local governments’ awareness of environmental responsibility.32 This creates strong policy pressure that compels local governments to intensify environmental governance and improve the implementation of energy-saving and carbon-reduction measures. Moreover, within China’s vertically structured governance system, the central government imposes environmental governance responsibilities on local governments through a “pressure transmission mechanism.”33 In this context, ECPDZs further reinforce local governments’ environmental pressure and policy incentives, making them more proactive in advancing energy conservation and emissions reduction.

(2)Green technological innovation. As a key driver of the low-carbon transition, technological innovation can play a positive role in reducing regional carbon emissions and energy consumption. Firms, as the main actors in implementing ecological civilization and advancing green and low-carbon development, are also the primary agents of green technological innovation and industrial upgrading. Given the dual externalities associated with pollution control and knowledge creation, firms often cannot rely solely on market forces to drive green innovation,4 which highlights the importance of government intervention. ECPDZs stimulate firms’ incentives for green research and development mainly through the dual channels of environmental regulation and market guidance. On the one hand, the establishment of ECPDZs promotes the introduction of stricter environmental policies, subjecting firms to stronger environmental regulation. According to the Porter hypothesis, the environmental governance policies embedded in the pilot zones can generate an innovation-compensation effect that offsets firms’ compliance costs, thereby encouraging the development and application of clean technologies characterized by zero-carbon emissions and sustainable development. This can increase the output of cleaner sectors without raising carbon emissions, thereby reducing CI.34 On the other hand, policy support and fiscal incentives for environmentally friendly development and technological progress can encourage firms to increase investment in energy-saving and emissions-reduction technologies, effectively improving productivity and energy efficiency and thus lowering both CI and EI.

(3)Industrial upgrading. Industrial upgrading can reduce carbon emissions and has substantial potential for energy conservation and emissions reduction. Transforming the growth model is an important pathway toward energy conservation and carbon reduction, as a declining share of traditional high-energy-consuming and high-emission industries helps improve overall energy efficiency and reduce carbon emissions.35 In terms of policy orientation, ECPDZs emphasize industrial restructuring by promoting energy-saving and environmental protection industries, restricting the expansion of high-energy-consuming and highly polluting industries, and fostering an industrial structure that conserves resources and protects the environment, thereby supporting sustainable economic development. Under these policy constraints, firms that rely on traditional extensive production modes face rising production costs and shrinking profit margins, making it increasingly difficult for them to survive under the competitive pressures of the pilot areas.36 More importantly, the ECPDZ policy can induce a shift in regional production from traditional extensive modes to greener and more environmentally friendly modes, continuously driving industrial upgrading. In doing so, it helps build a modern circular industrial system characterized by low energy consumption, low emissions, and high efficiency, reduces dependence on traditional fossil fuels, and ultimately lowers both carbon emissions and energy consumption.

(4)Public environmental attention. The public is an important participant in ecological civilization, and environmentally aware citizens and social groups have become a vital force in its advancement.37 Public participation, as an important driver alongside government and firms, can supplement information, constrain authority, and help address regulatory problems arising from information asymmetry between governments and firms.38 ECPDZs advocate the values of respecting, adapting to, and protecting nature, and seek to cultivate these values as mainstream social norms through public outreach, environmental education, and professional training, thereby promoting the integration of ecological culture with local cultural traditions. ECPDZs also encourage public participation in environmental protection and strengthen the public’s supervisory role in environmental governance.39 As a result, pilot areas are expected to place greater emphasis on ecological culture, raise public attention to environmental issues, and thereby help reduce energy consumption and carbon emissions.

Based on the above analysis, this study proposes the following hypotheses.

H3a: ECPDZs can promote urban energy conservation and carbon reduction by strengthening government environmental governance.

H3b: ECPDZs can promote urban energy conservation and carbon reduction by stimulating green technological innovation.

H3c: ECPDZs can promote urban energy conservation and carbon reduction by promoting industrial upgrading.

H3d: ECPDZs can promote urban energy conservation and carbon reduction by increasing public environmental attention.

Spatial weight matrix

Before conducting the spatial estimation, it is necessary to construct an appropriate spatial weight matrix. Commonly used spatial weight matrices in spatial econometrics include the geographic distance matrix (Wd) and the economic distance matrix (We). However, for ECPDZs as a typical place-based policy, the transmission of policy effects depends not only on geographic proximity but also crucially on economic linkages. Environmental policies often generate spillover effects in geographic space through channels such as technology diffusion, while the strength of these spillovers is further conditioned by similarities in economic development levels and structural characteristics across regions. Relying solely on a geographic distance matrix would overlook the “benchmark learning” effect among cities with similar levels of economic development, whereas relying solely on an economic distance matrix would fail to capture the common shocks experienced by geographically adjacent areas under environmental regulation. Parallel trend tests

To test the parallel trends assumption and characterize the dynamic effects of the policy, this study further constructs a SDIDs model within an event-study framework.41 Let DIDitk denote a set of event-time dummy variables relative to the policy implementation period, where k = 0 corresponds to the year of policy adoption. The year immediately prior to policy implementation (k = −1) is chosen as the reference period and is omitted from the estimation to avoid perfect multicollinearity. The coefficient βk captures the direct dynamic effect of the policy on the treated group relative to the control group in period k. If the estimated βk coefficients are statistically insignificant in all pre-treatment periods, this indicates the absence of systematic differences between the treated and control groups prior to the policy intervention, thereby supporting the parallel trends assumption. Furthermore, given that the baseline specification is a spatial Durbin DID model, policy effects may arise not only from local treatment but also through spatial interactions with neighboring regions. To account for this, we incorporate spatially lagged event-time variables, WijDIDjtk, into the model. The corresponding coefficients, δk, capture the dynamic spatial association effects of policy implementation in neighboring areas across different event-time periods. The definitions of the control variables and their spatially lagged terms remain consistent with those in the baseline specification.

The detailed results are illustrated in Figures 3 and 4. These findings suggest that there is no significant difference in carbon emission intensity or EI between the pilot cities and non-pilot cities within the ECPDZ, thereby validating the parallel trend assumption. However, it is noteworthy that the impact coefficient of the ECPDZ on EI becomes significant only after the fifth year post-policy implementation, indicating a time lag in the reduction of urban EI associated with this initiative.

Spatial correlation test

A prerequisite for using a spatial econometric model is the presence of spatial correlation among variables. To test this, we calculated the global Moran’s I for both carbon emission intensity and EI. As shown in Table 4, the Moran’s I values for 2004 (the start of the observation period), 2010, 2014 (first batch of pilots), 2015 (second batch), 2019, and 2023 (end of the observation period) were positive and statistically significant at the 1% level. This indicates a significant spatial spillover effect of carbon emissions and energy consumption among the 229 cities in China. In this situation, ignoring spatial dependence could lead to biased results, thus justifying the use of a spatial econometric model.

Spatial model selection

The significance of Moran’s I indicates the presence of spatial dependence, thereby motivating the use of spatial econometric models. The main classes of spatial models include the spatial autoregressive model (SAR), the spatial error model (SEM), and the SDM. Selecting an appropriate specification is critical for ensuring the validity of the empirical results. The model selection procedure follows four standard steps. First, Lagrange multiplier (LM) tests are conducted to detect the presence of spatial dependence. Second, likelihood ratio (LR) tests are employed to determine the appropriate fixed-effects structure (spatial, temporal, or two-way). Third, Wald and LR tests are used to assess whether the SDM can be simplified to either a SAR or SEM specification. Finally, a Hausman test is performed to choose between fixed and random effects. Applying this procedure to panel data for 229 prefecture-level cities in China over the period 2004–2023, and using an adjacency-based spatial weight matrix, we conduct LM, Hausman, Wald, and LR tests. The results, reported in Table 5, show that the statistics for LM (error), LM (lag), Robust LM (error), and Robust LM (lag) are all statistically significant, indicating the presence of spatial dependence and supporting the initial selection of the SDM. Furthermore, both Wald and LR tests reject the null hypothesis at the 1% level, suggesting that the SDM cannot be reduced to either SAR or SEM. The Hausman test also rejects the null hypothesis of random effects, indicating that a fixed-effects specification is preferred. Taken together, these results support the adoption of a two-way (city and time) fixed-effects SDM-DID model for the empirical analysis.

Table 8 shows that both the direct and indirect effects of the ECPDZ policy are significantly negative at the 1% level, indicating that the pilot policy not only reduces carbon emission intensity in local cities but also generates significant energy-saving and carbon-reduction effects in neighboring regions. Further examination of the spatial effect decomposition reveals that the spatial indirect effect of the ECPDZ policy is substantially larger than its direct effect, suggesting that the policy generates strong positive spatial externalities. It should be noted that, in the effect decomposition of the SDM, the direct effect mainly captures the average impact of the policy on local cities, whereas the indirect effect reflects the cumulative impact transmitted to other spatially connected cities through the spatial weight matrix. Therefore, a larger indirect effect does not necessarily imply that each neighboring city is more strongly affected by the policy than the pilot city itself. Rather, it indicates that the policy effect, after diffusing and accumulating across multiple spatially connected cities, produces a stronger overall spatial spillover effect. From the perspective of the underlying mechanism, the ECPDZ policy is inherently characterized by strong demonstration and diffusion effects. In the process of strengthening environmental regulation, tightening energy-saving and carbon-reduction constraints, optimizing green development policy instruments, and promoting industrial green transformation, pilot cities can generate observable and replicable governance experience. Neighboring cities or economically connected cities may actively learn from the governance models and policy instruments of pilot cities under the pressures of policy learning, regional competition, and performance evaluation, thereby improving local environmental governance and reducing carbon emission intensity. Meanwhile, cities are closely linked through industrial division of labor, cross-regional business operations, factor mobility, and market connections. The improvement of green production standards and environmental governance requirements in pilot cities may also be transmitted to upstream and downstream enterprises and related cities through supply chains and regional markets, prompting them to improve energy-saving technologies and environmental management capabilities accordingly. In addition, the fact that the direct effect is smaller than the indirect effect is also economically plausible. When the policy operates within pilot cities, its local energy-saving and carbon-reduction effects may be constrained by existing industrial structures, inertia in energy consumption patterns, enterprise adjustment costs, and the implementation cycle of the policy, leading to a certain time lag in local policy effects. By contrast, the spatial indirect effect reflects the cumulative impact of policy diffusion across multiple connected cities. When policy demonstration, regional competition, industrial-chain linkages, and green factor mobility operate simultaneously, the policy impact may be amplified over a broader spatial scale, thereby resulting in a stronger spatial indirect effect than direct effect. Overall, these findings suggest that the ECPDZ policy not only improves green development in pilot cities themselves but also promotes energy conservation and carbon reduction in surrounding cities through demonstration diffusion, regional coordination, and economic network transmission mechanisms.

Robustness test

Placebo test

To address the potential impact of unobserved confounding factors, we conducted a placebo test to enhance the validity of our findings. Specifically, we used a random sampling method to assign cities to a pseudo-treatment group and a control group, keeping the policy implementation time consistent with the main analysis. We then re-estimated the baseline model and repeated this process 500 times. As shown in Figures 9 and 10, the estimated coefficients are clustered around zero and follow a normal distribution. The vertical line, representing the true coefficient from our baseline regression, is positioned to the left of this distribution. This indicates that our benchmark regression results are not due to random chance, thereby confirming the robustness of our findings.

Heterogeneity analysis

Heterogeneity of city size

Cities of different sizes vary in their ability to acquire economic and political resources. Larger cities benefit from economic agglomeration, leading to higher efficiency in resource allocation and utilization. In contrast, small and medium-sized cities, with their weaker economic foundations, have relatively lower capacities for resource allocation and technological innovation, often lacking sufficient funding and policy support. These factors may affect the energy conservation and carbon reduction effects of the Ecological Civilization Pilot Zone policy. Following the methodology of,56 we divided the cities into two groups based on the “Notice on Adjusting the Standards for Urban Size Classification” issued by the State Council in 2014. Cities with a permanent population of over 1 million were classified as large cities (SIZE = 1), while the rest were considered small and medium-sized cities (SIZE = 0). We then constructed an interaction term between SIZE and DID and added it to the SDID model for estimation. The results in Table 17 show that the estimated coefficient of the interaction term is significantly negative at the 5% level, indicating that the Ecological Civilization Pilot Zone policy has a more significant promoting effect on energy conservation and carbon reduction in large cities. Furthermore, the policy exhibits a more pronounced spatial spillover effect in large cities. This can be attributed to the fact that large cities, with their higher overall development levels, stronger economic foundations, and richer resource allocation capabilities, can invest more financial, human, and technological resources to support the construction and implementation of these pilot zones. Consequently, the policy yields better outcomes for energy conservation and carbon reduction in large cities.

Heterogeneity of resource endowments

Cities with different resource endowments vary in both resource composition and utilization efficiency, which may condition the effectiveness of ECPDZs. Resource-based cities serve as critical strategic bases for China’s energy supply, and their sustainable transformation is essential for both economic resilience and the broader low-carbon transition. However, due to long-term resource dependence and the prevalence of high-emission industries, these cities often face more acute trade-offs between economic development and environmental protection than non-resource-based cities, potentially leading to heterogeneous policy effects. Following,57 we classify cities into resource-based (RES = 1) and non-resource-based (RES = 0) groups based on the National Sustainable Development Plan for Resource-Based Cities. We then incorporate an interaction term between RES and DID into the spatial DID framework. As reported in Table 18, the coefficient on the interaction term (RES × DID) is significantly negative, indicating that the ECPDZ policy exerts a stronger energy-saving and carbon-reduction effect in resource-based cities, both locally and through spatial spillovers. This result can be attributed to the structural characteristics of resource-based cities. Their relatively complete industrial systems and concentrated resource endowments provide greater scope for efficiency improvements through resource recycling, cleaner production, and technological upgrading. Moreover, the implementation of ECPDZs often entails stricter environmental monitoring and quantification of pollutant emissions and resource use, which enhances regulatory effectiveness and improves resource utilization efficiency. By contrast, non-resource-based cities typically lack comparable resource endowments and industrial concentration, limiting the scale and intensity of policy-driven improvements. As the policy progresses, resource-based cities are more likely to upgrade standards for green production and consumption, thereby generating demonstrable pathways for low-carbon transition. These experiences can be more readily transmitted to neighboring regions, reinforcing spatial spillover effects and amplifying overall gains in energy conservation and carbon reduction.

Discussion

As China advances toward its “dual carbon” goals, there is an urgent need to foster new drivers of low-carbon development through green technological innovation. A rigorous evaluation of the role of ECPDZs in promoting energy conservation and carbon reduction is therefore of great significance, both for identifying an ecological civilization model suited to China’s national conditions and for achieving the country’s energy-saving and emissions-reduction objectives. Against this backdrop, this study uses panel data for 229 prefecture-level cities from 2004 to 2023 and employs a spatial Durbin DID model (SDM-DID) to assess the actual effects of the ECPDZ pilot policy, with the conventional DID model and the decomposition framework of the spatial DID model used for cross-validation. It further explores the mechanisms through which ECPDZs affect urban CI and EI. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1)The construction of ECPDZs has a significant energy-saving and carbon-reduction effect, effectively lowering both urban CI and EI. Moreover, the policy not only reduces EI and CI in pilot cities, but also benefits surrounding areas through spatial spillover effects. This conclusion remains robust after a series of robustness checks, including placebo tests.

Policy recommendations

Based on the above findings, this study proposes the following policy implications.

First, regional collaborative governance should be strengthened to fully leverage spatial spillover effects and achieve coordinated emissions reduction across regions. The results indicate that the indirect effects of ECPDZs are significantly larger than the direct effects, highlighting strong positive externalities. Accordingly, policymakers should move beyond isolated pilot-based approaches and promote cross-regional coordination in low-carbon technology diffusion and industrial integration. This can be achieved by establishing interregional platforms for green technology sharing, reducing barriers such as patent licensing costs, and jointly developing cross-city innovation hubs. In addition, regulatory authorities should closely monitor industrial relocation to prevent the displacement of high-energy-consuming industries to neighboring areas, thereby ensuring genuine regional reductions in energy use and carbon emissions.

Second, differentiated development strategies should be adopted according to city size to fully exploit the low-carbon leadership potential of large cities. The heterogeneity analysis shows that large cities achieve significantly stronger energy-saving and carbon-reduction outcomes, largely due to their superior economic capacity and resource endowments. These cities should therefore focus on technological leadership, particularly by advancing frontier green technologies such as hydrogen energy and carbon capture and storage (CCUS), and leveraging scale effects to reduce innovation costs. In contrast, small and medium-sized cities should prioritize technology adoption and absorption. Policies such as expanding green credit access and increasing fiscal support can enhance their capacity to utilize spillover technologies from large cities, with emphasis placed on upgrading traditional industries rather than prematurely investing in high-cost frontier innovations.

Third, resource allocation should follow a place-based approach, with differentiated transition pathways for resource-based and non-resource-based cities. The findings suggest that resource-based cities achieve stronger carbon-reduction effects, primarily through resource recycling mechanisms. These cities should prioritize circular economy development by promoting waste recycling and energy recovery within integrated industrial systems, thereby reducing EI at the source. In contrast, non-resource-based cities should focus on improving systemic efficiency and promoting low-carbon lifestyles. By optimizing industrial structures, expanding the service sector, and advancing smart energy systems, these cities can enhance energy efficiency, while demand-side measures such as green consumption incentives can further support emissions reduction.

Fourth, a multidimensional governance framework should be established by strengthening coordination among governments, firms, and the public. The results indicate that ECPDZs operate through four key channels: government environmental governance, green technological innovation, industrial upgrading, and public environmental awareness. Policy design should therefore target these mechanisms in an integrated manner. Governments should refine accountability and evaluation systems to emphasize performance-based environmental outcomes. Support for green innovation should prioritize energy-saving technologies, cleaner production, and resource recycling, alongside the development of collaborative innovation platforms. Industrial policies should enforce stricter entry and exit standards for high-emission sectors, while public participation can be enhanced through improved environmental information disclosure and broader channels for social supervision.

Limitations of the study

First, as the construction of ECPDZs continues to advance, future research should place greater emphasis on examining policy effects at more granular levels, such as the county, district, and firm levels. Second, understanding the underlying mechanisms through which the policy affects energy conservation and carbon reduction is essential for a comprehensive evaluation of its effectiveness. This study explores four potential channels—government environmental governance, green technological innovation, industrial structure upgrading, and public environmental attention. However, other mechanisms may also play important roles and warrant further investigation in future research. Finally, due to space constraints and the specific focus of this study, we do not explicitly examine the interactions across different administrative levels. Future studies could extend this analysis by exploring the spatial spillover mechanisms of multi-level policies and their cross-level interactions.

Method details

Study design

This study evaluates whether the establishment of Ecological Civilization Pilot Demonstration Zones (ECPDZs) promotes urban energy conservation and carbon reduction in China. The policy is regarded as a quasi-natural experiment because pilot cities were designated by the central government in two batches during 2014–2015. Based on panel data for 229 prefecture-level cities from 2004 to 2023, this study estimates both the direct policy effects and their spatial spillover effects using difference-in-differences and spatial difference-in-differences models. Additional analyses were conducted to examine the underlying transmission mechanisms and heterogeneous policy effects across different types of cities.

Empirical strategy

This study employed a quasi-natural experimental design to examine the effects of the Ecological Civilization Pilot Demonstration Zone (ECPDZ) policy on urban energy conservation and carbon reduction. The staggered implementation of the ECPDZ policy across provinces during 2014–2015 provided exogenous policy variation, allowing causal identification through a multi-period difference-in-differences (DID) framework with city and year fixed effects. Carbon intensity and energy intensity were used as the main outcome variables, while a set of socioeconomic characteristics was included as control variables to reduce potential confounding effects. Considering the potential spatial spillover of environmental policies, the baseline DID model was further extended to a spatial difference-in-differences (SDID) framework based on the Spatial Durbin Model (SDM). Two spatial weight matrices, namely the geographic-distance matrix and the economic-distance matrix, were constructed to capture policy spillovers through geographic proximity and economic linkages. Prior to estimation, a series of spatial specification tests, including LM, LR, Wald, and Hausman tests, were conducted to determine the appropriate spatial econometric model. The validity of the identification strategy was assessed using an event-study specification to examine the parallel trends assumption. The robustness of the estimated policy effects was further evaluated through multiple sensitivity analyses, including placebo tests, alternative variable specifications, alternative spatial weight matrices, and additional robustness checks described in the results section. To further explore the channels through which ECPDZs affect energy conservation and carbon reduction, mechanism analyses were conducted by examining government environmental governance, green technological innovation, industrial upgrading, and public environmental attention within the same empirical framework. Heterogeneity analyses were subsequently performed across different categories of cities to investigate whether policy effects varied according to city characteristics.

Variable definitions

Dependent variable

Carbon intensity (CI). Carbon intensity measures carbon dioxide emissions per unit of GDP. By jointly capturing carbon emissions and regional economic development, it provides a useful indicator of the degree of coordination between emissions performance and economic growth, and thus reflects the effectiveness of energy conservation and emissions reduction. Achieving carbon neutrality requires reducing carbon intensity and building a green and efficient economic development system. Following,58 this study obtains China’s 0.1 × 0.1 gridded carbon emissions data from the Emissions Database for Global Atmospheric Research (EDGAR), spatially matches them to prefecture-level cities in China, and derives city-level carbon emissions. Carbon intensity is then calculated as the ratio of carbon dioxide emissions to GDP.

Energy intensity (EI). Energy intensity is an important indicator of the amount of energy consumed per unit of economic output and is typically measured as energy consumption per unit of regional GDP. Existing studies have shown a strong linear relationship between nighttime light intensity and energy consumption.59 Given the limited availability of continuous and comparable city-level energy consumption data, this study follows the approach of60 and indirectly estimates urban energy consumption using the correspondence between provincial nighttime light intensity and energy consumption. Specifically, it first identifies the relationship between nighttime light intensity and energy consumption at the provincial level, and then combines this relationship with city-level nighttime light data to infer urban energy consumption. Energy intensity is then calculated by dividing the estimated energy consumption by regional GDP. To reduce the potential influence of measurement error on the estimation results, this study controls for key variables such as population size and urbanization level in the empirical analysis, thereby enhancing the reliability of the findings.

Independent variable

The core dependent variable of this paper is DID_it, which is the dummy variable of whether a city is a pilot city in the ecological civilization demonstration zone. If city i is the pilot ecological civilization demonstration zone in year t, the DID value is 1; otherwise, it is 0.

Mediating variables

Government environmental governance attention (GER). Government work reports are the most important official documents for summarizing achievements in the previous year and setting priorities for the year ahead. Accordingly, the frequency and share of environmental terms in these reports can objectively reflect the importance local governments attach to ecological and environmental governance, as well as the intensity of their actual governance efforts. Following,61 this study collects government work reports for each city and measures prefecture-level government environmental governance attention by the ratio of the number of words in sentences containing environmental keywords to the total number of words in the report.

Green technological innovation (GTI). Most existing studies use the number of green patent applications or grants to measure the level of green technological innovation in a city or region. However, because patent approval typically involves a time lag after filing, this study follows50 and uses the number of green patent applications to measure urban green technological innovation.

Industrial upgrading (Industry). Because energy demand varies substantially across sectors, the tertiary sector—especially high-tech industries supported by new technologies—tends to rely less on fossil fuels, and its lower energy intensity is conducive to the energy transition. By contrast, the secondary sector, particularly heavy industry, has a much higher demand for fossil energy than other sectors. Accordingly, this study measures industrial upgrading by the ratio of the tertiary sector to the secondary sector.

Public environmental attention (Attention). Public oversight plays an important external role in environmental governance. At the same time, advances in information technology have made the Internet an important platform for expressing environmental concerns and participating in environmental affairs, given its openness and immediacy. Following,62 this study uses the Baidu Search Index to measure public environmental attention. The Baidu Index is a search-volume indicator generated from the frequency with which Internet users search for specific keywords, and it provides a visualized measure of public attention to those terms. Given that online search behavior may be influenced by short-term public opinion shocks and seasonal weather fluctuations, this study measures public environmental attention using the annual average daily search volume for the terms “environmental pollution” and “smog.” Because the Baidu Index is only available from a relatively late starting point, the mechanism analysis involving public environmental attention is estimated using the 2011–2023 sample.

Control variables

Following the existing literature, this study selects a set of commonly used control variables, including economic development level (PGDP), population density (Population), urbanization rate (Urban), government intervention (Gov), openness (Open), financial development (Finance), and economic agglomeration (Economic). Detailed variable definitions are reported in Table 1.

Data sources

Subject to data availability and other special constraints, this study focuses on the 100 Ecological Civilization Pilot Demonstration Zones approved in 2014–2015, with a study period spanning 2004–2023. China’s Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan regions, as well as the Tibet Autonomous Region, are excluded from the sample. Because policy implementation may differ across administrative levels and this study is primarily concerned with prefecture-level cities, the city samples under the five provincial-level pilot zones of Jiangxi, Guizhou, Yunnan, Qinghai, and Fujian are removed from the full sample. Related municipalities directly under the central government and corresponding prefecture-level city observations are also excluded from the analysis. Ultimately, the 47 demonstration-zone cities identified in the two batches are treated as the treatment group, while the remaining non-pilot cities serve as the control group. After dropping areas with severe data deficiencies, the control group consists of 182 prefecture-level cities. Data on the relevant city-level variables are drawn mainly from the China City Statistical Yearbook, the China Environment Statistical Yearbook, the China Statistical Yearbook, the website of the National Bureau of Statistics, provincial and municipal statistical yearbooks, and local statistical bulletins. Missing values in some data sources are supplemented using linear interpolation. Descriptive statistics for the main variables are reported in Table 2.

Mechanism analysis

Four potential transmission channels were examined, including government environmental governance, green technological innovation, industrial upgrading, and public environmental attention. Each mechanism was evaluated by replacing the dependent variable in the baseline SDID specification while maintaining identical control variables and fixed effects.

Heterogeneity analysis

Heterogeneity analyses were performed by estimating the baseline model separately for subsamples classified according to city size and resource endowment.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 18. The baseline policy effects were estimated using a multi-period difference-in-differences (DID) model with city and year fixed effects. To account for potential spatial dependence among cities, a Spatial Durbin Difference-in-Differences (SDM-DID) model was further employed under alternative spatial weight matrices, including geographic-distance and economic-distance matrices. Model selection was based on a series of spatial specification tests, including LM, LR, Wald, and Hausman tests, to determine the appropriate econometric specification. Standard errors were clustered at the city level to account for heteroskedasticity and within-city serial correlation. All statistical tests were two-sided. Statistical significance was evaluated at the 10%, 5%, and 1% levels, denoted by ∗, ∗∗, and ∗∗∗, respectively. Regression coefficients are reported together with clustered standard errors in parentheses. Sample sizes for all estimations are provided in the corresponding tables. The validity of the identification strategy was evaluated through an event-study analysis to examine the parallel trends assumption. The robustness of the estimated policy effects was further assessed using multiple sensitivity analyses, including placebo tests, alternative outcome variables, alternative spatial weight matrices, and additional robustness tests described in the results section. Mechanism and heterogeneity analyses were performed using the same empirical framework while modifying the outcome variables or estimation samples as appropriate.
