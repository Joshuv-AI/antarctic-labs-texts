Trends and risk factors of stroke and mortality after transcatheter aortic valve implantation in the Netherlands
Authors: Simon E van Putten, Romy R M J J Hegeman, Leo Timmers, Daniel C Overduin, Pythia T Nieuwkerk, Joyce Peper, Maaike M Roefs, Uday Sonker, Benno J W M Rensing, Martin J Swaans, Robert J M Klautz, Jurrien ten Berg, Patrick Klein, THI Registration Committee of the Netherlands Heart Registration
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke after transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is an infrequent but serious complication with important impact on morbidity and mortality. Contemporary real-world evidence on the risk factors of early stroke after TAVI is scarce. We aimed to evaluate the incidence, temporal trends and predictors of in-hospital stroke after TAVI and to assess its association with mortality.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, observational cohort study using data from the Netherlands Heart Registration of all TAVI procedures performed in the Netherlands between 2013 and 2023. The primary endpoint was the incidence of in-hospital stroke. The secondary endpoints were trends, mortality and risk factors associated with in-hospital stroke and early mortality as identified by logistic regression.ResultsAmong 23 593 TAVI procedures, the overall incidence of in-hospital stroke was 2.0% and remained stable after an initial decline. Independent covariates associated with in-hospital stroke included female gender (OR 1.29; 95% CI 1.07 to 1.56), peripheral arterial disease (OR 1.55; 95% CI 1.24 to 1.93), non-transfemoral access (OR 1.54; 95% CI 1.21 to 1.93) and postdilation (OR 1.40; 95% CI 1.10 to 1.76). In-hospital stroke was strongly associated with an increased risk of mortality at 30 days (OR 8.54; 95% CI 6.56 to 11.02), and 1 year (OR 4.38; 95% CI 3.55 to 5.40).ConclusionsIn-hospital stroke remains an important complication after TAVI with a strong impact on mortality. Identification of high-risk patients and procedural optimisation is essential in optimisation of outcome.Keywords: Heart Valve Prosthesis Implantation, Aortic Valve Stenosis, Stroke, Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement, Risk Factors

Introduction

Aortic stenosis is the most common valvular heart disease in the Western world, with increasing prevalence due to the ageing population.1 While surgical aortic valve replacement has been the cornerstone in aortic stenosis management, over the last two decades transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has developed into the recommended treatment modality for elderly, high surgical-risk patients and more recently also younger and intermediate surgical-risk patients.2

Despite the advancement in procedural technologies and improved safety of TAVI, stroke remains to be a catastrophic complication with strong association to both morbidity and mortality.3 The incidence of early stroke (within 30 days) in a meta-analysis of registry data was 2.3%.4 Early stroke after TAVI is commonly attributed to the procedural embolisation of debris caused by the interactions between the aorta, aortic valve and the TAVI device.5 6

Risk factors for early stroke after TAVI can be categorised as either patient-related or procedure-related factors.7 While patient-related characteristics are not modifiable, identifying a risk profile for stroke after TAVI could facilitate the implementation of targeted preventive interventions. This is of particular importance as TAVI is increasingly adopted as treatment for patients with lower surgical risk and longer life expectancy.8 9

The use of registry data, especially for relatively infrequent adverse events, has the advantage of large sample sizes and provides insights into the outcomes in a real-world population as opposed to the selected populations in, often industry-initiated, clinical trials. Understanding the risk factors for stroke after TAVI is essential for improving patient outcomes.

Previous studies have identified a variety of risk factors for stroke after TAVI.10,13 However, the findings have been inconsistent, possibly due to differences in study populations and the low incidence of stroke.13 Medical history of cerebrovascular events, chronic renal impairment and balloon postdilation have been identified as independent predictors of stroke after TAVI in multiple studies.12 13

We present a retrospective analysis on the incidence, risk factors and outcomes of in-hospital stroke using nationwide registry data of all TAVI procedures performed between 2013 and 2023 in the Netherlands.

Methods

Data collection

Data of cardiac interventions performed in the Netherlands are prospectively collected in the Netherlands Heart Registration (NHR) to monitor and improve quality of care.14 15 Data concerning baseline patient characteristics, procedural characteristics, complications and long-term survival are collected by the participating centres and submitted to a secured online database. The NHR performs annual auditing to secure and improve data quality at the level of each individual centre.16 Periodic analyses are performed by the NHR to assess quality of care within and between centres.

Study design

Retrospective analysis was performed using nationwide registry data of all TAVI procedures executed in adult patients (aged ≥18 years) in the Netherlands from 2013 until 2023. Procedures with and without postoperative in-hospital stroke were comparatively analysed. Unknown stroke status and failure to achieve vascular access were exclusion criteria.

Medical ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. The scientific committee of the NHR approved the use of registry data for this study.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint of this study is the incidence of postoperative in-hospital stroke. The definition of postoperative in-hospital stroke according to the NHR is a cerebrovascular accident with permanent dysfunction as diagnosed by a neurologist during or after the procedure and before hospital discharge. The secondary endpoints include risk factors, mortality and short-term outcomes associated with postoperative in-hospital stroke and 30-day mortality.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers and percentages. Continuous variables are shown as either mean with SD or median (IQR) depending on the normality of distribution as assessed through visual inspection of histograms. Χ² or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparison of categorical values between patients with and without in-hospital stroke. Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was performed for comparison of continuous variables. Two-sided p values lower than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

All categorical and continuous variables, except for age, were dichotomised according to predefined definitions to enhance interpretability. Univariate logistic regressions were executed to identify variables associated with in-hospital stroke, 30-day and 1-year mortality. Statistically significant covariates (p value<0.05) were included in multivariate logistic regression. ORs are reported alongside 95% CIs. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using logistic regressions after multiple imputation by chained equations for missing data and variance inflation factors to assess multicollinearity.

Data missingness

Variables were included depending on an overall completeness of at least 80% within the cohort of procedures with a known stroke status or an incidence of at least 2% in either study group. Online supplemental table 1 presents the overall and annual data completeness. The results were retrieved according to a complete case analysis protocol.

Results

Descriptive statistics and primary outcome

In the period from 2013 to 2023, a total of 23 593 TAVI procedures were performed in the Netherlands. After exclusion of patients with either missing in-hospital stroke status (n=1104) or unsuccessful vascular access (n=4), 22 485 patients (95.3%) were included in the analyses. The primary outcome of in-hospital stroke occurred in 2.0% of cases (446/22 485). The incidence of in-hospital stroke initially decreased from a maximum of 3.1% in 2013 to a minimum of 1.6% in 2016 and remained stable thereafter (figure 1).

Patients who experienced an in-hospital stroke were generally older (80.26 vs 79.57 years; p=0.039) and more often of female gender (52.0% vs 48.0%; p=0.016). Patients experiencing stroke had a higher prevalence of peripheral arterial disease (26.1% vs 17.4%; p<0.001), but were less likely to have a history of cardiac surgery (14.2% vs 18.9%; p=0.021). Procedural characteristics

Patients with in-hospital stroke more frequently underwent TAVI via an alternative to transfemoral access route (24.0% vs 15.1%; p<0.001). Predilation and postdilation were more frequently used in patients with postoperative in-hospital stroke, respectively, 50.6% vs 45.5% (p=0.044) and 19.5% vs 14.5% (p=0.006). Predictors of in-hospital stroke

Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed patient characteristics female gender (OR 1.29; 95% CI 1.07 to 1.56), peripheral arterial disease (OR 1.55; 95% CI 1.24 to 1.93), non-transfemoral access (OR 1.54; 95% CI 1.21 to 1.93) and postdilation (OR 1.40; 95% CI 1.10 to 1.76) as significant independent risk factors for in-hospital stroke after TAVI. Results of both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis are shown in table 3. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the consistency of observations for female gender, peripheral arterial disease, non-transfemoral access and postdilation with low multicollinearity (online supplemental table 2 and 3).

Mortality

Patients who experienced an in-hospital stroke had significantly higher mortality rates at 30 days and 1 year compared with those without stroke. At 30 days, the mortality rate for patients who had a stroke was 19.1%, compared with 2.7% in the non-stroke group, with an OR of 8.54 (95% CI 6.56 to 11.02), indicating more than an eightfold increased risk of mortality in the stroke group (p value<0.001). Similar patterns were observed at 1 year with mortality rates of 33.6% vs 10.3% (p value<0.001) and an OR of 4.38 (95% CI 3.55 to 5.40).

Risk factors for early mortality

Multivariate logistic regression identified major vascular complications (OR 12.98, 95% CI 10.04 to 16.69) and in-hospital stroke (OR 8.80, 95% CI 6.33 to 12.08) as most strongly associated with mortality at 30 days. Major vascular complications and in-hospital factors were the factors most correlated with 1 year mortality, with respective OR of 4.74 (95% CI 3.81 to 5.88) and 3.88 (95% CI 2.96 to 5.04). A multitude of factors reflecting patient characteristics was additionally found to be associated with early postprocedural mortality. The results of both univariate and multivariate analyses are summarised in tables4 5. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the logistic regression findings and low collinearity, as presented in online supplemental table 4–7.

Stroke and early complications

Patients with in-hospital stroke were more likely to (also) have a major vascular complication within 30 days (6.2% vs 2.7%; p value: <0.001). There was no difference in the rate of permanent pacemaker implantation within 30 days (11.3% vs 10.5%; p value: 0.672).

Discussion

This retrospective study using observational data from the NHR discusses the incidence and outcome of in-hospital stroke after TAVI and identifies risk factors associated with in-hospital stroke and mortality. This is the first nationwide and large-scale TAVI study focussing on preprocedural and postprocedural stroke over time and associated risk factors. Our result showed that 2.0% of patients experienced in-hospital stroke after TAVI, with even a stable trend in most recent years. Independent covariates for in-hospital stroke after TAVI included female gender, non-transfemoral access, peripheral arterial disease and balloon postdilation.

The incidence of in-hospital stroke of 2.0% found in our study is slightly lower than the mean incidence of cerebrovascular events of 2.3% in a meta-analysis of nearly 400 000 published registry patients and various additional retrospective cohorts.4 Potential explanations include the more recent time period described in our current study as well as discrepancies in the definition of stroke throughout the studies. Interestingly, as shown in a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, there is a constant disparity when comparing the incidences of stroke between randomised clinical trials (3.9%) and registries (2.3%), possibly explained by more frequent minor strokes or strokes with rapid resolution of (minor) deficits reported due to standard and more thorough neurological assessments in randomised clinical trials.4

After an initial decline in the early years of our study, the incidence of postprocedural stroke remained stable during recent years. Various trends have been described internationally, with stable stroke rates in the USA and Denmark, but a decline in Germany.6 17 18 The cause of international differences in the trends of stroke rates remains to be studied, but interpretation is complicated by regional variations in professional guidelines and healthcare reimbursement criteria, and the resulting differences in patients characteristics.

Peripheral arterial disease, non-transfemoral access and balloon postdilation were identified as the strongest independent risk factors for in-hospital stroke after TAVI.

Peripheral arterial disease has previously been identified as a risk factor for stroke after TAVI.17 19 As within the NHR definition, peripheral arterial disease can include a spectrum of diseases such as claudication, carotid stenosis, limb amputation or a variety of vascular interventions. Given our data, we could not distinguish causes, but an association between stroke after TAVI and carotid arterial disease appears unlikely.20 Knowledge regarding the influence of more broadly defined peripheral arterial disease indicating overall elevated atherosclerotic burden is limited.

There is broad consensus on the superiority of transfemoral access over non-transfemoral access routes, offering the advantage of TAVI under local anaesthesia with reduced procedural times and length of hospital stay.21 Differences in outcomes between access strategies should be carefully interpreted, as selection of non-transfemoral access reflects an intrinsic difference in patient characteristics (ie, peripheral arterial disease) associated with increased susceptibility to postprocedural stroke.22

An association between balloon postdilation and (early) stroke has been described in multiple retrospective studies.1 7 23 However, the nature of this correlation should be interpreted with caution due to potential confounding with anatomical characteristics such as bicuspid morphology and either severe or asymmetrical calcification associated with increased risk of paravalvular leakage or device underexpansion and the subsequent use of postdilation.24 The independent association between balloon postdilation and early stroke, however, advocates the selective use of postdilation, rather than a routine or liberal application.25

In contrast to a number of previous studies, we found no association between either previous stroke or chronic kidney disease and in-hospital stroke after TAVI.11 26 27 It could be hypothesised that these characteristics are associated with a higher overall risk of stroke after TAVI, but not directly related to the procedure.

The impact of stroke on early mortality after TAVI has previously been described in several studies. We found an OR of 8.5 for 30-day mortality, which is in line with meta-analyses describing a 6.5 and 7.4 times increase in their risk of 30-day stroke-related mortality.1 28 The extent of the impact of stroke on mortality, however, remains debatable, as other studies describe an OR between 3.2 and 3.5.6 29 Due to the definition of stroke with persistent symptoms as used within the NHR, the impact on early mortality is likely greater than in studies including minor strokes or strokes without persistent problems. The strong association between postprocedural complications such as major vascular complications and in-hospital stroke with mortality at 30 days as well as 1 year highlights the importance of continued endeavours to improve procedural safety.

Limitations

The most important limitations are inherent to the retrospective nature of the study. While quality and completeness of data within the NHR is high, covariates as well as outcome measures had a degree of missingness. Sensitivity analysis after data imputation, however, revealed overall robustness of outcomes. Potential heterogeneity in data and outcome reporting may exist across the NHR, given that individual healthcare centres independently record their own data.

Furthermore, the NHR lacks data on several important variables that may influence outcomes. These include anatomical characteristics such as the degree and distribution of calcification and valve morphology; procedural details such as the type of transcatheter heart valve prosthesis used (eg, self-expandable or balloon expandable) and use of cerebroembolic protection devices; and clinical variables such as traditional cardiovascular risk factors (eg, hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, atrial fibrillation and smoking status) and the use of anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy.

Additionally, data completeness of a limited number of variables was below 80% leading to the exclusion of these variables from analyses. Sensitivity analysis after multiple imputation revealed a potential impact of data missingness on some outcomes of multivariate logistic regression analyses, indicating non-random missingness and potential residual confounding.

In conclusion, this study presents a retrospective analysis on the incidence, risk factors and outcomes of in-hospital stroke after TAVI using registry data of one decade of experience in the Netherlands. Stroke remains to be a highly relevant complication after TAVI with strong association to both early and late mortality. Non-transfemoral approach, peripheral arterial disease and balloon postdilation were most strongly associated with in-hospital stroke after TAVI. The identification of modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors informs future advances in patient-tailored risk assessment and treatment.
