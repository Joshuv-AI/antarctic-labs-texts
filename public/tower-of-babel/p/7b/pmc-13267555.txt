CD39 mRNA therapy attenuates localized acute inflammation: A novel anti-inflammatory strategy using cationic nanoliposomes
Authors: Naomi R. Philosof, Aidan P.G. Walsh, Bangyan Xu, Anna M.D. Watson, Shulei Ren, Ludmilla Hann, Benedict Nathaniel, Anne Nguyen, Haikun Liu, Pengkai Shi, Viktoria Bongcaron, Hung Nguyen, Jonathan Noonan, Meltem Avci-Adali, Karlheinz Peter, Mark Louis P. Vidallon, Xiaowei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Messenger RNA (mRNA) therapeutics offer a promising strategy for treating inflammatory disease by enabling transient local expression of therapeutic proteins. CD39 (ectonucleoside triphosphate diphosphohydrolase-1) hydrolyzes pro-inflammatory ATP and ADP and plays a central role in localized immune regulation. We developed cationic nanoliposomes (NLps) for the delivery of CD39 mRNA and evaluated them in a murine model of localized inflammation induced by lipopolysaccharide and matrigel. Biodistribution studies showed substantial retention within the Matrigel matrix, with limited systemic distribution at 24 h. CD39 mRNA-NLps significantly reduced cellular infiltration at day 5, with decreased monocyte and macrophage staining via histological analysis. qPCR confirmed sustained local CD39 mRNA, while flow cytometry demonstrated increased CD39 protein staining in matrigel-derived immune cells, and phosphate release assays showed functional ectonucleotidase activity at the inflammatory site. Interleukin (IL)-6 levels were slightly reduced in matrigel extracts following treatment, supporting suppression of local inflammation. Hemocompatibility was confirmed using ex vivo in human blood and in vivo in treated mice, with liver enzymes and histological analysis supporting formulation’s safety and favorable tolerability. These findings establish CD39 mRNA-NLps as a safe, effective strategy for a spatially confined mRNA-based approach to regulate local inflammation.

Introduction

Dysregulated inflammation underpins a wide spectrum of acute and chronic diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and psoriasis, as well as neurological diseases like multiple sclerosis and cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis.1,2,3 These conditions impair quality of life and are major contributors to global morbidity and healthcare expenditure. While conventional anti-inflammatory drugs can effectively alleviate symptoms, their long-term use is limited by significant adverse effects, including heightened susceptibility to infections and increased cancer risk due to suppressed immune surveillance.3,4,5 These limitations highlight the critical need for novel therapeutic approaches that address the root of inflammation without broad immunosuppression.

CD39, also known as ectonucleoside triphosphate diphosphohydrolase-1 (ENTPD1), is a cell-surface ectonucleotidase that plays a key role in modulating inflammation. By hydrolyzing pro-inflammatory adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and adenosine diphosphate (ADP) into adenosine monophosphate (AMP), CD39 initiates the enzymatic cascade that ultimately generates adenosine, a molecule with well-established anti-inflammatory effects via CD73 activity.6 Extracellular ATP, a potent danger signal acting through the P2X receptors, amplifies immune cell recruitment and activation, fueling the inflammatory response.7 Thus, CD39 serves as a natural brake on this cascade, and its therapeutic augmentation offers a compelling strategy to mitigate localized inflammation.

Our team has extensively characterized the therapeutic benefits of recombinant CD39 protein in preclinical models of cardiovascular and thromboinflammatory diseases. We have demonstrated that CD39 dampens inflammation and reduces thrombotic risk, preserves cardiac function after ischemic reperfusion injury, and attenuates neurologic deficit after stroke.6,8,9 However, recombinant protein therapies are hampered by short half-life, rapid degradation, and the need for repeated and frequent dosing, which limit their utility in chronic diseases.

To address these challenges, we investigated a novel approach leveraging messenger RNA (mRNA) technologies to provide sustained, localized CD39 expression. mRNA therapy represents a transformative therapeutic strategy, enabling transient, cell-directed production of therapeutic proteins without genomic integration.10

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) facilitate delivery of mRNA into the cell cytosol, where it is translated into the desired protein.10,11 Ionizable lipid nanoparticles (iLNPs), widely validated in the SARS-CoV-2 virus vaccines,12,13 proved highly effective for transient mRNA expression. However, in some settings, their immunostimulatory properties and potential to induce lipid-specific immune responses may present challenges for repeated or chronic administration.14,15 Therefore, iLNPs may be less suitable for use in chronic protein replacement therapy and treatment of inflammatory conditions. Beyond conventional iLNPs, a broader range of nucleic acid delivery platforms has also been explored, including lipid-based systems with alternative ionizable chemistries and polymer-based carriers, each with distinct advantages and limitations related to transfection efficiency, tolerability, biodistribution, and suitability for local versus systemic delivery.12 Within this landscape, cationic nanoliposomes (NLps) represent an alternative platform for local mRNA delivery because their lipid composition can be tuned to support complexation, cellular uptake, and formulation stability.16 Comprising of DOPE (1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine) and 3β-[N-(N′,N′-dimethylaminoethane)-carbamoyl]cholesterol (DC-cholesterol), NLps formulated by benchtop extrusion have demonstrated effective mRNA transfection in mammalian cells in vitro,16,17 with the cationic DC-cholesterol component promoting electrostatic complexation and cellular uptake.

In this study, we evaluated the anti-inflammatory therapeutic potential of CD39 mRNA delivered via NLps in a model of acute localized inflammation, using a matrigel-based platform to simulate a defined inflammatory milieu that closely mimics the inflammatory environment.18 We demonstrate that CD39-smRNA-loaded NLps effectively reduce inflammatory responses in this biologically relevant setting, providing new insights into its potential as a novel treatment for inflammation.

Results

Characterization of cationic nanoliposome size and charge

Successful production of NLps was validated using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and electrophoretic light analysis, which measured a size distribution of 74.2 ± 1.1 nm diameter (polydispersity index [PDI] = 0.271 ± 0.006) and zeta potential of 23.8 ± 2.8 mV for the NLps (Figures S1, 1A, and 1B). The measured PDI of 0.271 indicates a moderately polydisperse population rather than a highly monodisperse formulation. However, the low variability across independent preparations supports acceptable reproducibility within this proof-of-concept study.19,20 Complexation with mRNA resulted in a modest increase in hydrodynamic diameter (∼4–7 nm; Figure 1A), consistent with the expected contribution of an adsorbed mRNA layer (∼2 nm),21 while maintaining high encapsulation efficiency (93% ± 3%). The morphology of the NLps (which falls under the category of nanolipoplexes or cationic nanoliposomes) was characterized using cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) (Figure 1C). The micrographs revealed predominantly spherical vesicles with a clearly defined electron-dense shell surrounding a low-electron density aqueous core, consistent with a liposomal architecture.

The mean particle diameter observed by cryo-TEM (∼65 nm; Figure S2A) was slightly smaller than the hydrodynamic diameter measured by DLS (∼75 nm). This difference is expected, as DLS reports the hydrodynamic diameter, which includes the hydration layer and associated counterions in solution, whereas cryo-TEM reflects the physical dimensions of the vitrified particles.22 Particles are unilamellar vesicles, each consisting of a single lipid bilayer with thickness measured to be 3.6 ± 0.6 nm (Figure S2B; Table S1), which falls well within the expected thickness range of a phospholipid bilayer.23,24,25 Together, these data confirm that the NLps are a monomodal population of unilamellar cationic nanoliposomes. Zeta potential analysis of NLps incubated with increasing amounts of mRNA demonstrated a progressive reduction in surface positive charge with increasing mRNA loading, followed by charge neutralization and inversion at higher mRNA inputs (Figure S3). The loading conditions used in this study for in vitro and in vivo remained well below the charge neutralization, consistent with loading of >90%.

In vitro transfection of eGFP mRNA

The ability of the NLps to transfect mammalian cells was demonstrated by formation of nanocomplexes with mRNA encoding enhanced green fluorescence protein (eGFP), followed by 24-h incubation with Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells. Transfection of CHO cells with eGFP mRNA NLps was visualized with fluorescence microscopy (Figure 1D). Flow cytometry demonstrated that cell populations incubated with eGFP mRNA nanocomplexes showed a significant increase in GFP-positive cells (93.77% ± 1.72%; Figure 1E), compared to cells incubated with PBS (0.92% ± 0.16%; p < 0.0001), eGFP mRNA only (0.66% ± 0.38%; p < 0.0001), or NLps only (3.83 ± 0.54; p < 0.0001). Further, there was a significant increase in mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in transfected cells (4.01×104 ± 0.69×104; Figures 1F and S4) compared to cells incubated with PBS (18.91 ± 1.17; p < 0.0001), eGFP mRNA only (17.67 ± 2.06; p < 0.0001), or NLps only (30.20 ± 4.54; p < 0.0001).

We next examined the kinetics of NLp-mediated transfection in CHO cells over a 6- to 72-h time course. Flow cytometry showed a progressive increase in GFP-positive cells, with maximal expression observed at 24 h (Figure 1E), together with a corresponding increase in MFI (Figure 1F). In addition, comparison with the commercial transfection reagent MessengerMAX™ at 24 h (Figures 1E and 1F) produced 53.57% ± 4.85% GFP-positive cells, with MFI of 2.88×103 ± 6.29×103. In vitro transfection of CD39 mRNA

To validate the functionality of the encoded CD39, our group previously transfected human embryonic kidney 293 cells and primary endothelial cells using lipofectamine and demonstrated successful ADP hydrolysis and platelet inhibition with the expressed protein.26 Now, to demonstrate successful CD39 mRNA transfection using NLps, CHO cells were incubated with NLp/CD39 mRNA nanocomplexes for 24 h before flow cytometry analysis using a CD39-targeted FITC probe. Cells incubated with CD39-mRNA nanocomplexes showed a significant increase in population positive for CD39 (74.50% ± 10.25%; Figure 1G), compared to cells incubated with PBS (1.12% ± 0.42%; p < 0.0001), CD39 mRNA only (1.17% ± 1.45%; p < 0.0001), or NLps only (2.62% ± 1.56%; p < 0.0001). Further, cells incubated with CD39-mRNA nanocomplexes showed a significant increase in MFI in the FITC channel (1607.00 ± 482.70; Figure 1H), compared to cells incubated with PBS (112.30 ± 17.10; p = 0.0003), CD39 mRNA only (109.00 ± 2.65; p = 0.0003), or NLps only (135.70 ± 30.01; p = 0.0003), indicating increased binding of the CD39-targeted FITC probe. To further confirm expression in an immune-relevant cell type, RAW cells were transfected with CD39 mRNA nanocomplexes, and flow cytometry demonstrated increased CD39 expression after mRNA delivery (47.97% ± 1.55%), compared to the vehicle control (6.59% ± 2.98%, p = 0.0002; Figure 1I). Consistent with this, ATP hydrolysis assays performed in CD39-mRNA-transfected RAW cells showed significantly increased free phosphate release compared with controls (34.18 ± 1.37 vs. 6.09 ± 0.05, p = 0.0008; Figure 1J), indicating that the expressed CD39 protein was functionally active.

In vitro hemocompatibility and hemolysis

The safety of the NLps was validated through incubation with human donor whole blood samples prior to their use in the animal model. Measurement with a Sysmex hematology analyzer showed that incubation with NLps caused no significant differences in counts of red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, lymphocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, eosinophils, or basophils between groups, indicating that our NLps have no adverse effect on hemocompatibility (Figure S5A). Further, no hemolysis was detected at any tested NLp concentration, from 10 to 300 mg/mL (Figure S5B).

Ex vivo IVIS imaging of the recovered matrigel matrix, liver, heart, lung, kidneys, spleen, fat, and muscle further confirmed this distribution pattern (Figures 2B and 2C). Only low-level signal was detected in the liver and kidneys, whereas minimal fluorescence was observed in the heart, lung, spleen, fat, and muscle. RT-qPCR confirms local CD39 mRNA delivery

Successful in vivo transfection and anti-inflammatory effects of CD39-mRNA-loaded cationic nanocomplexes were demonstrated using the matrigel model of localized inflammation in mice. Matrigel plugs were prepared under four conditions—PBS alone (sham control), LPS + PBS (LPS only vehicle control), LPS + unloaded NLps (empty NLp control), and LPS + CD39 mRNA NLps (treatment group)—before subcutaneous injection at the nape of the neck. After allowing localized inflammation to develop, the matrigel matrix was excised.

RNA extracted from the matrigel plug was analyzed by RT-qPCR and quantified as a fold change from GAPDH mRNA in the PBS group, to validate successful in vivo transfection of cells within the matrigel matrix, and ongoing presence of CD39 mRNA.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining reveals decreased cellular infiltration of matrigel after CD39 mRNA NLp treatment

Excised matrigel matrix were analyzed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) histology staining (Figures 3B and 3C). Compared to the sham mice (21.03 ± 5.33; Figure 3B), sections from LPS-only vehicle control mice (54.17 ± 38.91; p = 0.011) showed a significant increase in outer layer width. Sections from mice injected with LPS + unloaded NLps (44.74 ± 26.63; p = 0.024) similarly showed a significant increase in wall width, indicating that NLps alone did not affect inflammation. Sections from mice treated with LPS + CD39 mRNA nanocomplexes showed a significant decrease in wall width (19.90 ± 5.07), compared to the vehicle (p = 0.003) and empty NLp control groups (p = 0.008). Further, there was no difference in wall width between LPS + CD39-mRNA-nanocomplex-treated group and sham controls. An additional group using an Onpattro-like ionizable LNP formulation at the same CD39 mRNA dose did not reduce inflammatory cell infiltration in the matrigel model (79.20 ± 37.31), compared with the LPS group (p = 0.0005; Figure S6). This observation indicated a reduction in cell infiltration into the matrigel matrix and thus a dampened immune response.

Immunohistochemistry demonstrated CD31 expression in matrigel after CD39 mRNA NLp treatment

Matrigel sections were next stained for expression of the endothelial adhesion molecule CD31 (platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule [PECAM-1]) to determine the inflammatory state of the cells within and surrounding the matrigel matrix (Figure 4C).

Sections from sham mice (3.60 ± 1.06; Figure 4D) showed significantly lower CD31 expression than those from vehicle control group (9.53 ± 2.23; p < 0.0001), indicating an inflammatory response in LPS-treated mice. Mice injected with LPS + unloaded NLps (9.84 ± 2.00) showed no significant difference from the vehicle control group and remained significantly increased compared to the sham group (p < 0.0001). In contrast, CD31 staining in sections from mice treated with LPS + CD39 mRNA NLps (5.88 ± 3.16) was significantly decreased compared to the vehicle (p = 0.0172) and empty NLp control groups (p = 0.0088) but not significantly different from the sham group. This demonstrated successful downregulation of inflammation by our CD39 mRNA NLps. The reduction in CD31 may contribute to reduced leukocyte migration into the matrigel matrix.

Similarly, significant increases were observed in eosinophils (2,171 ± 497 vs. 1,484 ± 247; p = 0.0180) and neutrophils (1,731 ± 282 vs. 1,108 ± 300; p = 0.0040). To determine whether the encoded CD39 was functionally active at the inflammatory site, free phosphate release was measured in matrigel recovered from treated mice after digestion and incubation with ATP. Matrigel from the CD39-mRNA-NLp-treated group showed significantly increased phosphate release relative to LPS-only vehicle control, consistent with CD39 ectonucleotidase activity in vivo (1.37 ± 0.40 vs. 1.00 ± 0.25; p = 0.0332; Figure 5B). In parallel, IL-6 levels in matrigel plugs from CD39-mRNA-NLp-treated mice were decreased compared with the LPS-only vehicle group, supporting reduced local inflammation (15.30 ± 12.09 vs. 89.55 ± 79.72; p = 0.06; Figure 5C). Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), an early inflammatory cytokine following LPS stimulation, was also measured at day 5 and showed no significant difference between groups (Figure S7). These data demonstrate that CD39 mRNA NLp treatment produces functionally active CD39 at the inflammatory site and is associated with suppression of the local inflammatory response.

In vivo safety studies

To demonstrate in vivo safety of NLps 5 days after subcutaneous delivery, whole blood samples were analyzed for hematologic parameters, including cell counts and hemoglobin levels. Mice treated with unloaded NLps or CD39 mRNA + NLps showed no adverse effects on hemocompatibility relative to either the healthy sham or the LPS-only vehicle control groups. (Figures 6A and S8).

H&E staining of liver sections showed mononuclear cell infiltration in the LPS-only vehicle and empty NLp control groups, whereas infiltration was minimal in the sham and CD39-mRNA-NLp-treated groups (Figure 6B). Consistent with these observations, histopathological scoring demonstrated none to minimal inflammatory infiltration in the sham (0.14 ± 0.25) and CD39-mRNA-NLp-treated groups (0.13 ± 0.23), with a trend toward increased inflammatory infiltrate in the LPS-only vehicle (1.27 ± 0.64) and empty NLp (1.91 ± 0.85) control groups, although this did not reach statistical significance (P = NS; Figure S9). To further demonstrate hepatic compatibility, plasma levels of the liver enzymes alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine transaminase (ALT), and aspartate transaminase (AST) were measured. No significant differences were found between all groups for ALT and AST levels (Figure 6C). ALP levels were found to be significantly lower in mice injected with LPS-only vehicle (45.33 ± 7.23; p = 0.008) and empty NLp (36.25 ± 4.43; p < 0.0001) controls, compared to the sham group (110.50 ± 30.99). There was no significant difference between the LPS-only vehicle and empty NLp control groups, indicating that NLps themselves do not influence ALP levels. Cationic nanoliposome storage stability

Given the favorable biological activity, and safety of our NLps, we finally examined their storage stability under different conditions. A 1-month storage stability (Figure S10) study demonstrated that NLp particle size and PDI remained stable at room temperature (25°C) and at 4°C. In contrast, storage at −20°C resulted in an approximately 3-fold increase in particle size and a 2-fold increase in PDI. Although these changes are not expected to affect functional performance, it should be noted that a slight increase in zeta potential was observed across all storage conditions. This increase became statistically significant relative to the initial value after 4 weeks at 25°C and after 1 week at both 4°C and −20°C.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that locally delivered CD39 mRNA therapy delivered via cationic NLps can suppress inflammation with favorable short-term tolerability in a matrigel model of localized inflammation. In this setting, CD39 mRNA NLp treatment reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and decreased monocyte- and macrophage-associated staining, supporting a clear local anti-inflammatory effect. Increased CD39 mRNA was detected in cells isolated from matrigel plugs, and flow cytometry confirmed the staining of CD39 protein staining in several immune cell types following treatment. In parallel, excised matrigel plugs from treated mice showed increased phosphate release after ATP incubation ex vivo, supporting functional ectonucleotidase activity of the expressed CD39. These findings provide proof of concept that local mRNA delivery can generate functional CD39 activity and locally suppress inflammatory responses within an inflamed microenvironment.

Dysregulated inflammation is a hallmark of many chronic conditions, ranging from autoimmune conditions to cardiovascular disease.1,3 A major driver of inflammatory pathology is the recruitment and activation of immune cells, particularly monocytes and macrophages, which amplify local cytokine production and tissue injury.27 In atherosclerosis, for instance, infiltration of monocytes and T cells into the endothelial layer drives plaque formation and progressive vascular damage.28,29 Current anti-inflammatory therapies, such as methotrexate (MTX), colchicine, and biologics like canakinumab, can be effective but are limited by systemic adverse effects. MTX, widely used for chemotherapy treatment and the management of numerous autoimmune diseases, inhibits cell proliferation.30 Colchicine inhibits immune cell migration and has shown efficacy in reducing cardiovascular events.31,32,33 However, both MTX and colchicine cause dose-limiting gastrointestinal toxicity and increased infection, which limits their use for long-term suppression of chronic disease.32,34,35,36 Canakinumab, an IL-1β inhibitor, showed reduced cardiovascular events in the CANTOS trial but led to higher sepsis-related mortality.37 These limitations highlight the need for therapeutic strategies that act locally within diseased tissue rather than suppressing immune responses systemically.

CD39 offers a mechanistically distinct approach to inflammation control by hydrolyzing locally elevated extracellular ATP and ADP, both key drivers of immune cell activation and cytokine release, into AMP, a precursor to anti-inflammatory adenosine.38 Elevated extracellular ATP levels promote the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6 by monocytes and macrophages,39 driving the recruitment of monocytes and neutrophils, which in turn perpetuate chronic inflammation and hinder tissue repair.40 ATP also skews macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype.41 Thus, by degrading ATP and ADP, CD39 dampens inflammation and prevents thrombosis.6,9 Prior work from our group using recombinant CD39 fusion proteins demonstrated efficacy across models of acute thrombosis, myocardial infarction, and sepsis.6,8,9,42 These studies support the therapeutic versatility of CD39, but recombinant protein approaches remain limited by rapid clearance and the need for repeated administration. In contrast, mRNA delivery enables transient local production of the therapeutic protein directly at the site of disease.

Clinically validated ionizable LNPs, comprised of an ionizable cationic lipid, phospholipid, sterol, and polyethylene glycol (PEG)-lipid, have transformed nucleic acid delivery and remain the benchmark platform for systemic mRNA administration, particularly for COVID-19 vaccines and patisiran therapy for transthyretin amyloidosis.12,13,43 The LNPs used in SARS-CoV-2 vaccines act as adjuvants, eliciting strong innate, antibody, and T cell responses,14 in which the ionizable lipid component induces robust IL-6-dependent humoral and T follicular helper cell responses, while also activating innate immunity through the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway.44,45 Moreover, the increased immunogenicity of iLNPs has been shown to reduce protein expression in the encoded mRNA.12 While the immunostimulatory nature of iLNPs can be advantageous for vaccine development, it presents challenges for therapeutic mRNA applications, particularly in inflammatory diseases where additional immune activation may be undesirable.

An additional rationale for minimizing carrier-associated immune activation is that some clinically used LNP formulation, such as patisiran, requires routine premedication with corticosteroids, antihistamines, and paracetamol to mitigate infusion-related reactions.46 However, systemic corticosteroid use is associated with well-documented long-term side effects, including hypertension, immunosuppression, and bone demineralization, limiting its practicality for routine use in chronic conditions.47 Alternative strategies, such as incorporating lipophilic anti-inflammatory prodrugs into the nanoparticle itself,48,49 reduce immune response; however, the structural novelty of steroid-conjugated lipids introduces potential immunogenicity concerns and the risk of allergic reactions.50 These considerations support the development of a delivery system that can achieve local therapeutic activity without requiring corticosteroid premedication or built-in immunosuppressive modifications.

Our formulation employs cationic lipid DC-Chol and the helper neutral phospholipid DOPE. The positive surface charge facilitates intracellular uptake through electrostatic interaction with anionic lipids within the cellular and endosomal membranes. Cholesterol promotes membrane fusion and provides structural stability, improving the retention of NLps in the circulation.51,52 DOPE promotes the transition of the nanoparticle, from a lamellar to a hexagonal (HII) phase, allowing for endosomal membrane fusion and destabilization.53 Our formulation is PEG-free, thereby avoiding PEG-related concerns associated with some LNP systems. Because cationic lipids can also contribute to toxicity and immune activation, particularly at high charge density or dose,54,55,56 more charge-dense commercially available cationic lipid formulations (1:1 helper:cationic ratio) are mostly limited to in vitro use, where their high transfection efficiency is leveraged; yet they are often associated with greater toxicity concerns, which may limit their suitability for in vivo uses. To balance complexation efficiency, delivery performance, and tolerability, we selected a 2:1 DOPE:DC-Chol formulation with a moderated relative cationic lipid content. Furthermore, we also used a low local dose that was still sufficient to achieve anti-inflammatory efficacy without cytotoxicity. This design rationale was supported by complexation studies showing that the in vitro and in vivo loading conditions remained well below the charge neutralization threshold, consistent with efficient mRNA binding without evidence of overloading.

The murine model of acute localized inflammation using matrigel and LPS has been shown to have significant monocyte infiltration within the extracellular matrix by day 5 post-injection,18 providing a well-defined inflammatory microenvironment for evaluating our therapeutic strategy. The cytokine profile at this endpoint should be interpreted in the context of the timing of LPS-induced inflammatory signaling. TNF-α is generally associated with the early acute cytokine response following LPS stimulation, whereas IL-6 can remain informative at later time points during sustained inflammatory signaling.57,58 Consistent with this temporal distinction, treatment with CD39 mRNA-NLps reduced IL-6 levels at day 5, while TNF-α showed no significant difference at the same endpoint. This suggests that the day 5 endpoint is more suitable for assessing sustained local inflammatory signaling than the acute TNF-α response.

Within this setting, histological analysis revealed that CD39 mRNA-loaded NLps significantly attenuated immune cell infiltration and decreased markers of monocyte and macrophage presence. In a comparative in vivo experiment, an Onpattro-like ionizable LNP formulation tested at the same CD39 mRNA dose did not prevent inflammatory cell infiltration in the matrigel model, whereas the cationic NLp formulation retained anti-inflammatory activity, suggesting that the cationic platform may be better suited to this local inflammatory application.

Our mechanistic data support that the delivery of CD39 mRNA via NLps generated functional CD39 proteins within the inflammatory site. qPCR confirmed sustained local CD39 mRNA in cells isolated from matrigel plugs up to day 5 after administration. Flow cytometric analysis of matrigel-derived cells showed increased CD39 protein staining in monocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils following CD39 mRNA NLp treatment, with a trend toward increased expression in macrophages and non-immune cells, indicating that multiple cell populations contributed to local CD39 expression. Notably, monocytes have also been reported to be highly represented within the matrigel plug at this time point,18 which aligns with our flow cytometric findings and supports the relevance of monocytes as one of the cell populations contributing to local CD39 expression after treatment. Together, these findings support the concept that local delivery of CD39 mRNA can generate extended, spatially confined anti-inflammatory therapy within the diseased area.

The lower apparent transfection efficiency observed for CD39 compared with eGFP in vitro likely reflects both the biological complexity of the encoded protein and differences in assay readout. eGFP is a small soluble reporter that is directly detected by intrinsic fluorescence, whereas CD39 is a membrane-associated ectonucleotidase that requires proper folding, post-translational processing, and trafficking to the plasma membrane before cell-surface detection. In addition, CD39 expression was measured indirectly by antibody staining, which may underestimate the proportion of expressing cells relative to a directly fluorescent reporter. Importantly, despite lower expression measured by flow cytometry, increased phosphate release following ATP incubation confirmed that the expressed CD39 retained enzymatic activity.

The in vivo safety profile observed in this study showed hemocompatibility and favorable short-term in vivo tolerability, further supporting the suitability of the platform for localized administration. AST and ALT remained within the expected range across all groups, demonstrating no overt hepatic injury. A slight reduction in ALP was observed in the treated group as compared to sham mice; however, there was a more significant decrease present in the LPS-only vehicle and the empty NLp control groups, suggesting that this change was linked to the localized inflammatory challenge rather than to the nanoparticle formulation. Given that LPS was confined to the matrigel plug, the lower serum ALP likely reflects a secondary systemic biochemical response to localized inflammation rather than direct systemic LPS exposure. While ALP can dephosphorylate pro-inflammatory substrates such as LPS and extracellular nucleotides, this finding was not mechanistically investigated and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.59,60

From a formulation perspective, the NLps exhibited a moderately polydisperse population. While tighter size control would be desirable for translational development, the observed PDI was acceptable for the present proof-of-concept study, given the low variability across independent preparations and the reproducible physicochemical and biological performance of the formulation. Future development should focus on refining the manufacturing process to achieve a narrower size distribution and improved batch consistency.

Our nanocomplexes were formulated with an N:P ratio of 29.9, as the in vivo mRNA dose required was low, indicating that only a modest payload was required to achieve therapeutic activity in this model. In cationic lipoplexes, net positive charge is maintained to support electrostatic complexation of nucleic acids and interaction with negatively charged cell membranes, which should be considered when interpreting the relatively high N:P ratios commonly used in these systems.61 Our data also demonstrate that the encoded CD39 protein itself is sufficient to counteract both the underlying inflammatory stimulus and the inherent immune activation caused by the nanoparticle formulation. Beyond inflammation, CD39 may also have anti-thrombotic relevance through hydrolysis of extracellular ADP, which links purinergic regulation to platelet activation. This broader biology may be relevant in diseases where inflammation and thrombosis intersect, such as atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, and stroke.6,8,9

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The subcutaneous LPS-matrigel model provides a controlled platform for studying localized inflammation, but it reflects an acute inflammatory response and does not fully capture the complexity of chronic inflammatory disease. The study also did not include a direct comparison with recombinant CD39 protein in the same model, which would be informative for defining the relative advantages of mRNA-based delivery. In addition, species differences between murine and human inflammatory responses must be considered when interpreting translational relevance. Although the present formulation was suitable for local subcutaneous delivery, alternative lipid compositions and delivery strategies may be needed for targeting deeper tissues, vascular inflammation, or other disease settings. For example, adaptation to deeper tissues may be achievable through image-guided local injection, catheter-directed delivery, or other localized depot-based approaches, whereas application to systemic inflammatory conditions would likely require further optimization of formulation, biodistribution, and tissue targeting. Future development will also require attention to scalability and manufacturing robustness, particularly with respect to reproducible particle formation and batch consistency. Progressing this platform toward broader therapeutic application will require evaluation of repeat dosing, long-term safety, immunogenicity, optimal dosing, alternative delivery routes, and efficacy in models that better reflect chronic inflammation. Nevertheless, these findings represent an important step toward advancing mRNA-based CD39 therapy as a localized treatment approach for chronic inflammatory conditions.

Conclusion

Overall, this study demonstrates that CD39 mRNA nanocomplexes formulated with NLps can achieve local anti-inflammatory activity with favorable short-term tolerability in the LPS-matrigel model. Biodistribution studies showed substantial retention of the nanoliposomal formulation within the matrigel plug with limited systemic distribution. CD39 mRNA treatment reduced immune cell infiltration, decreased monocyte and macrophage markers in the matrigel matrix, while sustained local CD39 mRNA was detected by qPCR. Increased CD39 protein staining was observed in matrigel-derived immune cell populations, particularly monocytes, and phosphate release assays confirmed functional ectonucleotidase activity. The findings provide proof of concept for a spatially confined mRNA-based strategy to locally regulate inflammation and highlight the therapeutic potential of CD39 mRNA nanocomplexes for localized inflammatory conditions, particularly where current treatments are limited by systemic side effects and broad immunosuppression.

Materials and methods

Formulating cationic nanoliposomes

NLps were prepared via the dry-film method as described previously.17 DOPE (1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine) and DC-cholesterol (3β-[N-(N′,N′-dimethylaminoethane)-carbamoyl]cholesterol hydrochloride) were purchased in powder form (850725P and 700001P, Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabaster, AL) and dissolved in chloroform (C2432, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) at a concentration of 100 mg/mL. Lipids were mixed in a 2:1 mass ratio (DOPE:DC-cholesterol), dried under vacuum overnight, and rehydrated with nuclease-free sterile water to 3 mg/mL. The mixture was sonicated for 5 min at 50% Amplitude (Misonix, Farmingdale, NY), then passed 5 times at 18,000 psi through an LV1 Low Volume Microfluidizer Homogenizer (Microfluidics, Westwood, MA).

Characterization of NLp size and charge

Size distribution and zeta potential were measured in triplicate using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and electrophoretic light scattering (ELS) using the Malvern NanoZetaSizer (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). Ten microliters of the NLps were diluted in 990 μL of ultrapure water in a cuvette (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK) for measurement. In addition to characterization of freshly prepared NLps, samples were stored for 1 month at 25°C (room temperature), 4°C, and −20°C, with weekly DLS and ELS measurements performed to evaluate stability.

Cryogenic transmission electron microscopy

R1.2/1.3 300 mesh ultraAu grids (Quantifoil, Großlöbichau, Germany) were glow-discharged using a Pelco easiGlow (15 mA, 90 s). NLps (3.5 μL, diluted 1:10) were applied to the treated grids, which were subsequently vitrified automatically using an FEI Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI Systems). Vitrification was performed by blotting at 4°C under 100% relative humidity, followed by plunge-freezing into liquid ethane, maintained at −174°C by liquid nitrogen cooling. The vitrified grids were then stored in liquid nitrogen prior to cryoTEM imaging to preserve sample integrity and avoid structural alterations or degradation. Sample-loaded cryo-grids were mounted onto a Gatan 910 cryo-holder and then imaged using a Tecnai Spirit G2 (FEI Systems), 120 kV LaB6 cryo-electron microscope (ThermoFisher Scientific), equipped with a 4 × 4 k BM Eagle camera (ThermoFisher Scientific). Particle diameters and lamella (bilayer) thicknesses were digitally analyzed from the acquired cryoTEM images (100−200 measurements) using FIJI.62

Production and supply of mRNA

In house eGFP and CD39 mRNA were produced as described previously,16,63 or were supplied by the BASE mRNA facility (University of Queensland, Brisbane, Australia). The DNA sequence was amplified using a HotStar HiFidelity Polymerase Kit (202605, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) using the forward primer, “TTG GAC CCT CGT ACA GAA GCT AAT ACG” (Ella Biotech, Fürstenfeldbruck, Germany), and the reverse primer “T120 CTT CCT ACT CAG GCT TTA TTC AAA GAC CA” (Ella Biotech, Fürstenfeldbruck, Germany). The DNA sequences were purified using a MinElute PCR Purification Kit (28004, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and in vitro transcription was performed using a MEGAscript T7 Kit (AM1334, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), as per the manufacturer’s instructions (4 h incubation at 37°C). mRNA was modified with a 3′-O-Me-m7G(5′) ppp(5')G RNA cap structure analog (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA), pseudouridine-5′-triphosphate (TriLink Biotech, San Diego, CA), and 5-methylcytidine-5′-triphosphate (TriLink Biotech, San Diego, CA). An RNase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) was added. mRNA was purified using an RNeasy kit (74104, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and eluted in nuclease-free water, before dephosphorylation using an Antarctic Phosphatase Kit (M0289, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). Following this, the mRNA was purified again using an RNeasy kit.

Generation and characterization of nanocomplexes

NLp-mRNA nanocomplexes were prepared by incubating CD39 or eGFP mRNA with NLps for 20 min at room temperature. For in vitro assays, 0.5 μg mRNA was mixed with 5 μL NLps; for in vivo studies, 0.2 μg mRNA was complexed with 10 μL NLps. To determine the impact of mRNA complexation on the particle properties, dispersions of NLps without and with mRNA were also measured by DLS. An extended mRNA loading range (0.00–0.35 μg/μL) at a fixed concentration of NLps was also measured via ELS to measure zeta potential as a function of mRNA content of the dispersions. To determine mRNA entrapment efficiency, the ability of NLps to capture mRNA through electrostatic adsorption was evaluated using Quant-iT™ Ribogreen™ RNA Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). NLp samples (10 μL) were mixed with EGFP mRNA (0.2 μg, ∼3 μL) and incubated for 15 min to allow complexation. In parallel, NLps (10 μL) mixed with ∼3 μL of PBS were prepared to account for matrix effects (NLp control). Following incubation, for each sample, two sets of triplicates were prepared: Triton X-100-treated (2 μL NLps +1 μL Triton X-100 + 97 μL 1× TE buffer) to release entrapped mRNA and Triton X-100-free (2 μL NLPs +98 μL 1× TE buffer) to detect free mRNA. RiboGreen reagent was diluted 200-fold in the 1× TE buffer. Each sample (100 μL) was added to the wells, followed by 100 μL pre-diluted Ribogreen RNA reagent. Fluorescence was recorded using a BMG Labtech FLUOstar Omega microplate reader (excitation ∼480 nm, emission ∼520 nm). Triton X-100-treated samples were dilution-corrected (×100/92), and RNA concentrations were obtained by linear interpolation from a standard curve. Encapsulation efficiency was calculated using Equation 1.(Equation 1)EE(%)=(FITriton−treated−FITriton−free)(FITriton−treated)×100%

The N:P ratio was calculated as the molar ratio of protonatable amine groups from DC-Chol to phosphate groups in the mRNA backbone. For the in vivo formulation, 10 μL of NLps contained 10 μg DC-Chol, corresponding to 1.86 × 10−8 mol DC-Chol (MW 537.26 g/mol), assuming one cationic amine per molecule. For 0.2 μg mRNA, phosphate content was calculated using an effective nucleotide residue mass of 321.3 g/mol, giving 6.22 × 10−10 mol phosphate groups. This yielded an N:P ratio of 29.9.

In vitro cell transfection

CHO cells were cultured in DMEM (11960044, high glucose, Gibco, Waltham, MA) with 10% FBS, 1% L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (10099141, 25030081, 15140122, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). RAW264.7 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) antibiotic–antimycotic. Cells (1.5 × 105) were seeded onto six well plates and grown for 24 h at 37°C in a humidified, Steri-Cycle 5% CO2 incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). After washing with PBS, serum-free media was added to prevent cells overgrowth, and nanocomplexes were applied for 24 h to assess protein expression. Lipofectamine™ MessengerMAX™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) was used, according to Manufacturer’s protocol, as a positive control.

Fluorescence microscopy

After 24 h of incubation, cells were washed thrice with 37°C PBS and maintained in PBS for imaging. Cells and their eGFP expression were visualized using an Olympus IX81 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), using both brightfield and a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) fluorescence channel. Images were taken at 20× magnification. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ.

In vitro flow cytometry

Cells were trypsinized, centrifuged at 400 × g for 4 min at room temperature, in an Eppendorf 5810R centrifuge (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), and resuspended in 300 μL PBS. Flow cytometry was performed using a Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). CHO cells were identified using forward scatter and side scatter. Direct eGFP fluorescence was quantified using an excitation laser at 488 nm and an emission filter at 530/30 nm. CD39-FITC antibody (Clone A1, ab30423, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was used to detect CD39 expression on the surface of cells. Transfection efficiency was assessed by MFI and % FITC-positive cells.

ATP hydrolysis assay

Samples were resuspended in HEPES buffer and incubated with ATP at a final concentration of 250 μM for 15 min at room temperature. Following incubation, supernatants were collected, and 80 μL of each sample was transferred to a clear-bottom 96-well plate. Free phosphate release was quantified using a colorimetric phosphate assay kit (BioAssay Systems, POMG-25) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 20 μL of working reagent was added to each well, the plate was gently mixed, and samples were incubated for 30 min at room temperature for color development. Absorbance was measured at 620 nm using a microplate reader.

In vitro hemocompatibility

Blood from healthy volunteers with informed consent, in compliance with the relevant laws and institutional guidelines (Alfred Human Ethics, project number: 58/24; Baker Heart and Diabetes Institute, Melbourne, Australia), was collected in citrate tubes. NLps (up to 300 mg/mL) were incubated with whole blood at 37°C for 1 h on an orbital shaking incubator. PBS and Triton X (X-100, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) were used as controls. Following incubation, 20 μL of sample was added to 120 μL cellpack DCL solution (DCL-300A, Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) and analyzed with an XN-550 hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan).

In vitro hemolysis

Post-incubation plasma was separated by centrifugation (2,000 rpm, 10 min). Absorbance at 550 nm was measured using a FLUOstar Omega plate reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany). Hemolysis was calculated and normalized to 100% lysis (Triton X-100 control).

Animal model

Male C57BL/6 mice (20–30 g; 6–10 weeks of age) were purchased from the Alfred Medical Research and Education Precinct Animal Services and housed at the Precinct Animal Center, Baker Heart and Diabetes Institute. Mice were fed a standard irradiated laboratory chow diet (protein, 20.00%; total fat, 4.80%; crude fiber, 4.8%; digestible energy, 14.0 MJ/kg; Specialty Feeds, Glen Forrest, Australia) and received water ad libidum. All in vivo experiments were approved by the Alfred Medical Research and Education Precinct Animal Ethics Committee (E/1967/2019/B) and (E/8335/2022/B).

Matrigel model of induced inflammation

Local inflammation was induced by subcutaneous injection of a matrigel (BDAA356234, Corning Inc, Corning, NY) and LPS (L4391, derived from Escherichia coli, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) mixture as described by Temme et al.18 Mice were randomly assigned to one of four groups: PBS-only (sham) group (40 μL matrigel and 20 μL PBS); LPS-only vehicle control group (40 μL matrigel with 6 μL LPS (30 μg, and 14 μL PBS); LPS + unloaded NLps group (40 μL matrigel, 6 μL LPS, 4μL PBS, and 10 μL NLps); and LPS + CD39 mRNA NLps treatment group (40 μL matrigel, 6 μL LPS, 4 μL PBS, and 10 μL nanocomplexes incorporating 0.2 μg CD39 mRNA). Onpattro-like ionizable MC3 LNP formulation (40 μL matrigel, 6 μL LPS, 4 μL PBS, and 10 μL iLNPs incorporating 0.2 μg CD39 mRNA) was used as a clinical benchmark.64 To prevent premature gelling, solutions and all materials were kept at 4°C. Mice were anesthetized (3%–4% isoflurane induction; 1.5%–2% maintenance), and fur at the injection site was removed using Veet depilatory cream (Reckitt, Slough, UK). Mice were euthanized via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of ketamine/xylazine (300 and 60 mg/kg, respectively) at endpoint.

IVIS imaging and biodistribution analysis

At 24 h after subcutaneous administration of matrigel alone or matrigel containing Cy7-labeled NLps, mice were euthanized by i.p. injection of ketamine/xylazine and underwent whole-body IVIS fluorescence imaging using an IVIS Lumina III imaging system (PerkinElmer). Following whole-body imaging, the matrigel plugs and major organs/tissues, including liver, spleen, kidneys, lungs, heart, muscle, and adipose tissue, were collected for ex vivo imaging. Fluorescence signal intensity in each region of interest was quantified using Living Image software (PerkinElmer).

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR

Matrigel samples were stored in RNAprotect (76106, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) prior to RNA extraction using TriZolReagent (15596026, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, matrigel samples were incubated in 1 mL Trizole, followed by addition of chloroform to induce phase separation. Post-centrifugation, isopropanol was added to the supernatant to obtain RNA precipitate. After two washing steps with 75% ethanol, the resulting RNA pellet was air dried and resuspended in 20 μL RNAse-free water. RT qPCR was conducted using iTaq Universal SYBR Green One-Step Kit (1725151, Bio-rad, USA) and a QuantStudio 7 Flex system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Custom-ordered oligo primers (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA) included GAPDH (F:AGGTCGGTGTGAACGGATTTG; R:TGTAGACCATGTAGTTGAGGTCA) and CD39 (F:AAGGTGAAGAGATTTTGCTCCAA, R:TTTGTTCTGGGTCAGTCCCAC). GAPDH was used as a housekeeping reference gene, and results are expressed as fold change compared to GAPDH expression.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

At 5 days post-matrigel implantation, mice were euthanized via i.p. injection of ketamine/xylazine, and samples were excised. Excised matrigel matrixes with overlaying skin were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT; Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA), snap frozen on dry ice, and stored at −80°C. Samples were sectioned (6 μm) using a Leica CM1950 Cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany). Sections were stained with H&E and imaged at 10× magnification using an Olympus BX43 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with a DP28 camera and cellSens Imaging Software (Evident Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) under identical light conditions. Immune cell infiltration was quantified by measuring the matrigel border thickness in ImageJ. For immunohistochemistry, frozen sections were fixed in −20°C acetone for 20 min, quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide (room temperature, 30 min), and blocked using an Avidin/Biotin Blocking Kit (SP-2001, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Primary antibodies include MOMA-2 (MCA519G, 1:100 dilution, BioRad, Hercules, CA) against monocytes and macrophages and CD31 (550274, 1:500 dilution, BD Pharminogen, Franklin Lakes, NJ) against vascular endothelium marker, with matched isotype rat immunoglobulin G (IgG) controls (02–9602, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). After incubation with biotinylated secondary antibody (BA-4001-.5, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA), samples were incubated with Vectastain Elite ABC-HRP and DAB substrate (PK-6100 and SK-4100, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA) and counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin. Imaging was performed at 10× objective using the Olympus BX43 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and quantified in ImageJ (% positive area from five images/animals, v.2.14.0/1.54f).

Cell isolation from matrigel plugs

Matrigel plugs were excised and minced into small fragments using sterile scissors. Tissue fragments were digested in digestion buffer containing Liberase TL (0.4 U/mL) and DNase I (100 μg/mL) at 37°C with intermittent pipetting to facilitate dissociation. Following digestion, the cell suspension was passed through a 70-μm cell strainer to remove debris, and the filtered cells were collected for downstream analysis.

Flow cytometry for cells isolated from matrigel matrix

Cells were stained with a fixable viability dye (ViaDye Red, Cytek, Cat# R0-00008) and fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD45 (BUV563, clone I3/2.3, BD Biosciences), CD19 (BUV737, clone 1D3, BD Biosciences), CD3 (PerCP-Cy5.5, clone 17A2, BD Biosciences), NK1.1 (BV650, clone PK136, BD Biosciences), CD64 (AF647, clone X54-5/7.1, BD Biosciences), CD39 (FITC, clone A1, Abcam), Siglec-F (BUV395, clone E50-2440, BD Biosciences), CD11b (APC-Cy7, clone M1/70, BD Biosciences), CD11c (BV786, clone HL3, BD Biosciences), Ly6C (AF700, clone HK1.4, BioLegend), and F4/80 (PE-Cy5, clone BM8, BioLegend). Cells were incubated with antibodies for 60 min in PBS at room temperature, protected from light. Following staining, cells were washed and resuspended in PBS for acquisition. Data were acquired using a Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Cytokine quantification

Matrigel plugs isolated from mice were soaked in PBS followed by sonication using a Misonix sonicator (Qsonica) at 50% amplitude for 2–3 s to disrupt the matrix. Samples were then centrifuged at maximum speed for 5 min, and the conditioned media was collected. Cytokine levels in the conditioned media were quantified using the BD Cytometric Bead Array Mouse Inflammation Kit (BD Biosciences, 552364) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Liver histopathology, hematology, and liver function analysis

For liver histopathology, liver samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, 158127, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), cryoprotected in 20% sucrose overnight, AND embedded in OCT and sectioned (6 μm). H&E-stained sections were imaged at 20× objective using a BX43 microscope (Olympus) equipped with a DP28 camera. Mononuclear cell infiltration was assessed in a blinded manner using a semi-quantitative inflammation score (0–8) based on the number of inflammatory foci per field of view, with scores averaged across at least five randomly selected fields per animal.

Blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture into heparinized tubes. For hematologic analysis, 20 μL of whole blood was diluted in 120 μL of Cellpack DCL and analyzed using a Sysmex XN-550 analyzer. For liver function analysis, platelet-poor plasma (PPP) was isolated by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 10 min at room temperature and submitted to Alfred Pathology (Melbourne, Australia) for measurement of ALP, ALT, AST.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were assessed using GraphPad Prism 9 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA) and reported as mean ± SD. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For datasets of two groups with one independent variable and parametric data, with significant variance between groups, Welch’s t test was used. Outliers were excluded using Grubbs’ test. To analyze equality of variance for datasets with ≥3 groups, the Brown-Forsythe test was used. For datasets of >2 groups with one independent variable and parametric data, with equal variance between groups, one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc analysis by Tukey test was used. For datasets of >2 groups with one independent variable and non-parametric data, Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons post-hoc analysis was used. For datasets of >2 groups with one independent variable and parametric data, with unequal variance between groups, Brown-Forsythe and Welch ANOVA with Dunnett T3 multiple comparisons were used. For datasets of >2 groups with two independent variables and parametric data, with equal variance between groups, two-way ANOVA followed post hoc analysis by the Šídák test was used. Test results were considered statistically significant at values of p < 0.05.
