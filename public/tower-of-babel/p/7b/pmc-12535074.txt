Pyrroloquinoline quinone targets senescent osteoclasts and reduces spinal pain and endplate degeneration in a lumbar spine instability mouse model
Authors: Qinghe Geng, Yan Geng, Ke Heng, Juan Zhai, Xingchen Song, Liangwei Sha, Yinuo Geng, Peiru Nie, Fanshuo Liu, Huaiyuan Zhai, Junli Huang, Yanhong Liu, Mei Li, Guoqiang Wang, Rui Geng, Kui Xue, Qilong Wang, Wanying Huang, Shuangshuang Suo, Huanyu Zhang, Ying Geng, Yuanji Yang, Junyun Lan, Hengsheng Hu, Shengnan Liu, Xiangfei Meng, Yilong Guo, Jie Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPyrroloquinoline Quinone (PQQ) is a redox cofactor with potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. This study investigated the therapeutic potential of PQQ in alleviating spinal pain and degeneration in a lumbar spine instability (LSI) mouse model. LSI is commonly associated with spinal pain and structural degeneration, with senescent osteoclasts (SnOCs) involved in spinal hypersensitivity and degeneration.MethodsLSI was induced in 8-week-old C57/BL6 mice by surgically resecting the L3–L5 spinous processes and associated ligaments. The mice were treated with PQQ, and behavioral tests (spinal hypersensitivity, anxiety, and activity levels) were conducted. Spinal endplate structural integrity was assessed through micro-computed tomography imaging and histological analyses. Immunohistochemical staining was performed to identify SnOCs and inflammatory markers.ResultsPQQ treatment significantly reduced pain-related hypersensitivity and anxiety behaviors in LSI mice. Behavioral improvements were demonstrated by increased activity, quicker heat response times, and reduced mechanical hypersensitivity. PQQ treatment also preserved the microarchitecture of spinal endplates by reducing endplate porosity, trabecular separation, and osteoclast activity. Additionally, PQQ reduced the number of SnOCs and modulated their inflammatory secretory phenotype, thereby decreasing sensory nerve innervation and angiogenesis in the spinal endplates.ConclusionsPQQ alleviates spinal pain and degeneration by targeting SnOCs, modulating inflammation, and preserving spinal microarchitecture. These findings suggest that PQQ has potential as a therapeutic agent for managing pain and structural degeneration in spinal instability-related disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07212-9.Keywords: Pyrroloquinoline Quinone (PQQ), Lumbar spine instability (LSI), Spinal pain, Spinal degeneration, Senescent osteoclasts (SnOCs), Inflammation, Anxiety behaviors, Endplate degeneration, Sensory nerve innervation, Angiogenesis

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) affects individuals of all ages and is among the most prevalent and debilitating musculoskeletal conditions worldwide [1]. LBP is the leading cause of disability globally, with a 1-month prevalence rate of 23.2% [2]. Approximately 80% of people will experience LBP at some point in their lives; thus, it is a widespread and persistent condition [3]. In 2017 alone, approximately 577 million people worldwide suffered from LBP, which accounted for approximately 60 million years lived with disability, placing a substantial financial burden on healthcare systems and economies [4]. This condition is a major driver of disability, contributing to high medical costs and productivity losses.

Intervertebral disk (IVD) degeneration is a leading cause of LBP. Although IVD has been a major focus in LBP research, its role as the primary pain generator remains contentious due to its largely aneural nature, with nerve endings limited to the outer annulus fibrosus. In contrast, the vertebral endplate, which undergoes significant structural remodeling during IVD degeneration, has gained recognition as a critical contributor to LBP pathogenesis [5]. The cartilage endplate between the nucleus pulposus and the vertebral body is particularly susceptible to degeneration and damage due to high shear stresses [6]. Aging and responses to degenerative conditions result in progressive sclerosis of the cartilage endplate [7]. Magnetic resonance imaging findings of bone marrow lesions within the endplates, known as Modic changes, are highly predictive of localized LBP [8]. As degeneration progresses, osteoclastic activity and ossification render the endplates increasingly porous, promoting aberrant sensory nerve innervation [7, 9]. Notably, degenerative endplates exhibit a higher density of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)-positive sensory nerve fibers than healthy endplates [10], suggesting that CGRP plays a role in pain sensitization. A previous study employing the lumbar spine instability (LSI) model demonstrated that porous endplates are heavily innervated by sensory nerves, providing direct evidence that endplate pathological remodeling contributes to nociceptive pain [11]. These findings underscore the importance of targeting endplate pathology in the development of therapeutic strategies for LBP. Despite these observations, the exact mechanisms underlying LSI and aging-induced endplate sclerosis remain unclear.

Recent evidence underscores the critical role of cellular senescence in spinal degeneration and pain. Cellular senescence is a protective program that halts the division of cells with substantial damage. Once senescence is triggered, these cells irrevocably exit the cell cycle—even when exposed to growth signals or oncogenic factors—yet remain metabolically alive. Remarkably, senescent cells acquire enhanced survival traits that render them resistant to programmed cell death. Rather than remaining dormant, these cells actively remodel their surroundings by releasing a complex mix of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and extracellular matrix components. This secretory activity, known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), creates a locally disruptive milieu that can influence tissue function and promote chronic inflammation. Senescent osteoclasts (SnOCs), characterized by growth arrest and proinflammatory and neurotrophic factor secretion, are strongly linked to spinal hypersensitivity [11, 12]. SnOCs contribute to increased spinal pain by promoting sensory nerve innervation and H-type vessel growth in porous endplates [12]. Senolytics, a new class of drugs, have been developed to specifically target senescent cells and modulate the SASP [13]. Among existing senolytic drugs, navitoclax (ABT263) has shown promise in reducing SnOCs and alleviating spinal degeneration [12]. However, the clinical application of these drugs is limited by off-target effects and toxicity, highlighting the urgent need to explore alternative senolytic agents for treating spinal conditions that are effective and safer.

Pyrroloquinoline Quinone (PQQ) is a naturally occurring compound found in various food sources, including fermented foods, vegetables, and human breast milk. A growing body of research has consistently demonstrated that PQQ plays a vital role in several physiological processes, such as cellular energy metabolism, antioxidant defense, and neuroprotection [14]. As a crucial component of mitochondrial functions and ATP synthesis, PQQ acts as a cofactor for several redox-activated enzymes [15]. Notably, PQQ’s potent antioxidant properties, such as its ability to scavenge free radicals and reduce oxidative stress, suggest its potential in both preventing and treating age-related neurodegenerative diseases [16]. This potential is further evidenced in PQQ’s effect on cognition and neuroprotection [16, 17]. In addition to its neuroprotective effects, PQQ regulates multiple cellular metabolic pathways; thus, it may offer therapeutic benefits for lifestyle-related conditions, such as diabetes and obesity [18]. Some studies have also highlighted the compound’s potential in promoting cardiovascular health, revealing its ability to modulate endothelial function, reduce inflammation, and protect against ischemic injury [14]. Moreover, PQQ has shown promise in cancer research, as it can inhibit tumor growth and enhance the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents [19]. Our previous study demonstrated that dietary PQQ supplementation did not influence serum estradiol (E2) levels or uterine weight in ovariectomy (OVX) mice. However, the drug effectively prevented OVX-induced bone loss and enhanced bone strength by reducing oxidative stress, suppressing osteocyte senescence, and mitigating the SASP. These effects subsequently promoted osteoblastic bone formation while inhibiting osteoclastic bone resorption, yielding results comparable to those of exogenous estrogen treatment [20].

Here, we aimed to build on these findings by investigating the potential of PQQ as a treatment for LSI using a mouse model. PQQ has demonstrated promising effects in anti-aging research due to its antioxidant properties and ability to modulate cellular senescence processes. In this study, we used the LSI mouse model to assess whether PQQ administration could reduce the effect of SnOCs, thus alleviating endplate degeneration and spinal pain. This study may pave the way for new therapeutic approaches for age-related spinal disorders, offering a safer and more effective strategy for managing spinal degeneration and associated pain.

Materials and methods

Animals

Thirty 8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (25–30 g) were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University (Nanjing, China). All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Pizhou Hospital (Ethics No. 2024-01-13) and were conducted per NIH guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

LSI model

Spinal instability was induced in 8-week-old mice through surgical resection of the L3–L5 spinous processes and the associated supraspinous and interspinous ligaments. The procedure was conducted under general anesthesia with isoflurane, and postoperative analgesia was provided. Sham-operated mice underwent the same surgical exposure without resection of the spinous processes and served as controls (n = 10) [11, 12, 21].

PQQ administration

PQQ was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (catalog number: D7783, St. Louis, MO, USA). The Sham (n = 10) and LSI + Veh (n = 10) groups were fed a standard AIN-93G diet, whereas the LSI + PQQ (n = 10) group received the same diet supplemented with 4 mg/kg of PQQ, as we previously reported [20].

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis

Mice were euthanized with an overdose of isoflurane, and their spines were harvested and fixed in 10% buffered formalin for 48 h. Micro-CT imaging was performed using the Bruker Skyscan 1172 system at 55 kV, 181 µA, and a resolution of 7.0 μm. Image reconstruction was conducted with NRecon v1.6, and the subsequent analyses were performed using CTAn v1.9 (Bruker, San Jose, CA, USA). The region of interest was defined as the L4–L5 vertebral segment in the coronal plane, with a specific focus on the caudal endplates. Total porosity of the caudal endplates was quantified from 3D datasets. A 3D model was generated using six consecutive coronal sections via CTVol v2.0 (Bruker) [22–24]. The following structural parameters were calculated using CTAn software (v1.9, Bruker) per the manufacturer’s guidelines: bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp).

Behavioral assessments

Behavioral evaluations were conducted preoperatively and at weekly intervals following surgery, per previously established protocols [25]. All tests were performed by a single experimenter blinded to group allocation to ensure objectivity.

Vocalization threshold assessment

Mechanical hypersensitivity was assessed by measuring the threshold for vocalization in response to applied pressure. A force gauge (SMALGO algometer; Bioseb, Pinellas Park, FL, USA) was used to apply pressure directly to the dorsal skin at the L4–L5 vertebral level. Pressure was increased at a constant rate of 50 g/s until the animal produced an audible vocal response, which was recorded as the nociceptive threshold. BIO-CIS software (Bioseb) was used to monitor the pressure curve, with a maximum force limit set at 500 g. The average threshold was calculated from multiple trials [25].

Voluntary wheel running

Spontaneous locomotor activity was measured using a running wheel system (Campden Instruments, Loughborough, UK). Each cage was equipped with a 23 cm diameter wheel, and mice were allowed unrestricted access to food and water. Wheel activity was continuously monitored over 48 h using automated software that recorded various parameters, such as the total running time, distance traveled, average speed, and peak speed.

Hot plate test

The hot plate test was conducted to assess the nociceptive response of the mice to thermal stimuli. The mice were placed on a heated plate maintained at 55 °C, and their response latency (the time taken to exhibit nociceptive behavior such as paw licking, paw lifting, or jumping) was recorded.

Hind paw withdrawal

The hind paw withdrawal response to mechanical stimuli was assessed using von Frey filaments (0.7 mN and 3.9 mN; Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL, USA). The mice were allowed to acclimatize for 30 min before 10 filament applications were made to the midplantar surface of the hind paw. Withdrawal frequency was calculated as the percentage of positive responses.

Open field exploration

An open-field test was conducted to evaluate anxiety-related behavior and exploratory activity in a novel environment. The mice were placed in the center of a transparent Plexiglas chamber following a 1-hour acclimation period. Behavioral tracking was performed over a 10-minute session to measure the total distance moved and the duration spent in the central zone, indicating anxiety levels [25].

Elevated plus maze

Anxiety-like behaviors were further assessed using the elevated plus maze (O’Hara & Co., Tokyo, Japan). The apparatus comprised two open arms and two enclosed arms connected to a central square platform, elevated 1 m above the ground. Each mouse was placed on the central platform at the start of the test, and the time spent in the open arms was recorded during a 10-minute trial [25].

Histochemistry, immunohistochemistry, and histomorphometry

Following 48 h of fixation, spine samples were decalcified in 0.5 M EDTA for 2 weeks. The tissues were then embedded in paraffin or OCT, and 4-µm-thick coronal sections of the caudal endplate of L4/L5 were prepared for staining. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was used to identify osteoclasts. Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-βGal) staining was performed to detect senescent cells. PGP9.5 was used as a pan-neuronal marker to assess sensory nerve innervation. MMP-13 and Collagen X were used to evaluate matrix degradation and hypertrophic chondrocyte differentiation in the endplates, respectively [12, 25].

Endplate degeneration was assessed using a semi-quantitative scoring system, as previously described [26]. The parameters evaluated included chondrocyte clustering, microfractures, neovascularization, structural disorganization, and presence of sclerosis. Each parameter was scored from 0 (normal) to 2 or 3 (severe), and total scores were summed to reflect overall damage. A total score ≥ 6 was considered indicative of severe degeneration per prior literature and internal validation. Two independent blinded observers performed all assessments, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus to reduce subjectivity.

For immunofluorescent staining, 10-µm-thick coronal sections of the caudal endplate of L4/L5 were processed, including SA-βGal staining following a standard protocol. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against mouse MMP13 (1:1000, ab315267, Abcam), Col X (1:1000, ab184996, Abcam), p16 (1:200, ab211542, Abcam), TRAP(1:200, ab191406, Abcam), CGRP (1:500, ab81887, Abcam), PGP9.5 (1:200, ab108986, Abcam), Deleted in Colorectal Cancer (DCC; 1:1000, ab273570, Abcam), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2; 1:1000, ab179800, Abcam), CD31 (1:1000, ab222783, Abcam), Endomucin (EMCN; 1:200, ab106100, Abcam), osterix (1:1000, ab22552, Abcam), and osteocalcin (1:500, M188, Takara). The sections were then incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were labeled with DAPI (Servicebio, G1012) for immunofluorescence.

For immunohistochemistry, a streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase detection kit (Vector) was used to visualize immunoreactivity, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. A senescence β-gal staining kit (Biovision, USA) was used to identify senescent cells, per the manufacturer’s directions. Sample images were captured using a fluorescent microscope (IX73, Olympus). The area of the target’s lower endplate was measured and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH).

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from L4–L5 spinal endplate tissues using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the SuperScript First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen), per the manufacturer’s directions. Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) on a C1000 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Specifically, we assessed a panel of genes related to inflammation (e.g., IL-1α, IL-6, and TNF-α), cellular senescence (e.g., p16, p21, and p53), SASP regulation (e.g., NF-κB and CEBPβ), and matrix remodeling (e.g., MMP-13). GAPDH was used as the housekeeping gene. The sequences of the primers used are provided in Table 1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of IL-6 (BMS603-2, ThermoFisher), TNF-α (BMS607-3, ThermoFisher), Netrin-1 (EM56RB, ThermoFisher), and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) (SKGE004B, R&D Systems‌) in the L3–L5 endplates were determined using commercial ELISA kits per the manufacturers’ directions.

Western blotting

Proteins extracted from the endplate were separated using SDS-PAGE and then blotted onto nitrocellulose membranes (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA). The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against p16 (1:1000, ab211542, Abcam), p21 (1:200, ab109199, Abcam), p53 (1:500, ab26, Abcam), Sirt1(1:1000, ab110304, Abcam), BMI-1(1:1000, ab269678, Abcam), γH2A.X(1:1000, ab81299, Abcam), Endomucin (1:1000, ab106100, Abcam), CD31 (1:1000, sc-376764, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc.), Osterix (1:1000, ab209484, Abcam), Osteocalcin (1:1000, ab309521, Abcam), and GAPDH (1:1000, ab8245, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. Immunoblotting was conducted as previously described [20, 23, 24, 27–29].

Statistical analyses

The data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs) and were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10.0 (Boston, USA). Group differences were evaluated with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons or two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests for pairwise comparisons. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. Blinding was maintained throughout both data collection and analysis to minimize bias.

Results

PQQ attenuates LSI surgery-induced hyperalgesia and anxiety behaviors

LSI was induced in 8-week-old C57/BL6 mice through surgical resection of the L3–L5 spinous processes and the supraspinous and interspinous ligaments (Fig. 1A) [11, 12, 21]. To assess spinal hypersensitivity, a series of pain-related behavioral tests were performed on different groups of mice. We first measured the vocalization threshold in response to force applied on the L4/L5 disk region. Pressure tolerance decreased significantly after LSI surgery relative to that observed in sham-operated mice, indicating the development of low back pressure hyperalgesia (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, we monitored spontaneous wheel-running activity to determine the potential effect of spinal pain, assessing the distance traveled, maximum speed of movement, and active time per 24 h. The results revealed that each measure of spontaneous activity decreased significantly at 8 weeks after LSI surgery relative to that observed after sham surgery (Fig. 1C). The hot plate test revealed a significant delay in heat response times in LSI mice (Fig. 1D), indicating thermal hypersensitivity, which is a common sign of neuropathic pain following spinal injury. Moreover, we assessed mechanical hyperalgesia of the hind paw as referred pain by performing von Frey analysis as a secondary indicator of symptomatic LBP. We found that the paw withdrawal frequency increased significantly after LSI surgery (Fig. 1E). Importantly, PQQ treatment significantly improved behavioral outcomes. Treated mice showed greater activity levels, quicker responses to heat, and lower sensitivity to mechanical stimuli. These findings suggest that PQQ effectively alleviates hypersensitivity associated with spinal degeneration.

LBP and anxiety symptoms are closely linked, with several studies demonstrating a strong positive correlation between anxiety and pain catastrophizing [30]. Previous studies have employed open-field (Fig. 1F) and elevated plus maze tests (Fig. 1G) to assess anxiety-related behaviors in mice following LSI surgery [25]. Our results showed that, by 8 weeks after LSI surgery, mice in the vehicle group exhibited significantly increased anxiety-like behaviors. In contrast, PQQ treatment markedly alleviated these anxiety-related behaviors at the same time point. This effect was evidenced by an increase in the distance traveled, time spent in the center area, and duration spent in the open arms (Fig. 1F, G). These results suggest that PQQ effectively alleviates LBP and anxiety at 8 weeks following LSI surgery.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that PQQ treatment significantly alleviates both spinal hypersensitivity and anxiety-like behaviors following LSI surgery. Given the reduction in pain responses and improved activity and emotional state, PQQ shows promise as a potential therapy for managing LBP and its associated psychological effects.

PQQ reduces endplate porosity and trabecular separation

To evaluate endplate porosity in the LSI model, we analyzed the L4–L5 caudal endplates at 8 weeks post-surgery using three-dimensional micro-CT imaging and histological staining. Three-dimensional micro-CT reconstructions revealed distinct differences between the LSI and sham-operated groups. The LSI group exhibited porous endplates, whereas the sham group maintained an intact microstructure. Further structural analysis highlighted the therapeutic effects of PQQ; key morphometric parameters, such as BV/TV and Tb.Th, were preserved in the PQQ-treated group. Additionally, PQQ significantly reduced Tb.Sp. These findings indicate that PQQ effectively alleviates the structural degradation associated with LSI-induced spinal degeneration (Fig. 2A). Histological examination using Safranin O, Fast Green, and hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed bone marrow cavities in the degenerative endplates of LSI mice, whereas those in the sham group demonstrated well-preserved, homogeneous cartilaginous endplates. Notably, the PQQ-treated LSI group displayed a significant reduction in the endplate score, in contrast to the Veh-treated LSI mice, which exhibited an increased endplate score (Fig. 2B, C).

To investigate the cellular mechanisms underlying spinal degeneration, we selected a panel of markers involved in osteoclast activity (TRAP), cellular senescence (p16 and SA-βGal), extracellular matrix remodeling (MMP-13 and Collagen X), and neurovascular changes (PGP9.5, CGRP, CD31, and Endomucin), according to previous reports [12, 25] and their established roles in spinal pathophysiology. Immunofluorescent staining targeting matrix metalloproteinase 13 (MMP13) and type X collagen (Col X) within the endplates further highlighted the effects of PQQ treatment. The PQQ-treated LSI group showed a marked reduction in the distribution of MMP13 and Col X within the endplates versus that in the sham-operated group, whereas the Veh-treated LSI group demonstrated increased MMP13 and Col X distribution (Fig. 2D, E). Additionally, TRAP staining revealed a significant increase in the number of TRAP + osteoclasts within the endplates of LSI mice relative to that in the sham group. Importantly, PQQ treatment notably reduced the number of TRAP + osteoclasts within the endplates versus that observed with Veh treatment (Fig. 2F).

Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of PQQ as a disease-modifying treatment for spinal degeneration, particularly in preserving the microarchitecture of the spinal endplates and offering structural protection. Our results suggest that PQQ plays a pivotal role in mitigating spinal degeneration and maintaining endplate remodeling, indicating promising therapeutic implications for spine-related disorders.

PQQ mitigates spinal degeneration by reducing snocs and modulating inflammatory profiles

A significant increase in SnOCs was observed within the spinal endplates of LSI versus control mice. Costaining for senescence markers (e.g., p16 and HMGB1) and osteoclast markers (e.g., TRAP) confirmed the presence of SnOCs. Quantitative analysis revealed a marked increase in SnOC density in LSI mice, and PQQ treatment notably reduced SnOC populations in these mice. Immunostaining revealed a substantial decrease in the number of p16 + TRAP + and HMGB1 + TRAP + double-positive cells in the endplates of LSI + PQQ mice compared with that observed in LSI + Veh mice (Fig. 3A, B). Specifically, we performed costaining for the senescence marker p16 and the osteoclast marker TRAP in the lumbar vertebrae of the three experimental groups (Fig. 3C). Our results showed that, in comparison with the sham group, LSI did not increase SnOC numbers within the lumbar vertebrae. Moreover, PQQ supplementation had no significant effect on the number of osteoclasts in the vertebrae (Fig. 3C). This finding suggests that PQQ selectively eliminates SnOCs instead of broadly affecting osteoclast biology. PQQ treatment also significantly inhibited the LSI-induced increase in SA-βGal + cells in the endplates, as evidenced by SA-βGal staining (Fig. 3D). Western blotting further revealed that PQQ treatment significantly suppressed the expression of senescence-associated proteins, including p16, p21, p53, and γH2A.X, while increasing the expression of anti-aging proteins such as Sirt1 and BMI-1 (Fig. 3E). qRT-PCR demonstrated that PQQ treatment significantly reduced the LSI-induced elevation of SASP factors, including IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1, MMP-13, TNF-α, TGF-β, NF-κB, CEBPβ, GATA4, and PAI-1 (Fig. 3F). Additionally, PQQ appeared to modulate the secretory phenotype of SnOCs. The analysis of proinflammatory markers revealed a significant reduction in the levels of SASP factors in the spinal endplates of PQQ-treated mice; these factors, including IL-6 and TNF-α, are known to exacerbate the inflammatory microenvironment driving spinal degeneration (Fig. 3G).

The aforementioned findings suggest that PQQ effectively mitigates the pathological effects of SnOCs in the context of LSI by reducing their population and modulating their inflammatory profiles. These results underscore the potential of PQQ as a therapeutic agent for combating spinal degeneration and associated inflammatory processes, indicating a promising approach for future treatment strategies.

PQQ reduces sensory nerve innervation in LSI models

Previous studies have demonstrated that sensory nerve innervation occurs within the porous endplates, contributing to spinal hypersensitivity in both LSI and aged mouse models [11, 12]. To investigate the involvement of SnOCs in this process, we performed co-immunostaining for CGRP, a marker of peptidergic nociceptive C-fibers, and PGP9.5, a pan-neuronal marker, in the endplates of mice from various experimental groups. In the LSI model, we observed an increased number of SnOCs correlating with enhanced sensory nerve innervation in the porous endplates, as evidenced by elevated immunofluorescent signals for CGRP and PGP9.5 (Fig. 4A). Importantly, mice subjected to LSI and fed a diet containing PQQ exhibited a marked reduction in CGRP + PGP9.5 + nerve fibers compared with those observed in their vehicle-treated counterparts (Fig. 4A), suggesting that PQQ attenuates aberrant nerve growth associated with spinal instability.

Netrin-1, a well-known axon guidance cue, is secreted by osteoclasts and has been shown to promote sensory nerve outgrowth [31, 32]. In the LSI model, TRAP + osteoclasts have been identified as a major source of Netrin-1 within the sclerotic endplates following surgical induction of instability [11]. Consistent with this finding, we observed increased expression of the Netrin-1 receptor DCC in the LSI group (Fig. 4B). This receptor plays a critical role in mediating Netrin-1-induced axonal sprouting [33], further implicating the Netrin-1/DCC axis in the pathological neuroplasticity observed in LSI.

To further elucidate the inflammatory milieu contributing to nerve innervation, we assessed the expression of proinflammatory mediators via immunostaining and qRT-PCR. COX-2 expression was significantly elevated in the L4–L5 endplates at 8 weeks post-surgery (Fig. 4C, D). This finding aligns with previous reports linking aberrant endplate innervation with increased levels of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), a key mediator of spinal hypersensitivity and mechanical allodynia in degenerative conditions [11]. Concordantly, we detected increased mRNA expression of prostaglandin E synthase (PGES) (Fig. 4D) and elevated PGE2 concentrations (Fig. 4E) in the LSI group.

We also quantified Netrin-1 levels using ELISA and observed a significant increase in the lower lumbar endplates of LSI mice compared with that in the sham-operated controls at 8 weeks post-surgery (Fig. 4F), further confirming the enhanced neurotrophic signaling in this model.

Importantly, PQQ treatment effectively mitigated these pathological changes. In PQQ-treated mice, the levels of COX-2, PGES, PGE2, and Netrin-1 were all significantly reduced, suggesting that PQQ not only modulates inflammatory pathways but also attenuates neurotrophic signaling associated with pain and degeneration.

Taken together, these findings highlight the multifactorial role of PQQ in alleviating spinal degeneration and associated pain. By targeting SnOCs and downregulating neuroinflammatory and neurotrophic pathways, PQQ demonstrates therapeutic potential in the management of spinal disorders characterized by instability and aberrant nerve innervation.

PQQ reduces angiogenesis and osteogenesis in LSI models

Previous studies have identified CD31 + Emcn + type-H vessels as a capillary subtype that integrates angiogenesis and osteogenesis [34]. We previously demonstrated that LSI or aging triggers endplate sclerosis [11]. To explore the role of CD31 + Emcn + blood vessels in LSI-induced endplate sclerosis, we examined the caudal endplates of L4/5. Immunofluorescent staining revealed significant infiltration of CD31 + Emcn + blood vessels into the sclerotic endplates of LSI mice 8 weeks post-surgery, contrasting with the lack of vascular sprouting in the cartilaginous endplates of sham-operated mice (Fig. 5A). This finding suggests that angiogenesis is critical to the pathophysiology of LSI-induced endplate degeneration. Additionally, LSI mice exhibited increased osteoblast progenitors (Osx+) and mature osteoblasts (OCN+) in the endplates 8 weeks post-surgery in comparison with the sham controls (Fig. 5B, C), indicating enhanced osteogenesis during degeneration. Immunofluorescence analysis showed decreased CD31 + Emcn + vessel formation in PQQ-treated LSI mice, suggesting that PQQ suppresses aberrant angiogenesis associated with endplate degeneration (Fig. 5A). PQQ also reduced the presence of Osx + and OCN + osteoblasts in the endplates (Fig. 5B, C). Western blotting and qRT-PCR confirmed that PQQ treatment prevented the LSI-induced upregulation of Emcn, CD31, Osx, and OCN in the endplates (Fig. 5D). These findings suggest that PQQ may be a promising therapeutic strategy to suppress pathological angiogenesis and aberrant osteogenesis during spinal endplate degeneration.

Discussion

LBP is a widespread and debilitating condition, often resulting from spinal degeneration [4]. Our study highlights the therapeutic potential of PQQ in mitigating pain and degeneration in an LSI mouse model. The results provide compelling evidence that PQQ alleviates spinal hypersensitivity and anxiety behaviors and preserves the microarchitecture of the spinal endplates by targeting SnOCs.

Despite the widespread effect of LBP, its natural progression remains poorly understood, and effective therapeutic options to address this multifactorial condition remain lacking. The primary therapeutic approaches for managing LBP include behavioral management, pharmacological treatments such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or muscle relaxants, and surgical interventions, all of which aim to preserve function and alleviate symptoms [35]. However, these treatments are often accompanied by serious side effects, such as gastrointestinal complications, cardiovascular risks, and, particularly in the case of opioids, the potential for addiction. Given these challenges, there is an urgent need for safer and more effective treatment strategies that alleviate spinal pain and address the underlying causes of the condition.

A key finding of our study is the identification of PQQ as a potent senolytic agent that reduces the number of SnOCs in the spinal endplates. Cellular senescence is a hallmark of aging and a significant driver of various chronic diseases, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and osteoporosis [13, 20, 27, 29, 36]. Cellular senescence is a state of stable and irreversible cell cycle arrest induced by various stressors, such as telomere attrition, DNA damage, oxidative stress, oncogene activation, and other adverse environmental factors [13].

Senescent cells contribute to metabolic changes and organ dysfunction, triggering or accelerating disease initiation and progression, ultimately leading to accelerated aging [37]. Key characteristics of senescent cells include cell cycle arrest, morphological alterations, nuclear membrane defects, and metabolic changes [13]. Despite cell cycle arrest, these cells remain metabolically active and secrete various bioactive molecules, including proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and matrix metalloproteases, collectively known as the SASP [38]. The SASP involves molecules such as proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and IL-8), chemokines, matrix remodeling enzymes, and growth factors. These senescent cells are resistant to apoptosis and exhibit a distinct gene expression profile. Moreover, they can disrupt the function of surrounding cells, contributing to chronic inflammation, a phenomenon often referred to as inflammaging [38]. The cumulative harmful effects of senescent cells, such as reduced replicative capacity and the persistent inflammatory secretome, can disrupt tissue homeostasis, affecting both young and, more significantly, aging tissues [39]. The components of the SASP can modulate the local tissue environment through autocrine or paracrine signaling, with outcomes ranging from tissue repair and regeneration to chronic inflammation and tissue degeneration. In the skeletal system, increasing attention has been given to the role of cellular senescence in the development of osteoarthritis [40], osteoporosis [41], skeletal growth [42], and IVD nucleus pulposus degeneration [43]. Emerging evidence suggests that senolytic drugs may help delay age-related IVD degeneration [44].

SnOCs contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of spinal degeneration by promoting inflammation, bone resorption, and sensory nerve innervation [11, 12]. These cells are a key source of the SASP, which exacerbates the inflammatory microenvironment [39], driving both spinal pain and degeneration [11, 12]. Our results show that PQQ effectively reduces the number of SnOCs and their associated inflammatory markers, including IL-6 and TNF-α. This finding suggests that PQQ exerts its therapeutic effects by modulating the SASP and inhibiting the chronic inflammation associated with spinal degeneration.

In addition to reducing SnOC populations, PQQ treatment preserved the structural integrity of the spinal endplates. Through micro-CT imaging and histological analysis, we observed that PQQ treatment reduced endplate porosity, minimized Tb.Sp, and inhibited bone resorption. These findings highlight PQQ’s potential as a disease-modifying therapy for spinal degeneration, capable of addressing both symptomatic pain and the underlying structural degradation of the spinal tissues. The reduction in MMP13 and Col X expression further supports the notion that PQQ helps limit pathological remodeling, which is often associated with spinal instability and LBP [45].

One critical finding of our study is that PQQ reduces abnormal sensory nerve innervation in degenerated endplates, which contributes to its analgesic effect. Previous studies have identified endplate sclerosis in both LSI and aging models, with spinal pain arising from sensory innervation in the porous regions of the sclerotic endplates [11]. We demonstrated that LSI mice exhibit enhanced sensory nerve growth within the porous endplates, a feature that contributes to spinal hypersensitivity [7, 9, 10]. Notably, PQQ treatment significantly reduced sensory nerve innervation by modulating the expression of Netrin-1, a neurotrophic factor secreted by SnOCs [31]. This reduction in nerve sprouting aligns with the analgesic effects of PQQ observed in the behavioral tests, underscoring its potential to alleviate pain through neurotrophic signaling modulation.

Our findings also suggest that PQQ can reduce angiogenesis and osteogenesis in LSI-induced spinal degeneration. The formation of new blood vessels and osteoblasts in the endplates contributes to the pathological remodeling observed in spinal degeneration [11]. PQQ treatment significantly decreased the number of CD31 + Emcn + vessels and osteoblasts in the endplates, which may help preserve the structural integrity of the spine and reduce further degeneration.

Our findings highlight the multifaceted therapeutic potential of PQQ in spinal degeneration. By simultaneously targeting cellular senescence, reducing inflammation, and modulating neurovascular remodeling, PQQ offers a promising strategy for alleviating pain and preventing structural deterioration in degenerative spinal disorders. Furthermore, its antioxidant properties and selective action on senescent cells may reduce the side effects often associated with other senolytic drugs [20], making it a safer alternative for clinical applications.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that PQQ could be a valuable therapeutic agent for treating spinal degeneration and pain, particularly in disorders associated with instability and aging (Fig. 6). Future clinical studies are needed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of PQQ in human populations. Further exploration of the molecular mechanisms of PQQ, especially its effects on cellular senescence and neurovascular interactions, will help refine its potential as a disease-modifying therapy for spinal disorders.
