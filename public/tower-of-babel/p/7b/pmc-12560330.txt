Patient engagement in their healthcare decision making and associated factors among adult outpatients with non-communicable chronic diseases in public hospitals of North Shewa Zone, central Ethiopia: a mixed-method study
Authors: Worku Daba, Mathewos Mekonnen, Wude Biru, Tesfu Zewdu, Ababo Demeke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient engagement, involving patients in their healthcare and decision-making, is vital for promoting beneficial health behaviors. Disengaged patients face worse health outcomes and higher health costs. Despite the emphasis of the World Health Organization and Ethiopia’s national non-communicable diseases, studies in Africa, especially in Ethiopia, are limited and often overlook social support, a critical factor, which points to the need for comprehensive research. Therefore, this study assessed patients’ engagement in their healthcare decision-making and associated factors among adult patients with chronic non-communicable diseases in public hospitals of North Shewa, central Ethiopia.MethodsA mixed-method study was conducted from June to July 2024. A total of 265 patients were selected using systematic random sampling, and data were collected through interviewer-administered questionnaires. Patient engagement is measured by 13 items using the standardized Patient Activation Measure questionnaire. Qualitative data were collected through in-depth interviews with 14 purposively selected participants. Quantitative data were entered into Epi Data version 4.6 and analyzed using SPSS version 24. A binary logistic regression analysis was used to test associations, where variables with a p-value < 0.25 in bivariate analysis were included in multivariable analysis. Significance was set at a p-value < 0.05. Qualitative data were manually sorted and themed, and the meaning units were cited with relevant details.ResultsOnly 24.9%, 95% CI (19.8%, 30.6%), had high engagement in decision-making. Higher patient engagement was positively associated with: urban residence (AOR = 3.35, 95% CI: 1.02, 11.03), longer duration of diagnosis (≥ 5 years) (AOR = 3.73, 95% CI: 1.22, 11.42), health literacy (AOR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.14, 1.70), shared decision-making (AOR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.15, 1.58), and autonomy in decision-making (AOR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.12, 1.58). The qualitative study explored factors affecting patient engagement, such as physician-patient relationship, time shortage, lack of accessibility to healthcare information, organizational factors, and breach of patient autonomy.ConclusionPatient engagement in healthcare decision-making is generally low in the study area. Residence, diagnosis duration, health literacy, physician-patient relationship, shared decision-making, time shortage, lack of information, and autonomy breach influence patient engagement. Therefore, healthcare providers should enhance communication, improve information accessibility, and foster an environment that supports patient autonomy and informed participation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24853-z.Keywords: Patient-engagement, Chronic disease, Decision-making, Mixed-method, North Shewa

Introduction

Patient engagement signifies the extent to which patients actively participate in their healthcare [1]. Patient engagement in decision-making involves patients contributing to decision-making by providing information and sharing their needs, feelings, and opinions about various treatment options [2]. It encompasses both patient activation and treatment, aimed at increasing activation and fostering beneficial patient behavior, like regular exercise or preventive care [3].

Non-communicable chronic diseases result from an interplay of genetic, physiological, environmental, and behavioral factors [4]. It exhibits a protracted and slow disease course characterized by a gradual onset and extended incubation periods. These conditions often persist without a definitive cure, necessitating ongoing, medium-to-long-term strategies for their prevention and management [5].

Non-communicable chronic diseases are responsible for 41 million deaths annually, representing 74% of global mortality. Cardiovascular diseases contribute to 17.9 million of non-communicable disease deaths each year, while diabetes, including deaths from renal complications, accounts for 2.0 million deaths [4]. In Ethiopia, it causes 42% of all deaths, with 27% of these fatalities occurring before the age of 70 [6]. A recent study in Ethiopia indicated that more than 75% of chronic non-communicable disease follow-ups involve hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and heart diseases [7].

To manage and prevent complications arising from non-communicable diseases, lifelong care is essential, involving active participation from healthcare professionals and patients [5]. When patients engage actively in their healthcare decisions, they are better positioned to make informed choices about their treatment options [4]. This engagement also ensures the efficient use of resources, enables patients to confidently express their views, and promotes mutual understanding and accountability with healthcare providers. Moreover, such engagement enhances satisfaction for patients and providers, familiarizes patients with their medications and treatment plan, and ultimately improves adherence to their overall treatment protocols [4, 7, 8]. Patients who don’t actively engage in their healthcare decision-making are less likely to experience health improvements [9] and adhere to prescribed medications [10]. In contrast, they are more likely to visit emergency rooms or require hospitalization [11, 12], resulting in poor clinical outcomes, waste of healthcare resources, and increased complications [13]. Additionally, less engaged patients incur higher healthcare costs [12], are more prone to medical errors [14], delay seeking medical treatment, and have unmet healthcare needs [15].

Previous studies have identified several factors affecting patient engagement in decision-making, notably low levels of health literacy [16], preference for autonomy [17], residence, educational level, access to health information, and duration since diagnosis [4, 18]. While some variables such as age, sex, marital status, occupation, and source of medical funding have shown inconsistent associations, this study incorporated a broader range of potentially relevant variables, including socio-demographic, clinical, behavioral, and interactional factors. These include intentional health information seeking, awareness of patients’ rights to participate in decisions, shared decision-making practices, and perceived social support.

The World Health Organization’s global action plan for the prevention and control of non-communicable chronic diseases provides detailed recommendations to enhance patients engagement in their healthcare, post-visit care, and self-management [19]. Ethiopia’s national non-communicable disease plan involves consulting and cooperating with different stakeholders, consumers, communities, and civil society [6].

Despite these strategies, patient engagement in decision-making remains low and there has been limited data regarding patient engagement in Africa, particularly in Ethiopia. To the extent of our knowledge, there has been limited study that explored the deep-rooted attributes of patient engagement in decision-making. In addition, the majority of previous literature did not study the influence of social support, which has a strong link with patient engagement in decision-making. Therefore, this study assessed patient engagement in healthcare decision-making and its associated factors among adults with chronic non-communicable diseases in public hospitals of North Shewa, central Ethiopia, in 2024.

Methods

Study area, design, and period

An institution-based mixed-method (concurrent triangulation) study was conducted from June 10 to July 10, 2024, using a cross-sectional design for the quantitative part and a phenomenological design for the qualitative part. The study was conducted in public hospitals in the North Shewa Zone of central Ethiopia. The capital city of the zone is Fiche, which is found 115 km from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. There are five hospitals in the zone known as Muke Turi Primary Hospital, Dera Primary Hospital, Chancho General Hospital, Kuyu General Hospital, and Salale University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital.

Study participants and eligibility criteria

This study was conducted among adult patients with selected non-communicable chronic diseases (hypertension, heart failure, and diabetes mellitus) who attended the chronic outpatient department. All volunteer adult patients with cardiovascular diseases (hypertension and heart failure) and diabetes mellitus who had attended their appointments for at least 6 months were included in this study, whereas patients who were critically ill during data collection were excluded from the study.

Sampling size and sampling techniques

For the quantitative part, a single population proportion formula was used to calculate sample size assuming a 5% margin of error with a 95% confidence level and a 19.5% population proportion [7]. By adding a 10% non-response rate, the total sample size was 265. And 14 study participants were selected for the qualitative study based on the concept of information saturation.

To select study participants, first, from five hospitals in the Zone, three hospitals—Muke Turi Primary Hospital, Kuyu General Hospital, and Salale University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital—were randomly selected using simple random sampling. Based on the previous month’s patient flow, a total of 1,374 patients with chronic diseases were used as a baseline for sampling: 713 patients with hypertension, 564 patients with diabetes mellitus, and 97 patients with heart failure. Then, to obtain representative data in each hospital, proportional allocation of the sample was calculated based on monthly patient flow. Finally, individual participants were selected using a systematic random sampling method. The first eligible participant was chosen by lottery, followed by every fifth eligible patient attending follow-up visits during the study period (k = 5) (Fig. 1). For the qualitative study, interviewees were purposefully chosen, and in-depth interviews were conducted while maintaining their privacy and using a mobile recorder to document the interview process.

Study variables

The outcome of interest was patient engagement in healthcare decision-making. The explanatory variables included in the study were participants’ socio-demographic characteristics (such as age, sex, income, residence, education, marital status, occupation, source of medical wage (health insurance, self, non-governmental organization, university), intentional health information seeking, and source of health information), clinical related variables (including type of diseases, family history, duration of diagnosis, previous hospitalizations, patient’ awareness of the right to decision), patients’ health related behaviors (such as health literacy, and autonomy in decision making), preference for participation in decision making related factors (whether they preferred to make decision on their own, with care provider, or leave decisions to their care provider), patient-professional interaction (shared decision making) and social support.

Data collection tool and procedures

A validated interviewer-administered, structured questionnaire adopted from a previous study [7] was used. A face-to-face interview using a translated questionnaire was conducted by three data collectors and one supervisor after obtaining informed consent from participants. The tool had been previously tested for validity and reliability and comprised six sections.

The socio-demographic characteristics were assessed by 13 questions in section one. In section two, clinical characteristics of participants were assessed using 6 (yes/no) items. In section three, patient preference of participating in decision-making was assessed by using 3 (yes/no) items. And health literacy was evaluated using a 5-item Likert scale ranging from 1 (“cannot find”) to 5 (“very easy”), with scores ranging from 5 to 25 and a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.9. In addition, patient preference for autonomy was assessed using a 4-item, 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), with scores ranging from 4 to 20 and a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.79. This scale captures individuals’ desire for involvement and control in healthcare decisions, reflecting their motivational inclination toward autonomous engagement.

Furthermore, the shared decision-making section was assessed using 9 items on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), with scores ranging from 9 to 45 and a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.92. The standardized Patient Activation Measure questionnaire [7] was used to assess the level of patient engagement in healthcare decision-making. It included 13 items on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 4 (“strongly agree”), with scores ranging from 13 to 52 and a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.88. Lastly, part six assessed social support using the Oslo-3 Social Support Scale [7], which included 3 items with scores ranging from 3 to 14 and a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.80.

For the qualitative study, unstructured interview questions (interview guide) were prepared after reviewing various articles. Consequently, in-depth interviews were conducted while maintaining participants’ privacy and using a mobile recorder to document the interview process.

Data quality control

To ensure data quality, two-day training was given for the data collectors and supervisors regarding the tool and procedures. Two weeks prior to actual data collection, a pretest was done on 5% of the total sample at a non-selected hospital (Dera Primary Hospital). Additionally, the English version of the questionnaire was translated into Afan Oromo and then back into English by two language experts to ensure accuracy. Daily supervision was carried out to verify data completeness. To thoroughly explore the study’s thematic area, the transcribed data was read in its entirety, and the recorded materials were listened to multiple times for familiarization. The phone recordings were cross-checked with each point in the notebook to ensure consistency.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative data were edited and coded before being imported into EpiData version 4.6 and exported to SPSS version 24. Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the frequency and proportions for categorical variables and the mean and standard deviation for continuous variables. All variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariable logistic regression analysis were included in the multivariable analysis. The model fitness and multicollinearity were checked by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and VIF, respectively. A p-value < 0.05 along with AOR and 95% CI was used to announce the association.

For the qualitative data, two language experts who are fluent in both Afan Oromo and English translated and transcribed it into words and phrases. Then, it was entered into Atlas.ti version 7 software. Data were analyzed inductively by using the thematic data analysis approach. Finally, evidence was quoted from the interviews to illustrate each theme. After extensively reviewing the coded data, subthemes and themes were extracted. The findings of the qualitative study were integrated with quantitative findings using a triangulation approach.

Operational definition

Good engagement: individuals who scored 55.1% and above in patient activation measure were classified as having high engagement in healthcare decision-making [7].

Poor engagement: individuals scoring less than 55.1% of patient activation measure were classified as having low engagement in healthcare decision-making [7].

Results

A total of 265 participants took part in the study, yielding a 100% response rate. Among the participants, slightly more than half, 138 (52.1%), were female, and 102 (38.5%) participants were aged between 45 and 59 years. Around 140 (52.8%) of the participants were resided in urban areas. In terms of education, 117 (44.2%) of them had no formal education (Table 1).

Key informants’ profile and themes developed

In the qualitative part of this study, a total of 14 interviewees participated. These include two 32-year-old married women with primary and college-level education; a 65-year-old diploma-holding woman; a 30-year-old degree-holder man residing in an urban setting; a 63-year-old married man with primary education; a 58-year-old married female college graduate; a 27-year-old male from a rural area with secondary education; a 22-year-old rural female with secondary education; a 32-year-old rural male without formal education; a 60-year-old urban male with an MSc degree; a 59-year-old married urban male with a degree; a 52-year-old urban male with a diploma; and a 67-year-old rural female with no formal education. The total duration of follow-up ranged from 2 to 15 years (Table 2).

After thorough review, three major themes were identified during qualitative data coding and analysis, including participants health information seeking, patients’ level of engagement, and factors affecting patient engagement in healthcare decision-making. Factors affecting patient engagement have 6 subthemes, including physician-patient relationship, accessibility of healthcare information, shortage of time, organizational barriers, lack of awareness, and breach of patients’ autonomy. These themes are narrated and quoted by mentioning the interviewee’s code.

Heath information seeking

In this study, around 85 (32.1%) of participants had intentionally searched health information about their illness. Their major source of information was health professionals 184 (69.4%) (Table 1). Furthermore, this finding was supported by the qualitative findings. For instance, an in-depth interviewee stated:

“I need to understand my illness, including how it is treated, what I should and should not eat, and drink, the symptoms, and how to treat myself or take my medication if my blood sugar is high or low. So, I need to identify and search for information regarding my illness.” (R#08: A 27-year-old male patient).

Another participant added:

“I need to have a better understanding about my illness since it is difficult to judge if I don’t know about it. So, I must search for information about my illness. The hospital’s healthcare providers have offered health education as well, and I have heard much information from the media. But my doctor should properly inform me about my illness and its treatment when I arrive here.” (R#07: A 58-year-old hypertensive mother).

Participants’ clinical characteristics

Of total participants, 137 (51.7%) were hypertensive patients. About two-thirds, 177 (66.8%), of the participants do not have a family history of the same chronic disease. In terms of the duration since diagnosis, beyond half, 152 (57.4%) of the participants have been diagnosed with their condition for less than 5 years. Additionally, more than half (55.5%) of the participants had no prior hospitalizations related to their current condition (Table 3).

Level of participants’ health literacy

All items related to health literacy were summed and treated as continuous variables. The mean score and standard deviation of participants’ health literacy was 11.7 (SD ± 4.1).

Patients’ preference for autonomy

All items related to patients’ preference for autonomy were summed and treated as continuous variables. The preference for autonomy was found to have a mean of 8.4 (SD ± 3.3).

Participant’s extent of perceived shared decision making

All items in perceived shared decision-making were summed and treated as continuous variables. The mean score of shared decision-making was 20.6 (SD ± 4.5).

Patient engagement in healthcare decision making

According to this study, the prevalence of good engagement in healthcare decision-making was 24.9% (95% CI: 19.8, 30.6) (Fig. 2). The mean score of patients’ engagement in healthcare decision-making was 54.85 (SD ± 8.4). The minimum and maximum scores of patients’ engagement in healthcare decision-making were 38.5 and 76.9, respectively. The qualitative part also supported this finding. Accordingly, only 4 out of 14 participants had engaged in their health care decision-making. For instance, an in-depth interviewee stated:

Patient engagement in healthcare decision making among patients with non-communicable chronic diseases in public hospitals of North Shewa, 2024

“I can say I participated and made my own medical treatment decision. There was a time when I participated in changing my medication. Sometimes, I looked at my health condition and asked them to prescribe me another medication because I had learned a little bit about a health lesson, which is called ‘Tena Redat,’ before now. You probably may not know; ugh… Look, now I have finished my twelfth grade, degree, and master’s degree after that.” (R#011: A 60-year-old father).

Factors associated with a patient’s engagement in healthcare decision making

The multivariable analysis showed that the odds of having good patient engagement in decision-making were about 3.4 times [AOR = 3.35, 95% CI (1.018, 11.026)] higher among urban residents when compared to their rural resident counterparts. The qualitative finding also supported this association. For instance, an in-depth interviewee said that:

“…When I visit this hospital, I just inform my doctor about my health condition problems, and he decides for me what tests and drugs are suitable. But some healthcare providers do not engage patients coming from rural areas in their healthcare decision-making, as the rural people do not understand things quickly and easily. So, patients in this category do not engage in their medical treatment decision-making.” (R#014: A 67-year-old mother).

In this study, participants with a diagnosis duration of five years and above were about 3.7 times [AOR = 3.73, 95% CI (1.217, 11.42)] more likely to be engaged in their health care decision-making when compared with those who had a diagnosis duration of less than five years. The qualitative finding also supported this association. For instance, an in-depth interviewee stated that:

“…Now, it was for the first time when I heard about this disease. I have never seen this disease, nor is it in our family. I have not seen such a disease in our neighborhood. When they told me I had this disease, I was very shocked. Even I fainted here because the disease was new to me at that time. Ugh… what I want to say is, you may not be involved in your medical treatment decision when such a new condition is encountered.” (R#013).

This study also showed that, for one unit increase in participants’ health literacy score, there was a 1.4 unit increase in patient engagement in their healthcare decision-making [AOR = 1.39, 95% CI (1.135, 1.698)] on average. This result is supported by a theme from the qualitative finding. For instance, an in-depth interviewee mentioned that:

“…Only healthcare providers have the right and responsibility of deciding what is best for a patient because they are experts. Patients have only to abide by what healthcare providers decide and plan.” (R#014: A 67-year-old diabetic mother).

Additionally, this study revealed that, for one unit increase in participant’s shared decision-making score, there was a 1.3 unit increase in patients’ engagement in their healthcare decision-making [AOR = 1.34, 95% CI (1.146, 1.576)] on average. Similarly, for one unit increase in participant’s score on preference for autonomy, there was a 1.3 unit increase in patients’ engagement in healthcare decision-making [AOR = 1.33, 95% CI (1.119, 1.581)] on average (Table 4).

I.Physician-patient relationship: More than two-thirds of the participants, 10 (71.4%), perceived the physician-patient relationship as a factor that affects their healthcare decision-making. The following specific factors have been identified: partiality, a lack of patient education, carelessness or disinterest, trouble welcoming patients, a lack of interest in asking questions, and a failure to listen to patients. This was evident in the following statement made by one of the participants. “If I have problems and tell them, ‘this is my sickness’, some doctors can’t listen to me. Immediately they start pretending that they are in a hurry and respond with short answers. Such action is generally a big problem in this house, and I take it as a special weakness. If clients are overwhelmed, he should help as much as he can. But if he says he will get it all together and passes one without curing, it is a problem in itself. I understand that there are many patients in this hospital, but rather than running to reach everyone, it is better to do something of quality to make a difference for one, two, or three patients. Even…now, I am going to stop taking this drug and go ‘tsebel.’”(R#07: A 58-year-old mother). Other participant also stated: “Lack of familiarity with the clinician makes it uncomfortable to engage in decision-making, because if there is no relationship with a physician, they do not allow you to engage in medical treatment decisions. Having a relationship with a physician makes decision-making convenient and provides more opportunity to share decision-making power. For example, I would be more interested in sharing responsibility with a practitioner that I am familiar with, because he knows my history and my illness condition.”(R#02: A 32-year-old hypertensive woman).

Discussion

In this study, about one in four participants had good patient engagement in healthcare decision-making [24.9%, 95% CI (19.8, 30.6)]. This finding was lower than the findings of previous studies done in Australia (68%) [20], Malaysia (61.8%) [21], Iran (58.6%) [22], Turkey (55%) [23], and Nebraska, USA (38%) [24]. Systemic variations in healthcare infrastructure, policy focus on patient-centered care, and the existence of models like patient-centered medical home-care which are not commonly used in our country, may all be responsible for these disparities [7]. Similarly, this finding is lower than that of Southwest Ethiopia (35.8%) [18]. Its focus on higher-level hospitals and wider NCD coverage [18], in contrast to this study, may be the cause of the disparity in the finding. However, the finding of this study was higher than the study conducted in public hospitals of West Shewa, Ethiopia (19.5%) [7]. A possible explanation could be the difference in the type of care setting and duration of diagnosis, where patients with a longer diagnostic period undergo behavioral changes over time [8].

In this study, the odds of having good patient engagement in healthcare decision-making were about 3.4 times higher among urban residents when compared to their rural resident counterparts. This finding was consistent with the studies done at the University of Wyoming [25], Nebraska, USA [24], and Southwest Ethiopia [18]. The possible justification might be due to urban residents typically having greater access to information from diverse media such as TV, radio, newspapers, and online sources that provide health-related information and awareness, enabling patient engagement in healthcare decision-making [26]. Again, this result was also supported by the qualitative findings of this study. Accordingly, rural residents engage less due to lack of access to health-related information through posters and the internet.

Additionally, this study also revealed that participants with a diagnosis duration of five years and above were about 3.7 times more likely to be engaged in their healthcare decision-making when compared with those who had a diagnosis duration of less than five years. This finding is also supported by the findings of a study conducted in Australia [27], West Shoa [7], and Southwest Ethiopia [18]. This association might be due to the fact that patients became more ready to participate in their care as their behavior changed over time, their understanding of their illness improved, and their experience grew over time [27]. The qualitative part of the study also explains that newly diagnosed patients are confused and shocked with their health problem, which necessitates them leaving the decision to their healthcare providers.

This study also showed that, for one unit increase in health literacy score, there was a 1.4 unit increase in patient engagement in their healthcare decision-making on average. This finding was in line with the studies conducted in Malaysia [28], Iran [29], Nepal [30], West Shoa [7], and Southwest Ethiopia [18]. Patients with higher health literacy may be able to interact with healthcare professionals more effectively because they can locate adequate knowledge to manage their health, comprehend healthcare providers more easily, and are well enough to know what to do [8]. Additionally, the qualitative component also supported that patients who have little to no knowledge of their health-related issues prefer not to participate in healthcare decision-making.

In addition, for one unit increase in shared decision-making score, there was a 1.3 unit increase in patients’ engagement in their healthcare decision-making. This finding was in line with the studies done in Massachusetts [31], Hong Kong [32], and Uganda [11]. A possible explanation could be that better communication and a positive patient-clinician interaction encourage patients to take an active role in their care [33].

Lastly, for one unit increase in the score of participants’ preference for autonomy, there was a 1.3 unit increase in patient engagement in their health care decision-making. This finding was in line with the studies conducted in the US [34], West Shoa [7], and Southwest Ethiopia [18]. This might be due to patients’ need for autonomy and their engagement in healthcare decision-making are positively correlated, according to a behavioral study [8]. The qualitative component also revealed that patients are less likely to participate in healthcare decision-making when their autonomy is violated.

Limitations of the study

The limitation of this study includes the use of cross-sectional design, which limits causal inference, and presents a “chicken-and-egg” dilemma. Additionally, exclusion of patients from inpatient and emergency departments may reduce generalizability.

Conclusion

This study reveals a critical gap in patient engagement in healthcare decision-making, which remains generally low in the study area. Factors such as residence, diagnosis duration, health literacy, physician-patient relationship, shared decision-making, time shortage, lack of information, and autonomy breach influence patient engagement. These structural and interpersonal barriers, identified by both quantitative and qualitative results, necessitate targeted interventions. Therefore, healthcare providers should enhance communication, improve information accessibility, and foster an environment that supports patient autonomy and informed participation. Early, structured patient education and respectful, inclusive communication strategies are essential to ensure all patients are meaningfully involved in decisions about their care.
