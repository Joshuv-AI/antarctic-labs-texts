Evaluation of Pulmonary Artery Flow Reserve in Patients With Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension: A Pilot Study
Authors: Jinzhi Wang, Linfeng Xi, Xincao Tao, Haobo Li, Lu Sun, Zhihui Fu, Wei Li, Zhiyuan Wang, Xincheng Li, Kaiyuan Zhen, Yishan Li, Shuangshuang Ma, Rui Liang, Wanmu Xie, Qian Gao, Shuai Zhang, Yunxia Zhang, Dingyi Wang, Zhu Zhang, Yu Zhang, Jixiang Liu, Peiran Yang, Qiang Huang, Zhenguo Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPressure‐wire fractional flow reserve (FFR) can guide balloon pulmonary angioplasty in chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, but lesion crossing with a distal wire increases cost and procedural risk. We developed an angiography‐derived physiologic index—pulmonary artery flow reserve (PFR)—computed from selective pulmonary angiography using fluid‐dynamic modeling without distal pressure‐wire advancement.MethodsIn this prospective observational study, selective pulmonary angiography was acquired in 2 projections for type A (ring‐like), type B (web lesion) and type C (subtotal) pulmonary artery lesions. Proximal pressure (Pa) was measured at the target vessel ostium using a pressure‐sensor catheter; flow velocity was estimated by frame counting; pressure drop (ΔP) was computed by a proprietary computational fluid dynamics approach, and PFR was derived as (Pa − ΔP)/Pa. Pressure‐wire FFR (Pd/Pa) served as the reference standard (FFR ≤0.80). Diagnostic performance was assessed by receiver operating characteristic analysis and agreement by Bland–Altman analysis.ResultsFifty‐two vessels in 29 patients were analyzed. Mean FFR and PFR were 0.57 ± 0.23 and 0.58 ± 0.24; 44 vessels had FFR ≤0.80. PFR showed excellent discrimination for FFR ≤0.80 (area under the curve 0.981, 95% CI 0.935–1.000), with sensitivity 94.1% (95% CI 80.9%–98.7%) and specificity 87.5% (95% CI 64.0%–97.0%) at an optimal PFR cutoff of 0.77. Agreement was high (bias 0.0076; 95% limits of agreement −0.1037 to 0.1190), and correlation was strong (r=0.970, P<0.001). At 3‐month follow‐up (n=45 vessels), PFR maintained high performance (area under the curve 0.985; bias 0.0071; r=0.967; all P<0.001).ConclusionsAngiography‐derived PFR demonstrated strong agreement with pressure‐wire FFR for physiologic assessment of pulmonary artery stenoses in chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension and may provide a simpler alternative to distal pressure‐wire measurements to support physiology‐guided balloon pulmonary angioplasty.Keywords: balloon pulmonary angioplasty, chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, fractional flow reserve, pulmonary artery flow grade, pulmonary artery flow reserveSubject Categories: Vascular Disease, Treatment, Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiac Care

Research Perspective

What Is New?

Pulmonary artery flow reserve is a novel angiography‐derived index, calculated without the need for distal pressure‐wire advancement, offering a potential alternative to traditional pressure‐wire fractional flow reserve in assessing pulmonary artery stenosis in patients with chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension undergoing balloon pulmonary angioplasty.

What Question Should be Addressed Next?

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is classified as group 4 pulmonary hypertension (PH). 1 , 2 It is predominantly caused by unresolved thrombi leading to pulmonary artery obstruction. 3 , 4 , 5 It represents a relatively rare but life‐threatening pulmonary vascular disease if left untreated. The pathogenesis involves both mechanical obstruction of pulmonary arteries and pulmonary microvascular remodeling, resulting in progressive elevation of pulmonary artery pressure and potential right heart failure. 2 , 6 , 7 Extensive studies have shown that pulmonary endarterectomy, 8 , 9 balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA), 10 , 11 , 12 and targeted therapies for pulmonary arterial hypertension significantly improve patient outcomes. 13 , 14

BPA is a safe and effective treatment modality for CTEPH. 15 , 16 However, precise guidance and evaluation methods for BPA therapy in patients who were eligible for intervention remain lacking. Conventional approaches based on right heart catheterization data and pulmonary artery flow grading (PFG) 17 lack quantification and depend on operators' judgment. Fractional flow reserve (FFR), initially derived from the coronary artery flow assessment, can accurately guide the treatment of coronary artery lesions. 18 , 19 Some researchers have used pressure wires to evaluate pulmonary artery lesions, which showed an accurate assessment for clinical treatment effects. 1 , 20 , 21 Pressure wire‐based pulmonary artery FFR detection can be used to guide BPA and promote prognosis. 17 , 22 Since 2013, a pressure ratio of 0.8 has been used as the target for FFR in lesion assessment. 22 FFR has become widely utilized in clinical practice, playing a key role in therapeutic planning, interventional guidance, and post‐treatment evaluation. In parallel with wire‐based physiological assessment, emerging computational approaches have attempted to derive pulmonary FFR or stenosis‐related pressure loss from imaging and hemodynamic modeling. For example, a personalized pulmonary circulation modeling framework has been proposed to non‐invasively calculate pulmonary FFR for stenosis detection, reporting encouraging agreement with invasive measurements. 23 In addition, several multiscale or reduced‐order hemodynamic models have been developed for CTEPH to capture the distributed nature of thromboembolic obstruction and microvascular remodeling, providing a mechanistic basis for estimating pressure–flow relationships along the pulmonary arterial tree. 24 Subject‐specific 1‐dimensional fluid dynamics models have also been introduced to reproduce patient‐level pulmonary hemodynamics in CTEPH and to support rapid computation of pressure and flow distributions under different physiological conditions. Collectively, these studies support the feasibility of model‐based physiological quantification in pulmonary circulation, but they also highlight practical barriers for routine BPA guidance, such as the need for comprehensive inputs, boundary‐condition calibration, and workflow integration with catheter‐lab angiography, as well as the limited prospective evidence in BPA settings.

However, pressure wires are expensive and the wire advancement to the distal end of the lesion increases the risk of vascular injury. Therefore, a safe, effective and economical method to guide BPA is warranted. We aimed to construct a pulmonary artery flow reserve ratio (PFR) system based on computational fluid dynamics methods. Furthermore, the study evaluated the consistency between PFR and wire‐measured FFR.

METHODS

In line with the Transparency and Openness Promotion Guidelines, the data that support the study's findings can be obtained from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Study Design and Cohort

This prospective observational study was approved by the institutional review board of the China‐Japan Friendship Hospital (No. 2021‐136‐K94) and in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We retrospectively enrolled patients diagnosed with CTEPH who underwent BPA at the China‐Japan Friendship Hospital between April 2022 and December 2025. Inclusion criteria: (1) willing to conduct FFR measurements and had at least 1 ring‐like, web lesion or subtotal lesions in the pulmonary artery; (2) signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) poor quality of selective pulmonary angiography (SPA) images, which prevented the acquisition of PFR contours; (2) Type D and E; (3) incomplete clinical data. We conducted a 3‐month follow‐up for all patients, during which both PFR and FFR were reassessed to evaluate the efficacy of BPA. Patients were enrolled consecutively. Per procedure, 1–2 target vessels were chosen for paired FFR and PFR; if more eligible lesions existed, computer‐generated random sampling was applied to reduce operator bias. Only lesions meeting prespecified 2‐projection SPA reconstruction QC were included (typically types A‐C). Lesions failing QC‐often type D/E, severe overlap/foreshortening, or no stable distal reference segment‐were excluded to ensure reliable reconstruction and physiologic computation.

Clinical Characteristics

The baseline information, including sex, age, medical history was collected. Cardiopulmonary assessment revealed NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide), with WHO functional class distribution and a 6‐minute walking distance. Quality of life assessment indicated a pulmonary arterial hypertension score (emPHasis10). Hemodynamic parameters measured and calculated via right heart catheterization included mean right atrial pressure, mean pulmonary arterial pressure, cardiac output (CO), pulmonary vascular resistance, and mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2).

BPA Protocol

All procedures were performed under local anesthesia. The standard procedure has been described previously in detail. 25 An 80 cm 8F sheath was inserted via the femoral vein, and unfractionated heparin was administered at a dose of 50 U/kg. Hemodynamic measurements were performed, then a suitable guiding catheter, such as 6F was advanced through the sheath to the target vessel for SPA (Figure 1A). SPA was used to record angiographic images at a rate of 15 frames/s (Allura Xper FD20/10; PHILIPS Medical Systems, the Netherlands). The field of view ranged from 20 cm × 20 cm to 22 cm × 22 cm, with a matrix of 512 × 512. Tube current ranged from 500 to 800 mA, and tube voltage ranged from 60 to 120 kV. The contrast agent was injected at a flow rate of 4 mL/s using a high‐pressure injection pump to delineate lesion distribution. Using SPA, we randomly select ring‐like stenosis, web lesions and subtotal lesions for FFR measurement, PFG grading and BPA treatment. The PFG classification criteria are as follows 17 , 26 : Grade 0: No or minimal branch blood flow is observed; Grade 1: The distal branches of the pulmonary artery are opacified, but no pulmonary venous return is present; Grade 2: The distal branches of the pulmonary artery are opacified, with delayed pulmonary venous return (≥2 cardiac cycles); Grade 3: The distal branches of the pulmonary artery are opacified, with rapid pulmonary venous return (1 cardiac cycle). In addition, the procedure session was terminated when the fluoroscopy time reached 60 minutes, or contrast agent volume reached 300 mL.

FFR Measurement

All patients underwent FFR measurement using the Volcano S5 system and pressure wire (Verrata, Philips Volcano, San Diego, California, USA). After calibration and equalization, the pressure wire was introduced into the pulmonary artery and positioned distal to the stenosis. FFR was defined as the ratio of the mean distal pulmonary stenosis pressure to the mean proximal target stenosis pressure (Pa). Figure 1B showed FFR waveform and value. FFR measurement was conducted by 2 physicians with at least 6 years of experience in pulmonary interventional therapy. The FFR measurement value of 0.8 serves as the cutoff for BPA treatment.

PFR Calculation

The measurement of PFR requires the following steps: First, SPA was performed in 2 projections for the enrolled patients, and the acquired images were preprocessed. Then we further identify the affected vessels, computer processing of digitized imaging information was performed to eliminate skeletal and soft tissue images, enabling clear visualization of the corresponding vessels. Once 2‐dimensional pulmonary angiographic images from 2 different angles were obtained, a guiding catheter equipped with a dedicated pressure sensor (B.Braun Meisungen AG, Shanghai, China) was advanced into the affected pulmonary artery. Whenever feasible, we aimed for an angular separation of at least 25 degrees to strengthen geometric constraints; when this was not achievable, stricter reconstruction QC criteria were applied, and cases failing QC were excluded. The guiding catheter was positioned at the entrance of the evaluated vessel to measure the pressure (PFR‐Pa) at the entrance of the affected pulmonary artery. A specific frame of the SPA image was selected, and a simulated 3‐dimensional (3D) mesh reconstruction structure of the pulmonary artery was generated along the vessel path from the entrance to the farthest position (≥1 cm downstream of the farthest stenosis). Using frame counting, resting flow velocity (V, averaged from 2‐dimensional pulmonary artery angiographic images in 2 positions) was determined. As shown in Figure 1C, Figures S1 and S2, the final PFR was obtained through computer processing and fluid dynamics calculations. Offline PFR calculations were conducted by department of pulmonary and critical care medicine (China‐Japan Friendship Hospital, Beijing, China), with blinding between measurements of pressure wire FFR and PFR calculations. PFR was computed offline by an independent analyst who was not present in the catheterization laboratory and had no access to pressure‐wire FFR results or procedural reports at the time of analysis. FFR and PFR data sets were merged only after completion of both measurements for statistical analysis by an independent investigator, ensuring blinding of PFR computation to wire‐measured FFR. The primary end point was the diagnostic accuracy of PFR ≤0.80 for identifying hemodynamically significant pulmonary artery stenosis, using pressure‐wire FFR ≤0.80 as the reference standard. Detailed information about the methods, including PFR calculation, is provided in Data S1.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 3.6.3). Categorical variables were described as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using mean (± SD) or median (interquartile range). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess data normality. For normally distributed data, an independent samples t‐test was used to compare means of continuous variables; for non‐normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney test was applied. Categorical data were compared using the Chi‐square test or Fisher exact test. The performance of PFR for predicting functionally significant stenosis was assessed using sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. The area under the curve (AUC) on receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to assess the diagnostic accuracy of FFR and PFR. Agreement between angiography‐derived PFR and pressure‐wire FFR was assessed using Bland–Altman analysis, reporting the mean bias and the 95% limits of agreement, and visualized with Bland–Altman plots. The Pearson correlation analysis was used for correlation analysis of normally distributed metric data. A significance level of P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 29 patients with CTEPH were analyzed in this study (Figure 2), with a mean age of 63.6 ± 9.4 years. There were 15 female patients (51.7%). Past medical history revealed that 18 patients (62.1%) had pulmonary embolism, 10 patients (34.5%) had deep vein thrombosis, 6 patients (20.7%) had type I respiratory failure, 9 patients (31.0%) had a history of hypertension, 3 patients had coronary artery disease and diabetes, respectively (each 10.3%). Oral anticoagulant medications included warfarin in 6 cases (20.7%) and rivaroxaban in 23 cases (79.3%), while riociguat in 16 cases (55.2%), macitentan in 3 cases (10.3%), sildenafil in 1 case (3.4%). Diagnostic Performance of PFR Calculation

Bland–Altman analysis showed a small mean difference between the 2 methods (bias=0.0076), with 95% limits of agreement ranging from −0.1037 to 0.1190. Most paired measurements were distributed within these limits, indicating a high degree of agreement between the 2 methods across the clinically relevant range of flow reserve values (Figure 3A). Using an FFR threshold of ≤0.80 as the reference standard, PFR demonstrated high diagnostic performance. The cut‐off value of PFR was 0.77, The sensitivity of PFR for detecting functionally significant lesions was 94.1% (95% CI: 0.809–0.987), while the specificity for correctly identifying non‐significant lesions was 87.5% (95% CI: 0.640–0.970). The positive predictive value was 97.2% (95% CI: 0.809–0.984), indicating a high probability of true functional significance when PFR yielded a positive result. The negative predictive value was 87.5% (95% CI: 0.640–0.965), reflecting a moderate ability to exclude functionally significant disease. Overall, the diagnostic accuracy of PFR was 92.1% (95% CI: 0.812–0.969), supporting its reliability for functional lesion assessment. Receiver operating characteristic analysis further confirmed the excellent discriminatory ability of PFR, with an area under the curve (AUC=0.981, 95% CI: 0.935–1.000, P<0.001), indicating robust performance across the full range of PFR values (Figure 3B). PFR demonstrated a strong positive correlation with FFR (r=0.970, P<0.001), indicating good linear association between the 2 measurements (Figure 3C). Changes in FFR, PFR, and PFG Before and After Treatment

Using FFR value below 0.8 as a threshold for treatment guidance, a total of 44 vessels underwent BPA treatment. The average FFR after treatment was 0.66 ± 0.13, the average PFR after treatment was 0.67 ± 0.13, the average PFG was 3 (range 2 to 3) after treatment, demonstrated significant improvement (Figure 4H). Bland–Altman analysis of FFR and PFR demonstrated a minimal mean bias between the 2 methods (bias=0.0067), with 95% limits of agreement ranging from −0.0374 to 0.0509, suggesting excellent agreement and absence of systematic measurement error (Figure 4A). Receiver operating characteristic analysis further confirmed the excellent discriminatory ability of PFR, with an area under the curve (AUC=0.996, 95% CI: 0.976–1.000, P<0.001), indicating robust performance across the full range of PFR values (Figure 4B). Additionally, we observed a significant positive correlation between PFR and FFR (r=0.980, P<0.001) (Figure 4C).

At the 3‐month follow‐up, 1 patient who underwent treatment was lost to follow‐up; another patient was excluded because of poor image quality. The analysis was completed in 45 vessels (including 7 vessels did not undergo treatment), and changes in FFR and PFR before and at follow‐up visits were evaluated. The FFR was 0.72 ± 0.08, the PFR was 0.71 ± 0.08, and the average of PFG was 3 (range 2 to 3) (Figure 4I). Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated a minimal mean bias between the 2 methods (bias=0.0071), with 95% limits of agreement ranging from −0.0322 to 0.0464, suggesting excellent agreement and absence of systematic measurement error (Figure 4D). Receiver operating characteristic analysis further confirmed the excellent discriminatory ability of PFR, with an area under the curve (AUC=0.985, 95% CI: 0.949–1.000, P<0.001), indicating robust performance across the full range of PFR values (Figure 4E). Additionally, we observed a significant positive correlation between PFR and FFR (r=0.967, P<0.001) (Figure 4F).

DISCUSSION

We have developed a novel approach that applied selective pulmonary angiography images and uses fluid dynamics methods to derive PFR. It showed a good correlation and agreement with pressure wire‐derived FFR and had high sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic accuracy.

Current study on PFR in the field of pulmonary arteries is still limited, 23 but its methodology shares similarities with quantitative flow ratio in the coronary artery field. 18 , 27 In the coronary artery field, the applications of FFR and quantitative flow ratio are widespread and have been incorporated into guidelines. Numerous retrospective and prospective studies have demonstrated the good consistency of FFR and quantitative flow ratio. 19 , 27 However, in the field of CTEPH, research on the accuracy and consistency of FFR and PFR is lacking, with only a few Computational Fluid Dynamics studies investigating CTEPH effects on pulmonary arteries. 23 , 24 , 28

While FFR are widely used in clinical practice of patients with CTEPH, measuring FFR with a pressure wire presents challenges, such as the need for wire passage through the lesion to the distal end, which may lead to complications such as vascular injury, perforation, or bleeding, and the pressure wire is relatively more expensive. PFR can be a potentially safe, effective, convenient and economical method, based on the theoretical basis of FFR in clinical practice.

In our study, a total of 52 target vessels were selected from 29 patients with CTEPH. PFR was obtained by calculating data, FFR was measured by pressure wire was performed on the target vessels. Following a previous study, 22 with the FFR value of 0.8 as the cutoff value. the patients were divided into 2 groups: Group A (ratio ≤0.8) and Group B (ratio > 0.8). Both methods showed similar diagnostic performance and high consistency, with no statistical difference.

Agreement analysis revealed a high degree of concordance between PFR and FFR. This was supported by a strong correlation, minimal systematic bias on Bland–Altman analysis, and consistently excellent diagnostic performance. Specifically, Bland–Altman analysis showed a mean bias of +0.0076 between the 2 methods, with 95% limits of agreement ranging from −0.1037 to 0.1190. Receiver operating characteristic analysis further confirmed the excellent discriminatory ability of PFR for identifying functionally significant lesions, yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.981 (95% CI: 0.935–1.000; P<0.001), indicating robust performance across the full range of PFR values. Using FFR ≤0.80 as the reference standard, PFR achieved a diagnostic accuracy of 92.1%, with a sensitivity of 94.1% and a specificity of 87.5%. Collectively, these results indicate that microcatheter‐based physiological assessment can reliably reflect lesion severity in the pulmonary arteries. Moreover, correlation analysis demonstrated a strong positive relationship between PFR and FFR (r=0.970, P<0.001), underscoring the close agreement between the 2 measurements. Our findings suggest the potential value of this non‐invasive method of PFR to guide BPA treatment in the field of pulmonary arteries.

In addition, 3‐month follow‐up was completed in 45 vessels from 24 patients, including 7 vessels from 6 untreated patients and 38 vessels from 18 treated patients. Mean FFR and PFR values increased at follow‐up compared with baseline. These findings further confirmed the strong correlation between the 2 indices, minimal systematic bias on Bland–Altman analysis, and consistently excellent diagnostic performance. Correlation analysis demonstrated a significant positive association between FFR and PFR, reinforcing their agreement and consistency in the functional assessment of pulmonary artery lesions. The follow‐up analyses were primarily intended to evaluate the stability and agreement of PFR with pressure‐wire FFR over time, rather than to infer definitive clinical benefit from small numerical changes. Clinically, these results suggest that PFR may offer a simpler and potentially faster approach for physiology‐guided evaluation of the pulmonary arterial tree, with diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of the reference standard, wire‐based FFR.

Limitations of this Study

There are some limitations to this study. Firstly, the number of non‐significant vessels remains relatively small, which may widen CIs for specificity and NPV. In addition, within‐patient correlation in vessel‐level analyses may influence precision estimates; larger cohorts and multicenter external validation are warranted. Secondly, there is a lack of long‐term follow‐up data after treatment to validate the effectiveness of PFR guidance and evaluation, further study is warranted.

CONCLUSIONS

In CTEPH, fluid dynamics‐derived PFR correlated with pressure‐wire FFR and angiographic PFG, showing strong agreement and high discrimination for functionally significant lesions. PFR may guide and evaluate BPA as a cost‐effective, safer, wire‐free option in routine practice, though multicenter studies are required to confirm generalizability and clinical benefit in the future.
