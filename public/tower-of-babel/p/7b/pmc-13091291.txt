Research on the imbalance of metal homeostasis and ferroptosis mechanisms in neurodegenerative diseases
Authors: Peiyun zhang, Wei Jin, Xinxuan Lyu, Shuaibo Zhu, Yanbing Lu, Yuzhen Chen, Lu Han, Ming Tao, Lihong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the associations between urinary and serum metal levels and neurodegenerative diseases (NGDs) as well as all-cause mortality, and identify research trends through bibliometric.MethodsThis study first analyzed data from a large-scale national cross-sectional survey (2005–2018) involving 6288 participants. Multivariable regression models were used to assess the associations of urinary metals (Ba, Cd, Co, Cs, Mo, Pb, Sb, Tl, W) and serum metals (Fe, Cd, Pb, Hg) with the risk of neurodegenerative diseases (NGDs) and all-cause mortality. Subsequently, a bibliometric analysis was conducted on literature pertaining to "metals" and "NGDs" retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection to identify research trends and key underlying mechanisms.ResultsEpidemiological analyses revealed significant associations between several metals—such as Cd, Cs, Mo, Hg, and Pb—and either NGDs risk or all-cause mortality, with some exhibiting nonlinear patterns (e.g.L-shaped or inverted U-shaped relationships). Subsequent bibliometric analysis of 34,313 publications identified "iron" as a central hub keyword linking "metal exposure" to "neurodegenerative diseases". Mechanisms related to Fe—including "oxidative stress" and "lipid peroxidation"—emerged as research hotspot keywords. Notably, the cluster labeled "ferroptosis" demonstrated substantial scale in co-citation clustering. Research trajectories have evolved from broad pathological observations toward molecular-level investigations of the NLRP3 inflammasome, glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), ultimately converged on the ferroptosis pathway.ConclusionExposure to multiple metals is significantly associated with the risk and prognosis of NGDs. Integrated bibliometric evidence highlights ferroptosis as the central mechanism bridging metal dyshomeostasis and neuronal injury, offering a cohesive evidence chain from population data to mechanistic insights.

Introduction

As typical neurodegenerative diseases (NGDs), Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease (PD) have witnessed a sharp rise in their global prevalence [1], [2] and disease burden against the backdrop of population aging, emerging as a severe public health challenge [3], [4]. At present, their etiologies remain incompletely elucidated, and clinical therapeutic strategies are limited in their capacity to reverse disease progression [5], [6]. This highlights the urgency of exploring modifiable risk factors from the perspective of "environment-gene" interactions [7]. Among various environmental factors, metal exposure is recognized as a crucial exogenous trigger of neurodegeneration [8]. Notably, multiple metals such as iron and copper are essential trace elements for normal physiological functions, yet their homeostatic imbalance and excessive accumulation act as key inducers of ferroptosis—a novel form of programmed cell death [9], [10]. Characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis is highly consistent with the process of neuronal degeneration and death, thus serving as a core mechanistic bridge linking metal exposure to neurodegenerative pathogenesis [11]. However, most existing studies have focused on single or a limited number of metals; there remains a paucity of integrated evidence regarding the combined exposure effects of multiple metals in human populations and their systematic associations with disease clinical outcomes via the ferroptosis pathway.

As a field of research, research at the intersection of metal exposure, ferroptosis, and NGDs is experiencing explosive growth, with the knowledge system becoming increasingly sophisticated [12]. There is an urgent need to map the developmental trajectory of this field from a macroscopic perspective, identify structural bottlenecks and emerging trends, and thereby clarify the precise role of ferroptosis and consolidate key scientific consensus. Bibliometric methods and other knowledge mapping approaches provide powerful tools for this purpose, as they can objectively reveal research hotspots, evolutionary pathways, and mechanistic association networks within the field, thereby offering a strategic roadmap for future targeted investigations [13].

Therefore, this study adopts an integrated strategy combining macro-level bibliometric analysis with micro-level epidemiological investigation to systematically clarify the role of metal exposure in driving neurodegeneration via the ferroptosis pathway. On the one hand, through visual analysis of literature in this field, we focuses on delineating the knowledge framework centered on the "metal-ferroptosis-neurodegenerative disease" axis, identifying its research hotspots and theoretical gaps. On the other hand, based on large-scale population data, we analyzes the associations between internal metal exposure levels across multiple biological matrices, biomarkers related to ferroptosis, and the risk and mortality outcomes of NGDs, thereby examining the epidemiological relevance of this pathway at the population level. This study is expected to provide integrated evidence and a theoretical basis for the environmental etiology-based prevention of NGDs and the development of intervention strategies targeting ferroptosis.

Method

Study population

Participants were recruited from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a cross-sectional study of all individuals in the United States from 2005-2006 to 2017-2018. All data are publicly available on the website of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.htm). All participants provided written informed consent at the time of recruitment. A total of 70,190 participants participated in NHANES from 2005-2006 to 2017-2018. We first excluded participants who lacked covariates (n = 39289), and then excluded participants who were 40 years or older and 80 years or older (n = 10763). Also, participants without available results of serum metals, urinary metals, and creatinine were excluded (n = 13884).

Ascertainment of PD and AD

PD cases were identified by self-reported use of anti-PD drugs in the NHANES database (RXQ_RX module). In our study, PD patients were defined as those who reported using the following anti-PD drugs in the past month: Benztropine, Carbidopa, Levodopa, Methyldopa, Ropinirole, Entacapone, Amantadine, and excluding individuals who were undiagnosed or not receiving medication. This case-identification approach has been widely adopted in previous studies using the NHANES database. [14].

AD cases were also identified by self-reported use of anti-AD drugs in the NHANES database (RXQ_RX module). In our study, AD patients were defined as those who reported using the following anti-AD drugs in the past month: Rivastigmine, Galanthamine, Donepezil, and Memantine. This methodology has also been widely applied in prior AD research utilizing NHANES data [14].

Metal measurement

Urinary Metal Measurement: The research method refers to the previous literature [15]. In short, barium (Ba), cadmium (Cd), cobalt (Co), cesium (Cs), molybdenum (Mo), lead (Pb), thallium (Tl), and tungsten (W) are mainly measured from urine samples using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Use the limit of detection (LOD) divided by the square root of 2 to replace values below LOD. This substitution method is consistent with the conventional data processing protocol for trace element analysis in the NHANES database, which is routinely employed to address non-detectable values and reduce bias in subsequent statistical analyses. Metal levels were corrected for urinary creatinine and expressed as μg/g creatinine.

To comprehensively assess internal metal exposure, serum concentrations of Fe, cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), and mercury (Hg) were extracted from the corresponding NHANES survey cycles. As detailed in the NHANES analytical protocols [16], blood samples were collected by phlebotomists at Mobile Examination Centers (MECs), processed, aliquoted into vials, and stored under refrigerated or frozen conditions before being shipped to designated laboratories across the United States for analysis. Serum iron was quantified using a timed-endpoint assay performed on the DxC800 platform. In this method, the change in absorbance at 560 nm over a fixed time interval is directly proportional to the iron concentration in the sample. Concentrations of Fe, Cd, Pb, and Hg in serum were determined using quadrupole inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS).

Demographics, lifestyle and complications

NHANES collected demographic information through household interviews, including age, sex, race, education level, the Poverty Income Ratio (PIR), marital status, smoking status, alcohol use, body mass index (BMI), and other variables [14]. Participants were classified according to their level of education into two groups: "less than high school" and "high school or equivalent or above." Smoking was defined as: never smoking (smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in a lifetime), former smoking (smoked 100 or more cigarettes in a lifetime but not smoking at the time of the survey), and current smoking (smoked 100 or more cigarettes in a lifetime and still smoking at the time). A diagnosis of hypertension was established based on either the average of four blood pressure measurements (systolic blood pressure [SBP] ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure [DBP] ≥90 mmHg) or self-reported use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes was determined by fasting blood glucose testing (FBG≥126 mg/dl or HbA1 ≥6.5%) or by self-reporting of anti-diabetic drugs through a questionnaire. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) was defined as self-reported history of any of the following conditions: congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, or stroke. Additionally, histories of heart failure, liver disease, cancer, and blood transfusion were determined based on participant self-reports.

Statistical method

All analyses were performed using R studio (version 3.6.3) and were weighted to account for the complex sampling design of NHANES. Demographic characteristics of participants with Parkinson's disease (PD) were evaluated using the chi-square test, Student's t-test, or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Metal levels were analyzed both as continuous variables and as categorical variables by dividing them into quartiles (Q1-Q4). Multivariate Logistic regression was used to estimate the correlation between metal and PD, yielding odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Gender, age, race, education level, marital status, PIR, smoking, drinking alcohol status, BMI, blood transfusion history, hypertension, diabetes, liver disease, cancer, and CVD were included as covariates. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to determine the association between urinary metal levels and all-cause mortality in PD. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used for nonlinear association analysis.

Bibliometric Analysis Using CiteSpace

This study retrieved literature from the Science Citation Index Expanded (SCI-Expanded) within the Web of Science Core Collection (WoS Core Collection). The search strategy was constructed as follows: TS = (("barium" OR "Ba") OR ("cadmium" OR "Cd") OR ("cobalt" OR "Co") OR ("cesium" OR "Cs") OR ("molybdenum" OR "Mo") OR ("lead" OR "Pb") OR ("antimony" OR "Sb") OR ("thallium" OR "Tl") OR ("iron" OR "Fe") OR ("mercury" OR "Hg")) AND (("Parkinson's disease") OR ("Alzheimer's disease") OR ("Neurodegenerative Diseases")). The search covered publications from 2008 to 2025, limited to document types "Article" or "Review". A total of 34,313 records were ultimately included in the bibliometric analysis. CiteSpace software (version 6.4.R1) was used to perform keyword co-occurrence analysis, clustering, and timeline visualization. The time slicing was set to 1 year, the node type was specified as "Keyword", and the Pathfinder pruning algorithm was applied to simplify network structure and highlight pivotal connections. Research hotspots and and cutting-edge mechanisms were revealed through keyword co-occurrence networks and cluster labeling generated by the LLR algorithm.

Result

Characteristics of the study population

Of the 70,190 participants documented in the NHANES database from the 2005–2006–2017–2018 cycles, 6288 individuals met our inclusion criteria and were enrolled in this study (Fig. 1). Among the 6288 included participants, the entire cohort was stratified into two groups based on the presence of NGDs: 6196 non-NGD cases and 92 NGD cases. The baseline characteristics of the two groups are summarized in Table 1. We found that compared with the non-NGD population, participants with NGD were significantly older (P < 0.001), had a lower educational attainment (P = 0.028), and were of lower socioeconomic status (P = 0.005). Additionally, the proportion of alcohol consumers was significantly lower in the NGD group (P = 0.025). Table 3 evaluates the associations between multiple metal levels and PD risk, while Table 4 assesses the relationships between various metals and AD risk. Association of the weighted relationship between different metals and NGDs all-cause mortality multivariable cox regression analysis

Table 5 applied the Cox proportional hazards regression model to analyze the effect of urinary metal levels (as continuous variables) on all-cause mortality in patients with NGDs. After multivariate adjustment, among the nine urinary metals examined, levels of Co, Cs, Mo and Tl were inversely associated with NGDs all-cause mortality; whereas among the four serum metals, only Pb levels were positively correlated with NGDs all-cause mortality. In Model 4 (the fully adjusted model), a per-unit increase in urinary Co levels was associated with a 58.98% reduction in all-cause mortality (HR = 0.4102, 95% CI: 0.1887–0.8918; P = 0.0245); a per-unit elevation in Cs levels corresponded to a 76.01% decrease in all-cause mortality (HR = 0.2399, 95% CI: 0.1102–0.5223; P < 0.001); a per-unit rise in Mo levels led to a 75.28% reduction in all-cause mortality (HR = 0.2472, 95% CI: 0.0638–0.9575; P = 0.0431); and a per-unit increase in Tl levels was linked to a 54.48% decrease in all-cause mortality (HR = 0.4552, 95% CI: 0.2153–0.9623; P = 0.0394). In contrast, a per-unit increase in serum Pb levels was associated with a 64.53% increase in all-cause mortality (HR = 1.6453, 95% CI: 1.0966–2.4685; P = 0.0161). No statistically significant associations were observed between other metal exposure levels and NGDs all-cause mortality.

Discussion

The etiology and pathogenesis of NGDs remain unclear, with genetic and gene–environment interactions considered to be the main causes of NGDs [18]. Nearly 16% of the global population is affected by neurological diseases [19], mainly involving the elderly, but showing a trend of younger people [20], [21], which may be caused by environmental factors [22], [23]. Notably, metallic elements present in the environment has been suggested as a primary contributor to the induction of neurological diseases [24]. Metal elements serve a dual function in the human body [25]. In normal physiological processes, metal elements are intricately involved in a multitude of functions, such as catalysis, the enhancement of protein stabilization, the facilitation of electron transfer from electron donors to electron acceptors, the prevention of oxidative stress-induced ROS damage, and they play a pivotal role in these processes [26]. However, the excessive accumulation or deficiency of metal elements can exert toxic effects on normal cellular activities, leading to a decline in biological function and, in severe cases, promoting the onset of disease or even death. [27]. Based on data from 6288 participants from the National Health and NHANES database spanning 2005–2018, this study systematically investigated the associations between multiple metal levels in urine and serum and the risk of NGDs as well as all-cause mortality. The study identified Ba, Cd, Co, Cs, Mo, Pb, Sb, Tl, Fe and Hg as metals associated with NGDs. Furthermore, combined with bibliometric analysis using CiteSpace, this study revealed that ferroptosis exerts a core mediating role in the link between metal exposure and NGDs, thereby providing multidimensional evidence for elucidating the environmental etiological mechanisms of NGDs.

Ba is not an essential trace element for humans and can enter the body through water, food, industrial activities, and medical procedures [28]. The results indicated that higher urinary Ba levels were associated with a reduced risk of PD. Moreover, after quartile stratification, participants with urinary Ba levels in Q2 and Q4 had lower all-cause mortality related to NGDs compared with those in the Q1 reference group. Thus, Ba may act as a protective factor against PD, though its specific underlying mechanisms warrant further validation. A previous study reported no significant difference in serum Ba levels between PD patients and healthy controls, though a downward trend was observed [29]. Ba can inhibit voltage-gated potassium conductance, thereby contributing to the establishment and maintenance of the resting potential of cells [30], and the activation of potassium channels has been intimately linked to the underlying pathogenesis of PD [31] which may be one of the underlying mechanisms.

Cd has no essential physiological role in humans and is classified as a Group I carcinogen, which primarily enters the human body via food and air intake [26]. The results showed that Cd levels were inversely associated with the risk of AD and overall NGDs, with significant nonlinear relationships observed across these associations. Specifically, the relationship between Cd exposure and PD risk exhibited a U-shaped pattern, with PD risk being relatively low at both low and high Cd exposure concentrations, the association between Cd levels and overrall NGDs risk followed an L-shaped pattern, characterized by a rapid decline in risk after an initial increase, eventually reaching a plateau—suggesting the presence of an "effect saturation point". In contrast, the correlation between Cd exposure and NGDs all-cause mortality showed an inverted U-shaped pattern, with the lowest mortality rate detected at moderate Cd concentrations and relatively higher mortality rates observed at both low and high concentrations. This may be related to the fact that Cd promotes ROS production through various mechanisms, and increased ROS levels can lead to protein misfolding and aggregation, inflammation and neuronal damage, leading to NGDs [32].

Co is an essential component of vitamin B12, which serves as a critical coenzyme for erythropoiesis, DNA synthesis, and methylation reactions [33]. The present study found that elevated Co concentrations were associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality in patients with NGDs. This protective effect may be attributed to the Co-mediated maintenance of the structural stability and functional activity of vitamin B12, which in turn helps preserve nervous system homeostasis and mitigate neuronal damage and death [34].

Cs is commonly used in the production of photocells, various optical instruments, vacuum tubes, special glass and ceramics [33]. Physiologically, Cs partially mimics potassium, competing with K+ for transport across cell membranes and being primarily excreted by the kidneys [35]. In this study, higher Cs levels were associated with a reduced risk of NGDs and lower all-cause mortality. Although few studies have examined the relationship between Cs and NGDs, one previous report indicated a significant negative correlation between Cs and cognitive ability [36]. The underlying mechanism may involve the neuroprotective effect of Cs via inactivation of GSK3β [37], given that GSK3β is known to promote the pathogenesis and neuronal death in NGDs through mechanisms such as oxidative stress [38], [39].

Mo can decompose toxic substances and drugs, participate in human genetic and protein biosynthesis processes [40], and as a cofactor, promote the activation of a variety of enzymes [40]. The human body relies on food such as beans to consume the required Mo [41]. We found that increasing Mo levels increased the risk of NGDs but decreased the risk of death from NGDs. Furthermore, Mo levels exhibited an inverted U-shaped nonlinear association with NGDs all-cause mortality; specifically, within a certain exposure range, the risk of NGDs mortality initially increased and subsequently decreased, with a distinct peak observed. Previous studies have shown that the level of serum Mo in AD and PD is higher than that in the healthy group [29], [42], and in the progression from healthy individuals to those with subjective memory impairment, through mild cognitive impairment, and ultimately to AD patients, the level of Serum Mo gradually escalates, indicating that Mo may be involved in the occurrence and progression of AD [42]. Mo is present in the body as part of oxidoreductase enzymes [43], and an overabundance of Mo can result in oxidative stress and iron death [44]. Studies have shown that there is a correlation between the level of Mo in the body and iron overload [45], and iron overload is an important pathogenesis of PD and AD [46]. This may partly explain the dual effects observed in NGDs patients—namely, elevated Mo levels associated with reduced mortality risk—as well as the inverted U-shaped nonlinear pattern of association.

The main sources of Pb exposure are lead-containing dust, aerosols, paint, food, and water [47]. Our findings indicated that both urinary and serum Pb concentrations were inversely associated with the risk of PD, while serum Pb concentrations were additionally positively correlated with all-cause mortality in patients with NGDs. Studies have demonstrated that Pb is a neurotoxin that can pass through the blood meninges and cause neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress and apoptosis [48]. Although most studies suggest that Pb exposure is one of the reasons for increasing the risk of PD [49], some studies have shown that the level of Pb in PD patients is lower than that in healthy controls [50], and Pb is associated with reduced risk of PD [51]. Therefore, further exploration of the underlying causes is needed.

Sb enters the human body primarily via natural contamination, anthropogenic activities, and exposure to Sb-containing pharmaceuticals, and can be detected in the blood, urine, liver, kidneys, and brain tissues [52]. Its core biological effects are predominantly mediated by the trivalent state (Sb (III)), which binds to protein sulfhydryl groups, induces oxidative stress, and participates in detoxification processes via membrane transporters [53]. The present study found that elevated Sb concentrations were associated with a reduced risk of PD. We hypothesize that moderate Sb exposure may exert a protective effect by inhibiting α-synuclein aggregation, regulating PD-associated kinases, alleviating iron overload, activating antioxidant systems, and suppressing neuroinflammation. This protective effect may stem from the concentration-dependent inverse regulation of toxic pathways, which essentially modulates the key pathological processes of PD [54].

Tl primarily enters the human body via the ingestion of contaminated water and food [51]. Currently, it is believed that the toxicity of Tl mainly affects mitochondria and may cause oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation of cell membrane, and antioxidant mechanisms, but further studies are needed to elucidate the relevant mechanisms [55]. This study showed that Tl was inversely associated with all-cause mortality in NGDs. The relationship between Tl and the death risk of NGDs has not been evaluated in previous studies, but one study on cardiovascular disease found a significant negative association between plasma Tl levels and cardiovascular all-cause mortality [56]. This suggests that Tl may be a potential protective factor for all-cause mortality.

Fe is primarily derived from dietary intake, and following intestinal absorption, it enters the systemic circulation and the brain via transferrin and other transport proteins [57]. Its core physiological functions include acting as an enzyme cofactor to participate in mitochondrial energy metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis, and oxygen transport; however, excessive accumulation can induce toxic processes such as oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis [58]. The results of the present study revealed that Fe levels exhibited a complex multimodal nonlinear association with all-cause mortality in NGDs, indicating that NGDs mortality risk fluctuates with varying Fe levels in a pattern characterized by alternating peaks and troughs. This finding suggests that the impact of iron on mortality is highly dependent on specific concentration ranges. A previous clinical trial (NCT03234686) investigating deferiprone for the treatment of AD demonstrated that although iron chelators effectively reduced iron burden in the hippocampus and multiple cortical regions, they accelerated cognitive decline and brain atrophy [59]. This outcome is consistent with the dual role of iron in NGDs: at low concentrations, iron fails to meet the essential requirements for neuronal energy metabolism and Fe-S cluster synthesis, thereby exacerbating functional deterioration; at optimal concentrations, iron maintains iron homeostasis to support the normal operation of the nervous system and reduce mortality risk; at excessive concentrations, iron triggers lipid peroxidation chain reactions via the Fenton reaction, and interacts with pathological features including neuroinflammation, protein aggregation, and mitochondrial dysfunction to amplify ferroptosis-related neuronal damage and increase mortality risk. This may represent the potential mechanism underlying the alternating peaks and troughs in mortality risk observed across different iron concentration ranges [60].

Hg is a heavy metal ubiquitously present in industrial emissions, coal combustion, and certain fish species. It can cross the blood-brain barrier and accumulate in the brain, triggering oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, thereby leading to neuronal damage [61]. Variations in Hg concentrations were inversely associated with the risk of PD, overall NGDs risk, and NGDs all-cause mortality. Meanwhile, Hg exposure exhibited an L-shaped nonlinear association with AD risk, where the risk decreased at low concentrations and subsequently plateaued; it also showed a J-shaped nonlinear correlation with NGDs all-cause mortality, characterized by reduced mortality at low-to-moderate concentrations and elevated mortality at high concentrations. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that low-dose Hg potentially exerts a protective effect by activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and competitively reducing iron accumulation in the brain, whereas high-dose Hg exerts distinct neurotoxicity. These findings reflect the complex, concentration-dependent biphasic effects of Hg [62].

These population-level associations provide crucial clues for elucidating the environmental aetiology of NGDs. However, epidemiological evidence alone is insufficient to clarify how metal exposure specifically modulates NGDs pathology. It is therefore essential to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms to complete the full "exposure-effect-mechanism" research chain.

To clarify the core regulatory mechanisms, this study conducted a bibliometric analysis of 34,313 publications related to "metal exposure and neurodegenerative diseases" retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection using CiteSpace software. The results indicated that ferroptosis is the core research hotspot in this field. The regulatory pathway can be concisely summarized as follows: imbalanced metal exposure → dysregulated iron metabolism plus activated oxidative stress → (1) direct triggering of the ferroptosis pathway; (2) activation of neuroinflammatory responses → further exacerbation of iron metabolism disorder/oxidative stress → amplification of the ferroptosis pathway → initiation and progression of neuronal injury and glial cell death.

Specifically, metal elements act as essential participants in physiological processes (e.g.Fe mediates mitochondrial energy metabolism, Co constitutes vitamin B12 coenzymes, Mo activates oxidoreductases), and either insufficient or excessive exposure to these metals can disrupt bodily homeostasis [63]. On the one hand, excessive iron and molybdenum can induce lipid peroxidation through the Fenton reaction, and simultaneously inhibit the activity of glutathione GPX4, thereby directly initiating the ferroptosis process [64]. On the other hand, metals such as cadmium and mercury can compete with iron transporters for binding sites, leading to the abnormal accumulation of iron in the brain; alternatively, they can indirectly activate the NLRP3 inflammasome via the NF-κB pathway, amplify the oxidative stress effect, and thus indirectly promote ferroptosis-related neuronal injury [65], [66]. In contrast, metals with protective effects including Ba and Cs may indirectly suppress iron metabolic disorders and the ferroptosis pathway through mechanisms such as maintaining potassium ion homeostasis and inactivating GSK3β, consequently reducing NGDs risk [67].

Further bibliometric analysis provided robust support for this mechanistic pathway. The keyword co-occurrence network revealed a total of 1317 keywords, with iron emerging as the central hub linking "metal exposure" and "neurodegenerative diseases" due to its high co-occurrence frequency. The mechanism-related keywords closely associated with it (including "cerebral iron deposition", "glutathione", "lipid peroxidation", "mitochondrial dysfunction", and "neuroinflammation") are all prominent research hotspots, indicating that iron metabolism and ferroptosis occupy a central position in the association between metals and neurodegenerative diseases. Cluster analysis identifided 7 major clusters, among which the ferroptosis cluster displayed a substantial size and, together with the "oxidative stress" cluster, constituted the cutting-edge research directions in this field. The timeline map clearly depicted the evolutionary trajectory of research focuses: early studies concentrated on macro-pathological mechanisms such as "brain iron deposition" and "protein aggregation"; subsequent research gradually delved into specialized processes including "neuroinflammation", "iron metabolism", and "microglia"; in recent years, the focus has further narrowed to key molecules such as GPX4 and the NLRP3 inflammasome. Ultimately, all research directions have converged on ferroptosis as an integrated cell death pathway. This confirms that ferroptosis serves as the core hub mechanism bridging metal homeostasis imbalance and neuronal death, and the ferroptosis pathway can act as an intervention target for metal-related NGDs, thereby providing theoretical support for developing precision prevention strategies such as iron metabolism modulators and GPX4 activators.

The present study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishing causal relationship between metal exposure and NGDs, and a single-time-point metal measurement fails to reflect long-term cumulative exposure. Second, residual confounding may persist in the population data, and the joint effects of mixed-metal exposure and gene–environment interactions were not fully explored. Third, the mechanistic inferences rely largely on macro-level associations derived from bibliometric analysis and lack experimental validation of the specific molecular targets through which different metals modulate ferroptosis.

Conclusions

In summary, this study preliminarily investigated the associations between urinary and serum metal levels and the risk of NGDs and all-cause mortality, identifying ten metal elements (Ba, Cd, Co, Cs, Mo, Pb, Sb, Tl, Fe, and Hg) associated with NGDs. Furthermore, bibliometric analysis via CiteSpace revealed that ferroptosis serves as a core mechanism linking metal exposure to the pathological progression of NGDs. Keywords related to this mechanism represent active research hotspots with substantial clustering scale. The research trend has progressively advanced from macro‑pathological mechanisms toward more specialized topics such as iron metabolism and GPX4, ultimately converging on the ferroptosis pathway. This finding supports the potential of the ferroptosis pathway as an intervention target for metal‑related NGDs, providing a theoretical basis for developing precision prevention strategies—including iron metabolism modulators and GPX4 activators. In light of the limitations noted above, future studies should employ prospective cohort designs, validate underlying mechanisms using neural cell lines and PD/AD transgenic animal models, and develop ferroptosis‑targeted interventions. These efforts will help clarify the relationships between metal exposure and NGDs and their mechanisms, ultimately aiding the clinical translation of precision prevention for NGDs.
