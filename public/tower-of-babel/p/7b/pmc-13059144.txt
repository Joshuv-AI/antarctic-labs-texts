Defining “critical” maternal health information for healthy lifestyle self-management in pregnancy: perspectives of pregnant women and obstetricians in China and Malaysia
Authors: Xinxin Yu, Saiful Hasley bin Ramli, Habibah Abdul Hamid, Noris Mohd Norowi, Siti Suhaily Surip, Ganfeng Yu, Chunju Zhang, Zilu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPregnancy is a critical window for adopting and sustaining healthy lifestyle behaviors. This study explored perspectives of pregnant women and obstetricians on key lifestyle domains, barriers, and support needs during pregnancy in China and Malaysia.MethodsA qualitative in-depth interview study was conducted from October 2023 to February 2024. Pregnant women were recruited from two antenatal clinics in China and one general hospital in Malaysia. Obstetricians involved in routine antenatal care were also interviewed. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed thematically.ResultsTwenty pregnant/postpartum women and six obstetricians participated. Four integrated themes from pregnant women described “critical” maternal health information as: (1) bonding-oriented interpretability that motivates health behaviors, (2) lifestyle self-management under uncertainty requiring safety boundaries and red-flag clarity, (3) social support as psychosocial coping infrastructure, and (4) digital information appraisal in partnership with clinicians. Three complementary themes from obstetricians emphasized time-pressured clinical encounters shaping communication and trust, ethically/institutionally mediated disclosure practices, and a contested digital information ecology in which digital tools may support continuity while raising credibility concerns.ConclusionsPregnant women and obstetricians highlighted multi-domain lifestyle support needs that extend beyond clinical check-ups. Strengthening credible information access and tailored professional guidance may facilitate healthier lifestyle practices during pregnancy across contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08863-2.

Introduction

Pregnancy is widely recognized as a critical life stage for adopting and sustaining healthier behaviors, because maternal diet, physical activity, sleep, and psychosocial wellbeing are closely linked to pregnancy experiences and outcomes. The World Health Organization (WHO) describes a “positive pregnancy experience” as one that supports physical and sociocultural normality and maintains maternal and fetal well-being. Guidance such as prenatal nutrition counselling emphasizes adequate, varied dietary intake to support a healthy pregnancy [1].

At the same time, pregnant women increasingly navigate a dense digital information environment when making everyday health decisions. Social media and online platforms can provide rapid access to pregnancy-related information and emotional reassurance, but the quality and credibility of content are uneven, and misinformation may amplify anxiety or shape health decisions in unhelpful ways [2]. Evidence from recent studies also shows that pregnancy-related misinformation can circulate widely on social platforms and may influence risk perceptions and health behaviors, underscoring the need to understand how women evaluate and act on digital health information [3].

Mobile health (mHealth) interventions are increasingly proposed as scalable tools to support lifestyle change and maternal self-management. Systematic reviews suggest mHealth approaches can improve outcomes in areas such as gestational weight management and diabetes-related behaviors, indicating that digital support can be beneficial when interventions are well designed and clinically aligned [4]. However, effectiveness and acceptability depend on whether the content and functions correspond to what pregnant women perceive as critical in real-world decision-making, and whether digital tools complement (rather than compete with) time-limited antenatal consultations.

Clinical encounters remain central “anchors” for pregnancy decision-making, yet antenatal consultations are frequently constrained by workload and time pressure, which can limit opportunities for detailed explanations and shared sense-making—particularly when patients bring contradictory online information into the consultation [5]. These constraints highlight the importance of clarifying what “critical maternal health information” means from both women’s and clinicians’ perspectives, and how information can better support safe self-management, timely escalation to care, and trustworthy communication.

Despite a growing literature on pregnancy information seeking and digital maternal health tools, few studies explicitly triangulate the perspectives of pregnant women and obstetricians to define what constitutes critical information for maintaining a healthy lifestyle during pregnancy, particularly across differing health-system and platform contexts. To address this gap, this qualitative study examined the perspectives of pregnant/postpartum women and obstetricians in China and Malaysia. Specifically, it aimed to identify how participants define critical maternal health information for healthy lifestyle management, how women appraise and use digital information, and where clinician–patient perspectives converge or diverge, shaping decision-making and communication.

Literature review

Healthy lifestyle during pregnancy and the role of antenatal care

Pregnancy is often framed as a critical window for establishing or strengthening health-promoting behaviors because diet, physical activity, sleep, and psychosocial well-being interact with maternal experiences and pregnancy outcomes. Contemporary antenatal care guidance emphasizes not only risk reduction but also a positive pregnancy experience, with recommendations spanning nutrition, maternal and fetal assessment, preventive measures, and health-system interventions to improve care quality and utilization [6].

This framing suggests that “critical” maternal health information is information that helps women translate antenatal guidance into safe everyday routines and timely care-seeking—an assumption that we examine directly in our interviews.

Pregnancy information needs and information-seeking behavior

Research consistently shows that pregnant women actively seek health information across multiple channels (e.g., search engines, social media, peers/family, and clinicians) to reduce uncertainty, interpret symptoms, and support decision-making across pregnancy stages. A review synthesizing women’s internet-based pregnancy health information seeking highlights the breadth of online information behaviors and the importance of understanding how women navigate and evaluate digital content rather than treating online use as a single, uniform practice [7].

Work examining pregnancy-related consumer information needs points to the need for high-quality, customizable, and layperson-friendly information across pregnancy stages, similarly notes gaps between what consumers seek and what is reliably available [8], and underscores the need for high-quality, customizable, and layperson-friendly information across pregnancy stages.

These findings motivate our focus on defining what participants themselves consider “critical” information (not merely “commonly searched” information) and on describing the functions of information (e.g., reassurance, actionability, and escalation guidance) in real-world decision processes.

Digital information environments, reassurance seeking, and misinformation risk

Digital platforms can offer rapid access to pregnancy information and peer support, but the credibility of content varies substantially. Recent research documents how pregnancy-related misinformation can circulate on social media and shape beliefs, highlighting potential downstream effects on decision-making and emotional well-being [3].

Broader reviews of social media use in pregnancy also describe a dual pattern: social platforms can provide community support and peer-to-peer advice, yet may expose users to misinformation, unrealistic norms, and anxiety-provoking content [9].

This literature supports treating misinformation not only as an accuracy problem but as a behavioral and emotional risk. It also aligns with our triangulation goal: to examine how women’s reassurance-seeking and verification practices intersect with clinicians’ concerns about decision coherence and safety.

Clinician communication and the “online–offline” negotiation of trust

Antenatal consultations remain a key reference point for guidance and reassurance, yet in many health systems, they are constrained by time pressure and high patient volume. Under such constraints, online–offline conflicts (patients arriving with contradictory online information) can become a recurring challenge for trust and communication. This tension is increasingly recognized in research on pregnancy information ecosystems, where the practical question is not whether women use the internet, but how clinicians and women together manage uncertainty, credibility, and action under limited consultation time [8].

These issues justify our inclusion of obstetricians’ perspectives to triangulate women’s accounts—specifically to clarify where priorities converge (e.g., actionable safety guidance) and where tensions arise (e.g., the perceived clinical disruptiveness of social media reassurance).

mHealth interventions for pregnancy self-management

Mobile health (mHealth) tools are frequently proposed as scalable supports for pregnancy self-management, including lifestyle modification and monitoring. Evidence syntheses suggest that mHealth-based lifestyle interventions can show favorable effects in specific domains, such as the prevention of gestational diabetes among women with overweight/obesity, while also emphasizing the importance of intervention design quality and implementation feasibility [10].

Qualitative evidence also indicates that women’s perceptions of mHealth behavior change interventions are shaped by usability, relevance, trustworthiness, burden, and perceived fit with real-life routines and clinical care [11].

This literature supports a key premise of our analysis: digital support is more likely to be acceptable and effective when it matches what pregnant women and clinicians deem “critical”—namely, information that is interpretable, safety-bounded, and aligned with clinical pathways.

Research gap and rationale for the present study

Despite extensive work on pregnancy information seeking, social media use, and digital maternal health interventions, fewer studies explicitly triangulate pregnant women’s and obstetricians’ perspectives to define what “critical maternal health information” means in practice—especially across different platform ecologies and health-service pathways. Reviews highlight both the breadth of online information behavior and persistent gaps in high-quality, comprehensible consumer-facing information [7]. Meanwhile, emerging evidence on misinformation underscores the need to understand not only what content is inaccurate, but how digital information environments shape anxiety, reassurance-seeking, and care decisions [3].

Addressing this gap, our study uses interviews with pregnant/postpartum women and obstetricians in China and Malaysia to identify shared priorities and tensions in how “critical” information is understood and acted upon, and to clarify implications for improving decision coherence across digital and clinical contexts.

Methods

Study design and approach

This study employed a qualitative design using semi-structured, in-depth interviews with pregnant women and obstetricians in China and Malaysia. A qualitative approach was appropriate because the study aimed to explore participants’ perspectives on what maternal health information is “critical” for supporting healthy lifestyle-related self-management during pregnancy and how such information is sought, appraised, and used in decision-making—questions that require an in-depth, contextual understanding of meanings and experiences rather than hypothesis testing [12]. Triangulation across respondent groups (pregnant women and obstetricians) was used to enhance credibility by examining convergence and tension points across perspectives, consistent with qualitative methodology literature describing triangulation as a strategy to strengthen confidence in findings through the convergence (and interrogation) of evidence from multiple data sources [13, 14].In addition, this approach aligns with established trustworthiness criteria in qualitative research, where techniques such as triangulation contribute to credibility and transparency in reporting [15].

The overall research workflow included: (i) establishing research questions and objectives, (ii) conducting a literature review, (iii) obtaining ethical approval, (iv) developing and refining interview protocols, (v) recruiting participants, (vi) collecting interview data, and (vii) conducting thematic analysis (Fig. 1).

Setting, sampling and recruitment

Participants comprised two groups: (1) pregnant and/or postpartum women and (2) obstetricians. Purposive sampling was used to recruit information-rich participants with relevant lived experience (pregnant/postpartum women) and professional experience (obstetricians). To support cross-context interpretation of maternal information needs under different real-world service pathways and information ecologies, participants were included from both China and Malaysia. This study does not aim to conduct a formal cultural comparison; rather, the inclusion of two contexts was intended to strengthen transferability and to identify context-sensitive implications for maternal health information support.

Eligibility criteria—pregnant/postpartum women

Women were eligible if they (i) were between 12 and 37 weeks of gestation, or (ii) were postpartum with an infant aged ≤ 1 year; (iii) were able to communicate in Chinese or English; and (iv) were in adequate physical condition to participate in an interview.

Eligibility criteria—obstetricians

Obstetricians were eligible if they had ≥ 3 years of clinical experience and were working in public hospitals in China or Malaysia.

Sampling occurred iteratively alongside data collection and analysis. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as no new relevant insights emerging in consecutive interviews, as determined through research team discussion. Recruitment commenced on October 2023 and ended on February 2024.

Ethical approval and informed consent

Ethical approval was obtained from the Universiti Putra Malaysia Ethics Committee for Research Involving Human Subjects (JKEUPM) on 15 November 2023 (Appendix 1 in Supplementary Material). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation, using the consent form provided in Appendix 2 in Supplementary Material. To protect confidentiality, interview transcripts were de-identified and reported using participant codes (e.g., CP/MP for pregnant women in China/Malaysia; CD/MD for obstetricians in China/Malaysia).

Interview protocols and procedures

Two semi-structured interview guides were developed: (1) “Health information searching strategy of pregnant women” (Appendix 3 in Supplementary Material) and (2) “Critical health information for pregnant women” (Appendix 4 in Supplementary Material). The protocols were drafted in English and refined prior to the main study. To improve clarity and linguistic appropriateness, the phrasing and language of the interview guides were reviewed by a professor at Universiti Putra Malaysia with strong English proficiency. A pilot test was conducted with three participants to assess question comprehension and interview flow; feedback was used to refine wording and sequencing of questions.

Because the researcher was based in Malaysia during data collection, interviews with participants in China were primarily conducted online via WeChat video calls, whereas interviews with participants in Malaysia were conducted face-to-face. Interviews typically lasted 40–60 min. Interviews with China-based participants were conducted in Mandarin Chinese, and interviews with Malaysia-based participants were conducted in English. With participants’ permission, interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim in the original language.

Prior to each interview, the participant information sheet and written informed consent form (Appendix 2 in Supplementary Material) were shared with prospective participants, and participants were encouraged to review and retain the document for reference during the interview.

Participant reimbursement and refreshments

Participants received modest compensation for their time. Pregnant women in China received approximately CNY 60 per interview, and participants in Malaysia received approximately MYR 60 per interview. During face-to-face interviews in Malaysia, bottled water and chocolate were provided as light refreshments. Compensation and refreshments were provided after the interview and were not contingent on responding to any specific questions.

Transcription verification and data analysis

To enhance transcript accuracy, participants were contacted after transcription to confirm key content and clarify ambiguous segments (transcript verification). Data were analysed using thematic analysis. Data collection and analysis proceeded iteratively, such that early interviews informed subsequent probing and refinement of the analytic focus. Sampling continued until thematic saturation was reached (i.e., no substantively new insights were identified across consecutive interviews), as determined through research team discussion.

Coding combined inductive (data-driven) and deductive (concept-informed) strategies. Initial codes were generated through close reading of transcripts, then iteratively reviewed and refined to form a coding template. The coding template was updated across the analysis in consultation between the interviewer (XY) and the supervising researcher (SHR), with decisions recorded in an analytic log. Once the coding framework was stabilised, all transcripts were coded using ATLAS.ti (Mac version; ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, Berlin). Codes were then clustered into subthemes and themes through constant comparison across participants and respondent groups (pregnant women versus obstetricians), enabling explicit identification of convergence and tensions (triangulation). Themes were finalised by reviewing coherence within themes and distinctions between themes, and illustrative quotations were selected to support each theme.

Trustworthiness and rigor

The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [16]. Credibility was strengthened through (i) the use of semi-structured interviews with two participant groups (pregnant women and obstetricians) to enable data source triangulation, and (ii) transcript verification, whereby participants were re-contacted after transcription to confirm key content and clarify ambiguous segments. Dependability was supported by an iterative analytic process in which data collection and analysis proceeded in parallel, and coding decisions were documented through an analytic log and versioned coding templates. Confirmability was enhanced by maintaining an audit trail of code and theme development and by grounding themes in verbatim quotations. Transferability was supported by purposive sampling across two contexts (China and Malaysia) and by providing contextual detail regarding interview mode (online vs. face-to-face) and language of data collection, enabling readers to assess applicability to other settings.

Overview of themes

Thematic analysis generated four integrated themes from pregnant women’s interviews and three themes from obstetricians’ interviews. Overall, participants in both contexts described “critical” maternal health information as information that reduces uncertainty about fetal wellbeing, supports safe everyday self-management, and clarifies when and how to escalate concerns to professional care. While many needs were shared across settings, context-sensitive differences were most visible in (i) care-seeking pathways (online-first vs clinic-first), (ii) platform ecology (e.g., Red Note/Douyin versus Google/TikTok), and (iii) clinical governance and local practice (e.g., fetal sex disclosure).

Themes from interviews with pregnant women

Theme 1. Bonding-oriented interpretability motivates maternal health behaviors

Women described maternal–fetal attachment as a motivational driver that shapes lifestyle decisions during pregnancy. Bonding became “actionable” when fetal cues were interpretable—that is, emotionally reassuring but also accompanied by understandable explanations (numbers/text and clear meaning) that reduced uncertainty.

Subtheme 1. Visual/auditory bonding cues trigger positive emotions and health motivation

Women reported that seeing or hearing cues of fetal vitality (e.g., ultrasound/3D images, heartbeat) strengthened emotional connection and encouraged healthier routines. One participant described how fetal heartbeat evoked strong emotion and increased behavioural motivation: “I heard the baby’s heartbeat… a beautiful sound… I was overwhelmed with emotion… my daughter (fetus) encourages me to walk more…” (MP2). Another participant noted that earlier pregnancy tended to intensify vigilance and caution: “during the first pregnancy I was very cautious… but now… I’ve become [less anxious] with the second baby.” (MP5).

Subtheme 2. Embodied interaction (fetal movement) supports reassurance and risk appraisal

Women described bonding as an embodied process in which fetal movement helped them “feel the pregnancy as real” and guided daily self-management. Changes in perceived fetal activity could increase vigilance and prompt action. For example, women described heightened concern about fetal safety when considering physical behaviours (e.g., activity intensity) because “my considerations are mostly for the baby.” (MP6).

Subtheme 3. Bonding requires trustworthy interpretation, not generic visuals

Several women emphasised that simulated visuals can feel generic, and that text/numerical indicators are perceived as more reliable for decision-making. As one participant explained: “words are more accurate… numbers are more reliable… the picture is just a general simulation.” (MP6). This indicates that bonding-related features are most meaningful when paired with credible interpretive information, rather than relying on imagery alone.

Overall, attachment was strongest when fetal cues were not only emotionally resonant but also interpretable in ways that supported safe, confident behavioural choices.

Clinicians similarly positioned “critical” information as information that supports safe decisions and reduces uncertainty, but they often emphasised clinical authority and offline guidance when women’s interpretations are shaped by online sources. One clinician stated that when online information conflicts with medical advice, women should “prioritize the advice of your obstetrician” (CD2), highlighting alignment around safety but a tension around who should define interpretability (apps/peers vs. clinicians). Under time pressure, interpretive gaps may persist, increasing women’s reliance on self-interpretation and digital reassurance.

Theme 2. Lifestyle self-management under uncertainty requires safety boundaries and red-flag clarity

Women portrayed pregnancy lifestyle management (diet, activity, mental wellbeing) as decision-making under uncertainty, where “critical information” is information that clarifies what is safe, for whom, and when escalation is necessary.

Subtheme 1. Maternal health is treated as the prerequisite for fetal health

Women strongly linked fetal wellbeing to maternal physical and emotional wellbeing. Participants explicitly stated: “I think my baby will only be healthy if the mother is healthy” (MP7) and “Fetal health is essential, but mother’s health is more important because it is directly related to the health of the child.” (MP3). Emotional wellbeing was also framed as consequential: “Only when the mother is in a good mood will the fetus be healthy.” (CP6).

Subtheme 2. Exercise decisions are constrained by unclear safety thresholds

Many women described uncertainty about appropriate activity type and intensity, often resulting in avoidance or cautious restriction. One participant explained her fear-driven caution: “I feared preterm labor, or even miscarriage… I worry about not being able to control the intensity… my considerations are mostly for the baby.” (MP6). This reflects a need for explicit safety-bounded guidance and clear risk boundaries.

Subtheme 3. Self-monitoring and selective appraisal shape responses to symptoms and complications

Women described informal approaches to monitoring pregnancy-related conditions and deciding when to act. For instance, one participant described selective monitoring: “If I don’t feel uncomfortable, I usually don’t bother measuring it.” (CP8). Such strategies show how women manage uncertainty pragmatically but may also risk delayed escalation without clear red-flag thresholds.

Collectively, women’s accounts show that lifestyle decisions hinge on clear safety limits and escalation rules rather than general health education alone.

Clinicians converged with women in treating lifestyle guidance as “critical,” yet tensions emerged in how certainty is established. When women rely on online or informal sources, clinicians may respond by reasserting authority and discouraging further debate. One clinician reported advising women to follow hospital guidance and prioritise their obstetrician (CD2), while another expressed frustration (“…go check online… Don’t go here again.” (CD1)). This triangulation suggests a system-level gap: women need safety boundaries and escalation rules that are consistent with clinician expectations, but time-limited encounters can reduce opportunities to deliver them.

Theme 3. Social support functions as psychosocial coping infrastructure

Women described social support as an essential resource for emotional stability and sustained self-management during pregnancy. Support was both emotional (reassurance) and instrumental (help with chores/childcare), and it also intersected with information seeking through reassurance and normalization.

Subtheme 1. Partner support provides emotional buffering and reassurance

Spousal support was commonly framed as the most immediate coping mechanism. One participant said: “to relieve my emotions, I sometimes talk to my husband… he’ll comfort me a little bit… their support is important for managing this kind of stress.” (CP4). Another emphasised the centrality of partner support: “My husband was my biggest support.” (CP3).

Subtheme 2. Extended family involvement reduces daily burden and stabilises coping

Women also described support from family systems, including mothers-in-law, as influential for practical coping and relational stability. One participant recalled explicit support messaging from her mother-in-law: “Please take care of your wife… Do everything for your good.” (MP4). These accounts highlight how household support can protect women’s mental bandwidth for pregnancy management.

Subtheme 3. Peer reassurance via social media normalises symptoms and reduces anxiety

Some women used local social media to compare experiences and reduce worry for non-urgent concerns. As one participant explained: “I searched on Red Note… many pregnant moms had experienced the same thing…” (CP3). This form of reassurance complements family support but also introduces risks if inaccurate narratives dominate.

In short, support networks acted as a coping infrastructure that reduced stress and helped women sustain everyday self-management.

Clinicians recognised psychosocial vulnerability but were concerned that social platforms can amplify anxiety and misinformation, creating tension between women’s reassurance-seeking and clinicians’ preference for controlled, evidence-based guidance. This triangulation indicates that social support and information support must be designed together: reassurance channels are “supportive” only if they do not increase conflict with clinical advice or intensify worry.

Theme 4. Digital information appraisal and clinician partnership shape decision coherence

Women used multiple digital and interpersonal sources to obtain and verify maternal health information; “critical information” was information that helped them maintain decision coherence—especially when advice was conflicting. Clinician partnership remained a key anchor, but interactions were shaped by time constraints and platform feasibility.

Subtheme 1. Multi-source verification is a common appraisal strategy

Women described cross-checking information across platforms and sources rather than trusting a single channel. One participant reported structured cross-platform verification: “I’ll ask around to verify… I use both… and Red Note together, cross-referencing the two to check.” (CP9). Others described broad comparative reading to identify consensus.

Subtheme 2. Conflicting advice triggers offline confirmation with clinicians

When encountering contradictions, women often turned to clinicians to validate or resolve uncertainty. This reflects clinician-anchored appraisal rather than purely digital decision-making. Women’s experiences also showed that conflicting advice can be stressful and can intensify reassurance-seeking.

Subtheme 3. Online consultation is valued as a supplement, but clinician time constraints limit depth

Women valued online consultation for reducing unnecessary visits, but also reported that routine check-ups can be brief and explanations limited. This gap increases reliance on digital sources and makes the quality and structure of digital information more consequential.

Overall, information seeking functioned as a coordination task—aligning digital appraisal with clinician input to maintain coherent decisions under uncertainty.

Clinicians acknowledged the need for continuity and platforms but emphasised constraints in governance, credentialing, and trust management. Some clinicians supported professional exchange platforms (“platform where we can exchange knowledge…” (CD3)), while others highlighted participation barriers (“it requires a certain professional title… I might qualify next year.” (CD1)). Tension is most visible when women bring online content into consultations: clinicians may respond by insisting on hospital guidance (CD2) or expressing frustration (CD1), which can weaken partnership even when women are attempting responsible verification. Triangulation, therefore, shows that “critical information” is not only content but also a coordination mechanism between digital appraisal and time-pressured clinical care.

Themes from interviews with obstetricians (3 themes)

Theme 1. Time-pressured clinical encounters shape communication and trust

Obstetricians in both contexts described antenatal consultations as time-pressured encounters in which “critical information” is defined by what is actionable under limited time—especially schedule literacy, key indicators, and targeted behavioural guidance. Trust management becomes salient when women bring in conflicting online information.

Subtheme 1. Antenatal pathway literacy as “critical” information

Clinicians consistently framed “critical” maternal health information as knowing when to attend care and what examinations are due at each gestational stage. One clinician stated: “The most important thing is knowing when to see a doctor and which examinations should be done at each gestational week.” (CD1). Another reinforced this by describing women’s repeated questions as pathway-oriented: “Women ask mostly: What should I pay attention to? When is my next check-up? What am I lacking? How big is my baby now?” (CD2).

Subtheme 2. Preventive counselling and behavioural reassurance within limited time

Clinicians reported routinely providing prevention-focused guidance on diet, supplements, and safe activities, reflecting women’s everyday self-management needs. For example: “We provide education on diet/weight management and supplements (e.g., folic acid, calcium, DHA).” (CD3) and “Pregnant women often ask what to do to stay healthy—especially activities/exercise and food.” (MD2).

Subtheme 3. Managing online–offline information conflict to protect decision coherence

When women cited conflicting online advice, clinicians often re-centered authority on hospital-based guidance to preserve coherence and reduce risk. One clinician explained: “Follow the guidance of the hospital where you’re being cared, and prioritize the advice of your obstetrician there.” (CD2). Another expressed frustration with repeated conflicts: “Then I’d just say, just believe and go check online. Don’t go here again.” (CD1).

Overall, clinicians operationalized “critical information” as timely, actionable guidance that fits brief encounters, while trust was challenged when digital information disrupted clinical decision coherence.

This theme converges with pregnant women’s Theme 2 and Theme 4: women sought safety boundaries and clinician confirmation to reduce uncertainty, while clinicians emphasised pathway literacy and targeted guidance. However, tension emerged when women’s multi-source verification (pregnant women Theme 4) was interpreted by clinicians as undermining clinical authority, which may reduce women’s perceived access to interpretive reassurance (pregnant women Theme 1) under time constraints.

Theme 2. Gender disclosure practices are ethically and institutionally mediated

Clinicians described fetal sex disclosure as shaped by institutional rules and ethical judgement rather than purely patient preference. The governance logic differed across settings, affecting what information can be shared and how clinicians manage maternal emotions and family expectations.

Subtheme 1. Policy-bounded nondisclosure in China

Chinese clinicians reported that fetal sex disclosure is restricted: “In China, doctors are not allowed to tell parents the baby’s gender; we only know at birth.” (Context explained in interview).

Subtheme 2. Emotion-protective and family-sensitive disclosure in Malaysia

Malaysian clinicians described disclosure as generally permissible but sometimes withheld to protect maternal emotions or manage family dynamics. One clinician stated: “In Malaysia, we tell the mothers—but if I think the gender will upset the mother, we may not tell.” (MD2). Another gave a family-context example: “If a mother already has three girls and hopes for a boy… we may say we ‘don’t see’ to avoid upsetting her early.” (MD3).

In short, fetal sex information was governed by different decision logics—regulatory restriction in China and clinician-discretionary emotional management in Malaysia.

This theme partially converges with pregnant women’s Theme 3 (social support and emotional coping) by highlighting how clinicians actively consider psychosocial impact. The tension is that women’s desire for interpretable fetal information (pregnant women Theme 1) may be structurally constrained in certain contexts, implying that “what can be shown” must be context-sensitive to local policy and ethical practice.

Theme 3. Digital platforms are contested yet promising for maternal information and continuity of care

Clinicians viewed apps and social media as double-edged: they can extend education and continuity of care, but they also amplify misinformation and anxiety. Feasibility depends on governance, clinician workload, credentialing, and sustained user adoption.

Subtheme 1. Misinformation and anxiety amplification on social media

Chinese clinicians described some platforms as high-risk information environments: “Red Note (Xiaohong Shu) is almost a ‘disaster zone’—anyone can post; information is mixed and can increase anxiety.” (CD3). Clinicians also noted that strong narratives can distort decisions: “Some narratives push ‘C-section is best’, which can mislead women and affect real clinical decision-making.” (CD2).

Subtheme 2. Credible-source curation and correction as practical mitigation

Despite concerns, clinicians suggested pragmatic strategies for improving information quality. For example: “If you want to learn, follow reputable doctors from good local hospitals on Douyin to obtain higher-quality health education.” (CD2) and “Pregnant women mostly use Red Note… products can help ‘set the record straight’.” (CD1).

Subtheme 3. Continuity-of-care tools and personalization are constrained by governance and adoption

Clinicians—especially in Malaysia—highlighted continuity practices and the potential for digitization: “In Malaysia, mothers carry the ‘Pink Book’ everywhere… it records due date and pregnancy problems and is used across facilities.” (MD1). They also described a tracking-to-personalization logic: “An app should track BP, glucose, and fetal growth across visits; then it can provide personalized information instead of generic content.” (MD3). At the same time, feasibility constraints were emphasized: “Third-party online consultation is burdensome… while hospital ‘internet hospitals can provide more complete online services…” (CD2) and “An app only works if people buy in and use it, and if it responds to what patients want.” (MD3).

Overall, clinicians saw digital platforms as useful when they deliver credible information and continuity, but risky when they intensify misinformation, and difficult to implement without governance alignment and user uptake.

This theme aligns closely with pregnant women’s Theme 4, in which women described active multi-source verification while continuing to rely on clinicians as decision anchors; similarly, clinicians emphasized the need for credible curation and source transparency. However, a key tension emerges because women also use social media for reassurance (pregnant women’s Theme 3), whereas clinicians view the same platforms as anxiety-amplifying and potentially disruptive to clinical decision-making, underscoring the value of moderated reassurance pathways that maintain trust and decision coherence.

Taken together, findings from pregnant women and obstetricians revealed both shared priorities and points of tension in how “critical” maternal health information is understood and acted upon. Discussion

Principal findings

This qualitative study explored what pregnant/postpartum women in China and Malaysia consider “critical” maternal health information and how such information shapes everyday self-management and care-seeking, while triangulating these accounts with obstetricians’ perspectives. Four themes from women’s interviews described “critical information” as information that (i) reduces uncertainty about fetal wellbeing through bonding-oriented interpretability, (ii) supports lifestyle self-management under uncertainty by clarifying safety boundaries and red-flag thresholds, (iii) is embedded in psychosocial coping through social support, and (iv) enables decision coherence through digital appraisal in partnership with clinicians. Obstetricians’ interviews yielded three complementary themes that situated these needs within time-pressured clinical encounters, ethically/institutionally mediated disclosure practices, and a contested digital information ecology. Taken together, triangulation indicated clear areas of convergence—especially around uncertainty reduction and actionable guidance—alongside tensions, most notably the role of social media as reassurance for women yet anxiety-amplifying and clinically disruptive from clinicians’ perspectives (Table 5).

Interpretation of women’s themes

First, the theme of bonding-oriented interpretability suggests that attachment-related cues (e.g., fetal movement, scans, heartbeat) can motivate healthier behaviours when they are both emotionally meaningful and interpretable. Women did not merely seek “more information”; they sought reassurance that is understandable, credible, and connected to action. This aligns with qualitative evidence that pregnancy-related information needs are often driven by uncertainty reduction, reassurance seeking, and sense-making (e.g., wanting to feel in control and “doing okay”), rather than one-way knowledge acquisition [17–19]. The implication is that maternal health information is experienced as “critical” when it provides clear interpretive scaffolding—what a cue means, what is normal, and what to do next—thereby supporting both emotional reassurance and timely, appropriate decision-making [18, 20].

Second, lifestyle self-management under uncertainty highlights that many pregnancy behaviors are governed by perceived risk, incomplete clarity, and an amplified sense of vulnerability. Participants described caution and sometimes avoidance (e.g., uncertainty regarding appropriate exercise intensity), indicating that generic advice may be less useful than safety-bounded guidance—what is safe for whom, under what conditions, and when escalation is warranted. This pattern is consistent with work showing that heightened risk perception during pregnancy can shape affective responses and decision-making, often prompting conservative choices when evidence is unclear or contested [21, 22]. In the context of physical activity, qualitative studies similarly report that fear of miscarriage/bleeding and uncertainty about “safe” exercise are common reasons women reduce or stop activity during pregnancy [23, 24], despite clinical guidance emphasizing graded activity and explicit “danger signs” that should trigger stopping and clinical consultation [25]. Importantly, the results suggest that “critical information” is operationalized as decision support (boundaries and thresholds) rather than health education alone.

Third, social support, as a psychosocial coping infrastructure, shows that emotional and instrumental support (from partners, family, and peers) mitigates stress and facilitates adherence to everyday self-management. Peer reassurance—often mediated through digital platforms—served to normalize symptoms and reduce worry for non-urgent concerns. This resonates with prior work emphasizing that social support during pregnancy contributes to women’s emotional well-being and coping, and that organized or peer-based support can reduce anxiety and low mood by reducing isolation and strengthening self-efficacy [26, 27]. However, the same reassurance pathways may increase exposure to low-quality or contradictory information, as social media environments can simultaneously provide support and amplify misinformation or anxiety, thereby linking social support directly to the information-ecology challenge identified by clinicians [2, 28].

Fourth, digital appraisal and clinician partnership demonstrate that women in both contexts actively verify information across multiple sources rather than relying on a single channel. Clinicians remained “anchors” for consequential decisions, particularly when online advice conflicted. This mirrors prior qualitative and mixed-methods findings that pregnant women commonly use the web and social platforms for rapid information and reassurance, but then seek confirmation from health professionals—especially when uncertainty persists or advice appears inconsistent [29–31]. Survey evidence further suggests that consulting multiple sources is common in pregnancy and that conflicting information can generate confusion and prompt additional verification efforts [32]. Yet the present study clarifies that decision coherence is fragile when time pressure limits explanation and when digital sources introduce contradictory narratives, a challenge increasingly recognized in work describing how the modern digital information environment (including misinformation) complicates clinical communication and care delivery [33].

Clinicians’ perspectives and the added value of triangulation

Clinicians converged with women on the primacy of actionable guidance: antenatal pathway literacy (what examinations are due and when), preventive counselling (diet/supplements, safe activity), and escalation logic for risk. This convergence strengthens the credibility of the thematic framework and indicates that uncertainty reduction is a shared goal across stakeholder groups.

Triangulation also surfaced why tensions emerge. Clinicians described consultations as time-pressured, which can constrain the depth of explanation and shape how trust is maintained when patients introduce conflicting online content. In this context, clinicians may reassert “offline-first” authority to protect decision coherence, whereas women may interpret limited explanations as insufficient reassurance and continue verification elsewhere. The most pronounced tension concerned social media: women used it for reassurance and symptom normalization, while clinicians viewed the same platforms as anxiety-amplifying and potentially disruptive to clinical decision-making. Table 5, therefore, clarifies that “critical maternal health information” functions not only as content but also as a coordination mechanism between digital appraisal practices and time-limited clinical encounters.

Cross-context interpretation

This study does not aim to present a formal cross-cultural comparison between China and Malaysia; instead, the inclusion of both settings enabled a cross-context interpretation of how healthcare pathways, dominant digital platforms, and local governance shape information seeking and clinician–patient communication. Across both contexts, core needs were broadly similar in content (uncertainty reduction, safety-bounded self-management, and clinician anchoring), while context-sensitive differences were most visible in care-seeking pathways and institutional practices (e.g., disclosure norms) that may influence how information is shared and acted upon. This approach supports the design of context-sensitive adaptations rather than assuming a uniform “one-size-fits-all” maternal information strategy.

Strengths

This study has several strengths. First, it incorporated two stakeholder groups—pregnant/postpartum women and obstetricians—allowing convergence and tension to be examined explicitly rather than inferred. Second, data were collected across two real-world contexts, supporting cross-context interpretation of information needs and decision processes. Third, transparency was enhanced by presenting themes with subthemes and exemplar quotations and by providing an explicit triangulation summary (Table 5), supporting auditability and interpretive clarity.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, triangulation was primarily based on informant sources; incorporating secondary data (e.g., national maternal health guidelines, policy reports, or official health statistics) could further enrich contextual interpretation and strengthen system-level explanations, as suggested by reviewers. Second, interview mode differed by context (online vs. in-person) and interviews were conducted in different languages (Mandarin vs. English), which may influence disclosure, depth, and nuance; although participant checking was used to enhance transcript accuracy, translation and interpretation may still introduce meaning shifts. Third, the study did not employ formal peer debriefing, and future work could strengthen credibility through external audit or additional analytic triangulation. Fourth, while participants discussed online consultation and digital information seeking, barriers such as cost, internet connectivity, and digital literacy were not systematically examined; these factors may shape equity of access and should be explored in future studies.

Implications for practice and policy

These findings suggest that reducing maternal health misinformation requires more than increasing information volume; it requires delivering interpretable, actionable, and clinically aligned guidance that helps women distinguish normal symptoms from red flags and supports timely escalation to care. In practice, antenatal services could standardize brief, stage-specific “core messages” (e.g., safe activity boundaries, warning thresholds, and next-step pathways) and reinforce them through low-burden tools such as pre-visit question prompts and visit-linked summaries, thereby mitigating uncertainty in time-pressured encounters. At the policy level, partnerships between public maternity services and trusted digital channels could support credible-source signaling and moderated reassurance pathways, so that women’s emotional reassurance needs are met without amplifying anxiety or undermining clinical decision coherence. Together, these measures can strengthen trust, improve decision consistency, and reduce the likelihood that misinformation drives avoidable risk behaviors or delayed care-seeking.

Conclusion

In summary, pregnant/postpartum women in China and Malaysia described “critical” maternal health information as information that reduces uncertainty, supports safe self-management, and clarifies escalation to professional care. Obstetricians largely converged on these priorities but highlighted structural constraints and tensions arising from misinformation-prone digital environments and time-pressured encounters. Triangulation indicates that effective maternal health support requires not only credible content but also mechanisms that coordinate digital appraisal practices with clinical communication, with context-sensitive adaptation informed by local pathways and governance.
