Preventing Crohn’s recurrence, Kono-S and extended mesenteric excision: network meta-analysis category
Authors: Talia Shepherd, Devansh Shah, Joseph Do Woong Choi, Toufic El-Khoury, Nimalan Pathma-Nathan, Amy Cao, James Wei Tatt Toh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCrohn’s disease (CD) is a complex disease with rising incidence that is usually treated with medical management. However, surgery plays an important role for medically refractive cases, or Crohn’s related complications. New techniques such as extended mesenteric excision (EME), and Kono-S anastomosis (KSA) are being increasingly adopted. However, their potential for reducing CD recurrence remains unclear.MethodStudies were identified through searching Medline, Embase, Cochrane Library and JBI EBP from database inception to January 2025. Studies where patients with CD had intestinal resection and underwent at least two of four interventions being KSA, other anastomosis, EME and/or limited mesenteric excision (LME) were included. Two investigators independently identified studies and abstracted data. RCT quality was assessed using Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool and cohort study quality assessed using Newcastle Ottawa Scale. The study adhered to PRISMA guidelines. Bayesian network meta-analysis was conducted to assess risk of surgical and endoscopic recurrence at 5 years and 6–12 months respectively.ResultsThirteen studies composed of 3 RCTs and 10 cohort studies (n = 2,044) were included, assessing surgical and/or endoscopic recurrence after KSA, other anastomosis, EME or LME. There was statistically significant reduction in surgical recurrence in KSA compared to other anastomosis (odds ratio 0.07, 95% credible interval) but not EME compared to LME. There was no difference in endoscopic recurrence in any groups.ConclusionKSA was associated with reduced surgical recurrence compared with other anastomosis, but not endoscopic recurrence. There were insufficient studies to support similar improvement in recurrence in EME over LME.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00423-026-04103-5.

Introduction

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a condition characterised by lesions predominantly along the gastrointestinal tract. It has an incidence of 3–20 per 100,000 patients where the incidence has plateaued in western countries, but is rising in developing nations [1]. It has a multifactorial pathogenesis with attributions to the autoimmune, genetic, environmental (including the gut microbiome), and gut-immune axis factors [2].

Traditionally, CD is initially managed by medical therapy with surgery reserved for refractory cases, or due to complications such as fibrotic strictures, intrabdominal abscesses, fistulae, perforation and malignancy. Despite advances in medical management with biologic therapy, disease recurrence rates are high with up to 86% at 10 years and one third of these patients’ requiring surgery [3]. However surgery does not prevent recurrence with 17.7% and 31.3% of patients at 5 and 10 years respectively requiring a repeat resection [4] Recently the treatment paradigm for uncomplicated ileo-colic disease has shifted back towards a surgery-first approach as outcomes from minimally invasive techniques improve, with the revised European Crohn’s and Colitis Organisation (ECCO) guidelines suggesting a laparoscopic resection is a suitable alternative to infliximab or adalimumab in limited terminal ileal or ileocolic non-stricturing CD [5].

As our understanding of the disease pathophysiology changes, there has been interest in developing new surgical techniques to reduce disease recurrence, prevent repeat resection, and improve quality of life.

The standard anastomotic configuration is the stapled side to side anastomosis (STSA) [5] however in 2003, Dr Toru Kono first performed the Kono-S anastomosis (KSA), with its outcomes first published in 2011 [6]. It involved a handsewn 7 cm wide, antimesenteric transverse anastomosis which excluded the mesentery from the anastomosis with supporting columns that prevented mesenteric distortion and close mesenteric mobilisation to preserve the mesentery and neovascular supply. The KSA operated on the principle of excluding the mesentery from the anastomosis based on the observation that CD recurrence occurred on the mesenteric border [7].

Whether the mesentery plays a critical role in driving the disease process is contentious since Coffey et al. published their cohort study describing an extended resection of the mesentery [8]. The mesentery contains neurovasculature, lymphatics, stromal cells, and fibroblasts which secrete adipokines and neuropeptides, hence potentially contributing to immunomodulation and chronic inflammation.[9, 10] By resecting the affected mesentery at the root, they demonstrated a significant reduction in surgical disease recurrence compared to a standard STSA.

The efficacy of these techniques remains controversial and it is difficult for the surgeon to decide which to adopt if any, when operating on Crohn’s patients. Subsequent larger cohort studies and randomised controlled trials have published results both for and against each method with regards to disease recurrence. Unfortunately, most studies are only two-arm comparing either type of anastomosis (KSA vs. other) or approach to the mesentery (LME vs. EME) but few have compared these approaches to each other. The aim of this study was to compare the relative effectiveness of all four surgical techniques regarding disease recurrence.

Methods

The network meta-analysis was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Fig. 1). [11] The study was registered with PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews, registration number CRD42024499493).

PRISMA Flow diagram [11]

Literature search

We conducted a systematic literature search using Embase, MEDLINE, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and the Joanna Briggs Institute Evidence Based Practice (JBI EBP) Database from inception to January 2025. Only adult human studies reported in English were included. Participants were patients who underwent an intestinal resection for CD. Studies must have included a comparison of at least two of the four interventions (KSA, other anastomosis, EME or LME) following the PICO scheme. We used a combination of subject words and free words for our search strategy which included (1) Crohn’s disease” OR Crohn* AND (2) Kono-S or Kono* OR (3) mesenteric resection or mesenter* resect* or mesenter* excis* or mesenter* remov* or mesenter* cons*.

Study selection

After excluding duplicates in EndNote Clarivate, primary studies’ titles and abstracts underwent independent blinded assessment by two authors (TS and JC) using Rayyan software. Recent high yield conference abstracts were screened given the rapidly evolving evidence in this field. The inclusion criteria consisted of the following: (1) studies evaluating patients who had undergone intestinal resection due to CD; (2) studies where at least two of the interventions measured were compared including KSA, standard anastomosis (as defined by the authors and including end to end, end to side and STSA), extended mesenteric excision (EME) or limited mesenteric excision (LME) (3) randomised clinical trials (RCTs) and cohort studies, and (4) studies where CD recurrence was reported. Review articles, case reports, cohort studies with a single arm, and studies where disease recurrence was not specifically described were excluded. Discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved by consensus from the senior author (JT).

Data extraction

We extracted data on the baseline patient characteristics, study exclusion criteria, operative details including type of anastomosis or approach to the mesentery and post-operative details including disease recurrence rate and method of detecting disease recurrence, follow-up time and overall complications.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias was independently assessed by two authors (TS, DS) with RCTs assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 Tool (RoB 2), and cohort studies gauged with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). [11, 12]. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus between all authors.

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome measure was the incidence of surgical recurrence at 5 years. The secondary outcome was endoscopic recurrence at 6–12 months. Clinical recurrence could not be assessed due to insufficient data.

Statistical analysis

A network meta-analysis was performed using a Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo method in WinBUGS 1.4.3 (MRC Biostatistics Unit, Cambridge, United Kingdom) through the conduit of the Microsoft Excel-based macro NetMetaXL 1.6.1 (Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health). A convergence test was conducted for each analysis by checking whether the Monte Carlo error was < 5% of the standard deviation (SD) of the effect estimates or the variance between the studies. Convergence was achieved for all analyses at 20,000 “burn in” runs and 30,000 model runs.

Surgical and endoscopic recurrence rates were calculated by the pooled estimates of odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of direct comparisons between KSA, other anastomosis, EME and LME. Both direct and indirect evidence for all intervention groups’ comparisons were combined to estimate the examined outcomes with a 95% equal-tail credible interval (CrI). To represent the results visually, representation of probabilities of network plots with network nodes and lines, and forest plots were generated by the gemtc package in STATA (Stata MP, version 15; StataCorp LP). Pairwise comparison tables and graphical rankograms were generated using NetMetaXL. Along with rankograms, the relative efficacy of different intervention groups for preventing CD recurrence (surgical and endoscopic) was presented with their surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA). SUCRA values ranged from 0 to 1, with 0 having the highest chance of being ranked lowest and 1 having the highest chance of being ranked best. All results have been presented as relative effects (OR) and Bayesian estimates of the probability of developing CD recurrence.

Quality assessment

Cochrane RoB 2 found that two of the three RCTs were of a low risk of bias. The Trencheva et al. study was published as an abstract only and did not comment on the allocation sequence randomisation and concealment and there were some concerns in domain 1, leading to an overall quality rating of “some concerns” [13]. This is represented in Fig. 4 created using the Robvis tool [15]. The results of the NOS for cohort studies are summarised in Table 2. Eight of ten studies were of high quality, with the remaining two studies of moderate quality (awarded 6 points each). These two studies were the 2019 Shimada et al. study due to issues with comparability (difference in age and higher pre-operative biological therapy use in KSA group) and no comment on follow-up. The 2024 Uchino et al. had issues with comparability (higher post-operative biological therapy in KSA group) and no comment on length or loss to follow-up.

Surgical recurrence at 5 years

There was a statistically significant reduction in the risk of surgical CD recurrence in the KSA group compared to other anastomosis (OR 0.07, 95% CrI 0.02–0.20). However, there was no significant difference in surgical recurrence in LME compared to EME (OR 0.92, 95% CrI 0.44–2.09). Although the network meta-analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in surgical recurrence in EME vs. KSA (OR 0.00, 95% CrI 0.00-0.14), LME vs. KSA (OR 0.00, 95% CrI 0.00-0.14), LME vs. other anastomosis (OR 0.00, 95% CrI 0.00-0.01), and EME vs. other anastomosis (OR 0.00, 95% CrI 0.00-0.01), this may not be clinically applicable. Only one study (Holubar et al. 2024) compared KSA to EME and only one recurrence event was reported in the KSA group, with none in the EME group, likely skewing the indirect comparisons in favour of EME and LME [16, 17] As such LME was ranked first with SUCRA 0.86, EME second (SUCRA 0.80), KSA third (SUCRA 0.33), and other anastomosis last (SUCRA 0.74 × 10− 5) as demonstrated in Table 3 and rankogram displayed in Fig. 5. There was no statistically significant reduction in the risk of endoscopic CD recurrence in the KSA group compared to other anastomosis (OR 0.78, 95% CrI 0.54–1.13). Similarly, there was no significant difference in endoscopic recurrence in EME compared to LME (OR 0.94, 95% CrI 0.53–1.68). There was also no significant reduction in endoscopic recurrence in KSA vs. EME (OR 1.06, 95% CrI 0.20–5.14), KSA vs. LME (OR 0.99, 95% CrI 0.17–5.42), LME vs. other anastomosis (OR 0.79, 95% CrI 0.14–4.72), and EME vs. other anastomosis (OR 0.74, 95% CrI 0.14–4.02). Discussion

This is the first Bayesian network analysis to simultaneously compare KSA, other anastomosis, EME and LME regarding their role in preventing recurrence of Crohn’s disease.

Although the KSA preserves the mesentery as much as possible, the anastomosis itself is on the antimesenteric side of the bowel, thus “excluding” it from the anastomosis. By virtue of anastomotic configuration, EME assumes the same principle by resecting the mesentery adjacent to the anastomosis.

At present, the 2024 ECCO guidelines recommend STSA, but suggested that the KSA can be an alternative approach after ileocolic resection [5]. This is based on the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine Grade 3 evidence. However, they suggest that EME is not yet supported by evidence, based on Grade 4 evidence [5].

Our study found a significant reduction in CD surgical recurrence at 5 years in KSA vs. other anastomosis and ranked superior by SUCRA values 0.33 and 0.74 × 10− 5 respectively. Although no significant difference was found between LME and EME, both interventions were found by network meta-analysis to be superior to KSA and other anastomosis based on OR and SUCRA values. However, these findings need to be interpreted with caution. There were four studies directly comparing KSA to other anastomosis, three directly comparing LME to EME, one study comparing KSA to EME, and no study compared LME with KSA/other anastomosis, nor EME with other anastomosis. In addition, there was only one recurrence event in the KSA group and zero in the EME group and this one study formed the basis of indirect comparisons to generate OR and SUCRA values comparing KSA to LME, and other anastomosis with EME/LME, probably skewing results in favour of EME/LME and resulting in OR of 0.00. More studies directly comparing KSA with EME, LME and other anastomotic techniques would improve the statistical power to determine the optimal intervention for minimising surgical recurrence. Regarding endoscopic recurrence, no significant differences were found in CD recurrence at 6–12 months follow-up, however KSA was ranked first based on SUCRA values, followed by EME, LME and other anastomosis. There were six direct comparisons between KSA and other anastomosis, four comparing EME to LME and one study comparing KSA to EME with 4 events of recurrence in the KSA group and 6 in the EME group allowing for improved indirect comparison compared to the indirect statistical analysis of the respective surgical recurrence [17].

The KSA gained momentum particularly after the SuPREMe-CD study, where 6 month endoscopic recurrence (Rutgeerts ≥ i2) was significantly reduced in the KSA group (22.2%) compared to STSA group (62.8%) with OR 5.91 (p < 0.001) and longer period to clinical recurrence [18]. A major limitation of this study as well as others included in this network meta-analysis is that they graded endoscopic recurrence based on the original Rutgeerts score of ≥ i2, where 5 or more ulcers were found either at the anastomosis or at the terminal ileum. Ulcers along an anastomotic staple line are not an uncommon finding and can be present without true CD recurrence. Thus, the score was later modified to account for this with separation into i2a (5 or more ulcers at the anastomosis), and i2b (5 or more ulcers at the terminal ileum), with the latter more predictive of endoscopic CD recurrence [19]. Only three of the studies in this study used Rutgeerts score ≥ i2b to define endoscopic recurrence. Two of these compared KSA to STSA (one RCT and one cohort study), and the other was an RCT comparing EME vs. LME. None of these studies found any significant difference in endoscopic recurrence [13, 20, 21] The lack of difference in endoscopic recurrence between the groups, despite there being reduction in surgical recurrence in KSA compared to other anastomosis may be explained by fewer studies using the modified Rutgeerts score, causing there to be an overrepresentation of endoscopic recurrence in other studies that used to tradition Rutgeerts score. Though it is also possible that surgical recurrence itself is influenced by heterogenous factors that play into the decision to operate or manage the patient medically, making it a difficult endpoint to replicate between studies.

Another limitation of this study is the construct of cohort studies where the other anastomosis or LME groups were often from a historic cohort that compared KSA or EME group. Over time, the development of biologics has changed the landscape of CD, inducing and maintaining improved remission. Five of the 10 cohort studies had higher rates of pre- or post-operative biological use in the KSA or EME group compared to other anastomosis or LME respectively [6, 8, 14, 22, 23]. This may have introduced temporal bias, where the biologics may have played a role in maintaining post-operative remission rather than the surgery itself. In each of these studies where surgical recurrence was assessed, it was significantly lower in the KSA or EME group compared to other anastomosis or LME respectively. Unfortunately, only one RCT comparing surgical recurrence in KSA, constituting level 1b evidence, the SuPREMe-CD study was present, drawing greater influence of results from cohort studies [18]. As such prospective cohort studies or RCTs with longer term follow-up would be useful in reducing the confounding effects from biologics.

Furthermore, the other anastomosis group included both STSA and end to end anastomosis (ETEA). This may have underrepresented the strength of the STSA as it has been found to be similar or superior in preventing CD recurrence compared to ETEA [24, 25].

Overall, we must take into consideration what type of recurrence actually matters clinically. The presence of endoscopic recurrence from ulcers at the anastomosis or terminal ileum may necessitate changes to patient medications particularly in the symptomatic individual, but it may not necessarily warrant intestinal resection. As such, surgical recurrence represents a definitive clinical outcome, whereas, as we have discussed, endoscopic recurrence is a surrogate marker but limited by variability and evolution in scoring systems. Thus, the lower surgical recurrence in KSA compared to other anastomosis may be a more compelling finding, despite no significant reduction in endoscopic recurrence in the same comparison of the two groups.

Our study is consistent with the ECCO guidelines that there is currently insufficient evidence to recommend EME over LME. Whilst our study finds that KSA is superior to other anastomosis with regards to reduction in surgical recurrence at 5 years, these findings must be interpreted with caution due to the aforementioned limitations. Despite this, the KSA was not been found to be inferior to other anastomotic configurations with respect to CD recurrence, which is also supported in the latest ECCO guidelines [5].

Future studies comparing KSA to stapled STSA, as well as EME and LME combined with either KSA and/or stapled STSA is required. This along with endoscopic recurrence being defined as Rutgeerts ≥ i2b and measuring surgical recurrence beyond 5 years is expected to help inform surgeons on the most optimal anastomotic technique in CD. The authors eagerly await the results of three active RCTs that are attempting to provide further evidence: the European HAND2END and End2End studies will look at KSA against other anastomosis (due to be completed in 2028), the international MESOCOLIC trial will compare LME and EME (due to be completed in 2025) and the four by four MEErKAT trial (UK) which will compare KSA and EME independently and as a combined approach (due to be completed in 2026).

Conclusion

New surgical techniques have emerged over the last two decades for CD with the KSA establishing as an alternative anastomotic technique to the stapled STSA, and EME being investigated for its efficacy in reducing CD recurrence. Our network meta-analysis demonstrated a reduction surgical recurrence at 5 years with the KSA compared to other anastomosis but no significant differences endoscopic recurrence between KSA and other anastomosis or between EME and LME. Surgical recurrence between EME and KSA could not be appropriately compared due to limited number of studies. However, we advise surgeons to interpret the results within the limits of differing definitions of endoscopic recurrence, temporal bias from the effects of biological therapy, and the retrospective nature of some cohort studies. Further research directly comparing anastomotic and mesenteric techniques are required.
