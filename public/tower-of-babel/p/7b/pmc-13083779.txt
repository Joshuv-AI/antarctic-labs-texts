Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma/Waldenström macroglobulinaemia initially presenting as postmenopausal pelvic pain and bleeding: a rare case report with literature review☆
Authors: Mackenzie Dent, Mustafa Al-Kawaaz, Kadam Maruthi, Jenci Hawthorne, Whitney Goldsberry, Catherine Spencer, Samer Z. Al-Quran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Rare case of LPL / Waldenström macroglobulinaemia with predominant myometrial & cervical involvement.• Atypical perivascular lymphoid infiltrate prompted testing that showed IgM kappa paraproteinemia and MYD88 mutation.•Only two cases previously reported of LPL/WM in female genital tract..

Introduction

Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL) is an rare and indolent low-grade B-cell lymphoproliferative neoplasm that often presents with vague constitutional symptoms or anemia, though many patients never develop symptoms. Involvement of the bone marrow combined with IgM paraproteinemia is found in 95% of cases. Such cases are classified as IgM-type lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma / Waldenström macroglobulinaemia (LPL/WM) according the 2022 World Health Organization Classification of Hematolymphoid tumors (Gertz, 2015, Montes-Moreno et al., 2024, García-Sanz et al., 2001, Castillo and Treon, 2018, Lin et al., 2003).

While most commonly involving the bone marrow, LPL/WM can sometimes involve the lymph nodes and spleen, and rarely the central nervous system, skin, and pleural cavities. To our knowledge, there have been only two cases previously described in the literature of LPL/WM involving the female genital tract (Albawardi et al., 2013, Eulitt et al., 2019); both of which had prominent involvement of the ovaries. We henceforth describe a rare case of lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma/ Waldenström macroglobulinaemia with predominant myometrial involvement.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old postmenopausal female with pelvic pain presented to following a single episode of abnormal uterine bleeding. She endorsed an eight-month history of pelvic pain with associated urinary urgency and pelvic pressure, as well as fatigue; however, she did not have lymphadenopathy, weight loss, fever, or night sweats. Of note, three months prior to presentation, she had a pap smear that showed high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesional (HSIL) cells that were positive for human papillomavirus (HPV) with genotype 16. Subsequent colposcopy identified chronic cervicitis with reactive changes but was negative for dysplasia. Additional pertinent past medical and surgical history includes remote history of thyroid cancer, two spontaneous vaginal deliveries of living children and bilateral tubal ligation. She has a family history of cervical cancer in her aunt. Pelvic examination revealed right adnexal tenderness with uterine enlargement, mild vaginal atrophy, and a stenotic cervical os that lead to an unsuccessful endometrial biopsy. The need for sampling to rule out hyperplasia or malignancy was discussed with her and the decision was made to proceed to the operating room for endometrial sampling. Preoperative laboratory testing, including a complete blood count, liver enzymes, and serum electrolytes revealed hyperproteinemia with hypoalbuminemia and borderline anemia. A transvaginal ultrasound was unremarkable. The uterus was within normal limits with normal bilateral ovaries. No other pertinent imaging had previously been performed. Following discussion of the risks, benefits, and alternatives, she chose to proceed with hysteroscopy, dilation and curettage. Hysteroscopy was ultimately aborted due to cervical stenosis and inability to enter the uterine cavity. Due to her continued discomfort, the patient strongly desired to proceed with definitive surgical treatment at this point.

Following discussion of the risks, benefits, and alternatives, the patient underwent an uneventful total robotic hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy. Surgical findings were unremarkable. Specimens, including uterus, cervix, and bilateral fallopian tubes and ovaries, were placed in formalin and sent for pathologic analysis.

Results

Gross examination of the surgical specimens was unremarkable. No masses or tumor nodules were identified, and the ovaries were not enlarged. Representative sections of the anterior and posterior endomyometrium, bilateral ovaries, and bilateral fallopian tubes as well as the entire cervix were submitted for histologic examination pursuant to College of American Pathologists (CAP) protocols. Examination of histologic sections from the hysterectomy specimen showed atypical perivascular lymphoid aggregates involving the cervical stroma, myometrium, and bilateral fallopian tubes and ovaries, with predominant myometrial and cervical involvement (see Fig. 1). These aggregates were composed of small mature appearing lymphocytes with no increase in plasma cells. Immunohistochemical studies showed the predominance of CD20(+) B-cells which were negative for LEF1, CD3, CD5, CD10, CD23, Cyclin D1 and CD43 with low labeling of nuclei by Ki-67. These findings were highly suspicious for involvement by low grade B cell lymphoma. The differential diagnosis included marginal zone lymphoma and lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma. B-cell immunoglobulin gene rearrangement testing could not be performed. The patient was referred to an oncologist at the Bone Marrow Transplant Clinic. At the initial appointment, the patient complained of additional symptoms of generalized headaches, nausea, night sweats, and forgetfulness. Serum protein electrophoresis (SPE) with concurrent serum immunofixation electrophoresis (IFE) identified an IgM kappa monoclonal protein with an M−spike of 2.99 g/dL in the gamma region (See Fig. 2). Urine protein electrophoresis (UPE) showed similar results. Serum free light chains highlighted an elevated kappa/lambda (2.06). Elevations in serum IgM (4206), B2-microglobin (3.42), and viscosity (3.3) were also noted, though lactate dehydrogenase was not elevated. Hepatitis and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) testing were negative. Subsequently, molecular studies were performed on sections of the myometrium with the atypical lymphocytic infiltrates and demonstrated the presence of MYD88 p.L265P mutation. The patient was diagnosed with Waldenstrom macroglobulinaemia (WM). A positron emission tomography scan revealed hypermetabolic bilateral axillary (max SUV 1.4, largest 0.5 cm) and upper abdominal lymph nodes (max SUV 4.2, largest 2.4 cm). Microscopic examination of the bone marrow aspirate and biopsy showed a hypercellular bone marrow with a patchy focally extensive lymphocytic infiltrate (see Fig. 2) composed predominately of small lymphocytes, with scattered plasma cells. The neoplastic lymphocytes were positive for CD20 and IgM with a low labeling of nuclei by Ki-67(<5%). They were negative for Cyclin-D1. In-situ hybridization studies for Kappa and Lambda light chain mRNA showed mainly kappa-restricted plasma cells. Flow cytometric immunophenotyping of the bone marrow aspirate (see Fig. 2) demonstrated the presence of a monotypic population of CD5(−)/CD10(−) B-cells with kappa light chain restriction. These B-cells were positive for CD19, CD20, CD22, CD38, CD45, CD79b, and CD200. They were negative for CD23. Further molecular genetic testing did not reveal a CXCR4 mutation and cytogenetics resulted in a normal female karyotype 46,XX[20].

While the initial morphologic and immunohistochemical findings could not definitively differentiate between marginal zone lymphoma (MZL) and lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL), additional evidence including the features of IgM paraproteinemia with hyperviscosity symptoms, bone marrow involvement by clonal B-cells and plasma cells and presence of MYD88 p.L265P mutation confirmed the diagnosis of LPL/WM.

Discussion

Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL) is a rare and indolent low-grade B-cell lymphoproliferative neoplasm comprised of small B lymphocytes, plasmacytoid lymphocytes, and plasma cells. With an incidence of three-to-four cases per million people annually, it represents roughly two percent of all hematologic malignancies (Montes-Moreno et al., 2024).

Although often used interchangeably with LPL, Waldenström macroglobulinaemia (WM) is defined by the presence of both LPL in the bone marrow and an IgM monoclonal component in the blood. Approximately 5% of LPL cases have serum IgG or IgA monoclonal proteins or lack any monoclonal gammopathy (Cao et al., 2016). Furthermore, there have been cases of IgM-type LPL without bone marrow involvement. These patients have a higher prevalence of extramedullary involvement with fewer hyperviscosity symptoms and a lower rate of MYD88 mutations. Moreover, IgM monoclonal gammopathies have been found, though less frequently, in association with other non-Hodgkin mature B-cell lymphomas such as: marginal zone lymphoma (MZL), diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), small lymphocytic lymphoma/chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL/SLL), mantle cell lymphoma (MCL), and follicular lymphoma (FL) (Gertz, 2015, Montes-Moreno et al., 2024, García-Sanz et al., 2001, Castillo and Treon, 2018).

The most common symptoms at diagnosis are fatigue and constitutional symptoms. Anemia, hyperviscosity symptoms (epistaxis, blurred vision, headache, etc.), and/or neuropathy due to IgM paraprotein build-up may also be present; however, a large group of patients are asymptomatic at presentation, and many never develop symptoms. Other presenting symptoms tend to represent locations of extramedullary involvement. For example, lymphadenopathy when the lymph nodes are involved, cough and dyspnea when the lungs are involved, or pelvic pain and abnormal uterine bleeding when the female genital tract is involved (Gertz, 2015, Montes-Moreno et al., 2024, García-Sanz et al., 2001, Castillo and Treon, 2018, Winterbauer, 1974).

Extramedullary involvement at the time of presentation is uncommon with only 10–20% of cases having lymph node or splenic infiltration, and an additional 4.4% of patients with involvement of the lungs, soft tissue, central nervous system, kidneys and bones (Lin et al., 2003). Cases of LPL/WM involving the female genital tract.

A commonality among all three cases is that the definitive diagnosis of LPL could not be determined from the gynecologic specimen alone. In addition to the IgM monoclonal gammopathy in the blood, bone marrow specimens were required to establish the diagnosis of LPL/WM. Extranodal marginal zone lymphoma (EMZL) and LPL have historically shown significant morphologic, immunophenotypic and molecular overlap. Molecular testing is essential for the distinction between these two entities (Mason et al., 2017, Bassarova et al., 2015, Choong et al., 2019). MYD88 L265P is the driver mutation in 93–97% of LPL cases, though it has also been identified in 10–15% of cases of MZL. On the other hand, CXCR4 mutations are essentially exclusive to LPL/WM, though not required for the diagnosis as it is only seen in up to 40% of patients with LPL/WM (Hunter et al., 2016, Shekhar et al., 2021).

Another commonality this case had with the Albawardi, 2013 case was a gynecologic history of multiparity and HSIL. This may be a possible area for future research once more cases of LPL/WM in the female genital tract have been reported.

The patient underwent plasmapheresis in accordance with the National Comprehansive Cancer Network Guidelines, which decreased her IgM from over 4000 mg/dl to 1281 mg/dl, prior to initiation of chemotherapy. The preferred treatment regimen includes bendamustine/rituximab or treatment with a bruton kinase inhibitor (Zanubrutinib monotherapy or ibrutinib with or without rituximab) (NCCN guidelines). Her chemotherapy regimen was initially delayed due to complications from a methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) central line-associated bloodstream infection. She eventually started treatment with Zanubrutinib four months after surgery.

In conclusion, while LPL/WM involvement of the female genital tract is exceedingly rare, it should be considered on the differential diagnosis if atypical lymphoid aggregates or lymphocytic infiltrations are observed in gynecologic specimens and such findings should prompt further investigation.
