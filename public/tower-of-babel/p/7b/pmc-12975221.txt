Smartphone addiction and temporomandibular disorders among university students: A machine learning based multiple regression analysis study
Authors: Hazel Çelik Güzel, Ozan Bahadir Türkmen, Ahmet Kurtoğlu, Sule Keçelioğlu, Burcin Akçay, Ebru Kaya Mutlu, Neah Albasha, Safaa Mostafa Elkholi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) involve complex interactions among behavioral and musculoskeletal factors, and machine learning (ML) has increasingly been used to model these multidimensional relationships. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between smartphone addiction and TMD symptoms by evaluating the possible contributions of clinical variables such as head posture, cervical dysfunction, and masseter muscle sensitivity to this relationship and by examining the multivariate structure using ML methods. A single-blind, cross-sectional study was conducted among university students in Turkey. Participants completed several assessments: Smartphone Addiction Scale-Short Version (SAS-SV), Fonseca Anamnestic Index for TMD severity, Neck Disability Index, Numerical Rating Scale, forward head posture, and masseter muscle pressure pain threshold using algometry. Data were analyzed using Pearson correlation, logistic regression, and various ML classification algorithms (e.g., extreme gradient boosting [XGBoost], support vector machines). Shapley additive explanations analysis was applied to determine variable importance in ML models. In this study, XGBoost demonstrated the most successful performance in determining the relationships between smartphone addiction and TMD symptoms, while the support vector machines model showed the most successful performance in classifying participants. The findings suggest that this ML based approach, taking into account postural abnormalities and masseter muscle sensitivity, could contribute to the early identification of individuals at risk for TMD and the planning of targeted intervention strategies. Research findings have revealed complex relationships between SAS-SV and TMD symptoms among university students. In analyses conducted using ML algorithms, the support vector machines model stood out with the highest classification accuracy (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.94) and performance indicators. Shapley additive explanations analyses showed that smartphone usage time, masseter muscle sensitivity, and Fonseca Anamnestic Index score were the most decisive variables on SAS-SV. In TMD prediction, the highest predictive power was explained by temporomandibular joint pain. Ensemble models such as random forest and gradient boosting yielded more successful results with lower error rates and higher R2 values compared to classical regression methods. The findings indicate that smartphone use indirectly affects TMD through postural disorders and muscle sensitivity.

1. Introduction

The advent of new technologies has precipitated a considerable increase in the ubiquity of smartphones, which now boast an estimated global user base of approximately 5.1 billion.[1] Smartphones are no longer solely used for making calls or sending messages but also serve multiple functions, including internet access, email communication, gaming, and social networking.[1] However, excessive and uncontrolled smartphone use has led to discussions regarding whether smartphone addiction constitutes a true addiction. While some researchers classify this condition as behavioral addiction, others prefer the term “problematic use.”[2,3] Regardless of the terminology, studies suggest that excessive smartphone use contributes to both musculoskeletal and psychological issues. Among musculoskeletal disorders, neck, eye, shoulder, and wrist pain, as well as temporomandibular disorders (TMD), have been linked to smartphone use; however, the direct causative link remains under investigation.[4-9] Moreover, excessive smartphone use has been associated with psychological issues such as depression, anxiety, and low self-esteem.[6,10] The increased daily screen time and susceptibility to smartphone addiction among young people has led research in this field to focus primarily on university students.[11]

The etiology of TMD involves a combination of biomechanical, psychosocial, and neuromuscular factors, and in this context, the literature identifies several clinically relevant TMD, such as myofascial pain, joint-related pain, and disc displacement, that differ in their clinical presentation and symptom patterns.[12-17] Recent studies suggest that the increased use of digital technology may act as a novel risk factor triggering these mechanisms.[9,18] A moderate correlation has been found between smartphone addiction and TMD, with individuals who engage in intensive smartphone use exhibiting a 1.77-fold higher risk of TMD-related pain.[9] Smartphone addiction has been linked to prolonged cervical flexion, forward head posture (FHP), and musculoskeletal dysfunctions, all of which may contribute indirectly to TMD pathogenesis.[9,19,20] Understanding the underlying mechanisms of the smartphone–TMD relationship is crucial for developing preventive and therapeutic strategies. Traditional diagnostic approaches for TMD remain limited due to its heterogeneous clinical presentation and multifactorial nature.

In recent years, the potential of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) technologies in TMD diagnosis has become a growing area of interest.[18] Several studies have shown that AI algorithms can diagnose TMD with higher accuracy rates than traditional diagnostic methods and can be a valuable support tool for specialists by accelerating clinical processes.[18,21-24] For example, a meta-analysis highlights that integrating AI with clinical parameters can improve diagnostic accuracy.[24] Similarly, high accuracy rates were obtained using an artificial neural network-based model, proving the effectiveness of AI in TMD risk prediction.[22] In addition, a study using ML algorithms determined that the most critical parameters for TMD prediction were pain intensity, mouth opening, lateral excursion, and muscle tenderness. These findings demonstrate that ML models can facilitate diagnosis in clinical settings, particularly for inexperienced experts, and offer significant advantages over traditional methods.[18] These studies demonstrate that the role of AI in the healthcare sector is becoming increasingly critical and that AI and ML technologies not only increase diagnostic accuracy but also improve diagnostic processes through clinical decision support systems. They may be essential in diagnosing complex diseases such as TMD early.

The existing literature reveals that there is a limited number of studies examining the relationship between smartphone addiction and TMD,[6,9] and the mechanisms underlying this relationship (such as postural disorders and muscle sensitivity) are not yet fully understood. In particular, the indirect effects of FHP and cervical dysfunction caused by smartphone use on the masseter muscle and its contribution to TMD pathogenesis are still unclear. Moreover, traditional statistical methods may be insufficient in evaluating these complex relationships and offer limited insight in modeling nonlinear multivariate interactions.

In this context, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between smartphone addiction and TMD symptoms by assessing the contribution of clinical variables such as FHP, cervical dysfunction, and masseter muscle tenderness, and by analyzing the multivariate structure of these associations using ML methods. Additionally, it was hypothesized that ML algorithms would yield higher classification accuracy and predictive performance compared to traditional statistical methods in evaluating these multidimensional relationships.

2. Materials and methods

The study was conducted as a single-blind cross-sectional study. Approval for this study was granted by the Non-Interventional Ethics Committee of Bandirma Onyedi Eylül University Health Sciences (Approval number: 2024–27; Approval date: March 18, 2024). All procedures were conducted by the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, or with comparable ethical standards. Participants who volunteered for this study signed an informed consent form that included a description of the study’s aim and design. Participation in the study was voluntary, and no remuneration was paid for participation. Participants were assured that the data obtained would be used solely for research purposes. The clinical trial number of this study is NCT06818318. Additionally, this study has been reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines, which aim to improve the reporting quality of observational studies and have international validity. Following the headings in the STROBE checklist, the study design, sample selection, data collection process, variable definitions, statistical analysis methods, and presentation of findings have been systematically structured. This approach has strengthened the methodological transparency of the research and increased its scientific validity. Additionally, compliance with STROBE criteria facilitates comparability with similar observational studies and contributes to more reliable interpretation of the findings.

2.1. Participants

This study involved 110 university students from April 2024 to July 2024. The inclusion criteria encompassed Bandirma Onyedi Eylul University students, aged between 18 and 30 years, who were active smartphone users for at least 1 year. Active smartphone use was operationally defined within this study. Participants were considered active smartphone users if they reported using a smartphone for at least 30 min/d over the past year. This threshold was selected as a minimum exposure criterion to ensure consistent daily interaction, rather than to represent a validated cutoff. Active use was assessed through a direct self-report question (“How many minutes per day do you typically use your smartphone?”), and responses were used to confirm eligibility. The exclusion criteria were comprised individuals with a history of masseter Botox application, diagnosed/ being treated with TMD, and/or bruxism, as well as those presenting with musculoskeletal impairments associated with systemic or specific pathological conditions, including fractures of the hand, fingers, upper extremities, and/or temporomandibular joint (TMJ), or diagnosed with rheumatoid diseases. Additionally, individuals who had undergone surgical interventions related to hand, finger, upper extremity, and/or TMJ issues, those who had applied physiotherapy or rehabilitation services for these regions within the past 6 months, as well as individuals diagnosed with facial paralysis or psychiatric disorders, were excluded from the study. The sample size for this study was calculated using Pearson correlation analysis (ρ = 0.3, α = 0.05, 1 − β = 0.80) with GPower 3.1 software (Heinrich Heine, Dusseldrof University, Germany), resulting in a minimum sample size of 84 participants. To account for potential data losses and subgroup analyses, 110 participants were included in the study (Fig. 1).

2.2. Assessments

The participant information form assessed demographic data, while the Smartphone Addiction Scale-Short Version (SAS-SV) evaluated the risk of smartphone addiction. Temporomandibular disorders symptoms was determined using the Fonseca Anamnestic Index (FAI), and neck disability was measured using the Neck Disability Index (NDI). Temporomandibular joint pain was assessed using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), and FHP was evaluated with a goniometer. Additionally, the pressure pain threshold (PPT) of the masseter muscle was measured using an algometer.

2.2.1. Participant Information Form

The study data were collected by using “Participant Information Form,” including demographic information of the participants (age, height, weight, gender, smartphone usage time [SUT], masseter Botox application status, continuously used medications, TMD and/or bruxism diagnosis, musculoskeletal system problem with evidence of systemic, specific pathological condition such as fracture of hand, finger, upper extremity and/or TMJ, rheumatoid disease, hand, finger). The following were questioned with this 13-item form created by the researchers: status of any surgical operation related to upper extremity and/or TMJ problem, status of receiving physiotherapy and rehabilitation services related to hand, finger, upper extremity and/or TMJ in <6 months, status of facial paralysis, and status of having a diagnosed psychiatric disease.

2.2.2. Smartphone Addiction Scale-Short Version (SAS-SV)

The SAS-SV is a valid and reliable measurement tool for assessing individuals’ technology use habits. This short version, which preserves the psychometric properties of the original long form, is effective in measuring the main dimensions of smartphone addiction such as loss of control, impairment in social functioning and adverse effects on daily life. The SAS-SV, which is widely used in clinical research and field studies due to its practical applicability, differs from traditional scales in that it covers modern behaviors associated with digital addiction such as nomophobia, fear of missing out and overreporting control. In our study, this scale was used to assess the intensity and daily duration of smartphone use. Scores on the SAS-SV, a 10-item 6-point Likert-type scale, range from 10 to 60, with higher scores indicating an increased risk of addiction. Although the scale does not have a cutoff score in Turkey, it was determined as 31 for men and 33 for women in the Korean sample.[25] In the Turkish validity and reliability study conducted by Noyan et al, the Cronbach α coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.86.[26]

2.2.3. Fonseca Anamnestic Index (FAI)

In a review of the literature on the reliability of using questionnaires to assess the severity of TMD, researchers recommended the use of FAI due to its simplicity, rapid results, and cost-effectiveness.[27,28] This questionnaire consists of 10 items assessing the presence of pain in the TMJ, head, and neck joints, with response options categorized as “no,” “sometimes,” and “yes.” The questionnaire evaluates various parameters, including joint, head, and neck pain; pain experienced during masticatory activity; parafunctional habits; reduced joint mobility; occlusal impairments; and emotional stress. The scoring system classifies individuals into 4 categories: no TMD “0 to 15,” mild TMD “20 to 45,” moderate TMD “50 to 65,” and severe TMD “70 to 100.” The validity and reliability of the Turkish questionnaire were assessed by Kaynak et al in 2023.[29] The internal consistency analysis of the questionnaire resulted in a Cronbach α coefficient of 0.805. The intra-class correlation coefficient values for each index question ranged from 0.739 to 0.897. A high FAI accuracy level was determined (area under the receiver operating characteristic [ROC] curve 0.928). The results provided significant evidence that the FAI can be used as a screening tool for identifying TMD in the Turkish population.[29]

2.2.4. Neck Disability Index (NDI)

The NDI, whose validity and reliability were studied by Aslan et al, assessed disability caused by neck pain.[30] Developed by Vernon and Mior,[31] the NDI is a 10-item questionnaire with 4 items related to subjective symptoms (pain intensity, headache, concentration, sleep) and 6 items related to activities of daily living (self-care, lifting, reading, work, driving, and leisure activities). The researchers reported a score between 0 and 4 as no disability, between 5 and 14 as mild disability, between 15 and 24 as moderate disability, between 25 and 34 as severe disability, and above 34 as total disability.[32]

2.2.5. Numeric Rating Scale (NRS)

This scale is a widely used, simple, and reliable method for assessing pain intensity in clinical settings. It is particularly effective in evaluating the efficacy of both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions aimed at reducing pain.[33] Participants are instructed that a score of 10 represents the most severe pain they have experienced, while a score of 0 indicates no pain. They are then asked to report a numerical value between 0 and 10 corresponding to the pain intensity perceived in the TMJ region, which is subsequently recorded.

2.2.6. Forward head posture (FHP)

The craniovertebral angle was assessed by FHP using a goniometer. During the measurement, participants were seated comfortably with an upright back, lumbar support, feet flat on the floor, and knees at approximately 90° flexion. This angle was measured in degrees as the angle between the horizontal plane, the seventh cervical vertebra, and the ear hole.[34]

2.2.7. Pressure pain threshold (PPT)

The PPT of the masseter muscle on both the dominant and nondominant sides was evaluated before and after the activity using a manual algometer (2.5 kg–11 lb). Before the assessment, students received verbal instructions regarding the procedure. During the measurement, participants were seated on a height-adjustable stool with their trunks in an extended position, hips and knees flexed at 90°, feet placed flat on the floor, and hands resting on their thighs with elbows flexed. The probe of the algometer was positioned perpendicularly to the target muscle, and compression pressure was progressively increased at a rate of approximately 1 kg/s. Participants were instructed to indicate the moment the sensation of pressure transitioned into pain, at which point the compression was immediately terminated. A 30-second interval was observed between consecutive measurements, and the arithmetic mean of the recorded values was calculated.[35] For the masseter muscle, measurements were obtained at the midpoint of the muscle, specifically between its upper and lower borders and approximately 1 cm posterior to its anterior border.

2.3. Machine learning approaches

In the current study, the independent variables affecting SAS-SV results were estimated using different ML algorithms. First, a logistic regression (LR) model was applied to determine the linear predictors of SAV-SV.

2.3.1. Logistic regression (LR) analyses

In this method, different algorithms were tested within the scope of LR to predict smartphone addiction. The random forest (RF) and XGBoost models were the LR models used to determine this complex relationship. When evaluating the models, the regression coefficient (R2), mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean square error (RMSE) metrics were used. The independent variables of the model were masseter muscle PPT, NDI, FHP, TMJ pain, FAI, and phone usage time. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis was also performed to improve the explanation of the results. First, it was determined that there was no missing data in the study, and no action was taken for this. Standard Scaler was used to normalize the variables. This process was determined by dividing each value by the standard deviation.[36] In this study, RF, an ensemble learning method that generates ensemble predictions by creating multiple decision trees for the LR model, was employed by optimizing the number of trees (n_estimators) and maximum depth (max_depth) hyper parameters.[37] The gradient boosting model was favored as it generally improves the predictive power by reducing the error rate.[38] SHAP analysis was performed to visualize the contribution of each variable to the SAS-SV forecast.[39] For the LR analysis between FAI total scores and other dependent variables for TMD detection, RF, gradient boosting, ridge regression, which prevents overlearning of the model by reducing multicollinearity,[40] linear regression, which estimates the linear relationship between the dependent variable and the independent variable,[41] XGBoost, which is used for high accuracy and power,[38] and support vector regression (SVR) algorithms that minimize error tolerance and separate data points in the best way.[42]

In the study, a variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis was conducted to determine the potential effects of multicollinearity on the model. This analysis is an important step in ensuring the validity of regression models. As a result of the VIF analysis, it was observed that the VIF value did not exceed 5 for any of the independent variables used. In this context, SUT (VIF = 4.83), FAI (VIF = 4.86), NDI (VIF = 1.15), FHP (VIF = 1.98), Masseter (VIF = 3.84), PPT (VIF = 2.74), and TMJ (VIF = 3.50) variables were found not to create a significant multiple linear correlation problem in the model. These findings indicate that the variables used in multiple regression and classification analyses contribute independently from one another and that the results obtained are statistically reliable.

2.3.2. Classification ML algorithm analysis

Before building the classification prediction model of SAS-SV, the dataset was divided into 2 groups: 0 = phone addiction and 1 = no phone addiction, according to the criteria determined by Kwon et al.[25] The independent variables were the parameters used in LR (SUT, masseter muscle PPT, NDI, FHP, TMJ pain, and FAI). Linear regression to determine whether the dependent variable is dependent on any parameter,[43] RF, an ensemble learning method that makes predictions by creating multiple decision trees,[44] and support vector machines (SVM) to determine the best separation boundaries between data points,[45] K-nearest neighbors (KNN), a classification algorithm based on the similarity of data points,[46] gradient boosting, which uses sequential learning stages to reduce error rates and create a robust predictor,[47] and ensemble subspace discriminant, an ensemble learning model developed by combining multiple SVM models.[48]

Additionally, to ensure the independence of data between the model and test data and to analyze algorithmic structures, the data was divided into 80% training data and 20% test data. A 10-fold cross-validation analysis will be performed to better understand the model’s performance. The performance of the analyses performed in the model was calculated using a confusion matrix. This matrix presents the relationship between the actual boundaries and the predicted classes determined according to performance in a table with the number of predictions. True positive means the model correctly predicted the positive class, false positive means the model predicted the negative class as a positive class, false negative means the model incorrectly predicted a positive class as a negative class, and true negative means the model correctly predicted the negative class. Using these components, precision, accuracy, sensitivity, and F1 scores were calculated. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, area under the curve (AUC), and SHAP analysis were applied to determine the classification performance, and the accuracy model obtained in the confusion matrix was tested in this way. Indeed, the ROC curve shows the relationship between true positive and false positive of the model. The larger the area under the ROC curve (AUC), the better the model’s performance. In addition, SHAP analysis was used to evaluate the contribution of each parameter to the model’s performance.

2.4. Statistical analyses

In this study, R Studio was used for statistical analyses. Normality analysis was performed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and revealed that the data followed a normal distribution. Therefore, the mean and standard deviation values, as well as the minimum and maximum values, of the participants’ demographic information and data related to SAS-SV, FAI, NDI, FHP, masseter muscle PPT, and TMJ pain were provided. Additionally, Pearson correlation coefficient was used for the correlation analysis of SAS-SV, FAI, NDI, FHP, masseter muscle PPT, and TMJ pain. The level of significance in the study was set at 0.05.

Additionally, when considering MAE and RMSE values, the error rates are found to be at a minimum level in the RF model.

4. Discussion

This study aimed to understand the relationship between smartphone addiction and TMD among university students and to analyze the independent variables affecting these parameters. The findings of this study suggest that there are significant relationships between smartphone addiction and TMD through postural and neuromuscular factors. In particular, significant associations between levels of smartphone use and FHP, cervical dysfunction and masseter muscle tenderness suggest that behavioral patterns accompanying digital device use may be associated with specific postural and functional changes in the musculoskeletal system. These findings indicate that the relationship between smartphone addiction and TMD symptoms can be assessed not only psychosocially but also in combination with postural and neuromuscular parameters. Machine learning-based analyses, which extend beyond traditional statistical methods, have enabled a more comprehensive evaluation of nonlinear relationships between multiple variables. Analyses performed using these methods revealed that indirect markers, such as FHP and masseter muscle tenderness, were significantly associated with TMD, making the importance of these parameters more evident. The data obtained emphasize the importance of considering not only direct effects but also indirect relationships in the evaluation of TMD and suggest that ML-based methods can contribute to the understanding of such multivariate structures.

Temporomandibular disorders is a significant health problem that requires accurate diagnosis and treatment due to its complex etiology and various symptoms. Artificial intelligence and ML technologies have shown potential in identifying nonlinear relationships between clinical parameters and enhancing diagnostic accuracy in TMD.[21,22]. Deep learning models have demonstrated up to 92% accuracy in classifying TMD, highlighting the predictive role of factors such as pain intensity, mouth opening range, and muscle tenderness.[18] By quantitatively assessing the relative importance of clinical variables, AI techniques facilitate the exploration of intermediary mechanisms between smartphone use and TMD. The findings of our study support the existing literature. In particular, postural changes (FHP) associated with smartphone addiction and functional adaptations in the masseter muscle were identified as variables with high predictive power for TMD development in our ML models. Interestingly, contrary to traditional correlation analyses, SHAP values revealed that the chain of postural effects starting with FHP and extending to TMD through masseter hyperactivity was more determinant than the duration of phone use.

It is thought that TMD may also be seen among musculoskeletal system problems seen in the frequent use of smartphones.[6,9] In a study investigating the relationship between smartphone use and TMD severity, a moderate correlation was found between smartphone addiction and TMD, and it was determined that the risk of pain-related TMD was 1.77 times higher in intensive phone use. This was attributed to biomechanical factors such as increased neck flexion and FHP, increased neck disability, and psychological factors, depending on the assessment parameters used in the study.[9] In our research, it was observed that the prevalence of TMD increased as the duration of smartphone use and addiction level increased. However, when the SHAP analysis results were analyzed, it was found that the strongest predictors of TMD were TMJ pain, masseter muscle tenderness and FHP, respectively. Interestingly, it was observed that the duration of smartphone use had a low direct determinant effect on TMD. This finding suggests that SUT indirectly increases the risk of TMD, primarily through moderators such as masseter muscle tenderness, FHP and NDI. Indeed, in the first stage of the SHAP analysis, it was determined that the factors most strongly associated with smartphone addiction were SUT, masseter muscle tenderness and TMD score, followed by NDI and FHP. These results suggest that the effect of smartphone use on TMD is not direct but indirect through other clinical parameters.

Forward head posture is the most common cervical postural error in the sagittal plane and is directly related to modern lifestyles and especially prolonged smartphone use.[19,49,50] Studies show that intensive smartphone use causes the FHP, increasing the cervical spine load.[20,51] Furthermore, FHP is considered a predisposing factor for developing mechanical neck pain.[52] Smartphone addiction is particularly prevalent among young adults and university students and significantly increases the prevalence of neck pain.[53,54] Khan et al reported that 34.2% of university students experienced neck pain due to smartphone use.[55] These findings reveal that long-term smartphone use directly relates to the duration and severity of neck disability.[7] Our study observed that as the SUT and smartphone addiction level increased, FHP and neck disability also increased. These findings, in line with the literature, suggest that phone use significantly affects cervical posture and increases the risk of neck disability.[20,50]

Temporomandibular disorders is a multifactorial condition that affects the masticatory system and postural balance.[56] The literature has reported that individuals with TMD develop FHP. This postural disorder becomes more pronounced as the severity of TMD increases.[57,58] It has been determined that there is a mutual interaction between FHP and TMD, and FHP may exacerbate TMD symptoms and lead to abnormalities in the head and neck region.[59] In addition, it has been reported that the prevalence of neck pain is higher in individuals with TMD, and this may increase both postural disorders and TMD symptoms.[60,61] In our study, it was observed that the use of smartphones increased FHP and neck disability, which triggered the formation of TMD. It is known that prolonged head tilt during smartphone use causes tension in the neck muscles and postural disorders. This may exacerbate the already existing FHP and neck disability in individuals with TMD, leading to an increase in TMD symptoms. Therefore, the effect of smartphone use on TMD is not direct but indirect through intermediate variables such as FHP and neck disability. These findings suggest that smartphone use is essential in the relationship between postural disorders and TMD.

The masseter muscle is an essential masticatory muscle that generates the most significant force during mastication and has the most critical moment arm among the mandibular elevator muscles.[62] It is also the most common site of TMD-related pain and tenderness.[63] In our study, masseter muscle tenderness was the most crucial covariate that interacted with smartphone addiction and TMD at a similar level. This finding suggests that smartphone use may be a risk factor for TMD, primarily by increasing masseter muscle tenderness. This may be caused by the head posture affected by smartphone use.

The literature has reported that poor head and neck posture can negatively affect masticatory muscles and related structures.[64,65] Different cervical positions affect mandibular position and masticatory muscle function.[66] In the FHP, the activity of the nuchal muscles increases, and these muscles shorten in the long term; simultaneously, the anteroinferior mandibular muscles undergo elongation due to retractile force.[67] This may negatively affect the functional capacity of the masseter muscle, leading to increased muscle activity.[68] Tavangar et al showed that the masseter muscle was thicker in people with severe FHP, which may be associated with increased muscle activity.[69] Georgiakaki et al stated that increased electrical muscle activity increases muscle thickness.[70] Gadotti et al showed a significant increase in masseter muscle activity in the FHP condition.[71] Since it is known that FHP is directly related to prolonged smartphone use,[19,49,50] we may think that prolonged head tilt during smartphone use may cause the masseter muscle to show more activation and increased tenderness by disrupting the length-tension relationship.

Additionally, studies have shown that smartphone use may disrupt sleep physiology and increase the risk of TMD by triggering bruxism attacks during sleep.[72,73] While it is thought that smartphone use may be associated with TMD, the involvement of the masseter muscle during smartphone use has been examined by only 1 author in the literature in 2 studies, one of which was a pilot study.[74,75] The first pilot study observed that smartphone use decreased the median frequency in the masticatory and right upper trapezius muscles.[74] Six healthy female subjects were included in the study, and it was determined that fatigue developed in all masticatory muscles and that fatigue occurred in the right upper trapezius muscles as a result of the bite test after 30 minutes of smartphone use. In 2021, the same author evaluated the prevalence of smartphone addiction in 20 university students and its correlation with head and neck pain symptoms and chewing and trapezius muscle activity during rest before and after smartphone use. As a result of the study observed a statistically insignificant increase in root mean square values in the right and left masseter muscles and left trapezius muscles after smartphone use. Only in the right upper trapezius muscles was a significant change in muscle activity before and after 30 minutes of smartphone use.[75] To our knowledge, other studies have not replicated these 2 related studies. In conclusion, it is thought that smartphone use may be a risk factor for TMD by increasing masseter muscle tenderness. This is related to the adverse effects of postural disorders such as FHP and neck disability on the functional capacity of the masseter muscle. However, more long-term studies with larger samples are needed to understand this relationship better.

In this study, the primary relationships and predictive factors were determined using ML algorithms rather than traditional regression. XGBoost was particularly effective in capturing the complex, potentially nonlinear relationships between smartphone addiction, TMD severity, posture measurements, and masseter muscle sensitivity, while SVM provided the best classification performance for distinguishing participants based on smartphone addiction/TMD status. Our findings are consistent with previous ML-based TMD studies showing that clinical and functional parameters can be used to create highly accurate prediction models, although specific algorithms and feature sets may differ. Yildiz et al reported that a bagging algorithm using Mutivariate Adaptive Regression Splines excellently distinguished TMD patients based on pain intensity, maximum mouth opening, lateral excursion, and PPT of masticatory muscles, emphasizing classical clinical examination variables.[18] Similarly, Taborri et al demonstrated that a K-nearest neighbor classifier fed with baropodometric and cervical mobility data obtained from low-cost sensors could accurately identify TMD, highlighting the diagnostic value of objective posture and movement-related measurements.[76] At a broader population level, Lee et al used RF models to demonstrate that anthropometric, psychosocial, and socioeconomic factors such as BMI, stress, sleep, and working conditions are the primary determinants of TMD risk,[77] while Cui et al developed an interpretable RF model supported by SHAP-based explanations, revealing malocclusion, chewing habits, bruxism, and anxiety as key determinants.[78] Expanding on this literature, our use of XGBoost, SVM, and SHAP in our young adult university sample demonstrated that TMD symptoms associated with smartphone addiction can be modeled with similar or higher accuracy when head posture, cervical disability, and masseter PPT are evaluated together. Furthermore, smartphone usage’s effect on TMD can be modeled with similar or higher accuracy when postural changes and muscle changes are evaluated together. Head-neck posture, cervical disability, and masseter PPT can be modeled with similar or higher accuracy when evaluated together, and that smartphone usage may function as a distal behavioral factor mediated by postural changes and muscle hypersensitivity in its effect on TMD.

In this study, the relationships between smartphone use, FHP, neck disability, and TMD, particularly the potential role of masseter muscle tenderness in these relationships, were comprehensively examined. One of the strengths of the study is its multidisciplinary approach that brings together different disciplines such as smartphone addiction, postural disorders and TMD. Additionally, the use of ML algorithms enabled an in-depth assessment of complex and indirect relationships that are difficult to detect using traditional statistical analyses. With advanced methods such as SHAP analysis, it has been demonstrated that masseter muscle sensitivity emerges as a central variable in the indirect relationships between smartphone use and TMD. This study fills an essential gap in the literature by addressing the relationships between digital technology use and musculoskeletal health at both theoretical and applied levels. The findings reveal that smartphone addiction, which is becoming increasingly common in the digital age, should be considered not only at the psychosocial but also at the postural and neuromuscular level. Another significant contribution of the study is that it emphasizes the importance of considering indirect markers, such as postural disturbances and masseter muscle sensitivity, in the development of new clinical strategies for the early diagnosis and treatment of TMD. In this respect, the study makes a theoretical contribution to the literature and suggests a practical and innovative framework for clinical applications.

In this study, approximately two-thirds of participants (65.5%) met the SAS-SV cutoff for smartphone addiction, resulting in a substantially uneven distribution between addicted and nonaddicted individuals. Given this imbalance, conducting categorical group comparisons would have produced limited statistical sensitivity and inflated the risk of biased effect estimates. For this reason, smartphone addiction was treated as a continuous construct, and both SAS-SV scores and daily SUT were included in the analyses. This approach preserved the full variability of the data, enhanced statistical power, and allowed the multivariate and ML models to capture the graded expression of problematic SU rather than forcing an artificial dichotomy.

However, the study has some limitations. First, while the standardization of data collection processes was ensured by selecting a sample from a single university and ensuring homogeneity in the participants’ sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics, this limits the generalizability of the findings to all university students in Turkey. A significant limitation of this study is that the diagnostic criteria for TMD (DC/TMD), which standardize clinical diagnostic criteria for TMD evaluation and include physical examination findings, were not utilized; instead, only a screening questionnaire, such as the FAI, was employed. Although the FAI has been recognized as a valid and reliable tool for screening TMD symptoms,[27] caution should be exercised in interpreting the findings due to the lack of clinical examination. However, since the primary aim of this study was to examine the relationship between smartphone addiction and TMD symptoms rather than to diagnose TMD, the use of the FAI for epidemiologic screening purposes was deemed appropriate, used to screen for early risk factors instead of clinical diagnostic criteria. It allowed us to examine the early stages of the smartphone addiction–TMD relationship. In future studies, integration of DC/TMD criteria and clinical examination data is recommended. Another significant limitation of this study is that the participants were university students between the ages of 18 and 30. This limits the generalizability of the findings to the general population. However, the focus of this study was to preliminarily assess risk factors in the young adult population where technology use is intense. Future research is recommended to confirm this relationship using samples that include more heterogeneous age groups and diverse sociodemographic characteristics. Another, the scope of the study was limited by the fact that other factors that may affect TMD symptoms and smartphone addiction, such as stress and sleep quality were not controlled. In addition, the use of TMJ pain as both a diagnostic criterion and a predictor in our study represents a potential methodological limitation. Although this is consistent with biological feedback mechanisms in TMJ pathophysiology, it is recommended that future studies investigate these interactions with dynamic systems models. Since the main aim of our study was not to develop a definitive diagnostic tool but to explore the associative patterns between smartphone usage behaviors and TMD symptoms, smartphone addiction was assessed with the SAS-SV questionnaire. However, collecting data such as duration of smartphone usage and level of addiction through self-report methods may cause limitations such as recall bias or social desirability bias. The sample size and diversity may be limited for ML analyses and may affect the generalizability of the results. For future studies, it is recommended to use larger and demographically diverse samples, conduct longitudinal studies, and collect data such as smartphone usage with objective methods (e.g., smartphone applications and screen usage time). In addition, the performance and generalizability of the model can be improved by using different ML algorithms and larger data sets. Finally, the cumulative effect of digital device use cannot be fully differentiated. More controlled studies are needed to assess the specific role of tablets and computers in TMD development, which may contribute to postural disturbances. However, given the unique characteristics of smartphone use (such as more frequent head flexion and single-handed use); our findings are considered particularly applicable to this group of devices. In clinical applications, new protocols can be developed to evaluate the effects of smartphone use and postural disorders on TMD, and preventive strategies such as ergonomics training and postural correction exercises can be applied. This study fills an essential gap in the literature and maybe a guide for future research.

5. Conclusions

This study examined the complex relationship between smartphone addiction and TMD symptoms in university students using ML approaches in a multidimensional manner and demonstrated that masseter muscle sensitivity plays a critical role in the process. The findings indicate that the relationship between the level of addiction, represented by smart SAS-SV, and TMD is not direct; rather, it is shaped indirectly through mediating mechanisms such as FHP and neck disability. Explainable ML analyses (particularly SHAP) highlighted masseter muscle sensitivity as the most influential covariate in the addiction–TMD relationship; this suggests that smartphone use may increase the risk of TMD by exacerbating postural deterioration and muscle sensitivity.

A clear distinction in performance was observed among the ML models developed for the study’s objectives: the XGBoost model provided the highest accuracy in predicting SAS-SV total scores, best meeting the target of continuous score prediction. In contrast, the SVM model demonstrated superior performance in classifying individuals with smartphone addiction and emerged as a robust method for SAS-based classification. These results indicate that ML-based approaches have high potential to support clinical decision-making processes in both the quantitative estimation of addiction severity and the classification of addiction status; they can be used, in particular, for the early detection of at risk individuals and the planning of targeted protective strategies. However, findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the relatively limited sample size and the use of numerous predictor variables; although cross-validation and regularization methods were applied, the risk of overfitting cannot be entirely ruled out. Future studies should test these relationships with larger samples, preferably using longitudinal designs and external validation sets; it is also recommended to evaluate the effect of interventions targeting posture, neck function, and masticatory muscle sensitivity on TMD prevention/management.
