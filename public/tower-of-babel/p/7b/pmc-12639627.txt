Effects of pollination cultivars on the characteristic volatile organic compounds in pears using E-nose, HS-SPME-GC–MS, and HS-GC-IMS
Authors: Fengli Zhou, Azheng Wang, Xue Wang, Congcong Wang, Jing Zhao, Wenjing Cao, Rui Zhai, Chengquan Yang, Zhigang Wang, Lingfei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Xenia effect significantly influences fruit quality, with underexplored impacts on pear aroma. This study systematically evaluated eight cultivars' effects on ‘Starkrimson’ pears using electronic nose (E-nose), headspace-gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS), and headspace solid-phase microextraction gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS). Pollination with ‘Zaosu’ (ZS) pear maximized volatile organic compound (VOC) concentrations (terpenoids, esters, aldehydes) and achieved optimal solid-acid ratio (291.78), enhancing fruity, green, and sweet aromas. Conversely, ‘Huagai’ (HG) pear pollination reduced VOC accumulation and fruit quality. Other cultivars showed intermediate effects on total soluble solids (TSS), titratable acidity (TA), and aroma. GC–MS identified 14 key aroma-active compounds, while GC-IMS detected four critical small-molecule VOCs, demonstrating methodological complementarity. Xenia-driven pollen-parent effects regulated VOC biosynthesis through lipoxygenase (LOX) and mevalonate (MVA) pathways, enhancing aroma complexity. ZS pollination optimized ‘Starkrimson’ market value and consumer preference, providing actionable strategies for horticultural optimization through cultivar selection.

Introduction

Xenia effect, also known as the xenia or metaxenia, refers to the variation in the size, form, color, maturity, and composition of seeds or fruit with pollen from different parents. Given that the xenia effect directly alters seed and fruit traits, it is consequently a significant factor influencing fruit quality. These influences manifest in multiple aspects. This effect can regulate physical properties such as fruit set rate and fruit size, as evidenced by studies on kiwifruit, strawberries, plums, and blueberries (Chai et al., 2023; Dung et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Tomic et al., 2025). Furthermore, its impact is critical in shaping complex sensory traits, including the aroma quality in citrus and key flavor-related parameters in apples, plums, blueberries, and kiwifruits (Chai et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Tomic et al., 2025; Zhang, Liu, et al., 2019; Zhang, Wang, et al., 2019). Pear as a typical gametophytic self-incompatible species, requires the configuration of pollinizer trees in cultivation, which makes its fruit quality highly susceptible to the influence of pollen sources (Wu et al., 2018). This study used the European pear ‘Starkrimson’ (Pyrus communis ‘Starkrimson’) as the material, and this cultivar also shows obvious pollen sensitivity in production. However, there is currently a lack of systematic research on how different pollination treatments specifically regulate the formation of its postharvest quality and metabolic dynamics. Therefore, exploring the specific role of xenia in the formation of pear fruit quality is of great significance for the development of precise pollination strategies and fruit quality improvement.

As a crucial sensory attribute of fruit quality, aroma is composed of acids, alcohols, aldehydes, esters, and hydrocarbons (Chen et al., 2018). Different volatile compounds possess distinct odors, and the specific combination, concentrations, and proportions of these compounds lead to the unique aroma profiles of various fruits (Aragüez & Valpuesta, 2013). The concentrations and compositions of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are mainly determined by genetic variability (Chai et al., 2012). During the production process, VOCs can also be influenced by factors including cultivation practices, climatic conditions, and pollen (Liu et al., 2023). Pollen affects not only the appearance and intrinsic quality of fruit but also its aroma (Liu et al., 2023). For example, the number of esters greatly increased in fruit that was artificially pollinated with quince pollen from ‘Yan 3 Fuji’, ‘Yin Duo’, and ‘Liang ye Ji Xue’ apples (Zhang, Wang, et al., 2019). Citrus mangshanensis pollination has an inhibitory effect on pomelo (Citrus grandis) aroma quality, which demonstrates a pollen-sensing effect (Zhang, Liu, et al., 2019).

The synthesis of VOCs relies on specific metabolic pathways. Based on differences in biosynthetic precursors, the formation mechanisms of fruit aroma mainly include the LOX pathway, amino acid metabolic pathway, terpenoid synthesis pathway, and carbohydrate metabolic pathway (Baldwin et al., 2000; Defilippi et al., 2009). The aroma components of pears are complex and diverse, with different cultivars exhibiting unique aroma characteristics (Wang et al., 2023). Existing studies have shown that volatile aroma substances in pears mainly originate from the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway within the LOX pathway (Luo et al., 2021; Zang et al., 2024), which is a key process for the synthesis of C6 and C9 volatile flavor substances in plants (Defilippi et al., 2009). In addition, other metabolic pathways also participate in aroma formation; for example, farnesene derived from the mevalonic acid (MVA) pathway further enriches the sweet aroma notes of pears (López-Nicolás et al., 2009). These unique aroma characteristics are mainly determined by volatile compounds and their odor activity values (OAVs). OAV is defined as the ratio of a compound's concentration to its olfactory threshold (Yan et al., 2018), and compounds with an OAV ≥ 1 typically make important contributions to fruit flavor. Currently, systematic research has been conducted on the molecular mechanisms underlying pear fruit aroma formation (Luo et al., 2021; Zang et al., 2024). However, the mechanisms by which different pollination cultivars affect the aroma composition of pear fruits and related metabolic pathways remain unclear.

In previous studies, VOCs have been identified and quantified using electronic nose (E-nose) analysis, headspace solid-phase microextraction gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS), and headspace-gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS). E-nose analysis is an inexpensive, simple, and real-time method that does not require pre-treatment (Wilson, 2018). However, it is limited by the membrane materials used, immature manufacturing techniques, and data processing methods (Peris & Escuder-Gilabert, 2009). In contrast to E-nose analysis, the use of GC–MS and GC-IMS is expensive, complicated, time-consuming, and requires trained personnel. GC–MS serves as an efficient technique for the identification and quantification of VOCs. It is usually combined with HS-SPME and often requires complex pretreatment before analysis (Dong et al., 2019). Nevertheless, GC–MS cannot always distinguish between isotopes and isomers in the analytes (Hernández-Mesa et al., 2019). GC-IMS is an emerging technique for visualization analysis of VOCs. It has the advantage of not requiring pretreatment and high sensitivity, enabling it to be used for rapid characterization of changes in VOCs (Wang et al., 2020). Still, GC-IMS mainly detects small-molecule and low-abundance volatile components. The use of multiple detection methods for VOCs helps to distinguish key aromatic compounds.

Despite significant advances in understanding the molecular mechanisms of aroma formation in pears, the impacts of different pollinizing cultivars on volatile compound biosynthesis and metabolic pathways remain unclear. Specifically, there is a lack of systematic conclusions regarding how different pollinizing cultivars influence the biosynthesis of aroma-active compounds through the LOX and MVA pathways at both molecular mechanism and horticultural practice levels. In this study, by integrating three complementary approaches—E-nose, GC–MS, and GC-IMS, we systematically characterized the dynamic profiles of aroma components and key metabolic pathways in ‘Starkrimson’ pears in response to pollination by different cultivars for the first time. This research finding not only uncovers novel aspects of the biological mechanisms underlying fruit aroma formation but also constructs a comprehensive framework. This framework serves as a basis for the rational selection of pollinizer cultivars, the strategic improvement of fruit flavor profiles, and the creation of high-value-added products within the pear cultivation industry.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

During the bud stage, flowers from seven different pear cultivars were gathered. The anthers were carefully removed using tweezers, labeled, evenly distributed in a single layer on a stacked carton, and subjected to a gradual baking process under a lamp. After the pollen burst, the anther color changed to yellow. The anthers were then crushed, placed in 5-mL centrifuge tubes, and stored at −20 °C until needed.

‘Starkrimson’ pear trees (Pyrus communis L.) were planted using Cydonia oblonga as rootstocks in 2019, within an orchard at the Horticultural Farm of Northwest A&F University, Yangling District, Xianyang City, Shaanxi Province (34°17′46”N, 108°04′17″E, altitude 486 m). The trees were arranged in a north-south row orientation with a row spacing of 1 m × 4 m, and they were used as female parents. Pears were collected on July 6, 2022 (105 d after full bloom), which was when they reached their optimum ripeness for commercial use (average flesh firmness: 60 N). ‘Starkrimson’ pears that were openly pollinated under natural conditions (without artificial pollination) served as the control (CK). Fruits were collected from seven different cultivars and CK. The seven cultivars used ‘Korla Fragrant’ (KF, Pyrus sinkiangensis Yu.), ‘Dangshansuli’ (DSS, Pyrus bretschneideri Rehd.), ‘Zaosu’ (ZS, Pyrus bretschneideri Rehd.), ‘Huagai’ (HG, Pyrus ussuriensis Maxim.), ‘Sucui No. 1′ (SC, Pyrus pyrifolia Nakai.), ‘Huangguan’ (HGG, Pyrus bretschneideri Rehd.), and ‘Xiyanghong’ (XYH, Pyrus bretschneideri × Pyrus communis). Per treatment, 180 blemish-free and uniform-sized pears were chosen and split into three groups (60 pears per group). From each set, 30 pears were used to assess the fruit quality and 30 pears were used to analyze the aroma. Equatorial peel samples were collected from the pears, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground into powder, and stored at −80 °C.

E-nose analysis

The aroma characteristics of the samples from the seven cultivars and the CK were assessed using an E-nose (PEN3, Airsense, Schwerin, Germany) (Yang et al., 2024). The sample (1 g) was transferred to a 20-mL headspace vial and placed into a water bath maintained at 25 °C. After a 30-min equilibration period, the sample was subjected to analysis. Table S1 lists all of the sensors that are utilized by the E-nose. The sample flow rate was 400 mL/min, the injection flow rate was 10 mL/min, the injection time was 5 s, and the cleaning time was 300 s. The detection time was 60 s. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was conducted via the E-nose software.

GC–MS analysis

The samples were analyzed using an established method with some modifications (Zhang et al., 2010). The VOCs present in the pear peel were analyzed using SPME and GC–MS (ISQ & TRACE GC Ultra, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Next, 1 mL of saturated sodium chloride (NaCl) solution was added to a 20 mL headspace vial, and 2 μL of 3-nonanone (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) with a concentration of 0.04 g·L−1 was added as the internal standard. The addition of NaCl aims to utilize the “salting-out effect” to reduce the solubility of polar VOCs in the aqueous phase. This promotes the distribution of polar VOCs into the headspace, thereby improving the sensitivity of the method. The internal standard is mainly added for subsequent quantitative calculations. It is used to correct fluctuations in instrument response and injection errors during the analysis process. Immediately seal the bottle mouth with a silicone septum lined with tin foil to ensure the airtightness of the headspace system.

Place the sealed headspace vial on a constant-temperature stirring heater set at 50 °C. First, perform a 10-min headspace equilibration, maintaining magnetic stirring at 200 rpm during the equilibration period. This equilibration step aims to achieve the partition equilibrium of VOCs among the sample matrix, headspace, and fiber coating. Meanwhile, stirring helps accelerate this equilibration process and improve reproducibility. Subsequently, insert the aged DVB/CAR/PDMS SPME fiber (2 cm, 50/30 μm; Supelco) through the vial cap septum. Expose and extract it in the headspace for 40 min under the same conditions of 50 °C and 200 rpm stirring. The DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber is selected for its excellent adsorption performance toward a broad range of volatile and semi-volatile compounds. The extraction temperature, time, and stirring conditions are determined based on previous reports and preliminary optimization experiments (Luo et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2015). These parameters aim to ensure high extraction efficiency and reproducibility by enhancing mass transfer, while avoiding thermal degradation caused by excessively high temperatures. After the extraction is completed, retract the fiber immediately and quickly insert it into the GC injection port. Desorb it at 250 °C for 5 min, and start the GC–MS data acquisition program simultaneously.

To analyze and isolate the extracted VOCs, GC–MS was performed using a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm, Restek, Bellefonte, PA, USA). The carrier gas is helium flowing at a rate of 1.0 mL/min. The inlet temperature was 250 °C, and the column temperature program was as follows: 40 °C for 3 min; increase to 150 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min; the temperature was increased to 220 °C at 10 °C/min for 5 min. The following conditions for MS are ionization mode, electron shock in 70 eV; transmission line temperature at 240 °C; and the ion source temperature at 240 °C. The range for the full scan mode is configured to be 35–600 m/z. The compounds were identified and matched with the MS NIST17 library (NIST17, National Institute of Standards and Technology, Gaithersburg, MD, U.S.A.). The concentrations of VOCs were calculated using 3-nonanone as the internal standard.

GC-IMS analysis

GC-IMS (FlavourSpec®, G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany) was used to assess the VOCs in the pear samples. A modified approach based on a previously described method was employed (Peng et al., 2024). The pear peel sample (1 g) was placed in a 20-mL headspace bottle and incubated for 15 min at 40 °C, 500 μL of gas was extracted and splitless injected into the injection port at 85 °C. The gas in the headspace was isolated using an ion mobility spectrometry (IMS) column (MXT-5 capillary column, 15 m × 0.53 mm) maintained at 45 °C. Nitrogen (99.999 %) was used as the carrier gas and drift gas. The carrier gas flow rate was 2 mL/min for 2 min, increased to 10 mL/min, and then increased to 100 mL/min after >10 min. The flow rate of the drift gas was 75 mL/min. The n-ketones C4-C9 (FlavourSpec®, G.A.S.) were used to calculate the retention indexes of the VOCs. The retention indexes of the standard compounds in the GC-IMS library (G.A.S.) and the experimentally calculated retention time were used to distinguish the VOCs. The normalized ion mobility under standard temperature and pressure conditions (KO) was also used to distinguish compounds and was calculated using an established method (Zhang et al., 2020). The VOCs were quantified using their peak intensities in GC-IMS (Table S2).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 and graphed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Data were analyzed by independent one-way analysis of variance, with P < 0.05 as the significance level. Radar plots for the E-nose were calculated using OriginPro 2021. The GC-IMS Library Search, which is endowed with the GC-IMS instrument (G.A.S.), was employed to analyze “fingerprint” gallery plots. TBtools was employed to generate and analyze heat maps (Chen, Chen, et al., 2020). Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was employed to assess the similarities and differences among samples, utilizing the SIMCA14.1 software (Sweden).

Results and discussion

E-nose analysis of VOCs in pears from different pollination treatments

Firstly, the aroma components of 8 samples of pear fruit were analyzed by E-nose. In this study, following previous approaches, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was applied to discriminate samples from seven cultivars and the CK group, with the goal of maximizing inter-class variance and minimizing intra-class variance (Gerhardt et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2021). LD1 accounted for 67.75 % of the variance and LD2 accounted for 24.62 % of the variance (Fig. 1A). The distance between the samples from the different cultivars was relatively large, particularly between the samples of HGG pear and CK and those of the other six cultivars. These results showed that HGG pear and CK exhibited significant dissimilarities compared to the remaining samples. VOCs existed among different cultivars, primarily manifested in sensors W1W, W1S, and W2S. At certain measurement points, the response values of sensors W5S and W2W essentially approached each other, which indicated that their overall VOCs were similar. The response values of the other sensors were relatively low, which suggested that the corresponding VOCs in the pears were also low (Fig. 1C). Loading analysis provides insight into the extent to which various sensors contribute to distinguishing VOCs among different samples. This may be determined by examining the position of the sensor in the figure. The results showed that the first primary component had a contribution of 91.05 %, while the second primary component had a contribution of 4.99 % (Fig. 1B). The W1W, W1S, and W2S sensors responded more strongly to VOCs, which showed that organic sulfides, compounds with methyl groups, and alcohols contributed to the aroma of the ‘Starkrimson’ pears. These results suggest that ‘Starkrimson’ pears may contain more organic sulfides, compounds with methyl groups, aldehydes and ketones. Pears contain many other fragrant compounds. However, the E-nose cannot sensitively detect every aroma component in the pears. Therefore, only some of the olfactory characteristics can be accurately defined using an E-nose (Liu et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022; Zhang, Liu, et al., 2019; Zhang, Wang, et al., 2019)

GC–MS analysis of VOCs in pears from different pollination treatments

Identification of VOCs by GC–MS

E-nose had a limited ability to detect VOCs in the pears, so GC–MS was used to further analyze the VOCs in different samples. The results showed that xenia affected the quantity of VOCs in the ‘Starkrimson’ pears. In the samples from the various cultivars, a total of 91 VOCs were found. These compounds were in seven classes as follows: esters (n = 35), aldehydes (n = 12), alcohols (n = 15), ketones (n = 7), terpenes (n = 17), alkanes (n = 3), and other compounds (n = 2) (Fig. 2B). The VOC profile was consistently dominated by terpenes, esters, and aldehydes (Fig. 2A); among these, terpenes are characteristic products of the MVA pathway, while esters and aldehydes are typical of the LOX pathway. Notably, these dominant aroma classes are biosynthesized through two core metabolic pathways: fatty acids serve as the main precursors for aldehydes and esters, while the MVA pathway leads to terpene synthesis.Fig. 2Contents of classified volatile compounds (A) and heat map clustering of volatile compounds (B) of pear with different treatments by GC–MS. Each number in Fig. 2B corresponds to a substance, with names provided in Table S3.Fig. 2

Contents of classified volatile compounds (A) and heat map clustering of volatile compounds (B) of pear with different treatments by GC–MS. Each number in Fig. 2B corresponds to a substance, with names provided in Table S3.

In total, 60, 69, 66, 66, 58, 66, 65, and 74 VOCs were identified in the CK, KF, DSS, ZS, HG, SC, HGG, and XYH samples, respectively. These results indicated that GC–MS was capable of detecting diverse VOCs, covering esters, aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, alkanes, and terpenes. Thus, GC–MS is able to offer comprehensive insights for the analysis of VOCs (Fig. 2A). The different cultivars affected the concentrations of VOCs in the ‘Starkrimson’ pears. The VOC profile was consistently dominated by terpenes, esters, and aldehydes (Fig. 2A), which are the characteristic aromatic products of the MVA and LOX pathways, respectively. This finding is consistent with earlier research identifying esters, aldehydes, and terpenes as characteristic aromatic components in pear cultivars (Chen et al., 2006). We also found that except for the pears pollinated by HG pear, the total VOC concentrations in the pears pollinated by other cultivars were higher than those in the CK group. Among them, the pears pollinated by ZS pear had the highest VOC concentration. Following pollination with HG pear pollen, a notable reduction occurred in the total concentration of VOCs in the pears (Fig. 2A). Among the VOCs in the seven pollination treatments (excluding the CK), terpenes, esters, and aldehydes had the highest concentrations, and the concentrations of the other four VOCs (alcohols, alkanes, ketones and others) were low (Fig. 2A). Among these compounds, terpenes had the highest concentrations, followed by esters, and then aldehydes.

Interestingly, the concentrations of terpenoids, which mainly include α-farnesene (Fig. 2B and Table S3), esters, and aldehydes in the ZS sample were higher than the concentrations in other samples (Fig. 2A). This coordinated enhancement of compounds from distinct biosynthetic pathways is critically important: α-farnesene is a sesquiterpene primarily produced via the MVA pathway, whereas aldehydes and esters are mainly synthesized through the LOX pathway. The concentrations of terpenes, esters, and aldehydes in the CK and HG samples were lower than those in the other samples (Fig. 2A). This stark contrast suggests that ZS pollination actively upregulates both the LOX and MVA pathways, while HG pollination has a minimal or suppressive effect.

In conclusion, xenia influences the concentration of VOCs in pears by affecting the synthesis of aroma compounds via the coordinated regulation of the LOX pathway and MVA pathway.

Multivariate statistical analysis

To effectively distinguish different groups and identify important variables that cause differences between groups, we analyzed the data using OPLS-DA. We analyzed the data by examining the score plot to identify the distinctions and similarities among the samples. Samples from the seven cultivars were significantly different from the CK samples. Among the cultivars, the DSS and HG samples were significantly different, while the KF, ZS, SC, and HGG samples were clustered together (Fig. 3A). The fitted parameters of the partial least squares regression model (R2Y = 0.958, Q2 = 0.884) had excellent explanatory power and strong predictive ability. 200 permutation tests were performed to assess the model's viability (Fig. 3B). The OPLS-DA score plot revealed clear separations among the pollination treatments (Fig. 3A). Most notably, the CK group was distinctly separated from the cultivar-pollinated groups along the first component, underscoring a fundamental impact of xenia on the overall VOC profile. Among the cultivars, the DSS and HG samples were positioned distantly from others, indicating their unique and divergent effects on aroma composition. Conversely, the close clustering of KF, ZS, SC, and HGG samples suggests that these four cultivars imposed a somewhat similar influence on the VOC profile of ‘Starkrimson’ pears, despite ZS yielding the highest total VOC concentration.

The load plots were used to assess the major chemicals that were responsible for variations in the aroma profiles of the pear samples (Fig. 3C). For example, the concentrations of isoledene, methyl octadec-6,9-dien-12-ynoate, butyl propionate, 1-heptatriacotanol, and 1-octen-3-one in ZS were significantly higher than those in other samples, and these increased the apple and mushroom aromas of the pears obtained by ZS pollination. The concentrations of elaidic acid ethyl ester, 3,7,11,16-tetramethyl-hexadeca-2,6,10,14-tetraen-1-ol, and methyl 8-heptadeenoate were highest in the DSS samples. The KF, SC, HG, HGG, XYH, and CK samples were clustered closely together, which suggested a high degree of similarity among these samples. This indicates that KF, SC, HG, HGG, and XYH pears, as cultivars, have a minimal effect on the VOCs in the pears. Furthermore, their effects were similar to those of open pollination.

To identify the specific VOCs responsible for these separations, we employed variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis. Fourteen compounds with VIP > 1 were identified as the key markers discriminating the pollination treatments (Fig. 3D). Critically, this list of key discriminators is predominantly composed of esters (e.g., hexyl acetate, butyl acetate), aldehydes (e.g., hexanal, 2-hexenal, nonanal), and the terpene α-farnesene. This is not a random assortment; it provides direct statistical evidence regarding the regulatory mechanism of the xenia effect on pear aroma. Specifically, the xenia effect mainly modulates the LOX pathway (which produces aldehydes and esters) and the MVA pathway (which produces terpenes such as α-farnesene) to regulate pear aroma. Thus, the xenia effect alters pear aroma by differentially regulating the biosynthesis of these pathway-specific key compounds.

Collectively, the findings from GC–MS establish that esters, aldehydes, and terpenes constitute the main aromatic compounds in ‘Starkrimson’ pears, aligning with the volatile profiles reported in other pear cultivars (Chen et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023). More importantly, this study reveals the dualistic influence of the xenia effect on this profile. Pollination with ZS significantly enhanced the concentration of key volatiles like α-farnesene and hexyl acetate, suggesting a unique upregulation of the underlying aroma biosynthesis pathways. Conversely, HG pollination led to a significant reduction in VOC accumulation, a negative xenia effect mechanistically similar to that reported in ‘Yali’ pears (Liu et al., 2023). In conclusion, our results demonstrate that the xenia effect, mediated through the modulation of key biosynthetic pathways—the LOX pathway and the MVA pathway—is a decisive factor shaping the concentration and composition of characteristic VOCs in pear fruit.

GC-IMS analysis of VOCs in pears from different pollination treatments

Identification of VOCs by GC-IMS

To complement the GC–MS analysis and to capture a more complete volatile profile, we employed GC-IMS. This technique is particularly adept at detecting and visualizing highly volatile, small-molecule compounds (Wang et al., 2020), which often serve as direct precursors or early products in metabolic pathways and might be underrepresented in GC–MS analysis. In this research, GC-IMS was applied to analyze the VOCs in the samples. The natural constituents in the samples were identified by comparison. According to Tian et al. (2020), every point in the figure represents a VOC. Some VOCs were present at slightly higher concentrations than in the CK samples (red dots, Fig. 4A). Compared with the CK, the concentrations of VOCs in samples from the different cultivars increased. Therefore, we speculated that different cultivars affected the production and release of aroma and VOCs in the peel of ‘Starkrimson’ pears.Fig. 4Top-view plot (A) and gallery plot (B) of the volatile fingerprints of pear by GC-IMS.Fig. 4

Top-view plot (A) and gallery plot (B) of the volatile fingerprints of pear by GC-IMS.

GC-IMS analysis involves two-dimensional separation of the component analyte. This is in contrast to GC–MS, which uses chemical speciation to distinguish the analyte. The GC-IMS topographic map, which consists of many two-dimensional maps, can be used as a global fingerprint of VOCs in the sample (Gerhardt et al., 2019). The reactive ion peak (normalized) is shown in Fig. 4A (red vertical line at 1.0 on the x-axis). The plot presents the retention time in gas chromatography (y-axis) and the relative migration time (normalized, a.u., x-axis). Each point on either side of the reactive ion peak corresponds to a VOC, with peak intensity variations indicated by color depth—darker shades denote higher peak intensity. The VOCs in the pear peel were identified using the NIST library. Sixty peaks (representing 37 different VOCs) were initially identified, and 23 peaks were not identified (Table S2). The 37 different VOCs included 11 esters, six aldehydes, 11 alcohols, six ketones, and three other substances (Fig. 4A and Table S2). Notably, the compounds detected by GC-IMS were predominantly small molecules (C3 -C10) (Table S2), including many short-chain esters (e.g., ethyl acetate, butyl acetate), aldehydes (e.g., butanal, hexanal), and alcohols. These compounds are central to the LOX pathway. For instance, aldehydes like hexanal are direct products of lipid oxidation, and short-chain alcohols and esters are key players in the esterification cascade that generates fruity aromas. Consequently, the significant differences in the concentrations of these esters, aldehydes, and alcohols across cultivars (Fig. 4B) offer insights into how xenia influences the early and intermediate stages of the fatty acid-derived aroma pathway.

Xenia affected the concentration of esters in pears. The concentrations of esters, aldehydes, and alcohols in the peel of ‘Starkrimson’ pears showed significant differences with the different cultivars (Fig. 4B). These compounds included acetic acid methyl ester, ethyl acetate, propyl acetate, 2-methyl-2-propenoic acid methyl ester, 3-methyl-butanoic acid methyl ester, butyl acetate, hexanoic acid methyl ester, propyl butyrate, ethyl 3-hydroxybutanoate, benzene acetic acid methyl ester, 1-hexanol, and butanal. In earlier investigations, these compounds were identified via GC–MS (Liu et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2023; Zhang, Liu, et al., 2019; Zhang, Wang, et al., 2019). The concentrations of ethyl acetate and acetic acid methyl ester were higher in the KF, DSS, ZS, SC, and XYH samples than in samples from the other cultivars. Compared with CK, the KF, DSS, HG, and HGG samples had higher concentrations of propyl acetate. The concentrations of 3-methyl-butanoic acid, methyl ester, and butyl acetate in the DSS, HG, SC, and HGG samples were higher than in the samples from the other cultivars. The concentration of benzene acetic acid methyl ester was higher in the KF, SC, and HGG samples than in the samples from the other cultivars. Moreover, the analysis revealed that the content of butanal in the SC sample was higher compared to that in the samples from other cultivars. The concentration of 2-methyl-2-butenal (d) in the HGG samples was higher than in the samples from the other cultivars. The ZS samples exhibited elevated concentrations of 1-hexanol. The VOCs composition in each sample was different (Fig. 4B), thus demonstrating that the xenia effect exerts a significant influence on the pool of small-molecule metabolites in the LOX pathway. This GC-IMS fingerprint, rich in precursors and highly volatile compounds, offers a unique perspective that complements the profile of more complex, final aroma products (e.g., long-chain esters, terpenes) captured by GC–MS.

Multivariate statistical analysis

OPLS-DA can effectively distinguish different groups and identify important variables that lead to differences between groups. OPLS-DA was used for analyzing the aroma characteristics of the samples in this study. The first two principal components explained 53.9 % of the total variance (41.2 % for principal component 1 and 12.7 % for principal component 2) between the four groups (Fig. 5A). The results showed that different cultivars had significant effects on the VOCs. While the separation in the OPLS-DA score plot (Fig. 5A) was less pronounced than that observed with GC–MS data (Fig. 3A), it nonetheless confirmed that xenia induces discernible changes in the pool of small-molecule, highly volatile VOCs detected by GC-IMS. The model parameters, specifically R2Y = 0.897 and Q2 = 0.714, indicated that the model possessed excellent interpretability and predictive power. High concentrations of 4-methyl-2-pentanone, 5-methyl-2-furanmethanol (m), (E,E)-2,4-heptadienal, 1-octene, and n-pentanal were found in CK samples (Fig. 5C). The concentrations of (Z)-3-octen-1-ol, ethyl acetate (d), 2-hexanone, and acetic acid methyl ester in the KF samples were higher than in the samples from the other cultivars. The ZS samples were characterized by high concentrations of (Z)-3-hexen-1-ol and 1-hexanol. The concentrations of 4-methoxy-2,5-dimethyl-3(2H)-furanone, hexanoic acid methyl ester, (Z)-2-pentenol, 2,6-nonadienal (m), and ethyl 3-hydroxybutanoate were higher in the XYH samples than in the samples from the other cultivars. No VOCs were enriched in the DSS, HG, SC, and HGG samples, suggesting that the VOC concentrations in these samples were relatively low.

VIP can assess the influence degree and interpretive capability of each factor on the classification and discrimination of sample groups. A higher VIP value suggests a more significant difference in aroma compounds between groups (Xu et al., 2021). Thirteen compounds with VIP > 1 were isolated in this study. These compounds were butanal, 1-hexanol, 2-methyl-2-butenal (d), benzene acetic acid methyl ester, 2-furanmethanol (d), propyl butyrate, 2-methyl-2-propanol, acetic acid methyl ester, isomenthone, propyl acetate, 5-methyl-2-furanmethanol (d), 2-hexanone, and (Z)-3-hexen-1-ol (Fig. 5D). Strikingly, the key discriminators identified by GC-IMS VIP analysis were predominantly short-chain aldehydes (e.g., butanal), alcohols (e.g., 1-hexanol, (Z)-3-hexen-1-ol), and esters (e.g., propyl acetate). This profile of small, highly volatile compounds is distinctly different from the long-chain esters and terpenes highlighted by the GC–MS VIP analysis (Fig. 3D). This divergence is not a discrepancy but rather a powerful illustration of the complementary nature of the two techniques. GC-IMS provides a high-sensitivity snapshot of the upstream precursors and early metabolic intermediates, particularly within the LOX pathway, while GC–MS delivers comprehensive identification and quantification of the final, aroma-active end products. The high sensitivity of GC-IMS is evidenced by its ability to resolve monomers and dimers of compounds like 2-methyl-2-butenal, providing a level of detail for small molecules that can be challenging for GC–MS. Conversely, the statistical power of our GC–MS data, validated by the OPLS-DA model (R2Y = 0.958, Q2 = 0.884), unequivocally identified 14 key aroma-active compounds (VIP >1, OAV ≥1), solidifying the role of esters, aldehydes, and α-farnesene as the primary drivers of aroma differences due to xenia.

Our finding that the xenia effect simultaneously modulates this broad spectrum of VOCs—from small precursors to complex end products—aligns with and expands upon recent literature. For instance, the pivotal role of the LOX pathway in generating C6 aldehydes and esters, as highlighted in ‘Nanguo’ pears (Luo et al., 2021; Zang et al., 2024), is strongly supported by our GC–MS data. Furthermore, the coordinated enhancement of both LOX-derived esters and MVA-derived α-farnesene in ZS-pollinated fruit, as detected by GC–MS, reveals a more systemic regulatory mechanism than previously reported, where studies often focused on a single pathway (Liu et al., 2023). The abundance of short-chain esters like ethyl acetate captured by GC-IMS reinforces findings from ‘Yali’ pears (Liu et al., 2023) and underscores their contribution to fruity aroma complexity, a aspect sometimes underrepresented in GC–MS-centric studies.

In conclusion, the integrated application of GC–MS and GC-IMS, supported by multivariate statistics, has enabled an unprecedented level of insight. It demonstrates that the xenia effect fine-tunes the entire volatile continuum, and it provides an analytical framework that bridges the gap between precursor availability and final aroma expression, offering a comprehensive model for future research on fruit flavor biochemistry.

Odor profiles of VOCs in pears obtained from different pollination treatments

The flavor characteristics of fruits depend not only on the relative or absolute concentrations of the aromatic components but also on the OAV or relative odor activity value (ROAV) (Chen et al., 2021). The OAV or ROAV, calculated by dividing the concentration (or relative concentration) of a volatile by its odor threshold, enables the quantification of how aroma compounds contribute to fruit flavor (Yan et al., 2018). When the OAV or ROAV of a substance is greater than one, it indicates that the substance is a characteristic aroma component of the fruit. A higher OAV or ROAV signifies that the substance makes a more significant contribution to the overall aroma. In this study, OAVs and ROAVs were calculated for the VOCs detected by GC–MS and GC-IMS to evaluate the effects of different cultivars on key aromatic compounds with high odor activity in pears. A total of 14 key VOCs (seven esters, six aldehydes, and one terpene) with OAV values of ≥ 1 were identified by GC–MS. By contrast, GC-IMS identified only four important VOCs (Table 1): 2,6-nonadienal(m), 2,6-nonadienal(d), 1-octene, and 2,3-butanedione. For the samples from the different cultivars, hexyl acetate (OAV: 734.52–2600.12, fruity and herbaceous aroma) was identified as the predominant aroma compound in the ‘Starkrimson’ pear peel. The high OAV of hexyl acetate suggests that ‘Starkrimson’ pears have a distinct aroma with fruity and herbaceous characteristics. The OAVs of hexyl acetate in pears pollinated by KF, DSS, ZS, HG, SC, HGG, and XYH pears were higher than those in the CK, with values of 2484.55, 1458.99, 2600.12, 913.88, 1856.44, 1389.48, and 1497.58, respectively (Table 1). Compared with the CK, the pears from these seven cultivars had higher OAVs for amyl acetate (fruity aroma), butyl acetate (fruity, apple, and fresh aromas), octanal (green, fatty, and citrus-like aromas), (E)-2-hexenal (green aroma), decanal (fatty aroma), and nonanal (fatty aroma). The ROAVs of 2,6-nonadienal, 1-octene, and 2,3-butanedione showed slight variations in concentrations after pollination with the different cultivars, which suggested that their overall contributions to the aroma were subtly influenced by xenia. These results indicate that differences in cultivars lead to variations in the pear aroma. All seven cultivars enhanced the fruity, grassy, and fatty aromas of the pears. These results demonstrate that all tested pollinizer cultivars, to varying degrees, enhanced the fruity and green-fatty aroma notes in ‘Starkrimson’ pears compared to open pollination. Furthermore, ‘Starkrimson’ pears had the strongest aroma after pollination by ZS.

Compared with the other six cultivars, HG pear resulted in lower OAVs in the pears for hexyl hexanoate (fruity aroma), ethyl caprylate (brandy aroma), octyl acetate (orange and peach aromas), 2-hexenal, and α-farnesene (sweet aroma) than those in the CK. Similarly, the ROAVs of 2,6-nonadienal and 1-octene were lower in the HG samples than in the CK samples. In the GC–MS analysis, we also noted a reduction in the concentrations of VOCs after pollination with HG pear compared with the CK. These findings indicate that the pears pollinated by HG pear have the lowest total concentration of VOCs, which leads to a decrease in fruity and sweet aromas. This is consistent with our earlier metabolic interpretation, suggesting that HG pollen has a weak or suppressive effect on both the LOX and MVA pathways, ultimately failing to drive the substantial biosynthesis of key aroma-active end products. Compared with the other cultivars, ZS pear had a more significant effect on the OAVs and ROAVs of 11 VOCs in the pears. These VOCs were hexyl acetate, hexyl hexanoate, butyl acetate, octyl acetate, ethyl caprylate, 2-hexenal, (E)-2-hexenal (green aroma), octanal (green, fatty, and citrus-like aromas), decanal (fatty aroma), nonanal (fatty aroma), and α-farnesene. The fact that ZS pollination simultaneously enhanced the OAVs of esters, aldehydes (from the LOX pathway), and α-farnesene (from the MVA pathway) provides compelling sensory evidence at the odor activity level for our hypothesis that ZS coordinately upregulates these two key metabolic routes. The results demonstrate that ZS pear pollination enhances the aroma of ‘Starkrimson’ pears, with more pronounced fruity, green, fatty, and sweet notes. This finding aligns with the GC–MS results, which indicated a substantial rise in the overall concentration of VOCs in ‘Starkrimson’ pears following pollination with ZS pears.

The identification of hexyl acetate as the predominant aroma compound (OAV > 700 across all treatments) aligns with its consistent recognition as a character-impact odorant in numerous pear varieties, attributed to its low odor threshold and intense fruity character (Chen, Lü, et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). The OAV of hexyl acetate—a character-impact odorant in pears—was exceptionally high in ZS-pollinated fruit (2600.12), as evidenced by our results, which explains the superior fruity aroma intensity. Similarly, the high OAV of α-farnesene in ZS samples aligns with its known contribution to sweet, floral notes in apples and pears, and its biosynthesis via the MVA pathway provides a mechanistic link to the observed xenia effect (Ding et al., 2020; López-Nicolás et al., 2009). The fact that ZS pollination simultaneously enhanced multiple key aroma compounds with high OAVs across different chemical classes (esters, aldehydes, terpenes) demonstrates its unique capacity to broadly enhance aroma complexity.

Effect of xenia on the quality of ‘Starkrimson’ pears

Different cultivars also affected the total soluble solid (TSS), titratable acid (TA), and the solid-acid ratio of the ‘Starkrimson’ pears. The TSS was lowest after HGG pear pollination (14.60) and highest after DSS pear pollination (15.86) (Fig. 6B). ZS pear pollination gave the highest pear solid-acid ratio (291.78), followed by the CK (285.09), and the difference between these two groups was not significant (Fig. 6C). The pear solid-acid ratios obtained with the other cultivars were significantly lower than those obtained with ZS pear and control pollination (Fig. 6D). Potential application value and limitations

Practical implications and recommendations for horticulture

The findings of this study provide clear, actionable strategies for optimizing pear orchard management to enhance fruit quality and market value. Specifically, the superior performance of ZS pollen in enhancing VOC complexity, key aroma-active compounds, and the solid-acid ratio positions it as the strongly recommended pollinizer for ‘Starkrimson’ pear orchards. Growers can leverage this finding by prioritizing ZS in new orchard designs or through top-grafting to directly improve fruit flavor and consumer appeal. Furthermore, the identified aroma-active compounds, such as hexyl acetate and α-farnesene, serve as valuable metabolic markers for flavor-directed breeding, accelerating the development of new cultivars with superior sensory profiles. Additionally, the elucidation of underlying metabolic pathways (LOX and MVA) supports the development of precision horticulture practices. For instance, in established orchards with suboptimal aroma, targeted artificial pollination or the grafting of superior pollinizer branches could be employed to systematically enhance fruit quality.

Limitations of practical application

While this work demonstrates the potential of xenia in aroma enhancement, several limitations must be acknowledged when considering commercial application. First, the generalizability of these findings may be influenced by genotype-by-environment interactions. As this study was conducted at one location over a single growing season, further validation across multiple regions and seasons is essential to confirm the stability and robustness of ZS pollination effects under varying climatic and agronomic conditions. Second, effective implementation under practical orchard settings remains challenging. Commercial orchards often rely on open pollination, which does not guarantee the dominance of a specific pollinizer pollen. Achieving consistent xenia effects may require the adoption of artificial pollination techniques, which entail additional costs and logistical planning. Third, the xenia effect is genotype-specific. The conclusions here are based on ‘Starkrimson’ as the maternal parent, and optimal pollinizers for other pear cultivars may differ. Developing a comprehensive database of compatible pollinizer-scion combinations will be crucial for industry-wide adoption. Finally, this study focused primarily on aroma and basic quality attributes. Future work should examine whether the observed aroma improvements are associated with any trade-offs in other agronomically important traits, such as yield, fruit size, or postharvest storage performance, to provide a holistic assessment.

Conclusions

This study establishes that pollinizer selection, through the xenia effect, is a decisive factor shaping the aroma and quality of ‘Starkrimson’ pears. By integrating E-nose, GC–MS, and GC-IMS, we demonstrated that ZS pollen enhances the biosynthesis of VOCs through both LOX and MVA pathways. This resulted in fruits with a more complex VOC profile, characterized by higher concentrations of esters and terpenoids, and superior sensory attributes. Conversely, HG pollen negatively impacted aroma volatile accumulation. The combined use of GC–MS and GC-IMS enabled a comprehensive VOC analysis, with the former identifying 14 key aroma-active compounds and the latter detecting 4 critical small-molecule volatiles, thus providing a robust analytical framework for future flavor research. These findings reveal the metabolic mechanisms underlying xenia-driven aroma formation and offer horticulturists a strategy to enhance fruit marketability through targeted pollinizer selection. Future efforts should focus on decoding the associated transcriptional regulators to enable precision breeding and orchard management.
