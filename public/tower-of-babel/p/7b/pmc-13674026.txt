Extended reality–assisted localization of lung nodules in a hybrid operating room: A prospective study
Authors: Chih-Hsiang Chang, Ning Chien, Hsien-Yuan Hsieh, Kuang-Chen Yen, Yu-An Zheng, Yi-Ching Lee, Yang-Sheng Chen, Xu-Heng Chiang, Ping-Hsuan Han, Mong-Wei Lin, Hsao-Hsun Hsu, Yi-Ping Hung, Jin-Shing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the feasibility, accuracy, and safety of a novel extended reality–assisted percutaneous localization technique in a hybrid operating room for patients with small pulmonary nodules.MethodsIn this prospective, single-arm study, 20 adult patients scheduled for video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery sublobar resection for peripheral pulmonary nodules (2 cm or less) were enrolled. Participants underwent extended reality–guided percutaneous dye localization under general anesthesia. Patient-specific 3-dimensional holographic models were registered using optical markers to guide needle insertion, followed by confirmatory cone-beam computed tomography. The primary outcomes were technical accuracy and procedural safety.ResultsThe extended reality–assisted technique achieved a 90% technical success rate. The mean localization error was 7.9 mm in the intention-to-treat population. The procedure was efficient, with a median localization time of 13.5 minutes and a median of 1 confirmatory cone-beam computed tomography scan. The complication rate was 5%, involving a single case of pneumothorax; no intrapulmonary hemorrhage or air embolism occurred.ConclusionsExtended reality-assisted percutaneous localization in a hybrid operating room is feasible and safe, demonstrating acceptable accuracy for small pulmonary nodules. This approach may streamline surgical workflow by enabling continuous needle advancement and reducing repetitive interruptions compared with conventional cone-beam computed tomography guidance.Key Words: extended reality, pulmonary nodule, localization, hybrid operating room, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, sublobar resection, cone-beam computed tomography

XR holographic guidance for continuous lung nodule localization.

Central Message

Extended reality–assisted lung nodule localization in a hybrid operating room is feasible and safe, offering a streamlined workflow that minimizes repetitive tomographic interruptions.

Perspective

This study provides the first clinical description of extended reality–assisted localization in a hybrid operating room. By providing holographic visualization, this technique enables continuous needle advancement and reduces repetitive interruptions inherent in conventional workflows. These findings suggest extended reality can optimize procedural efficiency and safety during sublobar lung resection.

The widespread adoption of high-resolution computed tomography (CT) has exponentially improved the detection of small pulmonary nodules.1 Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery and robotic-assisted thoracic surgery with sublobar resection are the preferred treatment for these lesions2,3; however, precise preoperative localization is critical for nonpalpable and nonvisible nodules to ensure adequate surgical margins, especially in robotic-assisted thoracic surgery because of the complete lack of tactile feedback.

Currently, CT-guided percutaneous localization remains the clinical gold standard.4 Common techniques such as hookwire, microcoil, and dye marking carry risks of pneumothorax, hemorrhage, and marker dislodgement, particularly during patient transport.5 The hybrid operating room was introduced to address the logistical inefficiencies of the conventional 2-stage workflow, enabling a single-stage procedure with intraoperative cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT).6 Despite eliminating patient transfer, CBCT-guided localization remains inherently intermittent. Laser guides mark only the entry point on the skin but cannot visualize the trajectory through the air, forcing operators to rely on subjective angle estimation. Repetitive scans are needed to verify the needle’s trajectory, forcing the surgical team to cycle between the operative field and the control room for radiation safety. This “step-and-shoot” workflow impedes procedural continuity and precludes a single continuous puncture maneuver, potentially increasing the risk of pneumothorax as the result of multiple needle adjustments.7

Extended reality (XR) technology offers a promising solution to these workflow constraints. By overlaying 3-dimensional (3D) anatomical models directly onto the patient, XR provides intuitive, real-time holographic visualization of the target and planned trajectory. This continuous guidance potentially minimizes the reliance on repetitive confirmatory CBCT scans, allowing for continuous needle advancement. Although previous studies have explored augmented reality in conventional CT suites, to the best of our knowledge, no previous work has integrated this holographic guidance directly into the hybrid operating room workflow.8 This prospective, single-arm study presents the first clinical experience worldwide of a novel XR-assisted localization technique using a passthrough headset within a hybrid operating room, aiming to validate its feasibility, accuracy, and safety for the precise sublobar resection of small pulmonary nodules.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a prospective, single-arm feasibility study to investigate the intraoperative application of the XR-assisted technique in a hybrid operating room setting. This design was chosen to generate essential preliminary data to inform the planning of future large-scale randomized controlled trials. Consistent with the estimation method for pilot studies described by Viechtbauer and colleagues,9 a sample size of 20 was determined to be sufficient for detecting procedure-related adverse events with an anticipated incidence of 15% to 20%. This study is reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs (TREND) statement (Online Data Supplement 1). The study adhered strictly to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the National Taiwan University Hospital Institutional Review Board (protocol number 202502149RINB; approval date: April 18, 2025), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier NCT06976684).

Patient Selection

This single-center study recruited consecutive patients requiring surgical management of pulmonary nodules between June 1, 2025, and October 31, 2025. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 to 80 years who were scheduled to undergo parenchyma-sparing sublobar resection (Figure 1). To focus on lesions necessitating precise preoperative localization, we included only patients with small pulmonary nodules with a maximum diameter of ≤2 cm located in the peripheral outer third of the lung field. We excluded patients scheduled for lobectomy, individuals belonging to vulnerable populations, and those unable to provide written informed consent. Two independent investigators assessed and enrolled patients during the prehospitalization outpatient visit.

Procedure

Generation of patient-specific 3D anatomical models

Because most patients presented with diagnostic CT scans from outside referring hospitals, exactly one dedicated preoperative high-resolution CT scan was acquired the day before surgery. To ensure consistency, the patient was positioned in the lateral decubitus position using standardized aids (arm support, axillary cushions, and rigid board) to minimize soft-tissue deformation. Five radiopaque fiducial markers (electrocardiogram patches) were arranged in a U-shaped configuration on the dorsal and lateral thorax to avoid puncture sites. Skin sites were marked with tattoo stickers for accurate intraoperative reattachment. 3D models were reconstructed (Synapse 3D; Fujifilm Corp), optimized (MeshLab; ISTI-CNR), and deployed to a Meta Quest 3 headset (Meta Platforms Inc) via a custom Unity-based application. To ensure compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, all imaging data were fully deidentified on a secure hospital workstation before 3D reconstruction. Only anonymized 3D models devoid of protected health information were transferred to the headset, which operated completely offline.

Intraoperative image-to-patient registration

Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned identically to the preoperative setup. Electrocardiogram patches were reattached to the tattoo sites and overlaid with ArUco markers.10 To simulate preoperative lung volume, the anesthesiologist clamped the double-lumen tube at end-inspiration. The headset camera detected the markers, and spatial co-registration was achieved using the Perspective-n-Point and Kabsch-Umeyama algorithms.11, 12, 13 A root mean square registration error of <10 mm was required to proceed.

XR-guided localization

All XR-guided localizations and subsequent resections were performed by 2 attending thoracic surgeons (X.H.C. and C.H.C). An interventional radiologist (N.C.) was available as a backup. The surgeon used the superimposed holographic models to plan the trajectory (Figures 3 and 4). The system tracked the needle tip, providing a visual “Alignment Reference” that turned green when the needle orientation matched the planned vector. A 22-gauge needle was advanced to the target depth, and 0.2 mL of Patent Blue V dye was injected. Accuracy was verified via intraoperative CBCT. A deviation >2 cm between the needle tip and tumor margin was considered a technical failure, triggering conversion to conventional CBCT guidance. Otherwise, sublobar resection proceeded immediately.

Outcome Analysis

Outcomes were assessed in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, comprising all enrolled patients, and the per-protocol (PP) population, consisting of those completing the procedure without deviation. Primary end points included procedural safety and technical accuracy, the latter quantified as the shortest needle-to-target distance on confirmatory CBCT measured by a blinded independent radiologist. Secondary outcomes included perioperative results and efficiency metrics, specifically the number of CBCT scans and localization time. Localization time was defined as the interval from registration initiation to the verification CBCT. Continuous variables are reported as mean (SD) or median (range) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

Results

Patient Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

As outlined in the study flow (Figure 1), 24 patients were initially assessed for eligibility. After we excluded ineligible candidates, 20 patients were included in the ITT analysis. The ITT cohort comprised 11 men (55%) and 9 women (45%) with a mean age of 59.1 (11.1) years. The mean body mass index was 24.1 (3.0) kg/m2. With regard to clinical history, 9 patients (45%) were smokers, and the majority were classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status II (95%). Furthermore, 45% of patients had a Charlson Comorbidity Index of 1% to 2%, and 40% had a Charlson Comorbidity Index of ≥3 (Table 1).

Nodule and Surgical Characteristics

In the ITT population, targeted pulmonary nodules exhibited a mean maximum diameter of 15.2 (4.8) mm and a mean solid component diameter of 9.1 (5.3) mm (Table 1). These lesions were located peripherally, with a mean depth from the pleural surface of 2.5 (4.2) mm. The anatomical distribution showed the right lower lobe as the most frequent site (25%), followed by the right middle lobe (20%), left upper lobe (20%), left lower lobe (20%), and right upper lobe (15%). All 20 patients (100%) underwent uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery . Sublobar resection types included wedge resection in 17 cases (85%) and segmentectomy in 3 cases (15%). Pathologic diagnosis confirmed malignancy in 15 cases (75%), predominantly invasive adenocarcinoma (70%), whereas benign pathology was identified in the remaining 5 cases (25%) (Table 2).

Localization Procedure Outcomes and Complications

The XR-assisted technique was technically successful in 18 of the 20 enrolled patients (90%). These 18 patients constituted the PP population (Table 3). Two patients were excluded from the PP analysis as the result of technical failures that prevented valid completion of the experimental procedure. One exclusion was attributable to a major pneumothorax requiring immediate intervention. The second exclusion involved a needle-to-nodule deviation of 2.6 cm; this significant error occurred as the result of double-lumen endotracheal tube displacement, which caused significant lung collapse. Consequently, this procedure was converted to conventional CBCT-guided localization. This converted case required 5 CBCT scans and a total duration of 45 minutes, a figure encompassing both the initial XR-guided attempt and the subsequent CBCT-guided localization procedures.

The exclusion of these failed cases resulted in distinct performance metrics between populations. In the ITT group, the mean localization error was 7.9 (6.6) mm, whereas the PP group achieved an improved mean accuracy of 6.9 (5.1) mm (reflecting the exclusion of the aforementioned outlier with excessive deviation caused by lung collapse). Procedural efficiency was similarly impacted; although the median localization time was 13.5 minutes for both groups, the range in the PP group was significantly narrower (4-25 minutes) compared with the ITT group (4-45 minutes), because the prolonged converted case was removed. The overall workflow comprised 3 phases. Preoperative 3D model preparation required approximately 1 hour, which did not consume operative time. The reported intraoperative localization time consisted of an automated registration phase (≤1 minute) and the subsequent execution phase, including trajectory planning, needle insertion, and confirmatory CBCT scanning (10-15 minutes). Regarding overall surgical outcomes, the mean total operative time was 111.1 (26.4) minutes, and the mean postoperative hospital stay was 2.6 (1.4) days. The complication rate was low. Localization-related complications were limited to a single case (5%) of pneumothorax. Postoperatively, 1 patient (5%) experienced a prolonged air leak, which was attributed to severe pleural adhesions that were encountered during dissection. No intrapulmonary hemorrhage or air embolism occurred.

Discussion

The paradigm shift toward parenchyma-sparing sublobar resection for early-stage non–small cell lung cancer has necessitated the development of precise localization techniques for small, nonpalpable pulmonary nodules.14 In this prospective study, we validated a protocol for XR-assisted pulmonary nodule localization within a hybrid operating room (Figure 5). Our results demonstrate a high technical success rate of 90% in the ITT population, notably achieving a mean localization error of 7.9 mm. Furthermore, the procedure necessitated a median of only 1 intraoperative confirmatory CT scan, contributing to a rapid median localization time of 13.5 minutes. These findings suggest that XR guidance represents a feasible, intuitive, and convenient alternative to current mainstream localization techniques.

The 25% benign resection rate observed in our cohort reflects the clinical reality of managing indeterminate small nodules. Crucially, 80% of these benign cases were infectious lesions (caseous granulomas and fungal infections), for which localized surgical excision serves as a safe and effective diagnostic and therapeutic tool rather than a nontherapeutic procedure.15 This clinical reality highlights the absolute necessity of precise, parenchyma-sparing resections; by ensuring accurate margins, XR-guided localization minimizes the loss of healthy lung tissue, which is especially critical when a resected lesion is ultimately diagnosed as benign.

Although conventional CT-guided localization established the precedent for accuracy, it is intrinsically limited by the logistical risks of patient transport.16,17 The advent of the hybrid operating room and CBCT effectively resolved these transport-related inefficiencies by enabling a single-stage workflow.18 However, operational limitations persist. CBCT-guided procedures still rely on an iterative “step-and-shoot” workflow, often necessitating multiple scans to verify needle trajectory. This reliance stems from the limitation that laser alignment lights, while marking the entry point on the skin, remain invisible in the air. Consequently, surgeons struggle to align the needle angle accurately before skin entry, relying on subjective estimation rather than precise 3D vector guidance. This discontinuous process forces procedural pauses and necessitates repeated needle adjustments, maneuvers that are intrinsically associated with an increased risk of iatrogenic lung injury.19 In contrast, XR guidance addresses this bottleneck by providing continuous, real-time holographic visualization, allowing for continuous needle advancement.

This streamlined workflow likely contributed to our favorable safety profile. In our cohort, the incidence of pneumothorax was 5% (1/20) with no occurrences of intrapulmonary hemorrhage. This safety profile appears favorable when compared with large series of CBCT-guided percutaneous procedures, which have reported pneumothorax rates ranging from 31.8% to 34.9% and pulmonary hemorrhage rates between 14.5% and 32.8%.20, 21, 22 We postulate that by facilitating a continuous insertion path and minimizing the need for the “stop-and-adjust” corrections typical of CBCT guidance, the XR-assisted technique reduces pleural and parenchymal trauma.

Optimizing the workflow for XR-assisted localization requires carefully balancing registration accuracy with patient safety. Recently, a research team used an optical see-through HoloLens headset to perform augmented reality-guided localization directly within the CT suite.8 By performing the procedure with the patient under local anesthesia with breath-hold cooperation, this “in-suite” configuration minimizes registration errors caused by postural changes. However, this approach essentially retains a 2-stage workflow,23 necessitating patient transfer to the operating room after localization, which reintroduces the risks of marker dislodgement and the progression of pneumothorax. To eliminate these transport risks, we elected to perform the localization with the patient under general anesthesia within a hybrid operating room.

Although this decision prioritizes safety, it introduces geometric discrepancies between the preoperative CT (awake) and the intraoperative setting (anesthetized). To reconcile these discrepancies, we rigorously standardized patient positioning using specific support aids (arm support pillow, axillary cushions, and rigid boards) to replicate the exact lateral decubitus posture. Furthermore, addressing respiratory motion artifacts was paramount. To simulate the lung volume of the preoperative CT, we used a double-lumen endotracheal tube to mechanically clamp the operative lung at the end-inspiratory phase. In addition, precise spatial registration was achieved using ArUco markers and the Kabsch-Umeyama algorithm.12,13 This multimarker configuration minimizes the global root mean square error, ensuring robust registration even if the spatial coordinates of 1 or 2 markers deviate as a result of skin deformation.

Despite the high technical success rate, the analysis of specific technical failures serves as a critical learning opportunity. One complication involved a significant pneumothorax in a patient with emphysema, highlighting a specific ergonomic challenge of video passthrough XR headsets: the limitation of natural depth perception.24 Although the system confirmed trajectory alignment, the operator experienced perceptual uncertainty regarding depth, resulting in a slower speed of needle insertion; in emphysematous tissue, this prolonged pleural transit likely exacerbated the risk of air leakage. In addition, another patient was excluded from the PP analysis because of a 2.6-cm deviation caused by double-lumen endotracheal tube displacement and subsequent lung collapse. Because the current XR system relies on a static preoperative model, it could not account for such a drastic anatomical deformation. These experiences validate the necessity of maintaining conventional CBCT backup for complex scenarios.

Although not formally assessed in this study, the learning curve for mastering the XR interface warrants further investigation.25 Notably, our previous work demonstrated that this in-house developed system achieved a System Usability Scale score exceeding 70, suggesting that the proficiency barrier is likely surmountable.26 Indeed, the system's high usability enabled our thoracic surgeons to rapidly master the technique independently. However, broad generalizability for surgeons completely unfamiliar with XR interfaces remains to be evaluated in future multioperator trials.

This study has other limitations. First, although the video passthrough headset offers a wider field of view, inherent perceptual limitations such as the vergence-accommodation conflict remain.24 Second, the current rigid registration algorithm cannot perfectly conform to dynamic anatomy, especially given the nonrigid tissue deformations induced by the transition from spontaneous breathing during preoperative imaging to positive-pressure mechanical ventilation during surgery, as well as the anatomical shifts caused by the potential occurrence of pneumothorax during the procedure. Third, the strict inclusion criteria limiting targets to the peripheral outer third of the lung may restrict generalizability to deep-seated nodules. Finally, the single-center, single-arm design limits definitive statistical comparisons, highlighting the need for large-scale randomized controlled trials. To ultimately transition this custom-built technology toward broad global implementation, our ongoing translational roadmap encompasses the establishment of an academic spin-off and acquiring regulatory certification.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, the present study presents the first description of XR-assisted percutaneous localization using a passthrough headset within a hybrid operating room. By providing real-time, continuous holographic visualization, this technique offers a promising alternative to conventional CBCT guidance, potentially streamlining the surgical workflow by reducing the reliance on repetitive tomographic verification. These preliminary findings support the clinical feasibility and safety of this approach; nevertheless, large-scale randomized trials are warranted to rigorously evaluate its comparative efficacy against standard techniques.
