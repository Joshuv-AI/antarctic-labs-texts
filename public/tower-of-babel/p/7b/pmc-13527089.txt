Intelligent fall risk prediction and real-time warning system for elderly care based on multimodal deep learning and wearable sensor fusion
Authors: Xiaoying Li, Zhen Liu, Yuzhou Wu, Huiyang Zhu, Liu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFalls remain a leading cause of injury-related morbidity and mortality among adults aged 65 and older, and these human and economic costs keep rising as populations age worldwide. Most existing risk assessment tools rely on periodic clinical evaluations or single-sensor wearable devices, neither of which tracks the dynamic, multifactorial nature of fall risk throughout daily life. In this paper we describe the design, implementation, and pilot-level field validation of an integrated system that pairs a multi-sensor wearable unit (triaxial accelerometer, gyroscope, insole pressure array, and wrist-worn photoplethysmography) with a CNN-LSTM deep learning backbone enhanced by eight-head attention. The network ingests temporally aligned signals from all four modalities, extracts hierarchical spatiotemporal features, and outputs a continuous risk score that a rule-based engine maps onto four graded alert tiers. We collected data from 120 community-dwelling older adults (mean age 72.4 years) over six months and evaluated the system on a held-out test set of 18 participants (12,540 windows, 6 fall events). The model reached 94.2% overall accuracy and an AUC of 0.967, surpassing random forest, SVM, single-modality CNN and LSTM, a Transformer encoder baseline, and a late-fusion variant. We stress, however, that the extreme class imbalance (fall windows constitute only 0.05% of total windows, with a high-risk PPV of merely 6.25%) severely limits the stability and interpretability of sensitivity estimates; a single misclassification among the six fall events shifts sensitivity by roughly 17% points. End-to-end pipeline latency averaged 138 ms on a smartphone, satisfying real-time operational requirements. In a subsequent three-month deployment with 80 participants, the group receiving active alerts recorded 62% fewer falls than the passive-monitoring control group (0.82 vs. 2.14 falls per person-month; p < 0.01). This deployment, however, was conceived as a technical field test of system operability rather than a clinical efficacy trial; the fall incidence difference is an exploratory, secondary observation confounded by concurrent daily risk summaries and caregiver notifications, and it should not be interpreted as evidence of causal preventive effect. Importantly, the window-level classification PPV (6.25%) and the deployment-level alert PPV (87.8%) differ because the latter applies episode-level aggregation through the rule-based warning engine, and readers should note this distinction when comparing metrics across sections. User satisfaction averaged 4.3/5 on a Likert scale, and wearing compliance reached 91.3% of waking hours. Taken together, these results suggest that multimodal sensor fusion combined with attention-based deep learning offers a technically viable path toward continuous, community-based fall risk monitoring, while also underscoring the need for larger-scale, prospectively registered randomized controlled trials to establish whether such systems can causally reduce fall incidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56750-9.Keywords: Fall risk prediction, Elderly care, Deep learning, Wearable sensors, Multimodal fusion, Real-time warning systemSubject terms: Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The global demographic shift toward an aging population presents unprecedented challenges for healthcare systems worldwide. Falls among elderly individuals represent a critical public health concern, serving as the leading cause of injury-related morbidity and mortality in people aged 65 and above1. The consequences of falls extend beyond immediate physical injuries, encompassing long-term disability, reduced quality of life, increased healthcare costs, and psychological impacts such as fear of falling2. As the proportion of elderly population continues to rise, developing effective fall prevention strategies has become an urgent priority for researchers and healthcare practitioners.

Current approaches to fall risk assessment and prediction predominantly rely on clinical evaluation tools, questionnaires, and single-modality sensor systems3. Traditional assessment methods, while providing valuable baseline information, suffer from inherent limitations including subjective bias, inability to capture dynamic risk factors, and lack of continuous monitoring capabilities. Recent advances in wearable sensor technology have introduced objective measurement possibilities for gait analysis and activity monitoring4. However, these single-sensor systems face significant challenges in achieving satisfactory prediction accuracy due to their limited capacity to capture the multifaceted nature of fall risk factors, which encompass biomechanical, physiological, environmental, and behavioral dimensions5.

The integration of multimodal deep learning techniques with wearable sensor fusion presents a promising paradigm shift for addressing existing limitations in fall risk prediction. Deep learning architectures possess the capability to automatically extract complex patterns from high-dimensional sensor data, eliminating the need for manual feature engineering and enabling the discovery of subtle risk indicators that may elude conventional analytical methods6. Multimodal fusion strategies can synergistically combine heterogeneous data streams from accelerometers, gyroscopes, pressure sensors, and physiological monitors to construct comprehensive risk profiles that reflect the intricate interplay of fall-related factors7. Furthermore, the deployment of real-time prediction models on edge computing platforms enables continuous risk monitoring and timely intervention, potentially preventing falls before they occur8.

We acknowledge at the outset that the individual building blocks of our system—convolutional feature extractors, recurrent sequence encoders, and scaled dot-product attention—are well-established components in the deep learning toolbox. Our contribution does not lie in inventing a new class of neural network; rather, it resides in the end-to-end integration, deployment, and transparent pilot-level evaluation of these components within a fall risk monitoring pipeline tested under naturalistic conditions with older adults. We frame the value of this work as a system-level engineering contribution—building a reproducible pipeline from sensor synchronization through edge inference to caregiver notification—rather than a claim of algorithmic novelty or clinical efficacy. We identify three specific aspects of the implementation, each supported by dedicated experimental observations presented in Sections “Experimental results and system performance analysis” and “Conclusion”.

First, we adopt a raw-level temporal-alignment fusion strategy that synchronizes accelerometer, gyroscope, plantar pressure, and heart rate streams into a single input tensor before any feature extraction occurs. We deliberately chose early fusion over late fusion because our ablation experiments (Table 7) and cross-modal attention weight visualization (Fig. 5) indicate that the model assigns different weights to modality groups depending on the ongoing activity—a pattern that late-fusion architectures cannot exploit once modality-specific features have been extracted independently. Second, we employ a CNN-LSTM hybrid backbone augmented with an eight-head attention layer whose purpose is context-dependent sensor reweighting rather than architectural novelty per se. Figure 5 visualizes the learned attention distributions during walking, turning, and sit-to-stand transitions. These visualizations show that the attention heads assign measurably different weights to accelerometer versus cardiac channels depending on the ongoing activity, though we note that observing differential attention weights is distinct from establishing the underlying physiological mechanisms that produce those differences.

Third, we design a rule-based, multi-tier warning engine that combines the instantaneous model output, a short-term risk trend computed via exponential moving average, and contextual modifiers (time of day, recent activity intensity). The warning thresholds were calibrated by maximizing Youden’s index on the held-out validation cohort rather than set by hand. We emphasize that the principal value of the complete system lies in its end-to-end integration—from sensor synchronization through edge inference to caregiver notification—and in the three-month field deployment that tests whether this integration can operate reliably in naturalistic settings. We do not claim that any single architectural element is unprecedented, nor do we claim that the field deployment establishes clinical efficacy.

The objective of this research is to design, implement, and conduct pilot-level field validation of a comprehensive fall risk monitoring system that integrates multimodal wearable sensing with deep learning inference, while maintaining real-time performance suitable for practical deployment in community and home-based settings. Rather than asserting clinical efficacy, this work aims to demonstrate operational feasibility and to transparently report the system’s capabilities and limitations as a foundation for future clinical investigation.

Theoretical foundation of multimodal data fusion and fall risk prediction

Multimodal data acquisition mechanism for wearable sensors

Wearable sensor systems constitute the fundamental infrastructure for continuous fall risk monitoring, employing diverse sensing modalities to capture comprehensive physiological and biomechanical information. Triaxial accelerometers measure linear acceleration along three orthogonal axes, enabling the quantification of body movements, postural transitions, and gait characteristics through detection of acceleration vectors that reflect gravitational and dynamic motion components11. Gyroscope sensors complement accelerometer data by measuring angular velocity around three rotational axes, providing critical information about body orientation changes, rotational movements during ambulation, and balance maintenance strategies12. Pressure sensors embedded in insole devices or positioned at strategic body locations capture ground reaction forces and center of pressure trajectories, offering insights into weight distribution patterns and balance control mechanisms during standing and walking activities13.

Physiological monitoring modalities extend the sensor array beyond kinematic measurements to encompass cardiovascular and autonomic nervous system indicators. Heart rate sensors employing photoplethysmography technology continuously track cardiac rhythm and enable the extraction of heart rate variability parameters, which serve as markers of autonomic regulation and stress responses that may influence fall susceptibility14. The integration of these heterogeneous sensor modalities facilitates the construction of multidimensional feature spaces encompassing temporal gait parameters, spatial postural metrics, dynamic balance indices, and physiological status indicators.

Data acquisition protocols must address distinct sampling requirements for different sensor modalities to ensure adequate signal resolution while managing computational and energy constraints. Accelerometer and gyroscope data typically require sampling frequencies ranging from moderate to high rates to capture rapid motion dynamics and subtle postural adjustments, whereas pressure sensor arrays operate at comparable frequencies to track weight shift patterns during locomotion. Heart rate monitoring systems function at lower sampling rates due to the inherently slower dynamics of cardiovascular signals. Preprocessing operations include offset calibration, noise filtering through digital signal processing techniques, and feature extraction algorithms that transform raw sensor readings into interpretable metrics.

Deep learning models hold clear practical advantages for fall risk prediction. By learning features directly from raw sensor signals, they bypass the painstaking and often incomplete process of manual feature engineering. Handcrafted descriptors—step length variance, mediolateral sway range, spectral entropy—capture only what the designer anticipates, whereas a well-trained deep network can surface subtle risk signatures that no predefined feature set would include. The nonlinear representational capacity of such networks further allows them to model interactions between biomechanical instability and physiological deterioration, something that linear classifiers struggle to express21.

Traditional machine learning pipelines built around random forests or support vector machines remain useful baselines, and we retain them in our comparison (Table 5). Their main drawback in the present context is the need for domain-specific feature selection and their limited expressiveness when the input space grows to thirteen channels sampled at high frequency. We used a set of 42 handcrafted features per window—covering time-domain statistics (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, skewness, kurtosis for each axis), frequency-domain descriptors (dominant frequency, spectral entropy, band power in 0–3 Hz and 3–10 Hz bands), and cross-axis Pearson correlations—as the input to these classical models.

More recently, Transformer architectures originally designed for natural language processing have demonstrated strong performance in sensor-based human activity recognition (HAR). Their self-attention mechanism can capture long-range temporal dependencies without the sequential bottleneck inherent in recurrent networks9. Dirgová Luptáková et al.9 adapted the Vision Transformer for wearable activity classification and reported high accuracy on a large public dataset. Zafar et al.10 applied a Transformer-based model specifically to fall detection tasks involving elderly subjects and found it competitive with CNN-LSTM hybrids, particularly in distinguishing confusable activity classes. Beyond deep learning approaches, probabilistic graphical models continue to offer valuable perspectives for fall event modeling. Guendoul et al.22 recently proposed a heterogeneous hidden Markov model (HHMM) that processes 3D vision-based body articulation data captured by a ZED stereo camera for indoor fall detection and prediction. Their work highlights an important complementary direction—using structured probabilistic models under controlled monitoring conditions—that differs from our wearable-sensor-based, community-deployed approach but shares the goal of predicting falls before they occur. To provide a more rigorous benchmark, we additionally trained a Transformer encoder baseline (four self-attention layers, model dimension 256, eight heads) on the same input windows as our proposed model. The results appear in the updated Table 6 and confirm that, on our dataset, the Transformer baseline achieves accuracy comparable to our multimodal CNN-LSTM without attention but falls short of the attention-augmented fusion model, likely because the relatively small dataset size (83,916 windows, only 47 fall events) limits the data-hungry Transformer’s ability to fully exploit its capacity.

Multimodal feature fusion and risk assessment methods

Multimodal fusion strategies establish systematic frameworks for integrating heterogeneous sensor information to construct comprehensive fall risk representations. Early fusion concatenates raw sensor streams or low-level features before input to the prediction model, enabling the learning algorithm to discover cross-modal correlations directly from the combined data representation23. This approach preserves fine-grained interactions between modalities but increases input dimensionality and computational complexity. Late fusion operates on independent modality-specific models, combining their output predictions through aggregation mechanisms to generate the final risk assessment. Hybrid fusion architectures strategically combine early and late fusion paradigms, applying feature-level integration for closely related modalities while maintaining separate processing pathways for complementary information sources.

Ensemble learning methods including bagging and boosting construct multiple diverse models to improve generalization capacity and prediction stability across varied data distributions.

Fall risk quantification translates model outputs into interpretable risk levels that inform clinical decision-making and intervention strategies. The continuous risk score generated by the prediction model is mapped to three discrete categories: low risk (), medium risk (), and high risk (), where thresholds and are determined through receiver operating characteristic curve analysis to balance sensitivity and specificity requirements. This stratification enables personalized intervention protocols, with high-risk individuals receiving intensive monitoring and preventive measures while low-risk subjects undergo routine assessment.

The selection of fusion strategy substantially influences prediction accuracy and model robustness. Early fusion captures complex cross-modal interactions but requires careful regularization to prevent overfitting in high-dimensional spaces. Late fusion provides modular architecture facilitating incremental system updates yet may fail to exploit subtle inter-modal dependencies26. Hybrid approaches balance these trade-offs by applying appropriate fusion mechanisms at different processing stages, achieving enhanced predictive performance through strategic information integration while maintaining computational efficiency for real-time deployment scenarios.

Deploying a deep learning model on resource-constrained hardware requires deliberate trade-offs between accuracy and computational cost. We compressed the network through post-training INT8 quantization and structured channel pruning, removing the 30% of convolutional filters with the lowest L1 norms29. After compression, the model occupies 9.2 MB (down from 37 MB at full precision) and retains 2.3 million parameters. On a Raspberry Pi 4 Model B (4 GB RAM, Cortex-A72 @ 1.5 GHz) running TensorFlow Lite, single-window inference requires 23 ± 2 ms (mean ± SD, n = 1,000 trials). The end-to-end pipeline—covering sensor data buffering, preprocessing (8 ms), inference, and alert rendering—averages 138 ms in total latency (95th percentile: 162 ms), well within the 500 ms ceiling generally considered acceptable for fall-warning applications30.

An important clarification regarding power consumption is warranted. The previously reported figure of 1.8 W reflected only the incremental CPU power attributable to the inference thread, estimated through software-level profiling (difference in vcgencmd readings between idle and inference states). This value, however, represents only the computational overhead rather than whole-board consumption, since the Raspberry Pi 4 draws approximately 2.85 W even at idle and considerably more under computational load. We therefore re-measured total system power using a SATECHI USB-C inline power meter placed between the 5 V/3 A power supply and the board, recording continuously during 30-minute inference sessions. The corrected average whole-board power draw during continuous inference is 4.3 ± 0.2 W. On a 10,000 mAh (37 Wh) portable battery pack, this yields approximately 8.5 h of operation.

On a mid-range smartphone (Xiaomi Redmi Note 11, Snapdragon 680), inference latency drops to 11 ms and total pipeline latency to 89 ms. We did not conduct analogous inline power measurements on the smartphone, so we do not report smartphone-specific power figures.

Data flow mechanisms govern information transfer between architectural layers through standardized interfaces adhering to interoperability specifications. The acquisition layer streams timestamped sensor packets to the processing layer via RESTful APIs or message queue protocols, ensuring reliable delivery and temporal ordering. Processed feature vectors flow to the inference layer encapsulated in structured data formats compatible with deep learning frameworks. Prediction outputs propagate to the decision layer where risk assessment logic determines appropriate warning actions. User-facing layers receive synthesized information through event-driven notification mechanisms that trigger visual, auditory, or haptic alerts on personal devices31.

Hardware selection criteria prioritize wearability, computational capability, power efficiency, and communication reliability. Wearable sensor units employ miniaturized inertial measurement units and biosignal acquisition circuits integrated into unobtrusive form factors such as smartwatches, chest straps, or insole devices to maximize user acceptance and continuous wearing compliance. Edge computing platforms require sufficient processing capacity to execute model inference within real-time constraints while maintaining extended battery operation through energy-aware scheduling algorithms32. Storage subsystems accommodate local buffering of sensor data and model parameters, with capacity planning addressing retention requirements for historical data analysis and model retraining purposes. Communication modules implement dual-mode connectivity supporting both short-range body area networks and long-range cellular or Wi-Fi links for remote monitoring scenarios, with adaptive protocol selection based on data urgency and network availability.

The modular architecture facilitates iterative system refinement through independent component upgrades without disrupting overall functionality. Sensor arrays can be expanded to incorporate additional measurement modalities, inference engines can be updated with improved model architectures, and intervention protocols can be customized based on clinical evidence and user feedback, ensuring the system evolves to meet emerging requirements in fall prevention care.

Multimodal deep learning prediction model construction

The multimodal deep learning prediction model integrates convolutional neural networks with long short-term memory architectures to capture both spatial patterns within sensor modalities and temporal dynamics across sequential observations. Figure 2 illustrates the network topology, demonstrating the information flow from raw multimodal sensor inputs through hierarchical feature extraction stages to final risk classification outputs. The hybrid architecture exploits the complementary strengths of CNNs for local pattern recognition and LSTMs for long-range temporal dependency modeling, enabling comprehensive representation learning from complex sensor data33.

Architecture of the multimodal CNN-LSTM fusion network with attention mechanism, showing progressive feature abstraction from sensor inputs to risk prediction outputs.

Convolutional layers apply learned filters to extract discriminative local patterns from time-series sensor data, with each filter detecting specific motion signatures or physiological variations indicative of fall risk. The first convolutional stage processes raw sensor channels to identify fundamental motion primitives such as acceleration peaks and rotational patterns. Subsequent convolutional layers build higher-level representations by combining lower-level features into complex motion templates. Max pooling operations reduce temporal resolution while retaining salient features, enhancing computational efficiency and providing translation invariance to minor temporal shifts in gait patterns34.

The LSTM component processes the convolutional feature maps as sequential inputs, maintaining hidden state vectors that accumulate contextual information across time steps. Gating mechanisms regulate information flow, enabling the network to selectively retain relevant historical context while discarding irrelevant variations. Bidirectional LSTM configurations process sequences in both forward and backward temporal directions, capturing dependencies from both past and future contexts to improve prediction accuracy for the current time window.

Real-time warning and intelligent intervention mechanism design

The real-time warning system implements a multi-tiered alert framework that translates continuous fall risk predictions into actionable notifications tailored to risk severity levels. The hierarchical warning structure establishes distinct response protocols corresponding to escalating risk magnitudes, enabling proportionate intervention intensity that balances safety assurance with user autonomy and minimizes alert fatigue from excessive false alarms38. Warning thresholds are calibrated through analysis of the risk score distribution and clinical consultation to ensure sensitivity to genuine hazard situations while maintaining acceptable specificity.

A rule-based decision engine synthesizes multiple information streams to generate comprehensive risk assessments that inform warning triggers. The composite risk evaluation integrates the current prediction model output with temporal trend analysis of recent risk trajectories and contextual factors including time of day, activity type, and environmental conditions39. Experimental results and system performance analysis

Experimental data collection and model training results

Elderly participants were recruited from three community health centers and two senior care facilities in Huai’an, Jiangsu Province, China. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 65 years, (2) ability to walk independently without assistive devices for at least 10 m, (3) Mini-Mental State Examination score ≥ 24, and (4) willingness to wear the sensor system during waking hours for six months. Exclusion criteria included severe visual or hearing impairment, neurological disorders affecting gait (e.g., Parkinson’s disease, stroke with residual hemiparesis), and planned surgical procedures during the study period. A total of 120 participants (68 female, 52 male) completed the study. Their mean age was 72.4 ± 5.8 years (range 65–89), mean body mass index was 24.1 ± 3.2 kg/m², and 73% had at least one chronic condition (hypertension 58%, diabetes 31%, osteoarthritis 27%). Baseline Timed Up and Go scores averaged 11.2 ± 2.9 s, and Berg Balance Scale scores averaged 49.3 ± 4.743,44. All standardized clinical and usability assessment instruments used in this study — namely the Mini-Mental State Examination, the Timed Up and Go test, the Berg Balance Scale, the System Usability Scale, and the five-point Likert-type user satisfaction survey — were adapted from previously published and widely validated versions of these scales, applied without modification to their core validated structures, and used solely for non-commercial research purposes; no new questionnaire instrument was developed for this work, and no clinical or diagnostic claim is made on the basis of these adapted scales.

Each participant wore a custom sensor unit comprising an MPU-9250 inertial measurement unit (triaxial accelerometer ± 16 g, triaxial gyroscope ± 2000°/s) secured at the lower back (L5 vertebra), four FSR-402 force-sensitive resistors embedded in a shoe insole to capture plantar pressure at the heel, first metatarsal, fifth metatarsal, and hallux, and a MAX30102 photoplethysmography module worn on the wrist for continuous heart rate and heart rate variability monitoring. Accelerometer and gyroscope channels were sampled at 100 Hz, plantar pressure at 50 Hz, and heart rate at 1 Hz. All sensors transmitted data via Bluetooth Low Energy 5.0 to a smartphone application, which uploaded recordings to a secure cloud server every 15 min.

Data collection extended over six months per participant, capturing naturalistic activity patterns across diverse environmental contexts including indoor residential settings, outdoor walking paths, and community facilities. Participants maintained activity logs documenting fall incidents, near-fall events, and contextual information regarding activities performed and environmental conditions encountered. Sensor systems continuously recorded data at optimized sampling frequencies, with automatic upload to secure cloud storage servers via wireless communication protocols. Research staff conducted periodic home visits to verify system functionality, address technical issues, and collect supplemental clinical assessments including Timed Up and Go tests, Berg Balance Scale evaluations, and gait speed measurements44.

Dataset construction involved comprehensive preprocessing pipelines to ensure data quality and suitability for model training. Raw sensor signals underwent artifact removal through outlier detection algorithms that identified and corrected measurement anomalies resulting from sensor displacement or wireless transmission errors. Fall risk labeling followed systematic protocols correlating sensor data segments with documented fall incidents, near-fall events, and clinical assessment scores, establishing ground truth classifications for supervised learning. Data augmentation techniques expanded the training corpus through controlled perturbations while maintaining label integrity. To prevent data leakage, partitioning was performed at the participant level rather than the sample level, ensuring that all data windows from a given individual appeared exclusively in one subset. Of the 120 participants, 84 (70%) were randomly assigned to training, 18 (15%) to validation, and 18 (15%) to testing, while preserving similar fall-event proportions across subsets through stratified random allocation based on each participant’s six-month fall count. No participant contributed data to more than one subset. This subject-disjoint design guards against inflated performance estimates that can arise when temporally correlated samples from the same individual appear in both training and test sets45. As a supplementary robustness check, we also conducted leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation on a random subset of 30 participants; the resulting mean accuracy (92.8 ± 2.1%) was consistent with the primary held-out test results, confirming model generalizability across individuals.

The distribution of fall events across subsets reflects the natural imbalance between high-risk and low-risk periods inherent in elderly populations, necessitating specialized loss functions and sampling strategies during training.

Comparative analysis of fall risk prediction performance

We present the full classification results on the independent test set (18 participants, 12,540 windows) with complete transparency regarding class distribution and its statistical implications. Table 5 shows the three-class confusion matrix for the proposed model. Of the 12,540 windows, 12,287 (97.98%) belong to the low-risk class, 247 (1.97%) to medium risk, and only 6 (0.05%) to high risk (fall events). We do not shy away from the fact that this extreme imbalance means the reported overall accuracy of 94.2% is heavily driven by correct classification of the dominant low-risk class. A classifier that predicted “low risk” for every window would achieve 97.98% accuracy, which is actually higher than our model’s overall figure—the difference being that our model trades some majority-class accuracy for improved sensitivity to minority-class events, as intended by the focal loss objective. Because window-level and deployment-level metrics differ in their evaluation unit (individual 2-second windows versus temporally aggregated alert episodes filtered by the rule-based warning engine), readers should exercise caution when comparing the window-level high-risk PPV of 6.25% reported in Table 5 with the deployment-level alert PPV reported in Section Real-time warning system operational feasibility assessment; these numbers answer different questions about system performance.

With only 6 fall-event windows in the test set, a single misclassification shifts sensitivity by approximately 17% points. We therefore urge readers to interpret the sensitivity figure (83.3%, i.e., 5 of 6 correctly detected) as a point estimate with a very wide confidence interval rather than a precise measure of system performance. The McNemar p-values reported in Table 6, while nominally below 0.001 for most comparisons, carry limited persuasive power in this setting: statistical significance on such a small number of minority-class events does not equate to clinical certainty, and the data structure itself does not permit these tests to serve as strong evidence of meaningful performance differences for the high-risk class. The supplementary LOSO cross-validation (92.8 ± 2.1% accuracy, n = 30 participants) and the AUPRC (area under the precision-recall curve) of 0.412 for the high-risk class provide additional, and arguably more informative, measures of minority-class performance under severe imbalance. We report these figures not because they are impressive in absolute terms, but because they are honest.

Comparative benchmarking against alternative architectural approaches offers a relative ranking of the proposed multimodal attention-based fusion network. Table 6 presents a systematic comparison of prediction metrics across five distinct modeling strategies, ranging from traditional machine learning methods to advanced deep learning architectures. Traditional machine learning classifiers employing handcrafted features extracted from sensor signals achieve baseline performance but exhibit limited capacity to capture complex temporal patterns and cross-modal interactions inherent in fall risk dynamics47. Single-modality CNN models processing only accelerometer data extract spatial patterns within individual sensor streams but miss complementary information from other modalities. Single-modality LSTM networks capture temporal dependencies within individual sensor channels yet forgo opportunities for cross-modal feature fusion that enriches risk representations.

The multimodal fusion model without attention mechanisms achieves notable gains over single-modality approaches, supporting the value of integrating heterogeneous sensor information for broader risk characterization48. The proposed attention-enhanced fusion architecture obtains the highest scores across all evaluation metrics in our experiments, suggesting that adaptive weighting of modality contributions based on context can improve prediction consistency. Ablation experiments quantified each modality’s contribution by retraining the full model with one sensor type removed at a time (Table 7). Excluding accelerometer data caused the largest accuracy drop (− 7.8% points), confirming that linear acceleration captures the most discriminative gait and postural features. Removing gyroscope signals reduced accuracy by 4.1 points, primarily affecting detection of turning and rotational instability. Plantar pressure exclusion lowered accuracy by 3.3 points, diminishing sensitivity to weight-shift asymmetries. Heart rate and HRV removal had the smallest individual impact (− 1.9 points); the specific physiological mechanisms underlying this contribution—whether it reflects fatigue, arousal, or some other autonomic process—cannot be determined from the ablation experiment alone and would require targeted physiological validation studies. When all modalities were included, accuracy reached 94.2%, indicating complementary information gains that arise from cross-modal interaction captured by the attention mechanism.

Generalization capability across diverse elderly subpopulations constitutes a critical requirement for clinical deployment. Stratified analysis partitions the test set by age groups, comorbidity profiles, and functional status levels to assess performance consistency across demographic and clinical variations49. The model maintains robust predictive accuracy across younger-old and older-old age brackets, demonstrating effective learning of age-independent risk patterns while adapting to age-specific characteristics. Performance remains stable across participants with varying chronic condition burdens, indicating resilience to comorbidity-related confounders. Functional status stratification reveals consistent prediction capability across mobility spectrum from high-functioning to frail individuals, supporting broad applicability.

Standard classification metrics help characterize the system’s operating profile for fall risk screening, though their interpretation is substantially constrained by the small number of high-risk events in our test set. Sensitivity measures the proportion of actual high-risk cases correctly identified, critical for ensuring that at-risk individuals receive timely notification. Specificity quantifies correct identification of low-risk individuals, relevant for resource allocation and avoidance of unnecessary alerts. Positive predictive value indicates the probability that a high-risk prediction corresponds to genuine elevated fall risk—an area where our system’s window-level performance (PPV = 6.25%) reveals significant room for improvement. Negative predictive value offers reassurance that low-risk classifications are generally reliable. These metrics should be read as characterizations of a pilot-stage system, not as evidence of established diagnostic utility; meaningful performance claims will require validation on datasets with substantially more high-risk events50.

To examine cross-modal weighting behavior, we extracted and visualized the learned attention weights from the eight-head attention layer during three representative activity contexts: steady-state walking, turning, and sit-to-stand transitions. Figure 5 displays the mean attention weight assigned to each sensor channel group (accelerometer, gyroscope, plantar pressure, heart rate/HRV) across the eight heads, averaged over all test-set windows belonging to each activity type. During steady walking, accelerometer and plantar pressure channels received the highest combined weight (0.38 and 0.27, respectively), consistent with the dominance of linear gait dynamics. During turning episodes, gyroscope channels saw a marked increase (from 0.18 in walking to 0.31 in turning), consistent with the relevance of rotational velocity for balance assessment in directional changes. Sit-to-stand transitions exhibited a more distributed pattern, with heart rate and HRV channels receiving their highest weight (0.14, compared to 0.06 during walking). We emphasize that these observations establish only that the attention mechanism assigns different weights to different sensor groups depending on the activity context—they do not, by themselves, establish the physiological mechanisms driving those weight differences. For instance, the elevated cardiac-channel weight during sit-to-stand transitions is consistent with the hypothesis that autonomic responses carry predictive information for postural transitions, but confirming this interpretation would require dedicated physiological studies that isolate cardiac contributions from confounding biomechanical covariates. These patterns do provide empirical support for the narrower claim that early fusion, combined with learned attention, preserves cross-modal relationships that fixed-weight or late-fusion approaches would discard.

Real-time warning system operational feasibility assessment

The real-time warning functionality underwent systematic evaluation to assess its operational characteristics and practical feasibility in naturalistic settings. Warning accuracy metrics characterize the system’s capacity to correctly flag genuine high-risk situations while keeping false alarms within tolerable bounds. The false positive rate captures the frequency of unnecessary alerts triggered during low-risk activities, a critical usability consideration since excessive false alarms lead to alert fatigue and system abandonment51. Conversely, the missed detection rate measures the proportion of actual high-risk episodes that failed to trigger warnings, directly relevant to system safety assurance.

Temporal performance characteristics determine whether the system can operate within the latency constraints required for real-time applications. Response latency encompasses the complete processing pipeline from sensor data acquisition through model inference to warning notification delivery. The system achieved response times well within the temporal constraints necessary for timely notification, enabling users to modify behaviors or activate protective strategies upon receiving an alert. Computational efficiency optimizations including model quantization and edge processing ensure minimal delay between risk detection and alert generation, satisfying the operational timing requirements for practical deployment52.

An important clarification regarding the relationship between device operating time and the observed fall incidence data is warranted. The sensor unit itself is a low-power module (accelerometer, gyroscope, PPG, pressure sensors) that transmits data via Bluetooth Low Energy to a smartphone application, which performs all deep learning inference. The sensor module’s own power draw is modest (approximately 50 mW), and participants charged it overnight using a bedside cradle, giving it sufficient capacity for full waking-hour coverage. The computationally expensive inference runs on the participant’s smartphone, which was separately charged using its own charger. The corrected power figure of 4.3 W reported in Section “Overall system architecture and functional module design” applies only to the Raspberry Pi 4 used during laboratory benchmarking; in the field deployment, the smartphone served as the edge inference platform with pipeline latency of 89 ms. The original manuscript inadvertently conflated the laboratory benchmarking platform (Raspberry Pi) with the field deployment platform (smartphone), creating ambiguity about battery life during actual use.

Wearing compliance of 91.3 ± 5.2% of waking hours, as logged by the smartphone application based on continuous Bluetooth connection timestamps, reflects the proportion of time the sensor unit was both worn and wirelessly connected. It bears repeating that the deployment phase was conceived as an engineering field test, and the fall incidence comparison between groups is an exploratory, secondary observation rather than a pre-specified primary endpoint. The intervention group also received daily risk summary reports and periodic caregiver notifications, any of which could independently influence behavior. We therefore report the 62% lower fall incidence in the intervention group as an association that warrants further investigation, not as evidence of causal efficacy. Moreover, the strongly positive field outcomes in Table 8 (fall incidence reduction, near-falls prevented, high satisfaction scores) may appear difficult to reconcile with the limited reliability of the underlying window-level classifier (PPV = 6.25%). Several factors may partially explain this gap: the rule-based warning engine filters out many window-level false positives before they reach the user; behavioral changes induced by daily risk reports and the awareness of being monitored (a Hawthorne-like effect) could independently reduce fall incidence; and the non-randomized, non-blinded design cannot rule out placebo or expectation effects. Establishing whether real-time warnings themselves prevent falls would require a properly powered, prospectively registered randomized controlled trial with an attention-control arm designed to isolate the contribution of each system component.

User acceptance surveys assess subjective experiences and perceived value that influence long-term adoption and sustained usage. Participants report high satisfaction with system usability, indicating that wearable devices impose minimal burden on daily activities and warning notifications provide actionable guidance without excessive intrusiveness. Elderly users appreciate the sense of security afforded by continuous monitoring and timely alerts, while caregivers value the remote monitoring capabilities that enable proactive support without constant physical presence. Acceptance rates remain elevated throughout the three-month evaluation period, suggesting sustained engagement rather than initial novelty effects53.

Scenario-specific analysis evaluates system robustness across diverse activity contexts and environmental conditions. Indoor navigation scenarios including household ambulation and room transitions demonstrate consistent warning accuracy with appropriate sensitivity to carpet edges, threshold transitions, and furniture obstacles. Outdoor walking contexts exhibit reliable performance despite varying terrain characteristics, lighting conditions, and pedestrian traffic density. Stair climbing and descending situations, representing particularly high-risk activities for elderly individuals, trigger appropriately heightened alert sensitivity while maintaining acceptable specificity. This contextual adaptability validates the system’s capacity to function effectively across the full spectrum of daily living activities rather than being constrained to controlled laboratory environments.

Conclusion

The work presented here shows that fusing data from four complementary wearable sensor modalities through a CNN-LSTM backbone with learned multi-head attention can yield fall risk predictions that surpass those of traditional machine learning, single-modality deep learning, standalone Transformer encoders, and late-fusion alternatives on a naturalistic dataset collected from community-dwelling older adults. On the held-out test set of 18 participants, overall accuracy reached 94.2% with an AUC of 0.967, though these figures must be interpreted with considerable caution: the severe class imbalance (only 6 fall events among 12,540 windows) renders sensitivity, PPV, and associated p-values highly unstable, and the high-risk PPV of 6.25% indicates that the vast majority of high-risk window-level predictions are false positives. We view the principal contribution of this study not as a performance claim but as the transparent demonstration of a reproducible, end-to-end system—from sensor synchronization through edge inference to caregiver notification—that can operate reliably in naturalistic settings with acceptable wearing compliance (91.3% of waking hours) and real-time latency (138 ms on a smartphone). During a three-month field deployment involving 80 participants, the group receiving active alerts recorded 62% fewer falls than the passive-monitoring control group, but we cannot attribute this outcome to the real-time warning mechanism alone given the non-randomized design and the additional intervention components (daily risk summaries, caregiver notifications) that could independently alter behavior; this finding is strictly exploratory. The attention weight visualizations (Fig. 5) confirm that the model assigns different weights to sensor groups depending on activity context, but the physiological mechanisms behind those weight shifts—whether cardiac-channel weights during sit-to-stand transitions truly reflect autonomic contributions to fall risk—remain an open question that our data cannot resolve. Several additional limitations constrain these conclusions: the modest cohort size and single-region recruitment limit demographic generalizability; the corrected whole-board power draw of 4.3 W on the Raspberry Pi 4 yields approximately 8.5 h on a portable battery rather than the 18 h initially reported; the study simultaneously addresses two questions—can the classifier distinguish fall risk, and does the warning system reduce falls—neither of which is fully closed under the current data and design; and the black-box nature of the model, despite preliminary attention-weight visualizations, remains a barrier to clinical trust. Future priorities include multi-site replication with larger and more diverse cohorts, prospectively registered randomized controlled trials incorporating attention-control arms to disentangle causal mechanisms, federated learning for privacy-preserving distributed training, explainable AI techniques offering per-prediction rationale, and cost-effectiveness analysis to inform implementation decisions.

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The work was designed and carried out as a low-risk, non-interventional engineering and technology feasibility evaluation involving only non-invasive wearable sensor monitoring of community-dwelling older adults, with no medical intervention, no diagnostic or therapeutic claims, and no use of investigational drugs or medical devices. Under the institutional research governance policies of HuaiYin Institute of Technology and Nagasaki Institute of Applied Science that apply to non-interventional technology feasibility studies of this kind, formal ethics committee review was not required, and therefore no ethics approval number was issued for this work. The institutional research administration offices of both institutions reviewed the protocol prior to participant enrollment and confirmed that the study fell outside the scope of mandatory Institutional Review Board oversight. Notwithstanding the absence of a requirement for formal ethics approval, all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment in the study, in line with good research practice for studies involving human participants. The research protocol included comprehensive participant information regarding the purpose, procedures, potential risks, benefits, and data handling practices. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. Special consideration was given to the vulnerable nature of the elderly population, ensuring that consent procedures were appropriate for participants’ cognitive abilities and that family members or legal guardians were involved where necessary.

Clinical trial number

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the technical performance of a multimodal sensor fusion system and its deep learning prediction pipeline under real-world operating conditions—specifically, classification accuracy, inference latency, false alarm rate, and system usability. The three-month deployment phase involving 40 intervention and 40 control participants was designed as an engineering field test to assess whether the system could function reliably over an extended period in naturalistic settings, not as a trial intended to establish the clinical efficacy of a therapeutic or preventive intervention. The wearable unit is a research prototype that has not been submitted for medical device certification, and no diagnostic or therapeutic claims were made to participants during the study. Fall incidence data were recorded as part of routine safety monitoring and are reported here as an exploratory, secondary outcome to contextualize system performance; the study was neither designed nor statistically powered to demonstrate a causal intervention effect on fall rates. Under the ICMJE framework, purely observational studies and technology feasibility evaluations in which the health-related outcome is not the primary endpoint driving group assignment do not mandate prospective trial registration. The institutional research administration offices of both participating institutions reviewed this classification at the time of protocol design and confirmed that the engineering field-validation nature of the study did not require prospective clinical trial registration. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that the between-group comparison of fall incidence could be interpreted as resembling an interventional design. To maintain full transparency, we have clearly labeled the fall reduction finding as exploratory and associational throughout the manuscript, and we strongly recommend that any future study seeking to establish the clinical efficacy of the warning system be prospectively registered as a randomized controlled trial before enrollment begins.
