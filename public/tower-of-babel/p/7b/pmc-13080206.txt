Therapeutic drug monitoring and the role of pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics in optimizing beta-lactam antibiotic dosing for deep-seated infections: a systematic review
Authors: Cassandra Vale, Fekade B Sime, Menino O Cotta, Lars Eriksson, Jason A Roberts, Robert Horvath, Mohd H Abdul-Aziz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTherapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) may help optimize beta-lactam antibiotic dosing in patients with deep-seated infections. However, its impact on clinical outcomes remains unclear.ObjectiveThe objective of this review was to evaluate the existing evidence on the role of TDM and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD)-guided optimization of beta-lactam antibiotic dosing in achieving PK/PD targets, and improving clinical outcomes in patients with deep-seated infections.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review of studies reporting on beta-lactam TDM, PK/PD target attainment, and clinical outcomes in adult (≥18 years) patients with confirmed deep-seated infections, including complex bacteraemias with a suspected or proven deep-seated source, infective endocarditis, bone and joint infection, and epidural abscess. The search was conducted using MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, CINAHL and CENTRAL from inception to June 2025.ResultsTwelve studies were included in the final analysis. Considerable variability was observed in PK/PD target definitions and attainment, dosing adjustments and outcome reporting. Eleven of the twelve studies were rated as poor quality on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale due to lack of comparability. Only one study included a non-TDM comparator group and the authors reported no significant difference in clinical outcomes. Three studies showed a trend towards improved clinical outcomes with TDM-guided dosing. TDM frequently led to dose reductions due to concerns of beta-lactam antibiotic toxicity with standard dosing.ConclusionCurrent evidence supporting beta-lactam antibiotic TDM in deep-seated infections is limited by methodological heterogeneity and poor study quality. Well-designed trials are needed to establish the clinical utility of TDM in this setting.

Introduction

Deep-seated infections refer to localized infective foci or collections contained within an organ system and pose significant therapeutic challenges beyond those of most superficial or bloodstream infections.1 Although no universally accepted definition exists, deep-seated infections are broadly understood to include conditions such as infective endocarditis, bone and joint infections, epidural abscesses, and complex bacteraemias of suspected or proven deep-seated source, particularly those caused by pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus spp.2,3 This definition is practical and not intended to cover the full range of infections. Despite anatomical differences, these clinical syndromes commonly require prolonged antibiotic therapy and may present challenges in achieving adequate exposure at the site of infection, particularly in the presence of prosthetic material or implanted devices, impaired vascularity, high bacterial burden, or biofilm formation. Due to the unique nature of deep-seated infections, management is usually multimodal and often requires surgical intervention for adequate source control.

Beta-lactam antibiotics remain central to this management, and patients receiving these antibiotics often require prolonged courses of treatment to ensure successful pathogen eradication. However, most of the data on beta-lactam antibiotic dosing are derived from studies in healthy volunteers and do not account for the significant pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) variability seen in patients with deep-seated infection, including biofilm formation with limited antibiotic penetration and microbial latency of the infective focus.4 Consequently, treatment failure (including relapse), exposure-related toxicity, and antimicrobial resistance are important clinical concerns in the management of these difficult-to-treat infections.

Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is useful to guide the individualization of a patient’s dosages to achieve pre-specified plasma concentrations associated with clinical efficacy and toxicity. While beta-lactam antibiotic TDM is increasingly being integrated into international best practice standards to optimize drug exposure and limit potential toxicity,5–7 particularly in critically ill patients, there is no consensus on the optimal PK/PD targets for beta-lactam antibiotic TDM across a broad range of infections. Much of current clinical practice is therefore driven by anecdotal experience and clinician preference, which relies heavily on extrapolation from small and clinically heterogenous patient datasets. Furthermore, the relationship between target attainment and clinical outcomes is poorly understood.5,8,9 Uncertainty of antibiotic penetration at the site of infection and aforementioned barriers to pathogen susceptibility are likely to further impact on the role of TDM in optimizing beta-lactam antibiotic therapy. The lack of robust data to guide contemporaneous management of these patients is highlighted by a recent systematic review of optimal beta-lactam PK/PD indices in infective endocarditis by Robson et al.10 Current data assessing the impact of TDM on beta-lactam antibiotics dosing are largely based on small studies performed in specific patient populations (e.g. critically ill patients).5,8,11–13 Furthermore, there is discordance in the literature for defining therapeutic success, with varying emphasis on microbiological eradication, clinical cure, or other patient-centred outcomes such as mortality, making comparison of outcome data across studies difficult.14

We performed this systematic review to evaluate the role of beta-lactam antibiotic TDM in achieving PK/PD targets for therapeutic efficacy and improving clinical outcomes in patients with deep-seated infections.

Methods

The protocol for this systematic review was registered with the PROSPERO database (CRD42024533989). The review was performed and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) statement.15 Ethical approval was not necessary, as only previously published data were included.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All primary study types that were available at the time of the search were considered for inclusion. Studies were eligible if they involved adult human participants (>18 years) receiving beta-lactam antibiotics and underwent TDM or reported concentration data during treatment for a confirmed deep-seated infection. For the purpose of this review, deep-seated infections were defined as infective endocarditis, bone and joint infections, epidural abscess, or complex bacteraemia associated with a suspected or confirmed deep focus of infection. For bacteraemia, we considered studies to reflect complicated or persistent infection, particularly those involving Staphylococcus or Enterococcus species,1,2 rather than uncomplicated catheter-related bacteraemia or bacteraemia secondary to superficial infection (e.g. skin or soft tissue infection). Where the bacteraemia source/focus was not described, this was recorded as ‘not reported’. These clinical syndromes were grouped because they commonly require prolonged antibiotic therapy and may present shared challenges in achieving adequate exposure at the site of infection, particularly in the presence of prosthetic material or implanted devices, impaired vascularity, high bacterial burden, or biofilm formation. Studies were excluded if they were conference abstracts16 were not published in English, did not include beta-lactam therapy, reported only in vitro data or included patients with suspected but unconfirmed infections.

Search strategy

A systematic search of MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, CINAHL, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) databases from inception to June 2025 was performed. A search strategy was developed with the support of a research librarian (LE) (Text S1, available as data at JAC Online). The search strategy involved a combination of key words that included ‘therapeutic drug monitoring’, ‘beta-lactam antibiotics’, ‘deep-seated infection’ or ‘prosthetic joint infection’ or ‘bone OR joint infection’ or ‘infective endocarditis’ or ‘epidural abscess’ or ‘spinal abscess’ and ‘bacteraemia’. Manual searches of reference lists of included studies were also undertaken to identify additional studies. Two review authors (CV, MHAA) independently screened the titles and abstracts of identified references using the Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation). A subsequent full text review of relevant references was undertaken independently by both review authors (CV, MHAA) to identify studies that fulfilled the inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or by a third reviewer, if necessary (MOC).

Data extraction and analysis

Extraction of relevant data was independently performed by two review authors (CV, MHAA) using a standardized data collection form. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or by a third reviewer, if necessary (MOC). Available data including study characteristics, study design, study population demographic and clinical characteristics, study beta-lactam antibiotic and dosing details, TDM sampling strategy and drug concentration-related data (e.g. measured total concentrations, measured free concentrations or estimated concentrations), PK/PD targets and how these were defined (e.g. measured MIC, ECOFF or concentration-only thresholds), and study outcomes were recorded. Where reported, the method of MIC determination was also extracted. Given that only one included study had a non-TDM comparator group and there was substantial heterogeneity in study design and outcome definitions, a quantitative meta-analysis was not performed.

Risk of bias assessment

The quality of each included study was independently assessed by two review authors (CV, MHAA) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for Quality Assessment of Cohort Studies.17 The scale evaluates the risk of bias across three domains (selection, comparability, and outcomes). Each study is rated as either none, one or two stars in eight assessment questions, with four stars for selection, two stars for comparability and three stars for outcomes, up to a maximum score of nine stars. The Agency for Healthcare and Research Quality (ARHQ) cutoffs were used to determine the overall quality of each study: good quality (3–4 stars for selection AND 1–2 stars for comparability AND 2–3 stars for outcome), fair quality (2 stars for selection AND 1–2 stars for comparability AND 2–3 stars for outcomes), poor quality (0–1 stars for selection OR 0 stars for comparability OR 0–1 stars for outcome). Disagreements were resolved by consensus or by a third reviewer, if necessary (MOC). Given the inclusion of multiple infection syndromes (e.g. infective endocarditis, bone and joint infection, epidural abscess, and bacteraemia), variation in outcome definitions across studies was expected. Outcome heterogeneity was therefore considered a limitation for study comparison and synthesis rather than a source of bias in itself.

Results

Study selection and study characteristics

The study selection process is summarized in Figure 1. A total of 369 records were identified through the search strategy. After removing 36 duplicates, 307 records were further excluded during screening for not meeting the inclusion criteria based on patient population or antibiotic type. The full texts of 26 studies were assessed and a further 14 were excluded due to absence of deep-seated infection in the patient population.

PRISMA flowchart of search strategy and included studies.

A heterogeneous group of 12 observational cohort studies evaluating the role of beta-lactam antibiotic TDM or PK/PD-guided dosing in patients with deep-seated infections between 2005 and 2023 were included in the final review (Table 1). Of these, only one retrospective study directly compared TDM with a non-TDM comparator group.21 The remaining 11 studies (four prospective and seven retrospective) reported outcomes exclusively in patients who underwent TDM while receiving beta-lactam therapy.18–20,22–29 Study cohorts ranged from 10 to 322 patients who were recruited across Europe, the United States, and New Zealand. The duration of follow up, where reported, ranged from 28 days to 53 months. Study beta-lactam antibiotics included amoxicillin, ampicillin, aztreonam, benzylpenicillin, cefazolin, cefepime, ceftazidime, ceftazidime/avibactam, ceftobiprole, ceftolozane/tazobactam, ceftriaxone, cloxacillin, flucloxacillin, imipenem and meropenem. To aid interpretation given the clinical heterogeneity across infection syndromes, we summarized study characteristics and key findings by infection type in Table 1. Eight studies reported beta-lactam TDM and outcomes in specific cohorts with deep-seated infections caused by any organism, including blood stream infections,26 infective endocarditis,18–21 and bone and joint infections.23–25 The remaining four studies reported exclusively on methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA)27,29 and Enterococcus spp.22,28 deep-seated infections.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

All of the studies demonstrated a selected cohort representative of the intervention population. Only one study demonstrated comparability across intervention (TDM) and non-intervention cohorts,21 while all other studies received zero for comparability. Quality of outcome assessments varied significantly between all of the studies, primarily due to incomplete or inconsistent documentation and follow up. Given the observational nature and absence of a comparator group in most of our included studies, only one study met the criteria of good quality, whilst the remaining eleven studies were deemed to be of poor quality (Table 2).21

PK/PD sampling strategies and beta-lactam antibiotic dosing

Most studies used trough or steady-state (for continuous infusion) beta-lactam concentrations for PK/PD monitoring, but three studies reported mid-dose concentrations,19,27,29 and one reported peak concentrations.26 Only three studies measured free (or unbound) beta-lactam antibiotic concentrations.19,27,29 Four studies did not specify whether the measured concentrations were total or free beta-lactam concentrations.18,20,22,23 Five studies used published protein binding values to estimate free concentrations.21,24–26,28 Only one study measured beta-lactam antibiotic concentrations in both plasma and the target tissue.23 However, this was performed in only a small subset of patients, and the authors were unable to comment on any association between tissue and plasma concentrations or further explore any potential relationship with patient outcomes.

Actual MICs of the causative organisms were measured and reported in seven studies.18,19,21,24,26–28 Due to the retrospective nature of the analyses, one study used a combination of measured MICs and epidemiological cut-off values (ECOFFs) published by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST).20,30 The method of MIC determination varied across most of the studies and was inconsistently reported (Table 1). Due to this limitation, MIC-based PK/PD targets and estimates of PK/PD target attainment are not directly comparable across studies and targets were summarized descriptively according to whether they were based on measured MIC, ECOFF, or concentration-only threshold.

Five studies reported the use of intermittent infusion dosing of beta-lactam antibiotics,18,19,26,27,29 four reported the use of continuous infusions,23–25,28 and three reported the use of a combination of administration methods.20–22 TDM-guided dosing adjustments were undertaken and evaluated in 10 studies and were mostly at clinician’s discretion and inconsistently documented. PK/PD targets for TDM in the included studies were heterogenous, ranging from arbitrary and unvalidated ranges to more patient-focused targets, and are further discussed below.

TDM and PK/PD targets

Absence of a defined target

Tattevin et al. reported TDM of amoxicillin and cloxacillin in 15 patients with infective endocarditis without specifying a target PK/PD range.18 Dosing adjustments were made in approximately 50% of patients, but were incompletely documented. In a retrospective cohort study, Dorel et al. assessed the clinical impact of amoxicillin TDM in 206 patients with infective endocarditis.21 This was the only comparative study published at the time of our review, comparing outcomes in 114 patients who received TDM against those in 92 patients who did not. An arbitrary target range for amoxicillin TDM (20–80 mg/L) was used, which was not linked to the combination of measured MICs (method not specified) and ECOFF values used in the analysis. In the TDM group, 146 dosing adjustments were made in 76 patients (67%), with 12 patients (10%) receiving a dose reduction and 23 patients (20%) receiving dose increase. In contrast, only 16 dosing adjustments were made in 11 patients (12%) in the non-TDM group.

Generic targets

Two studies used pre-specified but generic beta-lactam TDM targets.20,23 Zeller et al. reported TDM of continuous infusion of cefazolin in 100 patients with bone and joint infections.23 A steady-state plasma concentration between 40 to 70 mg/L was used in this study, based on the reported poor penetration of cefazolin into bone tissue (4%–18%) and evidence suggesting plasma trough concentrations of 4×MIC are associated with treatment success in chronic osteomyelitis.31 Dosing adjustments were made in 47 (47%) patients, including 9 dose increases and 38 dose reductions. Macheda et al. evaluated the impact of TDM for four beta-lactam antibiotics (amoxicillin, cefazolin, ceftriaxone, and cloxacillin) in 306 patients with infective endocarditis.20 Regardless of the beta-lactam antibiotic used, a trough or steady-state plasma concentration range between 20 to 50 mg/L was targeted in this study to account for a presumed 20% to 40% antibiotic penetration into cardiac vegetation. Dosing adjustments were performed in 166/322 (51.5%) cases with dose reductions accounting for the majority (140/322; 43.4%).

MIC-related targets

Eight studies reported the use of patient-specific PK/PD targets based on the pathogen’s MIC, either measured or extrapolated cut-off values (ECOFF).19,21,22,24–29 Öbrink-Hansen et al. evaluated PK/PD target attainment in 46 patients receiving benzylpenicillin for infective endocarditis.19 Two targets, 50% time that free (unbound) drug concentrations remain above the measured MIC (e-test) during a dosing interval (fT > MIC) and 100% fT > MIC, were used, which were achieved in 43 (96%) and 32 (71%) patients, respectively. Dosing adjustments were not undertaken and reported. Al-Shaer et al. evaluated beta-lactam antibiotic PK/PD target attainment in 206 critically ill patients with bloodstream infections using measured MIC (Vitek and e-test).26 The primary PK/PD targets of 100% fT > MIC and 100% free (unbound drug) time four times above the MIC (fT > 4×MIC) were achieved in 183 (89%) and 148 (72%) patients, respectively. TDM-guided dose increases were undertaken in 12 patients, of which 4 subsequently achieved 100% fT > MIC and 2 achieved 100% fT > 4×MIC.

Goncette et al. evaluated a more aggressive target of 100% fT > 4–5×MIC in 10 patients receiving continuous infusion of ceftazidime/avibactam for multi-drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa or Klebsiella pneumoniae infections,25 using measured MIC (BMD and e-test). However, only two of these patients fit the definition of a deep-seated infection and both of these achieved the study’s PK/PD targets. The authors also aimed for target tissue concentrations of 100% fT > 4–5×MIC, but used plasma-to-tissue penetration ratios to extrapolate target attainment rather than measured tissue concentrations. Gomez-Junyent et al.24 evaluated a similarly aggressive target of 100% fT > 3–10×MIC receiving continuous beta-lactam antibiotics infusions in 52 patients with Pseudomonas aeruginosa osteoarticular infections. There were 17 dose adjustments undertaken in 12/52 (23%) patients, with 10 dose reductions in 8 patients and 7 dose increases in 4 patients. Measured MICs were used, although the method of determination was not specified. Moser et al. prospectively evaluated PK/PD target attainment in 50 patients with MSSA bloodstream infections receiving standard flucloxacillin dosing regimens (2 g 4 to 6 hourly).27 Two targets, 100% fT > MIC and 100% fT > 4×MIC, were used, which were achieved in 45/50 (90%) and 34/50 (68%) patients, respectively. Measured MICs (Vitek, e-test and BMD) were utilized. Dosing adjustments were not undertaken and reported. Gatti et al. assessed ampicillin PK/PD target attainment in a cohort of 12 patients with Enterococcal bloodstream infections, with or without concomitant infective endocarditis.28 A total of 36 dosing adjustments were made at the clinician’s discretion to achieve a target of 100% fT > 4–8×MIC. Of these, 20 (55.6%) were dose reductions and resulted in 11/12 patients (91.7%) requiring an ampicillin dose reduction of >50% during the course of therapy. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, a combination of measured MIC (method unspecified) and ECOFF were used. Campbell et al. prospectively assessed PK/PD target attainment in 100 patients receiving standard dosing regimens of cefazolin or flucloxacillin for the treatment of invasive MSSA infections.29 The primary target was 50% fT > 2×MIC, using measured MIC (disk diffusion), which was achieved in 85% of patients. Giuliano et al. evaluated ampicillin and ceftobiprole TDM in 21 Enterococcus faecalis invasive infections, aiming for maximum percentage of time above measured MIC.22 Specific dosing adjustments and PK/PD target attainment were not reported in the final analysis.

TDM and patient outcomes

Studies without TDM-guided dosing adjustments

Three of the twelve studies reported beta-lactam antibiotic concentrations without performing dosing adjustments. Moser et al.27 reported a 30-day mortality of 16% (32 patients) in their 50 patient MSSA cohort, and it was observed that mortality was higher when targeting 100% fT > MIC using ECOFF values over measured MIC (5/13, 39% versus 3/37, 8%; P = 0.02), although this difference was not statistically significant when adjusted for age and disease severity. Acute kidney injury occurred in 22/50 (44%) and neurotoxicity in 16/50 (32%), only the latter being correlated with excessive flucloxacillin concentrations (defined as 100% fT > 10×ECOFF). Öbrink-Hansen et al. reported one infection relapse but did not comment on any other clinical outcomes.19 Campbell et al. did not report on any outcome data.29

Studies with TDM-guided dosing adjustments

Without specifying a target PK/PD range, Tattevin et al. performed TDM to guide amoxicillin and cloxacillin dosing in cases of suspected intolerance or lack of efficacy.18 Dose reductions were made in 7/15 patients (47%), all of whom demonstrated subsequent improvement in their renal function. Although clinical cure was not clearly defined in the study, it was reported in four patients (27%).

Eight studies utilized TDM-guided dosing adjustments to achieve specified PK/PD targets. In the study by Zeller et al., 82 of 88 patients (93%) treated with cefazolin for bone and joint infections achieved probable (12 months) or proven (24 months) clinical cure, while one patient had infection-related mortality.23 In a cohort of 306 patients with infective endocarditis, Macheda et al. demonstrated that patients with beta-lactam concentrations of >50 mg/L, including amoxicillin, cloxacillin, cefazolin and ceftriaxone, had significantly longer hospital stays (25 days versus 20 days, P = 0.04), and reported 48 patients (16%) died during the study.20 In a cohort of 206 critically ill patients with bloodstream infections, Al-Shaer et al. showed that achieving 100% fT > MIC with ampicillin, cefazolin, cefepime, ceftriaxone, meropenem or piperacillin/tazobactam was associated with higher rates of clinical cure (OR 3.0, P = 0.0303)) and microbiological eradication (OR 2.6, P = 0.0476), and 100% fT > 4×MIC was associated with reduced emergence of resistance (OR 0.2, P = 0.0043).26 Longer duration to first TDM sample was associated with clinical failure (OR 0.9, P = 0.0072), prolonged ICU stays (P < 0.0001), increased in-hospital mortality (OR 1.1, P = 0.0178) and 30-day mortality (OR 1.1, P = 0.0387). Overall, 146 patients (71%) achieved clinical cure, defined as resolution of infective symptoms at the completion of treatment, and 35 patients (17%) died during their hospital stay. Goncette et al. reported one death and one clinical cure at 12-months, in the two patients with deep-seated infections included in their 10 patient cohort receiving ceftazidime/avibactam.25 Gomez-Junyent et al.24 reported a 18.4% treatment failure rate, defined as patients requiring implant removal or further debridement, relapsing symptoms during the 12 month follow up, or death, in 49 of their 52 patient cohort. Adverse events occurred in 11 patients (21%), although exact causation was difficult to establish as 10 of 19 patients within the study concomitantly received colistin for fluoroquinolone-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Gatti et al. did not report any persistent bacteraemias, despite 11 of 12 patients undergoing TDM-guided ampicillin dose reductions in their cohort.28 The three patients who died at by the 90 day follow up (25%) were documented as having underlying comorbidities. Giuliano et al. reported microbiological cure, defined as negative follow up blood or valve cultures, in 18 patients (86%) receiving ampicillin and ceftobiprole, but did not describe PK/PD target attainment in their cohort.22 The 28-day mortality rate was reported as 3/21 (14%).

Dorel et al. reported that patients who underwent TDM received significantly lower total amoxicillin doses during their treatment.21 However, no statistically significant differences were observed in clinical outcomes between groups, including relapse (2%), acute kidney injury (AKI) (20%) and encephalopathy (5%), which the authors attributed to the retrospective nature of the study. In-hospital (7%) and 12-month (16%) mortality were lower in the TDM group when compared with the non-TDM group (10% and 23%, respectively). Although 12-month mortality was significantly lower in the TDM group, this difference was not confirmed by survival analysis (HR 1.888 [0.995–3.565], P = 0.052).

TDM and infection sub-group

Infective endocarditis

Infective endocarditis (n = 5) was the best represented sub-group, but studies varied substantially in PK/PD target definitions, sampling strategies, dose adjustment practices, and outcome reporting.18–22 Dorel et al.21 and Macheda et al.20 showed that beta-lactam TDM was feasible in this setting and frequently led to dose modification when aiming for a predefined target TDM range, most commonly dose reduction, but comparator data were limited and no clear treatment effect could be determined.

Bone and joint infections

Evidence for bone and joint infection was limited to two retrospective studies23,25 and one prospective study.24 Zeller et al.23 reported frequent dose adjustment and high cure rates with prolonged continuous cefazolin infusion, while Gomez-Junyent et al.24 reported less frequent dose adjustments and higher treatment failure rates. Goncette et al. included only two deep-seated bone/joint cases and inferred tissue target attainment indirectly from plasma concentrations.25 Overall, heterogeneity in study design and reporting prevented meaningful comparison.

Complex bacteraemia

The bacteraemia subgroup (n = 4) was the most heterogeneous, with variation in source classification, causative organism, PK/PD targets, and outcome reporting,26–29 Al-Shaer et al.,26 Moser et al.,27 and Campbell et al.,29 reported high PK/PD target attainment in parts of their cohorts, and Al-Shaer et al.,26 also suggested an association between target attainment and improved clinical or microbiological outcomes. However, mixed cohorts and inconsistent reporting of bacteraemia source/focus limited interpretation of bacteraemia as a uniform deep-seated subgroup.

Discussion

This review demonstrates the considerable variation in PK/PD targets for management of deep-seated infections, ranging from broad, concentration-only targets based on assumed ECOFF values and unvalidated toxicity thresholds to individualized measured MIC-based concentration targets. Seven studies implemented TDM-guided dosing adjustments to achieve PK/PD targets, but target attainment was inconsistently reported. Four studies extrapolated free beta-lactam concentrations from population protein binding values to estimate PK/PD target attainment, despite the known variability of protein binding in deep-seated infections. Although infective endocarditis was the best represented sub-group (n = 5), meaningful sub-group analyses were not possible because study numbers remained small and heterogeneity was substantial even within the same infection syndrome. This was most apparent in bone and joint infection, where implant status and source control were variably described, and in bacteraemia, where source/focus reporting was inconsistent. Differences in beta-lactam antibiotic dosing regimens, sampling strategies, PK/PD targets and dose adjustment practices further limited comparability. Collectively, these findings support the view that TDM in deep-seated infections should not be approached as a ‘one-size-fits-all’ strategy and that future studies should evaluate syndrome-specific targets.

Consistent with previous observations by Mangalore et al.32 and Gulyas et al.5 in critically ill patients, definitions of clinical endpoints also varied widely in this review. Outcome data were inconsistently documented, and no single outcome measure was reported consistently across all studies. Even where clinical endpoints were reported, significant differences in definition and data collection precluded validity of comparison. Clinical cure was more frequently reported in studies that undertook TDM-guided dosing adjustments (five of nine studies) whereas none of the three studies without TDM-guided adjustments reported clinical cure data. Definitions of clinical cure varied vastly from symptom resolution at treatment completion to clinical, biological and radiological resolution of infection at 24-months. Mortality was reported in eight studies, but time-points varied considerably from in-hospital mortality to 12-month mortality. Concerns regarding beta-lactam antibiotic toxicity at standard dosing was a common theme across most studies, as reflected by the frequency of TDM-guided dose reductions undertaken. However, definitions of beta-lactam antibiotic related adverse effects or toxicity were inconsistent, causation often not established and less than half of the studies reported on this outcome.

Beta-lactam antibiotics have long been the cornerstone of therapy for deep-seated infections. Their time-dependent bactericidal activity is well established, with efficacy dependent on the duration that the free (unbound) drug concentration remains above the MIC of the causative organism.6,33,34 Traditional and conservative targets of 40–70%fT > MIC were based on in vitro data,35,36 but recent guidance favors more aggressive targets of 100%fT > MIC up to 100%fT > 4–5xMIC in critically ill patients.11,32,37 Most available data on beta-lactam antibiotic TDM are derived from these critically ill populations, who experience the most profound derangements in physiology and are therefore hypothesized to benefit most from TDM.13,33 Two recent meta-analyses in critically ill patients demonstrated that longer time within target PK/PD ranges was associated with moderate improvements in clinical and microbiological cure.32,38 A subsequent meta-analysis by Gulyas et al. reported improved clinical cure (OR 2.22; 95% CI 1.78–2.76; I2 = 0%) and microbiological eradication (OR 1.71; 95% CI 1.05–2.80; I2 = 0%) when TDM was used to guide beta-lactam antibiotic dosing in critically ill patients.5 However, this benefit did not extend to other patient-focused clinical outcomes, with no significant effect on hospital length of stay (MD 0.61; 95% CI − 3.65 to 4.87; I2 = 45%), in-hospital mortality (OR 0.73; 95% CI 0.49–1.09; I2 = 79%) or 28-day mortality (OR 0.75; 95% CI 0.11–5.01; I2 = 79%).5

However, the relevance of these PK/PD targets to empirical therapy remains uncertain, particularly when the causative pathogen and MIC are unknown or when ECOFF values are used instead of measured MICs.33,34,39 Use of ECOFF values is common in clinical practice due to laboratory resourcing, and these are often much higher than measured MICs, potentially leading to unnecessarily high target concentrations and increased risk of toxicity.40 Further complicating this, toxicity thresholds for individual beta-lactam antibiotics remain poorly defined,6,33 making it difficult to establish safe and effective therapeutic targets for TDM. Lack of rigorous definition of beta-lactam toxicity, particularly in the context of severe illness, often precludes accurate identification and causation of these outcomes. The mechanisms of beta-lactam antibiotic toxicity remain poorly understood, and to-date a clear exposure-response relationship between beta-lactam antibiotic concentration and most toxicity syndromes have not been demonstrated.41 Toxicity thresholds of beta-lactam concentrations >8–10×MIC have been proposed but remain unvalidated, and likely vary by individual agent.6,41 These proposed thresholds also raise concerns about applying a concentration that is MIC-related, relative to the inherent susceptibility of the microbe, rather than targeting microbe-independent or drug-specific parameters.41

Although trends towards improved outcomes with beta-lactam antibiotic TDM have been observed in clinically and microbiologically heterogeneous populations of critically ill patients,5,6,32 deep-seated infections present unique challenges for effective beta-lactam antibiotic dosing optimization. The PK/PD of beta-lactam antibiotics are known to be altered by extreme pathophysiological changes associated with severe infection, commonly including changes in volume of distribution and clearance.4,6 In infective endocarditis, hypoalbuminaemia is common and is likely to significantly affect the free fraction of highly protein-bound beta-lactam agents.42 Extrapolation of population-based protein binding values is common in practice, but may misrepresent free beta-lactam antibiotic concentrations in this patient population, leading to inaccurate assessment of PK/PD target attainment.

These known PK and PK/PD variations are further complicated by the unique way in which causative pathogens in deep-seated infections can adapt and persist within challenging tissue environments. Microbial adhesion to vascular surfaces is a common virulence feature of deep-seated infections. The presence of prosthetic material and biofilm are implicated in the development of chronic and difficult-to-treat infections,43,44 particularly in Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus species bacteraemias and endovascular infection. In infective endocarditis, the formation of a discrete cardiac vegetation of high-density, latent or stationary growth phase microbes on poorly vascularized endocardium creates challenges for both the penetration and the activity of beta-lactam antibiotics. These latent, high-density vegetation-associated microorganisms can also have a much higher MIC than cultured, circulating organisms.10,45,46 Similar challenges are encountered in the management of epidural abscesses, and bone and prosthetic joint infections, where impaired tissue vascularity or the presence of completely avascular prosthetic materials restrict antibiotic access and enable microbial persistence through biofilm formation and immune evasion.47,48 Collectively, these findings suggest that the role of TDM in deep-seated infections is likely to be syndrome-dependent and context-specific, rather than directly extrapolated from critically ill populations alone.

Although animal models suggest reasonable correlation between plasma and target tissue concentrations for certain antibiotics,49,50 it remains unclear whether ‘classical’ PK/PD indices based on plasma concentrations and conventional MIC (e.g. % fT > MIC) fully reflect antibiotic activity in biofilm-associated infection, which is common in device-associated and chronic deep-seated infections; and whether plasma concentrations serve as a reliable surrogate for tissue exposure in humans remains to be established.10 Biofilm growth can reduce antibiotic susceptibility and often necessitates higher exposures than predicted by MIC alone, a phenomenon better represented by the minimum biofilm eradication concentration (MBEC).51,52 Pre-clinical studies have suggested that beta-lactam antibiotic activity in biofilm-associated infections may be influenced by both the ‘time’ and ‘concentration’, rather than ‘time’ alone.52 This challenges the strict time-dependent kill characteristics for beta-lactam antibiotic in such infections and supports strategies that maximize sustained drug exposure (e.g. prolonged infusion). Additionally, standard MIC-based targets may require refinement for selected biofilm-associated infections. This raises important concerns regarding how best to define, measure, and achieve effective antibiotic concentrations at the true site/s of infection.53 Challenges include the wide heterogeneity of infection sites and causative organisms, and are further compounded by polymicrobial infection, the presence of prosthetic material or avascular tissue, biofilm formation, and variability in the feasibility and adequacy of surgical source control. Deep-seated infections often involve many different sites (e.g. endocarditis, bone and joint infection), which affects tissue penetration and means that plasma concentrations may not reliably reflect concentrations at the infection site/s. Treatment of deep-seated infection is often prolonged and may involve combination antibiotics and more than one organism, which makes it difficult to link outcomes to a single drug and PK/PD target. Importantly, clinical response is also strongly influenced by timely and adequate surgical/source control (e.g. drainage, debridement, and device removal), which is difficult to standardize across studies. Together, these factors contribute to the uncertainty and heterogeneity in PK/PD targets for deep-seated infections used in clinical practice.

Overall, the current evidence does not support routine beta-lactam antibiotic TDM for all patients with deep-seated infections. However, TDM should be considered in selected high-risk scenarios where sub-optimal exposure is more likely and the consequences of treatment failure are high. These include critically ill patients, patients with augmented renal clearance or rapidly changing renal function, patients receiving prolonged antibiotic therapy (particularly when targeting higher exposures), infections involving prosthetic material or other biofilm-associated infections, and when toxicity is a concern. Where TDM is used, it most useful when linked to a pre-defined exposure target with a clear dosing adjustment approach. The primary contribution of this review is not to define a universal PK/PD target, but to highlight that the current literature remains too heterogeneous to support a single target and to identify priorities for future research, including clinical comparative studies, syndrome-specific targets, and standardized outcome reporting.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. First, study inclusion was limited to English-language studies, potentially excluding relevant data.

Second, the overall quality of the included studies was poor, characterized by small, heterogeneous populations and inconsistent study designs. In addition, PK/PD targets were difficult to compare across studies because measured MIC values were available in only a minority of studies, MIC determination methods were variably reported, and some studies used ECOFF or assumed MIC values instead. Third, outcome data and reporting were inconsistent across studies and almost all of the studies were underpowered to detect clinically significant patient outcomes. Fourth, the retrospective design of the majority of studies limits the generalizability of findings to contemporary practice. Fifth, of the three prospective studies, none implemented a structured approach to apply TDM-guided dosing or reported sufficient outcome data to assess the impact of the intervention. Sixth, the absence of control or comparator groups significantly limits the ability to determine the effect of beta-lactam antibiotic TDM on the clinical outcomes. Finally, our definition of deep-seated infection was necessarily pragmatic rather than exhaustive. While our search strategy was comprehensive, other infection syndromes that may also be considered deep-seated are not represented in the current beta-lactam TDM literature and therefore were not captured in this review. In addition, several included studies reported bacteraemia source/cause only in broad or mixed categories, where multiple causes were represented and outcomes were not reported separately by infection sub-group. Some studies also included small numbers of skin and soft tissue infections. As a result, stricter exclusion of all non-deep-seated cases and more precise sub-group interpretation were not always possible.

Conclusion

While TDM holds promise in optimizing treatment with beta-lactam antibiotics generally, there is much to be elucidated around how, where and what it will provide the most benefit for. Currently, there is a lack of robustly validated data to recommend routine use of TDM in optimizing beta-lactam antibiotic therapy, particularly in the context of deep-seated infections. Three key elements remain to be addressed regarding the role of beta-lactam TDM in the management of deep-seated infection. Firstly, how plasma beta-lactam antibiotic concentrations correlate to target tissue concentrations, and whether plasma TDM is an appropriate surrogate marker of adequate target tissue penetration. Secondly, what definitions, clinical endpoints and outcomes are appropriate for monitoring and determining clinical success with beta-lactam therapy in this high-risk population. Lastly, what PK/PD targets and attainment will address these first two elements, whilst also avoiding beta-lactam overexposure and potential toxicity or development of antimicrobial resistance and treatment failure associated with underexposure. Future research needs to be well-designed and in the format of a prospective, randomized, controlled trial that would provide benchmarking and assessment of the role of beta-lactam TDM as an intervention.
