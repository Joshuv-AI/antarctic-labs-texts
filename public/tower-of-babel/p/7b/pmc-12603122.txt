Dynamic differential privacy technique for deep learning models
Authors: Emad Elabd
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep learning is a field within artificial intelligence that uses large datasets to train models capable of recognizing patterns and making predictions. One of the serious challenges facing model creators during data training is preserving the privacy of the data. Adversaries use membership inference attack to expose the privacy of the data used in training the model. They identify whether a specific data point was used in the training process or not. To protect these models against this type of attack, differential privacy approach can be used. Differential privacy involves adding noise to the training weights during the data training phase according to a specific probability distribution. At every step during the data training process, a fixed amount of noise is consistently added to the training weights. This paper utilizes a modified version of the differential privacy technique to defend against membership inference attacks. The new algorithm does not add regular noise to the training weights in each step but it adds it randomly during training process. Adding noise randomly increases the randomness in training phase and decrease the chances of prediction in the case of an attack. The model performance is evaluated using a set of metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and the privacy budget (). The results demonstrate that the Gaussian Randomized Noise Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (Gaussian RanN-DP-SGD) approach consistently outperforms other standard Differential Privacy (DP) methods across accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. Regarding privacy preservation, the Gaussian RanN-DP-SGD method achieves the most favorable privacy-utility trade-off, maintaining a satisfactory balance between model utility and user privacy. Introduction

The world is undergoing a revolution in artificial intelligence (AI), where AI has become essential for solving problems and completing tasks that were once difficult to achieve. Applications of AI span across all fields, including medicine, engineering, industry, and economics. Among the most important branches of AI is machine learning (ML). Deep learning is a subclass of ML built on neural networks. Deep learning models are built and trained on real data to adjust their parameters, then tested and deployed to predict output based on new inputs. Despite their widespread adoption, these models face risks, such as privacy breaches, especially through attacks that reveal the identities of individuals whose data were used in training. The membership inference attack (MIA) is a prominent example in which an attacker tries to determine if specific data were included in the dataset used to train the model1.

Membership inference attack (MIA)

A membership inference attack is a type of ML privacy breach in which an attacker tries to determine if an individual’s data is used to train the ML model. This is done by analyzing the model’s responses to specific inputs, particularly those related to the individual in question, and assessing whether the model’s behavior indicates familiarity with those data. Shokri et al. introduced this method in1, defining it as a classification problem by studying how the model behaves when exposed to both familiar and unfamiliar data. They also developed the concept of shadow training, in which “shadow models” are trained to simulate the behavior of the target model. Known datasets are used to train these shadow models, allowing the attack model, a binary classifier, to learn the patterns between inputs and outputs, ultimately revealing whether a certain data point was used to train the model.

Several defense techniques have been proposed to mitigate MIA, each with its own trade-offs. One commonly used method is differential privacy2, which adds noise to the model parameters, making it more challenging for attackers to determine whether specific data was included in the training phase. This noise introduces uncertainty into the model’s predictions, thereby masking individual data points.

Another approach involves using Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)3, to produce synthetic data that closely resembles real data. By training models on this synthetic data, the risk of privacy breaches is reduced. Model distillation4 offers a different solution by training a smaller, simpler model that retains much of the original model’s performance but is less susceptible to membership inference attacks.

While these techniques can be helpful in mitigating the risks of membership inference attacks, they also introduce certain trade-offs. For instance, differential privacy may decrease model accuracy as a result of adding noise, while GANs and model distillation may increase computational demands or reduce the model’s complexity, potentially affecting its performance. Choosing the appropriate defense strategy requires careful consideration of these trade-offs, balancing the need for privacy with the impact on model accuracy and efficiency.

As AI continues to advance, particularly in sensitive fields like healthcare, finance, and autonomous systems, the ability to manage these trade-offs becomes increasingly important to ensure both data security and effective model performance.

Differential privacy (DP)

Differential privacy is a cutting-edge method for safeguarding data privacy, known for its superior ability to prevent privacy breaches. Unlike traditional methods like k-Anonymity, de-identification, and aggregation, which can be susceptible to advanced attacks5, differential privacy has gained widespread acceptance, with companies like Google and Apple adopting it as a standard6. It works by embedding noise into data analysis results, effectively masking the influence of any individual’s data on the aggregate result. This noise is meticulously calibrated to achieve a balance between privacy protection and data accuracy. It can be adjusted to meet specific privacy needs, making it a flexible and powerful tool for maintaining data security while preserving the usefulness of the data.

Related work

In the DL training process, as a model trains for more epochs, it increasingly memorizes the data, making it more susceptible to membership inference attacks. To mitigate this, Song et al.7 recommend using the early stopping technique8–10. This strategy involves halting training before the model becomes highly specific to the training data, thereby balancing the need for accuracy with the reduction of privacy risks.

Song et al.7 present a new standard for membership inference attacks through the concept of “metric-based attacks”. This approach involves assessing the predictions made by the target model by measuring metrics like accuracy, confidence, and entropy. These metrics are then compared against predetermined threshold levels to find out whether an input sample belongs to the training set. Additionally, they introduce several non-neural network-based attacks for evaluating privacy risks, which only require observing the output predictions of the target model in a black-box scenario.

Leino et al.11 introduce a membership inference attack that focuses on the accuracy of predictions. Their approach relies on how correctly the model classifies the input data. In comparison, Song et al.12 and Yeom et al.13 present inference attacks that rely on prediction confidence. In these methods, an adversary determines that an input sample belongs to the training data when the prediction confidence is above a specified threshold, and classifies it as a non-member if the confidence is below that threshold.

Shokri et al.1 point to the variation in prediction entropy distributions between training and test data as a key factor underlying privacy risks in machine learning models. Building on this, Salem et al.14 demonstrate how prediction entropy can be leveraged for membership inference attacks. The core idea is that models typically exhibit lower prediction entropy for data they were trained on, while entropy is higher for unfamiliar test samples. This difference serves as a useful indicator for identifying whether a sample belonged to the training data. In membership inference attacks that use prediction entropy, the adversary measures the entropy of a model’s prediction for a given input and compares it to a predetermined threshold. In case that the entropy is below this threshold, the input is categorized as a member of the training set; if the entropy exceeds the threshold, it is identified as a non-member. This approach enables attackers to deduce the membership status of particular data points, highlighting the privacy vulnerabilities associated with machine learning models.

Defenses against membership inference attacks involve a range of techniques aimed at balancing privacy and performance. Common strategies, such as L2 norm regularization15 and dropout16, are typically used to mitigate overfitting in machine learning and have shown some ability to reduce privacy risks by making the model less likely to memorize training data1,14. However, these methods alone are often insufficient, as models may remain susceptible to attacks despite their application.

Differential privacy2 offers a stronger, mathematically guaranteed form of privacy protection, significantly reducing the risk of membership inference17–20. However, this robustness comes at a cost, frequently resulting in a notable reduction in model accuracy21 as noise is introduced to obscure individual data points.

Gu et al.22 investigated the vulnerabilities of deep neural networks to adversarial attacks and proposed gradient shielding to enhance robustness by focusing on sensitive image regions. Their adaptive methods, IGS and AGS, refine gradient selection to improve attack precision and efficiency. Qiao et al.23 developed an incentive mechanism for federated learning in vehicular networks that minimizes computational and communication overhead by rewarding participants based on data quality and contribution relevance, ensuring fair collaboration and improving model convergence. Lv et al.24 highlighted critical weaknesses in cloud-based content storage systems, revealing flaws in existing security measures, and proposed a double protection strategy emphasizing shared responsibility between Content Security Policy (CSP) mechanisms and cloud providers to enhance overall data security.

Lu et al.25,26 introduced AutoD, an innovative unpacking system using a JNI-based deception call chain to address Dex unpacking challenges, later extending it to DeepAutoD, a flexible framework for analyzing protected Android applications and detecting malware through distributed machine learning. Tan et al.27 proposed an automated method for identifying BGP community attributes using a Graph Neural Network (GNN), transforming AS-level routing data into topological graphs, achieving over 96% accuracy, and outperforming manual rule-based approaches for dynamic network routing analysis. Collectively, these studies highlight the evolving landscape of distributed and adaptive privacy-preserving systems, reinforcing the necessity of noise-based mechanisms such as the proposed RanN-DP-SGD.

Recognizing the limitations of traditional techniques, more sophisticated defenses have emerged, such as adversarial regularization28 and MemGuard29. These approaches specifically target the mechanisms of membership inference attacks and have demonstrated success in reducing attack accuracy to roughly 50% bringing it down to the level of random guessing. Despite their effectiveness, these methods also wrestle with trade-offs between enhancing privacy and maintaining the model’s predictive performance, making it a critical challenge to optimize both aspects simultaneously.

Random noise DP approach

Abadi et al.17 present the differentially private Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) in which the DP-noise is injected to the weights at each step during the training process. In this paper, we refer to the standard approach as Regular Noise DP-SGD (RegN-DP-SGD), and to our proposed dynamic DP-SGD as RanN-DP-SGD. Our RanN-DP-SGD algorithm extends standard DP-SGD (RegN-DP-SGD) by injecting noise into the model weights randomly during training, instead of at every optimization step. Therefore, it is not guarantee that the deferential privacy noise is added to the gradient in each step. Algorithm 1 outline the Ran-DP-SGD technique. In Algorithm 1, step 1 the training parameters are randomly initialized. Then the training data is sampled into batches (step 3). Each training example from the batch is use to train the model and the gradient is calculated and clipped (steps 4 and 5). After calculating the gradient for all the examples in the randomly selected batch, the gradient is updated with random noise or not based on the value of the random variable d generated in step 7. If d is one, the DP-noise will be added to the gradient. Otherwise if d is zero, the DP-noise will not be added to the gradient. In the last step, the parameter is updated based on the value of the gradient (Step 8) and the random value for each batch (Step 9).

In terms of the privacy-accuracy trade-off, the results demonstrate that stronger privacy guarantees-achieved through Differential Privacy (DP)-tend to reduce model accuracy, as injected noise perturbs the learning process. Notably, randomized noise methods (RanN) outperform regularized noise methods (RegN), particularly when Gaussian noise is used.

Among all configurations, the Gaussian RanN-DP-SGD model proves to be the most effective, offering a strong balance between privacy and accuracy. This suggests that randomized Gaussian noise should be the default choice for DP-SGD, as it provides a smooth and reliable privacy-utility trade-off. In contrast, while Laplace noise is theoretically sound, it fails to deliver robust performance in practice for deep learning tasks.

The findings are consistent with other metrics, including training/validation curves and test loss, reinforcing the stability and effectiveness of Gaussian DP mechanisms.

Conclusion and future work

In this research, I investigate the impact of replacing standard noise with randomized noise in the DPSGD algorithm to enhance model privacy. We trained and evaluated five models: No-DP, Gaussian Reg-DP-SGD, Gaussian RanN-DP-SGD, Laplace RanN-DP-SGD, and Laplace RegN-DP-SGD, to compare their effectiveness in defending against privacy-preserving attacks.The results show that if recall is the primary concern-such as in medical diagnosis-DP models, especially those using Gaussian noise, are suitable and can maintain high sensitivity. For scenarios where a balanced trade-off between accuracy and precision is essential, the Gaussian RanN-DP-SGD model proves to be the most effective DP strategy. On the other hand, Laplace RegN-DP-SGD performs poorly and may require further tuning, such as adjusting the clipping norm, noise scale, or training duration. As future work, we plan to explore the performance of the RanN-DP approach in differentially private federated learning (DP-FL) and test its scalability on large and complex models.
