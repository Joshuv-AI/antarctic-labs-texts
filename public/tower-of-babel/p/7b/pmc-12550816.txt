Where Have You Been? Backtracking Microplastic to Its Source Using the Biomolecular Composition of the Ecocorona
Authors: Amanda L. Dawson, Utpal Bose, Sophia Escobar-Correas, Kuok Yap, David J. Craik, Cassandra L. Pegg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Microplastics are a diffuse contaminant with various global sources, pathways, and sinks. This study aimed to backtrack microplastics across environments using metaproteomic and eDNA metabarcoding information stored within the ecocorona. Pristine polyamide (PA) fibers, polyethylene terephthalate (PET) fibers and fragments, and PET and PA preincubated in bovine serum albumin (BSA) were deployed into a tank housing Penaeus monodon to develop an ecocorona. Upon collection, BSA was detected within the ecocorona, along with P. monodon proteins, using mass spectrometry. BSA preincubation influenced the diversity and abundance of ecocorona proteins with pristine microplastics having more significantly enriched proteins. Most ecocorona proteins reflected the marine environment, confirming that the protein assemblage on microplastics records environmental signatures. Microplastic tracking was validated using polyethylene plastics unintentionally discharged from an aquaculture facility into Moreton Bay and collected after 7 days. Orthogonal Partial Least Square models predicted the source with 69–92% accuracy based on 16S eDNA taxa and 69–123% accuracy based on untargeted metaproteomics. Several identified taxa from both analyses were specific to the aquaculture source, including genera Leucothrix and Rugeria and species Salmo salar and P. monodon. Overall tracking of microplastics using the ecocorona proved effective over short time scales and reliably reflected the surrounding biological milieu.

Introduction

Plastic production over the past century has vastly outstripped global capacity for waste management, , leading to an abundance of plastic items and degraded plastic fragments across terrestrial, aquatic, and atmospheric compartments. , As plastics degrade, labels are lost, brandings fade, and they often fragment into particles that are unrecognizable compared to their original form (Figure S1). Furthermore, some plastic items may have never possessed recognizable features. Once plastics escape into the environment, it is extremely difficult to determine their original use − and their subsequent movements leading to their sampled location, which are typically estimated through modeling. ,− Despite these hurdles, the identification of point sources and producers of plastic debris is necessary for effective waste management, pinpointing emission hotspots, locating potential interception points, and guiding policy development.

Plastic items in the environment are subjected to abiotic and biotic weathering processes, which accelerate fragmentation into micro- and nanosized particles. Simultaneously, the plastic surface is rapidly colonized by exogenous biomolecules, , including proteins, lipids, and DNA, forming an ecocorona. The binding of individual biomolecules is dependent on many factors, including water chemistry, temperature, pH, surface area to volume ratio, surface chemistry, , surface morphology, polymer chemistry, , polymer additives and chemical residues, and protein–protein interactions, , as well as interactions with other biomolecules, in particular, DNA. Strongly bound biomolecules form the hard corona, where the binding is permanent. Surrounding this is a cloud of loosely associated molecules, known as the soft corona. The composition of both coronas reflects the environment where they were formed but does not necessarily mirror the complete molecular composition, as proteins and other biomolecules are enriched within the corona ,, depending on binding affinity and protein–protein interactions. In the case of proteins, highly abundant proteins typically attach to the surface first but are gradually replaced by proteins with a higher affinity to the surface.

Nanoparticles and microplastic beads (<5 μm) have been shown to develop multilayered stable protein coronas. A recent study demonstrated that polystyrene beads sequentially incubated in different protein solutions retained both initial (bovine serum albumin, β-lactoglobulin, etc.) and secondary proteins (fetal calf serum) in their corona. This suggests that, as microplastics travel through different ecosystems, their hard corona may retain biomolecular layers or a “fingerprint” reflecting each environment, potentially allowing particle migration to be traced back to the source.

This study aimed to assess whether the ecocorona can function as a bioindicator for the environments traversed by microplastics with the goal of tracing microplastics back to their release sources. To explore this aim, microplastics composed of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and polyamide (PA) were deployed into a complex marine environment, either precoronated with bovine serum albumin (BSA) or pristine, to develop an ecocorona. Thereafter, protein was extracted from the microplastic surface to (a) determine the permanence of proteins within the corona as plastics move between freshwater and marine systems, (b) determine if a microplastic ecocorona reflects the environment within which it was formed, and (c) examine the influence of a pre-existing ecocorona on the binding of new biomolecules onto the microplastic surface. Based on these findings, the applicability of the ecocorona as a tracking tool was explored in a real-world scenario by analyzing biomedia plastic beads that were unintentionally released from a known source and collected from adjacent beaches after spending a week at sea. The ecocorona formed on these plastics was used to develop a tracking model using a complementary proteomic and metabarcoding approach.

Methods

Microplastics

Virgin microplastics used in this study were noncolored PET irregular particles of <300 μm (Goodfellow); precision cut PA fibers (nominal length of 100 μm, width of 3.3 decitex (16–20 μm), 1.30–1.40 g cm–3) (Goonvean); and black PET flock fibers (nominal length of 500 μm, width of 3.3 decitex (∼17 μm) (The flocking shop) (Table S1). These polymers were selected based on the results of previous work which demonstrated high binding ability of PET and PA and were comprehensively characterized in terms of binding efficiency, polymer, size, shape, surface charge, additives, and leaching, among other factors. Physicochemical characteristics and characterization methods of the three microplastics standards used in this study are outlined in Dawson et al. Polymer composition of all microplastics was verified using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) (64 scans at 4 cm–1 resolution, wavenumber range = 4000–600 cm–1).

The environmental plastics used in this study were filter media (Biomedia) (nominal length: 8 mm; diameter: 10.5 mm; weight: 180 mg) used for biological filtration in aquaculture facilities (Figure ). The polymer was verified to be composed of polyethylene by using ATR-FTIR (Figure S2).

Plastic Standards

PET fibers, PA fibers, and PET fragments were deployed into a 10 m3 marine holding tank containing a single species of black tiger prawn, Penaeus monodon, to develop an ecocorona (Figure A). Stocking density of P. monodon was 2 m–3 with flowthrough seawater at a rate of 5 L min–1. Plastics were either pristine or preincubated in sterile filtered (0.45 μm mixed cellulose ester Whatman) 32 mg mL–1 BSA solution (3.2 g plastic), pH 7.0, for 24 h at RT (approximately 24 °C) on a tube roller (60 rpm) in the dark. BSA was selected as the model protein as successful binding has been established for these polymers, it has been applied in previous corona studies, and it was absent in the marine tank environment. Thus, the detection of BSA on deployed plastics would indicate stable corona retention across environments.

Each plastic (400 mg) was deployed in marine grade (316) stainless steel mesh bags (11 μm pore, Hollander weave) approximately 10 × 8 cm in size. Bags were deployed into the tank for 48 h at 28.4 °C, pH 7.81, and 33.04 ppt. After removal, bags were wrapped in foil and frozen at −20 °C until analysis.

Seawater

Seawater from the marine tank was collected in parallel to the plastic deployment for exoproteome analysis. For protein analysis, three 100 L samples were collected over the incubation period. A submersible pump was deployed into the tank 50 cm above the bottom to ensure sediments were not collected. Seawater was then filtered inline to remove large particulates using a 0.5 μm polypropylene spun sediment cartridge (High-Performance Filtration), followed by a 0.1 μm cartridge filter (Sawyer), before being collected in 60 L HDPE drums. Tubing, filter housing, and drums were prerinsed with reverse osmosis water followed by 80% ethanol. Seawater was stored at 4 °C for 4–5 days between collection and protein concentration. Protein concentration on the bulk water samples was carried out using tangential flow filtration. Water (100 L) was passed through three 10 kDa poly­(ether sulfone) ultrafiltration cassettes in tandom (Sartorius, 3 × 0.1 m2 surface area) under 0.6 bar of pressure. Retentate was concentrated to a final volume of approximately 200 mL before being collected and stored frozen at −20 °C until analysis.

Environmental Biomedia

Microplastics unintentionally released from a known source and recovered thereafter were used to assess if the composition of the ecocorona can be used to track plastics back to their source after they are deposited on the beach.

Biomedium beads were unintentionally released from the wastewater sump of an aquaculture research facility at Bribie Island, Australia, into Moreton Bay (27°03′14″ S 153°11′39″ E). Pristine beads were housed in the wastewater sump for approximately 24 h before release as the filtration system was being commissioned; thus, theoretically, the beads developed an ecocorona reflecting the aquaculture wastewater. During the cleanup event, Biomedium beads were recovered from a local proximal beach (Woorim Beach) after being free floating for approximately 1 week. Biomedia continued to be released into the wastewater sump for up to a month. Biomedium beads were also collected directly from the wastewater sump 7 days after the spill and 30 days after the spill (Figure B). Biomedia collection is outlined in the SI. New unused Biomedium beads were used as a control. Biomedia were stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Protein Extraction

Mesh bags were opened, rinsed with sterile Milli-Q water to remove salt, and transferred to protein LoBind Eppendorf tubes. Plastics were then vacuum-dried, weighed, resuspended in extraction buffer (8 M Urea, 2 M Thiourea, 0.5 M DTT, 4% CHAPS in 0.1 M Tris–HCl, pH 8.0), sonicated for 10 min, and then incubated at 900 rpm for 30 min at 22 °C. Samples were centrifuged at 20 800g for 15 min, and the supernatant was used for protein quantitation and on-filter digestion.

Microplastic protein samples were digested as outlined in the Supporting Information. Positive and negative controls were extracted and analyzed alongside deployed microplastics. Negative controls were composed of pristine microplastics. Positive controls comprised microplastics preincubated in BSA, which were not deployed into the tanks containing prawns.

Seawater (100 μL) was added to the extraction buffer (700 μL) and then digested with trypsin, as outlined in the Supporting Information, where a 50:1 protein to enzyme ratio was used. Samples were then desalted using Pierce Peptide Desalting Spin Columns, and the digested peptide concentration was evaluated as outlined below and reconcentrated in 0.1% formic acid at a concentration of 1 μg μL–1.

Biomedium beads were pooled (n = 6), cryomilled to facilitate protein extraction, and then split across 3 subsamples for analysis. Cryomilling allowed for the biomedia to fit into 2 mL Lobind tubes and increased surface area for extraction. The procedure is outlined in the Supporting Information. Three pooled samples of Biomedium beads were analyzed from the beach, the wastewater sump (7 days and 30 days after the spill), and unused beads, resulting in a total of 12 samples. Milled biomedia were extracted and digested as outlined in the Supporting Information.

eDNA Extraction and Metabarcoding

Five Biomedium beads were pooled, cryomilled, and then split across 5 subsamples with one pooled sample from each location (Beach, Wastewater sump 7 day, Wastewater sump 30 day, and Control). Samples were suspended in 540 μL of ATL buffer and analyzed by eDNA Frontiers. Extraction, PCR amplification of the 16S region (V3–4), and subsequent sequencing protocols are outlined in the Supporting Information. Raw metabarcoding sequence data was analyzed using the eDNAFlow pipeline, as outlined in the Supporting Information.

Targeted Proteomic Analysis

Pretreated and pristine microplastics were analyzed for the presence of BSA protein after a 48 h exposure to the marine tank containing P. monodon. Digested peptides (1 μg) were separated and detected with a Shimadzu Nexera UHPLC coupled to a 6500 QTRAP (SCIEX) utilizing multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) outlined in Dawson et al. BSA was quantified by interpolating a standard curve of pure BSA protein (8.078 mg mL–1) prepared at concentrations spanning 0.0005–2000 μg mL–1 using the BSA specific peptide LVNEL­TEFAK (Figure S3). Protein concentration was normalized by the extracted microplastic dry weight (μg mg–1 plastic).

Untargeted Proteomic Analysis

Untargeted proteomic analysis was carried out on the deployed microplastics, biomedia, and seawater extracts. Digested peptides (250 or 500 ng) were separated and detected using an Acquity M Class HPLC (Waters) coupled to a ZenoTOF 7600 MS (SCIEX). Samples were acquired using both data independent acquisition (DIA) and data dependent acquisition (DDA) modes. Instrument settings are detailed in the Supporting Information.

ProteinPilot version 5.0.3 software was used for protein identification using peptide spectrum matching and scoring. For the deployed plastic samples and seawater, the DDA data were searched against the Penaeus monodon protein library. The P. monodon genome was accessed from NCBI (GCF_015228065.2) and translated protein sequences were used in addition to Mendoza-Porras et al. to build a library containing 159,305 protein sequences. DIA data was processed using the SWATH Acquisition MicroApp (v2.0.1.2133) in PeakView (v2.2) using the ion library created by ProteinPilot and the following settings: shared peptides, allowed; peptide confidence threshold, 99%; false discovery rate, 1%; XIC extraction window, 3 min; XIC width, 30 ppm. The output was reformatted to apply a peptide FDR cutoff of 1%. Statistical analysis was carried out using the R package MSstatsshiny (DOI:10.18129/B9.bioc.MSstatsShiny) using RStudio 4.2.

Metaproteomic analysis of the biomedia followed a workflow modified from ref . DDA data were searched using the de novo sequence using PEAKS Studio 11. Trypsin was set as the proteolytic enzyme, and precursor and fragment mass tolerance was set to 10 ppm and 0.02 Da, respectively. Carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modification, and methionine oxidation was set as a variable modification. The average local confidence (ALC) was set at ≥90% and the false discovery rate (FDR), at 1%. Unique peptides (2319) identified across all samples and controls were searched in Unipept 2.0 to taxonomically classify peptides to the lowest common ancestor with the following settings: equate isoleucine and leucine, filter duplicate peptides, and advanced missed cleavage handling. Of these, matched peptides (Figure S4) were used to build a protein FASTA file. The first ranked UniProt entry for the unique peptides were retrieved using pept2prot with default parameters except that isoleucine and leucine were equated. Care was taken when reporting the identified proteins given that some peptides mapped to multiple proteins. The remaining unmatched peptides were searched through NCBI BLASTp using nonredundant protein sequences and an expected threshold of 1 ×10–6. Peptides were included if the query coverage percent was 100% and exactly matched the query sequence, which resulted in a further 98 unique peptides being identified. Identified peptides were used to generate a protein database, and these along with a species-specific library generated from the metabarcoding data, as well as known species present in the aquaculture facility (Table S3), were used to compile a protein library comprising 588,764 protein sequences. DDA data was then searched against this library using ProteinPilot v5.0.3 to create an ion library. Biomedia DIA data were then processed as outlined above using PeakView 2.2 and MSstatsshiny.

Data Analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out with GraphPad Prism 10.2.3 and Rstudio. BSA concentration was compared between treated and pristine microplastics using a 2-way ANOVA (p = 0.05) with Tukey’s Multiple Comparisons Test. Using MSstatsshiny, feature abundance was log2 transformed and normalized by the median. Missing values were imputed by using the MSstat model. Pairwise comparisons between coated and pristine microplastics samples and between seawater and all microplastics were carried out using log10 adjusted p-values. Orthogonal Partial Least Square (OPLS) predictive modeling analysis was used to model the relationship between BSA coating and polymer type (model 1) and the relationship between ecocorona and seawater (model 2) using SIMCA 18. The biomedia microbiome was analyzed using Phyloseqshiny to calculate diversity indices (Fishers and Brays similarity) and a pairwise PERMANOVA, corrected for multiple comparisons using Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Taxa identified in the control biomedia were removed from the sump and beach data sets. Fingerprint analysis was carried out using OPLS-DA multivariate analysis. Two models were developed by using the control adjusted microbiome data. The first model used only the microbiome from the two sump samples to determine if the microbiome communities from the beach samples were closely related (model 3). The second model included publicly available microbiome data from the Australian Microbiome Initiative (www.australianmicrobiome.com) from sites 21740 and 21741 (Figure S5, model 4). Sample sites were offshore from Bribie Island, Queensland, Australia, and encompassed the free-living pelagic microbiome. This data was included to discern if the beach biomedia community was more closely related to the oceanic community or the Aquaculture facility wastewater community. The biomedia metaproteome was statistically analyzed using MSstatsshiny as described above. Prior to analysis, protein abundance in the control biomedia was subtracted from the sump and beach data set. OPLS-DA analysis was carried out as described above (model 5).

Results and Discussion

Permanence of the Ecocorona

BSA was successfully extracted and quantified (235.0 ± 93.1 μg mg–1 plastic) from microplastics preincubated with BSA using targeted MRM spectrometry, thus confirming that BSA had formed a protein corona on the microplastic surface during the 24 h preincubation. In contrast, BSA was not quantifiable in microplastic samples that were not pretreated with BSA. PET fibers, PET fragments, and PA fibers deployed into the marine tank after preincubation with BSA were all still found to contain BSA within the ecocorona after 48 h, confirming that microplastics retain their corona composition when transferred between different environments over short timeframes (Figure A). In this case, they are a sterile freshwater environment and a complex marine environment, containing adult black tiger prawns, sediments, bacteria, and algae. There was a significant interaction between polymer type and BSA concentration (F(2, 12) = 8.432; p < 0.01), suggesting that BSA did not bind uniformly between the three microplastic types. In fact, pretreated PA fibers contained only slightly more BSA (0.29 ± 0.2 μg mg–1 plastic) than the background level quantified from the pristine fibers (0.002 ± 0.003 μg mg–1 plastic; p > 0.05). The ecocorona formed on PET microplastics contained a significantly higher concentration of BSA compared to the PA microplastics (Fibers p < 0.0005; Fragments p < 0.01). This finding agrees with previous study results using the same particles, reporting significantly more BSA bound to PET fibers compared to PA fibers.

BSA was also detected in the microplastic samples by using untargeted DIA analysis (Figure B), where the same trend was also evident. BSA levels were increased in the two PET samples and reduced in PA microplastics but still detectable compared to the pristine microplastics.

Ecocorona Composition

Proteomic analysis was carried out to determine whether the protein assemblage within the ecocorona reflects the environment. A total of 52 proteins were identified across all samples searching against the black tiger prawn protein library. The total number of proteins extracted from the pooled microplastics ranged from 42 to 50. Slightly more proteins were identified on the pristine microplastics compared to BSA coated microplastics (Table ), suggesting that qualitatively the pre-existing corona influenced the binding of further proteins to the plastics surface. For both pristine and BSA coated plastics, PET fragments had the highest number of proteins extracted, and the PA ecocorona contained the least. In terms of overall protein abundance within the ecocorona, the composition of the ecocorona was determined from pristine microplastics, BSA pretreated microplastics, and the seawater separated by PCA (Figure C). Interestingly, the protein composition of the ecocorona within pristine samples was more closely related to that of the seawater (Figure D). Qualitatively, across the three different microplastics, a total of 31 proteins (72–73%) were commonly identified from the BSA coated microplastics and 34 (68–77%) were identified from the pristine microplastics (Figure A). Pairwise comparisons between the three BSA-coated polymers revealed very little difference in the protein composition (Figure S6). Notably, the vast majority of identified proteins were not significantly different between the three microplastics. Pairwise comparisons of the pristine microplastics revealed slightly more variability in the ecocorona protein composition, particularly between PA and PET fragments (Figure S7C) than in the BSA coated microplastics. Within each microplastic type, the ecocorona formed on pristine microplastics contained several differentially abundant proteins compared with the BSA pretreated microplastics (Figure S7). For example, pristine PET fragments contained 13 significantly abundant proteins compared to BSA coated fragments. However, for the overwhelming majority of proteins identified, BSA treatment did not influence the abundance within the corona.

Among the 47 proteins identified in the seawater, 26 were present in every sample with 31 and 30 proteins isolated from pristine and BSA coated microplastics, respectively (Figure B), confirming that qualitatively the majority of proteins comprising the ecocorona do indeed reflect the protein composition of the environment.

Across all plastic types, the ecocorona of both pristine and precoated microplastics contained proteins that were increased in abundance compared to seawater (Table and Figure C–H). Although the number and type of enriched proteins within the ecocorona varied between the three microplastic types. For example, calmodulin was only found to be enriched within pristine microplastics. PET fibers had the least number of significantly differentially abundant proteins compared to seawater (Figure E,H). This is noteworthy, as a previous study exposing these same fibers to BSA found that the electrostatic additive present on the surface of the fiber actually increased the binding of BSA protein to the fiber surface. This additive was so influential that it negated the impact of the surface area to volume ratio. However, the opposite trend appeared for black tiger prawn proteins in seawater, suggesting that the additive repelled or blocked proteins from sorbing to the plastic surface. Another hypothesis is that the pH and salinity of the seawater were less conducive to protein binding than neutral and sterile laboratory conditions. PET fragments had the highest number of enriched proteins among the pristine samples (Figure D), possibly due to their high surface area to volume ratio. Yet, in the BSA pretreated samples, both PA fibers and PET fragments had an equal number of enriched proteins (Figure G), which suggests that surface chemistry and polymer type influence protein binding on pristine microplastics. However, these factors are not the primary driver of protein binding on BSA precoated microplastics. Overall, seawater was characterized by high values of Lamin Dm0, hemocyanin subunit, hemocyanin c chain like, and myosin 7 like isoform (Figure S8, Table S5, model 2). These proteins were less abundant in pristine samples and negatively correlated with those in BSA samples. BSA coated microplastics were characterized by abundant BSA, ATP synthase subunit mitochondrial and electron transfer flavoprotein subunit A, and pristine samples characterized by hemocyanin subunit, myosin regulatory light chain 2 tropomyosin isoform x36.

Influence of a Pre-existing Ecocorona

The presence of enriched proteins within the microplastic ecocorona compared to the seawater could be due to selective binding of proteins, as demonstrated in Krishna de Guzman et al. Ecocorona development is a dynamic process, where highly abundant proteins bind to a particle surface initially and can then be replaced by proteins with a stronger affinity to the particle. This would also explain the abundance of significant proteins within the seawater compared to the microplastics. These proteins, including Lamin Dm0, likely had a lower affinity to the plastics, and thus, binding was limited, compared to, for example, tubulin alpha chain-like, which was abundant within the ecocorona of pristine plastics but unable to be quantified in the seawater and BSA coated plastics.

Overall, the corona extracted from BSA precoated microplastics contained fewer proteins. The greater diversity among the proteins extracted from the pristine microplastics compared to the BSA coated microplastics indicates that the preexisting protein corona influences the ability of new proteins to bind to the microplastic surface. The results of this study follow that of Jasinski et al., which demonstrated multilayered stable protein coronas that varied according to precoating. However, contrary to Jasinski et al., the current study found that precoating with BSA decreased the complexity of the ecocorona, possibly due to the complex environmental matrix used in this study. Many of the proteins within the tank environment seemed to have a stronger affinity to the pristine polymer surface than the BSA coated surface. This aligns with BSA’s well-documented role in immunohistochemistry, ELISA, and other bioassays, where it prevents nonspecific protein binding by saturating available surface sites, likely limiting the attachment of environmental proteins to the microplastic surface in this study. Multivariate analysis confirmed this observation. The OPLS model (model 1) showed the ecocorona composition was dependent on the BSA coating (CV-ANOVA F(17) = 7.381, p < 0.05) (Figure S9, Table S4) well as the polymer type.

Overall, despite minor differences in the ecocorona composition between polymer types and between BSA-coated and pristine microplastics, the majority of proteins identified were common to both microplastics and the surrounding seawater. This indicates that the ecocorona formed during tank deployment largely reflected the marine environment. These findings demonstrate that microplastics, whether pristine or preconditioned, reliably record environmental protein fingerprints over short timeframes.

Microplastic Tracking of Spill Biomedia

Metabarcoding the Ecocorona Community

Biomedium beads collected from the wastewater sump one month after the spill were heavily biofouled, whereas samples collected from the beach and wastewater sump 7 days after the spill were only lightly discolored with minimal visible biofouling. Across all samples, 535 organisms were identified from the biofilm community, spanning five kingdoms (Bacteria, Chromista, Plantae, Protozoa, and Archaea). Fishers’ alpha diversity revealed variation between the samples, but within sample variation was minimal (Figure A). Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) of the control and environmental samples showed that the microbiome community isolated from control biomedia was distinctly different from the wastewater sump and beach samples (Figure B). This was confirmed using a pairwise PERMANOVA (FDR, p < 0.05). Despite the beach and 30 day sump samples clustering quite closely in the PCoA plot, these were also found to be significantly different. In fact, microbial diversity differed significantly between all three environmental locations (FDR, p < 0.05).

Microbial diversity analysis of the Biomedium beads. (A) Alpha diversity, (B) beta diversity, (C) number of bacteria identified from the three samples (control adjusted), and (D) top 15 identified microbiome genera. Others indicate all lower abundance taxa (>15).

Taxa identified from three or more replicates within the control Biomedium samples were excluded from the environmental samples. Conversely, organisms were considered to be present in the environmental samples when identified in four or more subsamples. Forty-five microbes were consistently identified from the three locations (Figure C). The 7-day sump samples only contained 3 unique identities, whereas both the beach samples and the 30 day sump contained 62 and 67 unique identities, respectively.

The 7-day sump microbiome community was dominated by the bacterial genus Leucothrix, followed by Rugeria and Sulfitobacter. As the biomedia were only added to the wastewater sump 24 h prior to release, it suggests these bacteria are initial colonizers of the surface. All three genera were still present on the biomedia taken from the sump 30 days after the spill and on the beach collected biomedia but were completely absent from the controls (Figure D). Leucothrix mucor is a common marine bacteria known to be pathogenic within crustacean aquaculture and is particularly associated with reduced water quality. Its presence in the wastewater sump of the aquaculture facility is therefore unsurprising. Conversely, Rugeria sp. are probiotic bacteria often used in fish feed , and, therefore, likely to have originated from feed or waste products entering the sump. Both are highly indicative of an aquaculture derived microbiome.

Multivariate analysis on the Biomedium samples from the sump 7 and 30 days after the spill was used to develop a microbiome signature reflecting the wastewater sump. The scores plot (Figure S10A) revealed a distinct separation between the 7 and 30 day samples with loadings confirming that Leucothrix was indeed a significant component of the 7-day community (Figure S10B,C). Table S6 shows that 79.1% of the data explained 100% of the location variable. Component 1 used 49% of the data to discriminate between the two sump time points, and the predictive power of the model was 0.986 (model 3, CV-ANOVA F(9) = 35.54, p < 0.05). Thereafter, the model was then used to predict the origin of the beach samples. For all 5 beach samples, 69–92% of the variance was explained by the 7-day sump signature (Table S7), confirming that the beach microbiome was more closely related to the community formed on the biobeads that resided in the sump for only 7 days rather than beads that resided in the sump for 30 days. The second OPLS-DA model (model 4) included microbiome samples from oceanic water close to Bribie Island (approximately 64 km) to determine if the microbiome from the biomedia washed up on the beach originated from the wastewater sump or the free-living marine microbiome. The model used 76.4% of the data to explain 98.8% of the location variable, i.e., Sump 7 days, Sump 30 days, and Ocean, and had a predictive power of 0.974 (Table S8, CV-ANOVA F(22) = 64.61, p < 0.05). Score plots showed good separation between the three locations (Figure S11). Even with the oceanic microbiome included, the model still successfully predicted the beach samples originated from the wastewater sump with between 80% and 88.4% of the variance explained by the 7 day sump signature (Table S9). As noted above, both Leucothrix and Rugeria strongly influenced the separation between the wastewater samples and oceanic samples (Figure S11).

Metaproteome of the Ecocorona Community

Only 335 taxa were identified to lower than the organism level using de novo peptide sequences. Of these, two were identified as viruses, 235 as eukaryotes, 92 as bacteria, and 6 as archaea. Interestingly, 64 sequences were identified as belonging to cnidarians with several species of sea anemones identified, all originating from the beach samples (Figure A). Several primate and human peptides were identified across all samples, which likely originated from handling from members of the public when collecting the biomedia or possibly during the packaging and production process. A total of 128 proteins were identified across the control, beach, and two sump samples. The beach proteome was reasonably distinct from the 7 day and 30 day wastewater sump samples (Figure B). After the control adjusted the protein abundance, 28 proteins were found to be unique to the beach biomedia, whereas both wastewater sump samples contained only four unique proteins (Figure C). As anticipated, the beach samples contained numerous enriched proteins compared to both sump samples and had the highest protein diversity (Figure D). The 7 day sump samples were dominated by porin (Figure E). Porin proteins are extremely abundant within the bacterial cell wall, although they can also be found in mitochondria. Porins in the 7 day sump samples likely originated from bacteria due to the abundance of bacteria within the sump. However, these proteins were almost completely absent in the 30 day and beach data. Two aquaculture species were represented in the biomedia metaproteome, and a single protein originating from Atlantic salmon and black tiger prawn was found in all three samples. Atlantic salmon are not found in Moreton Bay and thus conclusively demonstrate that proteins derived from aquaculture waste can bind to plastic debris and survive for extended periods as it travels to new environs.

The most abundant protein within the beach samples was also able to be taxonomically identified: actin, which is cytoplasmic-like and originates from Actinia tenebrosa, Australian red waratah sea anemones, common sea anemones found within Moreton Bay. These sea anemones are not known to be present in the aquaculture facility, so the proteins are likely to have joined the Biomedium ecocorona after their release from the wastewater sump. Despite a very small amount of these proteins also present in the sump samples, the concentration of cytoplasmic-like actin was significantly enriched on the beach samples compared to both sump samples (FDR, p < 0.05), suggesting sources outside the sump. Seawater for the facility is sourced from the bay and may have contained a level of background anemone protein, which may have accumulated on the biomedia in the wastewater sump, particularly as all retentate particulate matter from the intake line is diverted to the wastewater sump. Furthermore, the intake pipe is also colonized with sessile invertebrates, such as sponges, anemones, and soft corals, and protein may have persisted within the seawater as it transited through the facility before returning to the sump. This hypothesis would explain the observation of slightly elevated cytoplasmic-like actin concentrations within the 30 day samples compared to the 7 day samples, which suggests a slow accumulation of this protein on the surface of the biomedia as seawater is constantly refreshed over time. Numerous highly abundant proteins were identified to be originating from sea anemones within the beach samples, including from Exaiptasia diaphana, a non-native species found within eastern Australia. , These formed a key component of the beach metaproteome signature (Figure S12). Many of the highly abundant proteins identified from the beach samples were also identified within the sump samples, suggesting that they originated from the sump environment or the organism that produced the protein was present in both environments.

One outlier was excluded from the metaproteome signature model of the wastewater sump, originating from the 7 day sump samples (OPLS-DA, Model 5). Even with this outlier removed, the predictive power of the model was only 0.671 (Table S10, CV-ANOVA F(16) = 1.59, p > 0.05). The score plot showed a distinct separation between the 7 day and 30 day metaproteome (Figure S13). Despite the low predictability of the model, all 9 beach samples were predicted to be more closely related to the 7-day sump samples. However, the explained variance ranged from 69% to 123% (Table S11), and four beach predictions exceeded the 95% confidence limits (Figure S13).

Methodological Constraints and Future Work

Both the microbiome and metaproteome data predicted the beach samples to have originated from the 7 day wastewater sump rather than the 30 day samples. However, across all models, the microbiome data were more reliable at describing the relationship between the two wastewater sump time points and at predicting the origin of the beach samples. However, metagenomic eDNA analysis can be reasonably expensive, and metabarcoding may require several assays to cover all available taxa within an environment. This study only targeted the 16s region for identification; thus, it was limited to bacteria and fungi, while generalist assays covering more taxa are available, the resolution is limited. However, fingerprint analysis requires species and species level identification, whereas the nontargeted proteomics approach is reasonably inexpensive and can facilitate routine large-scale protein quantitation with high sensitivity, dynamic range, and reproducibility across multiple laboratory conditions. Conversely, environmental proteomes are less well studied, making analysis pipelines reasonably complicated. , Particularly, as taxonomic identification based on the proteome is dependent on unique peptide sequences, where multiple taxa have the same peptide sequence, the identification can be attributed only to the lowest common ancestor.

The combination of both eDNA and proteins provided a clear and thorough snapshot of the Biomedium ecocorona. The presence of cytoskeleton and muscle structure proteins (actin, myosin, keratin, tubulin, plectin, etc.) within the ecocorona, along with hemocyanin and ferritin, suggest origins from fish or invertebrate tissue. The presence of Rhodobacteraceae (e.g., Ruegeria, Sulfitobacter, Phaeobacter) are common in aquaculture and are prominent biofilm forming bacteria as well as aquaculture probiotics. Leucothrix and Thiothrix (Thiotrichaceae) are indicators of organic and sulfur-rich wastewater and present in oxygen gradient environs. , Lastly, the presence of decomposition bacteria such as Pseudoalteromonas, Planctomycetes, and Flavobacteriaceae suggests aquaculture sludge or a biofilm environment. − Overall, these point to an environment with high organic loading, eukaryotic and prokaryotic debris, and biofilm formation, which are all likely to occur with an aquaculture sump.

This data effectively demonstrated that plastic items can be tracked back to the point of release using the biomolecular signature accumulated on their surface throughout their journey through the environment. Further work to understand temporal variation in the ecocorona community will be necessary to facilitate the use of this technique on plastic samples with unknown provenance. Global microbiome mapping studies are already available, − which could provide a foundation for large scale tracking. However, in order to use this tool for unknown plastic particles, it is possible that fine scale resolution may also be needed to quantify the dynamic biomolecular composition of environmental media.

Polymer type, size, shape, and additive content may also be important factors to consider for analysis. Despite previous studies showing microbiome community structure to be independent of polymer, the current study showed some differential binding of proteins across PET and PA polymers. Previous works also demonstrated differential binding depending on surface area to volume ratio and the presence of chemical additives. Therefore, a pool of several microplastic types may be necessary to elucidate a complete tracking signature.
