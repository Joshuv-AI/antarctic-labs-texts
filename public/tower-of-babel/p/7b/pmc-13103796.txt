A Comparative Review of Phytochemical Composition, Bioactivity, and Health Implications of Single- and Multiple-Clove Garlic
Authors: Reechik Bandyopadhyay, Snigdha Pattnaik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Single-clove garlic (SCG) and multiple-clove garlic (MCG) exhibit notable differences in their phytochemical composition, bioactivity, and therapeutic potential. The major organosulfur compounds originate and undergo their enzymatic and chemical formation pathways from the precursor alliin. SCG displays a distinct structural morphology and consistently higher concentrations of key bioactive compounds, including allicin and phenolic constituents, contributing to enhanced antioxidant capacity. Consequently, SCG extracts demonstrate superior acute antibacterial effectiveness against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, along with stronger antibiofilm activity. In contrast, MCG shows more sustained effects in lipid modulation and broader antifungal spectra. Preharvesting factors (genotype, growing conditions, fertilization, and harvesting stage) along with post-harvesting conditions (e.g., processing, storage, and curing) significantly influence the chemical components of garlic, particularly its organosulfur compounds and phenolic constituents. The stability and bioavailability of critical compounds, for instance, allicin and S-allyl cysteine (SAC), are significantly affected by processing methods (e.g., crushing, heating, and aging). The principal therapeutic mechanisms of garlic include Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway inhibition (anti-inflammatory), thiol–disulfide bond disruption (antimicrobial), and Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) activation leading to apoptosis induction in cancer cells (anticancer). Garlic modulates cardiovascular risk factors through 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibition, hydrogen sulfide-mediated vasodilation, and antiplatelet activity. However, major challenges remain due to experimental variability, inconsistencies in garlic sourcing and preparation, and the chemical instability of allicin. Future research should emphasize the standardization of processing protocols, development of stabilization strategies (e.g., nanoencapsulation, high-pressure processing), and rigorous clinical validation of SCG-specific benefits. Overall, SCG exhibits enhanced acute bioactivity, while MCG confers more sustained therapeutic effects, underscoring the importance of morphology-specific applications in maximizing garlic’s pharmacological potential.

Introduction

Allium sativumis highly valued for its medicinal properties since ancient times in various civilizations, including India, China, Egypt, Greece, and Rome. Historical texts from these civilizations indicate that garlic was used as a potent cure for numerous conditions. Garlic was highly used in ancient Egyptian culture for improving physical ability and treating infectious diseases. The Ebers Papyrus (ca. 1550 BCE) is an ancient Egyptian medical text which documents garlic’s use in treating heart conditions, parasitic infections, and general malaise. Garlic has been valued in traditional Chinese medicine for more than two millennia to treat respiratory infections, aid digestion, and reduce fatigue. In Ayurveda, it is known as Rasona and classified as a Rasayana (rejuvenating herb) which is used to manage heart disease, arthritis, and infections. The Charaka Samhita (300 BCE–200 CE) describes garlic as beneficial for vata disorders and circulatory ailments.

The father of Western medicine, Hippocrates (460–370 BCE) use garlic for uterine tumors, pulmonary complaints, and digestive ailments. In natural history, garlic was listed as a remedy for gastrointestinal issues, snake bites, and even epilepsy. During the Middle Ages, garlic was commonly used to fight the plague and other infectious diseases. Its antimicrobial properties were later supported by Louis Pasteur’s 19th-century research, confirming its antibacterial effects.

Garlic has been studied extensively focusing on its medical properties and bioactive compounds. Despite so many studies, a critical gap remains in the morphological variants of garlic. A comparative study of single-clove garlic (SCG) and Multi-clove garlic (MCG) can significantly influence the therapeutic efficacy and phytochemical variation. This study is important to understand the differences in therapeutic efficacy. This comparative study can maximize its medical benefits, and future research can standardize techniques to ensure reproducibility in pharmacological outcome.

Garlic bulbs are composed of multiple cloves, and each of those cloves is enclosed by a paper-like transparent peel, which is commonly known as multiple-clove garlic (MCG). Single-clove garlic (SCG) consists of a single clove which is also enclosed by a single paper-like peel and it is composed of only a single bulb. Chemical Composition of Garlic

Overview of Key Active Compounds

Garlic (Allium sativum) is rich in bioactive compounds, particularly sulfur-containing molecules, that contribute to its various pharmacological properties. Alliin, allicin, ajoenes, vinyldithiins, and various sulfides such as diallyl sulfide (DAS), diallyl disulfide (DADS), and diallyl trisulfide (DATS) are well-known key active compounds of garlic and are also responsible for its aroma, flavor, and therapeutic effects (Figure ).

Structure of essential chemicals present in garlic (Allium sativum).

Alliin

Alliin (S-allyl-l-cysteine sulfoxide), formed in garlic, is the main precursor of allicin and comprises over 70% of the total thiosulfinates in garlic. This is a compound whose odorless form converts into allicin when we crush or chop garlic, due to the presence of the enzyme alliinase. The antioxidant effect of alliin has been found to regulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and inhibit mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways. ,

Allicin

Allicin (S-allyl cysteine) is the primary sulfur-containing compound responsible for garlic’s bioactive properties. Active in this form, it has a pungent smell and strong taste. Alliin is converted enzymatically by alliinase to get S-allyl cysteine. Allicin is highly unstable and decomposes into diverse substances such as ajoenes or vinyldithiins. As a result of such possessions, allicin has been observed to possess a widespread range of antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial activities. These sources of reactivity typically stem from molecular recognition by allicin of SH compounds, interactions with metals, and interactions between positively charged nitrogen atoms. ,

Ajoenes

Decomposition of allicin produces ajoenes which is a stable compound and present in two isomeric forms, E-ajoene and Z-ajoene. Ajoenes exhibit antithrombotic, anticancer, and antimicrobial activities. It acts as a platelet aggregation inhibitor and induces apoptosis in cancer cells, which makes them suitable candidates for therapeutic applications. ,

Vinyldithiins

Vinyldithiins (e.g., 2-vinyl-4H-1,3-dithiin and 3-vinyl-4H-1,2-dithiin) are known for their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. They also inhibit different preadipocytes, which may control their antiobesity effect. Vinyldithiins’ concentration increases with the aging of garlic as they are formed during the aging process of garlic. ,

Sulfides (DAS, DADS, DATS)

Diallylsulfide (DAS), diallyl disulfide (DADS), and diallyl trisulfide (DATS) are organosulfur compounds derived from allicin. These compounds show antioxidant, anticancer, and antimicrobial effects which are considered as most effective activities of garlic. Diallyl disulfide (DADS) has been shown to induce apoptosis in cancer cells and inhibit the growth of various pathogens. ,

S-Allyl Cysteine (SAC)

S-Allyl cysteine (SAC) is known for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties. SAC is a water-soluble compound found in aged garlic. It shows protection against oxidative stress and improves cognitive function, which makes it a potential active ingredient for neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease. ,

Flavonoids and Phenolic Compounds

Garlic contains flavonoids and phenolic compounds, which contribute to its antioxidant activity. These compounds help to scavenge free radicals and reduce oxidative stress, thereby protecting cells from damage.

Factors Affecting Composition

Garlic (Allium sativum) is one of the most widely consumed vegetables globally, valued not only for its culinary uses but also for its medicinal properties. The chemical composition of garlic, particularly its bioactive compounds, is influenced by various pre- and postharvest factors. These factors can significantly alter the levels of organosulfur compounds, phenolics, and other bioactive constituents, which are responsible for garlic’s health benefits, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, and cardioprotective properties.

Preharvest Factors

Genotype

The genetic profile of garlic significantly influences its chemical makeup. Various garlic genotypes differ in their levels of organosulfur compounds, phenolic substances, and other biologically active components. Research indicates notable differences in the concentrations of S-allyl cysteine sulfoxide (alliin) and total phenolics across different garlic varieties. These differences are largely shaped by the plant’s adaptation to diverse environmental conditions over its cultivation history. For instance, Hirata et al. (2014) reported substantial variation in the chemical composition of 103 garlic samples collected worldwide, emphasizing that genetic diversity plays a central role in defining garlic’s bioactive characteristics.

Growing Conditions

The growing conditions of garlic, such as soil type, climate, and altitude, play a vital role in shaping its chemical composition. Beato et al. (2011) reported that while the cultivation site had minimal influence on total phenolics and ferulic acid levels, it significantly affected the concentrations of caffeic, p-coumaric, and vanillic acids. Likewise, Montaño et al. (2011) observed that both genotype and growing location impact the levels of organosulfur compounds, with genotype exerting a stronger influence on the γ-glutamyl peptide content. ,

Irrigation

Water availability is a key factor that can modify the activity of antioxidant enzymes like catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), thereby directly affecting the overall levels of antioxidants.

Fertilization

Sulfur plays a vital role in the formation of organosulfur compounds such as alliin, a key precursor to many of garlic’s bioactive constituents. Research indicates that sulfur supplementation can enhance the levels of alliin, cysteine, and glutathione in garlic. In contrast, excessive nitrogen may negatively affect alliin concentration. Huchette et al. (2007) found a positive correlation between sulfur fertilization and alliin content, whereas the impact of nitrogen varied depending on the garlic cultivar.

Harvesting Stage

The harvest timing of garlic significantly influences its chemical composition. As the plant matures, alliin, the main precursor of garlic’s bioactive compounds, is transferred from the leaves to the bulbs. Delayed harvesting tends to increase the alliin concentration in the bulbs, thereby enhancing their bioactive potential. Research further indicates that modifying the planting schedule can affect the accumulation of organosulfur compounds; for instance, late planting in December has been shown to produce higher levels of alliin and isoalliin at harvest.

Postharvest Factors

Processing

Processing methods such as blanching, boiling, and frying can greatly influence the chemical composition of garlic. Blanching, in particular, reduces thiosulfinate levels and antioxidant activity, with the degree of reduction depending on both temperature and treatment duration. In contrast, crushing garlic cloves promotes the formation of organosulfur compounds (OSCs) by activating the enzyme alliinase, which catalyzes their synthesis.

Storage Conditions

Temperature, duration, and pH are critical factors in preserving the bioactive properties of garlic during storage. Low-temperature storage (around 5 °C) enhances the levels of S-alk­(en)­yl cysteine sulfoxides, such as alliin and isoalliin, by facilitating the conversion of γ-glutamyl peptides into sulfoxides. Maintaining storage conditions with 5–15% CO2 and 1–3% O2 helps slow down discoloration and decay, especially at temperatures between 5 and 10 °C. However, extended storage periods can reduce allicin levels and overall antioxidant activity. Studies have shown that while allicin content tends to decline over time, total phenolic content and antioxidant capacity often increase during storage. ,

Curing Process

The curing process, which involves controlled heat treatment, can enhance the antifungal properties of garlic oil by increasing the concentration of diallyl disulfide, a key active component. Proper curing not only boosts the stability and shelf life of garlic during storage but also helps maintain its overall quality. However, if curing temperatures are not properly regulated, they may cause unwanted sensory changes, negatively impacting the flavor and overall quality of the garlic.

Antioxidant Activity

Garlic’s antioxidant activity is highly influenced by processing methods. Fresh garlic, rich in allicin, provides strong radical-scavenging effects, demonstrating 35.66 ± 0.61% inhibition in 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assays. However, fermentation converts garlic into black garlica phenolic-rich superfood with enhanced antioxidant activity, achieving up to 92.1% DPPH inhibition. The extraction method is equally important: ethanol and methanol are more effective than water at extracting antioxidants, whereas steam distillation tends to lose many valuable polar compounds. , Heat treatment presents a paradoxwhile boiling degrades heat-sensitive allicin, reducing activity by 20–30%, the controlled heat of fermentation actually enhances antioxidant capacity through Maillard reactions. Antimicrobial Activity

Garlic shows extensive antimicrobial properties due to its bioactive compounds allicin, ajoene, and diallyl sulfides, which target bacteria and fungi. It has the ability to penetrate cell membranes via a thiol–disulfide exchange reaction, resulting in cell leakage followed by cell death. Research demonstrates that garlic oil has an effect on the cell wall and membrane of fungi of Penicillium funiculosum and Candida albicans. Organosulfur compounds also work by blocking essential enzymes (e.g., RNA polymerase, cysteine protease) which are responsible for building cellular components and energy production. They also trigger oxidative stress by producing reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can harm nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids inside cells. This ROS-induced damage interferes with vital cellular processes in pathogens, limiting their ability to survive and reproduce (Table ). −

Anticancer Activity

Garlic’s remarkable anticancer properties work through a sophisticated network of molecular mechanisms that precisely target cancer cells while protecting healthy ones. The organosulfur compounds trigger programmed cell death by flipping the apoptotic switchactivating executioner caspases while simultaneously suppressing the protective B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) proteins. They cleverly hijack the cell’s cycle regulators, putting the brakes on uncontrolled proliferation through Cyclin-Dependent Kinase (CDK) inhibition. What’s particularly fascinating is garlic’s dual redox personalityacting as a protective antioxidant in normal cells while turning up oxidative stress specifically in cancerous ones. The bioactive components also calm the inflammatory storm by silencing NF-κB and Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) signaling pathways that tumors exploit. Finally, they starve tumors of their blood supply and block escape routes by dialing down Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF) and Matrix Metalloproteinase (MMP) production. This multipronged attack makes garlic a promising natural chemopreventive agent that disrupts cancer at every stage of development (Table ).

Potential Mechanisms and Pathways

Garlic’s therapeutic activities are attributed to sulfur-containing compounds, primarily allicin (diallylthiosulfinate) which is formed during tissue damage caused by chopping or crushing. Allicin is an unstable compound which converts into diallyl sulfide (DAS), diallyl disulfide (DADS), diallyl trisulfide (DATS), and ajoene. These organosulfur compounds (OSCs) are responsible for the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and cardioprotective properties of garlic (Figure ).

Potential mechanisms and pathways of garlic (Allium sativum) bioactive compounds.

Antioxidant Effects

Garlic helps regulate oxidative stress by neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS), while its organosulfur compounds (OSCs) boost the activity of the body’s own antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx). Allicin, in particular, prevents lipid peroxidation and safeguards cell membranes from oxidative damage.

Cardiovascular Benefits

Garlic inhibits hepatic cholesterol synthesis by suppressing 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA) reductase and enhances cholesterol excretion via bile acid synthesis. Garlic-derived polysulfides convert to hydrogen sulfide (H2S), a vasodilator that enhances nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, relaxing vascular smooth muscle. Platelet aggregation is also inhibited by ajoene which blocks fibrinogen binding to glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptors.

Anti-inflammatory and Immunomodulatory Effects

Proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) were inhibited by garlic extracts which block nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) translocation. DADS modulates mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, reducing cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression. Garlic also enhances adaptive immunity, macrophage phagocytosis, and lymphocyte proliferation.

Antimicrobial Activity

Allicin is the key compound responsible for the antibacterial effect which is achieved by the disruption of microbial cell membranes by reacting with thiol groups in enzymes, and by inhibiting biofilm formation and virulence factors. Broad-spectrum activity against bacteria (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus, Helicobacter pylori), fungi (e.g., Candida albicans), and viruses (e.g., influenza) has been reported as part of garlic’s antimicrobial properties.

Cancer Chemoprevention

Organosulfur compounds (OSC) make apoptosis and cell cycle arrest through multiple pathways where Phase II detoxification and epigenetic modulation is involved. In Phase II detoxification, DAS increases glutathione S-transferase (GST) by activating nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), which binds to antioxidant response elements (ARE) in the GST gene promoter, enhancing carcinogen detoxification via glutathione conjugation and excretion. Diallyl trisulfide (DATS) inhibits histone deacetylases (HDACs), preventing histone deacetylation. This promotes chromatin relaxation, enabling the transcription of silenced tumor suppressor genes (e.g., p21, p53), thereby restoring their antiproliferative and proapoptotic functions in cancer cells.

Garlic organosulfur compounds induce apoptosis by activating caspase-3/9 through cytochrome c release from mitochondria, while suppressing antiapoptotic Bcl-2. This shifts the Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax)/Bcl-2 ratio, triggering the intrinsic apoptotic pathway in cancer cells. This has highlighted efficiency in prostate and breast cancer models.

Challenges and Limitations

Variability in Experimental Designs

Variability in experimental designsincluding preparation methods, dosage, model systems, and garlic sourcescontributes to inconsistent findings in garlic research. The administered dose of garlic in animal models for preclinical studies varies from 50–500 mg/kg body weight. Even human trials often use 300–1,500 mg of garlic powder daily. Challenges arise due to differences in bioavailability and metabolic responses between bioactive materials. Experimental models used in in vitro and in vivo condition studies show diverse approaches. For in vitro antimicrobial assays, bacterial cultures are used, where allicin shows strong effects at 25–100 μg/mL. In vivo (e.g., rodent studies) assess cardiovascular effects, with variations in strain, diet, and treatment duration. Clinical trials also faced variability in participant health status, genetic factors, and compliance.

All such variability occurs due to the chemical variability in garlic cultivars which varies by cultivar, growth conditions, and storage. Such natural variability necessitates precise reporting of garlic sources in studies. Preparation technique is also an important factor for such variability and challenges. Garlic’s bioactive compounds, such as allicin, are highly sensitive to processing. Studies use raw, aged, or powdered garlic, each yielding different biochemical profiles. For example, raw garlic converts alliin to allicin upon crushing in the presence of alliinase. Aged garlic extract (AGE) has stable sulfur compounds like S-allyl cysteine but lacks allicin. Standardization in study protocols and transparent reporting of methodologies are essential for reliable comparisons and clinical applications.

Standardization of Garlic Processing

Standardizing garlic processing is crucial for ensuring consistent bioactive compound levels in research and commercial products. Variations in crushing, heating, aging, and supplement formulation significantly impact garlic’s therapeutic potential.

Crushing and cutting of fresh garlic activate the enzyme alliinase, which converts alliin into allicin, the primary bioactive compound. On the other hand, the application of heat inactivates alliinase, reducing allicin formation. After extraction, freeze-drying can preserve more allicin compared to oven-drying. Garlic powder loses 30–50% of allicin during processing and storage due to moisture and temperature sensitivity. Studies recommend crushing garlic and allowing it to stand for 10 min before use to ensure optimal allicin production.

Similarly, boiling garlic decreases thiosulfinate content by up to 90%, while microwaving retains more bioactive compounds. Elevated temperature roasting can lead to the formation of different organosulfur compounds, such as ajoene and vinyldithiins, which have distinct biological effects. Aged Garlic Extract (AGE) is produced by soaking slices of garlic in ethanol for up to 20 months, which converts unstable allicin into stable compounds like S-allyl cysteine (SAC). AGE has higher antioxidant activity but reduced antimicrobial effects. Studies required the reporting of heat-treated garlic, precise temperature, and duration to ensure reproducibility. Aging duration should be specified by commercial AGE products and extraction methods to ensure consistency. Storage conditions of garlic powder should report moisture content, storage conditions, and particle size to control variability.

Limitations of Current Data

Garlic (Allium sativum) has been extensively studied for its potential health benefits. However, inconsistencies in research findings raise concerns about the reliability of current data, with key limitations in garlic research including variability in study designs, methodological flaws, and gaps in clinical evidence.

Many studies have small sample sizes and short trial durations (often <12 weeks) which fail to assess the long-term effects and result in reduced statistical evidence. , Studies also lack placebo-controlled, double-blind trials which introduces bias, particularly in human studies. For clinical trials, factors like participant genetics, medication use, and dietary habits can influence garlic’s effects, which are rarely controlled. Placebo effects are significant in trials measuring subjective outcomes like blood pressure.

In case of long-term use, data from in vitro and animal studies suggest anticancer and cardioprotective mechanisms; however, human clinical evidence remains weak. Most trials focus on surrogate markers (e.g., cholesterol levels) rather than hard endpoints (e.g., cardiovascular events). Despite promising findings, current garlic research suffers from methodological inconsistencies, lack of standardization, and insufficient clinical validation. Addressing these limitations is crucial for establishing evidence-based recommendations.

Future Directions

Further Research on Compound Stability

Chemical instability of garlic is a major challenge for research and therapeutic application even after it has various bioactive contents. Significant progress was made in past research where identification of content and its application were under focus, but regarding stability, bioavaibility, and standardized quality, a research gap remains. This article focuses on a few specific research gaps where future studies can be performed.

Limited Understanding of Degradation Kinetics

Most studies emphasize on the initial concentration of compounds rather than the degradation rate under different conditions. Impact of pH variation during the gastrointestinal transit of organosulfur compounds as well as temperature dependent degradation for allicin and ajoenes stability required further studies.

Inconsistent Methodologies for Compound Preservation

Standardized protocols are required for he stabilizing he unstable bioactives like allicin during extraction and analysis. Significant variations in garlic bioactive compounds are observed during sample preparation between freezing and lyophilization. No specific storage conditions are established for different compounds of garlic, especially for thiosulfinates.

Technological Limitations in Real-Time Monitoring

Nondestructive spectroscopic monitoring techniques like Raman and NIR spectroscopy methods need to be developed along with biosensors for continuous or real-time monitoring systems. Current HPLC methods are available, but they only provide a small portion of the content profile.

Bioavailability and Delivery System Challenges

Inadequate research and approaches on nanoencapsulation of bioactive compounds found to possess less than 5% bioavailability and has been reported for key organosulfur compounds due to their instability, which needs more novel approaches to enhance bioavailability.

Future studies may consider focusing on standardizing analytical protocols, optimizing different control parameters like crushing time and temperature, and reference standards preparation. It is also essential for future research to explore advance stabilization technology, cryoprotective formations, and AI-assisted prediction of optimal stabilization conditions. To overcome such research gaps, multidisciplinary collaboration among food, pharmaceutical, and clinical research is required.

Optimizing Garlic Processing for Health Benefits

Even after well-documented evidence of health benefits, the bioactive substances in garlic are vastly dependent on processing methods, which influence the concentration of bioactive materials and their stability. This article discussed some of the research directions to optimize the processing of garlic.

Allicin is highly unstable and degrades rapidly during processing. Commercial production of allicin yield varies due to unregulated processing conditions, which can be controlled by further optimization. Immobilized alliinase could enhance allicin production in supplements. The bioavailability of allicin is 5% in the oral route of administration which can be a major area of research for future aspects. Liposomal allicin shows 3–5× higher absorption than free compounds.

Thermal treatments reduce the thiosulfinate content by up to 90%, which can be controlled by high-pressure processing (HPP) that preserves thiosulfinates better than heat treatments. Pulsed electric fields (PEF) may enhance extraction efficiency without causing thermal degradation. Future research should prioritize the development of advanced processing methods like HPP, PEF, and enzyme stabilization, which can fully harness garlic health benefits. To improve bioavailability, different nanoencapsulation techniques should be approached which can also improve the stability by protecting the extract from the gastric environment.

Conclusion

Modern research highlights garlic (Allium sativum) as a prime example of food functioning as medicine, with its therapeutic benefits shaped by the intricate interaction between its bioactive compounds and human use. Its organosulfur compounds such as allicin (2.5–5.8 mg/g) and diallyl disulfide (DADS, 9.77 ± 1.64 mg/g)along with phenolics (4.1–9.8 mg GAE/g)form a multitargeted defense system. , Allicin delivers rapid antimicrobial effects but breaks down when exposed to heat, whereas aged garlic, rich in the more stable S-allyl cysteine (SAC, 0.3–1.2 mg/g), provides neuroprotective benefits. Fermentation alters garlic’s bioactive profile, as evidenced by black garlic’s increased ajoene content (0.1–0.5 mg/g) and enhanced antioxidant activity (92.1% DPPH inhibition compared to 78.5% in fresh garlic). Heat treatment can have mixed effects: boiling lowers allicin levels by around 30%, whereas carefully controlled heat during black garlic production boosts phenolic content by 40%. The selection of extraction solvent is also crucial, as it significantly influences the efficiency of extracting bioactive compounds and, consequently, the potency of the resulting extract, with ethanol extracts exhibiting twice the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) values of water-based preparations.

Cancer studies reveal that garlic’s bioactive compounds act in a dose-dependent manner where diallyl disulfide (DADS) can inhibit breast cancer cells at 50 μM, while S-allyl cysteine (SAC) requires a higher concentration of 2 mM to affect gastric cancer cells. , These observations are mirrored in cardiovascular research, where aged garlic extractrich in SACprovides superior endothelial protection compared to raw garlic. To fully harness garlic’s therapeutic potential, it is essential to align preparation methods with specific health goals: fresh garlic for antimicrobial effects, aged garlic for chronic disease prevention, and fermented garlic for enhanced antioxidant activity. Standardizing the active compounds is the crucial next step for translating these benefits into clinical applications.

Single-clove garlic (Allium sativum) is attracting attention in medical research because of its high allicin content, which provides strong antimicrobial, antioxidant, and cardioprotective effects. Its uniform structure allows for more consistent dosing, improving the reliability of drug formulations. Studies indicate potential benefits in managing hypertension and hyperlipidemia. Contemporary trends in the use of garlic indicate its traditional culinary values. Garlic is used as a multifunctional ingredient in food, nutraceuticals, and the wellness industry. The phytochemical composition of garlic, particularly organosulfur compounds, plays an important role in fulfilling this trend. The well-established biological activities of its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, cardioprotective, and immune-modulatory effects are perfectly aligned with consumer demand for natural health-promoting ingredients.

However, despite its promise, single-clove garlic remains relatively underexplored in pharmaceutical research. The majority of studies on bioactivities are in vitro, utilizing assays like DPPH and antimicrobial tests. These data do not show the critical information related to digestion, absorption, and metabolism which can only be reflected by in vivo studies. These studies lack comparative in vivo research that can validate the observed responses. This review identifies bioavailability as a significant gap in the literature. No studies have directly compared the bioavailability of key compounds in SCG and MCG. The lack of clinical trials and pharmacokinetic data limits standardization, and key gaps remain regarding bioavailability, optimal dosing, and long-term safety, highlighting the need for further investigation to confirm its therapeutic potential. ,
