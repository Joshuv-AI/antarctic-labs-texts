“I was devastated… sport is like an addiction nearly”: a qualitative study examining young athletes’ experiences with injury in sport
Authors: Ryan Summersby, Niamh Sheehan, Chris Bleakley, Brian Caulfield, Mark Matthews, Natalie Klempel, Sinead Holden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the developmental benefits of sport in adolescence, injury is common and can have a profound impact beyond just physical symptoms.This qualitative study aims to explore the lived experiences of adolescent athletes with sports- related injury in Ireland.This was a qualitative study using one-on-one semi-structured interviews and a phenomenology method. Twenty-one secondary school students from across the Island of Ireland aged 15–18 years who had experienced a sports-related injury within the last 2 years agreed to take part. Reflexive thematic analysis was used to analyse the collected data.Seventeen adolescents (eight female, mean age 16.2 (range 15–18) years) from five different sports were included for analysis. Predominantly negative experiences were described with four overarching themes identified through thematic analysis: playing through pain and injury, the social influence on playing through pain and injury behaviour, the psychological impact of sports injury and player confidence and its role in returning to sport.In conclusion, a culture of playing through pain and even injury is widely accepted in adolescent sports and is influenced by the social circles that surround these young athletes. The impact of sports injury goes beyond just the physical, with these athletes experiencing devastation and a loss of identity due to their absence from sport. Sports coaches can help reduce this negative impact by keeping injured players involved in the team/sport as much as possible. Conversely, sports injury also provided opportunity for personal growth in some cases, improving mental resilience and self-reliance.Keywords: adolescent, sport, injury

Introduction

Sports and physical activity can be central to adolescents developing self-esteem and social interaction skills, with team sports showing greater benefits compared with physical activity alone.1 However, sport can also result in injury, which can interrupt this period of development and lead to ‘unfavourable’ changes to body composition.2 Beyond physical impact, sports injuries can also negatively impact self-esteem and confidence.3 The biopsychosocial model4 is widely used in sports injury research to assess the interactions between biological, psychological and social factors that influence sports injury and recovery. The social and psychological interplay is often under-represented in research, yet it may be a key factor in adolescent sports injuries. With adolescents and adult athletes often reporting a predisposition to play through pain as they commonly report ignoring symptoms of injury,5 potentially increasing the likelihood of injury. Previous research on elite-level female gymnasts and senior-level rowers has shown that these athletes fear that they will be dropped from the team or perceived as weak by their teammates if they take time away due to pain or injury.6 7

The adolescent injury experience has not been as rigorously investigated in a mixed sport adolescent population, with most research focusing primarily on elite-level athletes or single sports.8,10 In a recent systematic review, we found that time-loss injuries can profoundly affect young athletes, leading to feelings of isolation, loss of identity, fear and frustration. Some youth athletes reported frequently ignoring symptoms of injury or modifying their participation levels as an alternative to taking time off.5 What drives these sports injury behaviours in adolescents is not well researched in an Irish sporting context to date. However, a recent scoping review highlights a growing body of research in recent years into the psychology of traditional Gaelic games in Ireland.11 This review highlights limitations within this research, with only 19% of the participants of the identified studies involving Gaelic games players under the age of 18 years. This underscores the need for targeted mixed-gender cohorts in Ireland to better understand the sports injury experiences of adolescents. It is important to consider the possibility of varying adolescent sports injury experiences within the Irish context, where cultural games including Gaelic football, hurling and camogie are popular. This contrasts with the North American context, where much of the existing qualitative research has been conducted.5 This is particularly relevant given the amateur nature and embedded community ethos of Gaelic games, where club teams often represent their local areas.

This study aimed to explore the lived experiences of youth athletes with sports-related injury in the general adolescent sporting population in Ireland. This can help inform clinical practice and youth sport governing body policy on factors that can improve adolescent sports injury experiences and the impact of injury.

Methods

Study design

This was a qualitative study using one-on-one semi-structured interviews and a phenomenological methodology, which seeks to describe participants’ perspectives and experiences of a particular phenomenon. In this case, it is important to understand the lived experiences of adolescent secondary school students who participate in sports regularly, have been impacted by a sports-related injury and the factors that influence their experiences. The study reporting follows the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ checklist (online supplemental material - COREQ checklist)).12 We focused on understanding the participants’ experiences without any preconceived ideas/theories using reflexive thematic analysis.

Participants

A convenience sample of participants was recruited through secondary school’s sports coaches and physical education (PE) teachers. Schools in Leinster and Ulster provinces on the Island of Ireland were contacted for potential engagement, as these are the provinces where the two collaborating universities—University College Dublin and Ulster University— are based. Schools where the research team had either previously engaged in research or knew the PE teacher/sports coach were targeted to allow recruitment of a convenience sample. However, there were no prior relationships between any of the participants and research team. Printed study information leaflets were distributed through points of contact in participating schools (sports coaches and PE teachers). School points of contact made the research team aware of any potentially eligible students who were interested in taking part. Students were eligible if they were secondary school students aged 12–18 years, who had experienced any sports-related injury within the last 2 years that impacted their sporting performance or ability to partake in sport. There were no restrictions on sports played, sporting level or injury type. A range of injury types was purposively included to ensure that findings reflected the diversity of sports-related injuries in adolescent cohorts. The cut-off time was 2 years from time of injury to reduce recall bias, which has been documented in similar studies.5 Recruitment continued until data saturation when the key domains outlined as part of the study aims had been described and no new themes or subthemes were being described.

Patient and public involvement

The interview guide was piloted with secondary school students who regularly played sport and had previously experienced a sports-related injury, to ensure suitability of the interview questions.

Interview guide

Semi-structured interviews involve an open discussion between interviewer and participant, with the discussion following a standard set of questions (interview guide) and probing for more detail, in order to answer the research question set out in the study. The key domains within the interview guide (table 1) were developed iteratively over several research team meetings and were informed by previous research and our collective research and clinical expertise. The semi-structured guide consisted of open-ended questions with the aim of exploring the domains of injury experience, injury management, impact of the injury on their sporting and personal life and culture/attitudes surrounding injury and return to sport (RTS). For each domain, there were two to three open-ended questions used to reduce risks of interpretive bias, with follow-up questions used to probe further and to capture vivid descriptions of the adolescents’ experiences. The interview guide was piloted with secondary school students who played sport regularly and had previously experienced a sports-related injury to ensure suitability of the interview questions. No changes were made to the interview guide after piloting, as students who elected to participate in the pilot reported they understood and found the questions appropriate to answer.

Interview and data collection procedures

Interviews were primarily conducted in a quiet room in the participants’ school with two researchers present in the room. The interviews were conducted by one researcher (NS or RS) while a second researcher (NS, SH or RS) was present in the back of the room to ensure participant comfort and child protection and welfare. To ensure consistency throughout the interview process, the researcher at the back of the room highlighted any questions missed by the interviewer and any areas related to the interview guide that were not sufficiently probed. Both NS and RS received targeted advice and direction on the qualitative methodology and interviewing skills prior to data collection from an independent qualitative researcher (AK). In addition, NS and RS had experience speaking to athletes about sports injuries as physiotherapists and piloted the interview guide independently to gain experience prior to commencing data collection. SH (female) has previous qualitative experience interviewing adolescents.

Interviews began with a short rapport building phase to put the participant at ease, where the interviewers explained who they were as researchers. Thereafter, for consistency, interviewers generally followed the interview guide with probing questions asked in order to gain more detail when needed. This allowed for flexibility within the interview process to explore new emergent topics related to the study domains of interest. Field notes were taken on initial thoughts and theories coming from interviews during/after each interview. In the event that any participant became upset, they were reminded that they could stop the interview at any time and were encouraged to speak to their sports coach or PE teacher if they felt the need for support. Interviews were audio recorded, then transcribed using a paid transcription service, which uses artificial intelligence for initial transcription and then a human proofreads the transcript to check for mistakes. Then any potentially identifying participant information was redacted prior to thematic analysis.

The average length of the interviews was 21 min (range 11–31 min). Shorter interviews (eg, 11–13 min) may possibly reflect participants’ level of engagement and sense of comfort in sharing. This may be considered short for some phenomenological studies. However, acknowledging the participants’ young age and the importance of maintaining their comfort throughout the interview process, interviews were participant-led, allowing them to speak freely about their experiences. Furthermore, despite the relatively short nature of these interviews, the interviews still yielded rich thematically relevant findings. Due to practical considerations, two of the 17 included interviews took place over Zoom video call.

Data analysis

A reflexive thematic analysis approach was used to analyse the data and was guided by Braun et al.13 Two researchers (NS and RS) read the transcripts multiple times to become familiar with the data, and then both independently coded each of the interview transcripts line by line using NVivo software (V.12). This then created an initial index of codes derived directly from the data from both coders. Quotes were coded at the lowest thematic level as either descriptive or interpretive codes. After coding each interview, NS and RS cross-checked each other’s codes to ensure valid interpretation of quotes and agreement in findings. Codes were then categorised into larger concepts and themes that helped to answer the domain questions of interest in this study. Data analysis was an iterative process, which involved discussing through peer evaluation as a research team with refinement and revising of themes and subthemes. Once the research team was in agreement, the themes were then provided with an overall description. Themes and matching codes were interrogated by senior researchers on the team (female: SH; male: CB, MM and BC) at all stages of data analysis to ensure transparency and validity of findings.

To ensure the trustworthiness and credibility of our findings, several methods were employed. This included negative case analysis. The majority of initial thematic analysis highlighted the negative mental impact of sports injury experiences, our analysis continued to systematically examine the interview data for reports that contradicted this emerging theme. For example, one participant described how their injury experience was a positive self-improvement experience with improved sense of self-reliance due to the rehabilitation away from the team environment. Instead of excluding this account, we included it in the analysis. Including non-conforming cases helped to refine and contextualise the varying individual participant experiences detailed throughout the interviews and ensure credibility in our findings.

Research team’s reflexivity

The research team consisted of four senior academic researchers with extensive backgrounds in sports injury rehabilitation and research, along with two postgraduate student researchers: a female research master’s student (NS) and a male PhD student (RS), both physiotherapists with experience working with adolescent athletes. The senior researchers added vital insights into the interpretation of the adolescents’ experiences and ensured a rigorous data analysis process throughout. Some of the researchers on the team have prior experience in competitive sport including field hockey, rugby, Gaelic football, climbing and running. This provided insightful perspectives on the cultures and injury experiences within these sports. Similarly, some of the research team have worked as sports clinicians helping to rehabilitate adolescents after sports injury. These experiences helped with interpreting the interview data and helped to contextualise some of the thematic findings. However, the research team’s background did come with the potential risk of findings being driven by the researchers’ own personal experiences and research knowledge, rather than emerging naturally from the participants’ descriptions of the sports injury phenomenon. To reduce the risk of interpretation bias, the coding of the interview transcripts was conducted solely by the junior researchers (RS and NS), and each team member had the opportunity to independently examine the study quotes. The research group discussed reflexivity and their interpretation as well as challenging the interpretation of other team members. Discussions continued until a group consensus was reached.

Results

Participants

Twenty-one students were recruited from Ireland (n=19) and Northern Ireland (n=2). Four participant interviews were excluded postinterview due to their injury occurring >2 years ago, which may have affected recall. These four interviews were retrospectively excluded as time since injury was only calculated after the interview to ensure the interviewer was focused during the interview process. Seventeen interviews were included in the analysis (nine males, eight females; mean age=16.2 years, age ranged 15–18 years of age; table 2). Injuries reported included both traumatic and gradual onset. The median time since injury onset to the interview was 8 months. Sports played were Gaelic football (n=10), field hockey (n=3), soccer (n=2), camogie (n=1) and basketball (n=1). Some participants played at school, club and county/regional level. The most common injury type was a fracture (n=6), followed by muscle injury (n=5) (table 2).

Playing through pain and injury within youth sports

Many participants expressed that playing through pain and even injury was common during their sports experiences and acknowledged this may cause greater injury, even disregarding medical advice in order to play on. This was common across various sports as illustrated in box 1. This behaviour often stemmed from demanding schedules, with athletes committed to multiple teams or sports, which necessitated training on consecutive days without adequate recovery. One of these athletes (Patrick) commented that they often have played through pain and have even continued to play despite knowing they had a serious ongoing injury.

Often I’m playing through pain, I’d say one out of three games I’m experiencing pain at the moment, definitely be playing through a lot ……I was playing with the stress fracture because I just did these [regional] trials……it definitely impacted my performance, I was almost annoyed that I didn’t get to show how good I actually was because it was sore the whole time.—Patrick (male, soccer, long-term injury)

Athletes competing at higher than school/club level (county/regional level) had the most difficulty balancing their commitments and felt additional pressures like higher performance expectations; some felt a perpetual need to ‘prove themselves’, leading them to persist with training and competition despite pain and injury like Patrick. Similarly, in Frank’s case, after sustaining a fracture in a championship semi-final, he is willing to play through ‘agony’ in order to play the final, which he perceives as priority over receiving appropriate medical management (casting).

I went to the hospital first……they (Doctors) wanted me to get a cast, I said no for the first few weeks because I wanted to play the championship final, then played the championship final taped up, probably did more damage, then went and got a cast, I was gone for six weeks…. I had to play, I had to play and I was in agony with any sort of movement.—Frank (male, Gaelic football, long-term injury)

Despite the ‘agony’ while playing and the acknowledgement by Frank that playing on through his injury may have led to more ‘damage’, he undoubtedly reports he would do it again if a similar situation arose again.

The social influence on playing through pain and injury behaviour within youth sports

Participants commonly illustrated how social factors influenced their decision to play on through pain and injury. Social factors—including the normalisation of playing through injury within team culture, pressure from envious teammates eager to take an injured player’s place and the coach’s attitude either supporting or discouraging such risk-taking behaviour—can significantly influence an athlete’s injury experience and decision-making. Playing through pain and injury as described by Frank and Patrick was commonly reported. This behaviour appeared to be influenced by their social circle with their teammates, coaches and even parents in some capacity supporting or refuting this behaviour. The immediate social influence of teammates’ behaviours and interactions helps to construct the team culture and in the case of Orla’s team their accepted use of painkillers to play through pain is evidence of the culture that may be present in youth sports. Orla describes the use of medication to mask pain to enable playing while injured, which appears to be an accepted culture within her team.

…almost everyone is carrying some sort of, they call them niggles, people have a sore knee or muscles are tight, we train quite a lot, so people will…… Sometimes (take painkillers) it would be definitely more common because people would feel they want to play.—Orla (female, hockey, long-term injury)

Similarly, participants described the social interactions with teammates when returning to the team postinjury could garner a sense of frustration or resentment among teammates. Lisa highlighted this resentment and added pressure to perform to prove her ‘worth’ within the team on her RTS due to comments from teammates that she perceived as contempt for her return to the team.

You’d hear them (teammates)……some of them were just like oh you’re coming training today I thought you weren’t playing, and oh you’re taking my spot… You could tell from some people the way they acted when you come in.—Lisa (female, hockey, short-term injury)

Our data also highlighted the pivotal role of the coach and their social influence on playing through pain and injury behaviour. The coach as an authority figure within youth sports sets the precedence of what is acceptable within the team. In Frank’s case, he is passively supported in this playing through injury behaviour by his coach who initially voices his disagreement to playing with a fracture but in the end still selects Frank to play.

(Coach) didn’t think I should play…they thought I was mad in the head playing, I said I had to play.—Frank (male, Gaelic football, long-term injury)

The coach in Frank’s case passively supports this risk-taking behaviour by selecting the player and illustrates that in some capacity they are accepting of it. Contrasting this, we also found that the social influence of the coach had the potential to discourage this behaviour, which in some cases led to a more positive and empathetic sporting environment.

With the club I felt a little bit safe not safe but belonged there because someone else (Coach) was on the same boat as me. Well, was on the same boat as he could nearly guide me what to do and what was best because he suffered from it.—Harry (male, Gaelic football, long-term injury)

This coach-athlete relationship was reinforced by the coach having suffered a similar injury during their own career; the shared experience fostered empathy and a sense of trust for the athlete where playing through pain and injury was actively discouraged by the coach and injury rehabilitation was prioritised.

The psychological impact of sports injury

In many cases, participants’ response to injury was complex and emotive regardless of sport played, gender or expected time away from sport as seen in box 1. Participants illustrated a generally negative injury experience with feelings of devastation, social isolation, a loss of identity and common emotional and psychological impacts. However, some positive impacts were reported, including improved self-reliance and mental resilience to complete their injury rehabilitation to RTS. Much of the negative experiences were underpinned by the central role that sport plays in their lives. For many of the participants, sport represented a central aspect of their identity, daily routine and social life, and injury-induced absence became an extremely isolating and frustrating experience.

Quinn encapsulates the complex and profound effect of injury on adolescents as he describes the variety of emotions and sense of loss he faced.

I was devastated. I had nothing, I was raging at having to see all my mates playing and I was just sitting on the sidelines, because I love it, but I didn’t know what to do with myself…if you got up in the morning and you had a match, you were happy and then you just always want to play, it’s just, like an addiction nearly…It’s a big part of my life, it’s probably what I look forward to most in life. Since I love it so much, not being able to play it was just devastating.—Quinn (male, Gaelic football, short-term injury)

Despite the overwhelmingly negative devastating psychological impact of injury participants described, we did find evidence that some participants used the adversity of injury as a catalyst for self-improvement and personal growth. In Alex’s example, he initially reports, “In every sense you’re going to feel like shit”, as he watches on helplessly while his team loses. However, he later acknowledges gaining a greater sense of self-accountability and mental strength through his rehabilitation journey.

If somebody had [similar injury], I’d tell them you’re going to feel like shit for a lot of the time, but the only way to get over it is to do the exercises, put the head down and get through it, you’ll come out the other side feeling better, not that you’ve done your hamstring but that you know what it’s like to not have sport and you know what it’s like to have to rely on yourself, because nobody else can do your exercises for you, and you have to do them, so you have to rely on yourself to get yourself back to where you need to be.—Alex (male, Gaelic football, short-term injury)

Player confidence and its role in returning to sport postinjury

Player confidence or lack of it played a critical role in determining postinjury outcomes and performance on RTS. Participants highlighted the importance of feeling both physically and mentally confident to RTS and perform effectively. However, fear and anxiety surrounding re-injury were commonly reported by participants on RTS irrespective of the sport played or the magnitude of injury (see box 1). This often led to a lack of confidence in the injured area, with many athletes holding themselves back and experiencing reduced performance on RTS. Participants’ confidence was heavily influenced by this anxiousness and fear of re-injury, sometimes stemming from the unexpected and seemingly simple nature of their initial injury.

I remember going back after [injury], you’re nervous doing anything, running, kicking for a few months after you’re back because it felt nothing before that, I just kicked the ball and it just went. So, you’d be nervous….—Ian (male, soccer, long-term injury)

As a result of this anxiousness and fear of re-injury, players struggled to fully commit to effort on the field due to concerns about exacerbating their injury, creating a cycle of lack of confidence and decreasing performance, as Ian and Quinn depict.

I was just quite annoyed a lot of the time because I knew I wasn’t playing my best. But there was nothing I could do about it, I wasn’t fit yet and I wasn’t ready yet and it was just tough…. I felt like I was almost wasted.—Quinn (male, Gaelic football, short-term injury)

Participants emphasised the importance of feeling both physically and mentally ready to give themselves confidence in their ability to RTS. This is facilitated by the player’s trust in the rehabilitation they have done, as Niamh describes.

I think first to feel confident mentally, I have to feel confident physically, so my first priority is to get better and making sure that it’s in top shape to then get back into training and then once I get back into training I can slot back into how it was and obviously I’ll have to work on the confidence mentally but I think that has to start with the physical side first.—Niamh (female, basketball, short-term injury)

Discussion

This study used a qualitative phenomenology approach, with four major themes identified as being integral to the youth sports injury experience. Figure 2 provides illustrative examples from our data, outlining how these four themes potentially impact injury experiences in youth sport. This figure was developed iteratively within the research team and represents some of the common experiences that we identified. The left side of the figure demonstrates an example of a negative injury experience, where risk-taking behaviours, like playing through pain and injury, can be reinforced by an athlete’s sporting social circle. This can create a negative feedback loop where risk taking is normalised, leading to rushed or inadequate rehabilitation, a premature RTS and ultimately a fear of re-injury. The right side of the illustration is an example of how the athlete’s injury experience can be ameliorated through a more supportive and empathetic environment. The athlete may still start out playing through pain and subsequently become injured, but they are involved in a more supportive and empathetic sporting environment, where rest and proper rehabilitation for their injury is encouraged. This may lead to a more positive injury experience, where in turn they can share their experiences within their sporting social circle, leading to a more supportive team culture.

Illustrative examples of potential injury experiences within a ‘playing through injury’ sporting culture (left) and a supportive injury sport environment (right).

A culture of playing through pain and injury within youth sports

The data revealed a pervasive culture of playing through pain and even injury, underpinned by several influencing factors which, to our knowledge, have not previously been highlighted in an Irish sporting context. In general, adolescents are more likely to have a sense of invincibility and proclivity for risk-taking behaviour.14 Neurodevelopmental models suggest that risk taking in adolescents is underpinned by weak behavioural control and a high sensitivity to reward.15 This is underscored by our analysis and other studies,14 which highlight that risk taking and poor decision-making, including playing through pain, were underpinned by short-term perspectives, the athlete’s perceived importance of sport and a perceived sense of immunity from injury. The Theory of Planned Behaviour is a framework that can be used to understand why athletes engage in this risk-taking behaviour16; it illustrates how certain behaviours, including playing through pain, are directed by the athlete’s attitudes towards the behaviour, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control.16 In the current sports injury qualitative research, this behaviour is often linked to athletes feeling the need to prove their toughness and commitment to the team. A culture of playing through pain—reinforced by the athletes’ social circle, including coaches, teammates and parents—further normalises this behaviour, establishing it as a subjective norm. Athletes’ attitudes towards the behaviour, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control collectively shape their intentions regarding sports injury prevention and rehabilitation.17,19

The psychological impact of sports injury

The psychological impact of sports injury on young players is profound, with a mix of generally negative emotions and a sense of isolation and loss due to being forced away from an activity they play so regularly, daily in some cases. A study with females (aged 16–23 years) post-ACL injury found that absence from sport evoked a strong sense of identity loss, which aligned with the depictions of many of the participants within our study.20 After an injury, the athlete is often annexed from much of their social network, which creates a fear of missing out similar to the experiences described by Quinn, Patrick and Alex. This has been shown to drive negative emotions, such as loneliness, frustration and a sense of social exclusion.21 Negative sporting experience, lack of social support and injury and burnout have been shown to impact premature dropout from sport.22 Optimal management of sports injuries is underpinned when the athlete is immersed in an empathetic and supportive social environment23 and suboptimal management can be exacerbated by players feeling a pressure from teammates and coaches to play, thus ignoring injury.24

Implications to practice

National youth sports governing bodies need to strongly challenge this widespread play through pain and injury behaviour within youth sports. Previous research in New Zealand has used transformative learning principles to tackle similar harmful youth sporting behaviours.25 This research using transformative learning principles, illustrated how employing reflective practice and challenging the attitudes of adults, youth and sport coaches has the ability to positively influence youth sporting behaviours.25 The transformative learning principles illustrated by Walters et al25 should be used by national youth sports governing bodies. These principles include critical reflection for sports coaches after engaging with emotive youth sporting stories to shift coaches’ attitudes towards a culture that prioritises well-being, inclusion and sustainable participation for adolescents. Similarly, educational courses emphasising the risk of more substantial injury associated with playing on through pain or more minor injury and ignoring injury symptoms should be implemented by national sports governing bodies.26 27

For sports clinicians, the findings of this study highlight the need for incorporating psychological components to assessing and managing injury for the promotion of sustainable and long-term participation in sport.28 When making decisions on an athlete’s readiness to RTS beyond physical assessment, sports clinicians would benefit from using the psychological assessment tools. The Injury-Psychological Readiness to Return to Sport Scale is one such psychological readiness tool that sports clinicians can use to inform their decision-making when deciding on an athlete’s RTS readiness.29 Similarly, the psychological impact of injury findings highlights the need for sports clinicians to be aware of the psychological impact of injury and to support young athletes through injury, providing potential resources that can help reduce the impact including self-talk, goal setting and education.30

Limitations

The research team aimed to avoid over-representation of one sport, school or gender in the participant sample by monitoring for a relatively even distribution across these variables. However, as this was a convenience sample involving adolescents who volunteered to engage in the research, an even split was not always achieved. The sample for this study included participants from the Island of Ireland who predominantly played Gaelic football, meaning that some of the findings may not be directly applicable to other cultural settings outside of Ireland. Similarly, although the inclusion criteria allowed for participants aged 12–18 years, no individuals younger than 15 years of age volunteered, limiting the generalisability of the findings to this younger adolescent group. This may highlight the difficulties of recruiting young adolescents for sports injury research. Another limitation of this study is the potential for recall bias due to the retrospective nature of participants’ experience with injury. Due to the heterogeneous sample of injuries reported from participants, the findings from this study are reflective of the overall general adolescent sports injury experiences and the exact impact of a sports injury on an adolescent will depend on a variety of factors including the severity of injury they experienced.

Conclusion

There appears to be a culture within youth sports of overtraining and playing through injury and pain, driven by the perceived importance of sport and the associated social figures in adolescents’ sporting lives. As a result, the psychological impact of injury was significant, with feelings of isolation, loss of identity and fear of missing out exacerbating the physical challenges of rehabilitation. This research may have implications for creating a culture shift in adolescent sport to focus on physical and mental health well-being, fostering a more supportive environment for injured players. This may be achieved by coaches creating a transparent supportive injury reporting environment.
