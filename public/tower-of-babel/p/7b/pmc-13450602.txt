A Comparison of postoperative hemodynamic trajectories in donation after brain death versus circulatory death heart transplantation1
Authors: Mark Petrovic, Christian Eidson, Chen Chia Wang, Walter Navid, Awab Ahmad, Kevin McGann, Aaron M Williams, John Trahanas, Swaroop Bommareddi, Tarek Absi, Eric Quintana, Aniket S Rali, Kelly H Schlendorf, Matthew Bacchetta, Ashish S Shah, Brian Lima

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac power output index (CPOi) has emerged as a prognostic marker after heart transplantation (HT), yet its application to donation after circulatory death (DCD) hearts remains unstudied. We compared early hemodynamic trajectories in DBD versus DCD HT and evaluated CPOi adjusted for vasoactive-inotropic score (CPOiVIS) as a screening tool for postoperative morbidity and mortality.MethodsThis is a single-center retrospective study of adult heart transplants (2020–2025), excluding multiorgan transplants, congenital heart disease, and patients requiring VA-ECMO within 72 h. Hourly CPOi, VIS, CPOiVIS, right ventricular CPOi, and pulmonary artery pulsatility index were compared between groups. ROC analysis identified screening thresholds for a composite endpoint (ventilation >72 h, ICU >15 days, renal replacement therapy, or 90-day mortality). The effect of donor type on hemodynamics was assessed using multivariable regression.ResultsAmong 500 patients (315 DBD, 185 DCD), hemodynamic trajectories did not differ across all metrics and timepoints. CPOiVIS was the strongest predictor of the composite endpoint (AUC 0.729–0.752 at T24-T72), outperforming CPOi alone. Optimal ROC thresholds demonstrated negative predictive values of 83–89%. After adjustment for recipient creatinine, hypertension, and ischemic time, donor type had no effect on any hemodynamic parameter. The composite endpoint occurred in 27.6% of DCD versus 23.2% of DBD recipients (p = 0.285).ConclusionsAmong HT recipients who did not require early mechanical circulatory support, early allograft function did not differ significantly between DBD and DCD recipients. CPOiVIS outperformed CPOi as a screening metric for morbidity and mortality, with thresholds applicable regardless of donor type.Keywords: cardiac power output index, donation after circulatory death, heart transplantation, hemodynamic monitoring, vasoactive-inotropic score

Introduction

Advances in donation after circulatory death (DCD) heart transplantation (HT) have significantly increased donor availability, with one UK center reporting a 48% increase in transplant activity through DCD utilization,1 and recent analyses estimating that widespread adoption could expand the donor pool by up to 30%.2, 3 As DCD HT continues to expand, robust tools for assessing early allograft function become increasingly important.

Cardiac power output index (CPOi), calculated as CO × (MAP − CVP) / 451 and indexed to body surface area, provides a continuous, quantitative measure of cardiac performance that integrates both pressure and flow.4, 5, 6, 7 CPO has demonstrated prognostic value across a spectrum of acute cardiac disease, including cardiogenic shock,8 cardiac ICU populations,9 and post-cardiac arrest care.10 In HT, Lim et al. demonstrated that lower CPOi at ICU arrival and 6 h post-transplant was strongly associated with severe primary graft dysfunction and 30-day mortality.4, 5 The same group introduced the concept of VIS-adjusted CPOi (CPOiVIS), normalizing cardiac power for vasoactive-inotropic support, though this metric has not been further validated.4, 5

Separately, the vasoactive-inotropic score (VIS) has been independently associated with morbidity and mortality after HT,11, 12, 13 and the pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi) has proven valuable for predicting right ventricular failure in LVAD populations,14, 15, 16 though its utility in the post-HT setting remains uncertain.17 To date, no study has simultaneously evaluated CPOi, CPOiVIS, right ventricular CPOi, and PAPi in a single transplant cohort, nor has any study examined these parameters in DCD recipients. While severe PGD remains the standard framework for evaluating early allograft dysfunction,18 continuous hemodynamic metrics such as CPOi offer a more granular alternative that can track graft function along a continuum rather than at a single categorical threshold.

We therefore aimed to (1) characterize continuous hemodynamic trajectories across five metrics in DBD versus DCD HT recipients over the first 72 postoperative hours, (2) evaluate CPOiVIS as a screening tool for a composite morbidity and mortality endpoint, and (3) determine whether donor type independently influences early allograft hemodynamic performance.

Methods

Study population

All adult HTs performed at a single center between January 2020 and June 2025 were retrospectively reviewed and stratified by donor type: donation after brain death (DBD) versus donation after circulatory death (DCD). Cases involving multiorgan transplantation, recipients with congenital heart disease, investigational preservation devices, and utilization of the rapid recovery with extended ultraoxygenated preservation (REUP) technique19 were excluded. Patients requiring venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) intraoperatively or within 72 h of transplantation were excluded, as hemodynamic data under mechanical circulatory support reflects adequacy of support rather than allograft function. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Vanderbilt University Medical Center (IRB #241909).

Hemodynamic definitions

Hemodynamic parameters were recorded hourly from ICU arrival through 72 postoperative hours. Cardiac power output (CPO) was calculated as CO × (MAP – mean RAP) / 451, where CO is cardiac output measured by thermodilution recorded as mean of three measurements, MAP is mean arterial pressure, and RAP right atrial pressure.5, 6, 7 CPO was indexed to body surface area (BSA) to derive the cardiac power output index (CPOi), with BSA calculated as sqrt(height × weight / 3600). The vasoactive-inotropic score (VIS) was calculated as dopamine (µg/kg/min) + dobutamine (µg/kg/min) + 100 × epinephrine (µg/kg/min) + 100 × norepinephrine (µg/kg/min) + 10 × milrinone (µg/kg/min) + 10,000 × vasopressin (U/kg/min) according to the 2014 ISHLT Consensus Statement.18 A composite hemodynamic efficiency index was defined as CPOiVIS = CPOi / sqrt(VIS + 1) × 100.5, 20 Right ventricular cardiac power output (RV-CPO) was calculated as: RV-CPO = CO * mean pulmonary arterial pressure * 0.0022.21, 22 Pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi) is defined as the ratio of PA pulse pressure to right atrial pressure.

Outcomes

The principal outcomes of interest were postoperative trends in CPOi, CPOiVIS, RV CPOi, and PAPi over the first 72 h. A composite morbidity and mortality endpoint was defined as the occurrence of any of the following: mechanical ventilation exceeding 72 h, ICU length of stay exceeding 15 days, renal replacement therapy, or 90-day mortality.23 This composite was designed to aggregate the interrelated downstream consequences of early hemodynamic insufficiency, namely diminished cardiac power and escalating vasopressor and inotrope support in recipients not requiring VA-ECMO. Additional outcomes assessed included ICU and hospital length of stay, and survival at 30 days, 1 year, and 2 years.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test and are reported as (n, %). Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and are reported as median (Q1 – Q3). Hemodynamic parameters (CPOi, VIS, CPOiVIS, RV CPOi, and PAPi) were compared at ICU arrival (T0), 6, 24, and 72 h post-transplant. These timepoints encompass the window within which primary graft dysfunction is defined by the 201418 and 202424 ISHLT consensus statements, as well as the early postoperative period evaluated in prior studies of cardiac power following heart transplantation.4, 5 Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed for each metric at each timepoint, with optimal screening thresholds identified using the Youden index as cutoffs for the composite endpoint. The discrimination of CPOiVIS and CPOi was compared at each timepoint using the DeLong test for paired ROC curves, with 95% confidence intervals for the difference in AUC estimated by bootstrap resampling (2000 replicates). The effect of donor type on each hemodynamic parameter was assessed using linear regression, both unadjusted and adjusted for recipient preoperative creatinine, hypertension status, and total graft ischemic time, with results expressed as percent difference from the DBD mean. P-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Of 539 patients meeting initial eligibility criteria, 22 of 337 (6.5%) DBD and 17 of 202 (8.4%) DCD cases were excluded due to VA-ECMO requirement within the first 72 postoperative hours. The final analytic cohort comprised 500 patients, including 315 (63%) DBD and 185 (37%) DCD recipients (Table 1).

Compared to DBD donors, DCD donors were younger (29 [23–38] vs 33 [26–42] years, p < 0.001), less likely to be female (20.0% vs 36.8%, p < 0.001), and more likely to have died from blunt trauma (27.6% vs 16.2%). DCD donors were also less likely to have comorbidities including a ≥20 pack-year smoking history (10.8% vs 14.9%, p = 0.020), diabetes (2.2% vs 6.6%, p = 0.026), and hypertension (18.9% vs 19.7%, p = 0.008). Predicted heart mass was similar between groups (0.93 [0.83–1.09] vs 0.95 [0.83–1.07], p = 0.967).

The distribution of preservation strategies for DBD hearts was 58.1% static cold storage (SCS) on ice, 37.8% SCS at 10°C, and 4.1% normothermic machine perfusion (NMP). Among DCD hearts, 154 (83.2%) were recovered using thoracoabdominal normothermic regional perfusion (TA-NRP); of these, 84 (54.5%) were preserved with SCS on ice and 70 (45.5%) with SCS at 10°C. The remaining 31 (16.8%) DCD grafts were recovered via direct procurement and preserved with NMP (p < 0.001 for overall preservation strategy distribution compared to DBD).

Recipients of DCD hearts had higher BMIs (30.3 [26.3–34.6] vs 29.0 [25.6–32.8], p = 0.014), were less likely to be transplanted as Status 1 or 2 (3.2% and 20.5% vs 8.6% and 28.6%, respectively; p = 0.018), were less likely to be inpatient at the time of organ offer (34.1% vs 48.6%, p = 0.002), and were less likely to be bridged to transplant using an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP; 10.3% vs 21.6%, p = 0.001). Intraoperative and postoperative outcomes were similar between groups, including cardiopulmonary bypass duration (131 [103−165] vs 143 [114−168] minutes, p = 0.084), requirement for new IABP (5.4% vs 4.8%, p = 0.832), renal replacement therapy (21.1% vs 17.1%, p = 0.286), ICU length of stay (7 [5–11] vs 7 [5–10] days, p = 0.397), hospital length of stay (17 [13–25] vs 17 [13–24] days, p = 0.343), and 2-year survival (94.1% vs 94.9%, p = 0.720). The composite morbidity and mortality endpoint (M-M) occurred in 27.6% of DCD and 23.2% of DBD recipients (p = 0.285). Postoperative Hemodynamics and VIS

Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated that CPOi showed moderate discriminative ability (AUC 0.627–0.671), peaking at T24 (0.671, 95% CI: 0.616–0.725) (Figure 3A). CPOiVIS was the strongest predictor of the composite endpoint across all timepoints, with largest AUCs of 0.671 (95% CI: 0.616–0.725) at T24 and 0.752 (0.702–0.802) at T72 (Figure 3B). RV CPOi did not demonstrate meaningful discrimination at any timepoint (AUC 0.512–0.569), with curves closely approximating the reference line (Figure 3C). PAPi demonstrated modest discriminative ability at T0 (AUC 0.572) that diminished at later timepoints (AUC 0.533–0.549; Figure 3D). Direct comparison confirmed that CPOiVIS discriminated the composite outcome better than CPOi from 6 h onward, with a progressively widening margin (ΔAUC +0.036, 95% CI: 0.006–0.068, p = 0.024 at T6; +0.058, 95% CI: 0.028–0.087, p < 0.001 at T24; +0.090, 95% CI: 0.052–0.127, p < 0.001 at T72), with no difference at ICU arrival (ΔAUC +0.015, 95% CI: −0.017–0.046, p = 0.330). Based on these findings, CPOiVIS was selected as the primary screening metric for further threshold analysis. CPOiVIS distributions were similar between DBD and DCD recipients at all postoperative timepoints (Figure 5). Median values increased in both groups from T0 through T72, consistent with graft recovery and inotrope weaning. Wilcoxon rank-sum testing confirmed no significant difference between groups at any timepoint (all p > 0.4). At each timepoint, comparable proportions of DBD and DCD recipients fell below the optimal screening threshold (T0: 64% vs 66%; T6: 39% vs 41%; T24: 47% vs 45%; T72: 45% vs 53%).

After adjusting for recipient preoperative creatinine, hypertension status, and total graft ischemic time, covariates selected based on their association with the composite endpoint (Supplemental Table 1), donor type had no significant effect on any hemodynamic parameter at any postoperative timepoint (Figure 6). CPOi values were within 2.9% of the DBD mean across all timepoints (all p > 0.27). CPOiVIS differences were similarly small, ranging from −3.1–2.4% (all p > 0.52). Discussion

This study, one of the largest single-center reports comparing early post-HT hemodynamics between DBD and DCD recipients in a contemporary cohort, found that DBD and DCD donor grafts followed comparable early hemodynamic trajectories following HT. In our cohort of 500 adult HT recipients who did not require post-HT VA-ECMO, those receiving DBD and DCD grafts had similar CPOi, VIS, CPOiVIS, RV CPOi, and PAPi profiles through the first 72 postoperative hours. CPOiVIS was identified as the strongest screening metric for a composite morbidity and mortality endpoint23 comprising mechanical ventilation exceeding 72 h, ICU length of stay exceeding 15 days, renal replacement therapy, or 90-day mortality. After multivariable adjustment for recipient hypertension, preoperative serum creatinine, and total graft ischemic time, donor type had no independent effect on any postoperative hemodynamic parameter. To our knowledge, this represents the largest institutional experience describing early postoperative hemodynamics following HT with a specific focus on the effect of DCD donor type.

Evaluation of early allograft function following HT is multifaceted, encompassing hemodynamic, echocardiographic, biochemical, and clinical assessments.5, 18, 25, 26 Severe primary graft dysfunction (PGD) remains the standard framework for this evaluation18 and carries significant prognostic importance for both short- and long-term survival.27, 28 However, severe PGD is a diagnosis of exclusion rather than a specific hemodynamic measure, which may encompass presentations ranging from failure to wean from cardiopulmonary bypass to delayed graft failure within 24 h.29 This binary classification groups patients who narrowly avoid mechanical circulatory support with those who have uncomplicated graft function, potentially masking clinically meaningful gradations of graft impairment.30 Moreover, there is increasing evidence that PGD may represent distinct biological phenomena in DBD and DCD hearts,31, 32, 33 further complicating its utility as a uniform metric across donor types. The composite endpoint used here reflects this continuum: difficult separation from cardiopulmonary bypass and inadequate early graft power and rising vasopressor requirements result in hypoperfusion and acute kidney injury, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and prolonged ICU length of stay.34 CPOiVIS, as a continuous measure integrating both cardiac performance and pharmacologic support, may better capture this continuum. In the present study, CPOiVIS demonstrated stronger discriminative ability for the composite endpoint than any individual metric at all postoperative timepoints, supporting the value of graded hemodynamic assessment beyond binary PGD classification.

We sought to benchmark these findings against the foundational work by Lim et al., who evaluated CPOi and CPOiVIS in 140 patients (2014–2019) using a composite endpoint of VA-ECMO requirement or death within 30 days.4, 5 Their study excluded patients established on VA-ECMO from cardiopulmonary bypass but included those who developed delayed-onset severe PGD, yielding AUCs of 0.90 and 0.92 for CPOi at T0 and T6, respectively. Notably, CPOiVIS offered no additional predictive value over CPOi alone in that cohort. Our optimal CPOi threshold at T0 (0.33 W/m²) closely approximated their published cutoff of 0.34 W/m², providing external validation in an independent, larger cohort that includes DCD recipients (Supplemental Figure S2). However, our AUCs for CPOi (0.627–0.671) were substantially lower, likely reflecting differences in endpoint severity and cohort composition: we excluded all patients requiring VA-ECMO within 72 h (7.2% overall; 6.5% DBD, 8.4% DCD) and employed a broader morbidity composite rather than VA-ECMO or death. By focusing on this subtler spectrum of graft dysfunction, adjustment for inotropic support adds discriminatory information. This may explain why CPOiVIS outperformed CPOi in our study but not in theirs. CPOiVIS demonstrated its strongest predictive value at T24 and T72, timepoints not evaluated by Lim et al., with thresholds that increased progressively from 7.5 to 15.4, reflecting the expected trajectory of graft recovery and inotrope weaning (Supplemental Figure S3). These findings are consistent with prior work from our center demonstrating comparable VIS between DCD and DBD recipients at 24 and 48 h35 and comparable VIS at ICU arrival and 24 h in a TA-NRP-specific comparison.36 The present study extends these observations by characterizing continuous hourly hemodynamic trajectories across multiple metrics through 72 h. Across all timepoints, these thresholds demonstrated high negative predictive values (83–89%), supporting their utility as screening cutoffs, though positive predictive values were lower (32–41%), consistent with the 24.8% event rate and an intended screening rather than diagnostic application. In practice, CPOiVIS would be best used as a continuously available screening metric, where a value below a timepoint-specific threshold would prompt closer surveillance and clinical caution. Any such utilization of CPOiVIS would require prospective validation, with careful documentation of the post-operative selection of vasopressors and inotropes. Variable contribution of each agent to the VIS may explain the modest, but statistically significant, improvement in predictive power of CPOiVIS compared to CPOi alone.

After multivariable adjustment for recipient creatinine, hypertension, and total graft ischemic time, DCD status had no significant effect on CPOi, CPOiVIS, or RV CPOi at any timepoint. PAPi at 6 h showed a trend toward lower values in DCD recipients (-11.1%, 95% CI: −22.8–0.5%, p = 0.061), though this did not reach statistical significance. Because RV CPO is the product of cardiac output and mean pulmonary artery pressure, a higher value may reflect greater output or higher afterload and is challenging to discriminate. PAPi had the weakest predictive value of the four hemodynamic parameters evaluated (AUC 0.533–0.572), and other studies have suggested that PAPi may behave differently in the transplant setting compared to LVAD populations,17 albeit based on the preoperative PAPi rather than postoperatively. Prior work has suggested that DCD grafts may be particularly susceptible to early RV impairment, with recovery to comparable function by 3 weeks post-transplant37; no significant donor-type differences were apparent as early as ICU arrival across all five metrics. However, TA-NRP accounted for 83% of the DCD recoveries in this cohort, and these results likely reflect our institutional experience with the technique. Additional studies with larger DCD cohorts stratified by recovery strategy, direct procurement with normothermic machine perfusion, thoracoabdominal normothermic regional perfusion, or REUP,19 are needed to further elucidate the effect of recovery method on RV function following HT.

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective, single-center design, which restricts generalizability to centers with different organ acceptance practices, operative protocols, and postoperative management strategies. Exclusion of recipients requiring VA-ECMO within 72 h, as well as those with adult congenital heart disease or undergoing multiorgan transplantation, limits applicability to those populations. The CPOiVIS thresholds reported here are derived and require prospective validation before implementation as clinical decision tools. The comparable hemodynamics observed with DCD grafts may reflect institutional experience bias, given our center's substantial expertise with TA-NRP recovery,38 which comprised 83% of the DCD cohort. Similarly, graft recovery and preservation strategies evolved over time and era effects are inevitably correlated with strategy. Although recovery method and preservation strategy were assigned independent of graft quality and recipient acuity, unmeasured temporal confounding cannot be excluded. RV CPOi and PAPi showed limited discriminative ability for the composite endpoint, which may reflect active management of pulmonary artery pressures in the postoperative period rather than a true absence of prognostic value for right-sided metrics. The composite endpoint combines measures of resource utilization with discrete clinical events, which may be influenced by institutional practices. Adjustment was limited to three pre-specified variables, and residual confounding is likely. Donor allograft quality may be inadequately captured by the demographic variables available for analysis. Despite these limitations, these findings represent an important contribution toward characterizing the spectrum of post-transplant graft function and support the comparable early hemodynamic performance of DCD grafts in a contemporary cohort.

Conclusion

In this large single-center cohort of 500 adult HT recipients who did not require post-HT VA-ECMO, DCD and DBD grafts demonstrated comparable hemodynamic trajectories across all metrics through the first 72 postoperative hours. CPOiVIS emerged as a superior screening metric compared to CPOi alone, with timepoint-specific thresholds that demonstrated high negative predictive values applicable regardless of donor type. These findings support the continued expansion of DCD HT and provide a framework for continuous hemodynamic monitoring in the early post-transplant period. Prospective validation of CPOiVIS thresholds as actionable clinical decision tools is warranted.
