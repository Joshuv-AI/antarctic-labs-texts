The role of parental consanguinity and familial aggregation in development of multiple sclerosis: a case–control study
Authors: Saeed Vaheb, Mohammad Yazdan Panah, Alireza Afshari-Safavi, Elham Moases Ghaffary, Aysa Shaygannejad, Vahid Shaygannejad, Omid Mirmosayyeb
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeveral studies pointed out the importance of genetic risk factors such as parental consanguinity (PC) and familial multiple sclerosis (FMS) in the risk of MS. This study aimed to investigate the PC and FMS among people with MS (pwMS) in Isfahan, Iran.MethodsThis case–control study was conducted on pwMS from the MS clinic of Kashani Hospital, Isfahan, Iran, in October 2023. A group of healthy controls (HC) were also recruited. Data on demographic and clinical characteristics and history of PC and FMS were collected from participants. The relationships between PC, FMS, and developing MS were assessed using multinomial logistic regression analysis. The odds ratio (OR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was computed.ResultsA total number of 4264 pwMS and 400 HCs were included. The prevalence of PC and FMS among pwMS were 29.3% and 24%, respectively. Multinomial logistic regression adjusted for age and sex indicated that the odds of developing MS were significantly associated with a history of PC (OR = 3.03, 95% CI 2.23 to 4.13, p < 0.001) and FMS (OR = 5.42, 95% CI 3.51 to 8.38, p < 0.001).ConclusionPC and FMS can increase the risk of developing MS. They should be considered along with other risk factors for developing MS. A comprehensive conclusion requires further research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-024-02094-x.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, inflammatory, and demyelinating disorder of the central nervous system (CNS) that leads to significant disability in young adults, particularly affecting women more than men. The pathophysiology of MS involves an autoimmune response that targets the myelin sheaths, which are responsible for transmitting nerve signals, leading to their destruction. This leads to disrupted nerve signal transmission and a range of clinical symptoms, resulting in significant morbidity [1]. Multiple symptoms include visual disturbances, muscle weakness, coordination problems, sensory disturbances, cognitive impairment, and fatigue, all of which contribute to the significant morbidity associated with the disease [2, 3].

The prevalence of MS varies greatly worldwide, but is generally seen to be higher the further from the equator a country or region is [4]. This variation of prevalence depending on geographic location has led to the investigation of various environmental and genetic factors in the pathogenesis of MS. The prevalence of MS in Iran stands at 29.3 cases per 100,000 individuals, with an incidence of 3.4 cases per 100,000 individuals, indicating a rising trend in both the incidence and prevalence of over time in comparison to previous studies [2, 3].

MS is recognized for its intricate and multifactorial nature, with various environmental and genetic risk factors contributing to its development [5]. The inheritance pattern for MS does not adhere to Mendelian model principles [6]; instead, it involves multiple polymorphism genes that can act independently or in combination, having a small-to-moderate impact on disease development [7]. Genetic factors significantly influence MS development, as demonstrated by the higher likelihood of the disease recurrence in twins, siblings, and married couples affected by MS [8], while adoptees show a lower likelihood, supporting the genetic role in MS etiology [9–12]. First-, second- and third-degree relatives of people with MS (pwMS) are also at higher risk [13, 14]. Notably, HLA class I and II alleles have been associated with MS risk, with the HLA DRB1 ∗ 1501 allele increasing the risk of developing MS by approximately threefold [15, 16].

Parental consanguinity (PC) is notably prevalent in Middle Eastern nations, including Iran, primarily to preserve familial structure and facilitate social and economic advantages. Consanguineous marriage, particularly between first cousins, has been linked to a higher prevalence of familial MS (FMS). FMS denotes families in which more than one case of the disease exists, mostly among first-degree relatives like parents, siblings, or children. FMS therefore points to a genetic factor for MS, supported by evidence of higher recurrence rates for MS in families compared with the general population. Studies have demonstrated that the risk of MS recurring in siblings of index cases is four times higher when consanguineous mating occurs in compared to non-consanguineous mating [17, 18].

One of the reasons for studying consanguinity and FMS in Iran is that there is a high prevalence of consanguineous marriages, which may elevate the genetic risk of MS. Also, with its high degree of consanguinity and a high prevalence rate of MS, the role of PC and FMS in the development of MS has become very important in Iran [9, 19]. This study has been undertaken to elucidate whether PC and FMS are risk factors for developing MS in Isfahan, Iran. This investigation has been undertaken as a necessary step for understanding the genetic and familial factors that result in MS in a region of consanguineous marriages.

Methods

Study design and procedure

This study was carried out in adherence to STROBE guidelines. This case–control study was conducted in the MS clinic of Kashani Hospital, Isfahan, Iran, in October 2023. Demographic, clinical, and social information of pwMS referred to neurological clinics and hospital wards from January 2012 to September 2023 were collected retrospectively from patients. Adult PwMS (age over 18 years) with the following criteria were included: (a) confirmed diagnosis of MS [20, 21], (b) attendance at MS clinics or hospital admission, (c) being capable of comprehending the questionnaire, and (d) providing informed consent. Patients with other neurological disorders (apart from MS) and other chronic disorders that may have a potential influence on PC were excluded. A group of healthy controls (HC) was recruited from staff of the hospital and wards and individuals referring to the clinics of the Isfahan University Health Center for annual screenings between September 2022 and September 2023. Individuals with any chronic disease (neurological, autoimmune, psychiatric, cardiovascular, cancer, etc.) or those having a family relationship with pwMS were excluded from the HC group. Data collection

Data on demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as history of PC, FMS, and familial autoimmune disorder (FAD), were collected from participants through interviews. Participants with PC were categorized into two groups according to the level of their parents’ kinship: (a) individuals whose parents were cousins were classified as second-degree, and (b) individuals whose parent had more distant kinship (uncle's grandson, aunt's grandson, paternal cousin, maternal cousin) were classified as third degree. Additionally, participants with a history of FMS or FAD were divided into two subgroups based on the level of kinship: (a) individuals whose first-degree relatives (parents, sibling, or child) had a history of MS or autoimmune disorders, (b) individuals whose second-degree relatives (uncle, niece, grandparent, aunt, nephew, halfsibling or grandchild, cousins) had a history of MS or autoimmune disorders.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Isfahan, Iran (IR.MUI.RESEARCH.REC.1398.770). The researchers adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All individuals signed the informed consent form, indicating their understanding and agreement with the study's objectives and procedures.

Sensitivity analysis and sample size estimation

A sensitivity analysis was performed to enhance the robustness of the findings. A subgroup of 400 individuals with MS, matched with healthy controls by age and sex, was randomly selected from the original cohort to evaluate the effect of sample size on the significance of the analyses. Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 25. Data distribution was evaluated for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous and discrete quantitative variables were described using descriptive statistics, including mean (standard deviation (SD)) and median (interquartile range (IQR)) following normality testing. Categorical variables were summarized as total counts and relative percentages of patients per response category. The Chi-square test was applied to compare categorical variables. Continuous variables were compared using an independent t-test or Mann–Whitney test. The association of PC, FMS, and FAD with the likelihood of developing MS was calculated using multinomial logistic regression adjusted for age and sex. A p-value lower than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

A total number of 4264 pwMS (77.6% female) and 400 HC (75%) were included in this study. PwMS had a mean (SD) age of 36.8 [10] years, a disease duration of 10.14 (6.63) years, and a median (IQR) Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) of 1.5 (2.5). A history of PC and FMS was reported by 1248 (29.3%) and 1023 (24%) of pwMS, respectively. The mean (SD) of age of HC was 42.49 (11.37) years. Of them, 48 (12%) and 22 (5.5%) had a history of PC and FMS, respectively. The frequency of having a history of PC and FMS was significantly greater in pwMS compared to HC (p < 0.001 for both). In contrast, the frequency of FAD did not differ significantly between these groups (p = 0.44). Among pwMS who had a positive history of PC, most of them (66.9%) had parents who were second-degree relatives. Similarly, in pwMS with a positive history of FMS, 78% had parents who were first-degree relatives. Sensitivity analysis

To mitigate the influence of a large sample size, a random selection of 400 pwMS was drawn from the original population. This group of PwMS was matched by age and sex to the HC group (p > 0.05 for age and sex). The mean (SD) of age and disease duration of pwMS was 42.37 (8.84) and 11.76 (7.32) years, respectively. PwMS had a median (IQR) EDSS of 1.5 (3.5). The frequency of PC (p < 0.001) and FMS (p < 0.001) was significantly higher in pwMS than in HC. However, no significant difference in the frequency of FAD was found between the two groups (p = 0.6). The majority of pwMS with a positive history of PC had parents who were second-degree relatives (66.9%). Moreover, most pwMS with a positive history of FMS also had parents who were first-degree relatives (78%). Further details are summarized in Supplementary Material, Table S3.

Relationships between PC, FMS, FAD, and the risk of developing MS

Regarding PC, individuals with a history of PC had significantly higher odds of developing MS compared to those without such a history (OR = 2.57, p < 0.001). Furthermore, a history of PC was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of developing RRMS (OR = 2.45, p < 0.001), SPMS (OR = 2.11, p = 0.012), and PPMS (OR = 5.55, p < 0.001) compared to those without this history (Supplementary Material, Table S4).

Concerning FMS, individuals with a history of FMS had significantly higher odds of developing MS than those without this history (OR = 4.94, p < 0.001). Moreover, FMS was associated with increased odds of developing RRMS (OR = 4.97, p < 0.001), SPMS (OR = 4.98, p < 0.001), and PPMS (OR = 4.53, p = 0.001) (Supplementary Material, Table S4).

There was no significant relationship between FAD and developing MS (p > 0.05), as well as the development of different subtypes of MS (p > 0.05 for all) (Supplementary Material, Table S4).

Association between PC, FMS, FAD, and demographic and clinical features of pwMS

Regarding PC, a significant sex difference was observed between PwMS with and without a history of PC (p = 0.031). However, no significant differences were found in other demographic or clinical characteristics between these groups (Supplementary Material, Table S6).

Concerning FMS, pwMS with a history of FMS had longer disease duration than those without (p < 0.001). Furthermore, RRMS and PPMS patients with a history of FAD had longer disease duration (p < 0.001) and greater EDSS at first diagnosis (p = 0.042) than others, respectively. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in other demographic or clinical characteristics between these groups (Supplementary Material, Table S7).

Regarding FAD, pwMS with a history of FAD had greater EDSS than those without such a history (p = 0.033). Furthermore, PPMS patients with a history of FAD had greater EDSS (p = 0.015) and EDSS at first diagnosis (p = 0.046) than others. On the other hand, the groups did not exhibit any significant differences in the rest of the demographic or clinical features (Supplementary Material, Table S8).

Discussion

In this study, we found that PC and FMS are significantly associated with an increased risk of developing MS in the population of Isfahan, Iran. The prevalence of PC and FMS among pwMS was higher compared to healthy controls, and the odds ratios for MS were significantly elevated in individuals with a history of PC and FMS.

Our findings align with previous studies conducted in the Middle East, which have also highlighted the role of consanguinity as a genetic risk factor for MS. For example, in Saudi Arabia, a study found a similar association between PC and FMS, with higher rates of consanguinity among patients with FMS compared to those without [18, 22]. There was a 29.3% prevalence of PC among pwMS in our study, which is consistent with other studies in the region [22, 23]. For instance, a nationwide study in Iran reported a consanguinity rate of 28.6% among pwMS. Our study also revealed that FMS is associated with an increased risk of developing MS supporting the notion that familial aggregation plays a significant role in genetic susceptibility to MS. This finding is in line with a study by Jbara et al. revealed that FMS patients had a PC rate of 41.9%, which was not significantly different from the PC rate of sporadic pwMS(40%) [24] (Table S9).

Our results also suggest that a combination of a genetic predisposition due to PC and FMS, coupled with environmental factors, may be a possible cause for MS. Recent studies have predominantly termed MS as a multifactorial disease wherein no single causative factor contributes to its pathogenesis. This realization brings out the relevance of considering both genetic predispositions and environmental exposures in the pathogenesis of MS [5, 9, 18, 22, 24]. Based on the results of this study, by consanguinity, MS risk was highest in the PPMS group, while in the SPMS group, it was by FMS. In some previous studies, PC and FMS were associated with an increased likelihood of MS, while other studies indicated no significant association. In line with our findings, Maghzi et al. found that compared to a HC, pwMS were less likely to be born from consanguineous unions [25]. In a study by Dessa Sadovnick et al., the risk of developing MS was four times higher among siblings of index cases when their parents have a consanguineous relationship compared to when the parents do not exhibit consanguineous mating. [17]. Steenhof et al.'s findings suggest a high likelihood of first-degree relatives sharing the same MS course, albeit with different ages at diagnosis [26]. The results of a study conducted on the Caucasian Canadian population demonstrated that offspring of consanguineous marriages were more likely to experience FMS recurrences [17]. It has been shown in a Danish study that FMS is more likely to develop RRMS and SPMS compared with sporadic MS [26]. Siddiqui et al. found in univariate analysis a significant association between MS and parental consanguinity [27]. An inherited susceptibility to MS may be influenced by both parents, at least in cases where MS is familial [17, 28, 29].

Studies have demonstrated that individuals with close genetic relationships have a greater likelihood of developing MS compared to those with more distant relatives [26, 30]. Roberts et al. have suggested that a patient's susceptibility to MS is determined by the genes inherited from both parents [31]. A consanguineous union may lead to homozygosity, indicating recessive inheritance of protective alleles [32]. It is possible that PC could reinforce several gene loci associated with MS [31]. In a study by Everest et al., FMS cases were significantly more likely to experience common rare risk variation than controls and control families [33].

Additionally, the results of our study indicated that, based on PC and FMS, the degree of PC or the type of FMS did not significantly affect the risk of MS. A study by Al Jumah et al. found that an individual with MS with any degree of PC is more likely to have another family member with MS, consequently increasing their risk of FMS. Regardless of the type, they also declared an association between familial disease aggregation and PC [18]. Salehi et al. declared that there is a strong relationship between FMS and MS risk in offspring, siblings, and twins, especially monozygotic twins [34]. Heinsiek et al. found similarities in clinical MS courses among siblings with MS [35]. Although we did not find a difference between the risk of MS among different degrees of PC and types of FMS, an Italian study found opposites. They indicated that the overall age-adjusted risk for relatives of an MS proband in the Nuoro population was 1.90%, with parents at 1.26%, offspring at 2.3%, and sibs showing a significantly higher risk than other first-degree relatives [36].

Previous studies have come to different conclusions regarding the relationship between PC and FMS and the risk of developing MS. These different results might be due to various methodologies used in this study. Pedigree calculations and patient reports are often subject to errors and recall bias, affecting results. According to the study conducted in the isolated population of Orkney and Shetland, inbreeding and consanguinity were not associated with MS risk in a large group of pwMS who were homozygous [37]. Furthermore, genotyping and assessing the inbreeding coefficient in the Faroe Islands population did not suggest that inbreeding was a risk factor for MS. The Faroe Islands study concluded that PC is not an important risk factor for MS [38]. In Saudi Arabia, a study found no association between MS and PC. Even though the likelihood of a positive FMS was greater among the cases than among the controls, this association did not appear statistically significant in that study [18]. MS is increasing more frequently in Saudi Arabia, but it is unrelated to family history [22]. Binzer et al. showed that inbreeding appears unrelated to MS, supporting the hypothesis that inbreeding does not contribute to the disease [38]. They concluded that other factors, such as exposure to certain environmental factors or a rare recessive allele with reduced penetrance, could be more promising [37, 38]. It has been demonstrated by McWhirter et al. that inbreeding and PC do not contribute to MS risk due to their lack of influence on genome-wide homozygosity [37]. According to a study, PC did not affect the MS disease course in patients with and without MS [25]. Offspring of consanguineous unions have a lower MS risk than do offspring of unrelated parents based on a cross-sectional study of the effect of PC on MS causality [24, 39].

This has been studied in Iran in relation to consanguinity and family history with regard to rheumatoid arthritis [40], systemic lupus erythematosus [41], and type 1 diabetes.[42, 43] Similar to MS, this research has generally demonstrated an increased frequency of consanguineous marriages and a positive family history in patients versus controls, thus suggesting a shared genetic background for these autoimmune diseases in populations where the rate of consanguinity is high. [40–43] While similarities support the role of genetic factors, differences probably represent variations in environmental exposures, epigenetic modifications, and specific genetic markers unique to each condition [44, 45].

Limitations and strengths

There were also some limitations in our study, including selection and recall bias, which are common in case–control studies. A distinctive limitation of our study is the use of retrospective chart reviews, which limit our ability to accurately identify case and control groups and our ability to test as many clinical variables as we would have liked. The controls were selected randomly without matching, but age and gender were adjusted in the regression analysis. No genome sequencing was conducted in this study to validate our discovery. Thus, this should be considered in future studies. This limitation along with others, prevents the findings from being generalized to the entire country of Iran. While this study has limitations, its major strength is that it provides new clinical information about PC and FMS in a large population of pwMS (4264 pwMS), which opens up future research opportunities. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive case–control study to investigate the role of PC and FMS in MS risk in a large population of pwMS.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we found that a history of PC and FMS can increase the risk of developing MS. Furthermore, compared to other studies, this study revealed a lower prevalence of PC and FMS among pwMS. Our findings suggest that, although MS is a multifactorial disease, genetics, PC, and FMS may contribute to its development. Considering that PC is common in many regions, these findings require further examination. Additional research is needed before drawing definitive conclusions.
