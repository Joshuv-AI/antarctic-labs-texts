Association between nonrestorative sleep and increased healthcare costs: a 6-year cohort study
Authors: Shota Matsunaga, Mitsuhiro Sado, Yoshitaka Kaneita, Norio Sasaki, Katsutoshi Tanaka, Kenichi Kuriyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNonrestorative sleep (NRS) refers to the subjective experience of not feeling refreshed after sleeping. Although associations between NRS and mental and physical health have been reported, their relationship with long-term healthcare costs has not been examined. This study investigated whether NRS is associated with increased long-term healthcare costs using a large-scale cohort dataset from Japan. We analyzed the annual health screening and medical claims data of 117,049 individuals enrolled in a health insurance association for civil engineering and construction workers. NRS was assessed in the 2018 health screening using a single item: “Do you feel refreshed after a typical night’s sleep?” Annual healthcare costs were calculated using medical claims data from fiscal years (FY) 2018 to 2023. Mixed-effects models for repeated measures, including interaction terms, were used to examine the association between NRS and healthcare costs over time. Sex-stratified analyses were also performed. At baseline, 45.7% of participants reported NRS. From FY 2020 onward, healthcare costs were significantly higher in the NRS group than in the restorative sleep (RS) group. In FY 2023, the cost difference was JPY 18,468 (95% CI: 7,306 to 29,630). Interaction analysis revealed that the cost gap between the NRS and RS groups widened significantly over time. Similar trends were observed in the sex-stratified analysis. NRS is significantly associated with increased long-term healthcare costs, suggesting an important medical and economic impact. Screening for NRS during routine health checkups and providing early interventions may be beneficial in reducing healthcare costs.Keywords: Nonrestorative sleep, Sleep disorder, Healthcare costs, Health economics, Longitudinal study

Introduction

Increasing healthcare costs are a significant social concern in many developed countries. For instance, annual healthcare costs account for 16.7% of gross domestic product (GDP) in the United States, 12.0% in Switzerland, 11.1% in Japan, 11.0% in New Zealand, and 10.0% in Chile [1]. In numerous high-income nations, healthcare costs exceed 10% of GDP, placing a substantial burden on national social security systems [1]. To address this issue, many countries have implemented health checkup programs aimed at controlling healthcare costs [2, 3]. These checkups aim to identify individuals at high risk for lifestyle-related diseases and reduce long-term healthcare costs through early intervention [3].

Recently, nonrestorative sleep (NRS) has been linked to lifestyle-related diseases. NRS is defined as “a subjective experience of unrefreshing sleep” [4] that is not necessarily associated with actual sleep duration or continuity and is often accompanied by symptoms such as fatigue and impaired cognitive function [5]. Although NRS can co-occur with insomnia, other sleep disorders, and physical and mental illnesses, some individuals experience NRS in the absence of any such conditions [6]. Therefore, NRS has been proposed as a distinct concept independent of other sleep disorders. The prevalence of NRS in the general population ranges from 1.4% to 42%, depending on the assessment method [4, 7, 8]. NRS has been associated not only with lifestyle-related diseases, such as hypertension [9] and diabetes [10], but also with mental health outcomes, including depression [11] and suicide [12]. These associations suggest that NRS may indirectly contribute to increased healthcare costs by increasing the risk of both physical and mental disorders.

Previous studies have shown that sleep disorders, such as insomnia and sleep-disordered breathing, are associated with higher healthcare costs. For example, an analysis of U.S. health insurance data found that individuals with insomnia incurred over USD 500 more in annual healthcare costs per person than healthy sleepers [13, 14]. Similarly, in Australia, sleep-disordered breathing is estimated to increase national healthcare costs by more than USD 17 million per year [15]. These findings highlight sleep disorders as important contributors to increased healthcare costs. Notably, since NRS is modifiable through appropriate assessment and lifestyle interventions, it may represent a promising target for cost-reduction strategies. However, the association between NRS and healthcare costs in Japan has not yet been investigated.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the impact of NRS on annual healthcare costs using data from a large Japanese cohort.

Methods

Participants

This retrospective cohort study used annual health screening and medical claims data from 185,639 individuals, including employees and their family members, enrolled in a large occupation-based health insurance association in Japan that serves workers in the civil engineering and construction sectors. A total of 117,049 individuals (63.1%) with complete data who could be continuously followed up from April 2018 to March 2024 were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics were based on health screening data collected between April 2018 and March 2019.

Annual healthcare costs

The primary outcome of this study was the annual healthcare costs between April 2018 and March 2024, corresponding to six fiscal years (FY) in Japan (each FY runs from April to March). The total annual healthcare costs included the costs for outpatient visits, inpatient care, and prescription dispensing (excluding dental costs), which were calculated based on medical claims data.

Nonrestorative sleep (NRS)

The primary explanatory variable was NRS at baseline. NRS was assessed using the following health screening questionnaire item: “Do you feel refreshed after a typical night’s sleep?” Participants responded with “yes” or “no,” and those who answered “no” were classified as having NRS, while those who answered “yes” were classified as having restorative sleep (RS). This item has been widely used in the Japanese Specific Health Checkups program, supporting its practicality and relevance for population-based screening. This question was administered during participants’ annual health screening.

Covariates

Based on previous studies [3, 10, 13], we included the following covariates for adjustment: demographic characteristics, use of prescription medication, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors. Demographic characteristics included sex and age. Prescription medication use was defined by the presence of a prescription record. Comorbidities were assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [16], which was calculated based on diagnosis codes from medical claims data.

Lifestyle factors included the following four covariates: exercise habit, dietary habit, smoking habit, and alcohol consumption. The definitions are as follows, based on the definition by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare [17]. Exercise habit was classified as appropriate if at least two of the following criteria were met: A. Engaging in moderate exercise (enough to break a light sweat) for ≥ 30 min at least twice a week for ≥ 1 year, B. Walking or engaging in equivalent physical activity for ≥ 1 h daily; C. Walking faster than peers of the same age and sex. Dietary habit was classified as inappropriate if two or more of the following behaviors were reported: A. Eating faster than others, B. Eating within 2 h before bedtime ≥ 3 times per week, C. Consuming snacks or sugary drinks between meals daily or occasionally, D. Skipping breakfast ≥ 3 times per week. Smoking habit was defined as having smoked ≥ 100 cigarettes or having smoked for more than 6 months, and having smoked within the past month (yes/no). Alcohol consumption was classified as appropriate if any of the following criteria were met: A. Not drinking alcoholic beverages, B. Occasional consumption of fewer than three standard drinks per session, or C. Daily consumption of fewer than two standard drinks. All other cases were classified as inappropriate. One standard drink was equivalent to 500 mL of beer or 60 mL of whiskey.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. First, differences in explanatory variables between individuals with and without NRS were examined using the chi-square test for categorical variables and t-test for continuous variables. Second, we used adjusted mixed-effects models for repeated measures (MMRM) to examine the differences in annual healthcare costs between individuals with and without NRS over time. Baseline NRS status was treated as a fixed effect, with annual healthcare costs as the outcome variable. The interaction terms between NRS and year were included to assess the cost trajectories. Sex-stratified analysis was performed using the same model. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3). Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P value < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study used existing anonymized secondary data. Therefore, it was not subject to an institutional ethical review according to national guidelines.

At baseline, 45.7% (n = 53,495) of participants reported experiencing NRS. Compared with those with RS, individuals with NRS were younger, less likely to engage in appropriate exercise or have healthy dietary habits, and more likely to use prescription medications. In FY 2023, the average annual healthcare cost was JPY 163,877 for the entire cohort, with significantly higher costs observed in the NRS group (JPY 169,319) than in the RS group (JPY 159,296; p = 0.015). This trend of higher healthcare costs in the NRS group also reached statistical significance in FY 2022 (JPY 166,047 vs. 158,530, p = 0.045), suggesting a widening gap in healthcare costs over time.

Association between NRS and healthcare costs

The results of the MMRM are presented in Table 2. While the overall main effect of NRS status was not statistically significant, annual healthcare costs were significantly greater in the NRS group than in the RS group in the later years of follow-up. The estimated differences were: JPY 13,481 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 2,320 to 24,643) in FY 2020, JPY 12,670 (95% CI: 1,508 to 23,832) in FY 2021, JPY 15,961 (95% CI: 4,799 to 27,123) in FY 2022, and JPY 18,468 (95% CI: 7,306 to 29,630) in FY 2023 (Table 3). Interaction analysis using FY 2018 as the reference year showed a significant widening of the gap in healthcare costs between the NRS and RS groups over time: JPY 7,898 (95% CI: 168 to 15,629) in FY 2020, JPY 10,378 (95% CI: 2,647 to 18,108) in FY 2022, and JPY 12,885 (95% CI: 5,154 to 20,615) in FY 2023 (Table 4). These results indicate a progressive increase in healthcare costs among individuals with NRS compared to those with RS.

Sex-stratified comparisons of healthcare costs

Table 5 presents the results of sex-stratified analysis. Among men, the difference in healthcare costs between the NRS and RS groups increased significantly over time, with an estimated difference of JPY 12,660 (95% CI: 2,880 to 22,439) in FY 2020 and JPY 15,695 (95% CI: 5,915 to 25,474) in FY 2023, using FY 2018 as the reference year. Among women, significant cost differences were observed as well: JPY 13,935 (95% CI: 2,290 to 25,579) in FY 2022 (reference year: FY 2018), JPY 19,420 (95% CI: 7,775 to 31,065) in FY 2022 (reference year: FY 2019), JPY 11,949 (95% CI: 304 to 23,594) in FY 2023 (reference year: FY 2019), and JPY 17,516 (95% CI: 5,871 to 29,160) in FY 2022 (reference year: FY 2020). These results indicate a widening cost gap between individuals with and without NRS in both men and women, although the timing and magnitude of the increase varied slightly by sex.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between NRS and healthcare costs in Japan. Using a large longitudinal dataset of approximately 120,000 Japanese individuals over a six-year period, we analyzed both health screening and medical claims data. Our findings demonstrate that NRS is associated with significantly higher long-term healthcare costs. This association was more pronounced in men than in women.

Prevalence of NRS

The prevalence of NRS in countries outside of Japan ranges from 2.4% to 16.1% [18–21]. Most of these studies defined NRS as experiencing unrefreshing sleep for at least three days per week. In contrast, studies using a single yes/no item, similar to the method used in this study, have reported higher prevalence rates. For example, a U.S. study using a binary-response question reported NRS prevalence ranging from 26.3% to 42.1% [22]. In Japan, studies using the same single-item yes/no format have reported prevalence rates between 19.2% and 36.8% in the general population [7, 23], which is comparable to international findings.

However, the prevalence of NRS in the present study was notably high (45.7%). This elevated rate may be attributable to the occupational characteristics of the study population, which primarily consisted of blue-collar construction workers. Blue-collar workers are known to have shorter sleep durations than other occupational groups [24] and are more likely to report poorer sleep quality because of physically demanding labor [25]. While physical activity is generally associated with improved sleep quality [26], the effects of occupational factors, such as irregular schedules, stress, or noise, may offset these benefits. Therefore, it is plausible that the participants in this study had a higher prevalence of sleep problems, including NRS. Given the safety–critical nature of the construction industry, proactive sleep-related interventions may be particularly effective in this population.

NRS and healthcare costs

NRS is known to increase the risk of both physical and mental health disorders, and this elevated disease risk may contribute to increased healthcare costs. NRS is associated with a higher risk of diabetes [10], hypertension [9, 10], and cardiovascular disease [27]. Several mechanisms may explain this association. First, insufficient sleep, which is a core component of NRS [28], is known to disrupt glucose metabolism. It leads to elevated blood glucose levels, partly through increased secretion of cortisol and catecholamines, and decreased leptin levels, which regulate appetite [29]. These hormonal changes promote hyperglycemia and contribute to the development of obesity and diabetes [29].

Second, NRS has been linked to systemic inflammation [30]. Inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein, which are associated with obesity, are elevated in individuals with poor sleep [30]. Thus, inflammation may mediate the relationship between NRS and obesity-related diseases. Third, insufficient sleep and obesity are believed to share common genetic determinants [31]. Additionally, sleep apnea syndrome, a common cause of NRS, has been linked to increased risk of oral health problems [32, 33]. These conditions may further exacerbate the physical burden associated with NRS.

NRS has also been associated with an increased risk of mental health conditions [11, 12, 20]. Although the mechanisms by which NRS increases the risk of mental disorders are not fully understood, one proposed hypothesis is that individuals with NRS may experience reduced rapid eye movement (REM) sleep [6]. REM sleep plays a crucial role in emotional regulation, particularly in modulating anxiety and fear through activity in the amygdala [34]. Sleep disruption has been shown to weaken functional connectivity between the amygdala and the ventral anterior cingulate cortex, a region involved in inhibiting amygdala activity [35]. Although a direct causal relationship between NRS and reduced REM sleep has not been clearly established, NRS may represent a pathway through which emotional dysregulation and the risk of mental disorders increase.

Thus, it is conceivable that NRS contributes to increased long-term healthcare costs by increasing the risk of lifestyle-related and mental health disorders. From a policy standpoint aimed at controlling national healthcare costs, and a public health perspective focused on preserving individual health and quality of life, routine screening for NRS during medical checkups and the implementation of targeted interventions may be beneficial in reducing long-term healthcare costs. Future studies are warranted to evaluate the effectiveness and cost-efficiency of specific intervention strategies for NRS management.

Limitations

This study had five primary limitations. First, the reliability and validity of the measurement of NRS may be limited. In this study, NRS was assessed using a single screening item. Although validated instruments could offer greater measurement precision, their use in large-scale occupational health checkups is impractical because of the burden on participants and the high administrative costs. Comprehensive clinical evaluations by physicians would be even more burdensome and unrealistic. Nevertheless, previous research suggests that a single-item question may be sufficient to capture the core symptoms of NRS [10]. Therefore, from the standpoints of feasibility and public health utility, the simple questionnaire used in this study may be an appropriate tool for screening NRS during workplace health examinations.

Second, the study was subject to selection bias based on participants’ occupational characteristics. As noted above, most participants were blue-collar workers, who are at a higher risk of occupational accidents, but may be at a lower risk of lifestyle-related diseases due to the amount of physical exercise than white-collar workers. Consequently, the pathways linking nonrestorative sleep to increased healthcare costs may differ from those in white-collar workers. Future studies should examine these pathways separately in blue- and white-collar populations.

Third, the dropout rate was high at 36.9%. This high dropout rate may have introduced survivorship bias, as individuals with poorer health conditions might have been more likely to leave the cohort. Additionally, this may be attributed to the withdrawal from the health insurance association due to retirement, marriage, or a change in employment, making continued follow-up impossible. Therefore, future research would benefit from the development of databases that allow longitudinal follow-up regardless of changes in health insurance coverage.

Fourth, only healthcare costs related to treatments covered by insurance benefits were used as outcomes, excluding over-the-counter (OTC) medications, in-house clinics, and self-funded medical treatments. However, because these costs are not part of public healthcare costs and account for only a small part of pharmaceutical sales in Japan, analysis from the perspective of reducing public healthcare costs may not be necessary. Nevertheless, an analysis that includes these costs is required from the perspective of individual household financing.

The fifth concern is the possibility of uncontrolled confounding factors. Sleep duration, working hours, and physical activity are reportedly associated with both sleep and healthcare costs. These variables were not examined in this study; thus, their effects could not be adjusted. Additionally, COVID-19 has been spreading in Japan since 2020, with special measures in place until May 2023, when it was reclassified into the same category as seasonal influenza. This may have significantly impacted medical consultation behavior and, consequently, healthcare costs. However, it was not considered in the analysis of this study, as it was assumed to have affected the entire target population equally. Future studies should investigate potential confounding variables at both individual and population levels and incorporate them into the model.

Conclusions

This study is the first to demonstrate that NRS is associated with increased long-term healthcare costs using a large-scale dataset of Japanese individuals. NRS is known to elevate the risk of both mental and physical disorders, which may in turn contribute to higher healthcare costs. These findings highlight the importance of early detection and timely interventions. Implementing routine screening and targeted strategies for NRS may not only improve individual health outcomes but may also be beneficial in reducing long-term healthcare costs at the population level.
