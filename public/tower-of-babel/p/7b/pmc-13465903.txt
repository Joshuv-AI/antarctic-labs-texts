Nutritional status and behaviour difficulties among adolescent girls in a rural area of Ludhiana – A cross-sectional study
Authors: Daljit Kaur, Sangeeta Girdhar, Sarit Sharma, Vikram Kumar Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Adolescence is a vital phase of physical and psychological development, often accompanied by nutritional and mental health challenges. Adolescent girls are especially vulnerable due to the physiological demands of puberty and evolving lifestyle behaviours.Objectives:To assess the epidemiological correlates of nutritional status and screen exposure among adolescent girls. To study the determinants of mental health difficulties through behavioural screening using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ).Methods:This community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from June 2023 to June 2024 among 230 adolescent girls aged 13–17 years in the field practice area of RHTC, Pohir, selected through systematic random sampling. Data on sociodemographic profile, diet, physical activity and screen exposure were collected using a pretested questionnaire. Behavioural screening was done using SDQ. Relevant statistical tests were applied.Results:Among the 230 participants, 24.9% were thin and 10.5% overweight/obese. Central obesity was found in 7.4% which was significantly associated with overweight/obesity (P < 0.001). Calorie intake showed a significant association with BMI (P < 0.001). Screen exposure >2 hours/day was reported by 57.8% and was significantly associated with insufficient sleep (P = 0.007), spectacle use (P = 0.01) and mother’s occupation (P < 0.001). Based on SDQ scores, 76.1% had normal, 14.8% borderline and 9.1% abnormal total difficulties scores.Conclusion:Adolescent girls exhibited malnutrition and mental health concerns, with screen exposure and dietary patterns playing a significant role. Early and targeted interventions are essential to address these risk factors and promote holistic adolescent health.

Introduction

World Health Organisation (WHO) defines adolescents as those between the ages of 10 and 19 years.[1] As per UNICEF report 2024, there are 1.3 billion adolescents worldwide.[2] As per WHO data 2024, India has the world’s largest adolescent population of 253 million.[3] Generally, around the early adolescence the onset of puberty occurs that brings about dramatic physical, sexual, psychological and hormonal changes. Teenagers develop specific behavioural patterns pertaining to diet, physical exercise, substance use and sexuality which establish the foundations of long-term health and well-being.

While infectious diseases and injuries contribute substantially to the disease burden in this age group, nutritional challenges including micronutrient deficiencies, stunting and malnutrition still remain significant public health concerns.[4] According to Comprehensive National Nutrition survey (2018) among Indian adolescents, the prevalence of thinness was 24.4% whereas overweight and obesity accounted for 4.8% and 1.1% of the burden, respectively.[5] Malnutrition among adolescents reduces their immunity, increases their proneness to diseases and results in premature death. Obesity is a key metabolic risk factor that contributes to the early onset of major non-communicable diseases like Cancer, Diabetes, Hypertension and Coronary Heart Disease.[6]

Additionally, academic pressure and excessive screen exposure in form of Television, smartphones and computers, further impacts adolescent health by affecting their sleep, impairing mood, memory and concentration. Indian studies have shown that 68–83% of adolescents exceed the American Academy of Paediatrics’ recommendation of less than two hours of screen time per day, contributing to sleep disturbances, daytime fatigue and visual strain.[7]

Mental health issues in adolescence warrant serious attention, as they may manifest as low self-confidence, anxiety, behavioural dysregulation, and increased vulnerability of self-harm. Studies done using Strength and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) to screen adolescents for mental health difficulties have found the prevalence of abnormal difficulties score among adolescents ranging between 10% and 40% with Emotional scale score higher among females.[891011]

Recognizing these diverse challenges, the Government of India launched the Rashtriya Kishor Swasthya Karyakram (RKSK) in 2014, a comprehensive initiative to address key adolescent health issues including nutrition, mental health, NCDs, substance use, injuries and reproductive health. However, its service utilisation remains suboptimal due to various barriers such as limited access, lack of awareness, parental neglect and inadequacies within the health system.

In this context, primary healthcare becomes critically important as it serves as the first point of contact for adolescents, offering preventive, promotive and counselling services close to the community. Strengthening adolescent-friendly services at the primary care level and training physicians to recognize early signs of mental health issues are crucial for timely intervention and equitable access to care.

Given the scale and complexity of adolescent health issues, further, research is warranted to comprehensively explore the epidemiological determinants influencing the physical and mental health of adolescents. Hence, this study was planned among adolescent girls in the age group 13-17 years to explore the nutritional status, screen exposure and mental health difficulties among adolescent girls and determine their epidemiological correlates.

This study mainly emphasizes on the influence of modifiable factors such as diet and screen exposure on adolescents. It contributes by enabling early detection of malnutrition and behavioural difficulties among adolescents by primary care practitioners, facilitating timely interventions. For adolescents, it enhances awareness regarding importance of healthy lifestyle practices to improve overall well-being.

Materials and Methods

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from June 16, 2023, to June 15, 2024, among adolescent girls aged 13–17 years residing in the field practice area of Rural Health Training Centre (RHTC), Pohir, under the Department of Community Medicine. A total of 230 participants were selected using systematic random sampling.

The sample size was calculated using the formula

n = Z²p (1–p)/d² where,

Z = 1.96 (95% confidence level); Estimated prevalence (P) = 16.05% based on prevalence of abnormal total difficulties score among adolescents based on a previous study conducted by Pahwa et al. (2019)[11] and an absolute precision (d) = 5%. The calculated sample size was 208. After taking in account a 10% non-response rate, final sample size of 230 subjects was taken. Adolescent girls who were unwilling to participate, non-cooperative, on psychiatric medication or suffering from chronic debilitating illnesses were excluded from the study.

Data were collected through house-to-house visits using a pretested, semi-structured questionnaire. Information on sociodemographic profile, personal and dietary history, physical activity and screen exposure was obtained through interviews. Socioeconomic status was assessed using the Modified Udai Pareek Scale.[12]

Anthropometric measurements included height, weight, waist circumference and hip circumference. Height was recorded in centimetres with the participant standing barefoot upright against a wall, using a non-stretchable measuring tape. Weight was recorded in kilograms using a calibrated digital weighing scale, with the participant standing barefoot in minimal clothing at the centre of the platform placed at hard and levelled surface. BMI was calculated using the formula: weight (kg)/height² (m²) and classified as per WHO growth reference for ages 5–19 years.[13] Waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the lower rib and iliac crest and hip circumference was measured at the widest part over the buttocks using a non-stretchable measuring tape to the nearest 0.1cm. Waist-Hip Ratio (WHR) was calculated and a WHR ≥0.9 was considered indicative of central obesity in adolescents.[14]

Dietary intake was calculated using 24-hour recall method and questions on dietary habits, meal frequency and consumption of fast food and carbonated beverages were asked. For assessment of calorie intake, Indian Council of Medical research (ICMR) 2020 guidelines were used.[15] Physical activity, screen time, sleep duration and household chores were also enquired. For analytical purposes, screen time was classified according to the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guideline, wherein <2 hours/day was considered adequate screen exposure. Participants with ≥2 hours/day of screen use were categorized as exceeding the recommended limit. Mental health difficulties were assessed using the self-report version of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) for adolescents aged 11–17 years.[16] The tool included 25 items across five domains—emotional problems, conduct problems, hyperactivity, peer problems and prosocial behaviour. Total difficulties score (range: 0–40) was calculated by summing all the scores excluding the prosocial score and categorized as normal (0–15), borderline (16–19) and abnormal (20–40).[16]

Statistical analysis

Data were entered in Microsoft excel and described in terms of mean ± standard deviation (±SD), frequencies and proportion (percentage) as appropriate. Chi-square (χ2) test and univariate analysis were applied with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

The study was duly approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. Written informed assent was obtained from the participants, along with written informed consent from their parents or legal guardians.

Results

Out of 230 adolescent girls interviewed, 151 (65.7%) were aged 13–15 years and 79 (34.3%) were aged 16–17 years (mean age 14.9 ± 1.3 years). More than half (52.2%) were residing in nuclear family. Majority of subjects (91.3%) belonged to Sikh religion followed by Hindu and Muslim religion. There were 21 subjects who had a deceased or separated parent. Of all the study participants’ parents, 64.7% of fathers and 62.5% of mothers had up to 8 years of schooling, while 6.5% of fathers and 10.2% of mothers had no formal education. Most subjects’ fathers (44.7%) were labourers, while 67.4% of mothers were housewives. Based on the Modified Udai Pareek Scale, 65.7% belonged to lower middle socioeconomic status, 28.7% to middle, and 5.2% to upper middle class. Of the 230 participants, 97.3% were currently enrolled in school, with 55.8% attending Government and 44.2% attending Private schools. In contrast, six adolescents had discontinued schooling, citing various reasons like lack of academic interest, financial constraints or parental decision.

Anthropometric measurements were taken for 229 participants as one was unable to stand (used wheelchair). Mean weight of subjects was 47.5 ± 10.7 kg and mean height was 157.1 ± 6.9 cm. Maximum study participants (64.6%) were having normal BMI, 24.9% were thin and 10.5% were overweight and obese as per WHO guidelines for BMI for age (5-19 years) [Figure 1]. The mean BMI was 19.6 ± 6.6 kg/m². Central obesity (WHR ≥0.9) was observed in 17 (7.4%) subjects. The mean calorie intake among the study subjects was 2283.2 ± 437.4 kcal.

Analysis of dietary and physical activity related factors demonstrated a significant association between BMI status and calorie intake. Nearly half of the thin girls consumed calories below ICMR (2020) recommendations, whereas 42.4% of overweight/obese girls reported excess caloric intake (P < 0.001). Frequency of meals <3/day was also more prevalent among thin girls, alongside higher exposure to other unhealthy lifestyle behaviours such as frequent fast-food intake, consumption of carbonated drinks and inadequate physical activity (<5 days/week). Furthermore, central obesity showed strong concordance with higher BMI (P < 0.001) [Figure 2].

Screen exposure more than the American academy of Paediatrics (AAP) recommended limit of 2 hours/day in form of watching TV/Mobile Phone/Computer was reported by 133 adolescents (57.8%). A statistically significant association was observed between screen exposure and mother’s occupation (P < 0.001), with over twice the proportion of girls whose mothers were housewives reporting excessive screen time (69.5%) compared to those whose mothers were working (30.5%). Additionally, use of spectacles was also significantly higher among those with excessive screen time (P = 0.01) [Table 2].

Discussion

Physical health among adolescents is a pivotal determinant of overall well-being as it shapes their overall well-being and future development. During adolescence, the body undergoes significant physical and hormonal changes, necessitating adequate nutrition, physical activity and timely healthcare to support optimal growth. In the present study, a dual burden of malnutrition was evident, with nearly one-fourth of adolescent girls being thin and 10.5% overweight or obese. These findings align with studies by Parida et al. (2025) and Gahlot et al. (2020), highlighting similar nutritional disparities.[1718]

BMI reflects a complex interplay of multiple factors including genetics, behaviour, environment and socioeconomic status. In the present study, thinness was more prevalent among older adolescents (16–17 years), whereas overweight/obesity was higher in the 13- to 15-year group. Beside increased academic stress, increasing consciousness regarding body image among older adolescents may partly explain this pattern, potentially influencing both their dietary choices and nutritional outcomes Similar age-related variations in BMI have been reported by Rao et al. (2022) and Baburajan et al. (2017), while Ravula et al. (2024) documented a higher prevalence of thinness in early adolescence.).[192021] Notably, a greater proportion of thin girls in the present study belonged to upper-middle and middle socioeconomic classes, although this association was not statistically significant. Pradhan et al.[22] (2022) in their study among schools going adolescents in South Odisha also documented similar higher pattern of thinness among upper middle and middle class.

A significantly higher proportion of thin girls had deficient calorie intake (50%), whereas most overweight/obese girls consumed excess calories (42.4%), indicating a strong association between dietary habits and BMI. Similar association was reported by Solanki et al. (2023) in Indore and Azrimaidaliza et al. (2024) in Indonesia, reinforcing the role of calorie balance in adolescent nutritional outcomes.[2324] In the present study, skipping meals and fast-food consumption were also more common among thin girls, possibly reflecting irregular eating habits due to reliance on foods consumed outside the home. Jain et al.[25] (2023) similarly highlighted the influence of carbonated drinks and fast-food intake on adolescent nutritional status. Among adolescents with insufficient physical activity (<5 days/week), 10.6% were overweight/obese while 26.4% were thin. This lack of interest in physical activities could be likely due to increased academic burden, lack of safe play spaces or preference for screen-based entertainment. A previous study by Moitra et al.[26] (2021) in Mumbai, reported a significant association between inadequate activity and adolescent obesity. Central obesity, which is a known risk factor for future non-communicable diseases like diabetes and cardiovascular conditions, was notable in the present study as nearly half (47%) of the participants with waist-hip ratio ≥0.9 were overweight/obese. This pattern aligns with findings from Krishnan et al.[27] (2021) in Kerala.

Screen exposure has emerged as an important behavioural factor affecting adolescent health. More than half of the participants (57.8%) exceeded the recommended limit. Similarly, high levels of screen time have been documented by Dubey et al. (2018) in Delhi and John et al. (2024) in Pune, indicating a growing trend of excessive screen use in both urban and rural contexts.[2829] Interestingly, higher screen exposure was more common among girls whose mothers were housewives. This could be because girls whose mothers were housewives had greater access to personal phones, reflecting higher device availability in the home, which may contribute to more unregulated screen exposure. Contrary to results of present study, Malhotra et al.[30] (2022) showed significantly higher screen time among adolescents whose mothers were working. Prolonged screen use was found to negatively affect duration of sleep among adolescent girls, consistent with findings from Maurya et al. (2022) and Negi M et al. (2024).[3132] Additionally, 29.3% of girls with high screen time wore spectacles, suggesting a possible visual impact due to increased exposure to Mobile phones, Computer or Television screens.

A total of 14.8% and 9.1% girls had borderline and abnormal total difficulties scores respectively. These findings were comparable with other studies by Kumar et al. (2022), Pahwa et al. (2019) and Puwar et al. (2018).[113334] Although no statistically significant association was found between total difficulties score and sociodemographic or lifestyle factors, the findings suggest that mental health difficulties were slightly higher among adolescent girls aged 16–17 years, those from lower socioeconomic status and those who had experienced death or separation of a parent. Previous studies by Dheeshan et al. (2020), Harikrishnan and Sailo (2021) and Sambaras et al. (2024) also observed similar patterns.[353637] Furthermore, in the present study, normal scores were higher among girls who were physically active ≥5 days/week and those with lower screen exposure, suggesting the potential influence of healthy lifestyle behaviours on psychological well-being. These patterns, underscore the need for further targeted research to better understand and address adolescent mental health needs.

Unlike previous studies that have examined nutritional status or mental health separately, the present study provides a comprehensive assessment by integrating nutritional status, screen exposure and behavioral difficulties among adolescent girls. It also offers community-based evidence from a rural setting and use of systematic sampling further enhances the representativeness of the findings.

Conclusion

These findings underscore the multifactorial nature of adolescent health and the need for integrated interventions. Strengthening school and community-based counselling on nutrition, hygiene and mental health, led by ASHA, Anganwadi workers, and trained primary care providers is essential. With rising malnutrition, excessive screen time and behavioural issues, integrated efforts involving healthcare teams, educators, and families are needed to promote healthy behaviours and ensure early intervention. Adolescent counselling on nutrition, hygiene, mental health and healthy lifestyles should be provided in schools and strengthened at CHCs and PHCs. Although physical activity is included in various school curricula across states, the lack of standard protocols and monitoring highlights the need for structured nutrition education classes and regular outdoor physical activities.

This study highlights the need for future research focusing on longitudinal studies to establish causal relationships and evaluate the effectiveness of school- and community-based interventions like nutrition education, screen time regulation and mental health counselling. Integration of such evidence into national programs can help strengthen adolescent-friendly health services and improve their utilisation.

Limitations

The study has certain limitations, including the potential underreporting of behaviours such as outside food consumption and screen exposure due to the presence of family members at the time of interviews.
