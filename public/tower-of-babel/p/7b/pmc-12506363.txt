Perceived teacher support moderate the relationships between adverse childhood experiences and social adjustment among migrant children: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Fang Wang, Xiaofeng Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) correlate with social adjustment. Notwithstanding the rising incidence of ACEs within migrant populations, there is a shortage of studies investigating ACEs patterns and the associated risk for social adjustment in migrant children.ObjectiveThe research seeks to examine the correlation between ACEs and social adjustment, and to investigate whether perceived teacher support moderates this relationship.MethodsA total of 821 migrant children (Mage = 13.5 years, age range 11to 16 years) living in Lanzhou were recruited from three junior middle schools between September and November 2023. They were evaluated with the 28-item Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ-SF), Perceived Teacher Support Scale and Adolescents’ Social Adjustment Assessment Scale. Latent profile and moderated analysis were conducted.ResultsThree profiles of adverse childhood experiences were founded and labeled as Low (15.6%), Medium (32.7%), and High (6.7%). There is variability in ACEs among migrant children. The link between adverse childhood experiences and social adjustment is significantly moderated by perceived teacher support. ACEs are prevalent among migrant children and are associated with decreased odds of social adjustment, especially among those with low perceived teacher support.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the need for interventions aimed at implemented to prevent ACEs and family- and school-based interventions can be provided to develop positive experiences among migrant children.Keywords: Adverse childhood experiences, Social adjustment, Perceived teacher support, Migrant children, Latent profile analysis

Introduction

China’s dual demographic challenges of low fertility rates and population aging have rendered the healthy development of all children a national priority. Simultaneously, rapid urbanization has transformed population migration patterns, with 34.9% of citizens now living apart from their household registration locations - an 88.52% increase since 2010 [1]. This migration has created a distinct population of 71.09 million migrant children (23.9% of all minors), typically defined as individuals under 18 who depart from their native domicile with their parents and have lived in urban cities for a minimum of six months [1, 2]. Contemporary migrant children demonstrate fundamentally different settlement patterns from their parents’ generation, around 3.74 years of urban residence with one-third exceeding six years [3]. This prolonged urban exposure creates a paradox: while these children develop strong urban identities and are unlikely to return to rural origins, many faces institutional barriers to full integration. Their successful adaptation represents both a pressing social challenge and a critical component of China’s human capital development strategy.

The relationship between the migration experiences of Chinese children and their psychological well-being has been extensively studied over the past decade. Previous research has revealed that migrant children in China are more inclined to s encounter mental health concerns, like anxiety, depression, and behavioral disorders, in contrast to non-migrant children [3, 4]. Social adjustment refers to the ability to fulfill societal role expectations (e.g., academic, interpersonal) and adapt to new social environments—a process often disrupted by systemic and structural barriers [5]—are particularly prevalent in this population. National surveys estimate that approximately 20–30% of migrant children exhibit clinically significant social adjustment difficulties, a rate notably higher than their urban peers [2]. Residential and educational mobility constitute substantial ecological disruptions, often leading to unstable schooling, discrimination, and family separation, all of which hinder successful adaptation [6]. The social adjustment of migrant children is affected by the hukou system, which assigns residence rights based on birthplace, hence correlating their location and hukou status with their rights and advantages. Empirical studies demonstrate that maintaining the existing hukou system of household registration might subject urban migrants to unfair treatment, rendering them a social group that is in risk [7]. Children and parents of migrants are not legally registered in the city. Children who migrate do not have the same opportunities as children in urban areas, resulting in unequal access to high-quality public-school education. In these situations, migrant children might lack consistent assistance from the city, potentially resulting in their social maladjustment.

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) described as traumatic occurrences happening prior to the age of 18 [6], are well-established risk factors for children’s social adjustment problems. These encompass a spectrum of exposures, including physical/emotional neglect, abuse (e.g., physical, sexual, or emotional), household dysfunction (e.g., domestic violence, parental incarceration, or substance misuse), and family instability (e.g., separation or mental illness) [8]. Children’s risk of exposure to ACEs is shaped by multilevel determinants spanning individual, familial, and community contexts. Key risk factors include housing instability, intergenerational ACEs transmission, and residence in socially disorganized neighborhoods [9–12]. Globally, epidemiological data estimate annual ACEs prevalence in the hundreds of millions [13], underscoring the scale of this public health challenge. Childhood adversities exhibit substantial prevalence in China, as demonstrated by large-scale empirical studies. A representative four-province survey of 14,500 adolescents (aged 10–20 years) identified that 6.3% reported high ACEs, while 21.4% and 45.5% experienced significant abuse and neglect, respectively [14]. These findings are corroborated by a comprehensive meta-analysis incorporating 68 child maltreatment studies, which revealed lifetime occurrence rates of physical abuse was 27%, mental abuse was 20%, sexual abuse was 9%, and neglect was 26% [15].

It’s essential to highlight that experiencing childhood adversity doesn’t imply that migrant children cannot achieve successful social adjustment, the key determinant is the presence of protective factors that counteract ACE-related risks. Among these factors, perceived teacher support—defined a student’s subjective assessment of the degree to which they can obtain needed assistance from their teachers [16, 17], represents a uniquely salient resource for migrant children, for two key reasons: First, migration-induced structural vulnerabilities (e.g., disrupted peer networks, strained parent-child relationships due to mobility and parental work demands [18] amplify the compensatory role of teacher support. When traditional support systems are compromised, schools emerge as rare stable institutions where teachers function as dual resources: academic facilitators and psychological “ad hoc attachment figures” [19, 20]. This aligns with attachment theory’s premise that perceived relational security fosters developmental resilience [21]. Second, the institutional nature of teacher-student relationships ensures stability unmatched by informal ties. Unlike fluctuating peer or familial support, teacher support is structurally reinforced through daily school routines [22], providing consistent scaffolding for self-worth [23] and adaptive functioning [24]. Empirical studies specifically document migrant children’s heightened responsiveness to perceived teacher support [25], suggesting its unique potency in mitigating relational instability.

Perceived teacher support is a crucial protective factor in mitigating the effects of ACEs, particularly for migrant children, who often face compounded stressors due to displacement and acculturation challenges. While prior studies have demonstrated that general social support buffers against ACEs-related mental health risks [26–29], teacher support may provide a uniquely stabilizing function for children in educational environments, where migrant youth allocate a considerable amount of their time. Unlike family or peer support, teacher support has been shown to independently promote students’ emotional, behavioral, and cognitive resilience, even in high-adversity contexts [30, 31]. For instance, perceived teacher support predicts improved mental health status in children subjected to early adversity [32], and recent findings indicate that supportive teacher-student relationships can attenuate the link between ACEs and emotional/behavioral problems [33]. However, whether these protective mechanisms extend to social adjustment—a key dimension of migrant children‘s well-being—remains understudied. Given that schools often serve as a primary stabilizing environment for migrant children, further investigation into perceived teacher support’s moderating role could inform targeted interventions to enhance their social adjustment.

Furthermore, Multiple hypotheses exist regarding the observed association between childhood adversity exposure and subsequent mental health vulnerability. Contemporary adversity research predominantly operationalizes this through the cumulative risk model [34–36], which posits that psychological maladjustment escalates with each additional ACEs exposure due to stress accumulation [34, 36]. This model typically employs an ACEs sum score, where higher scores indicate greater risk [37, 38]. However, the cumulative risk approach has notable limitations. By treating all ACEs as equally impactful, it overlooks potential differences in how adversity types (e.g., abuse vs. household dysfunction) and frequencies influence outcomes [39, 40]. Such a reductionist method may obscure distinct pathways linking ACEs to social adjustment. To address these gaps, this study employs a person-centered methodology—Latent Profile Analysis (LPA)—which identifies subgroups of children with similar ACEs configurations. Unlike cumulative scoring, LPA accounts for heterogeneity in adversity exposure, enabling nuanced analyses of how specific ACEs patterns interact with contextual factors like perceived teacher support [41]. Specifically, we examine: (1) Whether distinct ACEs profiles exist among migrant children; (2) How these profiles relate to social adjustment, and (3) Whether perceived teacher support moderates these associations. By clarifying these mechanisms, our findings could inform tailored school-based interventions for migrant children facing adversity.

Methods

Participants

This school-based cross-sectional study examines how perceived teacher support moderates connection between ACEs and social adjustment of migrant children in Lanzhou, Gansu Province—the northwestern capital Chinese city with the province’s highest concentration of this vulnerable population [2]. With approval from school headmasters, we used convenience sampling to select 24 classes from three junior middle schools in Lanzhou where the proportion of migrant students exceeded 60%. Participants were students in grades 7 to 9 who met the following criteria: (1) they had migrated to Lanzhou from other provinces or cities with their parents or guardians at least six months prior to the study, (2) they were enrolled in schools without a registered household (hukou) in Lanzhou, and (3) their parents were currently employed in Lanzhou. A total of 891 migrant children completed the online survey using the Wen Juan Xing platform. We excluded responses that were incomplete, completed too quickly, answered carelessly, or appeared random. After data cleaning, 821 valid responses were retained. Among them, 437 (53.2%) were boys and 384 (46.8%) were girls. The sample had a mean age of 13.5 ± 0.9 years (range 11–16), with an average Lanzhou residency duration of 3.0 ± 2.0 years.2.2. Procedures.

The research obtained approval from the Institutional Review Board of Northwest Normal University. Informed consent forms were distributed to all participants and their parents or guardians with the assistance of school administrators before data collection. Trained graduate assistants administered the study measures in the students’ regular classroom settings, providing verbal and written instructions to ensure clarity. Participants were given ample time to complete the measures and were instructed there was no correct or incorrect response. They were instructed to complete the questionnaires independently and refrain from discussing their responses with others, with the option to terminate at any moment. Researchers strictly ensured the confidentiality of collected data, limiting access to completed questionnaires to research staff for analysis purposes only.

Sample size

Based on a 95% confidence interval, a standard deviation of 15, an acceptable error of 2.0, and a 20% attrition rate, 274 was the minimum sample size needed [1]. Consequently, the final sample of 821 was suitable for analysis.

Measures

Adverse childhood experiences

A 28-item Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ-SF) created by Bernstein et al. (2003) [42] was utilized to assess adverse childhood events. The questionnaire was translated into Chinese by Zhao et al. (2005) [43]. The CTQ-SF comprises five subscales: emotional neglect (e.g., “felt loved”), physical neglect (e.g., “not enough to eat,” “someone to take care of and protect me”), emotional abuse (e.g., “someone in my family hated me”), sexual abuse (e.g., “I was sexually abused”), and physical abuse (e.g., “hit so hard that I had to see a doctor”). Each subscale includes five items assessing different types of maltreatment, with an additional three items serving as validity checks (e.g., “perfect childhood”). The responses were captured using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = very often), assessing the frequency of listed events experienced before age 16. Higher ratings indicated more exposure to adverse childhood experiences; total scores varied from 25 to 125. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the CTQ-SF in this investigation was calculated as 0.83.

Perceived teacher support

Perceived teacher support was assessed utilizing Ouyang’s (2005) [44] 19-item Perceived Teacher Support Scale, which gauged three subscales: academic support (e.g., “when I couldn’t answer a question, the teacher would often give me hints until I could answer it ”; 9 items), emotional support (e.g., “when I performed well in class, the teacher would praise me”; 6 items), and competence support (e.g., “the teacher often asked me to take responsibility for class matters”; 4 items). Survey responses were quantified using a standardized 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), yielding a composite score range of 19–95. Higher composite scores indicated stronger student-perceived teacher support. Previous research with Chinese samples has validated the scale reliability and validity [45]. The overall coefficient alpha for this study was 0.841.

Social adjustment

Social adjustment was evaluated using the Positive Adjustment Subscale of the Adolescents’ Social Adjustment Assessment Scale [46], comprising four dimensions: self-affirmation (e.g., “I am proud of myself for accomplishing things”; 5 items), prosocial tendency (e.g., “I am willing to share things with other students”; 5 items), efficacy in action (e.g., “I can manage my time well”; 5 items), and active coping (e.g., “I don’t think failure is a terrible thing”; 5 items). These dimensions collectively represent four domains: self-adjustment, behavioral adjustment, interpersonal adjustment, and environmental adjustment. Responses were collected using a 5-point Likert scale anchored by 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with aggregate scores spanning 20–100 points, where higher ratings imply greater adaptability. Higher ratings imply greater adaptability. The subscale demonstrated a coefficient alpha of 0.835 in this study.

Covariates

This study controlled for age (child-report in years), gender (male = 1, female = 2), family structure (parent-report: two-parent = 0, single-parent = 1, reconstituted = 2), parental education (primary school = 1, junior high school = 2, high school/vocational = 3, College degree or above = 4), and perceived economic situation (Likert scale: poor = 1 to excellent = 4)., as these factors demonstrate associated with social adjustment [47].

Statistical analysis

The complete statistical analyses were rigorously executed usings SPSS (version 26.0), SPSS PROCESS (version 4.0), and Mplus (version 8.3) software. Prior to analysis, responses with missing data were excluded due to low missingness across all variables (< 2%). First, descriptive analysis characterized the demographic and occupational profiles. Spearman’s correlation analysis gauged the relationships among adverse childhood experiences, perceived teacher support, and social adjustment. Harman’s one-factor model verified the issue of common method variance (CMV) [48]. Second, we conducted latent profile analysis (LPA) at the item level to identify distinct clustering patterns of adverse childhood experiences. Using Mplus 7.0, we evaluated one- to five-class solutions and compared model fit indices. Stepwise profiling utilized the 1–5 categories of the LPA model. Fit metrics, such as Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC), guided model evaluation, with smaller values implying superior fit. An entropy value exceeding 0.80 signified classification accuracy above 90%. Fit differences between k-1 and k-category models were examined using the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR) and the sample-based Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT); the k-category model was found to have a significant p-value in favor of it [41, 49].Lastly, the moderating role of perceived teacher support between LPA-based adverse childhood experiences profiles (categorical variable) and social adjustment was employed to evaluate the PROCESS macro (Model 1) of SPSS [50]. The gender, age family structure, parental education and perceived economic situation were controlled during the analysis of models 1.

Common method variance test

The self-assessment questionnaire used in this investigation might have been susceptible to common method bias. To address the issue, Harman’s one-way factor analysis was conducted to assess the existence of common method bias [48]. All study variables were then subjected to an exploratory factor analysis employing the principal component approach. The analysis revealed 67 factors with characteristic roots exceeding one with the first factor explained 20.85% of the variance, which is less than the crucial criterion of 40%. This implies that common method bias was not a significant issue in this investigation.

Discussion

The current research represents the attempt to explore latent profiles of ACEs using LPA within a cohort of migrant children. Through LPA, individuals can be grouped based on shared similarities, distinguishing them from those in other groups [51]. Our findings align with prior studies [52], indicating that migrant children in our sample encountered various forms of maltreatment alongside other early adversities. The class structure varied among migrant children, with a notably higher prevalence of ACEs observed in high ACEs, which exhibited a heightened likelihood of emotional neglect and physical neglect. Low ACEs demonstrated a lower probability of emotional neglect, resembling migrant children who experienced limited exposure to such adversity. Migrant children are predisposed to emotional neglect, potentially due to challenges in adapting to new environments. This transition can be emotionally taxing, leading to feelings of isolation or encountering discrimination, both contributing to emotional neglect. Moreover, migrant children may lack robust support networks, including extended family, close friends, and trusted teachers, further exacerbating feelings of loneliness and neglect.

The research also examined the correlation between childhood adversity and social adjustment among migrant children. Overall, we found a negative correlation between childhood adversity and social adjustment among migrant children. The research indicates that migrant children experienced adversity during childhood are more prone to exhibit poorer psychological functioning. Early exposure to adversity may result in persistent psychological vulnerability in maturity, and this vulnerability may be further exacerbated by significant stressors such as migrant experiences [53]. Firstly, Migrant children are those that frequently relocate their place of residence because of family migration, economic difficulties, or other reasons. This implies that the more adversity they face during childhood, the greater the difficulties they may encounter in social adjustment. These adversities include family instability, frequent relocation, and economic hardship, which may influence their mental health, academic performance, and social skills, thereby influencing their integration and adaptation in society [1]. Secondly, adversities such as frequent relocation, family conflicts, and economic poverty might contribute to the development of mental health conditions, particularly depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, and diminished self-esteem among migrant children. This can affect their emotional regulation and social skills, making it more difficult for them to adapt to various pressures and challenges in society. Moreover, childhood adversity among migrant children may also affect their academic performance and motivation. Frequent relocation may result in frequent interruptions in their education and an unstable learning environment. This can affect their learning progress and academic achievements, reduce their motivation and confidence in learning, and thus affect their competitiveness and adaptation in society [25]. Additionally, childhood adversity among migrant children may also affect their social skills and interpersonal relationships. Frequent relocation may make it difficult for them to establish stable friendships and lack long-term social support networks. This may make them appear reserved and introverted in social interactions, making it difficult for them to establish good interpersonal relationships and thereby affecting their integration and adaptation in society [54].

Perceiving teacher support significantly moderates the link between childhood adversity experiences and social adjustment among migrant children. Migrant children often develop in unstable environments and may face a series of adversities such as family instability, economic difficulties, and academic instability. These experiences might have a negative influence on their social adjustment abilities. However, teacher support can mitigate this impact to some extent and enhance the social adjustment abilities of migrant children, Consistent with the previous research [53]. Firstly, teacher support can provide emotional support. When facing adverse experiences, migrant children often feel stress, lonely, and helpless. In such situations, the understanding, care, and encouragement from teachers can become their emotional support, helping them overcome difficulties. When migrant children perceive teacher support, they feel more secure and respected, thereby enhancing their confidence, actively facing life’s challenges, and improving their social adjustment abilities [55, 56]. Secondly, teacher support can provide academic support. Migrant children might face academic instability and interruptions in learning result of family relocation, economic challenges, and other reasons. In such cases, guidance, encouragement, and additional learning support from teachers can assist them in surmounting learning difficulties and enhancing their academic performance. By gaining recognition and support from teachers, migrant children feel more motivated and confident, and thus strive harder in learning, thereby enhancing their social adjustment abilities. Furthermore, teacher support can provide behavioral support. Migrant children may encounter behavioral problems and adaptation challenges due to adverse experiences. In such cases, understanding, guidance, and encouragement from teachers can help them establish positive behavior patterns, cultivate good social skills and interpersonal relationships, and thus enhance their social adjustment abilities. Through teacher support, migrant children can learn how to cope with challenges and pressures, gradually adapt to new environments and roles, and improve their social adjustment abilities [57, 58]. Moreover, teacher support can also provide social resource support. As important figures in schools, teachers can help migrant children access more social resources like psychological counseling and social services. By providing support in these areas, teachers can help migrant children overcome the negative influence of adverse experiences and achieve social adjustment and development. In conclusion, perceiving teacher support significantly moderates the link between childhood adversity experiences and social adjustment among migrant children. Teacher support, including emotional support, academic support, behavioral support, and social resource support, can help migrant children overcome the negative influence of adverse experiences and enhance their social adjustment abilities [29]. Therefore, schools and society should value the role of teacher support, provide more support and care for migrant children, and help them grow up smoothly to achieve social adjustment and development.

When interpreting the current research findings, the several limitations should be considered. Firstly, the study’s sample of migrant children was selected from a single western Chinese city, potentially limiting the generalizability of the results to migrant children nationwide. Secondly, all measures of ACEs, perceived teacher support, and social adjustment relied on self-reporting. While self-reports might induce response bias, retrospective self-reports remain a valid method for assessing ACEs [59]. Lastly, the research’s cross-sectional design limits the capacity to determine causal linkages. To advance elucidate the link between early exposure to cis-heterosexism and elevated stress responses linked to psychological adjustment, future research should employ a daily diary survey couple with the measurement of biological markers, such as cortisol levels. The implications of our study findings are significant for educators aiming to avoid mental disorders in survivors of childhood adversity. First, interventions must be adopted for preventing ACEs; for instance, strategies that mitigate child maltreatment, such as home visitation programs that assist new mothers in baby care, along with those that aid parents of older children in delivering supportive care [60, 61]. Secondly, interventions focused on familial and educational settings can be implemented to foster good experiences within these environments. Programs for parent training aim to enhance attachment and communication between parents and children while assisting caregivers in overseeing children’s activities [63]. Comprehensive school and classroom curriculum are accessible to enhance kids’ dedication to education, academic achievement, and recognition of student accomplishments [63, 64]. Restorative disciplinary practices in educational institutions show potential in fostering good childhood experiences that mitigate the impact of adversity on children’s academic achievement and overall well-being [64, 62]. Enhancing the implementation of the programs within families, schools, and communities might mitigate the prevalence of ACEs and diminish the likelihood that children would react to adversity with detrimental health behaviors.
