Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms and Cognitive Impairment Among Participants in the REGARDS Cohort Study
Authors: Alayne D Markland, Richard Kennedy, Michael Crowe, Belinda Williams, Cami Munoz, Sara Elgayar, Kathryn L Burgio, Virginia J Howard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTo understand cross‐sectional associations between lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) and specific cognitive domains, we examined cognitive performance in older adults with and without LUTS.MethodsThe study comprised a subset of Black and White participants, aged 45 and older, enrolled in the REasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke (REGARDS) national, longitudinal cohort study 2003–2007. Computer‐assisted telephone interviews were conducted for follow‐up every 6 months including an established cognitive battery: Six‐Item Screener (SIS) for cognitive impairment, animal naming, letter F naming, word list learning, and delayed recall for verbal fluency/executive function and verbal memory. Validated LUTS questionnaires were administered beginning in 2020 at 6‐month intervals. Separate linear and logistic regression models for men and women estimated associations between LUTS and cognitive battery scores, adjusting for covariates.ResultsFrom the 10,464 participants (6026 women, 4438 men), 70% of women reported LUTS (mean age 69 ± 8 years) versus 62% of men (mean age 63 ± 7 years). More Black men reported LUTS compared to White men, 69% versus 59% (p < 0.001), respectively, without differences among Black and White women. Men and women with LUTS had decreased odds of being cognitively intact based on the SIS, OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.50, 0.85 and OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.46, 0.78, respectively. Men with LUTS had lower scores for Animal Naming (β −0.42, 95% CI −0.75, −0.08) and Delayed Recall (β −0.18, 95% CI −0.30, −0.05). Women with LUTS had lower test scores for Letter F Naming (β −0.29, 95% CI −0.55, −0.04), Word List Learning (β −0.40, 95% CI −0.68, −0.11) and Delayed Recall (β −0.13, 95% CI −0.25, −0.02).ConclusionsOlder adults with LUTS have subtle differences in cognitive function, especially in new learning, verbal memory, and verbal fluency/executive function that should be considered in treatment decisions.Keywords: cognitive impairment, epidemiology, lower urinary tract symptoms, older adults

Background

Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) include storage symptoms, such as increased urinary daytime frequency, nocturia, urgency, incontinence, and voiding symptoms, such as slow stream, splitting stream, intermittency, straining to void, terminal dribble, and hesitancy. LUTS are very common in older men and women [1, 2, 3, 4]. Overall prevalence of at least one LUTS ‘sometimes’ is 72% for men and 76% for women, and ‘often’ is 48% for men and 52% for women [1]. Prevalence increases with age, though older adults underreport symptoms and avoid seeking treatment [5].

Cognitive impairment (CI) is a broad categorization that can describe either overall dysfunction (e.g., global cognitive impairment) or difficulty in a particular cognitive domain (e.g., memory, language, and executive function). Having evidence of CI is required for diagnosis of dementia, the most common form of dementia being Alzheimer's disease [6]. LUTS prevalence is higher in those with dementia, prompting an interest in epidemiologic research on the link between LUTS and CI prior to the onset of dementia [7, 8]. Current literature on the association between LUTS and CI is unclear, though prior studies have identified an association between incontinence (UI) and CI [9]. Rait et al. found a significant association between UI and CI, measured with the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), in a U.K. cross‐sectional cohort embedded in a clinical trial of women aged 75 and older [10]. A study of Japanese community‐dwelling middle‐aged and older men and women found a significant relationship between UI and declining cognitive function across multiple cognitive tasks except those related to memory and visuospatial function [11]. Huang et al. in a U.S. observational study of 6361 women aged 65 years and older found no association between weekly UI and cognitive decline assessed by three cognitive tests [12]. However, they determined cognitive decline as a risk factor for “functionally disruptive” UI, since these women, owing to difficulties in coping with UI symptoms, were more likely to report their urine leakage interfering with activities than those without cognitive decline. The lack of robust evidence may in part be attributed to study differences in samples (longitudinal versus cross‐sectional design and representation), sampling methodologies, and age of the cohort, as well as inadequate definitions and measurement tools for LUTS and CI. Most studies evaluate only UI, a LUTS subtype, and are limited to CI measured by global assessments like the MMSE and not specific cognitive domains.

Our objective was to utilize data from a large, national cohort study of Black and White participants to examine cross‐sectional associations between LUTS and CI, including specific cognitive performance domains. We hypothesized that after controlling for key covariates, participants with LUTS would be more likely to have CI compared to participants without LUTS and more likely to show lower performance for specific cognitive domains.

Methodology

Data Source and Study Population

We studied participants from the REGARDS (REasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke) study, a national, population‐based, longitudinal cohort of 30,239 non‐Hispanic Black and White adults aged 45 years or older from all 48 contiguous U.S. states and the District of Columbia [13]. During the baseline visit between 2003 and 2007, participants completed a computer‐assisted telephone interview (CATI) for sociodemographic and medical history information and an in‐home visit for physical exam and blood and urine sample collection. Thereafter, they were contacted by CATI at six‐month intervals to obtain information on hospitalizations related to potential stroke, other health information and cognitive assessments. Informed consent was obtained initially by CATI and then in writing during the in‐person visit. All participating institutional review boards approved the study protocol. In 2020, an ancillary study was initiated to collect information on LUTS, which was included on each participant's next available follow‐up CATI.

Of the 13,603 participants available, 10,464 (4438 men, 6026 women) completed questionnaires on LUTS and had at least one cognitive performance assessment completed in 2019–2020.

Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms Assessment

LUTS was our exposure of interest. The presence of LUTS was based on participant responses on the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire Male/Female Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms (ICIQ‐MLUTS and ICIQ‐FLUTS) tools with established reliability and validity by the International Continence Society (ICS) for assessing urinary symptoms over the past 4 weeks and strong correlation to International Prostate Symptom Scores (r = 0.745) [14, 15]. Individual LUTS, urgency and incontinence, were scored as follows: “never (0), occasionally (1), sometimes (2), most of the time (3), and all of the time (4).” Urinary frequency was scored as follows: “1–6 times (0), 7–8 times (1), 9–10 times (2), 11–12 times (3), and 13 or more (4) and nocturia 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 or more times, with scoring from 0 to 4.” Additional questions for incontinence categorized “stress, urgency, and incontinence without an obvious reason” and were not used in scoring. Then, we created a dichotomous variable based on total ICIQ‐LUTS (range 0–12) scores for urgency, frequency, nocturia, and incontinence: absent/very mild (< 3) or moderate/severe (≥ 3).

Cognitive Performance Assessment

Similar to other REGARDS manuscripts on CI, we used a numeric score of CI from the Six‐Item Screener (SIS, range 0–6), and a dichotomous cutoff of ≤ 4 for CI and > 4 for no impairment and a numeric score from the abbreviated Montreal Cognitive Assessment Score (aMOCA, range 0–15) for telephone use using MoCA items for registration, delayed recall, and orientation [16, 17, 18]. Additionally, we analyzed specific cognitive domains in four cognitive tests: verbal fluency/executive function with Animal Fluency and Letter F tests, new learning with Word List Learning (WLL) test, and verbal memory with Word List Delayed Recall test [19, 20]. Lower raw scores represented worse cognitive performance.

Covariates

The sociodemographic factors considered were age, race, education, household income, health insurance status, and living location (urban, rural or mixed). Behavioral covariates included body mass index (kg/m2), smoking status, alcohol usage defined as none, moderate (0–7 per week for women, 0–14 per week for men), or heavy (≥ 8 per week for women and ≥ 15 per week for men), self‐reported sleep duration, and the perceived stress scale [21]. Medical covariates included hypertension, diabetes, stroke (prevalent stroke at baseline and/or incident stroke during follow‐up), obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and depression symptoms (mean score on Center for Epidemiologic Studies‐Depression‐4 item) [22]. Specific to men was the self‐reported use of medications for benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). Covariates in women included having a hysterectomy, menopausal status, and current hormone replacement therapy (HRT) usage. In men and women, dementia medications and overactive bladder (OAB) medications were classified into two separate categorical variables (Table S1).

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using R studio version 4.3.2. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous data are presented as mean ± SD. Descriptive statistics for the covariates included t‐tests and chi‐square analysis. Dichotomized SIS was analyzed for cognitive impairment using logistic regression, and aMOCA using ordinal logistic regression, with odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and p values. Beta coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and p values were calculated using linear regression to evaluate relationships between covariates and LUTS in separate models with cognitive tests as continuous variables.

Results

Prevalence of LUTS and Associated Factors

Of the original cohort of 30,239 participants, 13,603 were still active at the time the LUTS questionnaire was initiated, and 10,464 (6026 women, 4438 men) completed the questionnaire. LUTS were found in 70% of women and 62% of men (Table 1). For men and women, the 65+ years age group showed a significantly lower prevalence of LUTS compared to the 45–64 age group (p < 0.001). Men and women in the lowest income bracket (< $20 k) and with less than high school education had a higher prevalence of LUTS. Black men had a higher prevalence of LUTS compared to White men (69.1% vs. 59.1%, p < 0.001), while racial differences were not seen in women (69.0% Black, 69.7% White; p = 0.6). Heavy drinkers had a higher prevalence of LUTS compared to moderate drinkers or abstainers. Compared to those without LUTS, women with LUTS had less “adequate sleep” and higher levels of perceived stress were noted for men and women with LUTS. Hypertension, diabetes, BMI, depressive symptoms, OSA, and use of OAB medications were more prevalent in men and women with LUTS compared to those without LUTS, without differences noted for use of dementia medications. Use of medications for BPH and the use of HRT was more common among men and women with LUTS than without LUTS, respectively. More women with LUTS reported having prevalent stroke, a hysterectomy, being post‐menopausal compared to women without LUTS. Men with LUTS were more likely to report having incident stroke compared to women.

Association of LUTS With Cognitive Impairment

Overall, LUTS were associated with lower cognitive test scores in adjusted models (Figure 2). From the dichotomized SIS (score ≤ 4, Table 1), women with LUTS (8%, n = 144) had CI compared to those without LUTS (6%, n = 260, p = 02) and men with LUTS (9%, n = 145) had CI compared to men without LUTS (8%, n = 225, p = 0.6). After adjustment (Figure 2 and Table S3), women and men with LUTS had decreased odds of being cognitively intact with the dichotomized SIS, OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.46, 0.78 and OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.50, 0.85, respectively. For men and women with LUTS (Figure 2 and Table S3), aMoCA scores were not different (women: OR for higher scores 0.99, 95% CI 0.87, 1.12; men: OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.88, 1.13). In Figure 2 (Table S3), women with LUTS had lower test scores for Letter F (β −0.29, 95% CI −0.55, −0.04), Word list learning (β −0.40, 95% CI −0.68, −0.11), and Delayed Recall (β −0.13, 95% CI −0.25, −0.02). Men with LUTS had lower scores for Animal Naming (β −0.42, 95% CI −0.75, −0.08) and Delayed Recall (β −0.18, 95% CI −0.30, −0.05). Men with LUTS did not have significantly lower Letter F scores (β = −0.07, 95% CI −0.35, 0.20) and women with LUTS did not have lower Animal Naming scores (β −0.09, 95% CI −0.37, 0.19). These patterns also persisted in sensitivity analyses when women and men with prevalent and incident stroke were removed from the analysis (Table S4) and when LUTS scores were used as a continuous outcome (Table S5). When evaluating individual LUTS as a sensitivity analysis (Table S6) different patterns existed for women and men with LUTS compared to those without LUTS. Urgency represented a similar pattern to the primary analysis, as compared to nocturia and incontinence. Sensitivity analyses also included interaction terms for sex, age, and race (Table S7) with notable differences in cognitive tests by age among men and women.

Discussion

The high LUTS prevalence rates in REGARDS were comparable to other cohort studies among older adults, with some notable differences among Black and White men that were not found among women [1, 2, 3, 4, 23]. Specific to this study, we found associations between LUTS and CI among older men and women defined by poorer cognitive performance test scores, specifically for new learning, verbal memory, and executive function/verbal fluency, even after adjustment for covariates. Evaluation of CI among older adults with LUTS may have treatment implications, especially for medication usage.

Recent epidemiology and neuroimaging studies demonstrate that adults with LUTS, specifically overactive bladder (OAB), have significantly worse cognitive function compared to those without bladder symptoms. In US adults aged 60 and older in the National Health and Nutritional Examination Survey (NHANES), cross‐sectional analyses showed that OAB is associated with significantly lower cognitive scores across multiple domains, including the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease Word Learning test (β = −0.62), Animal Fluency Test (β = −0.57), and Digit Symbol Substitution Test (β = −2.84), all remaining significant after adjustment for confounders [24]. We found similar associations with lower scores in the cognitive battery performed in REGARDS, although not all the cognitive tests were similar. The associations existed in middle‐aged women from another NHANES analysis, where those with OAB showed nearly twice the prevalence of cognitive impairment compared to women without OAB (multivariable‐adjusted prevalence ratio: 1.88, 95% CI 1.52–2.24) [25]. In a US study of midlife women aged 42–57 years, better performance on cognitive testing was associated with lower odds of more severe LUTS/impact (OR 0.90, 95% CI 0.83–0.98) [26]. A large nationwide cross‐sectional study from Taiwan found that LUTS increased dementia risk more than cerebrovascular disease (adjusted hazard ratio 1.61, 95% CI 1.47–1.76 vs. 1.43, 95% CI 1.26–1.61) [27]. Neuroimaging studies reveal that cerebral white matter hyperintensity volume is independently associated with OAB (OR 1.82, 95% CI 1.11–2.98), and individuals with OAB show abnormal functional connectivity in brain regions associated with the brain‐bladder network [28, 29].

LUTS and CI often co‐occur in older adults due to shared risk factors, such as chronic conditions like diabetes, cardiovascular disease, depression, poor sleep, and neurologic conditions, including stroke [30]. While this study reported associations and cannot claim causation, cognitive decline may impair bladder control and LUTS may disrupt sleep and increase stress, potentially worsening cognitive function. While our study adjusted for these factors, we found similar patterns of CI with and without inclusion of these covariates (Supporting Information: Tables). Older adults with LUTS often report higher levels of depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances which are linked with CI [31, 32]. From a recent study, depression appears to mediate approximately 35% of the association between OAB and CI, while poor sleep quality mediates about 11% of the relationship in middle‐aged women [33]. Future research should evaluate if treatment of LUTS could also impact CI in older adults.

Our study had several strengths and limitations. We studied a wide age range of older Black and White men and women and used standardized, validated instruments to capture LUTS and domain‐specific cognitive performance. We did not evaluate physical performance or mobility knowing that LUTS are also associated with impaired physical performance, more often in women than men [34]. As a cross‐sectional study, we did not analyze longitudinal changes in cognitive performance. Additionally, since LUTS were self‐reported via a telephone interview without medical evaluation, responses may be less accurate than an in‐person assessment. Additionally, we did not account for prostate cancer in men, which was not collected in REGARDS. Future research should explore longitudinal changes, mobility changes, and stress across the lifespan that could mediate the relationship between LUTS and CI, along with more neuroimaging studies looking at potential mechanisms.

Conclusion

LUTS are associated with lower cognitive test scores across several different brief cognitive assessments. Our findings may have important implications for evaluating and managing LUTS in older individuals. Our study emphasizes the potential need to evaluate cognitive function when treating LUTS with medications, especially anticholinergic medications that could worsen cognitive function over time [35].
