3DViT-GAT: a unified atlas-based 3D vision transformer and graph learning framework for major depressive disorder detection using structural MRI data
Authors: Nojod M Alotaibi, Areej M Alhothali, Manar S Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent mental health condition that negatively impacts both individual well-being and global public health. Automated detection of MDD using structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) and deep learning (DL) methods holds increasing promise for improving diagnostic accuracy and enabling early intervention. Most existing methods employ either voxel-level features or handcrafted regional representations built from predefined brain atlases, limiting their ability to capture complex brain patterns. This paper develops a unified pipeline that utilizes Vision Transformers (ViTs) for extracting 3D region embeddings from sMRI data and Graph Neural Network (GNN) for classification. We explore two strategies for defining regions: (1) an atlas-based approach using predefined structural and functional brain atlases, and (2) an cube-based method by which ViTs are trained directly to identify regions from uniformly extracted 3D patches. Further, cosine similarity graphs are generated to model inter-regional relationships, and guide GNN-based classification. Extensive experiments were conducted using the REST-meta-MDD dataset to demonstrate the effectiveness of our model. With stratified 10-fold cross-validation, the best model obtained 81.51% accuracy, 85.94% sensitivity, 76.36% specificity, 80.88% precision, and 83.33% F1-score. Further, atlas-based models consistently outperformed the cube-based approach, highlighting the importance of using domain-specific anatomical priors for MDD detection.Keywords: Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), Structural MRI (sMRI), Deep Learning (DL), Vision Transformer (ViT), Cosine similarity, Graph Neural Network (GNN)Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Neuroscience

Introduction

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a common and extremely debilitating mental health condition that causes persistent low mood, cognitive impairment, and loss of interest in daily activities1. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than 322 million people worldwide suffer from MDD, making it one of the leading causes of disability2. Thus, it is crucial to diagnose MDD in an early and accurate manner to improve treatment outcomes and mitigate its negative economic and societal effects.

The conventional method of diagnosing MDD relies on clinical observations and patient-reported symptoms, which are susceptible to bias and inconsistency3. Therefore, leveraging neuroimaging techniques, particularly structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI), has gained increasing interest as a means of diagnosing MDD. sMRI provides high-resolution anatomical brain information, permitting researchers to identify structural abnormalities associated with the disorder, such as reduced gray matter (GM) volume in specific brain regions4.

Recently, deep learning (DL) has been widely recognized as one of the most effective methods for automating mental disorders diagnosis based on neuroimaging data. Specifically, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have shown great promise for MDD detection due to their ability to extract hierarchical spatial features from 3D brain volumes5,6. However, CNNs have several limitations, including their reliance on local receptive fields for feature extraction and their difficulty in modeling long-range spatial dependencies required for accurate diagnosis7. Furthermore, they exhibit limited generalizability, as their performance is often compromised when applied to datasets collected from multiple sites8.

To address these shortcomings, Vision Transformers (ViTs) have recently received significant attention for neuroimaging applications. Unlike CNNs, ViTs utilize self-attention mechanisms to capture local and global spatial dependencies, making them particularly useful for brain disorder classification tasks8. Several sMRI studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of ViT over CNN. For instance, Carcagnì et al.9, Sarraf et al.10, and Shaffi et al.11found that ViTs performed better than CNNs at detecting Alzheimer’s disease. Additionally, Bi et al12,13. have reported improved diagnostic accuracy with ViTs over CNN for diagnosis of schizophrenia. Although these findings are promising, few studies have examined ViTs for MDD classification using sMRI. However, most of these studies utilize patch-based methods, where sMRI volumes are uniformly divided into fixed-size 2D or 3D patches, thereby ignoring anatomically meaningful brain regions. To our knowledge, no prior work has systematically compared cube-based (3D patches) with atlas-based extraction strategies, where patches are derived from predefined brain atlases in ViT-based MDD detection frameworks.

This study addresses these gaps by proposing 3DViT-GAT, a unified atlas-based framework that integrates a 3D ViT and Graph Attention Network (GAT) for MDD detection utilizing sMRI data from the REST-meta-MDD dataset. Our key contributions are as follows:

Region-based embedding extraction: We developed an atlas-based ViT pipeline that creates patches based upon meaningful regions of interest (ROIs). For comparison, we also implemented a cube-based strategy using uniform 3D patches. For both pipelines, 3D ViT is used to extract region-level embeddings from sMRI data.

Related work

Structural MRI (sMRI) has been increasingly employed for the automated detection of major depressive disorder (MDD) using both machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques. Early ML-based methods relied on handcrafted feature extraction followed by classical classifiers. For instance, Kim et al.14 proposed a highly accurate prediction model to distinguish adolescents with MDD from their healthy counterparts (HC). In particular, features were manually extracted using unpaired t-tests, achieving 94.4% accuracy using support vector machines (SVM). Ma et al.15 introduced a radiomic-based approach to diagnose MDD, subthreshold depressions (StD), and HC. Structural radiomic features were extracted from sMRI and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) of gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM). The proposed model achieved accuracies of 86.75% for MDD vs. HC, 70.51% for STD vs. HC, and 54.55% for MDD vs. STD. Although these approaches demonstrated promising performance, they strongly depend on manually engineered features, which may limit robustness and generalizability.

Later, DL-based approaches became prominent due to their ability to automatically learn discriminative features. Mousavian et al.16 extracted feature representations using an entropy-based slice selection approach, a convolutional neural network (CNN), and pretrained neural networks such as VGG16 and Inceptionv3. Their study demonstrated the highest accuracy at 96%. More recent studies have explored 3D CNNs to better capture spatial hierarchy in brain data. Wang et al.5 developed a voxel-wise densely connected convolutional neural network known as 3D-DenseNet with transfer learning from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) dataset. Their model achieved a classification accuracy of 84.37% for MDD diagnosis. Similarly, Hong et al.6 proposed an automated 3D FRN-ResNet framework for identifying MDD from sMRI images. Their model was evaluated using the dataset from the Seventh Hospital of Hangzhou (SHH) and reported accuracy of 86.776%. Bindiya et al.17 proposed an end-to-end 3D CNN operating on GM images derived from sMRI, achieving a classification accuracy of 72.26%. However, these studies focused on local volumetric patterns and fixed receptive fields, making them less effective in explicitly model long-range inter-regional dependencies, which are crucial for understanding MDD-related structural alterations.

Recently, several studies have incorporated attention mechanisms into DL frameworks to enhance both interpretability and classification performance for sMRI-based MDD detection. Among them, Gao et al.18 proposed an attention-guided two-stage framework that integrates structural covariance network analysis and CNNs. In their method, structural covariance networks constructed using the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas were first used to generate attention heatmaps, which were then employed to enhance 3D GM images for final classification using a lightweight 3D CNN. Their model achieved an accuracy of 76.65% on the REST-meta-MDD dataset. Xiao et al.19 introduced a Transformer-guided framework, termed SQUID, which encodes non-overlapping 3D sMRI blocks using an autoencoder and reconstructs images through a Transformer-based module for MDD classification. Their method achieved an accuracy of 77.17% on the REST-meta-MDD dataset. Although attention and Transformer-based models improve global context modeling, most existing studies still relied on voxel-level representations. lacking explicit anatomical region modeling and structured inter-regional relationship learning.

Overall, most prior work on sMRI-based MDD classification relied on handcrafted features combined with ML models, or on DL trained on raw brain images or fixed-size image patches. Often, these approaches fail to capture the subtle spatial relationships between brain regions, which are essential for understanding the complex patterns of MDD. Moreover, handcrafted methods are highly dependent on the quality of manually extracted features, which can limit their generalizability and robustness. The absence of anatomical guidance in patching strategies can produce a loss of important structural information, thereby reducing the accuracy and interpretability of classification models. It is noteworthy that no prior studies have systematically compared anatomically informed (atlas-based) with uninformed (cube-based) region-extraction strategies for MDD detection.

To address these gaps, we proposed a novel 3DViT-GAT framework for MDD detection based on sMRI data. In this architecture, each brain volume is first divided into regions of interest (ROIs) based on anatomical atlases, enabling the model to focus on semantically meaningful regions. A 3D ViT model is employed to extract embeddings from each ROI, which capture local and global spatial patterns. These embeddings are then used to create subject-wise graphs, where nodes represent ROIs and edges reflect top-k cosine-similarity connections. Finally, a GAT model is trained to analyze these graphs by highlighting the most informative ROI connections, improving the accuracy of MDD classification. As a further evaluation of the influence of anatomical priors, we compared our atlas-based ViT-GNN model with a cube-based variant that extracts regions using fixed-size 3D patches.

Dataset description

REST-meta-MDD dataset

This study uses a dataset from the REST-meta-MDD consortium, which, to our knowledge, is the largest public MDD dataset20. This dataset comprises 2,428 participants from 25 sites, including 1,300 MDD patients (826 females and 474 males) and 1,128 HC20,21. Moreover, there are two types of imaging data, T1-weighted sMRI and rs-fMRI, collected at each site along with phenotypic data such as age, gender, medication status, episode status (recurrent or first episode), illness duration, and the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD)20. Participants in REST-meta-MDD gave their written informed consent prior to participating, and the local Institutional Review Board approved the collection of data at each site20,21.

Data preprocessing

The T1-weighted sMRI scans were preprocessed using Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration Through Exponentiated Lie algebra (DPARSF) software21. Preprocessing involved discarding the first ten volumes to ensure magnetization equilibrium, correcting head motion, segmenting into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CF), normalizing to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template, and spatial smoothing. Additionally, a duplicate site was discovered after consortium data sharing and discarded, and covariates were regressed to ensure data quality and consistency21. The resulting sMRI dataset contained 1586 subjects, of which 1276 were MDD patients and 1104 were HC.

Further, we utilized GM volumes in the MNI space to represent the original sMRI data. Each sMRI image had a standardized spatial dimension of voxels. A detailed description of the demographic characteristics of the subjects included in our study is provided in Table 1.

Methodology

This study presents a comprehensive framework for detecting MDD using sMRI data from the REST-meta-MDD dataset. This framework incorporates a 3D Vision Transformer (ViT) to extract brain-region features and a graph neural network (GNN) for modeling inter-regional relationships, enabling graph-level classification. The overall architecture of the proposed method is illustrated in Fig. 1. First, we extract region embeddings from each 3D sMRI image by using two different strategies: atlas-based and cube-based. In the atlas-based approach, the brain is segmented into semantically meaningful regions (ROIs) using four popular brain atlases, including Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas22, Harvard–Oxford (HO) atlas23, Dosenbach’s 160 functional ROIs (Dose)24, and Craddock’s clustering 200 ROIs (CK)25. This strategy preserves anatomical priors and facilitates biologically interpretable modeling of regional brain alterations associated with MDD. In contrast, the cube-based approach divides the whole brain into uniform 3D cubes without using any brain atlas. The extracted 3D ROIs or cubes are then treated as patches and fed into a 3D ViT model. The ViT is employed to learn discriminative region embeddings by modeling both local structural patterns and long-range spatial dependencies within each region. This transformer-based architecture enables effective representation learning without requiring handcrafted features, thereby capturing complex structural variations present in sMRI data. After that, we construct a cosine similarity matrix for each subject to model the inter-regional relationship between these embeddings. The similarity matrices are then represented as graphs. Finally, a Graph Attention Network (GAT) is applied to the constructed graphs to adaptively emphasize the most informative brain regions and their relationships, enabling effective graph classification. Region extraction strategies

Atlas-based extraction

Through this strategy, the brain is divided into meaningful regions based on predefined atlases. Specifically, this study used two structural atlases, AAL (116 ROIs) and HO (112 ROIs), as well as two functional atlases, Dose (160 ROIs) and CK (200 ROIs). Atlases provide a consistent spatial template that defines ROI boundaries, which facilitates the extraction of regions from sMRI scans.

In this case, each ROI is extracted as a volumetric patch, whereby the number of patches for each subject is equal to the number of ROIs defined by the selected atlas. The patch size is fixed to voxels to ensure uniform input dimensions across subjects and regions. These 3D patches represent localized structural features and serve as input to the 3D ViT model. Consequently, the model can learn region-specific discriminative representations, enhancing performance and accuracy for a subsequent graph classification task.

Cube-based extraction

In this method, the original sMRI images of size voxels are resized to a standardized shape of voxels. The resized images are then uniformly partitioned into non-overlapping 3D cubes of size voxels. Vision transformer model

The Vision Transformer (ViT) is a recent DL architecture that has exhibited impressive performance across various computer vision tasks, primarily image classification26. Contrary to CNNs, the ViT can model local and global dependencies across image patches via self-attention, enabling it to focus on features more relevant to classification27.

As part of this study, we employed a 3D ViT model to process sMRI images for MDD classification. The 3D ViT model operates on uniformly sized 3D patches, which are either extracted from a predefined atlas or generated via a non-overlapping partitioning process. In this manner, the model can leverage spatial context to detect subtle differences in sMRI images indicative of MDD, thereby increasing classification accuracy and robustness.

Graph construction

To capture inter-regional relationships, we constructed a subject-specific graph from region-wise embeddings generated by the 3D ViT model. In each graph, nodes represent individual regions defined as anatomical ROIs (in the atlas-based model) or as fixed-size 3D cubes (in the cube-based model). The edges between these nodes are determined by computing the cosine similarity between each pair of node embeddings. Cosine similarity is applied since it captures the directional consistency of high-dimensional embeddings while being invariant to vector magnitude, thereby reducing the variability resulting from the variation in feature scale and representation norms across subjects or scanning sites28,29. In addition, it does not introduce additional learnable variables and provides a computationally efficient construction of similarity-based graphs from deep MRI representations, as demonstrated in prior MRI-based graph learning studies2930. Graph Attention Network (GAT)

In graph-based learning, a Graph Attention Network (GAT) is a type of Graph Neural Network (GNN) that uses an attention mechanism to assign different weights to neighboring nodes based on their relevance to the target node31. In this manner, GAT can detect complex relationships and dependencies in graph-structured data, thereby capturing complex inter-regional relationships in brain graphs more effectively than traditional GNNs.

Formally, consider a graph G = (V, E), where V is the set of nodes (ROIs or 3D cubes) and E is the set of edges (similarity relationships). Results and discussion

Experimental results

In this section, we conduct an extensive analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed 3DViT-GAT framework under several experimental configurations. Three main scenarios are considered in our investigation. First, we assess the standalone performance of the 3D ViT model using two distinct input paradigms: (1) atlas-based extraction, which guides region segmentation using brain atlases, and (2) cube-based extraction, which divides the brain into uniform, non-overlapping 3D cubes. Second, our full 3DViT-GAT hybrid model is constructed by integrating ViT-derived embeddings into a GAT. Graph-based classification is similarly investigated utilizing both atlas-based and cube-based graph representations. Third, we compare the performance of our model with state-of-the-art methods to demonstrate its competitiveness and generalizability for detecting MDD from sMRI data. Finally, a model explainability analysis is conducted to identify the most critical brain ROIs associated with MDD, HC, and MDD vs. HC.

In the atlas-based approach, the brain is segmented into anatomically defined regions (ROIs) using four brain atlases (AAL, HO, Dose, and CK). Conversely, the cube-based approach divides the brain into uniform-sized volumetric regions.

They reported an accuracy of 72.26%, a sensitivity of 83.00%, a specificity of 59.00%, and an F1-score of 76.00%. Computational complexity analysis

Besides the classification performance, we also analyze the computational complexity of the proposed framework in terms of the number of trainable parameters. As shown in Table 9, the number of trainable parameters remains within a narrow range (approximately 5.52–5.58 million), despite substantial differences in the number of input tokens. his variation is caused primarily by token-dependent components of the ViT architecture, such as positional embeddings, which scale with the number of input tokens, while the Transformer encoder and GAT layers remain unchanged across models. The cube-based model has 36 tokens and the smallest parameter count (5.52M), but achieves lower accuracy (77.81%) than most atlas-based models. In contrast, the atlas-based models use ROI tokens defined by each atlas (116 for AAL, 160 for Dose, and 200 for CK), resulting in a modest increase in the number of trainable parameters. Among all models, the Dose-based configuration achieves the highest accuracy (80.88%) with 5.56M trainable parameters. Notably, the CK-based model has the largest number of tokens (200) and the highest parameter count (5.58M), but does not yield the best performance. Accordingly, classification accuracy does not directly correlate with the number of trainable parameters.

Ablation experiments

To further validate the effectiveness of the proposed 3DViT–GAT framework and to quantify the contribution of its key design components, we conducted a series of ablation experiments on the REST-meta-MDD dataset. All ablation experiments were performed using the Dose-based atlas model, as it achieved the best overall performance among all atlas-based and cube-based variants in the main experiments. Further, performance is evaluated using a stratified 10-fold cross validation strategy. Specifically, we investigate the following aspects of the proposed framework: (1) The impact of region token utilization in the ViT encoder (cls-only vs. cls+ROI); (2) The influence of different 3D patch sizes for region representation; (3) A comparison of feature extraction backbones (3D ViT vs. 3D CNN); and (4) The effect of neighborhood size (K) in graph construction.

Impact of region token utilization in the ViT encoder

The purpose of this ablation study is to explore the effects of incorporating ROI tokens into the Vision Transformer representation. Specifically, we compare a cls-only configuration with the proposed cls+ROI representation, where region tokens explicitly contribute to the final subject-level embedding. We evaluate whether preserving region-specific structural information improve classification performance compared to relying solely on a global representation. The results of this ablation study are summarized in Table 10. In this Table, the integration of ROI tokens with CLS token leads to consistent performance gains across all evaluation metrics. Specifically, the cls+ROI configuration increases accuracy from 74.96% to 76.72%, sensitivity from 81.87% to 82.19%, specificity from 66.91% to 70.36%, precision from 74.61% to 76.58%, and F1-score from 77.84% to 79.15%. These findings demonstrate that ROI tokens provide complementary and discriminatory information, which is not fully captured by CLS token alone. This analysis confirms that explicitly modeling regional structure enhances the learning of subject-level representations and supports the architectural choice made in this research.

Effect of K size in graph construction

This experiment investigates the impact of the neighborhood size (K) used in the graph construction process. The choice of K directly affects graph sparsity and connectivity, where overly small values may lead to insufficient relational information, while excessively large values may introduce noisy or redundant connections35. By varying K and keeping all other components fixed, this ablation evaluates the robustness of the proposed framework to different graph connectivity levels and identifies a suitable balance between structural completeness and noise suppression. The experimental results of our 3DViT-GAT model with four different K values are reported in Table 15. Based on the results, the model showed relatively worse performance across all evaluation metrics when a smaller neighborhood size (K = 5) is used. This behavior can be attributed to insufficient connectivity, which may limit the model’s ability to capture long-range and inter-regional structural dependencies. By increasing K to 7, significant improvements are observed across most evaluation metrics. Specifically, the model showed improvements of 0.63%, 2.63%, 1.74%, 0.26%, in terms of accuracy, specificity, precision, and F1-score, demonstrating that a denser graph structure improves the effectiveness of feature propagation. However, the best overall performance is achieved with K = 10, yielding the highest accuracy (80.88%), sensitivity (86.25%), precision (79.92%), and F1-score (82.90%). Despite a slight reduction in specificity and precision compared to K = 7, the overall improvement across most metrics suggests that K = 10 provides a better balance between structural completeness and noise reduction. A further increase to K = 15 results in a slight performance degradation across all metrics, likely due to the introduction of noisy or less informative edges that obscure discriminative regional interactions. Overall, these findings support our selection of K = 10, as it yields the most consistent and balanced performance across evaluation metrics while preserving meaningful inter-regional relationships.

Discussion

As part of this study, the 3DViT-GAT framework has been designed to overcome two fundamental shortcomings of existing sMRI-based MDD classification pipelines: the lack of anatomically informed region modeling and the lack of characterization of inter-regional structural relationships. In contrast to cube-based partitioning strategies, our framework utilizes predefined brain atlases to extract anatomically meaningful regions of interest (ROIs). Incorporating prior neuroanatomical knowledge into the learning process enables the model to focus on semantically consistent regions of the brain while reducing the influence of irrelevant background voxels. This design can capture more biologically relevant patterns, which enhances the model’s ability to distinguish between healthy and MDD-affected brains.

From a representation learning perspective, the 3D Vision Transformer (3D ViT) serves as a powerful backbone for capturing both local and global structural patterns within each ROI. By modeling ROIs as sequences of 3D patches, the ViT encoder learns long-range spatial dependencies that are difficult to detect using traditional convolutional kernels. Further, the ablation experiments indicate that combining the class token and ROI embeddings results in more discriminative representations than using only a global token, emphasizing the complementary role of region-level structural descriptors. In this manner, the model is able to encode a subject-level global context while preserving fine-grained morphological features.

Beyond isolated regional encoding, the integration of a Graph Attention Network (GAT) provides explicit modeling of inter-regional relationships that are crucial for characterizing brain abnormalities associated with mental disorders. This framework creates subject-specific graphs based on ROI embeddings to capture higher-order dependencies between distant but functionally and structurally related regions. Further, the attention mechanism enables the network to weight informative connections adaptively, thereby facilitating selective aggregation of region interactions rather than uniform local pooling. The consistently stable relationship between sensitivity and specificity observed for the atlas-based variants indicates that incorporating anatomical priors and graph-based relational modeling leads to improved robustness and reduced classification bias, thereby supporting the clinical reliability of the proposed framework.

From a clinical perspective, the architecture facilitates the development of anatomically grounded biomarkers through the alignment of model representations with established brain network organization. Moreover, the increased robustness of atlas-based variants across evaluation metrics indicates their potential effectiveness in real-world diagnostic settings, in which balanced detection of patients and controls is essential.

In spite of these strengths, there are several limitations. The framework relies on predefined atlases, whose performance is influenced by the atlas selection as well as ROI granularity. In addition, the constructed graphs emphasize static structural similarities and do not explicitly model temporal or causal relationships. Moreover, REST-meta-MDD is a multi-site cohort that may be affected by inter-site differences in scanners, acquisition protocols, and other factors that confound model generalization. While subject-independent stratified cross-validation was used to prevent subject-level data leakage between training and test sets, explicit site-stratified or leave-one-site-out validation was not conducted, thus residual site-related bias cannot be completely excluded. Finally, the current study focuses exclusively on structural MRI, but incorporating complementary modalities may enhance representational richness. A future study will explore these directions.

Model explainability and interpretation

To address our concerns about the interpretability and biological relevance of the extracted brain regions, we conducted a post hoc explainability analysis to identify which regions played the most significant role in distinguishing MDD from HC. To achieve this, we applied a node-level interpretability approach derived from GNNExplainer36. Moreover, the explainability analysis is conducted using the two best-performing atlas-based 3DViT-GAT models (Dose and AAL) in our study to ensure meaningful and representative results. For each trained model, the contribution of each node (ROI) is calculated across all subjects in the test set, separately for the MDD and HC classes and for overall classification. The ROI importance scores are then averaged over all test subjects to derive reliable estimates of regional significance. We visualized the top 10 ROIs on a cortical surface template using bubble plots, with bubble size and color intensity indicating each ROI’s importance.

Figure 2 (a-c) provides the explainability results derived from the Dose-based model, highlighting the most salient ROIs contributing to the classification of MDD, HC, and MDD vs. HC, respectively. All three explainability maps indicated that highly influential ROIs are located within the fronto-parietal network (FPN), occipital network (ON), cerebellum network (CN), sensorimotor network (SMN), and cingulo-opercular network (CON). A summary of the top 10 salient ROIs identified across these networks in all three analyses is presented in Table 16. The fronto-parietal network (FPN) plays a significant role in executive functions, cognitive control, and emotion regulation. In our analysis, FPN regions such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) and inferior parietal lobule (IPL) emerged as highly influential. Structural abnormalities in these regions have been consistently linked to MDD and associated cognitive deficits3,18,37. The occipital network (ON) has also been implicated in recent voxel-based morphometry studies of MDD, suggesting abnormal visual processing and attentional bias in depressed individuals38,39. The presence of inferior cerebellar regions in the cerebellar network (CN) aligns with evidence that the cerebellum contributes to affective and cognitive regulation through cerebello-cortical circuits and exhibits structural alterations in depression3,40,41. The Sensorimotor Network (SMN) emerged as a salient system for our model, with influential regions including the precentral gyrus (PreCG), parietal, and ventral/inferior frontal cortex (vFC). Structural MRI and voxel-based morphometry (VBM) studies have repeatedly reported GM abnormalities in the precentral gyrus in MDD, linking motor cortex alterations to psychomotor symptoms frequently observed in depression42. Similarly, parietal regions associated with the SMN, particularly the inferior parietal and postcentral areas, have demonstrated consistent structural alterations in MDD, supporting sensorimotor integration and attentional mechanisms playing a role in the illness43,44.Moreover, large-scale morphometric studies have identified cortical abnormalities in MDD within ventral and inferior frontal regions, which serve as an interface between sensorimotor and cognitive-affective systems45,46..

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed a unified atlas-based framework integrating 3D ViT with GAT for MDD classification using sMRI data. This model efficiently captures intra- and inter-regional relationships by using predefined brain atlases to extract ROIs, thereby enhancing its ability to identify complex brain changes associated with MDD. Results indicated that most atlas-based models (AAL, Dose, and CK) outperformed the Cube-based model, emphasizing the importance of anatomical priors for guiding the extraction of meaningful regional features. This process assisted in the precise identification of disease-relevant brain regions, improving classification performance for MDD detection. In future work, we plan to extend the proposed framework in several directions. First, we aim to investigate multimodal learning by incorporating resting-state fMRI and clinical features with sMRI. This integration is expected to provide complementary information that improves feature representations, optimizing classification accuracy and robustness. Second, leave-one-site-out cross-validation will be conducted to provide a more accurate evaluation of cross-site generalization and to improve the clinical robustness of the proposed framework. Third, we intend to examine more advanced graph learning techniques, including recent adaptive and hierarchical graph-based models, which have demonstrated strong representation learning capabilities in complex graph-structured data50,51. Finally, we plan to explore ensemble strategies that combine information from multiple atlases rather than evaluating each atlas separately. Such fusion approaches could benefit from the complementary strengths of different brain parcellations, thereby increasing model generalizability.
