Natural orifice surgery for rescue of stent misplacement in endoscopic gallbladder drainage
Authors: Hengyu Wang, Yufeng Lei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsLumen-apposing metal stent (LAMS) misdeployment in endoscopic ultrasound–guided gallbladder drainage is one of the primary adverse events, but few studies, to our knowledge, have reported the misplacement of the LAMS into the abdominal cavity and the following endoscopic rescue treatment.MethodsWe reported a case of the proximal flange misplaced into the abdominal cavity in a fully expanded condition, whereas the distal flange was attached to the gallbladder, resulting in a perforation between the gallbladder and abdominal cavity, and only the puncture site was identified on the duodenal wall. After multidisciplinary discussion, we performed natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery (NOTES) as a rescue therapy immediately.ResultsUsing NOTES, we identified the misplaced LAMS in the abdominal cavity and repositioned it with the basket. No other serious consequences occurred.ConclusionsNOTES was a feasible method to deal with LAMS misplacement in this case as a rescue therapy. However, the adverse event should be avoided carefully during the procedure in case further serious consequences occur.

Introduction

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided gallbladder drainage (EUS-GBD) with lumen-apposing metal stent (LAMS) placement has become an optional method to treat cholecystitis and cholelithiasis in different clinical settings.1, 2, 3 In addition, this technique is a relatively safe procedure with minimal adverse events in expert hands.4 LAMS misdeployment in EUS-GBD is one of the primary adverse events, with a low rate of 3.4%,5 but few studies have reported the misplacement of the LAMS into the abdominal cavity and the following endoscopic rescue treatment, to our knowledge.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old woman with intermittent abdominal pain for several years was referred to our hospital for severe abdominal pain reoccurrence. CT and ultrasound revealed gallbladder stone and cholecystitis (Figs. 1 and 2), which meant that this patient met the indication for cholecystectomy. However, this patient insisted on gallbladder preservation. Based on the technical view, gallbladder drainage can be performed under EUS guidance in almost all clinical situations. In addition, LAMS placement is the indication for EUS-GBD, even considered as the first choice in 2024.6 However, EUS-GBD with LAMS placement remains a second-line therapy for gallstone removal, especially in patients with acute cholecystitis who are unsuitable candidates for surgery because of comorbidities. However, EUS-GBD has demonstrated significant advantages in both safety and preservation of life quality. This method is even feasible for patients with massive ascites who are precluded from percutaneous gallbladder drainage operation, as well as those with coagulopathy or on antithrombotic/antiplatelet medications.4 Therefore, we decided to perform EUS-GBD with LAMS placement (Hot AXIOS; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Mass, USA), and fluoroscopy was also prepared for the alternative method in this patient after multidisciplinary discussion. The diameter of the LAMS was 15 mm, which was beneficial to access the gallbladder with a gastroscope after fistula formation because the diameter of the gastroscope was 9.8 mm. We deployed the LAMS with freehand in this case because, compared with the mechanical puncture of EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration, the electrocautery puncture capability of the LAMS we used is more convenient and facilitates direct puncture and deployment during EUS-GBD procedures. However, the adverse event occurred with the LAMS misplaced into the abdominal cavity during the stent deployment (Fig. 3). After the distal flange was deployed, we did not pull the deployed distal flange back and snug tightly against the gallbladder wall. On the contrary, the distal flange was pulled forward after locking the hub because of an operation mistake. Both EUS and endoscopic view did not provide effective monitoring in the stage of proximal flange deployment. In addition, fluoroscopy only revealed full expansion of the LAMS, while the exact site was not clear. EUS showed the distal flange attached to the gallbladder, and only the puncture site was identified on the duodenal wall when switching to the endoscopic view. Given that the gallbladder was free, we considered wire placement through the stent and coiling in the gallbladder to maintain access and prevent displacement of the LAMS, which may not be reliable, whereas natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery (NOTES) was a more-direct method. Therefore, we decided to perform NOTES with a gastroscope (GIF-260J; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) immediately without wire placement in the endoscopy suite after discussion with surgeons, and the surgery team provided surgery support as the backup (Video 1, available online at www.videogie.org). Full-thickness incision of the duodenal bulb wall through the original puncture site was made with an electrosurgical knife (MK-T-2-195; Micro-Tech, Nanjing, China), and no bleeding was encountered (Fig. 4). Cautery settings were as follows: the Erbe (Tübingen, Germany) VIO 200D electrosurgical unit in the ENDO CUT Q mode (effect 3, duration 2, interval 3) and the forced coagulation mode (effect 3, 45 W), used for precise mucosal and muscular incision with limited bleeding. Then using a transparent cap with a diameter of 12.4 mm at the distal end of the gastroscope, we used blunt expansion to enlarge the incision and access the abdominal cavity, during which the gallbladder wall was penetrated because of distal flange apposition and the proximal flange, which should have been attached to the duodenal wall, and achieved complete expansion, creating a perforation between the gallbladder and abdominal cavity (Fig. 5). Gastroscope view confirmed bile leaked into the abdominal cavity through the LAMS. Subsequently, the proximal flange of the LAMS was pulled back to the duodenal lumen with a basket used to grasp and reposition the proximal flange (Fig. 6). The omentum, which can also be used as a surgical patch, was grasped and pulled into the duodenal lumen with the basket. No additional bleeding caused by omentum avulsion occurred. Also, we prepared to perform endoscopic hemostasis if bleeding was encountered. Given that the fully deployed diameter of the LAMS was 15 mm, whereas the incision diameter was approximately 10 mm, no additional clip closure, endoscopic suture, or fixation was performed (Fig. 7). The vital signs of this patient were normal, and no other condition that needed to be resolved occurred after the misdeployment. The whole operation lasted 30 minutes. The other consideration for deciding to perform NOTES was estimation of the LAMS site and the technical maturity for NOTES. The diameter of the proximal flange during the retrieval process will be further reduced after the basket was tightened, and the basket was flexible, indicating the LAMS accessed the duodenal incision, and repositioning was feasible. In addition, endoscopic closure with drainage tube insertion can be performed to facilitate incision healing if the duodenal defect enlarges. Surgical operation was also an optional choice if the problem cannot be resolved. However, performing NOTES means an endoscopic incision that is nearly equal to a surgical incision. The duodenal leak was difficult because we had many closure approaches. Careful operation and stable distal flange apposition helped the distal flange to be within the gallbladder, and we confirmed the access between the duodenum and gallbladder under EUS view after LAMS repositioning. In addition, if the distal flange was pulled out of the gallbladder when the proximal flange was pulled back into the duodenum, we will perform LAMS removal and clip closure for the duodenal wall defect. Then we will perform EUS-GBD with LAMS deployment again with triple-lumen feeding tube positioning at the duodenal incision site. We combined EUS and gastroscopic views to confirm the position of the distal flange, so fluoroscopy and contrast agent were not used to assess whether the distal flange was deployed in the gallbladder, as well as the continuity between the duodenum and gallbladder without a leak. This patient received ceftazidime for 3 days, and CT scan showed no signs of peritonitis or gallstones in the abdominal cavity, as well as no fever and abdominal pain. Although this patient did not develop severe pneumoperitoneum, we still performed percutaneous abdominal decompression. A mature fistula was observed after a 3-week LAMS dwell time. Therefore, the LAMS was removed after the endoscopic examination of the gallbladder and gallstone extraction through the fistula. No adverse events or gallstone recurrence were found after 6 months after LAMS removal (Fig. 8).

Conclusions

This case highlights that LAMS misplacement is a rare but serious adverse event during the EUS-GBD procedure. NOTES was a feasible rescue therapy to manage the adverse event in this case. However, misplacement should be carefully avoided during the procedure to avoid further serious consequences.
