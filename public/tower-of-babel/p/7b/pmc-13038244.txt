The management of hepatorenal syndrome–acute kidney injury (HRS-AKI): A national survey of hepatology provider practices

ABSTRACT
Background:Hepatorenal syndrome–acute kidney injury (HRS-AKI) is associated with high mortality in cirrhosis. Recent FDA approval of terlipressin and updated clinical guidance have expanded management options, but real-world practice patterns remain unknown.Methods:We conducted a nationwide survey of U.S. hepatology providers regarding HRS-AKI management practices. The 33-question survey assessed provider demographics, vasoconstrictor selection, treatment timing, and adherence to guideline recommendations.Results:Among 162 respondents, most were hepatologists (94%) at academic centers (83%). Only 11% obtained a nephrology consultation at AKI detection, with 44% waiting until worsening renal function. Terlipressin was available at 80% of institutions but more commonly at academic centers (84% vs. 53%, p=0.003). Consistent with guideline recommendations, 77% of providers initiate vasoconstrictors only after completing a trial of volume expansion (if no renal improvement), particularly in academic centers (80% vs. 60% non-academic, p=0.03). Terlipressin (49%) and midodrine/octreotide (44%) were preferred first-line treatments, with providers at academic centers more likely to use midodrine/octreotide (46% vs. 30%, p=0.008). Mean arterial pressure was used by 62% of providers to guide vasoconstrictor dosing. Most providers (73%) discontinued vasoconstrictor treatment after 4 days if no improvement.Conclusions:Significant variations exist between guideline recommendations and real-world HRS-AKI management, especially first-line treatment choice, treatment monitoring, and nephrology consultation. These findings highlight opportunities to improve guideline implementation and identify areas where practice patterns might inform provider education and future guidance updates.

INTRODUCTION

Acute kidney injury (AKI) occurs in up to 60% of hospitalized patients with decompensated cirrhosis and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality.12 Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS-AKI), a unique form of AKI in patients with cirrhosis and ascites, carries an especially poor prognosis without prompt recognition and treatment. The pathophysiology of HRS-AKI is characterized by splanchnic vasodilation leading to decreased effective arterial blood volume and renal perfusion, which can be reversed with vasoconstrictor therapy.34 Terlipressin, a vasopressin analog that acts as a splanchnic vasoconstrictor, was recently approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of HRS-AKI after showing efficacy in improving renal function in the CONFIRM trial.56

Following the publication of the CONFIRM trial results and subsequent FDA approval in September 2022, both the Acute Disease Quality Initiative and International Club of Ascites (ADQI/ICA) and the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) released updated guidance statements for the management of HRS-AKI.1278 These statements provide recommendations regarding the timing of nephrology consultation, choice and titration of vasoconstrictor therapy, concurrent albumin use, and treatment discontinuation criteria. While the latest American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidance on HRS-AKI was published before terlipressin approval in the United States, its use was also recommended. Despite the range of existing recommendations and their inherent variability, the extent of implementation by healthcare providers in actual clinical settings remains undetermined.

Understanding real-world practice patterns in HRS-AKI management is crucial, as variations in care delivery may impact patient outcomes. In addition, identifying areas where clinical practice differs from guideline recommendations can highlight opportunities for education and quality improvement initiatives. We aimed to perform a national, US-based survey of provider practices regarding the diagnosis and management of HRS-AKI, focusing on vasoconstrictor selection, treatment initiation timing, and adherence to current guideline recommendations.

Survey questions

Survey participants and information

A nationwide survey was distributed to hepatology providers across the United States from May 6th, 2024, through July 6th, 2024, using SurveyMonkey (San Mateo, CA), an anonymous web-based platform configured to permit only 1 survey completion per respondent. Survey distribution utilized a convenience sampling approach, leveraging prior survey participants, institutional websites, and professional networks to achieve broad national representation. A designated provider at each participating institution was tasked with distributing the survey to hepatology providers within their institution and professional network while tracking and reporting the total number of recipients to calculate response rates. The initial contact email detailed the study’s purpose, emphasized its voluntary nature without compensation, and provided investigator contact information, with a follow-up reminder email sent 2 weeks later. The survey comprised 33 questions and required ~5 minutes to complete. The Mount Sinai Hospital Institutional Review Board approved this study protocol.

Statistical analysis

Survey responses were reported using descriptive statistics. Item non-responses to specific questions were excluded from the analysis. All variables were categorical, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were performed to identify factors associated with provider practices. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of <0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using R Studio Version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

This research was conducted in accordance with the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Mount Sinai University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the survey.

RESULTS

Survey participation

Surveys were sent to 321 participants with a return of 162 complete responses (50%) from 36 states and the District of Columbia in the United States (Table 1). The characteristics of respondents and their practice settings are summarized in Table 2. Most respondents were physicians (99%) specializing in hepatology (94%), with 53% having <10 years of practice experience. Most providers practiced at academic medical centers with liver transplant programs (83%), and 79% reported spending more than 50% of their time in clinical practice.

Timing of nephrology consultation

Despite ADQI/ICA and AGA guidance recommending early nephrology involvement, only 11% of respondents reported obtaining nephrology consultation at the time of AKI detection. Instead, respondents were most likely (44%) to wait until worsening or non-improving renal function on vasoconstrictor therapy. We found similar practice patterns with the majority waiting until worsening or non-improving renal function on vasoconstrictor therapy when analyzed by center type (Academic: 43% vs. Non-Academic: 58%, p=0.5), by years of practice (>10 years: 39% vs. ≤10 years: 49%, p=0.09), by geography (Midwest: 47% vs. Northeast: 38% vs. Southeast: 33% vs. Southwest: 50% vs. West: 46%, p=0.2), and by amount of clinical time (<75%: 48% vs. ≥75% 40%, p=0.8).

Availability of terlipressin and other vasoconstrictors

The availability of various vasoconstrictors is summarized in Table 3. Terlipressin was available at 80% of responding institutions but significantly more at academic medical centers than non-academic centers (84% vs. 53%, p=0.003). We found no geographical variation in the availability of terlipressin (percentage by region: 62%–90%, p=0.2). Among the 130 respondents with terlipressin available, the following restrictions were present: need for nephrology consult (29%), need for step-down level of care (27%), need for intensive care unit (ICU) (5%), and need for administration approval (18%). Fifty-two percent of respondents had no terlipressin utilization restrictions for hepatologists at their center; however, only 1% of respondents practiced at a center without any restrictions for all providers. When respondents were polled on their greatest concern for terlipressin, 42% reported pulmonary edema, 24% cost, 14% ischemic events, 12% stated no significant concerns, and 8% said other reasons. Regarding other vasoconstrictors, 12% reported the ability to utilize low-dose norepinephrine in a non-intensive care unit (ICU) setting. Low-dose norepinephrine was available at 13% of academic centers versus 5% of non-academic centers (p<0.001). Among centers with low-dose norepinephrine available, 72% were academic centers and 28% were non-academic centers.

Initiation of vasoconstrictors: in whom and when

While ADQI/ICA and AGA guidelines recommend against the use of terlipressin in patients with hypoxia, ongoing ischemia, or serum creatinine (sCr) values ≥5 mg/dL, respondents reported the following clinical scenarios as contraindications to terlipressin use: liver transplant candidates (11%), MELD 3.0 ≥35 (41%), sCr ≥5 mg/dL (66%), acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) grade 3 (60%), ongoing ischemia of non-renal organs (85%), hypoxia (87%), and history of cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease (37%). There were significant differences in the perceived contraindications to terlipressin use by clinical setting: liver transplant candidates (Academic: 9% vs. Non-Academic: 25%, p=0.047), sCr ≥5 mg/dL (Academic: 69% vs. Non-Academic: 40%, p=0.02), Hypoxia (Academic: 90% vs. Non-Academic: 65%, p=0.008). The only perceived contraindication significantly different by years in practice was MELD 3.0 ≥35 (>10 years: 51% vs. ≤10 years: 32%, p=0.02). The only perceived contraindication with significant geographic variation was a history of cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease (percentage by region: 10%–55%, p=0.01). None of the surveyed contraindications varied by percentage of clinical time (p>0.2 for all).

Respondents gave the following clinical scenarios in which they would cautiously use terlipressin: liver transplant candidates (44%), MELD 3.0 ≥35 (43%), sCr ≥5 mg/dL (17%), ACLF grade 3 (25%), ongoing ischemia of non-renal organs (4%), hypoxia (6%), and history of cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease (49%). The only clinical scenario in which non-academic providers would cautiously use terlipressin differently from academic providers was in patients with hypoxia (Academic: 4% vs. Non-Academic: 20%, p=0.02). The only significant geographic variation in the cautious use of terlipressin was in patients with a history of cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease (percentage by region: 23%–81%, p=0.003). None of the surveyed clinical scenarios for cautious use varied by percentage of clinical time (p>0.2 for all).

While the AASLD, ADQI/ICA, and AGA guidelines all recommend initiating terlipressin at the time of HRS-AKI diagnosis, this definition varies regarding the timing of volume expansion trials and the necessity of albumin. We surveyed respondents regarding when they start any vasoconstrictor treatment (eg, midodrine/octreotide, terlipressin, norepinephrine) for HRS-AKI (Figure 1). The majority of providers (77%) start vasoconstrictor treatment after a trial of volume expansion, while smaller proportions start after initial AKI workup (7.4%), when AKI is detected (1.8%), or have other approaches (12.9%). Providers practicing in academic settings were significantly more likely to defer vasoconstrictor treatment until after a trial of volume expansion (Academic: 80% vs. Non-Academic: 60%, p=0.03). There were no significant differences in the timing of initiation of any vasoconstrictor by years in practice, geographic location, or percentage of clinical time (p>0.05 for all).

Factors considered in initiating vasoconstrictor therapy.

Among the 126 respondents who would only start any vasoconstrictor treatment after a trial of volume expansion, we surveyed at which sCr thresholds they would use to start vasoconstrictor therapy: if sCr has not returned to baseline (18%), if sCr is unchanged (42%), if sCr only decreases by <25% (12%), if sCr increases irrespective of absolute level (21%), and other (7%). These thresholds did not vary significantly by clinical setting, years in practice, geography, or percentage of clinical time (p>0.05 for all).

Utilization of vasoconstrictors: Which agent, what targets

We asked respondents if they would use any vasoconstrictor therapy if the sCr was ≤1.5 mg/dL, with only 43% of respondents stating they would consider use. The utilization in patients with a sCr ≤1.5 mg/dL did not vary significantly by clinical setting, years in practice, percentage of clinical time, or geography (p>0.05 for all).

Fifty-five percent of respondents stated that they use mean arterial pressure (MAP) to guide starting vasoconstrictor therapy. Those respondents who utilize MAP to guide starting vasoconstrictor therapy were more likely to be from an academic setting (Academic: 57% vs. Non-Academic: 35%, p=0.04); there was no difference in the utilization of MAP to guide initiation of therapy by years of practice, percentage of clinical time, or geography (p>0.05 for all).

When asked, “Which vasoconstrictor do you prefer as first-line treatment for HRS-AKI?”, providers most commonly chose terlipressin (49.1%) and midodrine/octreotide (43.6%), while norepinephrine and other options were rarely selected (1.8% and 4.9%, respectively) (Figure 2). This response varied significantly by clinical setting, where respondents at Academic centers (46%) were more likely than Non-Academic centers (30%) to utilize midodrine and octreotide as the first-line treatment (p=0.008). We found similar findings if we looked only among the 130 respondents with terlipressin available at their center—respondents at Academic centers (39%) were more likely to utilize midodrine and octreotide as first-line treatment than respondents at Non-Academic centers (0%) (p=0.002).

Sixty-two percent of respondents stated that they used MAP to titrate vasoconstrictor dosing after starting HRS-AKI. The utilization of MAP-guided vasoconstrictor use was greater among respondents in an Academic center (66%) than in a Non-Academic center (35%) (p=0.008). There was no significant difference in the utilization of MAP to titrate by years of practice, percentage of clinical time, or geography (p>0.05 for all). Among the 101 (66%) of respondents who utilize MAP to titrate vasoconstrictors, 41% titrate vasoconstrictor dose to increase MAP by ≥15 mm Hg from baseline, 35% titrate vasoconstrictor dose to a specific absolute target, 13% titrate vasoconstrictor dose to any increase in MAP above baseline, and 12% titrate in a non-specified way. These practice patterns did not differ by clinical setting, years of practice, percentage of clinical time, or geography (p>0.05 for all).

Fifty-five percent of respondents use serum albumin levels to guide intravenous albumin (IVA) administration. These practice patterns did not differ by clinical setting, years of practice, percentage of clinical time, or geography (p>0.05 for all). Among the 90 respondents who utilize serum albumin to inform IVA administration, 64% start IVA only if pre-treatment serum albumin is <3.5 g/dL, 24% start IVA only if pre-treatment serum albumin is <3 g/dL, 5% start IVA only if pre-treatment serum albumin is <2.5 g/dL, and 8% follow a different pattern. These practice patterns did not differ by clinical setting, years of practice, percentage of clinical time, or geography (p>0.05 for all).

Discontinuation of terlipressin: When and in whom

When surveyed regarding the criteria used to discontinue terlipressin, allowing for more than 1 selection, 50% of respondents discontinue when the sCr improved to within 0.3 mg/dL of baseline, 50% discontinue when the sCr improved to <1.5 mg/dL, and 27% discontinued when the sCr improved but >0.3 mg/dL of baseline and up to 14 days of treatment. These responses did not vary when analyzed by clinical setting, years of practice, percentage of clinical time, or geography (p>0.05 for all). Regarding the futility of terlipressin, 73% of respondents would stop treatment when there was no change or worsening of sCr after 4 days of initiation. These responses did not vary when analyzed by clinical setting, years of practice, percentage of clinical time, or geography (p>0.05 for all).

Utilization of renal replacement therapy in HRS-AKI

Among respondents, only 20% would start renal replacement therapy (RRT) irrespective of liver transplant candidacy, 15% would start RRT if indicated only if the patient is a liver transplant candidate, 61% would start RRT if indicated, and liver transplant candidacy is being assessed, and 4% reported another approach to RRT.

Follow-up after terlipressin

After discontinuing terlipressin, 53% of respondents would start midodrine on discharge in patients with hypotension, and 25% would start on all patients treated for HRS-AKI. These practices did not differ by clinical setting (p=0.1), by years in practice (p=0.9), and by geography (p=0.4). Respondents reported that they would start a liver transplant evaluation in the following scenarios: all patients with HRS-AKI: 49%; if MELD 3.0 >15: 42%; other: 9%. These practices did not differ by clinical setting (p=0.1), by years in practice (p=0.9), and by geography (p=0.9).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this study represents the first nationwide survey examining hepatology provider practices, attitudes, and knowledge regarding the management of HRS-AKI and the utilization of terlipressin. This survey of predominantly physician hepatologists (99%) from academic medical centers (83%) revealed significant variations in the management of HRS-AKI. Only 11% of respondents obtained a nephrology consultation at AKI detection, with most (44%) waiting until worsening renal function. Terlipressin was available at 80% of institutions, more commonly at academic centers (84% vs. 53%), with pulmonary edema (42%) being the primary concern. There was general agreement regarding contraindications to terlipressin use between respondents, guidelines, and FDA prescribing information, including avoidance in ACLF grade 3. Most providers (77%) initiated vasoconstrictor treatment after volume expansion, with terlipressin (49%) and midodrine/octreotide (44%) being the preferred first-line treatments. Notably, 62% of respondents used MAP to titrate vasoconstrictor dosing, and 73% would stop treatment when there was no improvement in sCr after 4 days. About half of the respondents would start liver transplant evaluation for all HRS-AKI patients or start RRT (if indicated) in those under liver transplant evaluation. After terlipressin treatment, 53% would start midodrine on discharge in patients with hypotension.

Regarding the availability of various vasoconstrictors, our data highlight that most surveyed centers have terlipressin (80%) available, and 12% have low-dose norepinephrine in non-ICU settings. Despite this availability, significant restrictions and concerns remain regarding its use. Nevertheless, with greater use, future surveys should focus on changes in these views over time, and post-approval registry studies should inform the real-life occurrence of complications of concern, specifically pulmonary edema. The availability of alternative vasoconstrictors, namely low-dose norepinephrine in non-ICU settings, and the concerns regarding the cost and side effects of terlipressin may provide the rationale for comparator trials to confirm findings in non-US-based populations.910

Regarding the initiation and utilization of vasoconstrictors for HRS-AKI, we identified several key differences between provider practice and the recommendations from AASLD, ADQI/ICA, and AGA guidelines (Table 4).127 While ADQI/ICA and AGA recommend early nephrology consultation, only 11% of surveyed providers obtain nephrology consultation at initial AKI detection, with most (44%) waiting until worsening or non-improving renal function on vasoconstrictor therapy. Despite guideline recommendations for terlipressin as first-line therapy for HRS-AKI, only 49% of providers utilize it as their initial treatment choice, with 44% preferring midodrine/octreotide combination therapy—even in centers where terlipressin is available. This deviation from guidelines was particularly notable in academic centers (p=0.008), an unexpected finding given the explicit recommendation against using midodrine/octreotide when terlipressin is available. While we identified general concerns regarding terlipressin (primarily pulmonary edema 42% and cost 24%) and documented institutional restrictions, our survey did not assess specific clinical decision-making approaches, such as terlipressin rescue after midodrine/octreotide failure, for example. Understanding whether efficacy perceptions, safety considerations, provider knowledge, institutional barriers, cost, or other factors drive these preferences—and how these considerations differ between academic and non-academic settings—represents an important area for future investigation to inform targeted implementation strategies to align with guidelines. Regarding vasoconstrictor initiation timing, current guidelines define HRS-AKI diagnosis as requiring completion of a volume expansion trial without improvement in renal function, after which treatment should begin immediately. Consistent with this framework, 77% of providers initiate vasoconstrictors after completing volume expansion, particularly in academic centers (80% vs. 60% non-academic, p=0.03). The remaining 23% deviate from guideline-based diagnostic criteria by initiating treatment earlier—either at AKI detection (1.8%), after initial workup (7.4%), or through other approaches (12.9%). Notably, recent data suggest that the diagnostic criteria themselves may require refinement, as shortening the albumin challenge from 48 to 24 hours may lead to overdiagnosis of HRS-AKI, raising questions about optimal volume expansion duration rather than treatment timing once diagnosis is established.11 These findings demonstrate the need for further studies regarding the ideal timing to initiate vasoconstrictors and greater adoption of more effective agents.

Guidelines and real-world practices also differ regarding the use of hemodynamic parameters and supportive therapies during HRS-AKI treatment. No guidance statement specifically outlines the utilization of MAP to guide terlipressin-based therapy. Despite this, 55% of respondents use MAP to guide initiation, and 62% use it for dose titration, with academic centers more likely to follow this practice than non-academic centers. Even among those using MAP, targets have significant variability: 41% aim for an increase of ≥15 mm Hg from baseline, while 35% target a specific absolute value. Regarding IVA administration, while AASLD, ADQI/ICA, and AGA recommend its judicious use based on volume status, our survey found that 55% of providers use serum albumin levels to guide IVA administration, with most (64%) initiating IVA only when pre-treatment serum albumin is <3.5 g/dL. This variability in both MAP monitoring and albumin administration suggests a need for further studies to inform more specific guidance on optimal pharmacodynamic targets and volume management strategies during HRS-AKI treatment.

The timing of discontinuation of vasoconstrictor therapy also demonstrates important differences between guideline recommendations and real-world practice. The ADQI/ICA guidelines recommend stopping therapy when sCr returns to within 0.3 mg/dL of baseline, if severe adverse reactions develop, if kidney function does not improve after 48 hours on maximum doses, if RRT is indicated, or after 14 days maximum. The AGA guidelines differ slightly, recommending discontinuation 24 hours after sCr decreases to <1.5 mg/dL, after 14 days, or if there is no improvement in renal function after 4 days on the maximum dose. Our survey revealed variable practice patterns that only partially align with these recommendations—50% discontinued when sCr improved to within 0.3 mg/dL of baseline (aligned with ADQI/ICA), 50% when sCr improved to <1.5 mg/dL (aligned with AGA), and 27% when sCr improved but remained >0.3 mg/dL from baseline up to 14 days of treatment. Regarding futility assessment, providers favored the AGA approach, with 73% stopping treatment after no improvement at 4 days rather than ADQI/ICA’s 48-hour threshold, highlighting the need for more data regarding discontinuation timing.

Our study has several important limitations. First, respondents were predominantly hepatologists at academic centers, while HRS-AKI patients represent a complex cohort requiring care from multiple disciplines (eg, hepatologists, nephrologists, hospitalists, intensivists) and provider types (physicians, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, trainees) across various clinical settings, including safety-net hospitals. Second, the recent FDA approval of terlipressin, publication of the CONFIRM trial, and release of guidance statements mean that our findings may either represent temporary adoption patterns during a transition period or reflect an initial lag in implementing new recommendations—a distinction that can only be clarified through longitudinal assessment of practice patterns. On the other hand, the survey was performed almost 2 years after FDA approval, allowing time for prescribing experience. Third, as with any survey-based research, our findings may be subject to response bias, potentially limiting their generalizability. Regardless of these limitations, our data highlight the practice patterns of respondents and provide key opportunities to utilize “real-world” evidence to inform the design of future research addressing these knowledge gaps.

Our survey demonstrates significant variability in the management of HRS-AKI, which often differs from current guideline recommendations, particularly regarding the choice of first-line vasoconstrictor therapy, use of hemodynamic parameters to guide treatment, and timing of nephrology consultation. These findings highlight opportunities to improve guideline implementation, standardize care delivery, and identify areas where real-world practice patterns might inform future guidance updates. Further research is needed to understand the impact of these practice variations on patient outcomes and to develop more targeted strategies for optimizing HRS-AKI management across different practice settings.
