Outcomes After Acute Respiratory Failure in Patients With Interstitial Lung Disease
Authors: Rupali Sood, Haeun Lee, Matthew R. Lammi, Erica Farrand, Karthik Suresh, Michelle Sharp, David N. Hager, Sonye Danoff, Chad H. Hochberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterstitial lung diseases (ILDs) are a heterogeneous group of pulmonary disorders that can result in acute or chronic forms of respiratory failure. Prior studies have shown high mortality among patients with ILD receiving intensive care, but most have focused on idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and have not characterized other ILD subgroups.Research QuestionDoes 90-day transplant-free survival differ by ILD subgroup in patients with acute respiratory failure (ARF) requiring respiratory support?Study Design and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults with ILD admitted to 5 hospitals within our health system (2017-2024) using electronic record health data. We pragmatically defined ARF as receipt of ≥ 24 continuous hours of invasive or noninvasive respiratory support. We identified ILD diagnosis using validated International Classification of Diseases, version 10, Clinical Modification codes categorized into IPF/idiopathic interstitial pneumonia, connective tissue disease-associated ILD (CTD-ILD), exposure-related ILD, sarcoidosis, or other ILD. We evaluated 90-day transplant-free survival with multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for demographics, comorbidities, hospital type, and acute illness severity. Secondary outcomes included in-hospital death or hospice discharge and hospital length of stay.ResultsAmong 982 patients with ILD and ARF (median age, 70 years; 50% female), 269 (27%) were categorized as IPF/idiopathic interstitial pneumonia, 74 (8%) were categorized as CTD-ILD, 57 (6%) were categorized as exposure-related ILD, 171 (17%) were categorized as sarcoidosis, and 411 (42%) were categorized as other. By day 90, 466 patients (48%) had died or underwent transplant, with rates ranging from 42% in patients with CTD-ILD to 54% in exposure-related ILD. There were no significant differences in adjusted transplant-free survival or secondary outcomes overall, or when stratified by type of respiratory support.InterpretationIn this multihospital cohort of critically ill patients with ILD and ARF, 90-day transplant-free survival was poor and did not differ by ILD subgroup, even after adjustment for key confounders. These findings underscore the seriousness of ARF among patients with ILD.

Take-Home PointsStudy Question: Does 90-day transplant-free survival differ by interstitial lung disease (ILD) subgroup among patients admitted with acute respiratory failure requiring invasive or noninvasive respiratory support?Results: In this multihospital cohort of 982 critically ill patients with ILD and acute respiratory failure, 90-day transplant-free survival was poor (47% death or transplant) and did not differ significantly across ILD subgroups after adjustment for demographics, comorbidities, hospital type, and acute illness severity.Interpretation: Our results indicate that among patients with ILD who develop acute respiratory failure, outcomes are uniformly poor regardless of ILD subtype, suggesting that diagnostic category alone may not meaningfully inform short-term prognosis once critical illness occurs.

Take-Home Points

Study Question: Does 90-day transplant-free survival differ by interstitial lung disease (ILD) subgroup among patients admitted with acute respiratory failure requiring invasive or noninvasive respiratory support?

Results: In this multihospital cohort of 982 critically ill patients with ILD and acute respiratory failure, 90-day transplant-free survival was poor (47% death or transplant) and did not differ significantly across ILD subgroups after adjustment for demographics, comorbidities, hospital type, and acute illness severity.

Interpretation: Our results indicate that among patients with ILD who develop acute respiratory failure, outcomes are uniformly poor regardless of ILD subtype, suggesting that diagnostic category alone may not meaningfully inform short-term prognosis once critical illness occurs.

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a heterogeneous group of inflammatory and fibrosing lung disorders which can progress to chronic pulmonary impairment or exacerbate into acute respiratory failure (ARF). When ARF occurs, it can be from infection, be from medication toxicity, or reflect progression of the underlying disease, and may require ICU-level respiratory support.1, 2, 3 Although prior studies of patients with ILD-associated ARF receiving mechanical ventilation report mortality of 40% to 80%, these have predominantly been small studies focused on idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10

Although ILD subgroups have distinct natural histories and treatment paradigms, variability in outcomes in patients who develop ARF across these subgroups remain poorly defined. Most existing studies have been limited by small sample sizes, have been limited by restriction to few subgroups, or lack granular clinical detail about respiratory support and coexistent organ dysfunction.11,12 Where data do exist, they suggest outcomes differ across ILD subgroups. For example, mortality in patients with IPF receiving invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) has been reported to be as high as 85%, whereas patients with connective tissue disease-associated ILD (CTD-ILD) and pulmonary sarcoidosis, both of which may be modulated by immunosuppression, have been reported to have comparatively better outcomes.4,8,9 Furthermore, little is known about outcomes in patients receiving different types of respiratory support, namely IMV, noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV), or high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO).8,10,13, 14, 15, 16 Improving the understanding of how outcomes differ across ILD subgroups and respiratory support modalities could help clinicians more appropriately tailor clinical care and prognostic discussions.

To address these evidence gaps, we conducted a multihospital cohort study of patients with ILD and ARF supported with IMV, NIPPV, or HFNO and evaluated whether 90-day transplant-free survival (TFS) differed by ILD subgroup. We hypothesized that patients with IPF or non-IPF interstitial idiopathic pneumonia (IIP) would have worse TFS than other ILD subgroups.

Study Design and Methods

Study Design and Patient Cohort

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults (≥ 18 years of age) with ILD and ARF across 2 academic and 3 community hospitals in the Johns Hopkins Hospital System between July 1, 2017, and December 31, 2024. As our primary data source, we used an electronic health record registry of patients admitted to the ICU or intermediate care unit (IMC), which contains structured inpatient and outpatient data including detailed vital sign, flowsheet, laboratory, and mediation administration data and administrative data (eg, diagnostic and procedural codes for clinical documentation and billing).17

We included patients with ILD and ARF and excluded patients with a tracheostomy present within the first 24 hours of admission (to exclude those receiving chronic mechanical ventilation), and those who had received lung transplant (defined by International Classification of Disease, version 10, Clinical Modification [ICD-10-CM] and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes) (e-Appendix 1) before or within 5 days after respiratory failure episode. This study was approved by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board (No. IRB00411497), and the requirement for informed consent was waived for secondary use of data.

To identify patients with ILD, we adapted a previously validated algorithm using the ICD-10-CM codes from Farrand et al18 and defined the presence of ILD based on documentation of specific ICD-10 codes in inpatient and outpatient encounters within our health system (e-Appendix 1) before the start date or within 30 days after respiratory support was initiated. We evaluated the performance of 2 adapted versions of this algorithm in this critically ill cohort.18 The broad definition required the presence of an ILD ICD-10-CM code at any time before, during, or within 30 days after the respiratory failure admission. The narrow definition required documentation of an ILD ICD-10-CM code on > 2 occasions over the same time window. To assess the diagnostic performance of these definitions in the cohort, we performed manual chart review in a randomly selected subset of patients to confirm the presence of a clinical ILD diagnosis. Based on this validation, we selected the broad definition for all primary analyses.

We classified patients into mutually exclusive ILD subgroups using a recently validated claims-based approach (e-Appendix 2).19 Patients with diagnostic code groupings that could identify > 1 ILD subgroup had their primary ILD subgroup categorized according to the hierarchy used in the published algorithm, adapted to the addition of sarcoidosis in our study, and included the following: sarcoidosis > hypersensitivity pneumonitis > CTD-ILD > IPF/IIP > pneumoconiosis > other.19 For analytical purposes, hypersensitivity pneumonitis and pneumoconiosis were grouped into an exposure-related ILD subgroup.

We pragmatically defined ARF as receipt of IMV or noninvasive ventilation (NIV) (which included NIPPV and HFNO) for ≥ 24 continuous hours. To define continuous periods of NIV, we allowed for brief interruptions, provided that no single continuous break exceeded 2 hours within any 24-hour window. Medical records from outside institutions were not available or included in any data element definitions.

As covariates, we extracted demographic data (age, sex, race, and BMI) and Elixhauser comorbidity count.20 We used the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project classification of hospital admission diagnoses to define class of admission diagnosis into respiratory, circulatory, or infectious disease.21,22 We included prehospitalization data on use of corticosteroids, immunosuppressants (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate, methotrexate, and tacrolimus), antifibrotics (nintedanib and pirfenidone), and pulmonary hypertension medications (ambrisentan, bosentan, macitentan, epoprostenol, treprostinil, iloprost, riociguat, selexipag, sildenafil, tadalafil, and sotatercept), defined as a written prescription in the 6-month period before admission, and/or receipt of a home oxygen prescription in the year before admission.

We also extracted covariates characterizing acute illness, including type of respiratory support, modified nonrespiratory Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, lowest peripheral oxygen saturdation (Spo2)/Fio2 in the first 24 hours of ARF, and in-hospital receipt of therapeutics (corticosteroids, IV immunoglobulin, antibiotics, and other immunosuppressants). Modified nonrespiratory SOFA scores were defined as the maximum (worst) score in the first 24 hours after respiratory support start. We defined high-dose corticosteroid exposure as receipt of ≥ 500 mg methylprednisolone-equivalent on a single hospital day at any point during hospitalization, reflecting recommended doses for corticosteroid regimens for suspected exacerbations of ILD.23 Polymerase chain reaction tests for respiratory viruses were included if obtained within 14 days of ARF. In the subset of patients with available pulmonary function tests (PFTs) from the year before respiratory support, we extracted FEV1 and FVC using absolute values and % predicted based on Global Lung Initiative (2023) equations.24 e-Table 1 contains further detailed covariate definitions. For calculation of SOFA score, missing values for select nonrespiratory subcomponents (central nervous system, cardiovascular, liver, coagulation, and renal) were imputed as normal (score of 0).25 For all other covariates, we used complete-case analysis in adjusted models.

Outcomes and Statistical Analyses

Our primary outcome was 90-day TFS, defined as alive without lung transplantation at day 90 days after ARF. For patients discharged alive before day 90, we used a linkage to the National Death Index to determine vital status.26 We did not systematically assess for lung transplantation performed outside of our health system, which is 1 of 3 regional lung transplant centers. Secondary outcomes included in-hospital death or hospice discharge and hospital length of stay, with the occurrence of in-hospital death or hospice discharge penalized to the 99th percentile of the cohort length of stay to avoid bias in the setting of high incidence of mortality/hospice.27 Time zero for all time-based analyses was considered the start of respiratory support.

For analytical methods, we first described the cohort stratified by ILD subgroup using medians and interquartile ranges for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. We compared continuous variables across ILD subgroups using the Kruskal-Wallis test given nonnormal distributions, and for categorical variables we used the χ2 or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate.

To analyze the primary outcome of death or lung transplant by day 90 by ILD subgroup, we used the Kaplan-Meier method to construct survival curves and estimated univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to generate hazard ratios to describe the association between ILD subgroup and 90-day TFS. Covariates were prespecified and chosen based on a directed acyclic graph generated by the expert study team (e-Fig 1). These included age, sex, self-reported race, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, academic vs community hospital, nonrespiratory SOFA score, and lowest Spo2/Fio2 in first 24 hours of respiratory support receipt.

For secondary outcomes, we evaluated the association of ILD subgroup with hospice discharge outcome using logistic regression and continuous length of stay outcome with linear regression. In addition to the primary analysis, we performed a prespecified secondary analysis evaluating the association of ILD subgroups with outcomes stratified by receipt of IMV, NIV only, and with NIV further broken into NIPPV and HFNO.

To assess the robustness of our findings to alternate ILD subgroup classification approaches, we performed several sensitivity analyses, including (1) expanding the CTD-ILD group to include myositis-specific autoantibodies, (2) expanding the CTD-ILD group by including antibody-positive serologies meeting European Respiratory Society/American Thoracic Society criteria for interstitial pneumonia with autoimmune features (e-Appendix 3), and (3) restricting the prior IPF/IIP cohort to only strict IPF ICD-10 codes only.28 Additional sensitivity analyses included restricting the entire cohort to patients meeting narrow ILD criteria (meeting ILD criteria on ≥ 2 occasions),18 adjusting for prehospital ILD therapies (corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, pulmonary hypertension drugs, antifibrotics, and home oxygen) with inclusion of preadmission FVC (%) in a separate model. The models including prehospitalization medications and baseline PFT results were considered as sensitivity and not primary because there was likely informative missingness for patients not receiving outpatient care within our health system. Finally, we also included sensitivity analyses, adding calendar year to account for temporal trends in management and outcomes—including across the COVID-19 pandemic—and adding ICU vs IMC location to the primary model as an additional adjustor.

We performed data extraction and preprocessing using R version 4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and conducted statistical analyses in Stata version 18 (StataCorp). A 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Study reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology criteria (e-Table 2).

Results

After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 982 patients were included (Fig 1). To assess diagnostic performance of the ILD identification algorithm using ICD-10 codes, we randomly selected a subset of patients for manual chart review (n = 287; 29% of analytical cohort). Among patients in this validation sample who met the broad definition, 77% had a clinically confirmed diagnosis of ILD, representing the positive predictive value of the broad algorithm, and among the patients who met the narrow definition (n = 244), 84% had confirmed ILD. Given the similar diagnostic performance of the broad and narrow definitions, and the greater inclusiveness of the broad definition, we used the broad definition for all primary analyses. Of the 982 patients meeting the broad definition for ILD, ILD subgroups were classified as IPF/IIP (n = 269; 27%), CTD-ILD (n = 74; 8%), exposure-related ILD (n = 57; 6%), sarcoidosis (n = 171; 17%), and other ILD (n = 411; 42%). The other ILD group included diagnostic codes for organizing pneumonia, drug-induced ILD, and unclassifiable fibrotic ILD. Detailed ICD-10-CM codes within each group are summarized in e-Table 3.

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Patients with IPF/IIP were older (median age, 74 years; interquartile range, 65-81), whereas those with sarcoidosis were younger (median age, 63 years; interquartile range, 55-73). Female sex was most common in CTD-ILD (69%). White race predominated in exposure-related ILD (79%) and IPF/IIP (65%). Median comorbidity counts were similar across groups (range, 5-6). Among patients with baseline PFTs (n = 404), median FVC % predicted was 63% and did not significantly vary by subgroup. Pre-ARF antifibrotic prescriptions were largely confined to IPF/IIP (8%), whereas immunosuppressant prescriptions were most frequent in CTD-ILD (35%). Acute illness features also differed across subgroups. Nonrespiratory SOFA scores varied significantly by subtype, with higher median scores observed in CTD-ILD, exposure-related ILD, and sarcoidosis compared with IPF/IIP and other ILD (Table 1); missingness for imputed SOFA components was < 5%. IMV was administered in the following groups: 30% IIP/IPF, 47% CTD-ILD, 58% exposure-related ILD, 67% sarcoidosis, and 42% of other ILD. Respiratory viral infections were most frequent in CTD-ILD (70%) and least frequent in sarcoidosis (37%), whereas SARS-CoV-2 positivity was uncommon (≤ 9% across groups). Median Spo2/Fio2 ratios ranged from 125 to 147, with the lowest values in IPF/IIP and CTD-ILD. Inpatient corticosteroid therapy was widespread, with ≥ 70% of patients in all subgroups receiving corticosteroids; IV immunoglobulin was used predominantly in CTD-ILD (15%).

By day 90, 465 patients (47%) had died and 9 (1%) had undergone lung transplant. Subgroup incidence of death or transplant by day 90 was as follows: IPF/IIP = 50%, CTD-ILD = 42%, exposure-related ILD = 54%, sarcoidosis = 42%, and other ILD = 48% (P = .28 for cross-group comparison) (Table 2). Death or lung transplant by day 90 among patients managed with IMV ranged from 40% in CTD-ILD to 58% in IIP/IPF and exposure-related ILD. Among those treated with NIV, 34% to 50% across ILD subgroups died or were transplanted by day 90. In-hospital death occurred in 35% to 42% across subgroups, and discharge to hospice among survivors ranged from 4% in sarcoidosis to 15% in IPF/IIP. In analyses assessing the robustness of our findings across different ILD subgrouping classifications, the results were consistent when adjusting subgroup classifications by adding myositis antibody serologies to the CTD-ILD definition or adding interstitial pneumonia with autoimmune features serologic criteria to the CTD-ILD categorization, and when restricting the IPF/IIP group to IPF only ICD-10 codes (e-Fig 2, e-Table 4). Models adjusting for prehospital ILD therapies (immunosuppressants, corticosteroids, antifibrotics, pulmonary hypertension drugs, and home oxygen) in addition to including FVC (n = 404), restricting to patients meeting narrow ILD criteria (n = 769), or including calendar year of respiratory failure onset also produced similar results (e-Fig 3, e-Table 5). Similarly, inclusion of admission location (ICU vs IMC) in the primary model did not materially alter the results.

Discussion

In this multihospital, retrospective cohort of 982 patients with ILD and ARF requiring invasive or noninvasive respiratory support, we found that 90-day TFS was poor across all ILD subgroups and did not differ significantly after adjusting for prespecified confounders. The absence of subgroup-specific differences was consistent across those receiving noninvasive and invasive respiratory support. These findings emphasize the poor prognosis of ILD-associated ARF and the need for improved preventative strategies and ICU management approaches for this high-risk population.

This study adds to the existing literature by providing contemporary data on ILD outcomes in ARF across a group with diverse etiologies of ILD, as classified using a validated ICD-10-CM algorithm.3,5,7,10 The overall 90-day mortality rate (46%) aligns with prior meta-analyses of in-hospital mortality in mixed ILD cohorts, where about one-half had IPF.12 Notably, poor outcomes extended across all ILD subgroups, and generalize to patients supported with either IMV or NIV, reflecting the vulnerability of patients with ILD once ARF develops.4,6,12,15,16,29 Although CTD-ILD has been hypothesized to confer more favorable outcomes based on presumed connective tissue disease-related ILD biology and immunosuppression responsiveness, we found no associated survival advantage compared with IPF/IIP. This contrasts with prior studies reporting improved outcomes in CTD-ILD, which may reflect differences in cohort composition, illness severity at presentation, or variability in CTD-ILD definitions across studies.4,30 The similarly poor outcomes observed between IPF/IIP and CTD-ILD in this cohort suggest that during acute decompensation, potentially targetable autoimmune mechanisms may not mitigate the effects of critical illness. Collectively, these suggest that ICU decisions regarding escalation therapies should heavily weigh acute illness severity, prognosis, and patient goals rather than ILD subtype.

The high proportion of in-hospital deaths and or transitions to hospice transitions among survivors underscores the limited window for meaningful recovery after ARF admission. Early recognition of a trajectory or process that could yield ARF should prompt structured discussions regarding prognosis, escalation preferences, and postdischarge expectations. Integration of palliative care consultation during critical illness may help ensure care aligns with patient values and supports transitions to comfort-focused management when appropriate.31 The lower mortality observed at academic centers may reflect differences in patient selection, ICU resources, or access to subspecialty ILD and transplant expertise.

Our findings suggest that ILD subgroup classifications alone are not associated with significant differences in what are overall poor outcomes in this patient population; however, we may have been underpowered to detect some smaller but important differences. Future work that identifies important biologic differences across acute exacerbations and in those with rapid progression of underlying disease could help define clinically and prognostically distinct phenotypes of ILD-associated ARF. As a complement to such work, prospective clinical cohorts with standardized clinical adjudication and longitudinal pulmonary function data may further clarify prognostic differences and inform individualized ICU management strategies.

This study has limitations. The retrospective design limits causal inference, and despite use of a validated classification algorithm, misclassification of ILD subgroups remains possible.19 We were also unable to reliably determine whether ILD-associated ARF reflected newly diagnosed ILD, acute exacerbation of existing disease, or diffuse alveolar damage in patients without prior ILD, which may have contributed to diagnostic heterogeneity within subgroups. Furthermore, although we included data on use of pulmonary hypertension medications, we did not perform structured review of echocardiogram or right-sided heart data. Additionally, although CTD-ILD was identified using validated codes for CTD with pulmonary involvement, we were unable to reliably subclassify patients by specific diagnosis. Furthermore, although linkage to national death data reduced outcome missingness, transplants outside our system may have been missed. Finally, we did not adjudicate cause-specific death, and infection, sepsis, or cardiovascular complications may have contributed to mortality alongside ARF. Nonetheless, the large sample size, multihospital design, and inclusion of detailed clinical covariates strengthen the robustness and generalizability of these findings.

Interpretation

Outcomes in patients with ILD requiring respiratory support were poor regardless of underlying ILD subgroup classification. These findings suggest that once ARF occurs, prognosis is primarily driven by acute illness severity rather than ILD etiology. ICU decisions should emphasize the patient’s overall condition, clinical trajectory, and goals of care rather than diagnostic category. Recognizing this trajectory early may help guide escalation decisions and ensure care aligns with patient priorities. Future studies should focus on improving critical care management and identifying modifiable factors that influence outcomes across ILD subtypes.
