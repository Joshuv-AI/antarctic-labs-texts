Primary Dural-based diffuse large B-cell lymphoma mimicking meningioma in the olfactory groove: An unique case report and brief review of the literature
Authors: Mehmet Tiryaki, Halit Alioglu, Mert Yavuz, Omar Alomari, Beyzanur Guney, Nagehan Ozdemir Barisik, Bülent Güçlü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary central nervous system lymphomas (PCNLs) are malignant non-Hodgkin lymphomas solely affecting the central nervous system (CNS). Primary dural lymphoma, which is rare in the CNS, has not been previously reported in a study of the olfactory groove. We present the clinical course of a 77-year-old immunocompetent male who presented with nonspecific symptoms of fatigue and transient memory loss. Although the patient's neurological examination was unremarkable, cranial imaging identified a lesion highly suspicious for an olfactory groove meningioma. Consequently, the patient underwent a bifrontal craniotomy; however, intraoperative exploration proved incongruent with imaging as no distinct mass was identified. While the immediate postoperative recovery was uneventful, the patient experienced significant clinical deterioration 1 month later, necessitating admission to the intensive care unit. Subsequent histopathological analysis of the biopsy specimen provided the definitive diagnosis of primary dural-based diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), and comprehensive staging with FDG-PET and contrast-enhanced CT confirmed the disease was confined strictly to the CNS. This case underscores the importance of considering primary dural lymphoma in the differential diagnosis of anterior skull base lesions even when imaging mimics meningioma, and highlights the essential role of histological confirmation in navigating such diagnostic complexities.

Introduction

Meningiomas are the most commonly occurring tumors located on the anterior cranial fossa of the skull base. Olfactory groove meningioma (OGM) constitutes 4.5% to 18% of all intracranial meningiomas, originating from arachnoid cells of the lamina cribrosa and frontosphenoidal suture, progressively compressing both frontal lobes. Despite being histologically benign tumors, OGMs often infiltrate the bone and stimulate an osteoblastic response at the implantation site [1].

On radiographic evaluation, a variety of tumorous lesions can mimic to be meningiomas [2]. Primary dural-based lymphomas are predominantly classified as marginal zone lymphomas, exhibiting a similar immunophenotype to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) which is a rare occurrence in the CNS [3]. Dural-based lymphomas that imitate meningiomas have been rarely reported [4]. The publication of this case is justified by the profound diagnostic challenge it represents; misidentifying a high-grade lymphoma as a benign meningioma can lead to radical surgical interventions rather than the appropriate chemotherapy-based management. Herein, we present a unique case of dural large B-cell lymphoma mimicking olfactory groove meningioma. This report is structured in accordance with the Case Reports (CARE) guideline criteria [5] to ensure clarity, transparency, and educational value.

Case presentation

A 77-year-old male with a medical history of hypertension presented with a 1-week history of fatigue and transient memory loss. He was immunocompetent, was not receiving any regular medications, and had no prior history of surgical interventions. There was no known family history of neurological or malignancies. The patient denied any loss of consciousness, seizures, anosmia, subjective weakness, or fever.

On admission, the physical examination was unremarkable; the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 15, and the Karnofsky Performance Status score was 90. Cerebellar functions were intact, and cranial nerve examinations revealed no deficits. Admission laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis with a white blood cell count of 20.2\times 10^3/µL (reference range: 4.5-11.0\times10^3/µL). The differential count showed neutrophilia (81.1%; absolute count 16.37\times 10^3/µL) and increased immature granulocytes (1.3\times10^9/L). Other hematologic parameters were within normal limits: hemoglobin 15.9 g/dL (13.5-17.5 g/dL), hematocrit 47.2% (41%-50%), platelet count 300\times 10^3/µL (150-400\times 10^3/µL), and mean platelet volume 10.1fL. Coagulation profiles were normal (INR 0.9). Biochemical analysis showed serum glucose of 124 mg/dL, creatinine 1.14 mg/dL (0.7-1.3 mg/dL), and urea 59 mg/dL. Liver function tests, serum electrolytes (sodium 138 mmol/L, calcium 8.34 mg/dL), and thyroid function (TSH 1.09 mIU/L) were all within normal ranges. Serological markers for Hepatitis B (HBsAg), HIV, and Hepatitis C were negative.

Cranial imaging performed on the day of presentation revealed a significant space-occupying lesion in the anterior skull base. The noncontrast CT showed a 4.2 × 3.6 cm (mediolateral × anteroposterior) isodense mass with areas of internal hypodensity (Fig. 1). On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), the lesion appeared isointense on T1-weighted sequences and heterogeneously intense on T2-weighted sequences. FLAIR imaging highlighted prominent perilesional vasogenic edema extending into the adjacent white matter. Significant mass effect was observed, characterized by the effacement of adjacent sulci, compression of the right lateral ventricle, and a minimal midline shift to the left. Notably, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps revealed prominent diffusion restriction with low ADC values, indicative of a hypercellular tumor. Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging demonstrated a large, irregularly shaped mass measuring 4.2 × 3.6 × 3 cm (anteroposterior × mediolateral × craniocaudal) with heterogeneous, predominantly peripheral enhancement. Given the severe peritumoral edema, antiedema therapy with dexamethasone (4 mg qid) was initiated. Although initially hesitant, the patient consented to surgery 5 days later. A bifrontal craniotomy was performed. Intraoperatively, findings were incongruent with the radiological impression of a well-circumscribed meningioma. The lesion appeared hypervascular with a soft, friable texture, distinct from the firm, rubbery consistency typical of meningiomas. Unlike a meningioma, it lacked a distinct dural attachment and exhibited ill-defined margins, merging with the adjacent frontal lobe parenchyma without a clear arachnoid plane of dissection. Given the ambiguous intraoperative appearance and diffuse nature of the lesion, frozen section analysis was not performed; instead, multiple biopsy samples were obtained for definitive histopathological assessment to guide subsequent management.

Postoperatively, the patient was monitored in the intensive care unit (ICU). A brain MRI on postoperative day 1 demonstrated residual tumor (Fig. 2). The patient was discharged to the ward and subsequently home in stable condition. However, 1 month later, he was readmitted due to general clinical deterioration and impaired orientation (GCS 14). Histopathological examination of the biopsy specimen established a diagnosis of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), contradicting the initial radiological impression.

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the tumor cells were positive for CD20, BCL-6, and MUM1, while testing negative for CD3, CD5, CD10, cyclin D1, cytokeratin (AE1/AE3), and EMA (Fig. 3). This immunoprofile effectively excluded meningioma and other lymphoid neoplasms. The Ki-67 proliferation index was markedly elevated (85%-90%), indicating high aggressive potential. Epstein–Barr virus–encoded RNA (EBER) in situ hybridization was negative (Fig. 3). Staging was performed using 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) in accordance with standard protocols. Following a 6-hour fasting period and confirmation of euglycemia, the patient received an intravenous injection of [0.1 mCi/kg] of 18F-FDG. Whole-body images were acquired 60 minutes postinjection. The scan revealed a hypermetabolic mass lesion in the olfactory groove with intense focal uptake (SUVmax: 24.5). No abnormal metabolic activity was observed in the cervical, axillary, mediastinal, or inguinal lymph nodes, nor in the liver, spleen, or bone marrow. Additionally, contrast-enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was negative for other lesions. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of primary dural-based DLBCL.

The patient was referred to oncology but declined adjuvant chemotherapy. He was managed with close clinical and radiological surveillance. During an 8-month follow-up period, he remained clinically stable with no neurological decline. The patient ultimately passed away due to an acute myocardial infarction unrelated to the tumor.

Discussion

This case illustrates an exceptional presentation of primary dural-based diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) mimicking an olfactory groove meningioma (OGM). While primary CNS lymphomas (PCNSL) typically present as intraparenchymal masses, primary dural lymphomas (PDL) are a rare subclass, accounting for less than 1% of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas and approximately 2% of intracranial tumors [6]. The distinction between dural lymphoma and meningioma is fraught with diagnostic pitfalls. As observed in our patient, dural lymphomas can radiologically mimic meningiomas by exhibiting homogeneous enhancement, dural attachment, and even the ``dural tail'' sign [15]. The dural tail, traditionally considered pathognomonic for meningioma, reflects reactive dural thickening and hypervascularity rather than tumor invasion alone, and has been documented in rare cases of dural lymphoma [12,15]. In our review of similar cases (Table 1), the majority of dural DLBCLs were located over the cerebral convexities (parietal/frontal lobes) or the falx [[6], [8], [10], [12]]. The location in the anterior skull base (olfactory groove) seen in our case contributes to the diagnostic complexity, as tumors in this region are overwhelmingly assumed to be meningiomas.

A critical lesson from this case involves the impact of corticosteroids on diagnostic accuracy. Our patient received high-dose dexamethasone for 5 days prior to surgery to manage peritumoral edema. The subsequent intraoperative finding of a ``vanishing'' or indistinct mass likely represents the ``ghost tumor'' phenomenon, a rapid oncolytic response to steroids well-documented in CNS lymphomas [16]. This response, while therapeutically beneficial, obscured the tumor margins and complicated surgical resection. Consequently, in cases where imaging features are atypical, such as the marked diffusion restriction and severe edema seen in our case, clinicians should consider deferring steroids until after a biopsy is secured, or utilizing metabolic imaging like FDG-PET, which can differentiate the high metabolic activity of DLBCL (SUVmax >15) from the lower uptake typical of meningiomas (SUVmax ∼3-5).

Histologically, while most primary dural lymphomas are low-grade marginal zone lymphomas with a favorable prognosis, our patient presented with DLBCL, a high-grade and aggressive subtype [17]. This aligns with the demographic trends noted by Yamada et al. [18], who reported a male predominance and aggressive course in dural DLBCL variants. Although the pathogenesis remains unclear given the dura's lack of native lymphoid tissue, hypotheses include transformation from chronic inflammation or systemic seeding that remains isolated to the CNS.

Finally, the clinical implications of misdiagnosis are profound. The standard of care for meningioma is maximal surgical resection, whereas CNS lymphoma is primarily treated with chemoradiotherapy (eg, high-dose methotrexate-based regimens) [19]. Radical resection in lymphoma offers no survival benefit and increases neurological morbidity [2]. In our case, the initial misdiagnosis led to a craniotomy rather than a stereotactic biopsy. Although the patient declined subsequent chemotherapy, the rapid progression serves as a stark reminder of the aggressive nature of this disease.

A key strength of this report is its novelty; to our knowledge, it is among the very few cases of primary dural DLBCL localized to the olfactory groove. It also underscores the diverse radiological behavior of CNS lymphomas and the importance of considering them even in immunocompetent patients. However, there are limitations, including the retrospective and observational nature of the report, the inability to generalize findings, and the lack of outcome data due to the patient’s refusal of systemic therapy.

Conclusion

Primary dural DLBCL of the olfactory groove is a distinct diagnostic entity that mimics meningioma. This case underscores the necessity of including high-grade lymphoma in the differential diagnosis of anterior skull base lesions, particularly when extensive edema and diffusion restriction are present. The ``ghost tumor'' effect of corticosteroids must be anticipated. We advocate for early histopathological confirmation via frozen section or stereotactic biopsy in ambiguous cases to avoid unnecessary radical craniotomies and to facilitate prompt initiation of appropriate chemotherapeutic management.
