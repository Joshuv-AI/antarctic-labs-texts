Engineering Escherichia coli for the S-selective production of 2-hydroxyisovalerate
Authors: Peiling Wu, Haofeng Chen, Guojun Yang, Haoyu Qin, Qian Zhang, Yuxin Chen, Dongjiang Lin, Yang Zhang, Shuyong Lin, Shizhen Wang, Jifeng Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
2-Hydroxyisovalerate (2-HIV) is a value-added chemical that is widely applied in the synthesis of bioactive compounds and polymers. Here, we report an underexplored metabolic route for the de novo production of S-type 2-HIV (S-HIV) in Escherichia coli. In particular, we identified promiscuous activity of 4-hydroxymandelate synthase (HmaS) from Amycolatopsis orientalis towards the conversion of 2-keto-4-methyl-pentanoate (2-KMP, an immediate precursor for L-leucine) to S-HIV. Next, we designed a variant HmaS (S201F) with abolished activity for mandelate and 4-hydroxymandelate synthesis, thereby minimizing byproduct formation. Coupled with systematic optimization of the L-leucine biosynthetic pathway, we achieved de novo production of S-HIV at 8.1 ​mM (0.95 ​g/L) in shake flasks and 33.9 ​mM (4.0 ​g/L) in 2-L fed-batch fermentation. In summary, this work represents the first time to realize the efficient synthesis of S-configuration 2-HIV in metabolically engineered E. coli.

Introduction

α-Hydroxy acids (α-HAs) are important building blocks widely used in chemical synthesis, cosmetics, and biomedical manufacturing [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Among the various α-hydroxy acids, 2-HIV has attracted increasing interest because of its application potential in the pharmaceutical, chemical, and polymer industries [[5], [6], [7]]. As a naturally occurring metabolite, 2-HIV has been utilized as a biomarker for disease diagnosis and as a probe in the analysis of cellular signalling pathways [[8], [9], [10]]. In addition, it has been extensively explored as a monomeric building block for the synthesis of biobased polyesters [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. As a structural unit, 2-HIV enables the production of biopolymers with tunable thermal stability, mechanical strength, and degradation profiles, offering promising avenues for the development of sustainable materials. For example, Ann-Christine Albertsson et al. developed a polymerization process for oligo (lactic acid) and 2-HIV-containing oligomers, which improved their thermal stability and biodegradability [14]. Moreover, E. coli has been metabolically engineered to produce novel PHA copolymers containing 2-HIV. For instance, Choi SY et al. constructed an E. coli system capable of synthesizing poly(D-lactate-co-3-hydroxybutyrate-co-2-hydroxyisovalerate) [5], whereas Yang et al. developed an E. coli platform for producing poly(2-hydroxyisovalerate-co-lactate), thereby expanding the structural diversity and application potential of microbial polyesters [6].

Considering the limited amount of available fossil reserves, the development of green technologies that convert renewable feedstocks into value-added chemicals is highly important. Biosynthetic methods that use microbial cell factories to synthesize chemicals have attracted widespread attention [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Several metabolic engineering strategies have been developed to achieve efficient and sustainable synthesis of 2-HIV from glucose [6,[20], [21], [22]] (Supplementary Table S1). To date, most biosynthetic efforts have focused on the production of the R-enantiomer of 2-HIV (R-HIV) [20,21], which is synthesized via the stereoselective reduction of 2-ketoisovalerate by D-2-hydroxyacid dehydrogenase (PanE), in combination with the native L-valine pathway (Fig. 1a) [[23], [24], [25]]. Given that many biological systems exhibit strict enantioselectivity, establishing stereochemically defined synthetic routes is essential for improving the functional properties of chiral materials and enhancing the precision of biomolecular recognition in diagnostic and therapeutic applications. Despite this, no established biosynthetic route currently exists for the S-enantiomer of 2-HIV (S-HIV) [22]. To address the current lack of an efficient biosynthetic route for S-HIV, we developed a microbial platform for its de novo production in E. coli. Drawing inspiration from natural hydroxylation and decarboxylation chemistry (Fig. 1b), we determined that HmaS from Amycolatopsis orientalis can catalyse the transformation of 2-KMP into S-HIV (Fig. 1b). To increase product specificity and eliminate undesired side reactions, we rationally engineered the variant HmaSS201F, which effectively abolished the formation of the byproducts (S)-mandelate and (S)-4-hydroxymandelate (S-MA and S-HMA, respectively). Through systematic optimization of the native L-leucine biosynthetic pathway, we achieved a final S-HIV titre of 8.1 ​mM (0.95 ​g/L) in shake-flask cultures—representing an 8-fold improvement over the initial strain. Scaling up to a 2-L fed-batch fermentation further enhanced S-HIV production, achieving 33.9 ​mM (4.0 ​g/L) S-HIV from glycerol (Fig. 1c). These results highlight the first biosynthetic route for S-HIV in E. coli, providing a sustainable alternative to chemical synthesis. Moreover, the discovery of the decarboxylation–hydroxylation activity of HmaS towards aliphatic α-ketoacids reveals previously underexplored enzymatic potential. Given the structural diversity of α-keto acid intermediates in branched-chain amino acid metabolism, HmaS and its engineered variants may serve as versatile biocatalysts for the production of a broad spectrum of chiral aliphatic hydroxy acids from renewable feedstocks.

Materials and methods

General

E. coli DH5α was used for plasmid construction. E. coli MG1655 (DE3) was used for protein expression and fermentation. All engineered strains were constructed by standardized electroporation. Routine plasmid construction, strain construction, and seed liquid culture were performed using lysogeny broth (LB) medium (10 ​g/L tryptone, 5 ​g/L yeast extract, and 10 ​g/L NaCl) supplemented with the corresponding antibiotics. The antibiotics used were ampicillin (100 ​μg/mL), kanamycin (50 ​μg/mL), and chloramphenicol (34 ​μg/mL). All molecular cloning-related reagents, such as T4 ligase and restriction endonucleases, were purchased from New England Biolabs (Ipswich, USA). Authentic chemical standards were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). The primers were synthesized and ordered from Sangon (Shanghai, China).

Plasmids and strains

The acetolactate synthase gene AlsS was amplified from the genomic DNA of Bacillus subtilis 168 26. The 4-hydroxymandelate synthase gene HmaS from A. orientalis was cloned from previously reported plasmids [27,28]. To construct pET-HmaSS201F, the S201F mutation was introduced by overlapping PCR. The ketol-acid reductoisomerase gene IlvC and dihydroxy-acid dehydratase gene IlvD were amplified from the genomic DNA of E. coli [29], the L-leucine synthetic cluster gene LeuABCD was amplified from the genomic DNA of E. coli, and overlapping PCR was used to construct the variant LeuA∗ (G462D) [29]. All plasmids were constructed using standard restriction enzyme digestion and ligation-based molecular cloning techniques. DNA fragments were amplified by PCR and ligated into appropriate vectors using T4 DNA ligase, following the manufacturer's protocols. The resulting recombinant plasmids were verified by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing. The plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Recombinant plasmids were introduced into E. coli MG1655 (DE3) strains via standard electroporation. All the strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Enzyme activity characterization by biocatalysis

The recombinant strains EcT7-LAAD, EcT7-HmaS, and EcT7-HmaSS201F were cultured overnight in LB-liquid medium supplemented with ampicillin (100 ​μg/mL) to prepare the seed solution. The seed solution was inoculated into 100 ​mL of terrific broth (TB) medium (24 ​g/L yeast extract, 12 ​g/L tryptone, 0.4 ​% glycerol, 0.017 ​M KH2PO4, 0.072 ​M ​K2HPO4) at 1 ​% inoculum. Strains were grown at 37 ​°C and 250 ​rpm to an optical density (OD600) of 0.8–1.0. Afterwards, isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to the final concentration of 1 ​mM, and the cell cultures were transferred to 20 ​°C and 250 ​rpm conditions for induced expression of proteins under the T7 promoter for 16–18 ​h. The whole-cell biocatalysts were prepared according to our previous work [26,30]. Biocatalysis was carried out at 30 ​°C and 250 ​rpm for 24 ​h with a 1 ​mL system containing 20 ​g/L D-glucose, 10 ​g cell dry weight (CDW)/L biocatalysts, substrates, and phosphate buffer (100 ​mM, pH 7.0) for the enzyme cascade system of LAAD with HmaS containing 2 ​mM L-leucine or 5 ​mM L-phenylalanine, 10 ​g CDW/L EcT7-LAAD, 10 ​g CDW/L EcT7-HmaS, or 10 ​g CDW/L EcT7-HmaS∗. For the negative control, no substrate was added, and the volume was filled to 1 ​mL with phosphate buffer. After incubation at 30 ​°C for 24 ​h, 250 ​μL of the reaction sample was centrifuged at 14,000 ​rpm for 12 ​min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 ​μm filter membrane and then analysed by HPLC equipped with refractive index detection (RID).

Fermentative production of 2-HIV

Single colonies were inoculated from solid agar plates into 2 ​mL of LB medium supplemented with the corresponding antibiotics and cultivated at 37 ​°C and 250 ​rpm overnight to prepare the seed culture. One percent fresh overnight seed cultures were inoculated into 20 ​mL of modified M9 medium supplemented with 40 ​g/L glycerol and appropriate antibiotics. The cell cultures were incubated at 37 ​°C and 250 ​rpm for 4 ​h. IPTG was added to the media to a final concentration of 0.1 ​mM to induce gene expression, and the cell cultures were shifted to 30 ​°C and 250 ​rpm for 2-HIV production. Samples were periodically taken to monitor cell growth and the concentrations of glycerol, 2-HIV, and their byproducts. The fed-batch scale was conducted in a 2 ​L-scale bioreactor at a temperature of 30 ​°C. The pH was maintained at 6.8 using 1 ​M NaOH, and the dissolved oxygen concentration was maintained between 10 and 30 ​% using a responsive stirring protocol from 200 to 800 ​rpm.

Analytical methods

HPLC analysis was performed in a Shimadzu Prominence LC-20AD Liquid Chromatograph system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Glycerol, 2-keto-4-methyl-pentanoate, and S-HIV were quantified by HPLC equipped with a RID-20A detector and an Aminex HPX-87H column (300 ​× ​7.8 ​mm) (Bio-Rad, USA). The column temperature was maintained at 50 ​°C. The mobile phase was a 5 ​mM ​H2SO4 solution with a flow rate of 0.8 ​mL/min. S-MA and S-HMA were quantified by HPLC with a Shimadzu SPD-20A UV detector and C18 column (180 ​× ​4.6 ​mm ​× ​5 ​μm). The column temperature was maintained at 40 ​°C. Ultrapure water (95 ​%) and acetonitrile (5 ​%) were used as the mobile phase, and the flow rate was set at 1.0 ​mL/min.

The identity of 2-HIV was further confirmed by LC‒MS/MS on an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC connected to a Q-Exactive mass detector. The system was equipped with a SunFire C18 analytical column (2.1 ​× ​100 ​mm, 5 ​μm). The flow rate was 0.3 ​mL/min with 0.2 ​% formate in ultrapure water (B). The gradient started at 5 ​% B for 0.3 ​min and followed a linear gradient to 80 ​% B for 7 ​min, after which it was changed to 5 ​% B and held for 1 ​min. The MS equipped with a heated electrospray ionization source was set in negative-ion mode with the sheath gas set to 50 a.u. (arbitrary units), auxiliary gas to 10 a.u., and sweep gas to 1 a.u. The cone and probe temperatures were maintained at 325 and 380 ​°C, respectively, and the spray voltage was set at 3500 ​V. The scan range was 80–500 ​Da, and the time between scans was 50 ​ms.

Chirality identification

The enantiomeric configuration of the 2-HIV product was confirmed by chiral gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), as previously reported [31]. (S)- and (R)-2-HIV were analysed as their methyl esters. The methylation process was as follows: (1) samples were extracted by ether three times after being acidized by hydrochloric acid (the final concentration was 0.4 ​M); (2) the ether extracts were combined and dried under a nitrogen flow and then resuspended in a methanol: ether mixture (2:7); (3) the dissolved metabolites were precooled in methanol ether mix (90 ​μL) and added with trimethylsilyl-diazomethane (10 ​μL; T140538; Aladdin Inc.) and incubated at room temperature for 3 ​h. 2-HIV methyl esters were measured by using a CP-Chirasil-Dex CB (25 ​m ​× ​0.25 ​mm ​× ​0.25 ​μm) column with the following temperature program: 80 ​°C for 2 ​min, 2 ​°C min−1 to 114 ​°C, 10 ​°C min−1 to 194 ​°C and maintained for 3 ​min, a split ratio of 10:1, and an injection volume of 1 ​μL. GC‒MS was performed by an Agilent Technologies 7890B GC and 5977B MSD system.

Results and discussion

Biocatalytic approach reveals a novel activity of HmaS for S-HIV synthesis

HmaS from A. orientalis is a known Fe(II)-dependent dioxygenase [[32], [33], [34], [35]] that catalyses the hydroxylation and decarboxylation of aromatic ketoacids such as 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate (4-HPP) and phenylpyruvate (PPY), yielding S-HMA [36] and S-MA [[37], [38], [39]]. This enzyme has also been used to produce 4-hydroxyphenylglycine, a nonproteinogenic residue found in glycopeptide antibiotics such as vancomycin [40,41]. Mechanistically, the ketoacid moiety of 4-HPP coordinates with the active-site Fe(II), facilitating dioxygen activation and a cascade of reactions, including decarboxylation, hydrogen abstraction, and benzylic hydroxylation [34,35,42,43] (Supplementary Fig. S1). On the basis of the understanding that the reactive functional group recognized by HmaS is the ketoacid, we hypothesized that the catalytic scope of HmaS could extend to aliphatic ketoacids, such as 2-KMP, an intermediate in L-leucine biosynthesis. Given the structural similarity between 2-KMP and 4-HPP, we speculated that HmaS may catalyse the transformation of 2-KMP into S-HIV (Fig. 1b). If so, the de novo synthesis of S-HIV could be achieved by coupling HmaS activity with the endogenous L-leucine biosynthetic pathway (Fig. 1c).

To test this hypothesis, we constructed a biocatalytic cascade comprising L-amino acid deaminase (LAAD) from Proteus mirabilis and HmaS (Fig. 2a). In this system, PmLAAD deaminates L-leucine to generate 2-KMP, which is subsequently decarboxylated and hydroxylated by HmaS. Sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) confirmed the expression of both LAAD and HmaS: LAAD, a membrane-bound flavoprotein, exhibited weak but detectable expression, which was consistent with our previous study [44]; HmaS showed a prominent band at approximately 35–40 ​kDa (Fig. 2b). During whole-cell biocatalysis using 2 ​mM L-leucine at 30 ​°C for 24 ​h, LC‒MS/MS revealed that the product was identical to the authentic 2-HIV standard, with characteristic ion fragments at m/z 117.0538 and 71.0484 (Fig. 2c). These findings confirmed that HmaS could transform aliphatic 2-KMP to the corresponding 2-HIV. Chiral analysis further confirmed that the resulting product obtained from HmaS catalysis was the S-form of 2-HIV (Supplementary Fig. S2). Engineering HmaS with minimal byproduct formation

Wild-type HmaS is known to exhibit high catalytic activity towards aromatic ketoacids 4-HPP and PPY. A key determinant of substrate binding in HmaS is the serine residue at position 201 32,33. Previously, Eckhard Boles et al. demonstrated that the substitution of S201 with L-valine (S201V) reduced the affinity of HmaS for 4-HPP while increasing S-MA production [37]. To test this hypothesis, we constructed the S201F mutant and verified its soluble expression in E. coli. As shown by SDS‒PAGE analysis, the expression level of the mutant HmaS (S201F) was comparable to that of the wild-type HmaS, with a prominent band at 35–40 ​kDa (Fig. 3b). Functional verification using the PmLAAD-HmaS biocatalytic system revealed that wild-type HmaS produced S-MA and PPY from 5 ​mM L-phenylalanine. In contrast, the enzyme cascade comprising PmLAAD and HmaS (S201F) abolished the formation of S-MA, indicating successful repurposed substrate specificity (Fig. 3c).

To confirm that the engineered HmaS (S201F) retained the ability to convert 2-KMP into S-HIV, we conducted molecular docking simulations. As shown in Fig. 3d, the docked complex exhibited a binding energy of −4.04 ​kcal/mol, stabilized by four hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, with a relatively weak electrostatic contribution (1.05 ​kcal/mol). For comparison, docking with wild-type HmaS gave similar results (binding energy of −3.7 ​kcal/mol, four hydrogen bonds, and electrostatic energy of 1.02 ​kcal/mol). The docking model suggested that 2-KMP could still be accommodated in the active site of HmaS (S201F), with key interactions preserved as in HmaS. Interaction networks of HmaS and HmaS (S201F) were analysed by the Protein Contacts Atlas webserver (http://www.mrc-lmb.cam.ac.uk/pca/). The β-sheet S11, where the amino acid residue 201 is located, contacts the α-helix (H5, H9) and β-sheet (S12) through multilevel interactions (Supplementary Fig. S3a and S3b). The number of noncovalent contacts after the mutation of HmaS was calculated. A comparison of the number of atomic contacts near the residue before and after the mutation of HmaS revealed that the number of contacts greatly increased (Supplementary Fig. S3c and S3d). Noncovalent interactions are very important for the stability of proteins. The substrate channel was predicted by the Caver 3.0.3 plugin of PyMOL software (Supplementary Fig. S4). The channel length of HmaS is 14.2 ​Å. The average curvature of the channel is 1.18, and the channel bottleneck is 1.74 ​Å. The channel length of HmaS (S201F) is 15.6 ​Å. The average curvature of the channel is 1.37, and the channel bottleneck is 1.23 ​Å. This indicates that the mutation of residue S201F of HmaS causes its substrate channel to contract because the mutation enhances the weak interaction force of HmaS, leading to overall structural contraction and substrate channel contraction.

Consistent with this prediction, experimental validation using the PmLAAD–HmaS (S201F) cascade revealed that S-HIV was produced from L-leucine with the same retention time as the authentic S-HIV standard (Fig. 3e), confirming that HmaS (S201F) remained catalytically active on 2-KMP. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the S201F mutation in HmaS effectively suppresses the formation of aromatic byproducts while preserving the desired hydroxylation of 2-KMP to S-HIV, providing a more specific biocatalyst for stereoselective S-HIV production.

De novo synthesis of S-HIV in metabolically engineered E. coli

In this study, the plasmids pET-HmaS and pET-HmaS∗ were transformed into E. coli MG1655 (DE3) to construct the recombinant strains EcT7-HmaS and EcT7-HmaS∗, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4b, the strain harbouring wild-type HmaS (EcT7-HmaS) produced 1.27 ​mM S-HIV at 48 ​h, and no absorption peak was detected for the control strain. Notably, EcT7-HmaS accumulated substantial amounts of undesired byproducts, including S-HMA and S-MA (Fig. 4c), which was consistent with the predicted kinetic parameters of HmaS on aromatic substrates (Supplementary Table S4). In comparison, the recombinant strain EcT7-HmaS∗ expressing HmaS (S201F) also successfully produced S-HIV, a lower titre of 1.0 ​mM S-HIV at 48 ​h (Fig. 4b). Although the predicted kinetic parameters suggest that the kcat/Km of HmaS (S201F) with respect to 2-KMP is slightly greater than that of wild-type HmaS (Supplementary Table S4), the exact mechanism that leads to the lower S-HIV product titre of HmaS (S201F) remains to be further explored. As consistent with the results from the biocatalysis study (Fig. 3c), only trace amounts of byproducts (S-HMA and S-MA) were observed in EcT7-HmaS∗, once again confirming that S201F substitution effectively abolished the undesired activity towards aromatic ketoacids. Since we did not perform systematic mutagenesis to evaluate the functional consequences of different bulky substitutions at amino acid residue 201, it might be possible to use other aliphatic amino acids, such as L-leucine and L-isoleucine, to construct more catalytically favourable HmaS mutants for S-HIV production. The biomass of EcT7-HmaS∗ was greater than that of EcT7-HmaS (Fig. 4b), which might be explained by the blocking of the byproducts S-HMA and S-MA. To verify the chemical identity of the biosynthesized product, LC‒MS/MS analysis was also conducted. The retention time of the compound produced by EcT7-HmaS∗ (2.74 ​min) matched that of the authentic S-HIV standard (2.76 ​min) (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, MS fragmentation analysis confirmed the molecular identity of S-HIV, with characteristic ion fragments at m/z 117.0538 and 71.0484 (Fig. 4e). Overall, these results demonstrated that E. coli could be engineered to produce S-HIV from glycerol by coupling the native L-leucine biosynthetic pathway with engineered HmaS.

Optimization of the leucine pathway for enhanced production of S-HIV

As the utilization of HmaS (S201F) enabled minimal byproduct formation (S-HMA and S-MA), we employed a modular pathway optimization of the L-leucine biosynthetic pathway to further increase S-HIV production via the HmaS (S201F)-mediated route. In the first step, AlsS from B. subtilis was introduced to increase the condensation of two molecules of pyruvate. AlsS is a feedback-resistant AHAS with higher carboligation activity than the native E. coli AHAS isozymes (IlvBN, IlvGM, and IlvIH) [45,46]. The resulting strain, EcT7-sHIV∗, exhibited a significantly improved S-HIV titre of 4.6 ​mM, representing a 4.6-fold increase compared to strain EcT7-HmaS∗ expressing HmaS∗ alone (Fig. 5a). To further channel metabolic flux towards S-HIV synthesis, we overexpressed the IlvCD operon, which catalyses the conversion of α-acetolactate to 2,3-dihydroxyisovalerate and subsequently to α-ketoisovalerate. Given that LeuA, a key enzyme in the L-leucine biosynthetic pathway, is known to be allosterically inhibited by L-leucine, we next investigated a feedback-resistant LeuA variant of LeuA∗ (G462D mutation) along with the LeuBCD operon [47,48] to improve the S-HIV titre. In our previous study, the LeuA∗BCD construct was shown to significantly increase the supply of 2-KMP by alleviating feedback inhibition [29]. However, overexpression of LeuA∗BCD unexpectedly resulted in a reduced titre of S-HIV with obvious growth impairment (Fig. 5c). In contrast, expression of the wild-type LeuABCD operon led to an improvement in S-HIV production, with the titre increasing to 4.2 ​mM. These unexpected results suggested that excessively high intracellular levels of 2-KMP might inhibit the catalytic activity of HmaS, thereby limiting the overall flux towards S-HIV. These results highlight the importance of balancing precursor supply and downstream enzyme capacity to avoid metabolic bottlenecks during pathway optimization. Furthermore, co-overexpression of both the IlvCD operon and the LeuABCD operon in the EcT7-sHIV∗-IlvCD-LeuABCD strain led to a synergistic increase in production, resulting in a final titre of 8.1 ​mM from 40 ​g/L glycerol after 72 ​h of cultivation (Fig. 5d). Collectively, by modular optimization of the leucine biosynthetic pathway, we demonstrated that overexpression of AlsS, the IlvCD operon, and the LeuABCD operon could substantially increase the S-HIV level via a balanced metabolic flux.

Fed-batch fermentation for high-level production of S-HIV

To further evaluate the microbial production potential of S-HIV, we performed fed-batch fermentation using EcT7-sHIV∗-IlvCD-LeuABCD in a 5 ​L bioreactor with a 2 ​L working volume. Prior to fed-batch bioreactor fermentation, we investigated the effects of cultivation parameters, including temperature and IPTG concentration, on S-HIV production. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S5, increasing the fermentation temperature from 30 ​°C to 35 ​°C had a minimal effect on S-HIV production, whereas a further increase to 37 ​°C led to a noticeable decrease in the titre. In addition, increasing the IPTG concentration to 250 ​μM and 500 ​μM resulted in suboptimal S-HIV production. On the basis of these results, cultivation was carried out in modified M9 medium with controlled feeding at 30 ​°C with 100 ​μM IPTG induction, and the pH was maintained at 6.8 throughout the fermentation process. As shown in Fig. 6a, the strain exhibited robust growth, reaching an OD600 of ∼16.2, while glycerol was steadily consumed during the course of fermentation. Notably, the titre of S-HIV progressively increased and reached 33.9 ​mM (4.0 ​g/L) after 159 ​h of fermentation (Fig. 6b). However, substantial accumulation of acetate byproducts was also observed, with acetate concentrations exceeding 300 ​mM in the late stages of fermentation (Fig. 6b). In the future, genetic interventions to abolish acetate and other byproducts via the deletion of adhE, pta, and ldhA may help reduce the burden of overflow metabolism and further improve overall yield and process robustness [[49], [50], [51]]. Conclusions

Here, inspired by the naturally occurring decarboxylation and hydroxylation reactions of Fe(II)-dependent dioxygenases, we developed an artificial biosynthetic pathway for the de novo production of S-HIV. HmaS from A. orientalis, previously known for its aromatic substrate specificity, exhibits unexpected activity towards the aliphatic ketoacid 2-KMP for S-HIV synthesis. To suppress the formation of the undesired byproducts S-HMA and S-MA, we engineered an S201F variant of HmaS, which effectively abolished the natural activity towards aromatic ketoacids while retaining the desired activity towards 2-KMP. Through modular optimization of the L-leucine biosynthetic pathway, de novo production of S-HIV reached 8.1 ​mM S-HIV in shake flasks and 33.9 ​mM (4.0 ​g/L) S-HIV in a fed-batch bioreactor. Given the diversity of α-ketoacids derived from amino acid metabolism, our strategy could be extended to access a broader range of optically pure (S)-α-hydroxy acids. Future efforts may focus on engineering dioxygenases with tailored substrate scopes to further diversify the portfolio of chiral building blocks produced from renewable feedstocks.
