Fragility analyses and strong ground motion response of mechanically stabilized earth retaining walls with plate anchors
Authors: Marzie Ansari Targhi, Mohammad Safi, Ali Noorzad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReliable and cost-effective design of mechanically stabilized earth (MSE) walls in earthquake-prone regions remains a fundamental challenge in geotechnical engineering. The present study evaluates the seismic performance and failure levels of MSE walls. To this end, the influence of ground motion frequency content with different amplitudes on wall response has been investigated to compute the displacements, and to develop fragility curves based on multiple intensity measures. Numerical simulations were performed using PLAXIS 2D, incorporating the hardening soil model with small-strain stiffness to accurately capture the nonlinear deformation behavior of the backfill. The analyses include walls with single, double, and triple plate anchors of varying dimensions subjected to real near-fault earthquake records. The results demonstrate that triple plate anchors are more effective than single plate anchors in reducing horizontal and vertical displacements, earth pressure, and acceleration amplification. However, the internal forces along the walls remained similar across anchor configurations. These findings confirm the effectiveness of increasing the number of plate anchors in mitigating seismic damages to MSE walls. Specifically, the probability of seismic-induced failure can be reduced by approximately 25% with double plate anchors and up to 43% with triple plate anchors, highlighting a viable and cost-efficient strategy for enhancing structural resilience in seismically active regions.Keywords: Mechanically stabilized earth retaining wall, Plate anchor, Hardening soil small model, PLAXIS 2D, Fragility curvesSubject terms: Civil engineering, Engineering

Introduction

The earth is struck by many major earthquakes each year, damaging infrastructure such as retaining walls. Therefore, it is imperative to have a safe and optimal design for retaining walls in seismically active regions. For many years, most conventional retaining walls have been constructed as gravity or cantilever concrete walls. Over the past few decades, engineers around the world have increasingly focused on reinforced soil walls1–3. These earth-wall systems are now rapidly replacing conventional retaining walls, considering their more appropriate performance against seismic loads. The mechanically stabilized earth (MSE) system is a special type of retaining wall that employs tensile inclusions (such as strips, bars, sheets, mats, or nets) to reinforce soil and enhance shear strength. Compared to typical reinforced concrete walls, the advantages of using an MSE wall include significant cost reduction, less construction time, higher tolerance for deformations, inherent flexibility, and less skilled personnel requirements4–6. Current advancements in MSE wall technology have motivated the integration of plate anchors to improve the efficiency and durability of walls.

In this respect, plate anchors are burial reinforcement systems comprising single or multiple load-bearing plates with steel bar or cable, facilitating load transfer into the stabilized soil7–9. In recent years, a variety of geotechnical engineering projects have utilized various anchor types under different loading conditions. Soil anchors are generally classified into two key types: grout anchors and mechanical anchors10, 11. As a specific type of mechanical anchor, plate anchors can be used either horizontally or vertically. Vertical plate anchors are used to stabilize retaining walls. These reinforcements are installed in the soil after excavation and are connected to the wall facing, which is then backfilled. As a result, the soil mass in front of the plates plays a critical role. Soil resistance developed ahead of the anchor plates serves as the primary mechanism for their bearing capacity. This system can be easily installed incrementally until the desired height is reached. However, their installation requires careful attention to ensure the effectiveness of soil compaction around the plates. More specifically, after positioning the plates, each layer of backfill material is placed and compacted carefully to minimize voids around the plates and to maintain uniform density. Moghadam et al.12 have comprehensively reviewed the applications of all mechanical anchors, including their benefits and limitations. Despite substantial prior research on MSE walls, few studies have been focused on the dynamic performance of multi-plate anchor reinforcements under seismic loads. To address this gap, the present study evaluates the seismic response and develops fragility curves for single, double, and triple plate anchor configurations subjected to realistic near-fault ground motions, providing practical and probabilistic insights for design in earthquake-prone regions.

Background

The behavior of reinforced earth walls has been studied through experimental, numerical, and theoretical methods to enhance understanding of their performance. Singh and Mistri13 performed an extensive investigation of pullout capacity for plate anchors with horizontal and inclined configurations in sandy soils. Their findings confirmed that variations in soil density, anchor shape, and anchor orientation significantly influence anchor performance. Moghadam et al.14 experimentally examined the behavior of vertical plate anchors for MSE walls with various shapes and configurations. Their findings indicate that when a diamond arrangement is used instead of a square layout, the load-carrying capacity of the wall increases and its displacement along the horizontal axis decreases. In line with these findings, the authors explored the effects of crumb rubber-reinforced backfill to enhance the stability of the MSE wall. The results show that increasing the rubber content has led to a reduction in the displacement of the wall along the horizontal axis15, 16. Tilak and Samadhiya17 conducted an experimental investigation on the capacity of embedded circular multiplate anchors. They considered the effects of scale, spacing, and the placement ratio of anchors on the anchor’s performance. Based on their results, increasing both placement and embedment ratios directly improves multiplate anchor pullout performance. Moreover, greater inclination angles and larger plate spacings increase the breakout factor, defined as the ratio of pullout force to ultimate restraint capacity. Najafizadeh et al.18 conducted laboratory experiments to assess the static load response of back-to-back anchored walls (BBAWs). The results showed that more spacing between the BBAWs means less horizontal displacement. Azadimanesh et al.19 investigated the effects of plate anchor spacing, quantity, and inclination angle under static loading conditions. The outcomes indicate that the horizontal displacement increases and the bearing capacity decreases when the inclination angle is increased from 0 to 60 degrees. Moreover, reducing the d/H ratio from 0.4 to 0.2 leads to a decrease in the load-bearing capacity and an increase in the displacement along the horizontal axis. Bildik et al.20 analyzed the performance of plate anchors in sandy slopes. Their results show that bearing efficiency is primarily governed by anchor spacing and distance from the crest. Aldaikh et al.21 conducted parametric analyses to investigate plate anchor pullout behavior across varying embedment ratios and inclination angles. Using a nonlinear model incorporating soil hardening with small-strain stiffness, they found close agreement between numerical and theoretical results.

A review of previous studies indicates that, although the use of plate anchors in MSE walls has received considerable attention, most investigations, including the study by Moghadam et al.14, have primarily focused on static loading conditions and the pullout capacity of plate anchors. However, the seismic response of MSE walls reinforced with plate anchors has not been adequately examined in the scientific literature. Furthermore, the effect of increasing the number of anchor plates while maintaining a constant total reinforcement cross-sectional area has not yet been thoroughly studied through comprehensive nonlinear numerical simulations and fragility-based probabilistic analyses. This research gap, along with the absence of specific design guidelines for the application of multi-plate anchors under seismic conditions, underscores the need for a more in-depth and integrated investigation. To address this issue of concern, the present study adopts a multi-faceted approach by integrating advanced numerical modeling with probabilistic assessment. The study is based on finite element (FE) simulations using PLAXIS 2D22, in which MSE walls with single and multi-plate anchors are simulated under real near-fault earthquake records. In this context, the hardening soil small (HSS) constitutive model is employed to capture the nonlinear behavior of soil realistically. Also, incremental dynamic analysis (IDA) is performed using a set of earthquake records with varying frequency content. Based on the results, seismic fragility curves have been developed for various anchor configurations. A comparative evaluation under identical modeling conditions demonstrates that increasing the number of plates without increasing the total amount of reinforcement material can significantly enhance the system’s seismic resistance. Overall, this study makes a valuable contribution to advancing the engineering understanding of the seismic behavior of MSE walls reinforced with plate anchors by providing detailed numerical analyses, evaluating key seismic parameters, and developing performance-based design tools.

Methodology

Understanding the seismic behavior of MSE walls is a critical consideration for safe design and construction in seismic zones. These walls are commonly constructed as soil retaining systems to enhance the safety of slopes adjacent to roadways and residential areas. Given the nonlinear response of structures during strong ground motions, this study is concerned with assessing the seismic behavior of MSE walls with diverse plate anchors and generating fragility curves for evaluating damage states This research simulates a 9.0 m-high embankment using PLAXIS 2D22, reinforced with a precast facing panel and single, double, or triple plate anchor systems. To verify the modeling accuracy, the static response of a reduced-scale MSE wall with single plate anchors was simulated using PLAXIS 2D, comparing with the results of the experimental study by Moghadam et al.14. The numerical results for horizontal wall displacement and footing bearing capacity showed good agreement with the experimental observations. Two-dimensional modeling under the plane strain assumption not only reduces computational costs but also provides sufficient accuracy when compared to three-dimensional analyses or experimental results23. In this study, two distinct strategies were employed to investigate the impact of plate anchor configuration on the seismic performance of MSE walls. In the first approach, a square plate with dimensions of 60 cm (SP1) served as the baseline model. The total plate area was then divided into two or three equally spaced plates along the reinforcement length, creating configurations DP1 (double-plate) and TP1 (triple-plate), respectively. By maintaining a constant total plate area across SP1, DP1, and TP1, this approach enabled a cost-neutral comparison focused solely on the effect of plate distribution. The second approach modified the baseline SP1 model by increasing the single plate size to 70 cm, resulting in the SP2 configuration. This modification, which entails higher material costs, was carried out to evaluate whether enlarging a single plate could improve seismic performance more effectively than employing multiple plates with an equivalent total area. Therefore, the effectiveness of seismic performance relative to construction costs in MSE walls with plate anchors has been compared. At the next step, nonlinear dynamic analyses were performed to develop fragility curves based on intensity measures (IMs) under near-field seismic records. The incremental dynamic analysis (IDA) approach is among the most effective methods for obtaining fragility curves to estimate the effects of different seismic characteristics26. IDA curves were utilized to assess structural damage caused by strong ground motions. Fragility curves are developed by evaluating the probability of exceeding certain limit states under different IM levels. However, the accuracy of the fragility curves depends on the number of selected records. This study employed a consistent set of seismic records comprising 12 acceleration time histories to generate the curves (Table 2). The seismic records utilized here were obtained from the PEER ground motion database available from the Pacific Earthquake Engineering Research Center website27. This selection ensured that seismic parameters, including motion magnitudes, focal distance, and frequency content, are within acceptable ranges and suitable for exciting the seismic response of the MSE wall28. The stronger horizontal component of each earthquake was scaled such that the scale factors for each record commenced at 0.05 g, and increased by 0.05 g steps until the walls experienced failure. In the next step, these scaled earthquake inputs were applied to the numerical model. Residual horizontal displacements at the wall crest were computed as the engineering demand parameters (EDP) from incremental nonlinear dynamic analysis (IDA). Afterward, the fragility curves were generated based on the seismic response data and the set of damage states using damage probability. A total of 960 numerical analyses were conducted for MSE wall models. These models include single-plate anchors with varying dimensions, as well as double and triple-plate configurations.

Verifications of the models

The proposed model is verified against a reduced-scale experimental MSE walls with single plate anchors under static loading14. The MSE wall was 0.3 m in height (H) with a rigid panel facing and two layers of square plate anchors with a dimension of 0.04 m. The horizontal and vertical spacing of the reinforcements was both set to 0.15 m. A constant strain loading was applied until the footing settled by 0.024 m. The model is described in more detail in Moghadam et al.14.

In the present research, the two-dimensional PLAXIS software was employed to analyze the behavior of the MSE wall. To simulate the soil behavior, the hardening soil small model (HSS) was used. Numerical modeling

Geometry of the model and boundary conditions

The present study carefully designed the dimensions of the MSE wall to maintain a theoretical and simplified geometric configuration. Based on the defined dimensions, its height, denoted by H, is assumed to be 9.0 m above the foundation with precast concrete panel wall facings. Moreover, the foundation was modeled as bedrock with a depth of 6.0 m. In this research, the distance between the wall and the lateral boundary is assumed to be 3.5 H to minimize numerical boundary effects in seismic responses34. In addition, six reinforcement layers are included, beginning at 1.0 m above the base of the wall. Plate anchor lengths were assumed to be 0.5H at the bottom layer, 0.7H at the middle, and 0.9H at the top. In addition, 15-noded triangular elements under the plane strain condition were used, so that SP1 was meshed with 10,251 nodes and 1111 elements, SP2 with 10,310 nodes and 1155 elements, DP1 with 11,075 nodes and 1183 elements, and TP1 with 12,451 nodes and 1323 elements. Table 4 lists the properties of reinforcements in each model (e.g., vertical spacing (Sv), horizontal spacing (Sh), and the number of anchors along the height). These properties were selected to ensure internal and external stability in accordance with the specifications and procedures outlined by FHWA35 and the PLAXIS 2D Reference Manual22. The analyses were conducted using the staged construction approach. Moreover, each layer was compacted under a pressure of 15 kN/m2 and temporarily extended to 8.1 m behind the facing. This temporary extension was removed before placing the next lift36. Furthermore, the shear strength of the underlying soil and structure interface is lower than that of the surrounding soil22, 37. Consequently, Eq. (7)38 was used to simulate the interaction between the reinforcement and backfill soil, as well as between the backfill and the facing. The interface strength parameter Rinter was applied for this purpose.

Here, the interaction coefficient (R) between reinforcements and backfill soil was set to 0.67 times that of the surrounding material, while a value of 0.9 is adopted for the wall-facing elements and backfill soil interface. Additionally, mesh objectivity of the results was ensured by performing sensitivity analyses. Accordingly, a fine mesh size was chosen for the reinforced region. At the same time, the soil boundaries had coarser mesh sizes (Fig. 1). For the static analyses, the boundary conditions were fully fixed at the model’s base. In contrast, the vertical boundaries were normally fixed. Also, to prevent unwanted wave reflections and to ensure accurate modeling, free-field boundary conditions were used for the vertical edges of the model. The compliant base boundary was applied with a prescribed displacement along the horizontal axis at the base of the model. By prescribing displacements, the application of acceleration, velocity, or displacement time histories became possible throughout the computational process. Also, in this study, the convergence criteria provided by PLAXIS 2D were adopted in the iterative calculations, considering a tolerated error of 0.01. Furthermore, the dynamic time step was set equal to the time interval of the earthquake input motion to preserve the frequency content of the original record. The number of calculation steps was then selected such that the total analysis time matched the duration of the earthquake. For instance, a 57-s Northridge accelerogram with a time interval (Δt) of 0.005 s was analyzed using 11,400 steps, preserving the original time increment.

Material properties

The three key components that constitute the reinforced retaining wall system are: backfill materials, reinforcements, and facing elements. Soil behavior is a key element affecting the performance of the composite reinforced soil structure. Granular soils are commonly used in the construction of reinforced retaining walls because of their effective stress transfer capabilities, excellent durability, and good drainage performance. The same soil type was employed for both the retained and reinforced backfill materials in this study36. Also, the bedrock foundation was assumed to be rigid to ignore the foundation deformation effects on the behavior of the MSE wall. Similar to the numerical simulation for model verification, the behavior of the soil backfill was governed by the hardening soil small (HSS) model. Table 5 presents the soil parameters. Furthermore, the Rayleigh damping coefficients (α and β) for a 5% critical damping ratio were determined using two target frequencies. The first was the fundamental frequency of the soil profile to capture the system’s dominant dynamic behavior39. Following Kwok et al.40, the second was set to five times of the fundamental frequency to account for higher mode effects in the dynamic response.

The facing element in reinforced soil systems was designed to provide interlayer stability by preventing soil displacement between reinforcement levels. The precast concrete panel wall facing was designed with 1.5 m width, 1.5 m height, and 0.3 m thickness. In this study, precast concrete panels were simulated using plate elements with Young’s modulus (E), unit weight (γ), and Poisson’s ratio (ν) equal to 21 × 10⁶ kPa, 24 kN/m3, and 0.15, respectively14. Also, elastomeric ethylene propylene diene monomer (EPDM) bearing pads, with a plan area of 85 × 10–4 m2 and a thickness of 0.02 m, were positioned along the horizontal joints between concrete facing panels37. In the numerical model, these bearing pads were represented using plate elements with E = 15 × 103 kPa and υ = 0.5.

The reinforcements are significant components within MSE walls that considerably affect their structural responses. This research used plate anchors to reinforce the backfill behind the wall in single, double, and triple forms. The failure surface was defined based on Rankine’s theory. The reinforcements must have a minimum length of 0.5 m to attain the passive zone. Due to the presence of multiplate anchors in this configuration, the anchor rod lengths were reduced from top to bottom of the wall and were equal to 0.9 H, 0.7 H, and 0.5 H (H, height of wall), respectively. The plate anchors were modeled as rigid square plates, with their thicknesses selected accordingly14, 15. The overall area (A) of the plate anchors was maintained constant. Therefore, the dimensions of the side lengths of SP1, SP2, DP1, and TP1 were determined to be 0.6, 0.7, 0.424, and 0.346 m, respectively. Moreover, based on the findings of Perlow41, to prevent stress bulb interaction, the plates were spaced at intervals of 2.4–3.6 times the diameter of the circular plate anchors along the anchor rod. In addition, for the double and triple plate configurations, the distances along the anchor rod were 1.4 m and 1.7 m, respectively. The spacings in the horizontal and vertical directions were assumed to be 2 m and 1.5 m, respectively. The plate thickness and tie rod diameter were considered 0.03 m and 0.04 m for all reinforcement types, respectively. Dynamic analyses: results and discussions

Wall displacement

Figure 6a and b present the maximum horizontal displacements of the MSE wall reinforced with SP1, SP2, DP1, and TP1 configurations, subjected to the Northridge earthquake using two different records from the LA-Sepulveda and LA00 stations, respectively. The results indicate that increasing the number of plate anchors from one to three (while maintaining a constant total plate area and material volume) significantly reduces horizontal displacements and thereby enhances seismic performance of MSE walls. Under dynamic loadings with an acceleration of 0.35 g, replacing SP1 with SP2, SP1 with DP1, and DP1 with TP1 reduces horizontal displacement by 7.5%, 15%, and 9%, respectively, yielding an average reduction of approximately 24% for the triple-plate (TP1) configuration. Although increasing plate dimensions, as in SP2, also improves performance, multi-plate configurations result in greater displacement reduction without requiring additional material.

Maximum horizontal displacement along the wall height under (a) the Northridge earthquake in LA-Sepulveda station and (b) the Northridge earthquake in LA00 station.

The seismic performance of DP1 and TP1 is attributed to increase plate–soil interaction and more effective stress distribution, resulting from positioning the plates closer to the potential failure surface. This optimized plate distribution increases the efficiency of load transfer, reducing wall displacements. In contrast, the SP2 configuration achieves moderate improvement at the cost of higher material consumption. Overall, the findings demonstrate that plate distribution and plate-soil interaction play a more decisive role in seismic performance than plate size alone. Consequently, adopting multiple-plate anchors offers an economically and structurally efficient design approach for improving the seismic resilience of MSE walls.

In addition to the effects of plate arrangement and material utilization, the dynamic performance of MSE walls can be significantly influenced by ground motion characteristics, such as duration, velocity content, and frequency. Figure 7a and b present the horizontal displacement time histories for each numerical model at the top and middle of the wall during the different ground motion records of the Northridge earthquake. The input ground acceleration, its duration, and frequency content exhibit a complicated relationship with the maximum and residual lateral displacements caused by seismic loads. As observed, the MSE wall horizontal displacement increases along the height of the wall, and maximum displacements occur at the crest of the wall. In general, the wall reinforced with triple plate anchors not only reduces the maximum displacement but also decreases the residual displacement in MSE walls. Moreover, while the peak ground acceleration (PGA) of the Northridge records in different stations is the same, prominent differences in displacements indicate the essential role of velocity time histories. It is worth noting that the PGV/PGA ratio represents the key parameter in quantifying near-fault ground motion damage potential. Ground motions with higher PGV/PGA indicate more vulnerability to damage24. Moreover, as evident in Fig. 7b, increasing the frequency content of seismic loading at a constant acceleration level leads to a reduction in the seismic energy transferred to the system. This reduction lowers the stress levels within the backfill and effectively minimizes the horizontal displacement of the MSE wall. This understanding is important for optimizing the design of MSE walls in earthquake-prone areas, as considering input frequency enables effective control of displacements and minimizes potential damage.

Increasing the length of the plates and their number from single to double and from double to triple led to a decrease in settlement by 6%, 7%, and 13%, respectively. Axial force

Determining the peak tensile force within the reinforcement layer represents a key step in designing MSE walls. The interaction between reinforcement load and influencing parameters such as reinforcement arrangement, type of reinforcement, wall geometry, and loading conditions makes it difficult to determine this force42, 43. Figure 9a and b compare the maximum reinforcement loads in each layer induced by the Northridge earthquake, recorded at the LA-Sepulveda and LA00 stations, under a peak ground acceleration of 0.35 g and various types of plate anchors. It is evident that the tensile force distribution for all reinforcement types is nonlinear, and its trend changes among different rows. Based on the results, increasing the number of plate anchors installed on the anchor rods and plates with larger dimensions does not substantially affect the mobilized tensile force. For instance, the transition from SP1 to SP2, SP1 to DP1, and DP1 to TP1 results in reductions of 11%, 7%, and 10% of the mobilized force, respectively.

Comparison of maximum reinforcement load under the Northridge earthquake in (a) LA-Sepulveda station and (b) LA00 station.

As can be seen, the central layers have a larger mobilized force than the bottom and top layers. During earthquake loading, the central layers experience greater peak forces than other layers (e.g., dynamic earth pressure and seismic inertia forces). Consequently, the seismic stability of the wall highly depends on the central reinforcement layers. Therefore, to reduce the risk of excessive displacements, stiffer reinforcement in the central layers is employed. Also, increasing the PGV/PGA ratios increases the magnitudes of the maximum reinforcement force over the upper half of the wall.

Earth pressure

The computation of lateral earth pressure for MSE walls is complicated by both the presence of reinforcements and the inherent flexibility of the composite system. Up to now, no equation has been proposed for the lateral earth pressure of MSE walls under dynamic conditions. The current approaches are restricted to a set of simplified relationships that do not consider the significant influencing factors such as reinforcement geometry, distribution, and ground motion frequency content35. Additionally, it is essential to identify the types and configurations of reinforcement elements that are directly subjected to higher tensile forces during seismic events.

This research aims to achieve an optimal design of MSE walls by providing detailed numerical evaluations under near-fault ground motions. Figure 10a and b present the normalized resultant lateral earth pressure (Pae/0.5γH2 = Kae), the location of the resultant earth pressure (R), and the peak acceleration values of the Northridge earthquake at various stations. According to the findings of44, 45, the outcomes demonstrate that the value for the total dynamic earth pressure coefficients (Kae) increases nonlinearly with an increase in PGA in all models. In contrast, Kae decreases as the number of plates increases from one to three. Therefore, Kae values can be expressed as nonlinear functions that are affected by both the peak base acceleration and the number of plate anchors. The use of multi-plate anchors promotes more uniform stress distribution and improved dissipation of seismic energy. Figure 10a and b illustrate that changing the number of plate anchors does not significantly influence Kae under high-frequency ground motions. It is observed that Kae values exhibit increased sensitivity to low-frequency content (Fig. 10a), especially in the case of triple plate anchor configuration. The obtained results suggest that Kae under the Northridge earthquake decreases by 7% in the LA00 station (high-frequency) and by 19% in the LA-Sepulveda station (low-frequency) when replacing a single plate anchor with a triple plate anchor. These observations highlight the frequency-dependent nature of seismic pressure responses and confirm the benefits of plate distribution over merely increasing plate size. From a design standpoint, these findings suggest that seismic lateral loads prescribed in design codes could be refined based on reinforcement configuration and ground motion characteristics. Incorporating modifiers for dynamic pressure coefficients based on plate geometry and distribution would enable more performance-based and economically optimized design approaches, especially in seismic areas.

Fragility curves

There are significant uncertainties in evaluating the deformation of walls under seismic loading conditions. Fragility analysis serves as an effective approach for assessing motion responses, considering the associated uncertainties for the MSE wall during seismic conditions. Various approaches are available to develop fragility curves. Numerical simulations have been developed as a popular method to generate fragility curves, particularly under seismic loadings55. These curves are a function of a lognormal distribution that describes the probability of exceeding specified structural damage states. Here, the seismic performance of MSE walls was evaluated using IDA with a set of 12 accelerograms. The magnitude of these accelerograms ranges from 6.5 to 7.9 Richter, and their distances are between 0.62 and 12.1 km. Definition of damage index

The first step in performing fragility analyses is to define the damage levels associated with seismic loading conditions. Notably, the allowable displacement at the top of the wall is commonly used in the engineering literature. Accordingly, the residual horizontal displacement at the top of the wall facing was solely used to define the damage state criteria. In general, design standards implemented in many countries emphasize the importance of controlling lateral displacements as part of deformation-based design approaches for retaining walls56. However, the most pertinent standards do not examine the consequences of dynamic motion. Overall, there are two methods to control the displacement of retaining walls under earthquake loadings: post-earthquake inspection and shaking table testing. According to Kuwano et al.57, less than 1% of MSE walls suffered significant damage in the 2011 Tohoku earthquake in Japan. Huang et al.58 determined that the range between acceptable and failure displacement in reinforced soil walls is between 2 and 5% of the wall height. Based on observations from the Sichuan earthquake disaster area in China, three distinct performance levels were defined at 1% H, 3.5% H, and 6% H, where H is the total height of the retaining wall59. According to Li et al.49, a displacement index of 3.8% of the wall height was reported for geogrid-reinforced soil walls. Moreover, Cao et al.50 considered 3% H as a control parameter for the wall displacement of inextensible reinforcements. FHWA35 and AASHTO53 have recommended a displacement ratio ranging from 4% H to 9% H of the wall. The National Concrete Masonry Association (NCMA,60) suggests using 3.5% H as a control criterion for MSE walls. Hence, based on the observations and studies performed, the threshold damage indices for the MSE walls reinforced with plate anchors (Table 8) are defined as follows: intact, moderate damage, and extensive damage at displacement values of 0–0.5%, 0.5–3%, and > 3% of the wall height, respectively.

Evaluation of intensity measures

The first step is to identify the intensity measure that best corresponds with the response of the MSE wall, thereby creating the fragility curves. This study investigates the ability of seismic intensity measures, such as PGA and Sa, to predict structural damage in terms of the residual horizontal displacement (d) of the wall. The accuracy of the results is expressed by criteria such as efficiency and sufficiency rankings54, as shown in Figs. 17 and 18, for the SP1 and TP1. Efficiency is evaluated from the linear regression results for each model with various IMs in the ln (IM)-ln (d) space. To evaluate the discrepancy of data concerning efficiency criteria, both regression parameters and the determination coefficient (R2) were derived (Fig. 17). Indeed, the derived coefficients confirm the efficiency and capability of PGA and Sa (T₁, 5%) in predicting the displacement performance of the structure. Moreover, a sufficient IM is statistically independent of the magnitude (Mw) and distance (Rrup) of ground motion.

Evaluation of IM efficiency based on data dispersion in SP1 and TP1.

Linear regressions of the residuals of SP1 and TP1 models versus magnitude and distance in terms of (a) PGA and (b) Sa.

This criterion is evaluated using the P-value61, which represents the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis in the analysis of variance. The hypothesis typically suggests that two sets of variables are the same or that there is no significant difference. Since the most often used P-value in engineering applications is 5%, it is used as the threshold for evaluating this parameter62. An insufficient IM is indicated by a P-value smaller than this level, which suggests more evidence for rejecting the null hypothesis. Evaluating the sufficiency of seismic intensity measures involves analyzing the statistical relevance of regression residuals between residual displacement (d) and seismic parameters such as earthquake magnitude or fault distance63. In this study, correlation coefficients are computed between residuals of d–IMs and the seismic parameters Mw and Rrup. Generally, a satisfactory degree of efficiency and sufficiency is obtained for the PGA and Sa (T1, 5%) parameters. Based on the findings, fragility curves are generated using two intensity measures: PGA and Sa. PGA is among the prevailing parameters of seismic intensity in the analysis and design of structures, including retaining walls, dams, and bridges64, 65. Moreover, a set of fragility curves is also developed based on Sa since Sa has been obtained as a more comprehensive measure, especially for structures sensitive to dynamic effects. Unlike PGA, which represents a single value at the ground surface, Sa reflects the response of a structure at different periods. This criterion can more effectively capture the wall–soil dynamic interaction.

Generating fragility curves

Figures 19 and 20 present the fragility curves for four reinforcement configurations of the MSE wall (SP1, SP2, DP1, and TP1), developed based on seismic intensity measures, including PGA and Sa. These curves illustrate the variations in the wall’s behavior until reaching the defined failure state. Three damage states are defined for all four models. In this study, to understand the practical utility of the fragility curves for the performance-based design purposes, the extensive damage state (displacement > 28.8 cm) is defined as the threshold for unacceptable performance (Table 8). In the single plate model, the wider area of the curve shows an extensive damage state compared to the other models. For instance, under an earthquake with a PGA of 0.2 g, the fragility in the minor damage state for the SP1, SP2, DP1, and TP1 models is 79%, 75%, 70%, and 60%, respectively. In contrast, with an earthquake of PGA of 0.5 g, these fragility values are 90%, 85%, 70%, and 55%, respectively, for moderate damage. Similarly, given the occurrence of a strong earthquake with a PGA of 1 g, this 9.0 m wall has experienced 94%, 90%, 89%, and 84%, respectively, greater than 28.8 cm, for the extensive damage state. The derived median fragility curves provide the intensity measure and resistance level associated with 50% exceedance probability for each damage state. In this respect, considering the earthquake intensity measure (IM) as the Sa (T1, 5%), the minor damage state for the SP1, SP2, DP1, and TP1 models corresponds to Sa values of 0.25 g, 0.27 g, 0.3 g, and 0.35 g, respectively. Similarly, this probability for the moderate damage state occurs at Sa values of 0.66 g, 0.71 g, 0.79 g, and 0.85 g, while it should be 1.45 g, 1.5 g, 1.58 g, and 1.64 g for extensive damage. It can be seen that walls designed using Sa-based approaches have experienced lower levels of damage than those designed with PGA. In this regard, Sa should be the preferred measure for assessing seismic hazards in retaining wall design, especially when the natural period of the wall is close to the dominant period of the seismic input. In general, given that the aforementioned intensity measures are not all derived from a single seismic record, the estimation of failure probability can vary depending on the type of intensity measure selected for analysis. This highlights the necessity of employing multi-IM fragility curves, which, by accounting for the inherent complexities of seismic ground motions, provide a more precise and comprehensive understanding of structural failure probabilities. In effect, these findings underscore the challenges associated with identifying an optimal and unique intensity measure for fragility assessments, as the performance of each scalar intensity measure is influenced by the diverse and complex characteristics of earthquake excitations. Conclusion

Mechanically stabilized earth (MSE) retaining walls are essential infrastructure that provide slope stability and support various structures. Although reinforced earth walls exhibit resilience when subjected to seismic loads, it is crucial to employ meticulous design, minimize possible hazards, and guarantee structural integrity, especially in areas prone to earthquake loading. To achieve these objectives, the use of multi-plate anchors has emerged as a viable method to improve the stability and effectiveness of MSE retaining walls. Despite multiple studies on the performance of single and multi-plate anchors under static loading conditions, the seismic assessment of MSE walls with plate anchors has been pursued infrequently. Therefore, the present work aims to evaluate the seismic vulnerability of MSE walls with single and multi-plate anchors using fragility curves. In this research, fragility functions for MSE walls have been derived from the results of nonlinear time-history simulations. For dynamic analyses, four MSE walls with single plate anchors with various dimensions (SP1 and SP2), double plate anchor (DP1), and triple plate anchor (TP1) reinforcements and 12 input ground motions were implemented in more than 960 seismic analyses. Based on the obtained results, the concluding remarks of this research are as follows:

Increasing the number of plate anchors in the soil from one to three is more pronounced than changing the plate dimensions. The horizontal displacement decreases to around 24% under dynamic loading. The reduction can be attributed to increased interaction between the soil and the plate anchors located behind the failure wedge.
