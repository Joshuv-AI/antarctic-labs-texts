Mechanical energy storage of planar spiral structures based on two-dimensional nanomaterials
Authors: Shangchun Jiang, Jiaming Yan, Yihan Nie, Haifei Zhan, Chaofeng Lü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical batteries currently serve as the primary power source for a wide range of daily applications, yet they present substantial risks to human health and environmental sustainability. Drawing inspiration from clockwork springs and carbon nanoscrolls, this work proposes a planar spiral structure (PSS) using 2D materials (graphene, MoS₂, diamane) for mechanical energy storage, offering an eco-friendly alternative to conventional electrochemical systems. Molecular dynamics simulations reveal that elastic torsional deformation of the PSS occurs in two stages, including a delamination-wrapping process driven by bending strain and van der Waals interactions, followed by tensile deformation. Energy storage predominantly arises from the first stage, where strain energy increases almost linearly with rotation angle. A theoretical model is developed, correlating strain energy with torsional angle and quantifying contributions from bending and surface energy. Simulations show that materials with higher bending stiffness exhibit superior energy storage capacity due to dominant bending strain energy. The model validates that diamane PSS can achieve a high energy storage capacity, with bending energy governing its entire delamination-wrapping process. This work provides a low-carbon design for nanoscale energy storage, applicable to biomedical micro/nanodevices, and establishes a framework for optimizing 2D nanomaterial-based spiral architectures.

Introduction

Driven by exponential growth in global population and rising living standards over recent decades, energy consumption has surged unprecedentedly. This escalating demand imposes stricter requirements on energy storage technologies. Electrochemical batteries currently stand as the dominant solution to energy storage challenges1–3, powering diverse applications from portable electronic devices/systems and mobile robots to electric vehicles. Notably, the lithium-ion battery market is projected to reach $40 billion by 20254, a scale that exacerbates overexploitations of mineral resources and generates substantial hazardous chemical waste5. These issues pose critical threats to human health and environmental sustainability.

Mechanical energy storage, a timeless technology, ranges from ancient bowstrings for hunting to modern clock springs. At the micro/nanoscale, such systems require materials with exceptional mechanical robustness, efficient energy transduction, and structural stability. Low-dimensional nanomaterials, known with exceptional mechanical properties, show appealing promise for such applications6–8. For instance, researchers proposed the mechanical energy storage schemes based on CNT bundles9,10 and two-dimensional (2D) diamane11. Among them, carbon nanoscrolls (CNSs) with an open tubular structure formed by twisting 2D graphene sheets have gained attention12–14. They can be synthesized via chemical vapor deposition (CVD)15 or electrochemical methods16. CNSs inherit graphene’s excellent mechanical property and conductivity17, enabling applications in energy storage18, sensors19, and oscillators20. For energy storage, the primary source is the bending-induced elastic strain and van der Waals interactions in overlapping regions.

Inspired by CNS helical architecture, this work proposes a new planar spiral structure (PSS) for mechanical energy storage using 2D nanomaterials, such as graphene, MoS2, and diamane21. Based on the molecular dynamics simulation results, the energy governing equation as a function of the rotation angle is derived, quantifying contributions from bending strain and van der Waals interactions for optimal design. In addition to its theoretical relevance, the proposed PSS geometry is compatible with several established micro/nanofabrication strategies. At the micro- and nanoscale, spiral patterns with well-defined parameters can in principle be realized using electron-beam lithography or focused ion beam techniques, where the geometry is deterministically defined by the pattern design, exposure conditions, and etching bias22. Subsequent deterministic transfer and van der Waals stacking of two-dimensional layers enable precise control over the layer number and interlayer configuration23,24. For larger-area implementations, scalable approaches such as nanoimprint lithography and selective-area chemical vapor deposition on pre-patterned substrates provide viable pathways toward uniform spiral or curvilinear nanostructure arrays25,26. Also, research indicates that graphene sheets can fully self-scroll onto nanowires27 and nanoparticle28, forming a stable core/shell structure.

Torsional behavior

Firstly, we investigate the deformation behavior of the graphene PSS during the rotation process along its central rotating area. Figure 2 illustrates the potential energy change or strain energy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta E$$\end{document}ΔE) of the graphene PSS during the twist deformation, which can be divided into two distinct stages. In the first stage, the energy change increases steadily and almost linearly with the rotation angle. In comparison, the energy change experiences a much faster increase in the second stage. According to the average von Mises stress, the whole structure experiences a small stress in the first stage, while a much higher stress is observed in the second stage. According to the atomic configurations, the energy change originates from the morphological change of the graphene nanoribbon while it delaminates from the outer wall and wraps around the inner wall during the first deformation stage, i.e., a delamination-wrapping process. Afterwards, the graphene nanoribbon experiences tensile deformation as restricted by the rigid boundaries, which tightens the winding and yields a rapid increase in the strain energy. For energy storage purpose, we focus on the first deformation stage of the PSS. To note that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta E$$\end{document}ΔE exhibits obvious fluctuations in the initial deformation stage, which is originated from two primary mechanisms. One is the variation of the interlayer stacking from AA to AB sequence during the twist deformation, i.e., the stacking energy variation29,30. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, continuous relative displacement occurs between graphene layers during the winding process, causing persistent changes in stacking configurations and consequently leading to energy fluctuations. To note that, the spiral geometry imposes a continuously varying local registry between layers. Enforcing AB stacking globally would require incompatible relative translations and rotations along the spiral, which cannot be satisfied simultaneously. As a result, the system becomes geometrically frustrated: transitioning from AA to AB stacking would introduce substantial elastic strain or structural distortions that outweigh the energetic gain from locally favorable AB stacking. Consequently, the relaxation process converges to a metastable AA-stacked configuration, which is compatible with the imposed Archimedean spiral topology and represents a compromise between interlayer interaction energy and geometric constraints, rather than the global thermodynamic minimum associated with AB stacking in planar structures. The other one is related with the dynamic delamination and re-adhesion process occurring at the outer wall. As shown in Fig. 3b, the intermediate segments between the inner and outer wall experience dynamic delamination and re-adhesion process during the whole deformation process, which is more profound at the beginning of the twist deformation. In the simulations, the observed behavior is not a spontaneous or homogeneous delamination, but a mechanically driven, geometry-induced process. Specifically, the 2D monolayer is subjected to a pulling and rotational constraint imposed by the central rotating axis. As the axis rotates, it continuously drags the inner part of the monolayer, generating increasing tensile and shear stresses at the interface between the monolayer and the outer shell. When these stresses exceed the local interfacial adhesion energy, the monolayer detaches from the outer enclosure31,32. Subsequently, due to the combined effects of rotational dragging, bending rigidity, and van der Waals attraction, the detached monolayer re-adheres by wrapping around and conforming to the surface of the central axis, forming a spiral-like configuration. Therefore, the dynamic delamination should be understood as a stress-driven peeling followed by forced re-adhesion. By tracking the atomic configurations, the wound segment length displays a characteristic fluctuation pattern with an amplitude of approximately 0.9 nanometers. As compared in Fig. 5a, the fluctuations magnitude of the strain energy increases with rising temperature, which is originated from the small out-of-plane bending stiffness of graphene33. Although this study primarily focuses on the energy storage process, we explicitly investigated the energy release behavior of the PSS structure through unloading simulations. We simulated the energy release process by reversing the central rotating area of the PSS device, and the variation in device energy during the reversal is shown in Fig. 10b. The results show that the stored mechanical energy can be fully released upon unloading, with the structure returning to its original configuration without any observable residual deformation or structural change. This indicates that the energy storage and release processes in PSS are highly reversible and essentially lossless within the investigated deformation range. The fully recoverable structural response further suggests excellent cyclic durability. Since no permanent structural rearrangement, bond breaking, or plastic deformation is observed during loading–unloading cycles, the mechanical performance of PSS is expected to remain stable under repeated operation. These characteristics highlight the suitability of PSS for practical nanoscale mechanical energy storage applications.

Compared with other representative nanoscale mechanical energy storage systems reported in prior literature, the PSS structure investigated here exhibits fundamentally different energy storage characteristics. The PSS system stores mechanical energy mainly through controlled structural reconfiguration rather than conventional elastic deformation. This non-elastic-dominated storage mechanism enables a more compact structural design and facilitates precise control over the energy storage and release processes. Moreover, unloading simulations demonstrate fully reversible behavior, with complete energy release and no residual deformation or structural change, highlighting a key advantage over other systems where residual strain or structural distortion may lead to partial irreversibility. These distinctions underscore the unique performance benefits of PSS relative to previously reported nanoscale mechanical energy storage architectures.

Conclusions

Taking the graphene as an example, this work investigates the mechanical energy storage capacity of a planar spiral structure (PSS) based on atomistic simulations and theoretical analysis. The elastic deformation of PSS contains a delamination-wrapping process and a tensile process, with the former as the main energy storage mechanism. The delamination-wrapping process involves the bending deformation and the surface change of the nanoribbon, corresponding to the bending strain energy and surface energy. It is found that the newly attached segment length in the inner nanoscroll is approximately the same as the delaminated length in the outer nanoscroll, and the angular change of the attach points has a linear relationship. A theoretical model is developed to correlate the strain energy and the torsional angle. Further simulations reveal that MoS₂ and diamane PSS can store higher strain energy due to their higher bending stiffness, which aligns well with the theoretical predictions. The total strain energy relies on the interplay between the bending strain energy and the surface energy. The graphene PSS has a dominant surface energy change due to its low bending stiffness, while the bending strain energy dominates in the diamane PSS. A structural parameter and a material parameter are defined to quantify their relationship. For the PSS with a larger material parameter than the structural parameter, a high total strain energy can be achieved. To attain higher energy density, lighter materials with higher bending stiffness are preferred.

In summary, this work provides an in-depth understanding of the deformation process of the planar spiral structure. The obtained understanding and the theoretical model can be applied to guide the optimum design of the spiral structure for micro-scale mechanical energy storage, providing alternative energy supplies for advanced devices. From a practical perspective, the experimental realization of PSS with uniform geometry over large areas remains challenging, particularly for multilayer architectures. Factors such as line-edge roughness, transfer-induced defects, and alignment tolerance can affect the fidelity of the designed spiral parameters. In this context, the present results could serve as theoretical guideline for future experiments, while also being directly applicable to more mature layered materials.

Methods

To describe the C-C atomic interactions in graphene and diamane, we employed the widely used adaptive intermolecular reactive empirical bond order (AIREBO) potential, which can accurately capture the bond interaction, breaking, and formation. The cutoff distance was modified to 2.0 Å to avoid the nonphysical phenomenon during the rotation. Previous studies have shown that AIREBO can well demonstrate the mechanical behaviors of carbon nanomaterials43,44. The REBOMoS potential was adopted to describe the atomic interactions in MoS2. The potential was based on an earlier parameterization for MoS2 developed by Liang45, which can effectively describe the geometric and mechanical properties of Mo and S molecules, as well as the 2D/3D Mo and Mo-S structures. In the corresponding research on MoS2, this potential energy has also been successfully applied46–48.

The planar spiral structure (PSS) model was first minimized to alleviate potential internal stress and then relaxed under the isothermal–isobaric (NPT) ensemble using the Nosé-Hoover thermostat at 1 atm pressure to reach the thermal equilibrium state49. During the minimization and relaxation, periodic boundary conditions were applied along the y-axis directions. The relaxation simulation was performed for a total of 500 ps to allow sufficient time for the system to adjust from the initial configuration to a physically reasonable and energetically stable state, ensuring the reliability of subsequent simulation results.

After energy minimization and relaxation, the central rotating area of the model was rotated with a constant angular velocity ω, causing the graphene on the outer wall to peel off and wrap around the inner rotating area. A small time step of 0.5 fs was adopted for all simulations and all MD simulations were carried out using the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) software50.
