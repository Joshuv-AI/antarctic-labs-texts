Microbiota-directed lactic acid depleting nanoenzyme reactivates antitumor immunity and chemosensitivity in hypoxic tumor
Authors: Xinyue Lan, Fengling Zhang, Guangman Cui, Ben Hu, Yufang Lai, Huimin Lin, Huiqi Huang, Dan Zhou, Meng Yu, Guangyu Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe acidification of the tumor microenvironment (TME) remains a major obstacle contributing to malignant progression and impeding therapeutic development. While traditionally attributed to anaerobic glycolysis, increasing evidence suggests that hypoxia-induced colonization of intratumoral symbiotic microbiota, particularly anaerobes, produce lactic acid (LA) metabolites serves as a significant contributor to TME acidification. Although antibiotic-based combination therapies have been explored for the hypoxic tumor treatment, the efficiency was restricted in reversing acidification-induced immunosuppression and chemoresistance. To tackle this challenge, we engineered a delivery platform (TML NPs) for lactate oxidase (LOX) and chemotherapeutic drug tirapazamine (TPZ) by modifying the carrier with metronidazole (MTZ), an antibiotic bearing hypoxia-responsive functional group. By directly targeting the symbiotic anaerobic bacterial metabolism, this strategy introduces a novel paradigm for modulating TME acidification, reversing the LA-mediated suppression of anti-tumor immune responses and chemosensitivity. Our strategy offers a promising translational platform for the precise treatment of TNBC and other hypoxic malignancies.Keywords: Drug delivery, Lactate metabolism, Nanomedicine, Anaerobic bacteria, Anti-tumor immunity

Introduction

It has been reported that over 16 % of cancer incidence worldwide is associated with microbial infections within tumor tissues [1]. Nejman et al. conducted a comprehensive analysis of the tumor microbiome in 1526 tumors and their adjacent normal tissues, finding that breast tumors had a richer and more diverse microbiome than all other tumor types tested [2]. The intratumoral microbiome, especially anaerobic bacteria and their metabolites, can promote breast cancer progression and desensitize antitumor treatments through various pathways such as impacting host metabolism, inducing inflammatory responses, and inhibiting immune activity [[3], [4], [5]]. Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum), as the most representative tumor-promoting bacterial species, is present in most tumor tissues. F. nucleatum can activate autophagy through innate immune pathways such as Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MYD88), causing immunosuppression [6]. Moreover, tumor tissues rich in F. nucleatum and Lactobacillus exhibit severe chemotherapy resistance. An increasing number of researchers have begun to focus on intervening with the intratumoral microbiome to enhance tumor treatment efficacy, and this approach has gradually progressed to the clinical trial stage [7]. The use of antibiotics or bacteriophages to eliminate intratumoral pathogens has been proven to significantly improve the effectiveness of chemotherapeutic drugs [8,9]. The breast tumor exacerbation by Fusobacterium nucleatum can be effectively counteracted by antibiotic treatment with metronidazole (MTZ) [10].

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), as a prototypical hypoxic tumor, exhibits a more pronounced enrichment of intratumoral symbiotic microbiota compared to other tumor subtypes. Thus, as a key byproduct of both anaerobic bacterial metabolism and tumor cell anaerobic glycolysis, lactic acid (LA) accumulates to a greater extent in the TNBC tumor microenvironment (TME) [11,12]. LA facilitates the proliferation and pathogenicity of anaerobic bacteria through providing metabolic substrates, lowering pH, enhancing biofilm formation, regulating microbial interactions, and suppressing host immune responses [[13], [14], [15]]. The accumulation of LA further reduces the pH value of TME, subsequently inducing tumor cell resistance through mechanisms such as promoting angiogenesis, affecting tumor cell DNA repair capacity, and modulating apoptotic pathways [[16], [17], [18]]. Moreover, tumor acidification driven by elevated LA levels has been shown to directly suppress T cell proliferation and migratory capabilities while reducing NK cell activity and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) production in cancer patients [19,20]. This immunosuppressive effect is partially attributed to the involvement of LA in inhibiting T cell receptor (TCR)-induced signaling cascades, specifically the phosphorylation of JNK, c-Jun, and p38, which are critical regulators of IFN-γ production [21]. In addition, LA stimulates the production of immunosuppressive cytokines such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) [22]. Collectively, the enrichment of symbiotic microbiota within TNBC leads to excessive LA accumulation and contributes to immune suppression through multiple mechanisms, which may underlie the classification of TNBC as an immunologically “cold” tumor.

The LA depletion strategies have emerged as promising adjuvant approaches in combination with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, metabolic therapy, and H2O2-based oxidative therapy [23]. For instance, employing lactate oxidase (LOX) to deplete LA has been shown to effectively disrupt the tumor-promoting effects mediated by intratumoral microbiome, thereby enhancing chemosensitivity and restoring anti-tumor immunogenicity. However, delivering LOX specifically to tumor regions enriched with symbiotic bacterial populations remains a major challenge. As a typical hypoxic tumor, TNBC has been validated to exhibit a strong correlation between hypoxia and the accumulation of intratumoral microbiota [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. This observation highlights the potential of leveraging hypoxia-responsive prodrugs or hypoxia-activated drug delivery systems as a precision strategy for treating microbiota-enriched tumors.

Kulkarni et al. utilized an azobenzene structure as a linker to covalently combine polylactic acid (PLA) with PEG, synthesizing a hypoxia-sensitive copolymer [28]. This novel polymeric system demonstrated precise release of chemotherapeutic drug within the hypoxic TME, facilitating targeted drug delivery and enhanced chemosensitivity in hypoxic tumors. Additionally, the hydrophobic -NO2 group can be selectively reduced to hydrophilic -NH2 group under hypoxic conditions. This property has been widely exploited as a hypoxia-responsive “gatekeeper” on the surface of mesoporous nanocarriers, allowing for controlled drug release in response to hypoxia [29]. Intriguingly, MTZ which contains the -NO2 moiety, is also a potent antimicrobial agent [30]. Its dual functionality offers a unique advantage for the targeted treatment of infections associated with hypoxia-induced bacterial colonization. Considering the mutual feedback promotion between the hypoxic TME and intratumoral microbiota in TNBC tumor tissues, we designed a hypoxia-activated chemotherapeutic drug release system using MTZ as both a hypoxia-responsive gatekeeper and an antimicrobial component. This system is capable of disturbing the metabolic ecology of the intratumoral microbiome, thereby enhancing immune activation and sensitizing tumors to chemotherapy.

Therefore, based on the aforementioned requirements, we have developed a customized treatment system TML NPs against microbiome-symbiotic TNBC (see Scheme 1). This system incorporated both a chemotherapeutic agent and LOX within the mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs), with the nanoparticle surface functionalized with MTZ, which serves a dual role as a hypoxia-responsive gatekeeper and an antimicrobial agent. TML NPs enable the targeted delivery of LOX to hypoxic TNBC tissues and selectively eliminate hypoxia-induced intratumoral pathogenic bacteria, facilitating the localized degradation of LA within tumors, effectively disrupting LA-associated resistance mechanisms. Consequently, this synergistic action disrupted the tumor-promoting feedback loop mediated by intratumoral microbiome, and also blocked microbiome-induced immunosuppressive pathways, thereby restoring TNBC sensitivity to chemotherapy and significantly improving the therapeutic efficacy against microbiome-symbiotic tumors. Building upon conventional chemotherapy, this study introduced a rationally engineered system that addressed resistance mechanisms arising from the complex TME. This approach provided new insights and methods for designing TME-adapted cancer treatment strategies.

Results and discussion

Anaerobic bacteria have a propensity to colonize within breast cancer tissues

An increasing number of studies have noted that dysbiosis in breast tissue supports tumor invasion and reduces therapeutic efficacy [[31], [32], [33]]. In order to further investigate the mechanisms by which microbiota dysbiosis affects tumor progression and treatment outcomes, we have compared three breast cancer patients with varying degrees of malignancy and prognoses (Table S1 in Supporting information). Patient 1, who underwent a full course of chemotherapy prior to surgery, was subsequently identified as triple-negative breast cancer through post-operative immunohistochemical analysis, exhibited a higher malignancy level. During subsequent follow-up, Patient 1 developed brain metastases five months post-surgery, indicating poor prognosis. In contrast, Patient 2 (immunohistochemical analysis was not performed at patient's request) and Patient 3 (positive for ER, PR, and Her-2, indicating a lower malignancy level) did not receive any preoperative treatments, and neither patient required subsequent hospital readmission during the follow-up period. Microorganisms were extracted from the tumor tissues and adjacent normal breast tissues from the three breast cancer patients for a microbial diversity study based on 16 S rRNA gene sequence analysis. After amplifying and analyzing the hypervariable region V3-V4 regions of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene, we characterized the microbial composition in both tumor tissues and healthy breast tissues from each patient. Comparative analyses were performed to identify the differences in microbiota composition between these tissue types.

Fig. 1A demonstrated that both tumor and peritumoral tissues from the 3 patients exhibited high richness and diversity of bacteria, including Actinobacteriota, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria. However, Fig. 1B clearly showed that breast tumors possessed a more abundant and diverse microbiome compared to their adjacent normal breast tissues. PCoA analysis at the OTU level revealed differences in the microbial composition among the various tissue samples (Fig. 1C). Notably, the results from peritumoral tissues performed a closely clustering microbiota, indicating a similar microbial composition in healthy breast tissues. In contrast, the microbiota in tumor tissues was dispersed and exhibited significant differences. This suggested that dysbiosis in the tissues was potentially related to the carcinogenesis of breast tissue.

Therefore, to further explore underlying mechanism how the microbial composition influences cancer progression and treatment prognosis, we conducted predicted metagenomic functional analysis (Fig. 1E). The phenotypic prediction data demonstrated that, compared with the adjacent normal breast tissues from the same patients, breast tumors harbored more anaerobic bacteria, such as Lactobacillales and Bacteroidales (Fig. 1D). Notably, tumor samples from Patient 1, who exhibited poor prognosis, demonstrated markedly elevated proportion of Lactobacillales. This observation was supported by strong clinical associations that the induction of chemotherapy resistance by Lactobacillus iners or LA [34]. Lactobacillus iners not only induces resistance to chemotherapy or radiotherapy in cancer cell lines, but also promotes resistance through increased LA production in the TME, potentially contributing to the patient's unfavorable prognosis. Studies by Lishay et al. and Fu et al. have confirmed that treatment strategies targeting anaerobic bacteria significantly enhanced the therapeutic efficacy against TNBC. Therefore, we propose that eradicating these anaerobic bacteria and eliminating accumulated LA in the TME may represent a crucial approach for enhancing therapeutic outcomes.

Synthesis and characterization of anti-tumor nanomedicine with anti-anaerobic function

Inspired by the enrichment of anaerobic bacteria, specifically LA-producing species and their LA metabolites, in breast tissues and their tumorgenic functions, we engineered a hypoxia-responsive TML nanomedicine, capable of specifically inhibiting intratumoral anaerobic bacteria as well as killing tumor cell. TML nanopariticles were obtained by employing the mesoporous silica nanoparticles (Blank NPs) to carry TPZ and LOX (denoted as T NPs and TL NPs), with outer layer modified by MTZ as a hypoxia-responsive shield (denoted as TML NPs). Initially, Blank NPs were synthesized via hydrolysis of TEOS and TSD, and then cationically functionalized through reactions with APTES, resulting in a porous drug delivery carrier with high drug loading efficiency. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of the nanoparticles, both before (Blank NPs) and after drug loading (TML NPs), revealed spherical shapes with lattice fringes and uniformly coated surfaces (Fig. 2A and B). Meanwhile, organic groups of TSD were homogeneously distributed within the silica layer during the cocondensation process, forming an organic−inorganic hybrid siloxane framework, which resulted in a less compact organic-silica framework nanocomposite compared to its pure Si−O−Si counterpart, contributing to the greater drug loading capability. The pore size distribution and N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms indicated their primarily mesoporous structure (Fig. 2C and Figs. S1–2 in Supporting information). The pore size of the nanoparticles was approximately 7.97 nm, which was conducive to the adsorption and loading of macromolecular LOX into the channels. BET analysis showed a specific surface area of 355.03 m2/g and a pore volume of 0.31 cm3/g. Infrared spectroscopy detection revealed the disappearance of the primary characteristic peak of the surfactant CTAB at 2853 cm−1, indicating successful extraction of CTAB and thereby improving biocompatibility (Fig. S3 in Supporting information).

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements showed that the average particle size of the Blank NPs was 146 nm, while the size of TML NPs was increased, which was contributed by drug adsorption and HA coating (Fig. 2D). The positive surface charge modification by APTES was beneficial for improving the drug loading efficiency of the negatively charged TPZ. Subsequently, the negatively charged TPZ and the positively charged LOX (isoelectric point higher than pH 7.4) were loaded in sequence, and then MTZ (negative) and HA (negative) were used for hypoxia-responsive encapsulation and biocompatibility modification, respectively. The decreased surface charge of TML NPs confirmed the successful completion of each preparation step (Fig. 2E). Meanwhile, the synthesized nanoparticles were stored in PBS (pH 7.4), and the minimal changes in particle size demonstrated the good stability of the TML NPs (Fig. 2F).

After that, UV–vis spectra were recorded to identify the various drugs loaded during the preparation process, confirming the successful synthesis of TML NPs (Fig. 2G). The drug loading efficiency of TPZ and LOX in TML NPs was quantified using UV–vis methods and the BCA Protein Assay Kit, respectively, with values of 11.80 wt% and 6.23 wt%. To validate the potential of MTZ, which serves as both an antibacterial component and a hypoxia-responsive releasing decoration, to facilitate hypoxia-triggered release during treatment, we meticulously recorded the cumulative release patterns of hypoxia-activated prodrug TPZ from TML NPs under both hypoxic and normoxic conditions (Fig. 2H). These observations unveiled a notably accelerated release of TPZ under hypoxic conditions, thereby substantiating the hypoxia-responsive effectiveness of TML NPs. Furthermore, we investigated whether the simultaneously released LOX in this hypoxic environment retained its high enzymatic activity that catalyzing LA to produce H2O2 (Fig. 2I). With the addition of LA substrate in hypoxic culture conditions, the hypoxia-responsive release of LOX from TML NPs demonstrated efficient H2O2 generation capacity, whereas TM NPs without LOX existence did not exhibit similar enzymatic catalytic properties.

In vitro hypoxia-triggered cytotoxicity on 4T1 cell

To investigate whether TML NPs could inhibit tumor growth through the synergistic effects of anti-tumor and anti-anaerobic bacterial activities in microbiota-rich breast cancer tissues, we separately examined its cytotoxic effects on 4T1 breast cancer cell in vitro. 4T1 cell were cultured under hypoxic condition (1 % oxygen) to simulate the breast tumor tissue microenvironment and the cytotoxicity of TML NPs was then assessed using MTT assay, comparing with that under normoxic condition (20 % oxygen). Due to the hypoxia-responsive drug release property of TML NPs and the encapsulation of the hypoxia-activated prodrug TPZ, TML NPs exhibited significantly higher tumor cell killing efficiency under hypoxic condition compared to normoxic condition. This cytotoxicity was notably dependent on TPZ concentration, indicating that TPZ dominantly contributed to tumor killing effects in hypoxic tissues (Fig. 3A). At a TPZ concentration of 4 μg/mL, the cell viability was approximately 50 %. Therefore, this concentration was selected for subsequent cellular experiments. The Blank NPs and TL NPs groups without MTZ did not show obvious differences in cytotoxicity between normoxic and hypoxic environments, further confirming that TPZ is the primary component responsible for cytotoxicity in the TML NPs (Fig. 3B). Moreover, the hypoxia-activated cytotoxicity was more pronounced in TML NPs compared to TL NPs, suggesting that the hypoxia-triggered decoration by MTZ displayed a crucial role in achieving tumor-specific cytotoxicity and preventing premature drug release.

Subsequently, fluorescein FITC-labeled TML NPs was observed using CLSM to evaluate the cellular uptake of nanoparticles by 4T1 cell (Fig. 3C). TML NPs demonstrated intracellular accumulation and cytoplasmic localization that increased with incubation time. As shown in Fig. 3D–F, the cell apoptosis and the live/dead staining results were consistent with the MTT assay findings, highlighting that TPZ required hypoxic condition to be effectively released and exerted chemotherapeutic activity. Notably, under hypoxia-triggered conditions, LOX was also released from the nanocarrier simultaneously with TPZ. The presence of LOX not only converted LA, the metabolic product from tumor cell and introtumoral microbiome, into H2O2, but also further depleted oxygen, exacerbating the hypoxic conditions. Therefore, it was reasonable to speculate that the enhanced hypoxic levels within tumor in turn augmented antitumor activity of TPZ. Concurrently, this characteristic further ensured protection of normal tissues from the adverse effects of this hypoxia-activated chemotherapeutic drug.

Simultaneously, to assess whether the released LOX retained efficient enzymatic activity, we additionally introduced LA substrate in hypoxic conditions and allowed further reaction at 37 °C. By comparing the ability of TM NPs and TML NPs to continuously consume LA over time, we observed that TML NPs could persistently deplete LA within 2 h after its addition, while LOX-absent TM NPs showed no significant changes in LA levels. This revealed that the LOX released from TML NPs in response to hypoxia retained efficient catalytic activity (Fig. 3G). The process of LA consumption by LOX was accompanied by the production of H2O2, which not only counteracted the tumor-promoting effects of LA accumulation but also generated ROS-mediated tumor cell killing activity. These findings explained that the TML NPs, co-loaded with TPZ and LOX, exhibited more potent tumor cytotoxicity compared to nanoparticles loaded with a single drug.

LA depletion potentiates T cell-mediated immune response

LA facilitates tumor immune evasion primarily by impairing T cell function within the TME. Specifically, elevated LA levels disrupt the NAD(H) redox balance in T cell, thereby interfering with their metabolic processes and proliferation, which ultimately leads to suppressed anti-tumor immunity [35,36]. We co-cultured CTLL-2 cell and 4T1 cell under hypoxic environment, measuring the NAD+/NADH ratios and assessing the 4T1 cell apoptosis across different groups. The results indicated that the NAD+/NADH ratio in the TML NPs was higher than that in the Blank NPs (Fig. 3J). This difference can be attributed to the LOX loaded in TML NPs, which effectively consumes LA and prevents its reduction of NAD in T cell, thereby reversing LA-mediated T cell suppression.

Furthermore, under conditions of reduced LA levels by TML NPs, 4T1 cell co-cultured with CTLL-2 cell exhibited significantly increased apoptosis rates, indicating enhanced immune attack by reactivated T cell (Fig. 3I and Fig. S5 in Supporting information). Additionally, western blot analysis revealed a significantly higher level of phosphorylated p38 (p-p38) protein in the TML NPs group compared to the other two groups. This result confirms that TML NPs can reverse the LA-mediated suppression of p38 phosphorylation and enhance CTLL-2 cell function (Fig. 3H and Fig. S4 in Supporting information). Concurrent with LA reduction, significant increases were observed in T cell-secreted immunoactive cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), IFN-γ, interleukin-2 (IL-2), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) (Fig. 3K–N). These findings suggest that TML NPs can significantly enhance T cell-mediated tumor cell killing effect by reducing LA levels in the TME and reversing LA-induced constraints on T cell immune function.

In vivo anticancer efficacy

We further evaluated the in vivo anticancer efficacy of TML NPs on 4T1 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice. All animal experiments were conducted under the guidelines evaluated and approved by the Ethics Committee of Southern Medical University, China. When tumor volume reached 50 mm3, nanomedicine treatment was initiated via tail vein injection of TML NPs (TPZ:3 mg/kg), TM NPs, T NPs, or ML NPs solutions with an administration schedule of one injection every 2 days for 14 days, as illustrated in the experimental design schematic (Fig. 4A). Results revealed that the mono-chemotherapeutic T group exhibited only limited tumor growth suppression, likely due to the metabolites of anaerobic bacteria in hypoxic tissues exacerbating the TME unfavorable for therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 4B–D). Even after incorporating the antibacterial drug MTZ, the TM NPs group did not achieve the desired tumor suppression effect. Furthermore, in the presence of LOX, the TML NPs achieved a potent dual action by concurrently eliminating the bacterial metabolite LA and eradicating anaerobic bacteria. This combined strategy significantly enhanced the chemotherapy efficacy against microbiota-symbiotic tumors, resulting in nearly complete tumor growth suppression and the smallest tumor volume and weight among all groups. In addition, no obvious body weight loss was observed in mice treated with various medications (Fig. 4E). Histological examinations also showed no incidences of abnormalities in major organ tissues (Fig. S6 in Supporting information).

Subsequently, tumor tissues were harvested and subjected to hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and in situ terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) for cellular morphology observation and apoptosis analysis, respectively. According to the H&E histological examination (Fig. 4G), among all the applied treatments, tumor sections treated with TML NPs exhibited the most pronounced classical apoptotic features, including condensed and hyperchromatic nuclei. Furthermore, the TUNEL staining assay confirmed these findings, revealing the most significant increase of apoptotic tumor cell in tissues treated with TML NPs. Additionally, tumor cell proliferation following various treatments was evaluated using Ki-67 immunofluorescence staining. Tumor tissues in the chemotherapy-absent ML NPs group continued to exhibit highly expressed proliferative phenomena while chemotherapy (T NPs) and antibacterial-combined (TM NPs) groups achieved significant tumor inhibition, however they still displayed proliferative tendencies. Only when chemotherapy was combined with antibacterial and LA consumption approaches (TML NPs), there were virtually undetectable Ki67-positive tumor cell, indicating that the complete suppression of tumor progression was achieved only if the intratumoral microbiota-mediated tumor-promoting effect was fundamentally disrupted.

Given that LOX in TML NPs consumes the tumor-supporting bacterial metabolite LA to generate therapeutically beneficial H2O2, a key mechanism for overcoming microbiota-mediated limitations in anti-tumor therapy, we further quantified the LA concentration in tumor tissues post-treatment (Fig. 4F). As anticipated, due to the catalytic activity of LOX, TML NPs and ML NPs showed more effective LA consumption compared to TM NPs and T NPs, which did not contain LOX.

In vivo antitumor immune responses

Modulating the TME via TML NPs treatment elicited significant alterations in systemic immune response. The degradation of LA in the TME alleviated the T cell suppression, synergistically enhancing immunological function. We employed flow cytometry to quantify tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes. As shown in Fig. 5A–B, TML NPs treatment significantly increased the populations of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell in the tumor microenvironment, with a particularly pronounced elevation in CD4+ T cell. Researches have indicated that activated CD4+ T cell potentiate CD8+ T cell responses through multiple mechanisms, promoting their differentiation into cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and reinforcing the anti-tumor responses of CTLs. Furthermore, even in the absence of CD8+ T cell, CD4+ T cell can directly eliminate tumor cell via the IFN-γ-dependent mechanism, as evidenced in Fig. 5D [43]. The collaborative efforts of these two T cell subsets resulted in a potent activation of antitumor immune cytotoxicity, and these findings further corroborated by immunohistochemical analysis of tumor tissues (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, a significant decrease in Foxp3 expression was observed following TML treatment, indicating a weakening of tumor cell immune tolerance and immune evasion mediated by Tregs. This observation also suggested that the enhanced CD4+ T cell infiltration predominantly originates from non-Treg cell.

LA has been reported to suppress the IFN-γ and TNF-α production triggered by TCR activation [35]. Consequently, the depletion of LA from TME results in a significant elevation of these cytokines (Fig. 5D). Also, TML NPs treatment led to substantial increases in serum IL-6, interleukin-18 (IL-18), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) levels, suggesting a multifaceted inflammatory response (Fig. 5E). The targeted elimination of LA reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment by diminishing immunosuppressive signals and triggering the activation of resident immune cell, including macrophages and dendritic cell, which subsequently release pro-inflammatory cytokines [37]. Additionally, the clearance of anaerobic bacteria likely generates microbial-associated molecular patterns, further amplifying immune activation [38].

Transcriptomics analysis reveals TML NPs-mediated antitumor mechanisms

To further understand the molecular mechanisms underlying the TML NPs treatment, tumor tissues from the Control and TML NPs groups were collected for transcriptomics analysis. The analysis identified 2026 differentially expressed genes, including 528 upregulated and 1276 downregulated transcripts. The volcano plot in Fig. 5F showed the significant transcriptional differences between the groups with and without TML NPs. Notable among the upregulated genes was Hsd11b2, which encodes 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2, may reflect adaptive responses to metabolic alterations induced by LA degradation in TME. This enzyme plays a crucial role in regulating glucocorticoid metabolism, and may contribute to immunomodulatory processes by enhancing anti-inflammatory responses during tumor treatment [39]. Similarly, the upregulation of Actn3, encoding alpha-actinin-3, potentially indicates cellular restructuring and immune cell activation following anaerobic bacteria clearance. The involvement of this protein in actin filament interaction and muscle fiber function suggests its role in facilitating immune cell migration and response [40].

Moreover, studies have elucidated the modulation of NOD-like receptors (NLRs) as critical immune sensors for pathogen-associated and damage-associated molecular patterns [41]. The elimination of anaerobic bacteria appears to reconfigure the metabolic environment and microbial composition within TME, thereby triggering NLR activation. This activation cascade potentially initiates downstream inflammatory pathways, leading to enhanced immune cell recruitment and antitumor responses. These findings align with the KEGG pathway analysis results (Fig. 5G), providing mechanistic insights into the observed therapeutic efficacy.

Anti-anaerobic bacteria capability

The anaerobic bacteria colonize in the hypoxic TME of breast tumor tissue, leading to rapid tumor proliferation and treatment insensitivity. Amongst, Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum), a representative anaerobic pathogen in the breast cancer microbiota, has been identified as an important factor accelerating breast cancer tumor growth and metastasis progression. Similarly, Peptostreptococcus anaerobius (P. anaerobius) is also considered to have adverse effects on breast cancer development and prognosis [42]. Therefore, F. nucleatum and P. anaerobius were selected as two representative pathogens to further verify the anti-anaerobic bacterial effect of TML NPs. After co-culturing with TML NPs, TL NPs, M NPs and Blank NPs for 2 h, the growth of these two bacteria on blood agar plate was significantly impacted (Fig. 6A). At the same concentration, M NPs significantly inhibited the growth of both anaerobic bacteria strains, primarily due to the main anti-anaerobic bacterial effect of MTZ. Meanwhile, the MTZ and LOX co-loading TML NPs demonstrated the greatest antibacterial effect, possibly because the LOX in TML NPs achieved anti-anaerobic effect to some extent through the production of H2O2 from LA decomposition. This effect was also observed in the TL NPs loaded with LOX alone.

After 12 h of co-incubation of the employed nanomedicine with bacteria, the antibacterial rate was calculated by directly measuring optical density (OD) at 600 nm (Fig. 6B and C). The MTZ-loading groups TML NPs and M NPs exhibited almost complete inhibition of anaerobic bacteria growth, much more potent than the TL NPs, which showed some discrepancy with the results from the spread plate method. This suggested that during the hypoxia-triggered controlled MTZ release process, the low concentration of MTZ in the initial stage acted synergistically with LOX to exert antibacterial effects. As time progressed, the sustainedly accumulated MTZ continuously exerted highly efficient anti-bacteria effects.

In vivo, after TML NPs treatment, tumor growth in tumor-bearing mice is significantly inhibited, and changes occur in the tumor microenvironment's microbiota as treatment progresses. Analysis of microbial diversity in tumor tissues after treatment reveals that the abundance of various microorganisms in the Control group is higher than that in the TML NPs group (Fig. 6D and E). These microorganisms include Actinobacteria, Bacilli, Gammaproteobacteria, and Alphaproteobacteria. Particularly, the abundance of Actinobacteria in the Control group is notably higher than that in the TML NPs group. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) of tumor microbial diversity in Control and TML NPs groups post-treatment, as shown in Fig. 6F, indicates a considerable distance between the community compositions of the Control and TML NPs groups, suggesting significant differences in their microbial community compositions. The Venn diagram provides a more intuitive display of the similarity and overlap of microbial compositions between the Control and TML NPs groups, as illustrated in Fig. 6G. Both groups enrich 17 common microbial taxa, with the Control group having an additional 37 unique taxa, while the TML NPs group only has 5. The microbial compositions between the Control and TML NPs groups differ significantly, with the microbial diversity in the TML NPs group notably lower than that in the Control group, further confirming the inhibitory effect of TML NPs nanomedicine on the anaerobes within breast cancer tumor tissues (Fig. 6H).

Conclusion

We have developed a convergent nanotherapeutic strategy by targeting the enrichment of anaerobic bacteria in the breast cancer tumor microenvironment, along with its hypoxic and LA accumulation characteristics to conquer the microenvironment-associated therapeutic obstacles. By regulating the properties of mesoporous silica and exploring the self-assembly of hydrophobic drugs, we developed the hypoxia-responsive and tumor-killing integrated nanomedicine TML NPs. We innovatively utilized the hypoxia-responsive and antibacterial properties of MTZ to achieve mesopore blocking and targeted drug release to tumor cell. By achieving precise treatment through hypoxia-controlled drug release, site-specific antibacterial effects, and local activation of TPZ for tumor killing in the tumor microenvironment, we effectively inhibited the growth of breast cancer tumor cell and anaerobic bacteria, providing new insights for breast cancer treatment. By targeting and regulating the microbial community within the tumor microenvironment, we pave the way for a promising research avenue in achieving synergistic and effective anticancer therapies, particularly in the context of breast cancer treatment.
