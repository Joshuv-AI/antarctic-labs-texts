Imaging Insights into Midfoot and Forefoot Disorders
Authors: Sung Hoon Oh, Yun Sun Choi, Hollis G Potter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The forefoot and midfoot consist of various musculoskeletal structures that predispose patients to a wide range of disorders. However, small and complex anatomical features pose challenges for radiological diagnosis and increase the risk of misdiagnosis. This can significantly influence the selection of appropriate treatments and overall prognosis. In this pictorial essay, we review the imaging findings of significant disorders affecting the forefoot and midfoot, along with their relevant anatomical foundations, to provide essential insights for differential diagnosis and support accurate clinical assessment.

INTRODUCTION

The foot is a fundamental structure that supports the body weight and comprises various musculoskeletal components, including bones, muscles, tendons, ligaments, blood vessels, and nerves. It is anatomically divided into three regions, the forefoot, midfoot, and hindfoot, based on the tarsometatarsal (Lisfranc) and transverse midtarsal (Chopart) joints (1). Each of these regions is susceptible to numerous disorders commonly encountered in clinical practice. These conditions often present with pain and deformities, which significantly affect the quality of life of patients by causing gait disturbances. Therefore, ensuring accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment is crucial.

Multimodal imaging techniques such as radiography, CT, MRI, and ultrasonography (US) play key roles in diagnosing foot disorders. However, the forefoot and midfoot have small and complex anatomical structures, which render it challenging to detect and differentiate between pathological lesions. Understanding the common and significant conditions affecting these regions and recognizing their imaging characteristics are essential for accurate diagnosis.

In this review, we explored the imaging findings of prevalent and clinically significant disorders affecting the forefoot and midfoot, along with a concise anatomical context, to provide valuable insights for differential diagnosis and facilitate effective clinical management.

ARTHRITIS

GOUT

Gout is an inflammatory arthritis caused by the abnormal deposition of monosodium urate (MSU) crystals (2). Although the identification of MSU crystals via polarizing microscopy remains the gold standard for diagnosis, it is invasive and infrequently conducted. Instead, imaging modalities such as radiography, US, and dual-energy CT are commonly used to evaluate gout (3).

Gout predominantly affects the metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint, with a strong predilection for the first MTP joint. Typical imaging findings include periarticular soft tissue swelling and juxta-articular erosions, which appear as punched-out erosions with sclerotic margins and overhanging edges. Periarticular osteopenia is typically absent and the joint space is generally well preserved; however, in later stage, joint space narrowing may occur (4).

Gout tophi, resulting from granulomatous inflammation, manifest as hyperdense masses on radiographs and CT scans, with dual-energy CT reliably detecting color-coded MSU crystals. On MRI, gout tophi demonstrate variable signal intensity on T2-weighted images, often appearing with heterogeneous low signal intensity. They demonstrate intermediate signal intensities on T1-weighted images and are frequently enhanced by contrast media (Fig. 1) (5). On US, the deposition of MSU crystals on the surface of the articular cartilage and underlying subchondral bone is visualized as two parallel hyperechoic lines, known as double-contour cartilage sign (6).

RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS (RA)

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is one of the most common systemic inflammatory diseases and is characterized by symmetric involvement and synovitis of the small joints of the hands and feet. The MTP joints in the foot are typically affected early in the disease, with the fifth joint often being the first site of involvement (7).

Synovitis can lead to joint destruction through marginal erosion, cartilage loss, symmetrical joint space narrowing, and joint ankylosis (6). It can also affect the tendons and tendon sheaths surrounding the joints, resulting in tendinopathy and tenosynovitis (6).

Radiography is commonly used to identify joints affected by RA that show signs of marginal erosion, soft tissue swelling, and joint destruction. MRI and US can further help identify synovitis and erosion (Fig. 2). These imaging modalities are particularly useful for the early detection of RA because synovitis can be observed before the onset of erosions (8). Synovial hypertrophy can be detected using US and hyperemia can be evaluated using color and power Doppler US imaging (6). On MRI, subchondral bone marrow edema is often observed in the affected joints and is considered a precursor to erosion (9).

SPONDYLOARTHRITIS

Spondyloarthritis is a type of inflammatory arthritis primarily affecting the spine and peripheral joints. It includes distinct disorders such as ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, and reactive arthritis (10). The key pathological feature of spondyloarthritis is enthesitis, which is inflammation at sites where tendons, ligaments, or joint capsules attach to the bones. This can lead to tendon inflammation, tenosynovitis, and synovitis in the affected joints (11). The heel is the most involved, particularly at the insertion points of the Achilles tendon and plantar fascia; however, enthesitis can also occur in the phalanges (11).

Radiographs may reveal cortical irregularities, entheseal soft tissue calcifications, and bone changes around the entheses such as osteopenia and new bone formation (12). MRI and US can identify synovitis, joint effusions, bone erosions (Fig. 3), and extra-articular findings such as bursitis and tenosynovitis (12). Enthesitis appears as inflammatory soft tissue changes with thickening and intermediate-to-high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, accompanied by diffuse bone marrow edema around the enthesis (812). It may reveal low signal intensity on MRI, making early detection challenging without bone marrow signal changes owing to the fibrous nature of the enthesis (12).

Synovitis seen in spondyloarthritis has an asymmetric joint distribution and concomitant enthesitis, distinguishing it from synovitis observed in RA. Psoriatic arthritis, a well-known form of peripheral spondyloarthritis, is characterized by bone proliferation and erosion, particularly in the distal interphalangeal joints, and is often accompanied by dactylitis (8). Another well-known form of peripheral spondyloarthritis, reactive arthritis, predominantly affects the MTP joints. Reactive arthritis is characterized by periostitis and exhibits less severe erosive changes than psoriatic arthritis (13).

CHARCOT NEUROARTHROPATHY

Charcot neuroarthropathy is a progressive and destructive joint disease that affects patients with neurosensory deficits. Charcot neuroarthropathy have numerous causes, including syringomyelia, alcoholism, and syphilis. Diabetes is the most common etiology and primarily affects the feet and ankles. Charcot neuroarthropathy frequently involves the midfoot, particularly the tarsometatarsal and intertarsal joints (14).

Radiographic and CT findings are stage specific. Features such as osteopenia, fragmentation, and joint subluxation are observed during the development or fragmentation stages (stage 1 in the modified Eichenholtz classification). The absorption of debris, fragment fusion, and sclerosis characterize the coalescence stage (stage 2). In the reconstruction stage (stage 3), remodeling deformities such as joint arthrosis, fibrous ankylosis, and smoothing of bone fragments become evident (Figs. 4, 5) (15). MRI provides detailed visualization of bony abnormalities, including erosion, proliferation, fragmentation, and subluxation (16). In the prodromal stage (stage 0), clinical symptoms such as swelling, erythema, and warmth may be present even when radiographic findings are unremarkable. MRI can detect early changes, such as soft tissue edema and periarticular subchondral marrow edema, enabling earlier diagnosis (Fig. 5) (17).

Osteomyelitis may occur as a superimposed condition and should be suspected if the MRI shows sinus tracts, fluid collection with thick-rim enhancement and diffusion restriction, extensive bone marrow signal abnormalities, joint erosion, or joint fluid enhancement (Table 1) (16). When superimposed infection occurs, the bone marrow exhibits diffusely decreased signal intensity on T1-weighted images, resulting in poorly defined margins that render it difficult to distinguish the marrow from the surrounding tissues. However, the marrow is delineated more clearly on short tau inversion recovery or contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images. This finding is referred to as the “ghost sign,” and it is a useful indicator of superimposed infection (1418). Periarticular marrow signal alterations, soft tissue edema, contrast enhancement, and ulceration can occur in Charcot neuroarthropathy even without superimposed infection (16).

HALLUX RIGIDUS

Hallux rigidus is characterized by osteoarthritis of the first MTP joint accompanied by osteophytes that cause joint stiffness, significant discomfort, and reduced range of motion, particularly during dorsiflexion of the great toe (19). The development of osteoarthritis in the first MTP joint is often associated with a history of trauma, flat or chevron-shaped joint, and hallux valgus deformity (20).

Prominent osteophytes on the dorsal and lateral sides of the first metatarsal head are common in hallux rigidus. Similar to other joints affected by osteoarthritis, the associated changes may include subchondral sclerosis, joint space narrowing, juxta-articular cysts, and sesamoid proliferation (Fig. 6) (19).

The Coughlin and Shurnas classification grades hallux rigidus from 0 to 4 based on the degree of available dorsiflexion of the first MTP joint during physical examination, as well as the severity of osteophytes and joint space narrowing observed on radiographs (21). Grade 0 is defined as no radiographic findings with possible dorsiflexion in the range of 40°–60°. Grade 1 refers to dorsal osteophytes, minimal joint space narrowing, and dorsiflexion of 30°–40°. Grade 2 is characterized by multidirectional osteophytes, mild-to-moderate joint space narrowing and sclerosis, and dorsiflexion between 10° and 30°. Grade 3 involves more than one-fourth of the dorsal joint space on the lateral radiograph, with dorsiflexion possible of 10° or less. Grade 4 refers to the stage in which there are restrictions even with passive motion.

METATARSAL DISORDER

HALLUX VALGUS

Hallux valgus deformity is characterized by the abduction of the MTP joint caused by medial displacement of the first metatarsal head, lateral subluxation of the medial sesamoid, and lateral deviation of the proximal phalanx (Fig. 7) (20). The intrinsic factors contributing to hallux valgus include a long first metatarsal, metatarsal head morphology, and soft tissue imbalance around the MTP joint. Extrinsic factors such as footwear also play a role (22).

The hallux valgus angle is a valuable metric for classifying the severity of the deformity. This angle is measured between the long axes of the first metatarsal and the first proximal phalanx on weight-bearing foot anteroposterior radiograph (Fig. 7A). A hallux valgus angle of 15° or less is considered normal. Conversely, angles less than 20° are classified as mild, 40° or less as moderate, and greater than 40° as severe (23). Hallux valgus can also be diagnosed if the intermetatarsal angle is greater than 9° (Fig. 7B), or if the distal metatarsal articular angle is greater than 10° (Fig. 7C) (23). Hallux valgus can be categorized into four grades based on the position of the medial sesamoid relative to the long axis of the first metatarsal (Table 3) (19).

MORTON’S NEUROMA

Morton’s neuroma, also known as an intermetatarsal neuroma, is a focal fusiform thickening of the common plantar digital nerve that results from perineural fibrosis and neural degeneration (24). It occurs more frequently in middle-aged women, with the third intermetatarsal space being most commonly affected, followed by the second intermetatarsal space (2425). One or both feet can be affected by multiple Morton’s neuromas simultaneously (24).

The average diameter of the plantar digital nerve at the level of the metatarsal head is 1–2 mm, which renders visualization using US challenging. However, Morton’s neuroma appears on US as a well-defined hypoechoic ovoid or fusiform mass in the intermetatarsal space (Fig. 8). During a dynamic US examination, if the examiner squeezes the foot at the level of the metatarsal head while scanning the plantar surface, a hypoechoic mass corresponding to a Morton’s neuroma is displaced toward the plantar side. This finding is known as sonographic Mulder sign (26). MRI presents as an ovoid or dumbbell-shaped lesion with intermediate-to-low signal intensity on T1-weighted and T2-weighted images, often demonstrating varying degrees of contrast enhancement (242527). Fluid-sensitive sequences effectively differentiate Morton’s neuroma from bursitis or ganglion cysts, which typically demonstrate a high signal intensity (Fig. 8) (27).

Lesions larger than 5 mm are widely recognized to be highly associated with symptomatic presentations. However, this remains controversial as some studies suggest that lesions <5 mm can cause symptoms (2728).

INTERMETATARSAL BURSITIS

Normally, there is little to no fluid in the intermetatarsal bursa. However, fluid accumulation increases when the bursa becomes inflamed, forming a cystic structure that causes pain (29). Intermetatarsal bursitis appears as a compressible, anechoic, or hypoechoic lesion in the intermetatarsal space on US (29). On MRI, intermetatarsal bursitis presents as a well-defined fluid collection with low signal intensity on T1-weighted images, high signal intensity on fluid-sensitive sequences, and peripheral enhancement on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images (Fig. 8) (2529). Physiological fluid collection may be observed in the first three intermetatarsal bursae with a transverse diameter of 3 mm or less (30).

The intermetatarsal bursa is located dorsal to the deep transverse intermetatarsal ligament, and the digital neurovascular bundle is positioned on the plantar side of the ligament. The epicenter of the lesion can aid in differentiation, although ultrasonographic features are often very similar to those of Morton’s neuroma, making diagnosis challenging (29). Furthermore, the absence of the sonographic Mulder sign during dynamic US and the compressibility of the lesion are distinguishing features of intermetatarsal bursitis (29).

OSSEOUS DISORDER

STRESS FRACTURE

Individuals subjected to repetitive mechanical stress on their feet, such as soldiers and runners, may develop fatigue fractures. Contrarily, patients with osteoporosis may experience insufficient fractures even with minimal external forces (31). Stress fractures are commonly observed in the distal shafts of the second, third, and fourth metatarsal bones (25).

On MRI, stress fractures are typically observed as lines of low signal intensity running perpendicular to the cortical surface and connecting to the cortex (32). These fractures are often accompanied by periosteal edema and endosteal bone marrow changes, which appear as high signal intensities on fluid-sensitive sequences (Fig. 9) (32). Subchondral stress fractures can also occur in the metatarsal head, presenting with subchondral marrow edema, sclerosis, and flattening (3334). Stress fractures of the navicular bone typically occur in the middle third of the proximal dorsal margin where high mechanical stress is concentrated. These fractures are visible on the radiographs and CT scans as incomplete linear fractures (Fig. 10) (35).

ACCESSORY NAVICULAR SYNDROME

The most common accessory ossicle of the foot is the accessory navicular bone, which is classified into three types. Type 2 is the most prevalent form, characterized by a triangular secondary ossification center that forms a synchondrosis with the navicular tuberosity (36). In type 2 accessory navicular bone, attachment of the posterior tibialis tendon to the accessory ossicle can create shear stress on the synchondroses during repetitive weight-bearing activities. This stress can lead to cartilage proliferation, bone remodeling, inflammatory changes, and osteonecrosis, resulting in a painful condition known as accessory navicular syndrome (37).

On MRI, accessory navicular syndrome shows bone marrow edema in both the accessory navicular bone and navicular tuberosity, high signal intensity changes in the synchondrosis and surrounding soft tissue on fat-suppressed T2-weighted imaging, and synchondrosis widening (Fig. 11). Associated findings include tendinosis, tendon tears, tenosynovitis of the posterior tibialis tendon, and a fibrocartilage mass between the tendon and the accessory navicular bone (36).

FREIBERG INFRACTION

Freiberg infraction, also known as Freiberg disease, is a type of osteochondrosis characterized by adolescent metatarsal head necrosis. The etiology of Freiberg infraction is not clearly understood; however, various factors have been suggested, including repetitive microtrauma, vascular compromise, and genetic or altered biomechanics (25). It most commonly affects the second and third metatarsal heads and causes pain, swelling, and limited motion of the MTP joint (25). Although it primarily occurs in adolescent, its imaging finding is similar to that of subchondral stress fracture in adult (33).

Smillie classified the progression of Freiberg infraction into five stages, and these changes can be observed on radiographs (38). On radiographs, the ischemic epiphysis initially exhibits a linear fracture line or fissure (Smillie classification stage 1), followed by bone resorption (stage 2), and central subchondral bone collapse (stage 3). Advanced stages demonstrate separate osteochondral loose bodies (stage 4), metatarsal head flattening, and eventual arthrosis (stage 5) (Fig. 12). MRI enables early detection, showing low- and high-signal intensities on T1- and T2-weighted images, respectively, before changes become evident on radiographs (2529).

MÜELLER–WEISS SYNDROME

Müeller–Weiss syndrome is characterized by spontaneous osteonecrosis of the navicular bone in adults. This disorder typically occurs bilaterally in women aged 40–60 years and presents as chronic midfoot pain and deformities (39). Although the precise etiology remains unknown, biomechanical factors such as subtalar varus, which causes uneven compressive forces on the lateral side, delayed navicular ossification, and vascular compromise are associated with the development of Müeller–Weiss syndrome (4041).

Radiography shows lateral subluxation of the talar head, sclerosis of the navicular bone, and collapse of the lateral side, resulting in a characteristic comma-shaped deformity. Disease progression causes fragmentation and dorsal extrusion of the lateral aspect of the navicular, accompanied by talocuneiform neoarticulation (Fig. 13). Maceira and Rochera described five stages based on the morphology of the navicular and Meary–Tomeno angle, which is formed by the longitudinal axes of the talus and first metatarsal as seen on a lateral weight-bearing radiograph (41). In stage 1, the radiograph appears normal or shows a subtle lateral displacement of the talar head. Stage 2 is characterized by a subtalar varus deformity with dorsal angulation of the Meary–Tomeno line. In stage 3, the navicular shows compression or fragmentation, the foot has a low arch, and the Meary–Tomeno line remains neutral. Stage 4 includes further compression of the navicular, loss of the longitudinal arch, hindfoot equinization, and plantar angulation of the Meary–Tomeno line. Stage 5 is characterized by complete extrusion of the fragmented navicular and formation of talocuneiform neoarticulation. In the early stages, when radiographic findings appear normal or nearly normal, MRI can detect bone marrow edema and early arthritic changes (39).

Müeller–Weiss syndrome is distinct from Köhler disease in that the latter it occurs in children and eventually causes normal ossification. In contrast, Müeller–Weiss syndrome affects adults, with deformities progressing over time.

KÖHLER DISEASE

Köhler disease is a type of osteochondrosis characterized by navicular bone osteonecrosis in children. It typically affects boys aging 2–9 years old. Midfoot pain gradually develops and worsens during physical activity (42).

The navicular bone develops from a single ossification center around 3 years of age and gradually forms a boat-shaped structure. In most patients with Köhler disease, the navicular bone shows severe flattening and increased density. Over time, the remaining navicular bone undergoes reorganization, eventually leading to normal ossification (Fig. 14). In rare cases, the navicular bone diffusely increases in density, followed by partial absorption and the appearance of multiple ossification centers, resulting in bone remodeling (4243).

TENDON AND LIGAMENT INJURY

TURF TOE

The medial and lateral sesamoids are observed on the plantar side of the first MTP joint. They are connected by the intersesamoid ligament, whereas paired sesamoid phalangeal ligaments connect each sesamoid bone to the proximal phalangeal base. Paired metatarsosesamoid ligaments link the sesamoid bones to the metatarsal head-neck junction. The plantar plate of the first MTP joint includes these ligaments, as well as the central portion, which is a fibrous component originating from the volar side of the first metatarsal neck and inserting into the central portion of the proximal phalangeal base. These ligaments and the plantar capsule are collectively referred to as capsuloligamentous complexes (Fig. 15A) (20).

Injury to the plantar aspect of the MTP joint of the great toe is known as turf toe and results from extreme hyperextension or varus or valgus angulation of the great toe (2044). Turf toe is classified into three grades based on the severity of the injury. Grade 1 refers to mild sprains with minimal damage to plantar structures. Grade 2 refers to partial tears with a moderate disruption of the capsuloligamentous complex. Grade 3 involves full-thickness tears resulting in unstable joint conditions such as sesamoid fractures or diastasis (Fig. 15B-D) (45).

PLANTAR PLATE INJURY IN THE LESSER TOES

The plantar plate is a fibrocartilaginous structure located on the plantar aspect of the MTP and interphalangeal joints and plays a key role in preventing hyperextension of these joints. The second MTP joint is the most common site of plantar plate injuries (46). Such injuries can cause forefoot pain and lead to deformities, including dorsal dislocation, flexor tendon subluxation, and crossover toe (47).

The normal plantar plate appears as a homogeneous, mildly echogenic, curvilinear structure on US, whereas on proton density and T2-weighted MRI, it appears as a low signal intensity curvilinear structure (4748). Injuries to the plantar plate are visualized on US as anechoic or hypoechoic defects or diffuse thinning. These injuries are more prominent during toe dorsiflexion in the dynamic studies. On MRI, a torn plantar plate appears as a partial or complete discontinuity, with a tear gap showing high signal intensity. Complete ruptures cause retraction of the plantar plate and are best visualized in the sagittal plane (Fig. 16) (47).

Cartilage interface sign and pericapsular fibrosis are indirect findings of plantar plate injury (4748). On US, the cartilage interface sign is a curvilinear hyperechoic interface at the articular cartilage-fluid junction in plantar plate injuries caused by acoustic impedance differences (Fig. 16C) (48). Pericapsular fibrosis appears as a well-defined hypoechoic soft tissue lesion on US and as an intermediate signal intensity lesion on T1- and T2-weighted MRI (4748). Fibrosis attached eccentrically to a torn plantar plate can be differentiated from intermetatarsal space lesions such as Morton’s neuroma (49).

LISFRANC LIGAMENT INJURY

The Lisfranc joint complex comprises the bony and non-bony elements of the tarsometatarsal, intertarsal, and intermetatarsal joints. The intermetatarsal ligaments connect the metatarsal bases in the intermetatarsal space. However, there is no interosseous ligament between the first and second metatarsal bases. The only ligaments connecting the medial cuneiform (C1) to the second metatarsal base (M2) are the dorsal tarsometatarsal, interosseous tarsometatarsal (commonly known as the Lisfranc ligament proper), and plantar tarsometatarsal ligaments, which run obliquely and play critical roles in stabilizing the first and second metatarsals (Fig. 17A) (50).

In healthy individuals, an anteroposterior weight-bearing radiograph shows the lateral border of the first metatarsal base (M1) aligned with the lateral border of the medial cuneiform (C1) and the medial border of the second metatarsal base (M2) aligned with the medial border of the intermediate cuneiform (C2). The distance between C1 and M2 is <2 mm. Lateral displacement of the second metatarsal base may occur in cases of ligament injury, including a Lisfranc ligament injury. This is evident on anteroposterior radiographs as a step-off at the medial margin of the second metatarsal base and the intermediate cuneiform with an increased distance (>2 mm) between the second metatarsal base and the medial cuneiform (Fig. 17B) (51). A pathognomonic finding of Lisfranc ligament avulsion fractures, known as the fleck sign, may also be observed on radiography. This sign is a chip fracture fragment between the medial cuneiform and second metatarsal base (5152). On MRI, the Lisfranc ligament normally appears as a structure with homogeneous low-to-intermediate signal intensity, often exhibiting a striated appearance (50). Tarsometatarsal and intermetatarsal ligament injuries may manifest as complete or partial ligament disruption, ligament elongation, or edema of the surrounding soft tissue. These findings are best visualized on short-axis (coronal) and long-axis (axial) MRI (Fig. 17C, D) (51).

CONCLUSION

The forefoot and midfoot are small and complex anatomical structures that make it difficult to diagnose disorders in these regions. By thoroughly understanding and recognizing the imaging characteristics associated with prevalent and clinically significant midfoot and forefoot disorders, radiologists can play a pivotal role in facilitating the early and accurate diagnosis of these conditions, thereby contributing to timely and effective management strategies.
