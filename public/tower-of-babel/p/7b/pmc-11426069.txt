Building Public Health Workforce Capacity: Longer-Term Effectiveness of a Capacity Building Intervention to Improve Community-Based Public Health Prevention Work
Authors: Donna Leong, Jeanne W Lawless, Cheyanna Frost, Genevive R Meredith
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThere is an urgent need to build capacity among existing and incoming public health workers to enhance community-based work focused on prevention and health promotion. Public Health Essentials, a cohort-based facilitated asynchronous online capacity building intervention, was designed to build public health workers’ strategic skills and professional confidence. Earlier research reported on the short-term learning outcomes of the intervention; in this paper, the authors report on results from a longer-term outcomes evaluation, including skill retention, skill application, and possible indicators of workforce retention and community health improvement.MethodsA sequential mixed-methods research design was used to assess and explore longer-term outcomes among a sample of Public Health Essentials graduates working in community public health roles.ResultsSome 46% of eligible Public Health Essentials graduates (n=70) completed a skills survey at 3 time points: before Public Health Essentials completion, after Public Health Essentials completion, and 3–6 months after Public Health Essentials completion. Longitudinal analysis of responses showed statistically significant skill gains over baseline (beginner → proficient, p<0.005), despite a slight drop in self-perceived ability 3–6 months after Public Health Essentials completion. Qualitative interviews with respondents and focus groups with their supervisors suggest that Public Health Essentials completion benefits participants’ everyday work by developing strategic skills and grounding their work in shared public health language, paradigms, and values. Broader reported benefits include the ability to apply new skills to community public health prevention projects and expressed motivation to continue to work in public health.ConclusionsSuccessful completion of Public Health Essentials provides learners with durable skills applicable to public health work and may spur interest in continuing to work in the field of public health. The importance of developing and retaining a competent community-focused public health workforce cannot be overstated. Comprehensive-facilitated, online asynchronous capacity building programs may be an effective tool.Keywords: Public health, Public Health Workforce Development, Public Health Workforce Capacity Building, Training Outcomes Evaluation

INTRODUCTION

More than $4 trillion dollars are expended on health care in the U.S. each year1; however, excess deaths and significant disparities by myriad demographics persist.2,3 In 2017, the American College of Preventive Medicine Policy Committee recommended increased funding for the public health workforce,4 noting that “investment in…evidence-based community prevention programs can offer a…5-to-1 return in decreasing costs associate with targeted morbidity.”5 The benefits of public health work has been well documented,6 particularly in comparison with the high cost of U.S. curative health expenditures.7

The field of public health works to promote health and wellbeing through prevention. To implement community prevention programs, a well-equipped public health workforce serving in both governmental (federal, state, tribal, territorial, county, city) and community (e.g., community-based organizations, healthcare settings, schools) roles is required.8, 9, 10 However, the U.S. public health system has been under-resourced for decades, limiting the number of workers and their capacity.9,11, 12, 13 For example, current estimates suggest that at least 80,000 additional full-time government public health workers are needed to conduct core public health activities alone,9 and longitudinal research shows that the current government public health workforce has skills gaps,9,14,15 in part because only ∼14% of the governmental public health workforce has formal public health training; data are not available for nongovernment workers.16

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) public health emergency showed the risks of underinvestments in public health systems.11,13 To take steps toward heath as a human right, the public health sector needs more workers, the workers need more skills, and skilled workers need to be retained,8,17 in part by increasing workers’ sense of satisfaction and opportunities for growth and advancement.14,16,18 As an alternative to enrolling in formal public health education (e.g., earning a BSPH or MPH), many public health trainings and capacity building initiatives have been developed and deployed to support real-time workforce development, including by federally funded Public Health Training Centers and the National Network of Public Health Institutes. To support broader access, many trainings are available through the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's training site, CDC TRAIN. There is, however, a dearth of data reporting on what comes as a result of these initiatives, particularly changes in public health workers skills, abilities, or confidence to be a part of public health actions to prevent harm and promote health equity or changes in intention to remain working in the field of public health.

Intervention Description

To help upskill the public health workforce amidst and beyond the COVID-19 emergency,12,13 Cornell University's Public Health Program developed and launched a comprehensive just-in-time capacity building intervention in 2021: Public Health Essentials (PHE). PHE aims to rapidly orient people working in new public health roles, equipping them with the knowledge and applied skills needed for foundational public health work.19 The theorized longer-term impact is improved public health outcomes through community-based prevention work supported by an expanded, interdisciplinary, competent workforce (Figure 1).19

Public Health Essentials: Learning to action for community health impacts.

PHE is an online facilitated asynchronous training program that results in a certificate.19 To build comprehensive capacity, the program comprises 75 hours of learning across 18 modules, which are organized under 5 topic units. Course content and graded assignments are tagged to 54 specific learning outcomes aligned with the public health strategic skills (Appendix File 1, available online).19 To accommodate many learners, different time zones, and different work/life pressures, PHE is delivered online and is accessible 24 hours per day, 7 days per week. Over a 15–20-week period, learners build proficiency through 266 engaged learning elements, including videos, readings, challenge tasks, and the completion and submission of written assignments. Expert public heath mentors facilitate learning in the asynchronous environment through discussion boards, by providing competence assessment against 54 specific learning outcomes through graded assignments and feedback for improvement, and via optional live office hours.19 In addition, to assess changes as a result of PHE-driven learning, a skills survey, adapted from both the Public Health Workforce Interests and Needs Survey tool20 and the Midwestern Public Health Training Center workforce survey tool,21 is integrated into the training as a required assignment at 2 time points.19 The survey captures participants’ demographics and their self-assessed proficiency in core competency areas using an ordinal scale ranging from 1 (unable to perform) to 4 (expert) at 2 time points: before PHE training and after PHE training. A more detailed description of its curriculum and design are provided in a previously published article.19

Learners enroll in PHE through employer sponsorship. Employers cover all or part of the course costs and are encouraged to help their employees carve out time during the workday (up to 5 hours per week) to complete the course. As previously reported, from September 2021 to December 2022, >500 people completed PHE, and 321 completed both the pre-PHE and post-PHE surveys; these showed that graduates had statistically significant increases in self-assessed abilities across all public health strategic skills domains covered.19 A majority also reported gaining new knowledge and skills and reported applying these to their work to benefit their team and organization.19 Although this prior research was helpful in understanding the shorter-term outcomes of PHE, the implementation team wondered whether or how PHE may be leading to longer-term benefits; this study attempts to answer that question.

Evaluation Focus

Pedagogical research suggests that effective trainings help learners improve their capacity, enabling application in the workplace.22, 23, 24 However, pedagogical research also shows that learning may not be retained over time.25,26 Evaluating the outcomes of trainings at multiple time points elucidates benefits and limitations of trainings.22,23,27,28 Thus, to evaluate the longer-term efficacy of PHE,19 the authors explored the following 3 questions aligned with the Kirkpatrick Model of Training Evaluation29,30:

METHODS

A sequential mixed-methods study was used to assess and explore longer-term outcomes resulting from the PHE intervention (IRB exempt: Cornell University Protocol Number 0147246). Data were collected through a standard skill survey and interviews with PHE graduates; focus groups were also held with workplace supervisors of graduates. Inclusion criteria for graduates were (1) completion of PHE, (2) completion of both the pre- and post-PHE skills survey embedded in the online training, and (3) completed PHE between 3 and 6 months ago; inclusion criteria for supervisors were those who supervised 2 or more PHE graduates.

To assess retention of PHE-driven learning, the authors administered the standard skills survey mentioned earlier (Appendix File 1, available online) using Qualtrics (Qualtrics Software 2023) a third time to the PHE completers who opted in. The authors distributed this follow-up survey by email to eligible graduates (n=153) 3–6 months after PHE completion. Again, the survey gathered self-assessed proficiency in core competency areas using an ordinal scale ranging from 1 (unable to perform) to 4 (expert). Follow-up survey participants were offered modest gift card incentives ($5). Some 46% (n=70) responded, documenting informed consent and completing the survey.

Responses from the follow-up survey were matched longitudinally at the individual level to both the pre-PHE and post-PHE results; mean scores for each of the 9 domains were calculated at each time point. Changes in competence over time were calculated using a fixed-effects regression model in Stata (Version 18), assessing the differences in self-reported skills means within the domains at the different time points. This analysis shows within-person effects over time.

Survey respondents were invited to indicate interest in participating in a 30-minute key informant interview to gather richer, context-specific data to elucidate how participants integrate PHE-related knowledge and skills into public health work and the factors related to these behavior changes. Interviewees were offered modest gift card incentives ($20) for their time. A total of 39 respondents opted in, and a purposive sample of 12 graduates was chosen, sampled for varied education and public health experience.31 To supplement input from PHE graduates, and to explore how graduates integrate and apply PHE-supported abilities into their public health work, focus groups were conducted with supervisors. Invitations to participate in the focus groups were sent to supervisors who oversaw the work of 2 or more PHE graduates (n=50); participants were offered modest gift card incentives ($40) for their time. Fifteen supervisors self-selected to participate by documenting their informed consent. Three 1-hour focus groups of 5 supervisors each were conducted.32

Interview and focus group protocols (Appendix File 2, available online) were piloted by the research team and edited for clarity prior to launch. Interviews and focus groups were administered and recorded online through Zoom, Version 5.15.7 (Zoom Video Communication). Transcripts were generated through Rev.com (Rev Transcription Services) and were then cleaned and coded for themes by a team of 3 qualitative researchers using a shared codebook and Dedoose, Version 9.0.46. To improve reliability, the investigator triangulation method was used whereby the individual results of the coding were discussed until consensus was reached.

RESULTS

Demographics

A total of 153 PHE graduates were invited to participate in this study through email. Seventy respondents (46%) completed the follow-up survey. Of those, most respondents were female (n=57, 81%), and over half (n=43, 61%) had earned a master's degree or higher (Table 1); 36% of respondents (n=25) reported having a public health degree (bachelors, masters, or doctoral). Most participants (n=46, 66%) reported having between 0 and 2 years of experience working in public health at the start of PHE. Participants were based mostly in New York State County public health departments (n=51, 73%), New York State community-based organizations (n=9, 13%), and community-based organizations in other states (n=10, 14%), including Wisconsin, Michigan, and Arkansas.

Competence Gains and Retention

At baseline (before PHE), participants reported low self-assessed competence across all areas assessed (2.5 mean of means: beginner) (Table 2). After completing PHE, participants exhibited a statistically significant increase in mean competence scores across all areas assessed in the post-test compared with the pretest scores (3.4 mean of means: proficient). When reassessed 3–6 months after completion in the follow-up survey, participants reported a slight drop in their self-assessed ability from their post-training test scores (3.0 mean of means: proficient). Those with public health degrees reported slightly higher overall mean competence at this time point than those without public health degrees (3.1 vs 3.0 mean of means). Learners with public health degrees reported the largest competence backslide in the area of policy engagement (3.4 post-test, 2.6 follow-up); those without public health degrees reported the largest backslide in data for decision making (3.4 post-test, 2.8 follow-up) and policy engagement (3.2 post-test, 2.6 follow-up). Despite these slips, the fixed effects model analysis showed a statistically significant increase in competence over precourse levels across almost all domains (p<0.005); exceptions, which were not statistically significant when comparing follow-up with pretest means, were: (1) specialized skills for those without a public health degree and (2) data for decision making and policy engagement for those with a public health degree (Table 2).

Behavior Change

Both interviewees and supervisors noted several ways that knowledge and skills developed through PHE influenced graduates’ everyday work. Themes that were noted included having shared language; being grounded in public health paradigms and values; having specific, tangible skills; and being able to talk meaningfully about public health.

Speaking the shared language of public health. Interviewees stated that after completing PHE, they had a much firmer grasp of key concepts and nuances of the public health field (Table 3). Through the course lessons and materials, they were exposed to fundamental components of public health and acquired the language to discuss such topics. This built interviewees’ confidence because they could understand concepts being discussed at work and join in and contribute to conversations. In addition, interviewees felt able to effectively communicate with the public about core public health concepts, by translating the technical language into easily understood messaging. Supervisors noted that their employees who took PHE often had a better grasp of public health terminology than other employees who had been at the organization for longer but had not taken PHE.

Broadening the paradigm of public health work. Interviewees stated that PHE helped them appreciate how vast the field of public health is and how their work fits into the mission of public health (Table 3). Several interviewees stated that their previous public health experience was constrained to specific topics (e.g., nutrition education, substance use prevention), which provided a siloed view of the field. Interviewees noted that the PHE modules offered an overview of the different functions that comprise public health, from epidemiology to emergency preparedness. As a result, they stated that they understood how different issues within public health are interconnected. Accordingly, they noted that they can see how their role contributes to the larger mission of public health. Supervisors noted that employees often silo their skillsets into a particular view of their role (e.g., only being able to provide nutrition education services) but observed that PHE helped participants to expand those horizons and see the skillsets as transferrable to other public health roles.

Understanding how public health frameworks shape the values of their work. Interviewees stated that PHE helped them become acquainted with different frameworks within public health that they then saw applied in their daily work (Table 3). They specifically identified the social determinants of health (SDoH) and socioecologic models as frameworks that they needed to include in PHE-graded projects, which now guide their day-to-day work. Many interviewees stated that prior to PHE, they were unfamiliar with these frameworks and unable to articulate the values driving the projects they worked on. Supervisors noted that they observed a shift in thinking and action by PHE participants as they engaged in applied learning about SDoH: they started to understand how the programs they work on apply to or integrate SDoH foundational interventional framework.

Honing public health skills. Interviewees stated that PHE helped them to build skills. During the interviews, the following 2 skills emerged as particularly beneficial to interviewees’ everyday work (Table 3):

Data for decision making. Interviewees stated that the data skills they gained in PHE are invaluable to their day-to-day work. PHE provided them an overview of how public health data are collected, how they are interpreted, and how to use the data for community health planning needs. This helped them feel more confident in the data they use for projects, including community health improvement plans. Supervisors noted that PHE graduates often teach other colleagues how to find, scrutinize, and use data.

Community engagement. Interviewees stated that they had increased confidence in working with community members to build the trust needed for long-term impact. Through PHE, they recalled learning about community-centered design and how to support communities and build trust in public health messaging. Several interviewees shared that they integrate community engagement in their public health roles and stated feeling more confident in their abilities to work with community members.

Interpersonal knowledge transfer. Interviewees also noted that taking PHE and engaging with their learning cohort opened their eyes to interdisciplinary public health information and built their confidence to discuss public health topics with colleagues and friends. Several interviewees noted how the knowledge they gained was fun to share with others and that this unprompted interpersonal knowledge transfer reinforced their personal and professional interest in public health.

Projects that improve community health outcomes. Interviewees reported that with their new skills, they have been able to participate in several projects (Table 4). Two categories of projects were observed: contributing their new knowledge to projects that were already underway (e.g., writing training criteria for community health workers, identifying health equity gaps in community health needs assessments), and/or spearheading new projects using what they learned/developed through PHE (e.g., developing messaging for winter storm preparedness, creating population-specific messaging about the dangers of vaping). Supervisors noted that the expanded ideas and capacity PHE learners bring to the workplace has encouraged their team to expand approaches and apply for grants.

Augmenting public health workforce capacity. Interviewees reported that they see themselves making a difference within the public health sector (Table 4). Many interviewees stated that they plan to stay within the field of public health and have noted ways that they are actively pursuing new opportunities to continue to develop skills in public health. Some have enrolled in new training programs, whereas others have applied their skills to new roles or applied for new positions within public health.

DISCUSSION

Trainings and capacity building interventions are important strategies to reinforce the U.S. public health workforce, building skills and confidence in key competency areas.33 Many training modalities exist for public health workers, but limited data are available on the resulting longer-term outcomes. The authors add to the literature, reporting on outcomes from 1 capacity building intervention, PHE, designed for new and existing public health workers,19 and encourage other researchers to do the same. Effective, cost effective, and scalable evidence-informed solutions are needed to reinforce the U.S. public health workforce.

Building Knowledge, Skills, Competence

Foundational public health knowledge and skills contribute to the core competencies of public health.34 Few working in the field of public health have formal public health training14,35; thus, opening doors to professional development training—as an alternative to degree-granting education—is valuable in responding to worker and workforce demands.12,36 Prior research showed that PHE is effective in increasing public health competence in strategic skill domains, regardless of background.19 This study shows that statistically significant gains in all areas are maintained months after completion (Table 2), suggesting that facilitated, online asynchronous training can be effective in building the skills needed by the public health workforce.10,14 Although there was a slight drop in mean of means between the post-PHE skills survey and the follow-up survey, literature suggests that if learners do not actively use a skill or if learners recalibrate their understanding of a skill owing to ongoing work, self-assessment might decrease as they identify new ways to grow.26 For example, although the follow-up survey data showed a decline in self-assessed proficiency for the data for decision making and community engagement skills, interviewees provided numerous specific examples of how they put those competencies into practice in their work, corroborated by the supervisor focus groups.

Although not tested in this study, future research might explore the influence of engagement and practice on self-assessed skill level over time. For example, each module of PHE is designed around specific learning outcomes/performance competencies. Learners are introduced to the focus, learn related knowledge, practice related skills, and receive feedback on a competency-specific assignment. PHE is designed to build ability across 9 strategic skill domains; introduction of competencies and scaffolded competency development through applied activities and feedback is at the core of competence-based education. The competency gains measured at the pre, post, and follow-up surveys are perhaps not surprising given the intentional design of PHE. Similarly, after completing PHE, if those same skills are not used or practiced, ability and self-confidence might wane. For example, learners reported a big increase in skill and confidence related to data for decision making and community engagement as a result of PHE, and interviewees and supervisors corroborated this by describing behaviors and actions being taken. However, these are 2 areas that showed as large decline in self-assessed competence in the 3–6 months after PHE completion in the follow-up survey. Future research could explore the reasons for this discordance and examine methods to support competence retention after training, such as hosting communities of practice, live discussions, ongoing minitrainings, webinars, or other methods.

Translating Learning Into Action

Training interventions typically aim to build knowledge and skills, and learners can be assisted in translating gains into actions through engaged and applied/action learning approaches.12,23,24,26,37 This approach is critical when upskilling cadres of workers to fill critical public health roles.12,19,37 PHE was specifically designed for this: to help learners translate learning into action through hands-on and applied assignments, complemented with facilitator feedback, aiming to empower learners with confidence.19 This study shows that 3–6 months after completing the 4-month training, graduates were able to describe specific contributions of their participation in PHE to their work and how the intervention developed their abilities to engage further in public health. In addition, supervisors noted that PHE graduates had a better grasp of the language and framework of public health than other new employees.

At inception, PHE was designed to build just-in-time skills to support the COVID-19 emergency response, while also ensuring deep focus on foundational public health frameworks and cross-cutting skills so that graduates could take on non–COVID-19–related roles and contribute to a stronger prospective public health workforce,19 providing low-barrier access training to support a robust workforce. Supervisors noted that that was an effective way for their employees to develop a fuller appreciation of their public health role, to understand the nuances of the public health sector, and to embody the mission of the local/state health departments and community-based organizations working in public health. Participants themselves noted that skills acquired through PHE, including working across silos, improving community engagement, and better use of public health data in their decision making, increased their workplace competence and enabled them to apply skills to projects that improve public health outcomes. Importantly, several participants noted that participating in PHE opened their eyes to the breadth of possible roles in the field of public health, which encouraged them to remain in the field and pursue further public health education or credentialing. This may indicate that employer investments in this mode of capacity building—particularly one that focuses on broad and foundational cross-cutting skills—might pave a pathway to employee retention.

Worker Satisfaction and Workforce Retention

An a priori projected long-term outcome related to the PHE intervention is worker satisfaction and retention in the public health workforce. Data reported in this paper, 3–6 months after completing PHE, cannot yet document effect; however, respondents shared indications of satisfaction, a sense of relevance, and an interest in staying in the field of public health. Furthermore, respondent supervisors shared specific examples of how they perceive real value of PHE graduates on their team, perhaps translating to a supportive work environment. Future evaluations of PHE cohorts will examine the career trajectories of PHE graduates and their contribution to the U.S. public health workforce.

Limitations

Although these results are informative and promising, there are limitations to generalizability of these results. First, the respondent sample for the follow-up survey was small (46%), and although sample demographics were akin to the full population, it is possible that key perspectives of nonrespondents would have been different, particularly because the PHE graduates interviewed self-selected to participate (as did the supervisors). Although authors invitations were based on the a priori sampling plans, participation may be skewed toward those with more positive experiences of PHE. Second, the quantitative survey data reported are learner self-assessment scores. Although it is common practice in skills assessments and learning outcome assessment, including Public Health Workforce Interests and Needs Survey, it is important to acknowledge that individuals may perceive their knowledge and skills differently depending on their personal background and experience and are not externally validated. To overcome this challenge, the authors used previously validated survey tools and reported on skill changes over time by matching longitudinal surveys at the individual level, which attempted to control for individual-level variation such as tending to overstate or understate ability. In addition, the survey analysis may be subject to bias in interpretation because the authors do not have a control group.

CONCLUSIONS

To support public health and disease prevention, there is a critical need to expand the size and capacity of the workforce. A large, competent public health workforce is a prerequisite to effect major, much-needed improvements in community-based public health prevention work in the U.S. PHE aimed to reinforce the public health workforce and to improve workers’ ability to improve community health; outcomes appear promising.19 Applied and engaged just-in-time trainings such as PHE can be important tools in (re)building and retaining the public health workforce. Being delivered online and in an asynchronous manner allows for easy scalability and reach, and although the applied and facilitated approach requires funding, competence gains, retention, and application suggest that this could be a worthy workforce development and retention investment.
