Linking maternal BMI trajectories from prepregnancy to postpartum with breast milk fat composition: a retrospective longitudinal study
Authors: Zhi Huang, Yu-jie Duan, Ming Chen, Zi-ming Li, Xiao-yan Peng, Yu-ming Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFat, as one of the macronutrients in breast milk (BM) plays an important role in infant growth and development. This study aimed to explore the impact of maternal body mass index (BMI) trajectories in different pregnancy periods on the fat composition of BM and clarify the possible metabolic mechanisms.MethodsA retrospective longitudinal study was conducted in central south China and a total of 195 lactating women was recruited. Prepregnancy and delivery maternal weights were obtained from the Medical Birth Registry, while postpartum weight and height were measured during the interview. BM macronutrients and fatty acid (FA) profiles were analyzed. Maternal BMI trajectories reflected the combined weight status in different pregnancy periods was identified by Latent Class Growth Model. The associations of BM fat compositions with maternal weight status were used by multiple linear regression analysis.ResultsTwo maternal BMI trajectories characterized by high and low maternal BMI were found from prepregnancy to postpartum. The maternal BMI trajectories was positively associated with BM fat (β = 0.233, 95%CI: 0.092, 0.374), energy (β = 0.208, 95%CI:0.065, 0.351), and saturated fatty acids (SFAs) (0.160, 95%CI: 0.014, 0.306). Moreover, these fat compositions were also positively associated with prepregnancy overweight and obesity. The subgroup analysis by lactaing period found that in both subgroups, BM fat concentration was positively associated with maternal BMI trajectory and prepregnancy overweight and obesity. In addition, the results also revealed that in the lactation period of 90–180 days, maternal prepregnancy overweight and obesity were positively associated with BM concentrations of SFA, n‑6 PUFA, and total FAs. BM fat and energy were positively correlated with BM main FAs C14:0, C16:0, and C18:1n-9, which were associated with diet intake and endogenous synthesis of SFA.ConclusionHigh maternal BMI trajectory from prepregnancy to postpartum impacted BM fat and energy. The impact mainly depended on the maternal prepregnancy weight status. The potential metabolic mechanisms of maternal weight status on BM fat were associated with SFA endogenous synthesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13006-026-00831-y.Keywords: Breast milk, Fatty acids, Fat, Maternal BMI, Trajectories

Introduction

Breast milk (BM) is the best source of nutrients and energy for infants, which was recommended for exclusively breastfeeding for the first 6 months of infants by WHO [1]. Subtle changes in BM composition are intimately associated with infant growth and development or their long-term health later in life [2, 3]. Fat as the one of macronutrients in BM plays an important role in these effects, especially for infant physical [4] and cognitive development [5].

BM fat concentration is highly dynamic, and influenced by maternal nutritional status, diet intake, genetics, and lactation period [6]. There are increasing suggestions that maternal weight is associated with changes of lipids composition in BM [7, 8], while the mechanisms by which this association is transmitted from mother to BM are not fully understood. Fatty acids (FAs) are major components of fat, which provide over 50% of energy content as a nutrient [6]. In recent years, FA profiles have been widely used to identify metabolic pathways of fat-related health risks and elucidate molecular metabolic mechanisms [9, 10].

Emerging evidence suggests that maternal weight status correlates with FA composition [7, 11]. However, the results of the impact of maternal weight on BM FA profile composition remain inconsistent and controversial. A systematic review showed the majority of studies supported that maternal body mass index (BMI) was positively associated with the ratio of n-6 to n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in BM, whereas results for saturated fatty acids (SFAs) and monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) were indecisive [12]. In addition, there are considerable changes in maternal weight from prepregnancy to postpartum [13]. The combined effect of maternal weight on fat composition was neglected. The timing of maternal weight status assessment (i.e. assessment at either prepregnancy or postpartum) was also inconsistent in previous studies, and this may have affected the association, which may reduce the precision of results.

This retrospective longitudinal study primarily investigated the independent and combined effects of maternal weight status at different pregnancy stages on BM fat composition. Secondarily, it aimed to elucidate the underlying lipid metabolic mechanisms through FA profiling, thereby providing evidence to inform strategies for improving infant health.

Methods

Design and study population

This retrospective longitudinal study was conducted in Xiangtan City, located in central south China. Lactating women who delivered in local maternal and child health care hospital in 2023 were recruited. Lactating women for a lactation period of 20 to 180 days, aged between 18 and 40 years old, and delivering infants over 37 weeks of gestation remained eligible. In addition, we exclude the lactation women who had mastitis, infectious diseases, chronic diseases (e.g. heart, liver, kidney, and nervous system diseases), cancer, or a history of taking drugs for nutrient metabolism. Our final sample was 195. This study was approved by the Hunan Provincial Centre for Disease Prevention and Control Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Maternal weight status assessment

In this study, the maternal weight at prepregnancy and delivery were retrieved. The prepregnancy weight represented the weight status within 1 month before pregnancy. Maternal delivery weight represented the weight before delivery. The data on maternal prepregnancy and delivery weight was retrieved from the Medical Birth Registry. The postpartum weight and height were measured by health doctors using a height and weight device during the interview. The weight was expressed in kg and height was expressed in cm. Maternal prepregnancy, delivery, and postpartum BMI were calculated as different pregnancy periods weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m), respectively.

The maternal BMI trajectories was used to reflect the combined weight status in different pregnancy periods and was identified by the Latent Class Growth Model. In this model, maternal prepregnancy, delivery, and postpartum BMI were included. The optimal number of groups was determined by the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Sample-Size Adjusted BIC (aBIC), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Entropy, and p values of the likelihood ratio test index (LMR) and the Bootstrap-based likelihood ratio test index (BLRT). The model with the lowest BIC, aBIC, and AIC values was the best. Entropy reflects the accuracy of classification. The higher Entropy represented the higher classification accuracy. The p values of LMR and BLRT of the k-category model reach a significant level, which indicates that the k-category model is significantly better than the k-1-category model [14].

Prepregnancy and postpartum overweight and obesity were used to assess maternal weight status in prepregnancy and postpartum. The prepregnancy and postpartum BMI over 24 kg/m2 and above were identified as prepregnancy and postpartum overweight and obesity according to the Working Group on Obesity in China [15]. In addition, the gestational weight gain (GWG) overabundance represented maternal weight status at delivery was selected. GWG was calculated by subtracting prepregnancy weight from maternal weight at delivery. GWG overabundance was identified by the Institute of Medicine GWG Guidelines 2009 in America [16].

BM macronutrient analysis

Mothers were asked to provide a BM sample during the interview. BM collection was performed according to the Specifications for the Collection and Storage of Human Milk Samples for Research Purposes [17]. BM samples were collected between 8:00 and 12:00 a.m., two hours after the infant’s last feeding. Mothers completely emptied both breasts using either an electric or manual breast pump, and the total expressed milk volume was collected and subsequently aliquoted for analysis. One part of them was used to measure the content of protein, fat, lactose, total dry matter, and energy within 4 h of sampling by Miris HMA, a BM analyzer (MIRIS, Sweden). An internal calibration was used for quality control. The repeatability for fat, protein, and lactose was below 0.1 g/100 mL and accuracy was ± 15%. Another part of them was stored at − 80 ℃ for FA analysis.

BM FA profile analysis

FA extraction and derivatization from BM following the previously described procedure [18] with some modifications. Briefly, 50 µL of BM was added with 1 mL0.5 mol/L KOH methanol solution and reacted at room temperature for 10 min. After cooling the vials to room temperature, 2 mL of hexane was added, mixed well and centrifuged. Lastly, 100 µL of the supernatant, 200 µL of internal standard working solution, and 700 µL of hexane were mixed well for detection.

The gas chromatography-mass spectrometer (GC-MS, QP2010, Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) was used to analyze BM FA profiles, according to our previous study [19]. GC-MS Solution Software (Shimadzu) was performed for data acquisition and processing. The GC column was HP-88 column (dimensions, 100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.20 μm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Analyses were carried out by injecting 1 µl of the sample via autoinjector mode. The injector and detector were maintained at 230 ℃. Temperature programs of 50 ℃ for 1.5 min, 50 to 190 ℃ at 30 C/min, holding 190 ℃ for 5 min, and 190 to 230 ℃ at 8 ℃/min, holding 230 ℃ for 20 min were used. Helium was used as the carrier gas. Each sample run was completed in 35 min. FA peaks were observed between 9.6 and 32.5 min. The SupelcoTM 37 Component FAME Mix (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used to identify key FAMEs via chromatograms. Quantification was based on calibration with methyl nonadecanoate (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) as the IS. The results are expressed in µg/ml. To evaluate FA recovery, three concentrations of palmitic acid methyl ester standard (Aladdin) were added to BM samples, and the above-mentioned protocol of extraction, derivatization, and quantification was followed, with a recovery of 85.62 to 104.29%. A pooled sample for repeated FA analysis was done to test the reproducibility of the GC-MS. The coefficients of variation were 1.32 to 10.07%.

Other related variables

Sociodemographic information comprised maternal age, lactation period, occupation (including workers and peasants, official and staff, private industry and commerce, and others), education (including college and above, high and secondary technical school, and junior school and below), family annual income (including 50000 yuan and above, 20000 to 50000 yuan, and below 20000 yuan), gravidity (including once, twice, three times, and four times and above). Maternal age was further divided into groups below 25 years, 25 to 30 years, 30 to 35 years, and 35 years and above. The lactation period was further divided into groups of 20 to 89 days and 90 to 180 days based on the frequency distribution of mothers across different lactation stages.

Statistical analysis

The Latent Class Growth Model in this study was conducted by Mplus software (version 7.0). The data analysis was performed by SPSS software (version 25.0 for Windows). The continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) due to most of them with non-normal distribution. The categorical variables were expressed by frequencies and percentages. Differences between continuous and categorical variables according to maternal weight status were assessed using the Mann-Whitney Test and chi-square tests, respectively. The correlations of BM fat and energy with FA profiles were conducted by Spearman’s correlation analysis. The associations of BM fat and FA composition with maternal weight status were used by multiple linear regression analysis. The dependent variables were fat and FA composition, and the independent variable was maternal weight status from prepregnancy to postpartum, respectively. The models were adjusted for maternal sociodemographic variables. β(Standardized regression coefficient) with 95% CI(confidence interval) are reported to determine the strength of associations. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of this study, P values are reported without adjustment to minimize the risk of Type II errors when identifying potential novel associations.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

In total, 195 lactation women were included in this study. Most lactation women’s age was 25 to 35 years. More than half of them were college and above for education level and 50,000 yuan and above for family annual income. Nearly 70% of them had twice and above gravidity. The higher frequency of gravidity had high prepregnancy, delivery, and postpartum BMI (P < 0.05). No significant difference was observed between the lactation period, occupation, education, family annual income, and maternal BMI (Table 1).

Independent impact of maternal weight status from prepregnancy to postpartum on BM fat composition

Table 4 also shows the association of BM fat composition with maternal overweight and obesity in different pregnancy periods. Adjusting for sociodemographic variables, BM fat (β = 0.213, 95%CI:0.072, 0.355), and energy (β = 0.205, 95%CI:0.062, 0.349) concentrations were positively associated with prepregnancy overweight and obesity. Moreover, BM fat (β = 0.165, 95%CI:0.020, 0.311) level also had a positive association with postpartum overweight and obesity. However, no association was found after adjusting for prepregnancy overweight and obesity (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Figure 3(a) shows that compared to the group of prepregnancy non-overweight and obesity, the concentrations of BM C16:0, C18:0, C20:0, C18:1n-9, C20:3n-6, and C20:4n-6 (all P < 0.05) were higher in the group of maternal prepregnancy overweight and obesity. Compared to the group of GWG non-overabundance, maternal GWG overabundance had higher levels of BM C18:3n-3 and C18:2n-6 (both P < 0.05) (Fig. 3(b)). In addition, BM SFA (β = 0.161,95%CI:0.014, 0.307) concentrations were positively associated with prepregnancy overweight and obesity. No associations of FA with GWG overabundance and postpartum overweight and obesity were found (Table 4).

Lactation-specific analysis of the impact of maternal weight status from prepregnancy to postpartum on BM fat composition

Stratified subgroup analysis by lactation stage was conducted, with results presented in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. The subgroup analysis results further support that higher maternal weight status during pregnancy increases BM fat content. It was found that in both subgroups, breast milk fat concentration was positively associated with maternal BMI trajectory and prepregnancy overweight and obesity (Pregnancy BMI trajectory: β = 0.299, 95% CI: 0.108, 0.491 during lactation days 20–89, and β = 0.225, 95% CI: 0.015, 0.435 during lactation days 90–180; Prepregnancy overweight and obesity: β = 0.280, 95% CI: 0.083, 0.478 during lactation days 20–89, and β = 0.215, 95% CI: 0.007, 0.423 during lactation days 90–180). In addition, the results also revealed that in the lactation period of 90–180 days, maternal prepregnancy overweight and obesity were positively associated with breast milk concentrations of SFA (β = 0.295, 95% CI: 0.094, 0.496), MUFA (β = 0.226, 95% CI: 0.019, 0.433), n‑6 PUFA (β = 0.226, 95% CI: 0.023, 0.428), and total FA (β = 0.258, 95% CI: 0.053, 0.462).

Potential mechanisms of maternal weight on BM fat composition

Maternal weight status was associated with BM fat and energy in previous results. Breast milk fat and energy were positively correlated with BM C14:0, C16:0, C18:0 C20:0, and C18:1n-9 (Fig. 4(a)). These FAs represented the main FAs compositions. Figure 4(b) shows the metabolic pathways of these FAs [20]. C14:0, C16:0, C18:0, and C20:0 belonged to the same endogenous synthesis pathway. C18:1n-9 also can be endogenously synthesized from C18:0. These FAs were associated with diet intake and endogenous synthesis of SFA.

Discussion

This retrospective longitudinal study explored the impact of maternal BMI trajectory in different pregnancy periods on the fat composition of BM. We observed that two maternal BMI trajectory groups characterized by high and low maternal BMI were found from prepregnancy to postpartum. The high maternal BMI trajectory group was positively associated with BM fat, energy, and SFA. In addition, these fat compositions were also positively associated with prepregnancy overweight and obesity.

In this study, BM energy was positively associated with maternal high BMI. Several systematic reviews showed no conclusive associations were found between maternal weight status and BM energy levels [21]. However, some researchers assert that maternal overweight and obesity have a higher level of BM energy, which may be attributed to fat because fat represents the most energy-dense of the macronutrients and contributes nearly half of BM energy [22, 23]. Fat was the most variable component in BM. It has been reported by the majority of studies that a positive association between maternal BMI and BM fat content [24, 25].

In our study, we found prepregnancy weight status had associations with BM fat. This finding was consistent across subgroup analyses, corroborating evidence from earlier studies. Many studies considered that the reasons may be related to the high risk of metabolic disorders and dietary fat intake in mothers with overweight and obesity [26, 27]. However, other studies reported no significant correlation and a significant negative correlation of maternal weight status with BM fat level [28, 29]. The reasons may be associated with maternal breast expression, lactation period, or sampling protocols and analysis techniques of BM fat [21]. In addition, the inconsistent timing of maternal weight status assessment may be one of the reasons.

We found BM fat level also had a positive association with postpartum overweight and obesity. This result suggested the influence of maternal weight status on BM fat originates from the comprehensive effects of weight changes during pregnancy. However, the association of the fat composition of BM with postpartum weight status was attenuated and became non-significant after adjusting for prepregnancy overweight and obesity status. This indicates that prepregnancy weight is a strong confounding factor influencing the association between postpartum weight and BM fat. Our previous studies also demonstrated that prepregnancy BMI affects gestational weight status and adverse birth outcomes [30]. This result also suggested that prepregnancy weight status plays a more critical role in affecting BM fat composition.

Furthermore, our trajectory analysis captures the dynamic changes in maternal weight during pregnancy. The results primarily identify two distinct trajectories: a high-BMI trajectory and a low-BMI trajectory. This finding differs to some extent from the study by Muñoz‑Manrique et al., which reported six distinct maternal weight trajectories during pregnancy [31]. However, our study revealed a positive association between BM fat concentration and a high maternal BMI trajectory in both the overall population and subgroup analyses. This further confirms that the relationship between maternal weight and breast milk fat is influenced by the dynamic changes in maternal weight during pregnancy. The findings suggest that dynamic weight management during pregnancy holds significant implications for infant growth and development.

BM fat provides FAs that play an important role in an infant’s brain development. It has been reported a consistent result that maternal BMI was associated with n-3 and n-6 PUFAs, especially for the ratio of n-6:n-3 PUFAs [12]. Several studies considered the possible mechanistic pathways of BM PUFAs in that diet PUFAs were directly absorbed into the blood and then transferred to BM during lactation [6]. Many studies have confirmed that mothers with a high intake of n-3 PUFA foods during lactation had higher levels of BM n-3 PUFAs [32, 33]. In our study, no association was found between maternal weight status with BM n-3 PUFA. We presume the reason may attribute to the low intake of n-3 PUFA foods in overweight and obese mothers because our previous study found a low intake of marine food enriched n-3 PUFA for populations in the same areas [34].

However, our subgroup analysis revealed a positive correlation between BM n-6 PUFA levels at 90–180 days postpartum and prepregnancy overweight and obesity in mothers. A substantial body of prior research confirming that the stage of lactation is a significant factor influencing the composition of FA in BM [35]. Furthermore, studies have confirmed that differences in breast milk metabolites caused by maternal obesity are more pronounced at 6 months postpartum than at 1 month [36]. This suggests that the impact of prepregnancy overweight and obesity on breast milk n-6 PUFA is a time-dependent metabolic effect with delayed manifestation.

In addition, our results showed BM SFA concentrations were positively associated with high maternal BMI trajectory. Only a few previous studies supported that maternal weight had an association with BM SFA. More than 60% of BM FAs originate from the mother’s reservoirs or are synthesized by mammary alveolar epithelium [37]. The higher level of BM SFA concentrations in high maternal BMI may be associated with FA endogenous synthesis. We found the main SFAs of BM (e.g. C12:0, C14:0, C16:0, and C18:0) content were higher in high BMI mothers. A similar trend was found in C18:1n-9 represented the main MUFA. However, these FAs can be synthesized from the same endogenous synthesis pathway of SFA [20]. Besides, we also found BM fat and energy were positively correlated with these FA of BM. This result suggested the roles of maternal weight in regulating BM fat are associated with SFA metabolism.

This study was longitudinal and clarified the impact of maternal BMI trajectory on the fat composition of BM in pregnancy periods. Moreover, we also explore the potential mechanisms of the impact of maternal weight status on BM fat. One limitation of this study is the use of maternal BMI as the primary exposure variable. Although BMI is widely utilized and facilitates comparisons across studies, it does not distinguish between fat mass and lean body mass and therefore may not accurately reflect body composition. More direct measures, such as maternal body fat percentage, could provide more informative evidence regarding potential associations. Furthermore, maternal dietary information—which is closely related to maternal weight status and breast milk FA composition—was not collected, potentially confounding the results. Additionally, infant growth and development were not assessed. Future studies should further examine how maternal weight status and breast milk lipid profiles influence infant health outcomes.

Conclusion

In this retrospective longitudinal study, we found high maternal BMI trajectory from prepregnancy to postpartum impacted BM fat and energy. The impact of maternal BMI trajectory on BM fat composition mainly depended on the maternal prepregnancy weight status. The potential metabolic mechanisms of maternal prepregnancy weight status on BM fat and energy were associated with SFA endogenous synthesis. Our results provided a precise evidence for improving BM fat compositions.
