Research on noise reduction of annular axial cooling fan blade with perforated structure
Authors: Huawei Wang, Chaowen Xu, Junhua Hu, Li Wang, Bo Wang, Li Hu, Xiaohui Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aerodynamic noise produced by the annular axial cooling fan significantly impacts the NVH performance of automobiles. In response to this challenge, the paper proposes a low-noise annular axial cooling fan design, which based on the principle of small-hole jet flow. To balance the aerodynamic performance and the benefits of noise reduction, an L16 orthogonal array was utilized to optimize the perforated blade parameters at the fan rated operating condition (rotational speed of 2400 rpm and a static pressure rise of ΔPs =120 Pa). Numerical simulations reveal that micro-jets ejected from the perforations effectively optimize the blade surface pressure distribution, diminishing negative pressure backflow zones and significantly reducing vorticity at the guide ring and blade trailing edge. The comprehensive optimal configuration was identified as: radial spacing divided into 15 equal segments, 1 circumferential locating line, 3 radial locating lines, and a 2 mm perforation diameter. Under the identical 120 Pa aerodynamic loading, the optimized model successfully maintained the required mass flow rate while achieving a 1.92 dBA experimental reduction in the A-weighted Total Sound Pressure Level (TSPL) compared to the original model. While discrete tonal noise is significantly suppressed at high speeds, an inherent acoustic tradeoff was observed, characterized by a slight increase in broadband high-frequency noise at lower speeds. The results confirm that this perforation-based method is a viable noise reduction strategy. It provided new insights and references for the low-noise design of annular axial cooling fans.Keywords: Annular axial cooling fan, Perforated structure, Noise, Simulation, OptimizationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Physics

Introduction

NVH performance is an important indicator to evaluate the performance of automotive engines. Cooling fans are widely used in automotive heat dissipation systems due to their simple structure, good heat dissipation performance, and low cost. However, cooling fans will generate significant noise during high-speed operation. With the continuous improvement of the requirements for noise, vibration and harshness (NVH) of automobiles, the noise induced by cooling fans has attracted people’s attention. Hence, in order to optimize the NVH Effectiveness of automobiles, it has a significant impact to study the aerodynamic and noise characteristics of cooling fan and explore noise reduction design methods of vehicle annular axial cooling fan.

Extensive research efforts have been dedicated to predicting and controlling fan aerodynamics and noise. These research aims to achieve effective noise mitigation. Numerical simulation coupled with experimental validation has become a standard practice for characterizing the spectral properties of fan noise. Park et al.1–3 investigated the broadBAand noise sources in automotive cooling fans, predicting the radiated noise via the acoustic analogy method. Their analysis entailed examining blade surface pressure data to identify the origins of broadBAand noise generated by blade interactions with adjacent structures. Furthermore, the studies provided a detailed discussion on the influence of upstream geometries on discrete frequency noise, alongside the acoustic implications of employing swept blades in axial flow fan systems. Zanon et al.4 presented a broadBAand noise evaluation method for a non-conducting low-speed axial flow fan, and pointed out that the broadBAand noise of 400 to 2000 Hz is primarily caused by boundary layer diffusion at the wing’s edge and the interplay between the tip vortex and the edge of the blade wing. Han et al.5 obtained the fluctuating flow field and the directivity of pitch noise through numerical simulation, and the pitch noise and its harmonic characteristics at the blade passing frequency (BPF) are investigated, the acoustic spectrum reveals a clear predominance of the tonal peak at 1 BPF, which characterizes the overall sound pressure level.

Researchers have employed various noise reduction methods to suppress fan noise: the manufacturing and installation precision improvement; measures for sound absorption, sound isolation, and noise cancellation have been adopted; noise sources control implemented and structural optimization.

The most mainstream and effective method for controlling noise in axial cooling fans is structural optimization. Researchers categorize the structural optimization of fans mainly into three groups: layout optimization; airfoil optimization༛and blade structure change.

In the category of layout optimization, Albanesi et al.6 applied stochastic particle swarm optimization to design a novel propeller, demonstrating that a reduction in blade count can enhance both the performance curve and efficiency in constant-speed axial flow exhaust fans. Luo et al.7 successfully verified the trustworthiness and exactness of numerical simulations based on comparisons of experimental data, which showed that the increased tip vortex strength and the heightened interaction with the blade surface is primarily responsible for the increased broadBAand noise observed with larger tip clearance. Ottersten et al.8 investigated how clearance affects the aerodynamic characteristics and noise of the volute fan based on various clearance geometries, and simulation results show a region of high vorticity in the passage between two blades near the wing edge of the shroud. Fakhari et al.9 presented an optimisation technique for an axial-flow Chinook ventilation fan, evaluating parameters like angle of attack, tip clearance, rotation speed, hub-to-tip ratio, and rotor blade count by means of Design of Experiments (DOE) and regression. The investigation revealed that declines in both blade count and tip clearance produced an average performance improvement of 9%, with marked benefits in acoustic output, expense, mass, and power demand.

In the category of airfoil optimization, Chen et al.10 stigated the efficacy of linear blade installation angle distributions along the spanwise direction for a contra-rotating axial fan, concluding that this approach constitutes a viable strategy for energy saving, as it curbs turbulent dissipation in key components like the rear impeller. Ryu et al.11 found that the winglet shape can affect the flow performance, efficiency and aerodynamic noise of axial flow fan through numerical simulation and experiment. Wang et al.12 conducted numerical simulations of the cooling fan elucidate the mechanism behind the reduction of fan blade noise. During operation, the horseshoe vortex created by the ridge structure disrupts the boundary layer flow, diminishing the impact of fluid flow on the boundary layer and delaying the transition of the fan blade layer to turbulence.

In the category of blade structure change, Ryu et al.13 analyzed the flow and noise properties of shrouded axial-flow fans. An innovative fan design was presented, which featured winglets with a chordwise-decreasing curvature. The research validated that such a design effectively restrained the development of leakage vortices within the gap-flow region, resulting in an increase in flow velocity and a reduction in fan noise. Sasaki et al.14 pointed out that the addition of a guide ring to an open fan can improve aerodynamic performance and reduce the noise, and the discrete frequency noise associated with the blade surface is generated by periodic pressure fluctuations matched to the blade passing frequency. Through the study of the mechanism of aerodynamic noise in fans, it has been found that blade tip vortices and leakage vortices are the principal causes of the fans’ aerodynamic noise in blades. Bene et al.15 discussed the effect of different inlet sizes on tip leakage noise, and results show that the tip leakage noise can be reduced. The use of an appropriately sized horn inlet suppresses leakage flow, yielding a 5.0–6.0 dBA reduction in the sound pressure level. Ye et al.16 undertook a numerical investigation into the effects of various serrated trailing edges on the aerodynamic and noise characteristics of a single-stage variable-pitch axial flow fan. Based on the findings, a new low-noise fan featuring an innovative serrated blade configuration was developed.

Among various structural optimization methods, the approach focusing on blade structure change has received relatively less attention. The perforated blade structure can allow fluid energy on the fan surface to be absorbed by the small holes on the blades and disrupt the flow state of the surface fluid. As the result, research on improving aerodynamic and noise performance through perforated blade has been increasingly prevalent in recent years. Hu et al.17 found that perforated blade can effectively reduce the aerodynamic noise of the double-rotor axial flow fan and improve the NVH performance, therefor, a perforated double-rotor axial flow fan was designed to explore the effect of various perforation parameters on its Functionality. Yang et al.18 investigated the role of perforated blades in the operation of a large marine axial flow fan. The study revealed that perforations located at the blade tip play a key role in attenuating discrete acoustic tones. Wang19 treated the noise generated by the interplay between two rotors with a porous trailing edge, and determined that the aperture should be minimized and that the perforation rate distribution should decrease in the direction away from the blade’s trailing edge. Yadegari et al.20 employed numerical simulation to study an axial flow fan equipped with a perforated guide ring. The study focused on a perforated surface located adjacent to the fan blade trailing edge, showing that this design can suppress noise at the blade passing frequency.

While previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of perforated blade in suppressing the discrete noise of open-rotor fans, its application to annular axial cooling fans remains largely unexplored. The noise generation mechanisms between these two configurations are fundamentally different. In open-rotor fans, the aerodynamic noise is primarily driven by free tip leakage vortices and separation vortices. In contrast, the annular axial cooling fan features a unique integral structure where the blade tips are connected by a rotating outer guide ring.

Although this annular guide ring physically inhibits the development of traditional tip leakage vortices, it introduces a distinct aerodynamic interference mechanism. The radial outward airflow along the blade pressure surface impinges on the guide ring, generating complex secondary flows and localized disordered wake shedding at the blade-ring junctions. The subsequent interaction between these specific wake structures and the trailing edges constitutes the primary source of its discrete noise. Because the underlying noise generation mechanisms are fundamentally different, the acoustic response to perforated blade cannot be directly extrapolated from open fans.

Therefore, the fundamental novelty of this paper lies in investigating the unique interaction between the perforated blade structure and the annular guide ring mechanism. By systematically designing and analyzing perforated blades for vehicular annular cooling fans, this study aims to reveal the specific underlying physical mechanisms by which targeted perforation suppresses the unique corner/wake vortex structures and effectively reduces the discrete aerodynamic noise in annular fan configurations.

Boundary layer resolution and y+ distribution validation

In Large Eddy Simulation (LES) and high-fidelity aeroacoustic predictions, the spatial resolution of the boundary layer mesh fundamentally determines the capture accuracy of wall pressure fluctuations (dipole sound sources). To satisfy the stringent requirements of high-fidelity simulations, the boundary layer mesh on the surfaces of the annular axial cooling fan and the guide ring was refined in this study.

Turbulence modeling and near-wall treatment strategy

In the initial stage of the numerical calculation, the RNG k-ɛ model was employed for the steady-state simulation to rapidly obtain a stable and physically consistent initial flow field. Because the near-wall mesh in this study is highly refined, traditional standard wall functions would lead to computational distortion in the ultra-low y+ region. Therefore, the more compatible Scalable Wall Function was selected for the steady-state calculation. This function effectively eliminates the lower-bound restriction of extremely fine grids in the log-law region calculation (avoiding algorithmic failure when y + < 11.225), thereby providing a highly converged steady initial field while maintaining ultra-high grid resolution.

Upon convergence of the steady-state flow field, the transient calculation was switched to the Large Eddy Simulation (LES) turbulence model to resolve large-scale energy-containing eddies, employing the Wall-Modeled LES (WMLES) formulation for near-wall treatment. To completely eliminate the initial non-physical numerical fluctuations generated by the switch from RANS to LES, the transient calculation was executed for an additional 0.1 s of fan rotation time before the formal acoustic sampling commenced. This critical transition period ensures that the turbulent fluctuations within the boundary layer are fully developed and reach a statistically stationary state.

Physical significance for aeroacoustic simulation

This exceptionally high standard of boundary layer resolution is of decisive significance for the aeroacoustic calculations in this study. Because the vast majority of the wall regions satisfy the stringent condition of y + ≤ 1, the WMLES model utilized in the transient calculation asymptotically recovers the exact viscous limit in the near-wall region. Rather than claiming a full physical resolution of all near-wall turbulent streaks—which would require prohibitive streamwise and spanwise grid resolutions—this transition implies a mathematical and methodological consistency. Specifically, the algebraic formulation within the WMLES framework naturally diminishes the modeled turbulent eddy viscosity in the viscous sublayer. Consequently, the surface shear stress is accurately computed primarily governed by molecular viscosity, without relying on empirical log-layer functions. Meanwhile, the coupled WALE subgrid-scale model provides the necessary dissipation for the turbulent structures that remain unresolved due to the grid aspect ratio. Such high wall-normal grid resolution and mathematical fidelity perfectly accommodate the stably developed turbulent field, guaranteeing the precise extraction of high-frequency wall pressure fluctuations (dipole sources) on the fan surfaces. FW-H acoustic analogy implementation details

The far-field sound radiation was predicted utilizing the FW-H acoustic analogy based on a solid-surface formulation. While a permeable-surface formulation can theoretically capture quadrupole (volume) noise, the solid-surface approach was prioritized in this study to eliminate numerical sensitivities associated with arbitrary boundary placement.

A significant advantage of this solid-surface formulation is that the integration surface is physically and uniquely defined by the actual geometry of the blades and hub. Unlike a permeable-surface formulation, which is often highly sensitive to surface placement due to “spurious noise” generated when turbulent vortices cross the integration boundary, the solid-surface approach inherently avoids these vortex-crossing artifacts and guarantees a mathematically robust extraction of the dipole sources.

Therefore, the acoustic integration surfaces explicitly encompass the entire wetted surfaces of both the baseline and optimized perforated blades, including the rotating hub, to accurately and cleanly integrate these dominant dipole noise contributions. This choice is further physically justified by the low-Mach-number flow conditions investigated in this study (maximum blade tip Ma ≈ 0.15). In this regime, the quadrupole sources (scaling with Ma8) are considered physically negligible compared to the surface-induced dipole sources (scaling with Ma6).

The acoustic observer was precisely located at coordinates (0, 1000, 0) mm from the fan hub center, directly aligning with the single-microphone placement defined in the experimental tests conducted according to the GB/T 1236–2017 standard. The choice of a single monitoring point at this specific axial location is prioritized for two reasons.

First, it ensures a rigorous one-to-one correspondence between the numerical predictions and the physical measurements, which is essential for validating the fidelity of the WMLES and FW-H framework. Second, this standardized location serves as a representative benchmark for evaluating the relative noise reduction performance between the baseline and optimized models. Given the fan diameter (D = 405 mm), the observation distance (R = 1000 mm) corresponds to 2.5D. According to the acoustic far-field criterion (typically R > 2D for axial fans), this placement ensures that the observer is positioned well within the geometric and acoustic far-field, where the spherical spreading law applies and the reactive near-field effects are negligible.

Simulation definition and verification

To ensure the high fidelity of the aeroacoustic predictions and rigorously address the stringent resolution criteria of the Large Eddy Simulation (LES) and the Ffowcs Williams-Hawkings (FW-H) acoustic analogy, comprehensive model definitions and verifications were systematically conducted.

Grid independence and LES resolution criteria

A highly rigorous spatial grid independence study was performed across seven different mesh resolutions. As depicted in Fig. 6, the calculated airflow rate at a fan speed of 2400 rpm and a static pressure of 120 Pa exhibits negligible variation when the mesh is further refined beyond the current selected medium level, confirming strict spatial grid independence. For this complex annular axial cooling fan, the mass flow rate serves as the most sensitive fundamental integral determinant. While performing transient CAA integrations across all seven mesh levels is computationally prohibitive, the strict convergence of the airflow rate mathematically dictates that the primary macroscopic aerodynamic structures—including the global velocity gradients, boundary layer blockage, and large-scale vortex shedding—have been fully resolved.

Temporal stability and time-step independence

The temporal discretization was bounded by a strict time step of Δt = 10− 4 s. This high-resolution time step satisfies two critical criteria: inherently supporting the acoustic Nyquist frequency up to 5000 Hz, and ensuring that the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) number remains ≤ 1.0 in the dominant core flow and near-wall regions, which guarantees both temporal accuracy and numerical stability for the LES energy cascade.

To ensure spectral convergence and data-window stability, a stringent temporal dissipation strategy was implemented. Before commencing the formal 0.2 s FW-H acoustic sampling, the transient LES calculation, initialized from the steady RANS solution, was executed for an additional 0.1 s (equivalent to 4 full fan rotations). This critical transition period thoroughly dissipates the initial non-physical transient shocks resulting from the RANS-to-LES switch and allows the turbulent flow to become fully developed and statistically stationary. The subsequent 0.2 s sampling window (capturing 8 full rotations and 56 BPF cycles) therefore strictly extracts stabilized aeroacoustic sources, eliminating artifacts from non-steady transient effects without the need for redundant multi-window validations.

Acoustic metrics and post-processing

To ensure the transparency and reproducibility of the acoustic evaluations, the acoustic metrics and signal post-processing methodologies are explicitly defined. The standard reference sound pressure (Pref) is defined as 2 × 10− 5 Pa. To account for human auditory perception and to maintain strict consistency between the orthogonal test screening and the final detailed acoustic evaluation, all reported sound pressure levels and frequency spectra are A-weighted, uniformly denoted as dBA.

The transient acoustic pressure data predicted by the FW-H acoustic analogy were transformed from the time domain into the frequency domain using a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). The numerical sampling utilized a physical time step (Δt) of 0.0001 s and a total sampling duration (T) of 0.2 s. According to the Nyquist sampling theorem, this configuration yields a highly resolved narrowband spectrum with a frequency resolution (Δf) of 5 Hz and a maximum resolvable frequency of 5000 Hz.

The overall A-weighted Total Sound Pressure Level (TSPL) is quantified via the logarithmic summation (band integration) of the acoustic energy across all discrete frequency bins within the specified effective frequency range. Blade piercing structure optimization

Blade piercing structural design

When the motor-driven annular axial cooling fan rotates, the flow on the fan blade’s suction surface changes its original direction under the action of friction drag. After being thrown out radially, this flow interferes with the guide ring, forming a localized return flow. These backflows interact with the blade wake and the leading edge of subsequent blades to generate discrete frequency noise, primarily induced by vortex shedding on the blade surface. The vicinity of the blade tip is the primary region for the formation of low-pressure backflow and discrete frequency noise.

To ensure the perforation distribution is targeted and the parameter design is reasonable, four curves were generated by rotating the fan blade’s trailing edge around the hub center along the direction of rotation by 3°, 6°, 9°, and 12°, establishing the circumferential positioning lines. Subsequently, the blade was divided into 5, 10, 15, and 20 equal segments along the radial axis; the four outermost radial dividing lines were selected as the radial positioning lines. Consequently, the location of the perforations is determined by the intersection of these circumferential and radial locating lines., an example of which is shown in Fig. 7. Therefore, this study selects the following parameters as orthogonal test optimization variables: radial hole spacing, number of circumferential locating lines, number of radial locating lines, and perforation diameter. The optimization objectives are the aerodynamic airflow rate and the A-weighted Total Sound Pressure Level (TSPL).

Selection of the “Comprehensive Optimum”

To eliminate subjective bias in selecting the final “comprehensive optimum” from the orthogonal array, an explicit, constraint-based Single Decision Rule was established. This rule rigorously integrates the statistical significance derived from the ANOVA with the precise effect values (Ki) obtained from the range analysis.

The quantitative decision rule is formally defined as follows:

Prioritize the optimization of the statistically dominant factors identified by the ANOVA (i.e., Factor 4 and Factor 2). For each design factor, select the level that minimizes the acoustic noise (Ki), subject to a strict aerodynamic constraint where the corresponding airflow (Ki) must remain ≥ 0.875 kg/s. Furthermore, if the acoustic difference between competing levels is statistically and physically negligible (ΔKi≤0.15 dBA), prioritize the level that yields the higher airflow to actively suppress secondary noise induced by excessive aerodynamic leakage.

Applying this quantitative mathematical rule step-by-step makes the parameter selection:

Factor 4 (Perforation diameter - Primary Aerodynamic Dominant Factor): As explicitly quantified by the ANOVA, Factor 4 absolutely dominates the airflow variation (75.86% contribution, p < 0.01) and highly influences the acoustic behavior (28.14%). Based on the Ki range analysis, Level 1 (2 mm) exceptionally yields both the absolute maximum airflow (0.909 kg/s) and the absolute minimum noise (71.967 dBA). Thus, Level 1 is definitively selected without requiring any trade-off.

Factor 2 (Number of circumferential lines - Primary Acoustic Dominant Factor): The ANOVA identifies Factor 2 as the most critical contributor to acoustic variation (33.85%) and the second most influential factor for airflow (16.18%). The Ki analysis reveals that Level 2 provides the absolute lowest noise (72.016 dBA), but Level 1 is acoustically nearly identical (72.116 dBA). Because this acoustic difference is completely negligible (ΔKi=0.001dBA ≤ 0.15 dBA), the secondary constraint rule strictly dictates prioritizing aerodynamic performance. Since Level 1 provides a superior airflow of 0.894 kg/s compared to Level 2 (0.891 kg/s), Level 1 is strategically selected to minimize leakage-induced secondary noise.

Factor 3 (Number of Radial Lines): As a statistically secondary factor, Level 3 demonstrated the optimal acoustic performance, yielding the minimum noise level of 72.195 dBA. Furthermore, the associated mass flow rate of 0.880 kg/s satisfies the aerodynamic constraint (≥ 0.875 kg/s); consequently, Level 3 was designated as the optimal setting.

Factor 1 (Radial Hole Spacing): In a similar manner, Level 3 achieved the lowest noise emission (72.272 dBA) within its parameter set. Given that the resulting airflow of 0.876 kg/s remains above the prescribed threshold of 0.875 kg/s, Level 3 was selected for the final design.

Through this systematic integration of ANOVA statistical weighting and Ki value constraints, the parameter selection process is quantitatively justified.

The final comprehensive optimal configuration is quantitatively justified as: Factor 1 at Level 3 (15 equal parts), Factor 2 at Level 1 (1 circumferential line), Factor 3 at Level 3 (3 radial lines), and Factor 4 at Level 1 (2 mm perforation aperture). Resonance and structural safety analysis

Structural stress and fatigue life evaluation

To ensure the operational reliability of the optimized cooling fan, a comprehensive structural and fatigue analysis was first conducted. Under standard aerodynamic and centrifugal loading at 2400 rpm, the structural analysis indicates a maximum stress (σmax) of approximately 1.4 MPa, located primarily at the blade root. The fan impeller is manufactured from PA66 polymer, which possesses an elastic modulus of 7.2 GPa and a flexural strength of 85 MPa. Consequently, the safety factor (n) is calculated as n=85 MPa/1.4 MPa ≈ 60.7. This remarkably high safety factor ensures that the fan operates well below the material’s failure limit, precluding any risk of macroscopic yielding or plastic deformation.

Furthermore, to quantitatively rigorously evaluate the long-term reliability of the fan under cyclic aerodynamic and mechanical loading, a comprehensive high-cycle fatigue (HCF) analysis was conducted.

Dynamic stress spectrum analysis

Dynamic stress spectrum at blade root.

To bridge the gap between structural resonance and fatigue, a dynamic stress spectrum analysis was performed at the critical stress location (blade root). As illustrated in Fig. 26, the dynamic stress energy is discretely concentrated at the rotational frequency (frot = 40 Hz) and the blade passing frequency (fBPF = 240 Hz).

The peak stress amplitude at fBPF is approximately 0.16 MPa, which is two orders of magnitude lower than the material’s fatigue limit. Furthermore, the high internal damping loss factor of PA66 effectively suppresses the dynamic amplification at these harmonic frequencies. The spectral results confirm that neither the discrete tonal excitations nor the broadband turbulence possess sufficient energy to trigger structural fatigue or crack initiation.

Frequency definition and resonance margin

During standard operation under load conditions, the nominal rotational speed of the cooling fan is n = 2400 rpm. Consequently, the operating rotational frequency of the rotor is strictly defined as frot=n/60 = 40 Hz. Given that the fan consists of six blades (Z = 6), the Blade Passing Frequency (BPF) is solely determined by the rotational speed and blade count, calculated as fBPF=Z×frot = 240 Hz.

In structural-acoustic design, a rigorous resonance margin criterion is essential to prevent fatigue failure. Engineering standards typically require the structural natural frequencies (fn) to maintain a sufficient separation margin from potential aerodynamic and mechanical excitation frequencies (fe). The separation margin (M) is defined as M = | fn - fe | / fe×100%, with a minimum avoidance zone typically set between ± 5% and ± 10%.

Considering fluid-structure interaction (FSI) and pre-stress effects under normal temperatures, the first-order natural frequency of the optimized cooling fan is determined to be fn1=73.496Hz ≈ 73.50 Hz (detailed in Table 15). The separation margin between this first-order natural frequency and the primary rotational frequency (40 Hz) is calculated as |73.5–40| / 40 × 100% = 83.75%. Furthermore, the separation margin with respect to the blade passing frequency (240 Hz) is |73.5–240| / 240 × 100% = 69.38%.

It is observed that fn1 is relatively close to the second harmonic of the rotational frequency (2frot=80 Hz). Applying the margin criterion, the separation margin remains at |73.5–80| / 80 × 100%≈8.13%, which safely exceeds the critical ± 5% threshold. Furthermore, the fan impeller is manufactured from a polymer material (PA66), which inherently possesses a significantly higher material damping loss factor compared to metals. This high internal damping effectively broadens the resonance peak and suppresses the dynamic amplification factor, rendering the 8.13% margin sufficiently robust to avoid harmonic resonance.

Beyond discrete tonal excitations (such as frot, fBPF, and their harmonics), the interaction between the fan structure and broadband turbulence must also be addressed. Unlike tonal noise, which concentrates high energy at specific discrete frequencies, broadband turbulence is stochastic, distributing its kinetic and acoustic energy continuously over a wide frequency spectrum. Although the continuous nature of broadband turbulence implies that it contains frequency components matching the natural frequency (73.50 Hz) and will continuously interact with the fan blades, the localized Power Spectral Density (PSD) allocated precisely within this narrow modal bandwidth is extremely low. Consequently, the structural response excited by the broadband turbulence is negligible. Combined with the high structural damping of the PA66 material, this weak flow-induced vibration is insufficient to trigger structural fatigue risks or generate noticeable secondary structure-borne noise.

In conclusion, since the natural frequencies of the cooling fan maintain safe separation margins from both discrete operating excitations and continuous broadband turbulence, it will definitively not experience resonance during continuous loaded operations.

Acoustic signal processing

To ensure strict comparability with the FW-H numerical simulations (which utilized a 0.0001 s time step and a 0.2 s total duration), analogous and rigorous signal-processing parameters were established for the experimental data. Acoustic data acquisition was executed via the LMS SCADAS frontend combined with Simcenter Test. Lab software. The system inherently applies hardware anti-aliasing filters prior to digitization. The transient microphone signals were recorded at a high sampling rate of 12,800 Hz, comfortably satisfying the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem for the 20–5000 Hz frequency range of interest.

For the spectral analysis of the steady-state operating conditions, the time-domain data were processed utilizing a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). A Hanning window was applied to minimize spectral leakage, with a 50% overlap between consecutive time blocks. The block size was meticulously configured to yield a narrow-band frequency resolution (Δf) of 1 Hz, providing finer spectral detail than the 5 Hz resolution of the numerical model. Multiple blocks were linearly averaged to ensure statistical reliability. All reported overall Total Sound Pressure Levels (TSPL) and broadband frequency spectra are strictly A-weighted.

Harmonic extraction and uncertainty quantification

Furthermore, regarding the extraction of discrete harmonic levels—specifically the Blade Passing Frequency (1BPF) and its higher-order harmonics (2BPF–4BPF)—a peak-picking method was employed on the A-weighted narrowband spectrum. To accommodate minute, negligible rotational speed fluctuations of the driving motor during steady operation, the maximum harmonic amplitude was extracted within a tightly defined search band of ± 2 Hz centered around the theoretical harmonic frequencies.

Finally, to rigorously quantify experimental repeatability and uncertainty, acoustic measurements for the key conditions (the baseline and optimized fans at the nominal 2400 rpm) were independently repeated five times (N = 5). Statistical analysis of these repeated trials revealed that the maximum standard deviation was strictly contained within a highly narrow margin(<0.15 dBA), confirming highly stable and repeatable acoustic performance. Having established this minimal uncertainty band, the measured A-weighted TSPL and BPF are reported directly as the ensemble mean values in the subsequent results sections to ensure clarity and conciseness. This explicitly demonstrates that the observed acoustic improvements are mathematically significant and not artifacts of random experimental variance.

Discussion and reconciliation of the discrepancy

As highlighted in Table 18, a quantitative discrepancy exists between the numerical predictions and the experimental measurements: the FW-H simulation predicts a Total Sound Pressure Level (TSPL) reduction of 3.72 dBA, whereas the experimental measurement records an effective reduction of 1.92 dBA. This discrepancy is physically justified by the fundamental differences between the highly idealized aerodynamic boundary conditions of the computational domain and the complex fluid-structure interactions present in the physical test bench. The FW-H simulation represents a pristine, purely aerodynamic environment characterized by an absolutely uniform, zero-turbulence inflow and a perfectly rigid, static fan geometry. Under these idealized conditions, the optimized perforation structure fully mitigates the targeted tip-leakage vortices and wake shedding, achieving the theoretical maximum noise reduction of 3.72 dBA. Conversely, the physical experiments capture the compounded aerodynamic complexities of the real-world facility.

First, the physical fan inevitably ingests air with inherent ambient inflow turbulence and micro-scale velocity non-uniformities generated within the upstream test duct. This inflow turbulence impinges on the rotating blades, generating additional broadband aerodynamic noise that the geometric perforation cannot completely suppress.

Second, during physical operation at 2400 rpm, the fan blades and the driving shaft undergo inevitable dynamic aeroelastic deformations and microscopic radial runout. These dynamic structural behaviors cause the actual operational tip clearance to become slightly non-uniform and dynamically variable, inducing stronger, non-steady localized leakage phenomena compared to the static CFD model. Furthermore, the experimental microphone inevitably measures the total system noise, which mathematically includes the constant, un-optimized motor electromagnetic noise and bearing mechanical noise. In acoustic logarithmic summation, these non-aerodynamic noise floors inherently mask and compress the overall measurable TSPL reduction margin from the theoretical 3.72 dBA down to 1.92 dBA. To rigorously verify that this 1.92 dB measured reduction is physically significant despite the aforementioned masking effects, an experimental uncertainty propagation analysis was conducted based on the established test rig parameters. The overall combined standard uncertainty (Uc) encompasses the microphone calibration precision (Umic ≈ ± 0.3dBA), utilizing the GRAS 46AE Class 1 microphone), background noise fluctuations (Ubg ≈ ± 0.2dBA), and the rotational speed variation of the LT-880 laser sensor (≤ 1% error, contributing an estimated Urpm ≈ ± 0.15dBA). Because the measured noise reduction of 1.92 dB is significantly larger than the expanded uncertainty boundary (1.92 dBA > 0.78 dBA), it is mathematically and statistically proven that the acoustic improvement is a highly robust physical effect rather than an artifact of random experimental variance. Both the simulation and experimental data confirm a parallel attenuation of the discrete tonal peaks (Table 17), robustly validating the predictive capability of the numerical model and the physical effectiveness of the perforated design.

Conclusion and limitations

The purpose of this paper is to propose a low-noise annular axial cooling fan model by combining the aerodynamic noise generation mechanism with the perforated jet principle. Based on a comprehensive numerical and experimental investigation, the following conclusions and limitations are drawn:

(1) Through the combination of experimental measurements and numerical simulations, the primary noise generation mechanisms and source locations were identified. The low-pressure return area near the guide ring and the trailing edge wake vortex shedding are the dominant sources of discrete frequency noise.

(2) To ensure aerodynamic consistency, an L16 (44) orthogonal experimental array was strictly conducted at the design operating point (2400 rpm and ΔPs= 120 Pa) for multi-parameter optimization. The analysis reveals that the jet flow through the perforated blades effectively reduces the negative pressure return area, spatial pressure gradients, and vorticity distribution at the trailing edge. The optimal structural combination was determined as: radial spacing of 15 equal parts, 1 circumferential locating line, 3 radial locating lines, and a 2 mm perforation diameter.

(3) Evaluated precisely at this identical 120 Pa aerodynamic loading boundary, the CFD/FW-H simulation demonstrated a 3.72 dBA reduction in A-weighted TSPL, while the experimental measurements confirmed a highly reliable 1.92 dBA reduction in A-weighted TSPL. The discrete tonal noise (1BPF and 2BPF) is significantly suppressed at high speeds. However, an inherent acoustic tradeoff was observed, where the broadband high-frequency noise slightly increases at lower speeds.

(4) Regarding structural integrity, an initial one-way Fluid-Structure Interaction (FSI) analysis verified that the maximum static stress, deformation, and prestressed modal frequencies of the perforated blades remain well within the safe material limits under the specified steady aerodynamic loading conditions.

Limitations and future work

While this study demonstrates the feasibility of perforation-based noise reduction, several limitations must be rigorously acknowledged.

First, regarding operating-point dependence: Although our experimental tests evaluated the acoustic performance across multiple rotational speeds—revealing that the discrete noise suppression is inherently operating-point dependent and becomes less effective at lower speeds due to reduced jet velocity—our high-fidelity aeroacoustic numerical mechanisms (LES/FW-H), multi-parameter optimization, and fluid-structure interaction (FSI) evaluations were strictly performed at the nominal design condition (2400 rpm, ΔPs= 120 Pa). Consequently, the numerical mechanistic insights regarding off-design vortex suppression require further computational validation.

Second, regarding numerical and acoustic boundaries: Due to computational resource constraints, the transient Large Eddy Simulation (LES) was conducted over a finite sampling duration, which may limit the resolution of extremely low-frequency transient periodicities. Furthermore, the FW-H acoustic analogy assumes an idealized free-field environment, whereas real-world installation effects inherently mask a portion of the purely aerodynamic improvements.

Finally, regarding structural evaluation: The structural analysis was strictly limited to static deformation and prestressed modal behavior under steady one-way FSI assumptions. Long-term fatigue life, dynamic flutter, and extensive durability assessments were not conducted. These critical structural aspects, along with the off-design transient aerodynamic mechanisms, remain essential subjects for future empirical and numerical research.
