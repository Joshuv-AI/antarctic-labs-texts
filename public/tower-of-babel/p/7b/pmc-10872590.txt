Donor white blood cell differential is the single largest determinant of whole blood gene expression patterns
Authors: Grant C. O’Connell, Jing Wang, Christine Smothers
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
It has become widely accepted that sample cellular composition is a significant determinant of the gene expression patterns observed in any transcriptomic experiment performed with bulk tissue. Despite this, many investigations currently performed with whole blood do not experimentally account for possible inter-specimen differences in cellularity, and often assume that any observed gene expression differences are a result of true differences in nuclear transcription. In order to determine how confounding of an assumption this may be, in this study, we recruited a large cohort of human donors (n = 138) and used a combination of next generation sequencing and flow cytometry to quantify and compare the underlying contributions of variance in leukocyte counts versus variance in other biological factors to overall variance in whole blood transcript levels. Our results suggest that the combination of donor neutrophil and lymphocyte counts alone are the primary determinants of whole blood transcript levels for up to 75% of the protein-coding genes expressed in peripheral circulation, whereas the other factors such as age, sex, race, ethnicity, and common disease states have comparatively minimal influence. Broadly, this infers that a majority of gene expression differences observed in experiments performed with whole blood are driven by latent differences in leukocyte counts, and that cell count heterogeneity must be accounted for to meaningfully biologically interpret the results.

Introduction

Because of its ease of sampling, whole blood is by a wide margin the single most profiled tissue in high-throughput human transcriptomic investigations [1]. To date, thousands of studies have performed either primary analyses or secondary meta-analyses of gene expression data generated from whole blood in attempts to better understand the immune pathophysiology associated with a broad range of human disease states, or to identify biomarkers with potential diagnostic utility [2–8].

It has become widely accepted that the gene expression patterns observed in any transcriptomic experiment performed with bulk tissue are largely a product of two factors: the relative proportions of functionally distinct cell populations present in the sample, and the nuclear transcriptional state of the cell populations themselves [9]. It is likely that the former plays a substantially greater role in determining transcript levels in whole blood compared to transcript levels in solid tissues. Unlike solid tissues which are relatively static in terms of cellular composition, the cellular composition of whole blood is highly dynamic and can differ substantially between donors. With the exception of hemoglobin transcripts, which are often selectively depleted prior to gene expression profiling [10], virtually all of the mRNA in whole blood originates from white blood cells; furthermore, a majority of this mRNA is contributed by neutrophils and lymphocytes specifically, as they collectively make up well over 90% of the circulating leukocyte pool [11]. The ratio between the circulating counts of these two functionally and transcriptionally unique cell populations, commonly referred to as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), ranges from roughly 4:1 (4.0) to 1:2 (0.5) in healthy adults [12], with more extreme values observed in a wide variety of disease states [13–19].

Given this extreme degree of cell count heterogeneity, without additional information, it is impossible to know whether experimentally observed differences in whole blood transcript levels are driven by true alterations in nuclear transcriptional state or simply by underlying differences in donor leukocyte counts. However, a large number of the transcriptomic investigations being performed with whole blood today do not experimentally account for possible inter-specimen differences in cellularity and often report their results under the assumption that any observed gene expression differences are a result of true differences in nuclear transcription [20–37], a practice which in many instances could be leading to biological misinterpretation of findings. Thus, in order to determine how confounding of an assumption this may be, in this study, we sought to quantify and compare the underlying influence of variance in leukocyte counts versus variance in other biological factors on genome-wide transcript levels observed in whole blood, as well as assess how the decision whether or not to control for cell count heterogeneity could influence the results of conventional differential expression analyses.

Results

Donor white blood cell differential is the primary determinant of whole blood transcript levels for a majority of genes

In order to quantify the underlying drivers of whole blood gene expression patterns, we first collected venous blood samples from a diverse cohort of 138 human donors presenting to the emergency department of an academic hospital with symptoms or complications associated with a variety of highly prevalent chronic pathophysiologic states (Table 1). Then, next generation RNA sequencing was used to generate whole blood gene expression profiles, optical flow cytometry was used to generate matched circulating neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and eosinophil counts, and manual electronic medical record review was used to collect a wide range of clinical and demographic information including, age, sex, race, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), tobacco use, and final documented diagnoses for ten different chronic health conditions. Finally, a linear mixed model-based variance decomposition approach [38] was subsequently used to quantify the underlying contributions of variance in relative leukocyte counts versus variance in the other aforementioned biological factors to the observed overall inter-individual variance in whole blood transcript levels for all reliably detected protein-coding genes. Note that in this analysis, we chose to model relative neutrophil counts and relative lymphocyte counts together as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio given their inherently strong inverse interdependence.

Across the 15,896 genes for which we detected mRNA, a median of 53.2% of the variance in whole blood transcript levels were collectively explained by just the 19 latent factors we modeled, which is a substantial proportion, considering the limitless number of drivers that can theoretically influence gene expression, as well as the fact that a meaningful proportion of the residual variance was likely attributable to technical variability. Genome-wide, donor neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was overwhelmingly the largest contributor to this modeled variance, whereas monocyte count, eosinophil count, and the other biological factors we considered such as sex, age, race, ethnicity, and chronic disease diagnoses made comparatively negligible contributions (Fig. 1A). In fact, donor neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was the factor that accounted for the maximum amount of modeled variance for 68.3% of the 15,896 total genes with detectable transcript (Fig. 1B), and 75.6% if we only considered a subset of 11,826 genes that had detectable transcript in all samples, indicating constitutive expression in whole blood (Supplemental Table 1).

Inter-donor differences in leukocyte counts result in near genome-wide differential expression

Given that the results of our previous experiment suggested that donor neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio is the predominant underlying driver of whole blood gene expression patterns, we wanted to determine the degree to which differences in neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio could alter whole blood transcript levels. To do this we used the DESeq2 pipeline [39] to carry out a conventional genome-wide differential expression analysis in which we compared whole blood gene expression levels between samples from subsets of donors with neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios in the upper quartile (NLR high, n = 35) and lower quartile (NLR low, n = 35).

Donors with the highest neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios were older and exhibited lower body mass index values than those with lowest neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios; however, no other significant inter-group differences in any other clinical or demographic characteristics were observed (Table 2). In terms of transcript levels, we observed dramatic and near genome-wide differences; 70.2% of genes were identified as being significantly differentially expressed between groups in an initial zero order comparison, and these differences remained almost wholly intact when repeating the comparison while controlling for donor age and body mass index (Fig. 2A), as the gene level fold differences yielded from both comparisons were highly correlated (r = 0.997, Fig. 2B).

In order to investigate whether these gene expression differences were truly driven by differences in sample cellular composition versus technical artifacts or other biological factors, we compared them to gene expression differences that we subsequently assessed between publically available gene expression profiles originating from isolated human neutrophils (n = 6) and isolated human lymphocytes (n = 35, Fig. 2C). Gene-level fold change values were highly correlated genome-wide (r = 0.603, Fig. 2D), suggesting that the differential expression pattern we observed in whole blood between groups was indeed attributed to differences in mRNA levels resulting from the underlying difference in neutrophil and lymphocyte counts. Furthermore, in order to determine whether the extreme degree of differential expression we observed would be influenced by or was in any way attributable to our choice of computational workflow specifically, we repeated the comparison using both limma-voom [40] and edgeR [41], two other commonly used differential expression pipelines. The total number and identities of genes output as significantly differentially expressed in both analyses were nearly identical to that which we observed with DESeq2, and there was a high degree of agreement in terms of fold change values (Table 3), providing additional evidence that the extent of gene expression differences we observed in whole blood between groups were reflective of a true biological phenomenon as opposed to an analytical confound, and also that they are meaningful enough to be consistently detected regardless of specific computational workflow.

In order to contextually evaluate the strength of the phenomena observed in our prior experiments, we wanted to determine how the degree of whole blood differential expression that we observed as a result of underlying heterogeneity in sample cellular composition compares to that which can be driven by true underlying differences in nuclear transcription associated with heterogeneity in other biological factors. To do this, we again used DESeq2 to perform a series of differential expression analyses between subgroups of n = 35 and n = 35 donors which were blindly selected via computational iterative solving to exhibit complete separation in terms of a single clinical or demographic characteristic, but be closely matched in terms of all others, and have <5% difference in median neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio. Specifically, subgroups were generated to make comparisons between donors of male and female sex, donors over and under 65 years old, donors with and without metabolic syndrome, donors of Hispanic and non-Hispanic ethnicity, and donors who smoke versus do not smoke (Table 4). Given that the samples from the subgroups associated with each of these comparisons had nearly identical cellular composition, we assumed that a majority of the gene expression differences we would observe would be reflective of actual differences in nuclear transcription.

Across these comparisons, in most instances, we observed an astonishingly low degree of differential expression, especially compared to that which we observed between donors with differing neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios. Our comparisons of sex, age, ethnicity, and smoking respectively yield only 29, 91, 57, and 2 significantly differentially expressed genes, which in each instance, is well under 1% of the 15,289 total genes detected (Fig. 3A, B, D, E). Our comparison of donors with and without metabolic syndrome yielded a greater number of differentially expressed genes, 431, which is not surprising given that metabolic syndrome is a constellation of multiple underlying pathologies such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and obesity which could all alter gene expression (Fig. 3C); however, these gene expression differences still pale in comparison to those observed between samples with differing neutrophil and lymphocyte composition.

It is important to note that it is unlikely that the limited number of gene expression differences that we observed in these comparisons were due to a methodological confound, as the differences that we did observe were all biologically relevant. For example, in our sex comparison, a majority of the genes that were identified as significantly differentially expressed were located on the X or Y chromosomes, and subsequent signaling pathway enrichment analysis using g:profiler [42] (Supplemental File 6) revealed significant enrichment for genes involved in sex determination. Similarly, genes identified as being significantly differentially expressed in our metabolic syndrome comparison were enriched for those involved in mitochondrial function and cellular metabolism, while the only two genes identified as being differentially expressed in our smoking comparison, GPR15 and BOLA2B, have both been reported in prior epigenetic studies as being hypo-methylated in smokers [43–45]. Notably, the genes which were differentially expressed in our comparison of Hispanic and non-Hispanic donors were significantly enriched for those involved in interferon signaling, PD-1/PD-L1 signaling, and SARS-CoV-2 response, which is highly consistent with recent work that has demonstrated worse outcomes in Hispanic cancer patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-1 and PD-L1 [46], data indicating higher COVID-19 infection, hospitalization, and mortality rates in the Hispanic population [47], as well as numerous prior studies reporting differences in therapeutic interferon response in Hispanic individuals [48–50].

Given the numerous potential drivers of gene expression, and the relatively limited biological scope of the five aforementioned comparisons, we performed a modified permutation analysis in which we made one million unique comparisons between pseudo-randomly selected groups of n = 35 and n = 35 which were constrained only by the requirement to have median neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios within 5% of each other. Due to their sheer number, these collective permutation comparisons should theoretically capture a wide range of differences in biological factors that could alter nuclear transcription outside of those that we explicitly evaluated. Across these million comparisons, the median number of significantly differentially expressed genes was zero, and not a single comparison came close to yielding the number of differentially expressed genes that we observed between donors with differing neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios (Fig. 3F).

These data collectively suggest that inter-group heterogeneity in sample cellular composition can drive widespread differences in whole blood transcript levels in conventional differential expression analyses, and that the number and magnitude of these differences have the potential to be far greater than those which can be driven by heterogeneity in nuclear transcriptional state.

The presence or absence of inter-donor differences in leukocyte counts dramatically alters the results of differential expression analyses

Given the outsized influence of underlying sample cellular composition versus nuclear transcriptional state on whole blood mRNA levels suggested by our prior experiments, we wanted to know how the decision whether or not to control for cell count heterogeneity could influence the results of conventional differential expression analyses. To do this, we considered the results of the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio constrained differential expression analyses of sex, age, metabolic syndrome, ethnicity, and smoking that we performed in our prior experiments to be representative of the true differences in nuclear transcription associated with the aforementioned biologic and demographic factors, and performed a series of modified permutation analyses in which we repeated these contrasts one thousand times each using pseudo randomly selected groups no longer constrained to match in terms of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and compared the number and identities of genes output as differentially expressed.

With respect to age and metabolic syndrome, the median number of genes output as significantly differentially expressed across permutation comparisons of groups with unconstrained neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios was substantially greater than those observed in our comparison of groups with matched neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios, and there was incomplete overlap in terms of gene identity (Fig. 4B, C). For example, in our analysis of age, the median number of genes output as being significantly differentially expressed across unconstrained permutation comparisons was 421% greater than the number observed in our matched comparison, and a median of only 12.4% of significant genes output across unconstrained permutation comparisons overlapped with those observed in our matched comparison in terms of identity; furthermore, across unconstrained permutation comparisons, a median of 40.7% of genes which were significantly differentially expressed in our matched comparison failed to demonstrate a significant difference. This suggests that across most age and metabolic syndrome permutation comparisons, nearly all of the genes which were identified as significantly differentially expressed were driven by an underlying inter-group difference in sample cellular composition, and that inter-group differences in cell counts also obscured the ability to detect a substantial number of differences in nuclear transcription. This is not surprising given the results of our prior experiments, and the fact that there is a well-established shift toward lymphopenia with aging [51,52], as well as a shift toward neutrophilia associated with chronic inflammation in metabolic syndrome [91]. While relatively modest, these established alterations in circulating cell counts were reflected in our analyses, as the median inter-group percent difference in neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios were 26.6 and 20.3 across age and metabolic syndrome permutation comparisons respectively, with maximum values of 84.4 and 68.4.

With respect to sex, while median number of genes output as significantly differentially expressed across permutation comparisons of groups with unconstrained neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios was still greater than those observed in our comparison of groups with matched neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios, it was not as dramatic as what was seen with age and metabolic syndrome, and there was a greater degree of overlap in terms of gene identity (Fig. 4A). This was likely because there are not substantial inherent differences in neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio between sexes that would be consistently represented across permutation selections as there are with the aforementioned two factors, which was reflected in the fact that the median inter-sex percent difference in neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio across permutation comparisons was 16.4, with maximum value of only 41.2. These differences in neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio were not large enough to drive a dramatically higher number of significant genes, especially in such a modest sample size, as fold differences driven by cell type heterogeneity should be roughly linearly proportional to the magnitude of the underlying difference in cell count [53], nor could they meaningfully interfere with the ability to detect true differences in nuclear transcription, given the extremely high magnitude of the fold changes (in many case well over 100 fold) associated with the genes identified as differentially expressed in our matched comparison.

Similarly, our unconstrained permutation comparisons of ethnicity and smoking only captured relatively small inter-group percent differences in neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, with respective median values of 17.9 and 17.0, and maximum values of 48.2 and 36.2, which were again not large enough to drive substantial increases in the number of significant genes via cell count heterogeneity (Fig. 4D, E). However, these small differences in cell counts did appear to interfere with the ability to detect true differences in nuclear transcription, which is not surprising, as unlike in our analysis of sex, the genes identified as differentially expressed in our matched comparisons exhibited relatively small fold changes (in most cases <2 fold). In fact, the median number of significant genes observed across unconstrained ethnicity comparisons was 52.6% less than the number we observed in our matched comparison, with minimal overlap in terms of gene identity.

Taken together, these data suggest that in the presence of even modest inter-group differences in neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, the majority of significant genes produced in a conventional differential expression analysis are likely to be differentially expressed simply as a result of the underlying difference in cell counts, and that many differences in nuclear transcriptional state are likely to be obscured. Furthermore, they also demonstrate that in the presence of inter-group differences in donor neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, the decision whether or not to experimentally control for cell count heterogeneity has the potential to dramatically alter the results.

Discussion

Considered collectively, our results broadly suggest that donor white blood cell differential, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio specifically, is by a wide margin the single largest determinant of gene expression patterns observed at the level of whole blood. Our results also infer that the majority of significantly differentially expressed genes yielded from conventional differential expression analyses performed in whole blood are likely driven by underlying cell count heterogeneity as opposed to true differences in nuclear transcriptional state if there are even modest inter-group differences in the counts of these two leukocyte populations. Not only do these findings provide insight into those of the countless transcriptomic studies that have been carried out in whole blood to date, but they also suggest that the potential effects of cell count heterogeneity need to be more broadly considered than they currently are in future investigations in order to generate more biologically interpretable and meaningful results.

Over the past three decades, thousands of genome-wide gene expression profiling studies have been performed in whole blood, originally via microarray, and more recently by RNA sequencing. Almost none of the earliest of these studies took any steps to experimentally account for potential cell type heterogeneity, and they largely reported their results under the assumption that any observed gene expression differences were a result of true differences in nuclear transcription. While this practice has started to change, especially over the past five years, large numbers of whole blood experiments that are being performed today are still being carried out and reporting their results under this assumption [20–22,24–36]. Our findings clearly suggest that in many of these studies, this assumption is likely false. Most commonly, these prior studies have been case-control studies of disease; given the wide range of disease states that alter neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio [13–19] and the strong influence of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio on whole blood gene expression patterns which we observed here, it is likely that an overwhelming majority of genes identified as significantly differentially expressed in many of these investigations were driven simply by underlying inter-group differences in cell counts as opposed to nuclear transcriptional state as assumed.

This faulty assumption has likely led to widespread biological misinterpretation of results. For example, tens if not hundreds of investigations performed across a broad range of health conditions have inferred that altered toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling is a central driver of the immune pathology associated with the condition of interest based on observations of disproportionate differential expression of genes involved in toll-like receptor signaling in whole blood [23,54–62]. However, given their role in innate immunity [63], the various toll-like receptors themselves, as well as many of their associated downstream signaling molecules, are to a substantial degree more broadly and abundantly expressed in neutrophils than they are in lymphocytes [64,65]; this, taken with our findings, suggests that the differential expression of these genes in whole blood is likely often simply reflective of a disease-associated shift in neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, which would mean that altered regulation of toll-like receptor signaling is unlikely to be a central driver of these pathologies as many of these studies have concluded.

Given the outsized influence of donor white blood cell differential on whole blood gene expression patterns that we observed, our findings suggest that in order to produce valid results, experimentally accounting for potential cell type heterogeneity needs to be an essential component in any future transcriptomic studies of whole blood where the primary experimental aim is to identify differentially expressed genes driven by true alterations in nuclear transcription, which is often the case when trying to reveal the mechanistic drivers of immune pathologies. This can be achieved through a multitude of strategies. Where possible, performing direct enumeration of cell counts in parallelly collected blood samples with flow cytometry as we did here is ideal. In situations where it is confirmed that there are minimal differences in sample composition between donors or experimental groups, downstream analyses can likely be performed using traditional differential expression pipelines without further consideration. However, if potentially confounding differences in sample composition are observed, sample composition needs to be controlled for in the actual evaluation of gene-level fold changes and statistical significance; while there are more complex strategies, this can often be achieved by simply including the observed cell counts as covariates in the analysis [66,67], which is easily implemented using common RNA-seq software packages such as DESeq2 [39], limma-voom [40], and edgeR [41] that allow for the inclusion of covariates in experimental designs.

Unfortunately, direct measures of donor leukocyte counts are often not available. In many primary analyses, flow cytometry is often not feasible within the context of clinical sample collection, as it often needs to be performed immediately. Furthermore, in the case of secondary analyses and meta-analyses of the large volume of publically available gene expression data from whole blood, it is extremely rare to be able to retrieve accompanying information on donor cell counts. In these scenarios, while not ideal, it is still possible to successfully account for potential inter-donor differences in leukocyte counts informatically using cellular deconvolution. In its simplest implementation, cellular deconvolution can be used to correct whole blood gene expression levels for inter-donor differences in sample composition via a general two step approach; first, cellular deconvolution can be used to generate inferred cell counts for the major leukocyte populations based on the observed whole blood gene expression patterns, and then the resultant inferred cell counts can be used in lieu of actual cell counts as covariates in a conventional differential expression analysis as described above [68]. Furthermore, there are also more advanced deconvolution approaches such as CIBERSORTx [69] that have demonstrated the potential to both enumerate cell counts and also estimate the isolated gene expression patterns of each contributing cell population, which has the added advantage of being able to identify within which specific cell population any observed transcriptional changes have occurred.

Another possible way to avoid potential confounds associated with inter-donor heterogeneity in cell counts when attempting to identify true alterations in nuclear transcription in situations when directly controlling for cell counts is not feasible would be to institute a highly conservative fold change cut-off when evaluating differential expression. Many human transcriptomic studies currently being performed with whole blood institute either no or very modest fold change cut-offs during differential expression analysis [20,28,32,70], which is one of the reasons why we decided not to institute one in the work presented here. However, given that differences in whole blood RNA levels driven by cell type heterogeneity should be roughly linearly proportional to the magnitude of the underlying difference in cell count [53], and relative cell count values, particularly those of neutrophils and lymphocytes, are limited to a relatively small dynamic range, genes with higher observed fold differences are more likely to be driven by true changes in nuclear transcription. Thus, instituting a relatively high fold change cut-off such as 4 fold could significantly reduce false positive rates, however, it would also undoubtedly increase the rate of false negatives.

It is important to note that while all of the aforementioned strategies can be used to successfully control for cell count heterogeneity to identify true alterations in nuclear transcription if implemented correctly, it is unlikely that they are able to reliably reveal the entirety of such alterations in many situations, considering that the use of mathematical inference undoubtedly results in incomplete recovery of information, as well as the degree to which our results suggest cell count differences can obscure true transcriptional changes. From this perspective, it could be argued that our findings call into question the future value of whole blood as source material in prospective studies where the primary aim is explicitly identifying changes in nuclear transcriptional state, especially given the advent of single-cell RNA sequencing and the fact that it allows for direct cell-level assessment of gene expression [71], which inherently eliminates potential confounds associated with cell type heterogeneity. However, single cell sequencing currently presents barriers in terms of clinical sample collection and processing that still need to be addressed before it can be widely adopted for use in human investigations [72]; this, combined with the prevalence of existing archived whole blood samples in biobanks, will push researchers to continue to use bulk profiling of whole blood as a way of exploring immune pathology for the foreseeable future.

It is also important to note that the identification of true differences in nuclear transcriptional state is not always the primary objective of differential expression experiments performed with whole blood. Given the ease of sampling and the minimal processing required, many biomarker discovery studies profile whole blood with the goal of identifying disease-specific patterns of differences in transcript levels that could be leveraged diagnostically as part of RNA-based multianalyte algorithmic assays [2–4,8,73]. In these studies, the focus is often less on understanding the drivers of disease, and more on simply identifying differences in transcript levels that are reliably detectable in condition of interest, regardless of the mechanism by which these differences arise. In such studies, experimentally controlling for cell type heterogeneity in the actual differential expression analysis is potentially counterproductive, as gene expression differences driven by underlying disease-specific alterations to white blood cell counts still have the potential to be diagnostically useful. However, in these instances, it is still highly advisable to take steps to parse out the underlying drivers of any significant gene expression differences when interpreting and reporting the results; not only does this give biological context as to the mechanism by which the transcripts may serve as biomarkers, which is important in terms of potential clinical adoption, but also allows for consideration of whether they provide additive information relative to existing less expensive clinical diagnostic tools, such as standard hematology analysis. This can be done by simply examining the correlations between whole blood transcript levels and empirically measured donor leukocyte counts if available [74], or by performing a cell count-controlled differential expression analysis in tandem with the primary uncontrolled analysis, and assessing the pattern of overlap between the results.

Like all studies, the work performed here is not without limitations. Perhaps most notably, we had to assume we were assessing nuclear transcriptional state in our analyses by matching donors in terms of cellular composition, as opposed tomeasuring cell-level gene expression directly. Theoretically, we could have performed single-cell sequencing of the circulating leukocyte pool in parallel with bulk sequencing of whole blood, however, given the large number of donors needed to capture adequate biological variability, as well as the fact that most common single-cell sequencing sample preparation protocols which can be used in clinical settings have difficulty capturing myeloid cell populations, particularly neutrophils, such an approach would not have been feasible in terms of either cost or logistics. Furthermore, given the definitive nature of our results, it is unlikely that this alternative approach would have meaningfully altered our conclusions. It could also be argued that another limitation lies in that there are a plethora of additional factors that could alter nuclear transcription outside of those that we explicitly investigated such as circadian differences, medication use, and dietary variability. However, it is unlikely that any of these factors would have the potential to drive gene expression differences to a greater degree than heterogeneity in the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, given how much greater of an influence the counts of these cell populations had on overall gene expression levels than the other factors we did consider. This is supported by the fact that in our neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio constrained permutation analysis, which given the scale, theoretically should have been adequate to capture variability in these other latent factors, not a single one of the million permutation comparisons produced anywhere close to the number of significantly differentially expressed genes as we observed in our comparison of donors with differing neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios.

In spite of these potential limitations, considered collectively, our results provide clear evidence that donor white blood cell differential, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio specifically, is the single largest driver of gene expression patterns observed at the level of whole blood, and that true variability in nuclear transcriptional state has a comparatively small influence. These findings provide a more complete context that should help better interpret the results of the thousands of prior transcriptomic studies that have been carried out in whole blood to date, which appear to have often overestimated the contribution of differences in nuclear transcription to observed differential expression. However, they also indicate that the potential effects of cell count heterogeneity need to be more broadly considered than they currently are in future investigations, either in the experimental design or in the interpretation of the results, in order to produce findings which are more accurately reflective of underlying biology, and ultimately more meaningful.

Materials and methods

Patients

Adult donors were recruited in the emergency department at Dell-Seton Medical Center (Austin, Tx) as part of a larger ongoing acute care biomarker discovery investigation, and presented with symptoms or complications directly associated with a chronic health condition, as identified by the International Classification of Diseases version 10 (ICD-10) [75] codes designated to be linked to the 30 chronic health conditions currently recognized by the United States Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) [76,77]. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Dell-Seton Medical Center. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects or their authorized representatives prior to any study procedures.

Collection of clinical and demographic information from the electronic medical record

All demographic and clinical information, including chronic disease diagnoses, were manually extracted from the electronic medical record via an electronic case report form built and hosted using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) secure web application [78]. Chronic disease diagnoses were identified by the International Classification of Diseases version 10 (ICD-10) [75] codes aforementioned in the above subsection.

Blood sampling

Parallel venous whole blood samples were collected via PAXgene (PreAnalytiX GmbH, Hombrechtikon, CH) and K2EDTA (Becton-Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ) vacutainers at the same time as the initial clinical blood draw in the emergency department, prior to initiation of any treatment. PAXgene vacutainers were moved to −80 °C for storage until downstream RNA isolation, while K2EDTA vacutainers were used immediately for flow cytometry analysis.

White blood cell differential

White blood cell differential was immediately assessed in EDTA-treated blood via four angle optical flow cytometry on the Cell-Dyn Sapphire automated clinical hematology system using the Cell-Dyn WBC reagent pack (Abbott Diagnostics, Santa Clara, CA). Relative counts of leukocyte subpopulations were generated by dividing absolute subpopulation cell counts by the absolute total leukocyte count. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was calculated as relative neutrophil count divided by relative lymphocyte count.

RNA sequencing of whole blood

Total RNA was isolated from archived PAXgene-stabilized whole blood using the PAXgene IVD Blood RNA Kit (PreAnalytiX GmbH). RNA purity was assessed using spectrophotometry (NanoDrop 3300, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA), RNA concentration was assessed using fluorometry (Qubit RNA broad range assay kit, Thermo Scientific), and RNA integrity was assessed using chip capillary electrophoresis (2100 Bioanalyzer, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA).

Ribosomal RNA and globin mRNA-depleted cDNA libraries were prepared from 500 ng of total RNA using the illumina TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Ribo-Zero Globin kit (Illumina, Santa Clara, CA). Paired-end 150 bp sequencing was performed on the illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Pre-processing of raw data was performed using R (R Project for Statistical Computing) [79]. Low quality reads were filtered, and remaining reads were aligned to human reference genome GRCh38 using the HISAT2 pipeline via the ‘rhisat2’ package [80]. Counts of mapped reads summarized at the gene level using the featureCounts() function of the ‘Rsubread’ package [81], and the biologic functions of said genes were retrieved using the ‘biomaRt’ package [82]. Genes that exhibited read counts of >0 in at least 25% of samples were marked as reliably detected. Read counts associated with genes which were annotated as both protein-coding and reliably detected were used for downstream analyses.

Retrieval and processing of gene expression data from isolated leukocyte subpopulations

RNA sequencing data generated from isolated human neutrophils, as well as various lymphocyte populations including naive CD4+ T cells, naive CD8+ T cells, memory CD4+ T cells, memory CD8+ T cells, naive B cells, and memory B cells, originally sampled from the peripheral blood healthy donors by Uhlen et al. [83] were retrieved as gene-level summarized transcripts per million (TPM) values from the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/about/download). Gene annotations were updated, and the biologic functions of said genes were retrieved, using the ‘biomaRt’ package [82] for R. Genes that exhibited TPM values of >0 in at least 25% of either neutrophil or lymphocyte samples were marked reliably detected. TPM values associated with genes which were annotated as both protein-coding and reliably detected were used for downstream analyses.

Decomposition of gene expression variance

Genome-wide decomposition of variance in whole blood gene expression levels was carried out using the ‘variancePartition’ package [38] for R. Filtered read counts were scaled by sequencing depth to yield normalized values in terms of reads per million (RPM). Normalized counts, as well as any non-normal continuous predictor variables, were log transformed prior to analysis. The fitExtractVarPartModel() function was used under default settings to model genewise variance and generate the estimated underlying fractional explained variance attributable to each predictor.

Iterative solving for generation of matched experimental subgroups

An R-based computational iterative solving strategy was employed to pseudo-randomly select the experimental subgroups used in both unperumutated and permutated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio constrained differential expression analyses, as well as permutated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio unconstrained differential expression analyses. A combination of the repeat() and sample() functions of base R were used to repetitively randomly select sets of n = 35 and n = 35 donors from the overall subject pool split according to the biological or demographic factor of interest until a set was found which met the relevant constraints for the specific experimental context in terms of inter-group matching. For unpermutated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio constrained differential expression analyses, outside of the factor of interest, groups were required to exhibit <5% difference in the medians of any clinical and demographic characteristics measured on a continuous scale, including neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, as well as an absolute difference of <0.1 in the proportions of any clinical and demographic characteristics measured on a categorical scale. For permutated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio unconstrained differential expression analyses, the same constraints were applied for group selection within each permutation, lest that on neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio. For permutated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio constrained differential expression analyses, only the constraint on neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was applied for group selection within each permutation.

Permutation analyses

Permutation analyses were carried out using basic scripts employing the repeat() function of base R and were paralyzed using the ‘doSNOW’ package. Within each permutation, groups were selected using the iterative solving strategy outlined in the above subsection, and differential expression was assessed using the ‘DESeq2’ package.

Differential expression analyses

DESeq2-based differential expression analyses were carried out using the ‘DESeq2’ package [39] for R. In the case of whole blood RNAseq data, analyses were performed on filtered read counts, which were corrected for sequencing depth by providing scaling factors using the sizeFactors() function. Scaling factors were calculated for each sample by dividing the observed library size of said sample by the minimum library size observed across all samples. In the case of RNAseq data from isolated leukocyte subpopulations, TPM values were first multiplied by 10,000 and rounded to yield integer values, which were subsequently used for analysis. Genewise log2 fold differences adjusted for dispersion, as well as any covariates where applicable, were generated via negative binomial generalized linear model using the DESeq() function, and statistical significance was evaluated via Wald test using the results() function.

Limma-voom-based differential expression analysis was carried out using the ‘limma’ package [40] for R. Analysis was performed on filtered read counts, which were corrected for sequencing depth by passing the observed library sizes for each sample to the voom() function. Genewise log2 fold differences adjusted for dispersion were generated via linear model using a combination of the voom(), lmFit(), and contrasts.fit() functions, and statistical significance was evaluated via empirical Bayes moderated t-statistic using the eBayes() function.

edgeR-based differential expression analysis was carried out using the ‘edgeR’ package [41] for R. Analysis was performed on filtered read counts, which were corrected for sequencing depth by passing the observed library sizes for each sample to the DGEList() function. Genewise log2 fold differences adjusted for dispersion were generated via negative binomial generalized linear model using the estimateDisp() function, and statistical significance was evaluated via exact test using the exactTest() function.

P-values were corrected for multiple comparisions using the Benjamini-Hochberg method [84] in all of the aformentioned analyses. Furthermore, note that in all of these analyses, the only data normalization that was performed was simple correction for sequencing depth; this was an intentional choice, as one of the fundamental assumptions of a majority of commonly used global scaling strategies such as median-ratio, trimmed mean of m-values, and quantile normalization is that overall transcriptome composition is relatively similar across samples [85], and the extreme cell type heterogeneity which was inherently present in a majority of our comparisons would result in violation of this assumption. Example code depicting the exact settings used in each differential expression pipeline are available via GitHub (https://github.com/oconnell-laboratory/project-005).

Pathway enrichment analysis

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the g:Profiler toolkit [42]. Differentially expressed gene lists were queried against biological pathways annotated in the KEGG [86], Reactome [87], WikiPathways [88], Gene Ontology [89], and Human Phenotype Ontology [90] databases while using the entirety of genes reliably detected by sequencing as background. P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the set counts and sizes algorithm developed by Raudvere et al. specifically for gene set enrichment experiments [42].

Statistics

All conventional statistical tests were carried out using the R ‘stats’ package. Mann-Whitney U test was used for the comparison of continuous variables, while Fisher’s exact test was used for the comparison of categorical variables. Spearman’s rho was used to assess the strength and significance of correlational relationships. Sample sizes were arbitrarily determined. In the case of all statistical testing, the null hypothesis was rejected when p < 0.05. The parameters of all statistical tests performed are outlined in detail within the figure legends.

R versioning and high performance computing

All R analyses were carried out using R version 4.3. R scripts were executed via bash shell on a high performance computing cluster built using intel CPUs and a Red Hat Enterprise Linux framework (Core Facility for Advanced Research Computing, Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, OH).
