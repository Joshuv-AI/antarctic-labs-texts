The therapeutic effects of the VEGF decoy receptor fusion protein VEGF-Grab in chronic inflammatory diseases
Authors: Youngeun Lee, Hee-Young Lee, Hye Seong Lim, Jin A. Shin, Hyun Gul Yang, Saseong Lee, Grazia Mercadante, Valeria Cicatiello, Sandro De Falco, Hyungmin Kim, Chaerin Kim, Su-Hyun Lee, Yu-Mi Kim, Seung-Ah Yoo, Seung-Hyo Lee, Wan-Uk Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPlacental growth factor (PlGF) plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and multiple sclerosis (MS), by enhancing angiogenesis, disease-aggravating features of fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs), and pathogenic T helper type 17 (Th17) cells. The therapeutic potential of a VEGF decoy receptor fusion protein, VEGF-Grab, which potently binds and neutralises VEGF and PlGF, has not been studied in autoimmune diseases. Therefore, we investigated the therapeutic effects of VEGF-Grab in RA and MS by using in vitro and in vivo models.MethodsTwo forms of VEGF-Grab (PB101 and PB102) were engineered to enhance their binding affinity for VEGF and PlGF. In vitro assays-including Transwell migration and wound-healing assays of endothelial cells and FLSs, as well as Th17 cell differentiation-were employed to evaluate the effects of VEGF-Grab on angiogenesis and immune-cell function. To assess therapeutic efficacy in vivo, mouse models of inflammatory and autoimmune arthritis, as well as experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), were utilised.FindingsOur results indicate that VEGF-Grab exerted significant inhibitory effects across various animal models of autoimmune diseases, targeting abnormal angiogenesis, cartilage invasion, and pathogenic Th17 cells. Furthermore, combined treatment with IFN-β in the EAE model suggested enhanced efficacy of therapeutic effects.InterpretationOur findings highlight the multifaceted therapeutic potential of VEGF-Grab in RA and MS, targeting angiogenesis, the aggressiveness of FLSs, and the pathogenicity of Th17 cells, all driven by the ‘angio-lymphokine’, PlGF. Thus, VEGF-Grab has the potential to be a therapeutic candidate for the treatment of RA and MS, warranting further clinical exploration.FundingThis study was supported by grants funded by the National Research Foundation of Korea (RS-2024-00442793 to W.U.K.), and Biomlogic/GenoFocus and Panolos Biosciences (to S.H.L.).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

The global incidence of autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and multiple sclerosis (MS) is steadily increasing worldwide. Yet it remains unclear why some patients respond to current therapies whereas others do not. Patients who are not responsive to first-line therapy eventually move on to second-line therapies, which are mostly potent immune suppressive agents and carry high risks of infection. Therefore, a fundamentally new approach to therapy that does not inhibit the overall immune system but selectively blocks abnormal autoimmune responses is needed. In previous works, we investigated the role of placental growth factor (PlGF) in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases through the regulation of Th17 cells and angiogenesis. However, PlGF has not been extensively explored as a therapeutic target in autoimmune diseases. In this study, we assessed the therapeutic potential of a vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) decoy receptor fusion protein, VEGF-Grab, which neutralises both VEGF and PlGF in the context of RA and MS.

Added value of this study

Two forms of VEGF-Grab (PB101 and PB102) were engineered to enhance their affinity for VEGF and PlGF. In in vitro assays using an endothelial cell line and fibroblast-like synoviocytes derived from patients with RA, VEGF-Grab effectively suppressed angiogenesis and the invasive behaviour of these cell types, as well as inhibiting in vitro Th17 cell function and differentiation. To assess the therapeutic effects of VEGF-Grab, various in vivo models of RA and MS were induced, demonstrating efficacy in reducing pathogenic manifestations. Of the two forms of VEGF-Grab, PB102, the optimised variant, exhibited higher therapeutic efficacy. Taken together, the results of this study support VEGF-Grab as a therapeutic approach for inflammatory and autoimmune diseases such as RA and MS.

Implications of all the available evidence

VEGF-Grab targets both VEGF and PlGF, two key proteins involved in inflammation, immune dysregulation, and abnormal angiogenesis, thereby addressing the limitations of current therapeutic approaches for autoimmune diseases. Experimental results from various animal models demonstrated reduced severity of joint destruction and CNS inflammation. This study also suggests that combining VEGF-Grab with IFN-β, a current first-line therapy for patients with MS, might improve outcomes in those who are unresponsive to first-line treatments. These findings support VEGF-Grab as a dual-action therapeutic strategy by inhibiting VEGF and PlGF, offering potential benefit for currently intractable diseases such as RA and MS.

Introduction

Placental growth factor (PlGF) is a pro-angiogenic factor belonging to the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) family, with approximately 53% sequence homology to VEGF.1,2 Although both PlGF and VEGF bind to VEGFR1, VEGFR2 serves as a receptor exclusively for VEGF, not for PlGF. In addition to VEGFR1, PlGF also transmits signals through heparan sulphate and neuropilin-1/2 receptors.3 PlGF has been reported to play a crucial role in autoimmune diseases by amplifying pathogenic angiogenesis and increasing inflammatory responses through the induction of autoreactive T helper type 17 (Th17) cells.4, 5, 6 In rheumatoid arthritis (RA), it activates synoviocytes and mononuclear cells, thereby exacerbating inflammation and articular damage.7, 8, 9 Furthermore, it is known to promote synoviocyte proliferation and increase angiogenesis in the articular region, thus contributing to pannus formation.7,10,11 Beyond its role in RA, PlGF-induced Th17 cells have a significant impact on neurological autoimmunity conditions such as multiple sclerosis (MS).4 Animal models have demonstrated that PlGF deficiency attenuates autoimmunity against myelin sheaths and reduces the severity of neuropathy. However, to date, no previous studies have reported the use of PlGF-neutralising tools to treat autoimmune diseases.

VEGF-Grab is an Fc fusion protein that includes the second and third Ig-like domains of VEGFR1, the common receptor for PlGF and VEGF.12 VEGF-Grab exhibits enhanced in vivo inhibitory effects on PlGF, VEGF-A, and VEGF-B through the introduction of several glycosylation sites, which reduce non-specific binding to the extracellular matrix (Supplementary Fig. S1). VEGF-Grab has been shown to have greater anti-angiogenic efficacy and VEGF-neutralising effects than aflibercept (Eylea),13 leading to effective inhibition of tumour-associated angiogenesis and synergistic efficacy with radiation therapy that suppresses tumour progression and metastasis.14 However, the therapeutic efficacy of VEGF-Grab in autoimmune diseases remains to be elucidated.

In this study, we investigated the potential of VEGF-Grab in two distinct autoimmune disease models, RA and MS, and demonstrated that VEGF-Grab effectively inhibits PlGF-mediated pathogenic angiogenesis and disease-aggravating inflammatory responses, including Th17 cell activation, in these diseases. Furthermore, we confirmed the therapeutic effects of VEGF-Grab in animal models of RA and MS. Our findings suggest that VEGF-Grab has a potential for development as a therapeutic agent targeting PlGF-driven autoimmune diseases such as RA and MS. Lastly, VEGF-Grab exhibits an improved pharmacokinetic profile and minimal toxicity compared to a Fc-fusion protein using native VEGFR1 domains,12 and is amenable to large-scale production. Therefore, our data substantially advance the understanding of the PlGF-driven pathogenesis of RA and MS and offer an approach to clinical intervention.

Methods

Cell culture

The human endothelial cell line EA.hy926 was purchased from ATCC (catalog CRL-2922; ATCC, Manassas, VA) and maintained according to the instructions of the distributor. Cell line authentication was performed by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling (Cosmogenetech Inc., Seoul, South Korea), and mycoplasma contamination was routinely excluded using the e-Myco™ VALiD Mycoplasma PCR Detection Kit (catalog 25239; iNtRON Biotechnology, Sungnam, South Korea). The cells were cultured in DMEM (catalog LM-001-09; Welgene, Gyeongsan, South Korea) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; catalog S001-07; Welgene) and 1% antibiotics/antimycotics. Fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs) were obtained from the synovial tissues of patients with RA as previously described.15 RA-FLSs from passages 3 to 7 were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics/antimycotics. Cells isolated from spleens, lymph nodes (LNs), and the central nervous system (CNS) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (catalog LM011-01; Welgene) containing 10% FBS, 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (catalog 15140122; Gibco, Waltham, MA). All cells were incubated at 37 °C and supplied with 5% CO2.

Patients and ethical approval

In total, eight patients who met the 2010 RA classification criteria of the American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) were included in the FLS studies.16 The study population was not restricted by sex during the design phase; however, consistent with the known female predominance of rheumatoid arthritis (approximately a 3:1 female-to-male ratio),17 seven of the eight enrolled patients were female (Table 1). All participants were of Asian ethnicity. Biological sex and ethnicity were recorded for all participants and cross-verified against official medical records to ensure accuracy. The mean age of the patients with RA was 58.7 ± 8.6 years. The mean disease duration was 6.2 ± 1.7 years. Among the eight patients with RA, six (75.0%) were positive for rheumatoid factor and seven (87.5%) were positive for the anti-cyclic citrullinated antibodies. All patients had moderate-to-high disease activity (Disease Activity Score in 28 joints [DAS28] >3.2) at baseline. The mean age of the non-RA controls was 61.9 ± 11.7 years old (Table 1). The experimental procedures with FLSs derived from patients with RA were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Catholic University of Korea (approval number: KC21TISI0123). Mice

Wild-type C57BL/6 mice and DBA/1 mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbour, ME) and Japan SLC, Inc. (Shizuoka, Japan), respectively. PlGF transgenic (Plgf-Tg) mice, in which the murine Plgf gene orthologous to the human PlGF-2 gene is overexpressed specifically in T cells, were obtained as previously described.18 The number of mice used in the in vivo experiments was determined by the maximum number of animals that could be housed per cage at each facility. All mice were maintained under specific-pathogen free conditions. All animal experiments were conducted with approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Catholic University of Korea (approval number: CUMC-2022-0046-03) and the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST; approval number: KA2021-072).

Wound-healing assay and Transwell migration assay

For the wounding-healing assay, EA.hy926 endothelial cells and RA-FLSs were seeded into 6-well plates the day before wounding at cell densities of 4 × 105 and 1.5 × 105, respectively. The attached cells were manually wounded by scratching with a 200 μL yellow pipette tip and then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Next, the cells were treated for 12 h with medium containing VEGF (200 ng/mL; catalog 100-20; Peprotech, Cranbury, NJ) or PlGF-2 (100 ng/mL; catalog 465-PL; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) with or without PB101 (2, 5, or 10 μg/mL) and supplemented with 1% FBS for EA.hy926 cells and 0.1% FBS for RA-FLSs. The recombinant PlGF-2 used in this study was generously provided by Dr. Sandro de Falco from the Institute of Genetics and Biophysics in Italy and was generated as previously described.4 The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan) and stained with crystal violet solution. The cells that migrated beyond a reference line were manually counted. For the Transwell migration assay, EA.hy926 cells and RA-FLSs were detached from the culture plate, and 1 × 105 of them were loaded into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size; Falcon, Union City, CA) that had been precoated with 0.1% gelatin for 1 h at 37 °C. The lower chambers were loaded with medium containing 0.1% FBS for EA.hy926 cells and 1% FBS for the RA-FLSs. Cell migration toward the underside of the Transwell insert membrane was enhanced by adding VEGF (100 ng/mL) or PlGF-2 (40 or 100 ng/mL) to the lower chamber, both in the presence and absence of PB101 (2, 5, or 10 μg/mL). After 12 h, the cells remaining on the upper side of the membrane were removed, and the cells that had migrated to the lower side were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet solution. The numbers of cells that migrated to the lower side of the membrane were manually counted.

Tube formation assay

The capillary tube formation assay was conducted using EA.hy926 endothelial cells. 50 μL of Matrigel (catalog 354234; Corning, New York, NY) were added to each well of a 96-well plate and allowed to solidify by incubation for 30 min at 37 °C. EA.hy926 cells (3 × 104) were suspended in a serum-free medium containing either VEGF (500 ng/mL) or PlGF-2 (500 ng/mL), with or without PB101 (10 or 50 μg/mL). These cell suspensions were added to the Matrigel-coated wells and incubated for 5 h. The area of the capillary tubes formed was measured using ImageJ software.

Humanised synovitis model using severe-combined immunodeficient (SCID) mice

Male SCID mice aged ≥10 weeks were implanted with RA-FLSs and normal human cartilage, as described previously.19 RA-FLSs (2 × 106 per 100 μL) were resuspended in culture medium containing PlGF-2 (100 ng/mL) with or without PB101 (2 μg/mL) and absorbed onto a gelatin sponge (catalog 05610101; Mascia Brunelli, Milano, Italy). A piece of human cartilage was then embedded in the sponge. The cartilage–sponge complexes containing RA-FLSs were subcutaneously implanted into the SCID mice on the left side (primary site), and complexes without RA-FLSs were implanted on the right side (contralateral site). This procedure was maintained for 60 days, accompanied by intraperitoneal injections of PB101 (2 mg/kg) twice a week. Subsequently, the cartilage tissues were excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. The degree of invasion into the cartilage was assessed as previously described20: score 0 = absence of invasion, score 0.5 = invasion spanning one to two cell layers, score 1 = invasion extending through three to five cell layers, score 1.5 = invasion across three to five cell layers observed at three distinct sites within the cartilage, score 2 = invasion encompassing six to ten cell layers, score 2.5 = invasion spanning six to ten cell layers observed at three independent sites, score 3 = invasion exceeding ten cell layers, score 3.5 = invasion extending beyond ten cell layers observed at two independent sites, score 4 = invasion extending beyond ten cell layers observed at three or more sites within the cartilage. Each cartilage specimen was subdivided into three distinct regions, which was evaluated separately, and the resulting regional scores were treated as independent data points.

In vivo Matrigel plug assay

Splenic CD4+ T cells were isolated from PlGF transgenic mice and stimulated with anti-CD3/CD28 Abs (1 μg/mL, catalog 100340; catalog 102116; BioLegend, San Diego, CA) for 48 h. Matrigel (500 μL) plugs containing recombinant PlGF-2 (200 ng/mL), PlGF-overexpressing CD4+ T cells (5 × 105), or their supernatant (250 μL) with or without PB101 (50 μg/mL) were implanted subcutaneously into the flanks of 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice. Some mice were intraperitoneally injected with PB101 (50 mg/kg) every other day. After 14 days, the solidified Matrigel plugs were excised, cryosectioned into 7 μm thickness using optimal cutting temperature compound, and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E). To quantify the macrophage infiltration, immunohistochemical staining was performed using an anti-F4/80 Ab (catalog MCA497GA; Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). F4/80-positive cells were manually counted in three random fields per section.

The in vitro differentiation and re-stimulation of Th17 cells

Immune cells were isolated from the spleens and LNs of C57BL/6 mice and subsequently purified by magnetic separation using a naïve CD4+ T cell isolation kit (catalog 130-104-453; Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). For Th17 cell differentiation, a 96-well flat-bottom plate (catalog 30096; SPL, Pocheon, South Korea) was pre-coated with mouse 0.5 μg/mL anti-CD3 antibody (catalog 100340; BioLegend). Then, 1.5 × 105 cells were cultured with 0.25 μg/mL anti-CD28 (catalog 102116; BioLegend), 1 μg/mL anti–IFN–γ (catalog 505834; BioLegend), 1 μg/mL anti-IL-4 (catalog 504122; BioLegend), and 1 μg/mL anti-IL-2 (catalog 503706; BioLegend) antibodies; 20 ng/mL mouse IL-6 (catalog 406-ML; R&D Systems); 1 ng/mL human TGF-β (catalog 7754-BH; R&D Systems); and 55 μM β-mercaptoethanol. For pathogenic Th17 cells differentiation, 20 ng/mL mouse IL-β (catalog 554577; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) and 30 ng/mL human IL-23 (catalog 11349-IL; R&D Systems) were added to the Th17 differentiation conditions. For re-stimulation, Th17 cells were rested for three days in 1.6 ng/mL mouse IL-2 and 1 ng/mL mouse IL-6, and then re-stimulated under the same Th17 differentiation conditions. All procedures were performed using RPMI 1640 medium with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. During the differentiation, cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspension samples were stained with Fixable Viability dye 780 (catalog 565388; BD Biosciences), Fc-block (catalog 553142; BD Biosciences), and fluorochorme-conjugated antibodies (BD Biosciences, BioLegend) at 4 °C for 30 min, and were fixed and permeabilised using an intracellular fixation and permeabilization buffer set (catalog 00-8222-49; eBioscience, San Diego, CA). For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were re-stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, 50 ng/mL, Sigma–Aldrich), ionomycin (1000 ng/mL, Sigma–Aldrich), brefeldin A (3 μg/mL, catalog 00-4506-51; eBioscience), and monensin (2 μM, Sigma–Aldrich) for 4 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Prior to surface staining. After the same staining and fix procedure, the samples were permeabilized with permeabilization buffer (catalog 00-8333-56; eBioscience) and stained at RT for 30 min using anti-cytokine fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (BD Biosciences, BioLegend) diluted in permeabilization buffer. Except for intracellular staining, all staining and washing processes were carried out with FACS staining buffer consisting of 2% FBS, 2 mM EDTA, and 0.02% sodium azide in Dulbecco's PBS (catalog LB001-02; Welgene). Data acquisition was performed within 24 h using an LSR Fortessa X-20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analysed with the gating strategies described in Supplementary Fig. S2.

Adjuvant-induced arthritis (AIA) and collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse models

To induce AIA, 50 μL of complete Freund's adjuvant containing 2 mg/mL of heat-killed Mycobacterium tuberculosis (CFA; catalog 7009; Chondrex, Woodinville, WA) was injected subcutaneously into the footpads of the hind limbs of 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice. The same amount of incomplete Freund's adjuvant (IFA; catalog 7002; Chondrex) was administered to the opposite paw as a negative control. VEGF-Grab (PB101 or PB102) was administered to the AIA mice daily via intraperitoneal injection, and swelling in the ankle and footpad was measured using a microcalliper every day for 2 weeks. The changes in thickness of the arthritic limbs are expressed as a percentage calculated by dividing the daily measured thickness of the ankle and footpad to the dorsum by that measured on day 0. To induce CIA, 8-week-old male DBA/1 mice were immunised with bovine type II collagen (catalog 20021; Chondrex), as previously described.21 Briefly, type II collagen (4 mg/mL in 0.01 N acetic acid) was emulsified with CFA containing 4 mg/mL of heat-killed M. tuberculosis in a 1:1 ratio. The mice were injected intradermally at the base of the tail with 50 μL of the emulsion (containing 100 μg of type II collagen) as a primary immunisation. Two weeks after the primary immunisation, booster injections with 100 μg of type II collagen in IFA were given through the foot pad. VEGF-Grab (PB101 or PB102) was administered intraperitoneally twice a week, and arthritis severity was determined by visual inspection, as previously described.21 For the histological assessment, the arthritic limbs from AIA and CIA mice were harvested after sacrifice, fixed in 4% formaldehyde, and decalcified using a decalcifying solution-lite reagent (catalog D0818; Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Tissue sections were obtained and stained with H&E. The degrees of inflammation, synovial proliferation, and bone destruction were evaluated (grade 0–3) as reported previously.21 For CIA tissues, anti-F4/80 Ab (catalog MCA497GA; Bio-Rad) and anti-CD31 Ab (catalog ab28364; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) were applied for immunohistochemical staining. F4/80-positive cells and CD31-positive blood vessels were manually counted in three random fields per section. The percentage of F4/80-positive macrophages was determined as the ratio of F4/80-positive cells to total nucleated cells. The extent of pannus formation was scored (grade 0–4) as described by Tang et al.22: grade 0, no signs of synovial proliferation; grade 1, mild hyperplasia of the synovial lining; grades 2 through 4, increasing degrees of synovial proliferation and synovial protrusion into the joint cavity.

Experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) mouse model

To induce EAE, 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were immunised subcutaneously on both sides of their abdominal flanks with 200 μg of MOG35-55 peptide (MEVGWYRSPFSRVVHLYRNGK, GenicBio, Shanghai, China) emulsified in 200 μL of 4 mg/mL CFA (catalog 7001; Chondrex). On days 0 and 2, the mice received additional intraperitoneal injections of 200 ng of pertussis toxin (catalog 180; List Biological Labs, Campbell, CA). Following the primary immunisation, the mice were re-immunised with the same MOG and CFA emulsion on day 7. The EAE phenotypes were scored as follows: score 1 = partially paralysed tail, score 2 = fully paralysed tail, score 3 = hind limb paresis, score 4 = one hind limb fully paralysed, score 5 = both hind limbs paralysed, score 6 = weakness in forelimbs, score 7 = one forelimb fully paralysed, score 8 = both hind limbs and forelimbs paralysed, score 9 = moribund.

ELISA

Cytokines in the culture medium were quantified by sandwich ELISA using 96-well plates (catalog 655061; Greiner Bio-One, Kremsmünster, Austria) according to the manufacturer's instructions (BD Biosciences). Briefly, ELISA plates were pre-coated with 50 μL of 1:250 diluted capture antibodies and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C or overnight at 4 °C, and then blocked with 200 μL of I-block or 10% FBS. After washing, 50 μL of each sample was transferred to the 96-well plate. After 2 h of reaction, 50 μL of 1:500–1000 detection antibodies were added and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C or RT. After a further washing step, 50 μL of streptavidin-alkaline phosphatase (catalog 554065; BD Biosciences) or streptavidin HRP (catalog 554066; BD Biosciences) was added and incubated at RT for 30 min. After the final washing, 70–100 μL of alkaline phosphatase substrate (5 mM of p-nitrophenyl phosphate substrate in 0.1 M alkaline buffer, Sigma–Aldrich) or TMB substrate solution (catalog 421101; BioLegend) was added and incubated at RT for colour development. When sufficient colour development had occurred, the reaction was terminated by adding 40 μL of 0.5 N sodium hydroxide (NaOH) or 1 N sulphuric acid (H2SO4). Optical density was measured at 405 nm or 450 nm using an ELISA plate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA). Data are presented as the mean and standard error of the mean (SEM). For reliable statistical evaluation, all experimental groups consisted of at least three independent data points. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using an unpaired or paired t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, Welch's t-test, or Wilcoxon matched-pairs test, as appropriate. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's multiple-comparisons test or the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's multiple-comparisons test was applied. Time-course data from animal models involving two or more groups were analysed using two-way ANOVA. P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant, whereas values above this threshold were denoted as non-significant (ns). To minimise bias, no in vitro or in vivo data were excluded from the analyses, except in cases where experimental animals died (one mouse in the CIA model and multiple mice in the EAE model treated with doses exceeding 2 mg/kg). All statistical analyses were also performed by an independent researcher who was not involved in the experimental procedures.

Results

Mitigation of angiogenesis by VEGF-Grab

In inflamed rheumatoid joints, the invasive pannus is characterised by increased vascularity with synovial hyperplasia, and the use of anti-angiogenic strategies has been shown to reduce pannus formation.23 To test the therapeutic effects of VEGF-Grab (PB101), a VEGF- and PlGF-targeting soluble decoy receptor protein, we first investigated whether it affects endothelial cell migration and tube formation. In a previous study, VEGF-Grab was used at 14 nmol/L (approximately 1–2 μg/mL) in endothelial cell migration and tube-formation assays in the presence of VEGF; this concentration was therefore used as a reference to define 2 μg/mL as the minimal effective working concentration.12 In the present study, in both Transwell chemotactic migration and wound-healing assays, PB101 significantly reduced the VEGF- and PlGF-mediated migration of EA.hy926 endothelial cells (Fig. 1A and B). Furthermore, PB101 almost completely inhibited the capillary tube formation by EA.hy926 cells induced by VEGF and PlGF treatment (Fig. 1C). Next, to test the anti-angiogenic efficacy of PB101 in vivo, we conducted a Matrigel plug assay, in which mice were injected with Matrigel plugs mixed with VEGF or PlGF to promote blood vessel formation within the plugs; based on the effective anti-angiogenic dose of PB101 in vitro (5 μg/mL), a ten-fold higher concentration (50 μg/mL) was selected for the in vivo experiments. Compared with groups that received only PlGF in the plugs, mice given PB101 both within the plugs and intraperitoneally exhibited markedly lower levels of blood vessel development in the plug (Fig. 1D). Moreover, PB101 significantly mitigated the pro-angiogenic effects of PlGF-overexpressing CD4+ T cells and culture supernatants derived from PlGF-overexpressing T cells (Fig. 1E). Consistent with its anti-angiogenic effects, treatment with PB101 reduced macrophage infiltration into the plug, which was largely induced by PlGF and VEGF (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. S3A). Taken together, these findings suggest that VEGF-Grab has potent anti-angiogenic properties, both in vitro and in vivo.

Suppression of the aggressive behaviour of RA-FLSs by VEGF-Grab

In addition to increased angiogenesis, synoviocyte hyperplasia is a pathological feature of the invasive pannus in RA.24 In particular, during the pathogenesis of RA, RA-FLSs are known to undergo abnormal proliferation, significantly contributing to the formation of pannus, a major pathogenic symptom of RA.24 RA-FLSs have excessive motility and secrete proteases that crucially facilitate the degradation of cartilage, positioning RA-FLSs as a central contributor to the pathology driven by pannus.24 Thus, we examined whether PB101 alleviates the motility and cartilage destruction capabilities of RA-FLSs. In both Transwell migration and wounding-healing assays, PB101 significantly reduced the migration of RA-FLSs induced by PlGF and VEGF (Fig. 2A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S3). There was no significant difference in the extent of suppression between VEGF- and PlGF-stimulated responses following treatment with the same dose of PB101 (Supplementary Fig. S3B and C). Additional studies have shown that both PlGF-1 and PlGF-2 activate extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signalling via the Flt-1 and neuropilin-1 receptors, thereby promoting RA-FLS survival, migration, and invasion.7 As expected, PlGF stimulation of RA-FLSs markedly enhanced ERK phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Notably, the PlGF-induced increase in FLS migration was abrogated by either PB101 treatment (5 μg/mL) or the ERK inhibitor PD98059 (20 μM), suggesting that PB101 attenuates the pro-migratory capacity of RA-FLSs, at least in part, through suppression of the ERK pathway (Supplementary Fig. S3E and F). To evaluate the in vivo inhibitory effects of PB101 on the ability of RA-FLSs to degrade cartilage, we used a humanised synovitis model that incorporated human cartilage into a gelatin sponge impregnated with RA-FLSs (Fig. 2C). In this SCID mouse co-implantation model, the invasion of contralateral cartilage-which is initially devoid of FLS-serves as a quantitative measure of the haematogenous dissemination of RA-FLS from the primary site (Fig. 2C).25 The results showed that PB101 significantly reduced the destruction of cartilage mediated by RA-FLSs at the primary site (Fig. 2D). Notably, PB101 administration profoundly attenuated the contralateral invasion score (Fig. 2D), demonstrating its potent therapeutic efficacy in blocking the joint-to-joint spread of pathogenic FLS. Production of an improved version of VEGF-Grab, PB102

To improve the function of VEGF-Grab, PB101 was modified through targeted protein engineering, including mutations to increase its negative surface charge and structural modifications that enhanced its molecular stability via intermolecular covalent linkages. These modifications substantially increased the quality, molecular stability, and functionality of PB101, and this improved molecule was named PB102. An analysis of CHO cell-produced proteins by SE-HPLC revealed that PB102 had significantly higher monomer content than PB101, with reduced high-molecular-weight aggregates (PB102: 13.6%, PB101: 39.7%) (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Given the various factors in serum that can degrade proteins after injection, the stability of PB101 and PB102 in serum was evaluated. Both PB101 and PB102 were spiked into serum and incubated under physiological conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). PB102 maintained stable concentrations throughout the 168 h, whereas PB101 showed time-dependent degradation (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Pharmacokinetic studies in Sprague–Dawley rats (3 mg/kg, intravenous) showed that PB102 had superior in vivo stability, with detectable levels persisting through 336 h, with approximately 3-fold higher area under the curve compared with PB101, which was undetectable after 96 h (Supplementary Fig. S4C). To evaluate the antagonistic effects of PB101 and PB102 against VEGF-A or PlGF, IC50 values were determined using an in vitro model of angiogenesis with EA.hy926 cell line. Compared with PB101, PB102 exhibited a modestly enhanced antagonising effect. Consistently, in PlGF-stimulated EA.hy926 cells, PB102 showed a slightly lower IC50 value than that of PB101, indicating both PB101 and PB102 effectively antagonise PlGF (Supplementary Fig. S4D and E).

Next, to assess the safety profiles of PB101 and PB102, cynomolgus monkeys (Macaca fascicularis) were administered with high doses of PB101 (100, 200, and 400 mg/kg). All animals survived for at least 4 weeks post-administration without any symptoms of lethargy or other overt clinical symptoms (Supplementary Fig. S5A). Similarly, mice received PB102 at doses of 100, 200, and 400 mg/kg, and no mortality or weight loss was observed (Supplementary Fig. S5B and C). Consistent with these findings, PB102 administration did not alter serum levels of total protein, albumin, globulin, creatine, urea nitrogen, alanine aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, and c-reactive protein (Supplementary Fig. S5D). Moreover, total cell counts and immune cell subsets were comparable (Supplementary Fig. S5E). The weight and gross morphology of major organs, including the spleen, kidney, and liver were also unaffected by PB102 administration (Supplementary Fig. S5F-H). Finally, no significant weight loss or mortality was observed in mice treated with PB102 (10 mg/kg) during CIA induction (Supplementary Fig. S5I). These results collectively indicate a favourable safety profile for both PB101 and PB102 under the tested condition.

Lastly, an SPR analysis revealed that PB102 exhibited enhanced binding affinities to key ligands: human VEGF-A165 (15.3-fold), mouse VEGF-A164 (5.5-fold), human PlGF-1 (12.8-fold), human PlGF-2 (8.3-fold), and mouse PlGF-2 (91.0-fold) (Supplementary Fig. S6A and B). Still, both PB101 and PB102 exhibited decent degree of affinity against VEGF-B owing from its structure derived from VEGFR1 (Supplementary Fig. S6C). But previous studies reported marginal correlation between VEGF-B and autoimmune diseases compared to that of VEGF-A.26,27 Importantly, PB102 fully maintained the strong inhibitory effects of PB101 on PlGF-mediated cell migration in vitro (Supplementary Fig. S7A), while providing significant advantages in molecular properties described above. These data demonstrate that PB102 is a significantly improved molecule with enhanced monomer content, superior serum stability, extended in vivo half-life, and increased binding affinity for the target proteins, suggesting its potential as a more effective therapeutic candidate than PB101.

Inhibition of in vitro Th17 cell function by VEGF-Grab

Treatment with either VEGF-Grab significantly reduced Th17 cell differentiation, as well as pathogenic Th17 cell differentiation (Fig. 3A). The addition of VEGF-Grab significantly diminished IL-17 production only with PB102, not with PB101, reflecting the improved molecular characteristics of PB102 (Fig. 3B). To test physiological antigen-specific T cell-mediated immune responses, we utilised OT-II CD4+ T cells. Whereas activation with OVA peptides effectively induced Th17 differentiation in OT-II CD4+ cells in Th17-differentiating conditions, PB102 significantly reduced the differentiation of both Th17 and pathogenic Th17 cells (Fig. 3C). Therapeutic effects of VEGF-Grab in arthritis animal models

To assess the therapeutic efficacy of VEGF-Grab in vivo under arthritic conditions, we conducted several experiments using two animal models. First, we compared the efficacy of PB101 and PB102 in the AIA model, in which mice were injected with CFA into the joint to induce inflammation. PB102, the improved version of PB101, showed therapeutic effects superior to those of PB101 (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Subsequently, PB102 exhibited significant alleviating effects in AIA model mice (Fig. 4A–D). Next, in the CIA model, mice were immunised with bovine type II collagen and CFA as antigens to trigger an autoreactive response against collagen that ultimately leads to joint inflammation across multiple joints. Although PB101 did not show any significant inhibitory effects in this model (Supplementary Fig. S7C), PB102, particularly when administered at a high dose (10 mg/kg), displayed pronounced anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 4E). Moreover, 10 mg/kg of PB102 treatment significantly reduced pannus formation, macrophage infiltration, and levels of angiogenesis in the inflamed joints of the CIA model mice (Fig. 4E–K). Additionally, consistent with PB102-mediated suppression of Th17 responses shown in Fig. 3, administration of PB102 in mice with AIA or CIA significantly reduced IL-17 production by splenic CD4+ T cells upon T-cell receptor stimulation (Fig. 4D and K). In contrast, treatment with control IgG Fc did not affect the progression of either AIA or CIA compared with vehicle treatment (Supplementary Fig. S8). Therapeutic effects of VEGF-Grab in an animal model of MS

Based on our findings that VEGF-Grab regulates Th17 cell function, we evaluated its therapeutic effects in another Th17-dependent animal model, EAE, an established animal model of MS. As described in Fig. 5A, wild-type C57BL/6 mice were immunised with a MOG/CFA emulsion to induce EAE, with concomitant dose titration of VEGF-Grab. Among the different doses of VEGF-Grab, 1 mg/kg of PB101 showed the highest efficacy without any side effects (Supplementary Fig. S9). PB102, the improved version of VEGF-Grab, exhibited better efficacy than PB101 in 0.33 mg/kg and 1 mg/kg without inducing weight loss or lethargy (Supplementary Fig. S10). Using 1 mg/kg of PB102, we examined its therapeutic effects in the EAE model (Fig. 5A). Clinical symptoms of EAE, including tail and limb paralysis, were significantly improved in the PB102-treated group (Fig. 5B). Similarly, the infiltration of pathogenic Th17 cells and neutrophils, the key mediators of EAE pathogenesis,29,30 was reduced in the CNS lesions (Fig. 5C), and reduced pathogenic Th17 cell differentiation was also observed in the draining LNs of the PB102-treated group (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, the levels of inflammatory cytokines (GM-CSF, IL-17, and IFN-γ) and PlGF in the culture supernatant of CNS-infiltrating immune cells correlated well with the overall symptoms of EAE (Fig. 5E). Combinatory effects of VEGF-Grab and IFN-β in an animal model of MS

IFN-β is a standard treatment for MS, but its efficacy is limited.31 Given the relapsing-remitting nature of MS,28 we compared the therapeutic effects of VEGF-Grab with those of IFN-β and investigated their potential synergistic effects. PB102 (1 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally twice a week while IFN-β (104 units) was administered five times a week, as described previously32, 33, 34 (Fig. 6A). All treatment groups exhibited attenuated EAE clinical symptoms without observable adverse effects, and PB102 demonstrated moderately enhanced therapeutic efficacy compared with IFN-β monotherapy. Notably, the combined treatment with PB102 and IFN-β exhibited the most pronounced therapeutic effects compared with either monotherapy (Fig. 6B). The combination treatment showed the greatest reduction of pathogenic Th17 cells and neutrophil recruitment to the CNS (Fig. 6C). Consistently, the production of inflammatory cytokines (IL-17, IFN-γ, and IL-6) in peripheral lymphoid organs such as the spleen and LNs, was most effectively suppressed in the combination group (Fig. 6D and E). These results indicate that VEGF-Grab not only modestly outperforms IFN-β monotherapy but also exerts enhanced therapeutic efficacy when combined with IFN-β. This synergistic effect appears to be primarily mediated through modulation of pathogenic Th17 cell responses. Moreover, serum PlGF levels positively correlated with EAE clinical symptoms (Supplementary Fig. 11A). Additionally, the drug-treated groups exhibited decreased T follicular helper (Tfh) cell, accompanied by reduced MOG-specific IgG1 and IgG2c production, suggesting that inhibition of pathogenic Th17 cells also suppressed B cell-mediated responses (Supplementary Fig. S11B and C). Although B cells play a relatively minor role in EAE,35 autoantibodies are known to be significant contributors to the pathogenesis of MS.36 Therefore, the suppressive functions of VEGF-Grab on both T and B cell responses might represent a therapeutic advantage when treating patients with MS. Nevertheless, potential concerns regarding this therapeutic strategy warrant careful consideration. Similar to our previous study, the concentration of PlGF negatively correlated with the proportion of splenic Treg cells (Supplementary Fig. S12A).4 As VEGF-Grab neutralises PlGF and VEGF, it is possible that it interferes with the function of other CD4+ T cell subsets. However, in vivo administration of VEGF-Grab, either alone or in combination with IFN-β, did not alter Treg cell frequencies nor immune suppressive cytokines in the CNS or LNs (Supplementary Fig. S11D–G). In line with these findings, analyses of VEGF-Grab on the differentiation of other CD4+ T cell subsets revealed no significant effects (Supplementary Fig. S11H and S12B–D). Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that VEGF-Grab, a VEGF receptor fusion protein, exerts promising therapeutic effects in autoimmune diseases such as RA and MS, in which PlGF plays a central pathogenic role, as summarised in Fig. 7. VEGF-Grab targets both VEGF and PlGF, two key mediators in the pathogenesis of these autoimmune diseases. Our data present that VEGF-Grab effectively attenuates PlGF-induced angiogenesis and suppresses the aggressive behaviour of RA-FLSs as well as the pathogenic activity of Th17 cells, which are critical drivers of RA and MS pathogenesis. Findings from multiple animal models of RA and MS further support the therapeutic potential of VEGF-Grab, suggesting that dual inhibition of VEGF and PlGF represents a comprehensive treatment strategy.Fig. 7Effects of VEGF-Grab on autoimmune diseases. It effectively reduces the angiogenesis induced by PlGF, and suppresses the aggressive behaviour of RA-FLSs and the pathogenic activity of Th17 cells, which are key contributors to RA and MS.

Effects of VEGF-Grab on autoimmune diseases. VEGF-Grab provides promising therapeutic effects for autoimmune diseases such as RA and MS, through targeting both VEGF and PlGF. It effectively reduces the angiogenesis induced by PlGF, and suppresses the aggressive behaviour of RA-FLSs and the pathogenic activity of Th17 cells, which are key contributors to RA and MS.

VEGF plays a multifaceted role in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases, extending beyond its canonical function in angiogenesis. It also modulates immune responses by promoting the survival of RA-FLSs through the Akt and ERK signalling pathways and by stimulating the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6.37 However, the pleiotropic effects of VEGF, including the potential promotion of Treg cell differentiation, indicate that therapeutic strategies targeting VEGF alone may have unintended consequences.38 This highlights a critical therapeutic dilemma: while selective VEGF inhibition may reduce angiogenesis and inflammation, it could simultaneously impair Treg cell generation, which is beneficial for restraining autoimmune diseases. Thus, a strategy that selectively modulates VEGF-driven pathology without compromising Treg cell homoeostasis might offer improved therapeutic outcomes.

In contrast, PlGF, which is secreted by synoviocytes and Th17 cells, not only promotes angiogenesis but also drives the expansion of autoreactive Th17 cells, thereby exacerbating autoimmune inflammation in both synovial joints and the CNS.4,7,8 PlGF potently enhances Th17 differentiation while simultaneously reducing Treg population, an ambivalent factor particularly detrimental in autoimmune diseases. Notably, the ability of PlGF to promote Th17 differentiation is comparable to that of IL-6, a well-established Th17-inducing cytokine.4 These intricate immunovascular interactions may explain why anti-VEGF monotherapy shows limited efficacy in treating autoimmune diseases and underscore the need for a dual-targeting therapeutic approach. VEGF-Grab fulfils this requirement by neutralising both VEGF and PlGF and may therefore modulate both immune and vascular components of autoimmune disease progression more effectively than currently existing therapies.

In RA, the formation of pannus-abnormal tissue proliferative tissue in the synovium-is driven by an excess of RA-FLSs and enhanced angiogenesis, both of which are regulated by VEGF and PlGF.39 PlGF and VEGF levels were substantially higher in the synovial fluid of patients with RA than in those with non-inflammatory osteoarthritis.8,10 Moreover, the ultrasonographic findings from our previous study demonstrated a positive correlation between synovial hyperplasia, vascularity in RA joints, and the concentrations of VEGF and PlGF.10 Our current results from murine models of arthritis corroborate these observations, demonstrating that VEGF-Grab significantly reduces pannus formation, vascularity, and inflammatory cell infiltration, findings that are consistent with clinical observations in patients with RA. Furthermore, in our EAE model, we found a strong positive correlation between PlGF levels in affected tissues and disease severity, indicating that PlGF plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of MS and further supporting the therapeutic potential of VEGF-Grab for the treatment of MS.

Several therapeutic approaches targeting VEGF and its receptors have shown promising effects in oncological settings. For instance, bevacizumab and ramucirumab are FDA-approved monoclonal antibodies, while tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as sunitinib and sorafenib are clinically used to inhibit VEGF signalling.40 In preclinical models of autoimmune disease, angiogenesis inhibitors have also shown therapeutic potential. For example, bevacizumab reduced arthritis scores and VEGF/TNF-α expression in a CIA model, while ramucirumab alleviated disease severity in an AIA model.23 In EAE models, bevacizumab delayed disease onset and improved clinical outcomes, while VEGFR2 inhibitors revealed high efficacy in reducing disease severity.41 However, these approaches have not yet been clinically validated for autoimmune diseases, and their application remains largely experimental. Further studies are required to translate these findings into approved therapies for autoimmune diseases. Interestingly, VEGF-Grab offers a more targeted approach by simultaneously inhibiting both VEGF and PlGF, thereby overcoming the limitations of therapies that target solely VEGF. This dual inhibition strategy represents a potentially more effective approach for the treatment of autoimmune diseases in which PlGF plays a pivotal pathogenic role.

In the context of MS, there is substantial and urgent need for immunomodulatory therapies. Although IFN-β is widely prescribed as a first-line treatment for MS, its efficacy is limited, with only approximately 47% of patients showing favourable clinical responses.42 Patients who fail to respond to IFN-β typically progress to second-line treatments, such as dimethyl fumarate, fingolimod, or natalizumab. While these agents aim to regulate aberrant autoimmune responses, they are associated with an increased risk of infection and other adverse effects.31 In severe cases refractory to even second-line treatments, options are often restricted to high dose corticosteroids or plasma exchange using donations from healthy individuals.43,44 Given these limitations, the development of other strategies with improved safety and efficacy profiles is imperative. In this study, we investigated the therapeutic potential of VEGF-Grab in combination with IFN-β, hoping to achieve enhanced efficacy without substantial side effects. By acting synergistically with IFN-β, the most commonly used first-line therapy, VEGF-Grab may represent a viable option for the approximately 53% of patients with MS who do not respond adequately to IFN-β, while avoiding the profound immunosuppression associated with many second-line treatments.

Unlike animal models of RA, the EAE model exhibited a favourable therapeutic response at relatively low doses of VEGF-Grab, whereas doses exceeding 5 mg/kg resulted in detrimental effects (Supplementary Fig. S9A). This divergence in optimal dosing between the two autoimmune disease models might reflect their differential dependence on VEGF- or PlGF-driven pathways. The pathogenic progression of RA is driven by both VEGF and PlGF, whereas that of MS seems to be less reliant on VEGF-mediated angiogenesis. This mechanistic discrepancy may account for the approximately tenfold higher dose of VEGF-Grab required to achieve therapeutic efficacy in RA compared with MS. In addition, excessive neutralisation of VEGF by high-dose VEGF-Grab might compromise blood–brain barrier integrity during EAE induction, leading to more severe adverse effects than those observed in RA. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that animal models of RA or solid tumours tolerated high doses of VEGF-Grab (10–50 mg/kg) without signs of lethargy, giving a clue to the fine-tuned nature of the CNS in the pathogenicity of MS.

Still this study possesses several limitations that warrant further consideration. First, although our data demonstrate that VEGF-Grab suppresses the activities of Th17 cells, FLSs, and endothelial cells, the relative contribution of each of these mechanisms to overall therapeutic effects against RA and MS remain unclear. Additional mechanistic studies will be necessary to delineate the dominant pathway(s) responsible for disease attenuation. Second, in the context of RA, this study did not include direct comparisons with currently approved anti-rheumatic therapies, particularly biologic agents, such as TNF inhibitors and IL-6 receptor inhibitors. Therefore, it remains uncertain whether VEGF-Grab provides superior efficacy relative to established treatments or it may exert additive or synergistic effects in combination regimens. For MS, assessment of the inhibitory effects of VEGF-Grab on CD4+ T cells isolated from cerebral spinal fluid or CNS tissues was limited by the practical difficulty of obtaining such specimens from patients. Consequently, direct quantification of PlGF expression within CNS lesions also remains to be addressed. Lastly, although the combination of VEGF-Grab with IFN-β exhibited the most pronounced therapeutic effects compared with either monotherapy, this study did not include head-to-head comparisons with other advanced biologics or small molecule inhibitors targeting IL-17, the IL-6 receptor, or JAK signalling pathways. Future study should therefore evaluate VEGF-Grab alongside currently approved anti-autoimmune agents to appropriately define its relative therapeutic value and positioning within the treatment landscape.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that VEGF-Grab effectively inhibits PlGF-induced angiogenesis and attenuates the inflammatory and tissue–destructive activities of RA-FLSs and Th17 cells. The therapeutic efficacy of VEGF-Grab was validated in multiple animal models of RA and MS, including its ability to suppress synovial tissue destruction in a humanised synovitis model. Furthermore, molecular engineering to enhance the pharmaceutical properties of VEGF-Grab, leading to the development of PB102, which exhibited improved quality, stability, and efficacy. Collectively, these findings indicate that VEGF-Grab represent a promising therapeutic approach with a multifaceted mechanism of action in autoimmune diseases. By simultaneously targeting both angiogenesis and pathogenic immune cell activity, VEGF-Grab may offer advantages over therapies that selectively inhibit VEGF or immune response alone. Nevertheless, extensive clinical studies are required to establish the safety and efficacy of VEGF-Grab in human patients. This research lays the groundwork for the development of innovative therapies for autoimmune disorders and advances our understanding of dual VEGF and PlGF targeting as a disease-modifying strategy.
