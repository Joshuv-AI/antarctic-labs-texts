Effectiveness of clinical algorithms combined with point-of-care testing to improve antibiotic prescribing practices of physicians in outpatient setting of a low- to middle-income country: A pre-post–intervention study
Authors: Sonia Qureshi, Sadaf Ahmed, Nosheen Nasir, Naveed Ahmad, Afia Tariq, Vinod Kumar, Muhammad Jawwad, Farah Naz Qamar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Antimicrobial resistance is driven by the misuse and overuse of antibiotics.•Point-of-care C-reactive protein–based clinical algorithms guided antibiotic prescribing decisions.•Appropriate antibiotic prescriptions increased by 11.9% with clinical algorithm use.•Clinical algorithms reduced azithromycin prescriptions in pediatric upper respiratory tract infection by 11.4%.•A total of 72% physician adherence demonstrates the feasibility of clinical algorithms.Keywords: Clinical algorithms, Point-of-care C-reactive protein, Antibiotic prescriptions

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a serious global threat, with widespread implications, including increased morbidity, mortality due to treatment failure or relapse, and rising health care costs. The emergence of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs), often referred to as “superbugs,” is alarming because these bacteria are resistant to existing antimicrobials, making them challenging to treat [1,2]. Key factors driving AMR include the misuse and overuse of antimicrobials across human, animal, and environmental sectors, as well as the global transmission of MDROs and resistance genes within and between these sectors [1]. In 2019, an estimated 1.27 million deaths were directly attributable to bacterial AMR worldwide [1]. In response, the World Health Organization adopted a Global Action Plan on AMR in 2015, outlining a five-point strategy to address AMR by raising awareness through communication, education and training, reducing antimicrobial misuse, and improving prescribing practices among physicians [3]. Antimicrobial stewardship programs were also developed to combat inappropriate antibiotic use and minimize AMR by supporting evidence-based prescribing practices among health care providers to improve patient outcomes [4].

Various studies from high income countries have highlighted trends of antimicrobial overuse and misuse, observed in inpatient and outpatient settings. Antibiotics are frequently prescribed for self-limiting conditions, thereby increasing the risk of resistance [[5], [6], [7]]. In contrast, data from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), such as India, Bangladesh, and Pakistan, are limited. An inverse relationship has been observed between gross national income and AMR rates [8], with the highest AMR related mortality reported in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa [9]. Most data from LMICs come from tertiary care hospitals in major cities, resulting in a lack of insight into AMR trends at the community level [10,11]. In Pakistan, irrational antimicrobial use is a major driver of the growing AMR crisis. Unregulated and inappropriate prescribing, particularly, among general practitioners significantly contributes to resistance, with studies showing that more than three drugs are prescribed per patient and 70% receive antibiotics. In addition, the availability of over-the-counter antimicrobials further exacerbates the problem [12].

The increasing use of azithromycin and carbapenems to treat uncomplicated enteric fever in outpatient settings since 2016, driven by the spread of extensively drug-resistant Salmonella Typhi in Pakistan is a major concern. Reports of azithromycin-resistant S. Typhi from countries such as Vietnam and India indicate that widespread resistance is likely and may spread worldwide [13]. To address this issue, it is crucial to evaluate routine antimicrobial prescribing practices among general physicians in Pakistan. Studies have shown that educational and behavioral interventions in health care, such as standardized prescription guidelines, training workshops, outreach visits, audits, and point-of-care tests (POCTs) for rapid diagnostics, can significantly reduce antibiotic prescription rates for common infections [5,[14], [15], [16], [17]]. These interventions also provide economic benefit in primary care settings. Ideally, rapid pathogen identification would be the most effective way to avoid unnecessary antibiotic use; however, the limited availability of pathogen specific POCTs makes this approach less feasible [18,19]. A promising alternative is the use of POCTs to detect host response biomarkers, which can aid in timely decision-making and reduce unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions. One such test is C-reactive protein (CRP), widely used in hospitals to monitor systemic inflammation caused by immunological disturbances, infections, or tissue damage. CRP, an acute phase protein released by hepatocytes in response to cytokines such as interleukin 6 or tumor necrosis factor, rises rapidly in serum during infection [20,21]]. Several studies suggest that CRP can help differentiate bacterial from viral infections, thereby limiting the irrational antibiotic prescribing in community settings [20,22]]. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of clinical algorithms and POC-CRP testing in reducing antibiotic prescriptions for common infections (acute watery diarrhea [AWD], upper respiratory tract infections [URTIs], and acute undifferentiated fever [AUF]) among children and adults in an outpatient setting at Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH), Pakistan, pre- and post-intervention. Secondary objectives included comparing the proportion of all-cause 30-day hospitalizations and emergency visits within 7 days of enrollment and evaluating physicians’ adherence to the intervention.

Methods

Study design and setting

A pre-post–intervention study was conducted from January 2022 to September 2024 at the AKUH; the Aga Khan Hospital (AKH) for Women, Karimabad; and the AKH-Garden in Karachi, Pakistan. AKUH is one of the largest tertiary care hospitals in the country, offering comprehensive health care services, including well-equipped adult and pediatric emergency departments and intensive care units, with a capacity of almost 550 inpatient beds. The AKH-Garden and AKH-Karimabad are secondary care hospitals providing dedicated inpatient and outpatient maternal and child health services, along with urgent care and outpatient services across various sub-specialties.

Participants and procedure

Children aged ≥6 months and adults of any age who visited the outpatient clinics of study sites for AWD, URTI, or AUF and consented to participate were included. AWD was defined as diarrhea lasting ≤14 days, with three or more loose, watery stools within 24 hours, without blood. URTI was defined as a cough lasting ≤2 days, accompanied by documented fever (temperature ≥38°C) and no signs of pneumonia or asthma (e.g. shortness of breath, crepitations or wheezing on auscultation, chest indrawing, hypoxia SpO2 <92%, or cyanosis). AUF was defined as a history of fever lasting ≥3 days, with documented fever (temperature ≥38°C) and no clear source of infection. Individuals were excluded if they had received antibiotics, antiprotozoals, or antimalarials within the past week; were immunocompromised (due to primary or acquired immunodeficiency, e.g. HIV); had malignancy, bone marrow failure, any underlying chronic illness or autoimmune disorders; or were children with severe acute malnutrition.

Data collection

Physicians were selected based on patient volume and relevance to the diseases of interest, as nominated by the department heads at each study site. At the Department of Family Medicine, AKUH, 12 of 40 family physicians were invited, of whom seven participated in the intervention. Two of the five excluded physicians who had participated in the pre-intervention phase left the organization during the study, whereas the remaining three were excluded due to low patient turnover in their clinics. At AKH-Garden, four family physicians and five pediatricians were eligible, six were invited, and five participated (three pediatricians and two family physicians) in the intervention. One family physician was excluded from the study due to inconvenient clinic schedule and low patient turnover. At AKH-Karimabad, all three nominated pediatricians participated in the intervention. Data were collected from eligible participants who consented to participate in the study electronically using standardized forms and Android tablets in the pre- and post-intervention phases from May 24, 2022 to April 30, 2024. The pre-intervention phase spanned ∼9-15 months (AKH-Garden: May 24, 2022 to February 27, 2023; AKH-Karimabad: May 24, 2022 to February 21, 2023; AKUH main campus: June 1, 2022 to August 7, 2023) and post-intervention was conducted over ∼8-11 months (AKH-Garden: March 30, 2023 to November 21, 2023; AKH-Karimabad: April 5, 2023 to February 16, 2024; AKUH main campus: May 3, 2023 [partially implemented for some physicians] to April 27, 2024). Data for each study phase were recorded electronically by trained research staff using a structured questionnaire. Information collected included patients’ sociodemographic details, reason for the visit, clinical symptoms, duration of illness, clinical diagnosis, investigations performed, and antibiotic prescriptions, including name and number. Antibiotic use was classified as appropriate when prescribed according to the disease-specific clinical algorithm, based on patient symptoms, POC-CRP results suggestive of bacterial infection, available culture results (if performed), and/or physician clinical judgment. Inappropriate use was defined as any prescription of antibiotics not indicated by the algorithm, based on symptoms, POC-CRP results not suggestive of bacterial infection, available culture results (if performed), and/or physician discretion. Each patient was followed up with weekly phone calls for 1 month after enrollment (day 7 ± 2, 14 ± 2, 28 ± 2) to gather information on the resolution of clinical symptoms (day 7 ± 2), as well as the number of clinic revisits, emergency visits, or hospitalizations (day 7 ± 2,14 ± 2, 28 ± 2).

Intervention

The semi-quantitative POC-CRP test was incorporated into disease-specific clinical algorithms for AWD, URTI, and AUF. These algorithms were developed by a team of infectious disease clinicians in collaboration with the hospital’s antimicrobial stewardship committee, taking into account national guidelines, the local antibiogram, and the country’s AMR profile. Physicians at each site participated in structured group training on the implementation of the algorithms in routine clinical practice, along with guidance on the use and interpretation of the POC-CRP test. Each physician was provided with a handbook outlining the algorithms for pediatric and adult patients (Supplementary Files A & B).

The intervention was conducted at two sites between February and March 2023: at AKH-Garden from February 22 to March 8, 2023 and at AKH-Karimabad on March 15, 2023. At the AKUH main campus, the intervention was implemented in phases, beginning on March 22, 2023 and concluded on August 9, 2023. Patients seen by physicians who had received the intervention training were enrolled using the same process as in the pre-intervention phase. Physician adherence to the clinical algorithms was assessed by tracking the number of patients referred to the study team for POC-CRP testing. At the conclusion of the post-intervention phase, feedback was gathered from participating physicians regarding the feasibility of the intervention and their satisfaction with it.

Staff training

The research team received training before the start of the study to standardize all procedures related to screening and enrollment, obtaining consent, follow-up processes, and the introduction and interpretation of the POC-CRP test. Refresher training sessions were conducted as needed. Hands-on training for the POC-CRP test was provided by the laboratory manager of the AKUH Infectious Disease Research Laboratory (IDRL) before the commencement of the intervention phase.

CRP test

The Actim CRP test is a visually interpreted, semi-quantitative immunochromatographic dipstick test used to determine and monitor CRP levels in whole blood samples [23]. Trained staff performed the test using a fingertip prick method in a designated outpatient area under aseptic conditions. A CRP level of ≥10 mg/l was considered significant for prescribing antibiotics, alongside the physician’s decision, based on clinical algorithms. The quality control of the Actim CRP test was overseen by the IDRL of AKUH. The study staff performed the CRP test, interpreted the results, and promptly communicated them to the treating physician at the point of care.

Sample size estimation

Using PASS software, a sample size of 373 paired prescriptions per phase was calculated to detect a 20% reduction in antibiotic use, with 90% power (two-sided McNemar test, α = 0.01). Accounting for a 20% non-response rate, the final sample size was 450 prescriptions per phase (total N = 900). To reflect differences in patient volume across sites, the sample was stratified by disease and site based on weighted calculation as AKUH-Garden (N = 117), AKUH-Karimabad (N = 135), and main AKUH (N = 198) while maintaining the overall total (Supplementary File C).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA v18.0. For descriptive statistics, mean ± SD was reported for quantitative variables, such as participant age and the number of antibiotics prescribed. For qualitative variables, such as gender, clinical diagnosis, antibiotics prescribed, hospitalizations, clinic revisits, etc., frequencies and percentages were reported. A generalized linear model with an identity link was applied to assess differences in the use of selected antimicrobials (azithromycin, cefixime, ciprofloxacin, amoxicillin, and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid), inappropriate and appropriate antibiotics use, clinic revisits, all-cause 30-day hospitalizations, and other independent variables between the pre- and post-intervention periods. Graphs were created to visualize the overall and monthly utilization of selected antibiotics before and after the intervention. Comparisons focused on data from patients of physicians who participated in the intervention.

Results

We enrolled 457 of 471 and 460 of 487 eligible patients in the pre-intervention and post-intervention phases, respectively (Figure 1). Approximately two-thirds of participants were children: 296 of 457 (64.8%) pre-intervention and 269 of 460 (58.5%) post-intervention. The mean ± SD (years) age of the pediatric population (≥6 months to ≤18 years) was 4.1 ± 4.4 in the pre-intervention phase and 3.7 ± 4.1 in the post-intervention phase. For adult participants, the mean ± SD age was 36.6 ± 14.2 years in the pre-intervention phase and 34.6 ± 14.4 years in the post-intervention phase. The majority of participants in both phases were male: 275 of 457 (60.2%) pre-intervention and 248 of 460 (53.9%) post-intervention. Most participants presented with AUF (pre-intervention: 178 of 457 [39.0%]; post-intervention: 180 of 460 [39.1%]). Blood cultures were performed in a limited number of patients in both phases (pre-intervention: 41 of 457 [9.0%], post-intervention: 37 of 460 [8.0%]) (Table 1). There were only a few patients with AUF whose physicians prescribed blood cultures in both pre- (26 of 178; 14.6%) and post-intervention phases (25 of 180; 13.9%) (Supplementary Table 1). Antibiotics were prescribed to 132 of 457 (28.9%) patients in the pre-intervention phase and 177 of 460 (38.5%) in the post-intervention phase. Among those who received antibiotics, most were prescribed only one: 129 of 132 (97.7%) in the pre-intervention and 174 of 177 (98.3%) in the post-intervention phase. A 21.9% increase in antibiotic prescriptions was observed in children with AUF during the post-intervention phase. There was also an 11.9% increase in appropriate antibiotic prescriptions across all age groups. The proportion change of appropriate antibiotic prescriptions was highest (% point difference: 29.6% [95% confidence interval {CI}; 13.4-45.9%], P ≤0.001) among patients with URTI. Among children with URTI, the use of azithromycin decreased by 11.4% (95% CI: −33.1 to 10.3), and cefixime use decreased by 18.7% (95% CI: −34.7 to −2.6). However, an opposite trend was noted among adults with URTI, particularly, for azithromycin, which increased by 15.2% (95% CI: −15.3 to 45.6) (Table 2). There was a 37.4% (95% CI: 12.1-62.7%, P = 0.005) increase in appropriate use of antibiotics among patients with URTI at the AKUH across all ages. A 41.4% reduction was observed in the use of azithromycin in children with URTI post-intervention at the AKH-Karimabad (Supplementary Table 2). There was a significant increase in the use of first-line antibiotics after the intervention (Figure 2). At day 7 follow-up, clinical improvement was high and comparable between groups (95.0% pre-intervention vs 95.9% post-intervention; P = 0.51). Overall clinic revisits decreased significantly by 7.8% post-intervention (P = 0.006), with the largest reduction observed among patients with AUF (36.5% vs 26.1%; P = 0.03), including a significant decline at AKUH (point difference −24.7%, 95% CI −37.9 to −11.6; Supplementary Table 3). The purpose of revisits shifted from routine clinic follow-ups (80.3% vs 28.3%; P <0.001) to laboratory report collection (13.4% vs 66.3%; P <0.001), whereas emergency visits remained low and unchanged (6.3% vs 5.4%; P = 0.79). Hospitalization rates through day 28 were low and did not differ significantly between groups (2.0% vs 1.5%; P = 0.61) (Table 3). Overall, 72% (339 of 460) of patients were referred by physicians for the POC-CRP testing across all sites. The highest adherence rate (161 of 199, 81%) was observed among AKUH physicians (Table 4, Supplementary Table 4). All physicians who participated in the feedback session (14 of 14: 100%) were satisfied with the intervention and agreed it supported their clinical decision-making (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study found a significant improvement in the proportion of appropriate antibiotic prescriptions post-intervention across all age groups, with the greatest improvement observed among patients with URTI. The intervention, which was composed of disease-specific clinical algorithms for AWD, URTI, and AUF, along with semi-quantitative POC-CRP test, led to a notable reduction in the use of azithromycin (41.4% decrease) and cefixime among children with URTI. Furthermore, there was a significant reduction in clinic revisits within 7 days across all disease categories, with the most notable decline observed among patients with AUF. Most physicians adhered to the intervention and referred patients for POC-CRP tests, with high level of satisfaction were reported among participating physicians.

Our study used a pragmatic algorithmic-based approach for managing community-acquired infections incorporating POC-CRP testing in the outpatient setting. The role of biomarkers in antibiotic stewardship has been evaluated in multiple studies, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs) worldwide. Majority of the studies have been conducted in inpatient settings. A similar strategy was used in a pediatric intensive care setting in Philadelphia for patients with sepsis, using eight serum biomarkers. The study found that CRP in combination with other biomarkers was useful for antibiotic stewardship [24]. An RCT conducted in non-intensive care unit settings in the Netherlands demonstrated that CRP- and procalcitonin-guided treatment algorithms reduced the duration of antibiotic use in hospitalized patients with community-acquired pneumonia [25]. Fewer studies have explored the utility of POC-CRP tests in antibiotic stewardship in low-resource settings and majority have evaluated this strategy in respiratory infections. A stepped wedge cluster randomized trial conducted in 15 villages of Uganda incorporated CRP measurement into integrated community care management algorithms and found that this approach reduced antibiotic use in children with febrile acute respiratory illness, without worsening clinical outcomes [26]. These findings are corroborated with our study, and we did not observe any significant differences in adverse outcome pre- and post-intervention; rather, we found a statistically significant reduction in the number of clinic revisits within 7 days. This decline may be attributed to the intervention, which supported clinicians in prescribing appropriate treatment, or it could be related to the close follow-up by study staff to monitor the clinical resolution of illness.

A Cochrane review of 13 RCTs concluded that primary care patients who exhibit signs of an acute respiratory infection are probably less likely to receive an antibiotic prescription when POC-CRP tests are used as a supplement to conventional care. Moreover, patient recovery rates and 28-day mortality rates were unaffected by the use of POC-CRP diagnostics. Our study comprehensively evaluated the intervention for several common clinical presentations, including URTI, and similarly found the highest number of appropriate prescriptions for URTI in the post-intervention phase [27].

In our study, 72.0% of the physicians were adherent to the intervention and referred their patients for POC-CRP tests across all sites. Although the reasons for non-adherence were not formally recorded, periodic meetings and end-of-study feedback sessions revealed no specific barriers. Most physicians supported scaling up CRP use in outpatient settings. This contrasts with a study from Vietnam where only 14% uptake of the test was observed suggesting barriers to implementation [28]. Hence, there is a potential for scalability of this intervention in our settings.

Antimicrobial stewardship interventions may influence prescribing behavior by reducing unnecessary antibiotic use while improving identification and treatment of patients who truly require antibiotics. Our study found an overall increase in the proportion of antibiotic prescriptions post-intervention. Previous stewardship research has demonstrated that interventions designed to improve diagnostic certainty or guideline adherence may increase prescribing in some clinical settings, particularly, when previously undertreated infections are identified [29]. The increase in prescriptions in our study may reflect changes in clinician risk perception and diagnostic confidence. Evidence from LMICs suggests that combined antimicrobial stewardship programs and POCT interventions are generally effective but highly context dependent, with heterogeneous prescribing effects across implementation environments. In settings with high baseline diagnostic uncertainty, limited microbiology access, and patient expectations for antibiotics, clinical algorithms may initially increase prescribing as clinicians transition toward structured decision-making [30]. Although our intervention improved structured clinical assessment, the CRP-POC component may have inadvertently lowered antibiotic prescribing thresholds in our resource-limited outpatient setting.

Our study did not find significant differences in the clinical outcomes post-intervention phase. These findings are consistent with an RCT evaluating POCT interventions, showing that despite substantial changes in antibiotic prescribing patterns, there were no major differences in recovery, hospitalization, or symptom resolution, supporting the safety of stewardship intervention [31].

Our study has several strengths because it evaluated three common clinical scenarios in outpatient setting using a pragmatic approach in tertiary and secondary care centers serving a diverse population in an LMIC. However, because all sites were urban centers, generalizability to rural settings or other LMIC health care systems with different infrastructures and patient demographics may be limited. Moreover, clinician behavior may have been influenced by study staff presence, CRP testing availability, and training on algorithm use, introducing a potential Hawthorne effect. Other limitations include exclusion of immuno-compromised individuals, those with chronic illnesses, or children with severe acute malnutrition, which limits the applicability of the results to these high-risk populations who are often at high risk for complications related to AMR. Finally, the 1-month follow-up might not capture long-term outcomes related to AMR or sustained prescribing behaviors. The intervention improved structured clinical assessment but may not have sufficiently incorporated restrictive stewardship safeguards with the possibility of unintended promotion of unnecessary antibiotic use. Future iterations should integrate clearer antibiotic stop rules, delayed prescribing strategies, or audit-feedback mechanisms. This study has several important limitations related to its pre-post–intervention design. Because participants were not randomized and no concurrent control group was included, the study is vulnerable to temporal confounding, regression to the mean, and unmeasured external influences that may have affected prescribing behavior or clinical outcomes independent of the intervention. Hence, the associations observed in this study should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of intervention effectiveness. Nevertheless, such designs provide valuable real-world implementation data, particularly, in resource-limited outpatient settings where randomized evaluation may not initially be feasible. Future well-designed randomized or cluster RCTs are needed to more rigorously evaluate the causal impact of clinical algorithms combined with CRP-POCT on antibiotic prescribing practices and patient outcomes.

Conclusion

We observed an improvement in the prescription of appropriate antibiotics for common infections post-intervention. It is possible and potentially beneficial to incorporate a clinical algorithm alongside POC-CRP measurement in outpatient settings to promote appropriate use of antibiotics. These findings support the need for broader and more equitable access to basic diagnostics to enhance antibiotic stewardship in outpatient settings in LMICs.
