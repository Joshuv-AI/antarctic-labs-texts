β-Hydroxybutyrate enhances malate dehydrogenase 2 β-hydroxybutyrylation to alleviate hepatic steatosis in MASLD
Authors: Jingshu Cai, Wenxuan Li, Chunli Wu, Hongbin Ni, Haiying Ran, Yi Huang, Xiufang Tang, Wenjun He, Yuanfeng Gu, Yuehua You, Jiayu Li, Xiaoqiu Xiao, Li Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the past three decades, the global prevalence of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has rapidly increased, leading to significant economic and clinical burdens. However, aside from resmetirom (Rezdiffra™), an oral thyroid hormone receptor-β agonist, there remains a lack of approved targeted pharmacological treatments for MASLD, emphasizing the need for more optimized therapeutic strategies. Given the limitations in the safety and efficacy of the ketogenic diet, β-hydroxybutyrate (β-OHB) has emerged as a crucial regulator in MASLD treatment, but the precise mechanisms underlying its therapeutic effects remain unclear. This study aims to investigate the therapeutic effects of β-OHB on MASLD mice and elucidate the underlying mechanisms. In this study, we demonstrate that β-OHB ameliorates lipid deposition and increases pan-β-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb) levels in both MASLD mice and in vitro. Additionally, β-OHB also improves excessive ROS accumulation and enhances mitochondrial respiratory capacity. Furthermore, β-OHB protects against impaired fatty acid oxidation (FAO) activity in MASLD. Proteomic analysis of β-OHB-treated mice identified a significant Kbhb modification at K239 on malate dehydrogenase 2 (MDH2), which was associated with increased MDH2 enzymatic activity. Overall, this study demonstrates β-OHB exhibits therapeutic effects on hepatic steatosis and mitochondrial dysfunction in MASLD mice. Introduction

With the dramatic global rise in obesity and type 2 diabetes (T2DM), metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has become the leading cause of adverse liver outcomes [1]. MASLD, the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome, encompasses a spectrum from isolated hepatic steatosis to advanced liver diseases, including inflammation, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). It also presents with multiple extrahepatic manifestations, such as cardiovascular disease (CVD) and various extrahepatic cancers [2–4]. Over the past three decades, the global prevalence of MASLD has escalated from 25.3% to nearly 38.2%, resulting in significant economic and clinical burdens [5]. However, aside from resmetirom (Rezdiffra™), an oral thyroid hormone receptor-β agonist, there remains a lack of approved targeted pharmacological treatments for MASLD, underscoring the need for more optimized therapeutic strategies [6].

β-Hydroxybutyrate (β-OHB), the most abundant ketone body, is primarily synthesized in the liver and plays a role in various physiological and pathological processes [7]. Beyond serving as an alternative energy source, numerous studies have highlighted β-OHB as an important signaling molecule and epigenetic regulator involved in the regulation of metabolic diseases [8]. Researches have shown that β-OHB can reverse metabolic disorders in the aging liver by activating the HCAR2/PKA/AMPK pathway, and that liver-specific deletion of PPARα reduces β-OHB levels, leading to hepatic steatosis and inflammation [9, 10]. Our previous study demonstrated that a 2-week ketogenic diet (KD) effectively treated hepatic steatosis in high-fat diet (HFD)-induced MASLD mice, largely due to elevated β-OHB levels [11]. Although targeting β-OHB production has been recognized as a potential strategy for MASLD treatment, the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

Lysine β-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb) is a post-translational modification (PTM) mediated by β-OHB, which plays an important role in regulating gene expression and protein activity [12]. Aberrant Kbhb patterns have been observed in various diseases. For instance, β-OHB increases citrate synthase activity via Kbhb at K395, inhibiting fatty acid uptake and reducing mitochondrial hyperacetylation in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) mice [13] Additionally, Kbhb of CaMK II-α, induced by KD, reduces its autophosphorylation and diminishes cocaine-induced reinstatement in mice [14]. Furthermore, p53 Kbhb inhibits p53 acetylation, leading to reduced apoptosis and cell growth [15]. However, most studies on Kbhb in metabolic diseases have focused on histone Kbhb, leaving the role of non-histone Kbhb in regulating metabolic disease pathogenesis largely unexplored [16].

Malate dehydrogenase 2 (MDH2) is a key enzyme in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, responsible for the reversible oxidation of malate with NAD+ as a coenzyme [17]. While aberrant MDH2 function has been linked to tumors such as endometrial and prostate cancers, few studies have explored the regulatory relationship between MDH2 and MASLD [18, 19]. Recent findings suggest that MDH2 is modulated by several PTMs. For example, glucose concentration modulates MDH2 acetylation at Lys185, Lys301, Lys307, and Lys314, leading to its activation [20]. Moreover, S-palmitoylation of MDH2, catalyzed by ZDHHC18, supports mitochondrial respiration and enhances the malignancy of ovarian cancer [21]. Therefore, it is reasonable to hypothesize that MDH2 may also be regulated by Kbhb and play a significant role in MASLD treatment.

In this study, we found that β-OHB treatment alleviates hepatic steatosis and mitochondrial dysfunction in MASLD mice, an effect associated with β-OHB-induced Kbhb modifications. Through Kbhb proteomics analysis, we identified K239 on MDH2 as a specific Kbhb site, which increases MDH2 enzymatic activity, essential for mitochondrial homeostasis and lipid utilization.

Methods and materials

Animal models and diets

The 5-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). All mice were raised in the SPF animal room under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle with standard condition. Littermates wild-type (WT) mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD, 60% kcal fat) (D12492, Research Diet, USA) or a normal chow diet for 12 weeks. All efforts were made to minimize the suffering of the mice.

For β-OHB treatment, the mice were administered β-OHB (166898, Sigma-Aldrich) (300 mg/kg, i.p.) twice daily for 10 consecutive days as reported protocol [14] while control mice were injected with same volume of saline. Mice were sacrificed at 24 h post last injection. All mice were fasted prior to GTT (12 h) and ITT (6 h) experimentation. All experiments procedures were allowed by the Ethical Review Committees of Chongqing Medical University (IACUC-CQMU-2024-0405).

Cell culture

AML-12 and HepG2 cells were derived from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). AML-12 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, USA) supplied with 10% FBS (BI, Israel), 1% ITS (Gibco, USA, 41400045) and 1% penicillin (Beyotime Biotechnology, Chnia, C0222). HepG2 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin. To generate lipid deposition cell model, AML-12 cells first were treated with 150 µM BSA conjugated oleate (OA, Sigma, Germany, O7501) while HepG2 cells were treated with 300 µM OA for 24 h. Fatty acid free BSA was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (ST025). After 24 h-OA stimulation, cells were treated with indicated concentration β-OHB for 24 h. All cells were grown in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37℃.

Plasmids and SiRNAs transfection

The GV712-MDH2-6His, GV712-MDH2-K239R-mutant plasmids were constructed by Genechem Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). BDH1 siRNAs were purchased from GENE CREATE (Wuhan, China). A proper cell density was set up before transfection with Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher, USA,11668019). After transfection, the cells were exposed to the respective treatment. Western blotting

Tissues and cells were lysed by RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China, P0013B) with protease phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime Biotechnology, China, P1046) and PMSF (Solarbio, China, P0100). Protein lysate concentration was normalized by BCA Protein Quantitative Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China, P0009). Protein samples were separated with 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to the PVDF or NC membrane (Millipore, USA). Membrane was blocking with 5% skim milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated with the indicated antibodies. Anti-GAPDH (1:1000, 60004-1-Ig), anti-BDH1(1:1000, 15417-1-AP) and anti-MDH2 (1:1000, 15462-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech (Rosemount, IL, USA). Anti-PPARα (1:2000, AF5301) was purchased from Affinity Bioscience (Jiangsu, China).

Co-immunoprecipitation

Tissues and cells were lysed by IP lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, P0013) with protease phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime Biotechnology, China, P1046), Trichostatin A (MCE, USA, HY-15144) and Nicotinamide (MCE, USA, HY-B0150). The protein lysate was incubated with primary antibodies at 4℃ overnight and then incubated with Protein A/G beads (Beyotime Biotechnology, China, P2055) for 3 h. Next, the protein was eluted with SDS Loading buffer at 100℃ for 10 min.

Histology analysis and BODIPY staining

Tissue samples from mice were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, China, G1101) and sent to Servicebio (Wuhan, China) for Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) and oil red O staining. BODIPY staining for treated AML-12 cells, 4% paraformaldehyde was used to fix cells for 15 min. The fixed cells were washed by PBS and then incubated with diluted BODIPY staining buffer (MKBio, MX5404) for 20 min and counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, China, C1002) for 5 min. The intensity of staining was measured by Image J software.

Serum samples of mice were obtained by a centrifugation of 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4℃. Serum levels of alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), triglycerides (TG), total Cholesterol (TC), low density lipoprotein (LDL) and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) were measured with a AU5800 biochemical analysis system (Beckman Coulter, USA) at the Laboratory Department of the People’s Hospital of Yubei District, Chongqing.

β-OHB level of mice serum was measured according to the protocol provided by the β-OHB assay kit (Solarbio, China, BC5085).

LC-MS/MS-Based β-hydroxybutyrylation proteomics

Mice liver tissue was ground into powder using liquid nitrogen and transferred to sterile tube. Subsequently, four volumes of lysis buffer (8 M urea, 1% protease inhibitor cocktail) were added to the tissue powder. The mixture was sonicated on ice for 3 min and then the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble debris. The resulting supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was quantified using a BCA assay kit following the manufacturers’ protocol. After protein extraction, the sample was gently mixed with trichloroacetic acid (TCA) at a final concentration of 20% (m/v) to facilitate protein precipitation, then vortexed and incubated samples at 4 °C for 2 h. The precipitated protein was subsequently isolated by centrifugation at 4500 g for 5 min at 4 °C and the protein pellet was washed three times with pre-chilled acetone and briefly dried for 1 min. The protein was redissolved in 200 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB), ultrasonically dispersed, and digested overnight with trypsin (1:50, w/w). Reduction was performed with 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 56 °C for 30 min, followed by alkylation with 11 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Peptides were desalted using a Strata X SPE column.

To enrich post-translationally modified peptides, tryptic peptides were dissolved in NETN buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5% NP-40, pH 8.0) and subsequently incubated with pre-washed antibody-conjugated beads at 4 °C overnight under gentle agitation. Following incubation, the beads were subjected to four sequential washes with NETN buffer, followed by two additional washes with ultrapure H2O to remove non-specifically bound peptides. The specifically bound peptides were then eluted from the beads using 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). The eluted fractions were pooled and concentrated by vacuum centrifugation. For subsequent liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis, the peptide samples were desalted using C18 ZipTips (Millipore) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After trypsin digestion and affinity enrichment, the identification of tryptic modified peptide was subjected using timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometry. The resulting MS/MS data were processed using MaxQuant search engine (v.1.6.15.0). Tandem mass spectra were searched against Mus_musculus_10090_SP_20230103.fasta (17132 entries) concatenated with reverse decoy and contaminants database. According to the p-value, the primary data with p ≤ 0.05 and difference ratio ≥ 1.5 was conforming for further analysis and experiment.

Measurement of L-malic acid

Samples stored at −80 °C were thawed slowly at 4 °C. Subsequently, 200 µL of pre-chilled methanol and 10 µL of internal standard (10 mM/L) were added, followed by homogenization for 2 min. Then, 400 µL of pre-chilled chloroform was added, and the mixture was homogenized for another 2 min, followed by vertexing for 10 min. After adding 100 µL of deionized water, the mixture was vortexed again for 10 min. The solution was centrifuged at 14,000rcf in10°C for 20 min. The upper aqueous layer was collected (chloroform forms the lower layer), and the supernatant was dried under vacuum or nitrogen gas at room temperature. The dried samples were stored at −80 °C. For mass spectrometry analysis, the samples were reconstituted in 100 µL of acetonitrile-water (1:1, v/v), vortexed for 30 s, and centrifuged at 14,000 rcf in 10 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was collected for analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC system. Samples were maintained at 4 °C in the autosampler. The column temperature was set at 35 °C. Mobile phase A consisted of 50 mM ammonium acetate in water with 1.2% ammonium hydroxide, and mobile phase B was 1% acetylacetone in acetonitrile. The flow rate was 300 µL/min, and the injection volume was 2 µL. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–1 min, 70% B; 1–10 min, Linear decrease from 70 to 60% B; 10–12 min, Linear decrease from 60 to 30% B; 12.1–15 min, 30% B; 15–15.5 min, Linear increase from 30 to 70% B; 15.1–22 min, 70% B. Quality control (QC) samples were interspersed at regular intervals throughout the sample queue to monitor system stability and reproducibility. A standard mixture of target analytes was included for retention time correction. A 5500 QTRAP SCIEX mass spectrometer was applied for identification and MultiQuant 3.0.2 software were employed for data acquisition and analyses.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out at least 3 times or 3 replications. Data were presented as means ± SD using GraphPad Prism 8.0 v (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA). Statistical analyses were conducted using a 2-tail unpaired Student t-test for two independent groups or two-way ANOVA post hoc Tukey test for multi-groups. P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistical significant.

Results

β-OHB ameliorates hepatic lipid deposition of MASLD mice

To determine whether β-OHB is involved in the improvement of MASLD, we established a MASLD mouse model by feeding the mice a HFD for 12 weeks. The mice were then administered β-OHB (300 mg/kg, i.p.) twice daily for 10 consecutive days as reported protocol [14] while control mice were injected with same volume of saline. For HFD-fed mice, in the absence of a significant difference in energy intake (Fig. S1A), β-OHB administration resulted in significant reductions in both body weight and liver weight compared to the saline group, while minimal changes were observed between the β-OHB and saline groups in normal diet (ND) mice (Fig. 1A, B). Additionally, serum β-OHB levels were elevated in the β-OHB-treated groups, regardless of diet (Fig. 1C). HE and ORO staining revealed hepatic steatosis in HFD-fed mice, with an improvement in hepatocellular lipid deposition in response to β-OHB treatment (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, β-OHB treatment also attenuated the HFD-induced elevation in the NAFLD activity score (NAS) (Fig. S1B). A decrease in hepatic triglyceride (TG) content was observed, as well as total cholesterol (TC) (Fig. 1E, F). Further analysis of serum biochemical indices showed reductions in alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), TG, TC, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) in HFD-fed mice treated with β-OHB compared to the control group (Figs. 1G, H, S1C-F). Besides, improved glucose tolerance and lower blood glucose levels in response to insulin were also observed in HFD-fed mice treated with β-OHB (Fig. S1G-H). BODIPY staining indicated that β-OHB reduced lipid accumulation in AML-12 cells stimulated by oleate (OA) (Fig. 1I, J), with the reduction in lipid accumulation being dose-dependent (Fig. S1I). However, both OA and β-OHB treatment exhibited modest effects on AML-12 cell proliferation (Fig. S1J). To minimize potential proliferative interference while maintaining biological efficacy, we selected 1 mM β-OHB as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments. Additionally, intracellular TG and TC levels were decreased by β-OHB compared to control cells under OA conditions (Fig. 1K, L). Overall, these results demonstrate that β-OHB contributes to the reduction of lipid deposition both in vivo and in vitro.

β-OHB regulates overall protein β-hydroxybutyrylation

β-OHB functions not only as an energy metabolite but also as a donor for lysine β-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb). Given the observed increase in serum β-OHB following administration to mice, we initially assessed the pan-Kbhb levels of proteins in mouse liver tissue. An increase in protein pan-Kbhb levels was observed in both ND and HFD mice treated with β-OHB (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, β-OHB administration resulted in a more pronounced upregulation of protein pan-Kbhb in HFD-fed mice compared to ND mice, suggesting that β-OHB may act as a more targeted and persistent regulator in MASLD. In addition to serving as a substrate for Kbhb, β-OHB is also considered an epigenetic regulator that inhibits histone deacetylases, and we indeed found a parallel increase in protein pan-acetylation levels with Kbhb (Fig. S2A). β-OHB also increased the pan-Kbhb protein levels in AML-12 cells (Fig. 2B). We then conducted a β-hydroxybutyrylation proteomics analysis on liver tissues from HFD-fed mice treated with β-OHB or saline, using an LC-MS approach. A total of 3,315 proteins and 10,282 modified sites were identified, including 360 differentially Kbhb-modified proteins and 424 differentially Kbhb-modified sites. Among these, 30 proteins and 36 sites exhibited significant upregulation. Gene Ontology (GO) biological process analysis revealed that 72.22% (260 of 360) of the differentially Kbhb-modified proteins were enriched in cellular metabolic processes (Fig. 2C). Subcellular distribution analysis indicated that 72 Kbhb-modified proteins were localized in the mitochondria, second only to the cytoplasm in the number of modification sites (Fig. 2D), highlighting the widespread cellular impact of Kbhb, with mitochondria being a key site for Kbhb modification. Moreover, KEGG pathway analysis revealed enrichment in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, which is associated with MASLD development (Fig. 2E).

β-OHB improves mitochondrial oxidative stress and dysfunction in vivo and in vitro

Given that the hepatic mitochondrial matrix is the primary site for ketone body synthesis and the TCA cycle, and that Kbhb modification sites are highly enriched in mitochondria and the TCA cycle, we examined the effect of β-OHB on mitochondrial function and lipid accumulation in hepatocytes. We first assessed the liver ultrastructure of mice treated with β-OHB using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Fig. 3A, HFD-fed mice exhibited swollen and fragmented mitochondria (disrupted outer membrane, diminished or vanished cristae) with numerous lipid droplets in the cytoplasm close to the mitochondria. β-OHB treatment reduced vacuolar degeneration and fragmentation, along with smaller lipid droplets. As a result of hepatocyte steatosis, the electron transport chain (ETC) was disrupted by the compromised mitochondrial membrane, leading to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation [22]. To further investigate the influence of β-OHB on mitochondrial function in vitro, we stimulated AML-12 cells with OA to induce ROS production and then treated cells with β-OHB for 24 h, followed by flow cytometry analysis. Under OA pretreated condition, ROS production was significantly reduced in β-OHB group compared to untreated cells (Figs. 3B, S3A). Similarly, reduced mitochondrial ROS production was observed using MitoSOX staining (Figs. 3C, S3B), suggesting β-OHB plays a protective role against ROS accumulation. Additionally, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) levels, measured using JC-1 staining, were elevated after β-OHB treatment in OA-pretreated AML-12 cells (Fig. 3D, E). To further explore the effects of β-OHB on mitochondrial respiration, we measured the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in AML-12 cells. The results revealed that β-OHB significantly enhanced basal, maximal, and spare respiratory capacity (Figs. 3F, S3C-E).

β-Hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase 1 (BDH1) serves as the principal rate‐limiting enzyme in ketone body metabolism, playing a key role in the interconversion of acetoacetic acid (AcAc) and β‐OHB [23]. We performed BDH1 knockdown using siRNAs and assessed the persistence of β-OHB’s effects. As shown in the Fig. S2C, OA treatment significantly downregulated BDH1 expression in scrambled AML-12 cells, indicative of suppressed β‐OHB oxidation. β-OHB treatment remained capable of elevating pan-Kbhb levels under BDH1 knockdown conditions. Notably, BDH1-knockdown cells exhibited substantially higher pan-Kbhb levels compared to scrambled groups. This phenomenon may result from substrate accumulation, where BDH1 knockdown blocks β-OHB metabolism, leading to increased substrate availability for Kbhb modification. Consistent with the pan-Kbhb levels alternation observed, β-OHB still had an effective amelioration on OA-induced lipid accumulation and mitochondrial oxidative stress (Fig. S2D-E) in BDH1-knockdown cells. The preservation of β-OHB’s therapeutic effects in BDH1-knockdown cells strongly suggests functional independence of cellular Kbhb on ketone body metabolism.

Clinical studies report conflicting Fatty acid oxidation (FAO) activity in MASLD/NASH patients, with studies demonstrating either enhanced [24] unchanged [25] or reduced [26] FAO levels compared to healthy controls. These discrepancies may reflect variations in disease stage, metabolic heterogeneity across patient populations, or methodological variations. We first monitored FAO activity in live cells and observed significantly impaired FAO with OA stimulation, likely attributable to concurrent mitochondrial dysfunction and downregulation of PPARα expression (Fig. S3I-J), while β-OHB could restore the impaired FAO activity (Fig. 3G, H). To extend this finding, we also measured OCR using exogenous palmitate-BSA substrate. Strikingly, β-OHB significantly increased OCR in the presence of the FAO substrate compared to OA-pretreated cells (Figs. 3I, S3F-H), suggesting that β-OHB has a crucial impact on promoting the FAO activity.

β-OHB increases MDH2 K239 β-hydroxybutyrylation and MDH2 enzymic acivity

Based on β-hydroxybutyrylation proteomics analysis, we identified several regulated proteins and sites associated with the TCA cycle. Among these, K239 of MDH2 was significantly increased after β-OHB treatment (Fig. 4A). Although the β-hydroxybutyrylation proteomics analysis identified a greater number of down-regulated sites, we have confirmed that β-OHB intervention can increase the level of pan-Kbhb modification in vivo and in vitro, parallel with the increased serum β-OHB concentrations observed (Figs. 1C and 2A and B). Since MDH2 occupies a central position in TCA cycle regulation, and the MDH2-Kbhb level consistent elevation of pan-Kbhb levels, we prioritized MDH2 K239 for further investigation.

We first confirmed the elevated MDH2 Kbhb levels in liver tissues, AML-12, and HepG2 cells treated with β-OHB (Figs. 4B, C, S4A), indicating that MDH2 is highly β-hydroxybutyrylated by β-OHB. Additionally, we observed a mass shift at the K239 residue of MDH2 (Fig. 4D). Homology analysis of MDH2 protein sequences between humans and mice revealed that the sequence near residue 239 is highly conserved (Fig. 4E), suggesting that the K239 site is a conserved and potentially significant Kbhb site on MDH2. PTMs typically have a dynamic impact on protein activity, localization, and biological processes, often with minimal impact on total protein expression [27]. We found no significant alteration in MDH2 expression levels in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 4F). Next, we modeled the protein structure of human MDH2 using the protein data bank (PDB entry: 4WLN). Molecular docking analysis indicated that the K239 residue is adjacent to the MDH2 catalytic center, suggesting a potential effect of K239 Kbhb on MDH2 enzymatic activity (Fig. 4G). We then assessed the enzymatic activity of MDH2 in real-time following β-OHB treatment and observed increased MDH2 activity in the liver of HFD-fed mice treated with β-OHB, peaking at 40 s (Fig. S4B). The relative MDH2 activity, calculated at 40th second, showed a significant increase in β-OHB-treated groups compared to HFD controls (Fig. 4H). Similar increases in MDH2 activity were observed in AML-12 cells treated with β-OHB in OA conditions (Figs. 4I, S4C). Concurrently, we measured L-malic acid levels, the substrate of MDH2, using LC-MS in AML-12 cells. Notably, L-malic acid level decreased in β-OHB-treated cells with OA pre-stimulation (Fig. 4J). These data conclusively demonstrate that β-OHB-mediated Kbhb of MDH2 K239 upregulates MDH2 activity independently of changes in protein expression.

Kbhb of MDH2 K239 is essential for mitochondrial homeostasis and lipid deposition reduction

To further validate the specificity of Kbhb of MDH2-K239, we generated homology models of wild-type MDH2 (MDH2WT) and its inhibitory mutant for the Kbhb site of MDH2 lysine-to-arginine (MDH2K239R) and overexpressed these plasmids in AML-12 cells prior to OA and β-OHB treatment. We first assessed the expression levels of pan-Kbhb and MDH2-Kbhb in the MDH2K239Roverexpressed-cells and MDH2WTgroups. A considerable increase of expression of pan-Kbhb was observed in vector and MDH2WT cells, while this increase was completely abolished in MDH2K239R groups with the presence of β-OHB (Fig. 5A). Notably, the expression of pan-Kac did not show a substantial reduction in MDH2K239R cells like Kbhb by comparison to MDH2WT (Fig. S5A). Furthermore, COIP analysis showed that the expression of MDH2 Kbhb was specificity elevated by β-OHB treatment in MDH2WT cells and this effect was eliminated by MDH2K239R transfection (Fig. 5B), suggesting that β-OHB specifically induced MDH2 K239 Kbhb. Besides, elevated MDH2 enzymic activity mediated by β-OHB was repressed in MDH2K239R cells (Fig. 5C).

As a key enzyme in the TCA cycle, MDH2 mediates the NAD⁺-dependent reversible interconversion between malate and oxaloacetate. The NADH generated through this reaction serves as a vital electron carrier for the ETC, facilitating oxidative phosphorylation and ATP generation [28]. Consequently, proper MDH2 function plays a fundamental role in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis and modulating ROS levels. As previously demonstrated, β-OHB effectively reduced OA-induced ROS levels in vitro. To further explore the relationship between specific Kbhb of MDH2 K239 and mitochondrial oxidation, we measured the level of ROS production using flow cytometry, the result showed that OA-induced ROS production was significantly more pronounced in MDH2K239R overexpression cells compared to the vector and MDH2WT groups with β-OHB treatment (Fig. 5D). A similar trend was observed with MitoSOX staining (Figs. 5E, S5E), indicating that mitochondrial ROS production increased owing to MDH2 K239 Kbhb restrain. In addition, β-OHB led to a significant increase in OCR in MDH2WT cells whereas a gradual decrease in MDH2K239R cells (Figs. 5F, S5B-D). The MMP declined in MDH2K239R overexpression cells (Figs. 5G, S5F), confirming that suppression of MDH2 K239 Kbhb has an impact on mitochondrial injury repair by β-OHB. Moreover, a decreased trend of FAO activity was also observed in MDH2K239R overexpression cells with β-OHB treatment (Figs. 5 H, S5G) and OCR in the presence of exogenous FAO substrate declined in MDH2K239R cells while a significant increase was observed in MDH2WT cells (Fig. S5H-K). Next, further experiment were performed to validate whether Kbhb of MDH2 K239 whereby β-OHB influences aberrant lipid metabolism in vitro. A significant decrease of lipid deposition was observed in MDH2WT cells while the improvement of lipid accumulation via β-OHB could be abolished in MDH2K239R cells (Figs. 5I, S5L). Similar to the result of BODIPY staining, MDH2K239R overexpression cannot abolish the increase of TG and TC content in AML-12 cells induced by OA (Fig. 5J, K). These findings suggest that Kbhb of MDH2 K239 is essential for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and reducing lipid deposition in vitro.

Discussion

There was a concept that the advantage of KD may appear inflated in recent studies as KD cannot induce a more significant body fat loss than other dietary patterns such as low-fat diet and has a higher risk of hyperglycemia [29, 30]. Due to the controversial debate, KD is not recommended as a MASLD therapy considering the safety, sustainable effectiveness and patient compliance. Our previous study found that a short-term of 2 weeks KD induced a more rapid weight loss than low-fat diet whereas numerous metabolic disorders including severer hepatic steatosis and liver injury occurred in consistent use of KD, which is mainly associated with the level of β-OHB but the further regulation mechanisms of β-OHB remain unclear [11]. In this study, we confirmed that β-OHB induces a parallel weight loss in MASLD mice as KD and improves hepatocyte lipid deposition accompanied with an increase of protein Kbhb in vivo and in vitro. MDH2 Kbhb plays a vital role in the regulation of lipid accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction in MASLD and K239 is a key Kbhb site which increases MDH2 enzymatic activity.

Importantly, we observed that short-term β-OHB administration led to a 11.11-24.6% reduction in body weight in HFD-fed mice, while a more modest decrease was seen in ND-fed mice. Moreover, β-OHB treatment did not cause significant changes in organ indices or fasting blood glucose levels in ND-fed mice. HFD-fed mice exhibited higher serum β-OHB levels than ND-fed mice following β-OHB administration. Previous reports suggest that β-OHB levels vary among MASLD patients at different stages, and improving β-OHB flux may inhibit the progression of MASLD to NASH [31, 32]. As an endogenous metabolite, β-OHB can be synthesized in the liver via fatty acid β-oxidation or directly supplemented through exogenous ketone supplements, offering a safe and non-invasive therapeutic approach without the need for additional nutritional support.

In our study, the serum β-OHB concentration in mice reached a maximum level of 0.67 mmol/L following β-OHB administration, which essentially falls within the normal physiological range (0.2–0.5 mmol/L), significantly lower than the threshold for ketoacidosis (> 20 mmol/L) [33]. Notably, while the achieved β-OHB levels close to physiological range, they were sufficient to elicit systemic effects. This phenomenon may be attributed to β-OHB ‘s multiple roles including signaling molecule and epigenetic modifier. For example, β-OHB serves as a high-affinity ligand for G-protein coupled receptors (GPR109A and GPR41), mediating significant anti-inflammatory effects and lipid oxidation activation via receptor-dependent signaling [34, 35]. Except for Kbhb, β-OHB also acts as modification substrate to regulate a series of epigenetic phenomena such as Kac and microRNAs, to affect the transcription level of genes [36]. Collectively, the crosstalk of β-OHB’s multiple regulations may contribute to the integration between metabolic status and epigenetic changes, suggesting that β-OHB fluctuations may serve as a potent metabolic signal under both physiological and pathological conditions.

Additionally, a typical KD is characterized by restricted carbohydrate intake, which often leads to reduced dietary fiber consumption. This may result in a potential decline in gut microbiome health and a diminished production of short-chain fatty acids, including butyrate (a known ligand for GPR109A). Compared to a KD alone, exogenous β-OHB supplementation could mitigate these limitations. Despite only a slight elevation in circulating β-OHB levels, the synergistic action of butyrate and β-OHB could partially replicate the beneficial metabolic effects associated with the KD [37]. Furthermore, some systematic meta-analyses have shown that exogenous β-OHB supplementation does not induce adverse effects such as hunger, gastrointestinal discomfort or blood pressure fluctuations [38, 39]. Thus, β-OHB could serve as a safer and more sustainable alternative to the KD for MASLD management particularly given KD’s potential risks and exhibits poor patient compliance.

Given that lipid deposition is a hallmark of MASLD, four major pathways regulate the imbalance between lipid acquisition and disposal: circulating lipids uptake, de novo lipogenesis (DNL), FAO and lipid export [40]. In DNL process, acetyl-CoA is carboxylated to malonyl-CoA by acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and then esterified with 3-phosphoglycerol to synthesize TG. However, we did not find any significant alternation of the expression of enzymes related to DNL (FASN, ACC, SCD1, ACLY) and fatty acid metabolic processes (CPT2, FABP1/4) by proteomic analysis in the liver of MASLD mice with β-OHB treatment (Fig. S2B), suggesting that there exists a complex mechanism engaged in the regulation of β-OHB on hepatic lipid deposition.

To uncover further regulatory mechanisms of β-OHB, we conducted a Kbhb proteomic analysis and found that Kbhb-modified proteins were enriched in the TCA cycle. Since hepatic steatosis usually accompanies mitochondria insufficiency including ROS accumulation, impaired mitochondrial respiration as well as decreased TCA cycle activity [40]we first verified the effect of β-OHB on mitochondrial dysfunction. Except for improving hepatocyte mitochondrial integrity in MASLD mice, β-OHB also has ability to reduce ROS production and elevate mitochondrial respiration in vitro. Additionally, we identified MDH2, a key enzyme in the TCA cycle responsible for malate oxidation, as being specifically β-hydroxybutyrylated by β-OHB at the K239 site. This modification appears to enhance MDH2 activity independent of changes in protein expression.

Notably, although the β-hydroxybutyrylation proteomics analysis identified a greater number of down-regulated sites, it may have physiological relevance since TCA cycle operates as a tightly regulated metabolic network where each enzymatic product serves as the substrate for the subsequent reaction. Elevated MDH2 activity induced by β-OHB leads to accumulation of oxaloacetate (OAA), which in turn stimulates citrate synthase (CS) activity through substrate-level activation. This is particularly relevant as CS has been reported to undergo Kbhb modification [13, 41]. Furthermore, MDH2-driven NADH production increases the NADH/NAD⁺ ratio, resulting in feedback inhibition of NAD⁺-dependent dehydrogenases including isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) and α-Ketoglutarate Dehydrogenase (OGDH). Consistently, our Kbhb-proteomic analysis identified both IDH and OGDH as targets of Kbhb modification (Fig. 4A), suggesting that perturbations in MDH2-Kbhb may exert cascading effects, potentially modulating both the enzymatic function of other TCA cycle components and the expression levels of pathway-associated proteins. Such secondary regulatory effects could consequently influence the pan-Kbhb modification status.

Mitochondria, as the primary place for FAO, are intimately associated with liver steatosis [42]. According to our findings, β-OHB improves mitochondrial integrity and dysfunction in MASLD. In addition, β-OHB also results in an alternation of dynamics of the mitochondria (data unshown) but the further mechanism is still uncertain.

Compared to liver, a slighter raise of expression of Kbhb was also found in kidney (data unshown), but not other metabolic tissues such as skeletal muscle, white adipose, intestinal epithelial cells, cerebral cortex as well as heart [43]. Nevertheless, the discrepancy of tissue-specific modification of Kbhb warrants further investigation.

β-OHB is commonly employed as an inhibitor of histone deacetylases (HDAC) as well [44]. In our study, it is worthy to mention that Kac was found upregulated in parallel with Kbhb as a result of substantial increase in acetyl-CoA response to β-OHB treatment, unveiling a complex PTMs network between Kac and Kbhb [45]. Besides, we previously established that Kbhb mediates lipid deposition improvement in a β-OHB concentration-dependent manner. It is plausible that β-OHB directly binds to CoA, forming β-OHB-CoA, which serves as the substrate for Kbhb, while the precise mechanism by which β-OHB promotes acetyl-CoA activation for Kac remains unclear [46] thus, Kbhb has a higher priority than Kac under high concentration of β-OHB condition. Further differentiation was confirmed, as Kac expression did not decrease in MDH2K239R cells as Kbhb did, indicating that MDH2 K239 is a precise target of Kbhb.

Notably, since antibody-based detection remains widely used in PTM studies, recent evidence has highlighted significant challenges regarding H3K9bhb antibody reliability, particularly for structurally similar acylations [47]. In our study, we focused on the non-histone target-MDH2 rather than histone H3K9. We therefore employed Co-IP/LC-MS for MDH2-Kbhb analysis rather than site-specific antibodies. Our approach was further validated through mutagenesis experiments in vitro demonstrating that MDH2K239 serves as a specific modification site for Kbhb, as evidenced by acetylation level measurements following K239R transfection (Fig. S5A). However, we acknowledge that the absence of cross-validation with other acylation databases represents a technical limitation. This constraint is shared by many PTM studies due to the current incompleteness of comprehensive modification databases.

Furthermore, aside from the ameliorative effects of Kbhb, a recent study demonstrated that β-OHB-amino acids (particularly BHB-Phe) are generated during fasting and act in an anti-obesogenic manner, providing an intriguing mechanistic link to the observed weight loss phenotype in β-OHB-treated MASLD mice [48]. This β-OHB shunt pathway leads to the formation of BHB-Phe, which suppresses appetite by activating specific neuronal circuits in the hypothalamus and brainstem. The discovery of β-OHB-amino acids as endogenous appetite suppressors provides a broader framework to explain the weight loss and MASLD amelioration.

Given the higher clinical prevalence of MASLD in males and to avoid potential confounding effects of estrogen-mediated protection as well as estrous cycle variability in females, we utilized male mice exclusively for this study [49, 50]. However, we acknowledge that biological sex differences extend far beyond hormonal cycles, encompassing genetic, epigenetic, and metabolic dimensions. Our single-sex design may limit the drug development where sex-specific responses in pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and safety are increasingly recognized. Future studies should systematically evaluate both sexes to identify potential divergent mechanisms and optimize therapeutic strategies for all patients.

Conclusion

We identified that β-OHB increases MDH2 enzymic activity via Kbhb modification at MDH2 K239, leading to the alleviation of hepatocyte lipid deposition and improvement in mitochondrial function and integrity. Given its multiple targeting potential, β-OHB holds promise as a safe and effective therapy for MASLD.
