End-tidal CO2 changes as predictors of significant blood pressure variations during general anesthesia
Authors: Amorn Vijitpavan, Kochakarn Luengwattanakit, Kakanand Srungboonmee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to establish thresholds for end-tidal CO2 (EtCO2) changes that indicate hemodynamic alterations occurring between intermittent blood pressure measurements. Given that EtCO2 reflects cardiac output under conditions of stable systemic vascular resistance, it may serve as a continuous hemodynamic surrogate when noninvasive blood pressure monitoring is intermittent.MethodsThis prospective observational study included participants (ASA I-IV) undergoing elective surgery with general anesthesia and controlled ventilation. EtCO2 and blood pressure were recorded every 3 min intraoperatively. Changes in EtCO2 (ΔEtCO2) between consecutive recordings were analyzed against corresponding percentage changes in systolic blood pressure (%SBP).ResultsAnalysis of 4,068 paired measurements from 120 patients showed 77.1% of events maintained hemodynamic stability (± 10% SBP change). For substantial blood pressure elevations (%SBP ≥ 20%), ΔEtCO2 ≥ 2 demonstrated sensitivity 95.9%, specificity 97.7%, and negative predictive value 99.8%. For severe blood pressure reductions (%SBP ≤ -20%), ΔEtCO2 ≤ -2 showed sensitivity 94.7%, specificity 97.3%, and negative predictive value 99.9%. Both thresholds exhibited strong positive likelihood ratios (42.5 for increases, 35.0 for decreases) and minimal negative likelihood ratios (0.04 for increases, 0.05 for decreases), with superior performance in predicting severe (%SBP ± 20%) versus moderate (%SBP ± 10%) blood pressure changes.ConclusionsΔEtCO2 effectively predicted significant systolic blood pressure changes in anesthetized patients, with ± 2 mmHg thresholds showing excellent diagnostic performance for detecting clinically significant blood pressure variations. These promising findings suggest ΔEtCO2 could serve as a valuable component in hemodynamic monitoring, particularly when integrated with other clinical parameters. Future studies should explore artificial intelligence models incorporating multiple parameters alongside ΔEtCO2 for more accurate hemodynamic monitoring.Keywords: End-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2), Hemodynamic monitoring, Blood pressure prediction, Intraoperative monitoring, General anesthesia

Introduction

Intraoperative hemodynamic stability maintenance necessitates vigilant blood pressure monitoring during surgical anesthesia. Current standard protocols mandate continuous blood pressure assessment at 5-minute intervals [1]. Oscillometric non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP) measurement represents the predominant modality in contemporary practice; however, this methodology exhibits inherent limitations in temporal resolution that may preclude timely detection of acute hemodynamic perturbations during complex procedures. Delayed recognition of severe hypotension is associated with increased risk of adverse perioperative outcomes, including myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular events, and acute kidney injury [2–5]. While continuous arterial pressure monitoring via intra-arterial catheterization offers superior temporal resolution for early hypotensive episode detection [6], this invasive technique carries potential complications including arterial wall trauma, thrombosis formation, pseudoaneurysm development, and distal ischemia [7, 8].

End-tidal carbon dioxide concentration (EtCO2) monitoring provides a noninvasive assessment of expired CO2 partial pressure. EtCO2 values primarily reflect the physiologic dead space to tidal volume ratio (Vdphys/Vt), with determinants including metabolic CO2 production, pulmonary perfusion (cardiac output), and alveolar ventilation [9–12]. Hemodynamic alterations affecting cardiac output consequently influence systemic blood pressure; therefore, analysis of EtCO2 fluctuations may serve as a surrogate marker for real-time hemodynamic changes. This monitoring approach potentially addresses the temporal limitations inherent to traditional non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP) measurement, which operates on predetermined intervals and may fail to detect clinically significant hemodynamic perturbations occurring between scheduled measurements.

Methods

The protocol of this study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine, Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand (MURA2020/927, May 28, 2020) and registered in the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20210204003) prior to patient enrollment.

Design of the study and participant recruitment

This prospective observational study enrolled participants scheduled for elective surgery under general anesthesia with controlled ventilation between February 8 and May 26, 2021. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Exclusion criteria included neurosurgery, cardiothoracic procedures, upper abdominal surgery, laparoscopic surgery, and patients with pulmonary disease or sepsis.

Ventilation management and circuit integrity

To ensure stable alveolar ventilation throughout the study period, several measures were implemented. Laparoscopic surgeries were excluded to avoid effects of CO₂ insufflation, and upper abdominal surgeries were excluded to prevent interference from diaphragmatic movement. Neuromuscular blocking agents were administered to all patients to maintain controlled ventilation. Circuit integrity was maintained through continuous monitoring of inspiratory and expiratory tidal volumes and evaluation of pressure-volume loops on the anesthesia machine display. Prior to induction, circuit leak checks were performed, and following intubation, endotracheal tube cuff inflation was verified by auscultation before initiating data collection.

Anesthesia

Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was implemented, including 3-lead ECG, pulse oximetry, and automated oscillometric noninvasive blood pressure (NIBP) measurement (Philips Medical Systems, Eindhoven, Netherlands) at 3-minute intervals. End-tidal CO2 (EtCO2) was monitored via the anesthesia machine (GE HealthCare, Madison, WI, USA). Soda lime is replaced daily as part of standard protocol in our institution. Throughout the study, fractional inspiratory CO₂ (FiCO₂) was monitored to ensure it remained at zero, confirming effective CO₂ absorption and absence of rebreathing. This quality control measure helped verify that soda lime exhaustion did not confound our EtCO₂ measurements.

Anesthesia induction was performed with intravenous propofol (1.5–2 mg/kg) and fentanyl (2 µg/kg), and endotracheal intubation was facilitated using either cisatracurium (0.15 mg/kg) or atracurium (0.6 mg/kg). Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane in an oxygen-air mixture targeting 1.25 MAC, with cisatracurium (2 mg) or atracurium (10 mg) administered every 30 min. Mechanical ventilation was initiated with a tidal volume of 6 ml/kg, PEEP of 7 cmH2O, and FiO2 of 0.4. The respiratory rate was adjusted to maintain an EtCO2 of 30–35 mmHg, after which ventilation parameters remained unchanged.

Hypotension during induction and maintenance of general anesthesia was defined as any of the following: a systolic blood pressure (SBP) below 90 mmHg, a decrease in SBP exceeding 30% from baseline or a mean arterial pressure (MAP) below 65 mmHg. Management included administration of 6 mg ephedrine and a 250 ml bolus of balanced salt solution, repeated as needed if MAP remained below 65 mmHg. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure exceeding 160 mmHg [13, 14] or increasing by more than 20% from baseline. In such cases, 0.4 mg of nicardipine was administered, and the sevoflurane concentration was increased.

Anesthesia was discontinued at the end of the procedure, and muscle relaxation was reversed with atropine (0.02 mg/kg) and neostigmine (0.05 mg/kg). Tracheal extubation was performed once extubation criteria were met.

Data collection

Baseline characteristics (age, sex, body weight, BMI, comorbidities, ASA classification, type of surgery, and preoperative blood pressure) were documented. EtCO2 and SBP were obtained simultaneously at three-minute intervals, starting immediately after endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation initiation until the termination of controlled ventilation (Fig. 1). At each predetermined time point, the SBP was taken and EtCO2 value at the synchronized time stamp was recorded. Changes in EtCO2 between consecutive recordings (ΔEtCO2), absolute difference (current value - previous value), and percentage changes in blood pressure between consecutive measurements (%SBP) were calculated and documented as time-paired entries. %SBP were calculated using the formula: [(current BP - previous BP)/previous BP] × 100%. The initial measurement, taken immediately after intubation was completed, served as the first reference point for subsequent calculations.

Schematic timeline of data collection. End-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO₂) and systolic blood pressure (SBP) measurements were recorded at 3-minute intervals. The change in EtCO₂ (ΔEtCO₂) and percent change in SBP (%SBP) were calculated from the differences between consecutive measurements

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using StataCorp Version 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, 2023). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages.

End-tidal carbon dioxide difference (ΔEtCO2) was calculated as the difference between consecutive EtCO2 measurements, while systolic blood pressure changes (%SBP) were expressed as percentage deviations from preceding values. Diagnostic ΔEtCO2 cut-offs were determined by histogram visualization. The predictive performance of these cut-offs for hemodynamic changes was evaluated using prevalence, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values (PPV, NPV), and likelihood ratios (LR+, LR-).

Table 2 demonstrated how the 2 threshold of ΔEtCO2 (ΔEtCO2 ≥ 2) was able to predict %SBP in acceptable range (−10% < %SBP < 10%), more than 10% increase (%SBP ≥ 10%) and 20% increase (%SBP ≥ 20%), which accounted for 93.93% of all events. ΔEtCO2 ≥ 2 demonstrated varying predictive capabilities for different thresholds of systolic blood pressure elevation. For %SBP ≥ 10%, which had a prevalence of 12.6%, despite its exceptional specificity (99.5%) and positive predictive value (93.3%), the low sensitivity (46.00%) suggested limited utility in screening. However, for %SBP ≥ 20%, with a lower prevalence of 4.23%, the parameter exhibited superior diagnostic performance with excellent sensitivity (95.9%) and specificity (97.7%). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was 0.970 (95% CI: 0.956–0.984). The notably high negative predictive value (99.8%) and very low negative likelihood ratio (0.04) for %SBP ≥ 20% indicated that ΔEtCO2 ≥ 2 was particularly effective at ruling out significant blood pressure increases. The positive likelihood ratios for both thresholds (96.2 and 42.5) suggested strong diagnostic value when the test was positive. These findings indicated that ΔEtCO2 ≥ 2 was a more reliable predictor for substantial blood pressure elevations (%SBP ≥ 20%) compared to moderate increases (%SBP ≥ 10%), making it a valuable tool for identifying patients at risk of significant hemodynamic fluctuations.

For %SBP ≤ −10%, despite its exceptional specificity (99.4%) and positive predictive value (88.3%), the low sensitivity (37.71%) suggested limited utility in screening. However, for %SBP ≤ −20%, the parameter exhibited superior diagnostic performance with excellent sensitivity (94.7%) and specificity (97.3%). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was 0.956 (95% CI: 0.929–0.984). The notably high negative predictive value (99.9%) and very low negative likelihood ratio (0.05) for %SBP ≤ −20% indicated that ΔEtCO2 ≤ −2 was particularly effective at ruling out severe blood pressure decreases. The positive likelihood ratios for both thresholds (65.5 and 35.0) suggested strong diagnostic value when the test was positive. These findings indicated that ΔEtCO2 ≤ −2 was a more reliable predictor for severe blood pressure drops (%SBP ≤ −20%) compared to moderate decreases (%SBP ≤ −10%), making it a valuable tool for identifying patients at risk of significant hemodynamic deterioration.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that ΔEtCO2 thresholds of ± 2 were effective predictors of significant blood pressure changes during general anesthesia. While both thresholds showed limited sensitivity for moderate blood pressure changes (≥ ± 10%, prevalence 12.6% for increases and 10.3% for decreases), they exhibited superior diagnostic performance for detecting substantial blood pressure variations (≥ ± 20%, prevalence 4.23% for increases and 1.8% for decreases). For increases in blood pressure, ΔEtCO2 ≥ 2 demonstrated excellent sensitivity (95.9%) and specificity (97.7%) for %SBP ≥ 20%, with strong positive likelihood ratio (42.5) and minimal negative likelihood ratio (0.04). Similarly, for decreases in blood pressure, ΔEtCO2 ≤ −2 showed comparable performance in predicting %SBP ≤ −20%, with sensitivity of 94.7%, specificity of 97.3%, positive likelihood ratio of 35.0, and negative likelihood ratio of 0.05. The exceptionally high negative predictive values (> 99.8%) for both thresholds highlighted ΔEtCO2 monitoring’s potential as an early warning tool for significant blood pressure variations during anesthesia, particularly in detecting severe hemodynamic changes. These findings are especially noteworthy given the relatively low prevalence of substantial blood pressure changes in our study population.

Blood pressure and EtCO2

Blood pressure (BP) is determined by cardiac output (CO) and systemic vascular resistance (SVR). Under stable SVR conditions, BP variations correlate with end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) changes, which reflect CO. During surgery, while BP was measured intermittently at 5-minute intervals, changes in EtCO2 between consecutive recordings (ΔEtCO2) could estimate CO and BP variations, assuming stable alveolar ventilation and SVR. EtCO2 has been established as an indicator of fluid responsiveness in numerous previous studies, with several investigations demonstrating that alterations in EtCO2 values can predict fluid responsiveness associated with elevated CO levels [15–20]. The change in EtCO2 can also reflect blood pressure alteration as observed in the hemorrhagic shock and volume resuscitation [21, 22].

A threshold of ΔETCO2 ≥ 2 has been identified in previous studies, aligning with our findings but differing in applications. Toupin et al. [15] demonstrated that ΔETCO2 ≥ 2 mmHg predicted systolic blood pressure changes ≥ 10 mmHg during passive leg raising tests, while Baloch et al. [20] found ΔETCO2 ≥ 2 to be the optimal predictor of fluid responsiveness in cardiogenic shock. Although these studies validate ΔEtCO2 ≥ 2 as a significant threshold, our study demonstrated its superior performance in detecting more substantial blood pressure variations (%SBP ≥ 20%) rather than moderate changes (%SBP ≥ 10%). This difference may be attributed to the distinct clinical contexts. Notably, our findings extend the utility of ΔEtCO2 monitoring beyond fluid responsiveness assessment to continuous hemodynamic surveillance during general anesthesia.

Continuous blood pressure monitoring

Both invasive and noninvasive continuous blood pressure monitoring methods are available. Invasive monitoring has demonstrated reduced hypotension events in ASA III-IV patients undergoing moderate-to-high-risk noncardiac surgery under general anesthesia [23] and remains essential for prompt detection of significant blood pressure changes [23, 24]. While noninvasive methods, including finger cuff volume-clamp [25–29] and tonometry [30, 31], offer continuous monitoring capabilities, they share common limitations as invasive methods, such as measurement inaccuracies during severe vasoconstriction, vascular disease, hydrostatic offset between measurement site and heart level, and patient movement [31]. However, Kim SH et al. [32] demonstrated that the magnitude of inaccuracy and imprecision was greater with continuous noninvasive arterial pressure monitoring compared to invasive methods.

The findings of this study suggest that continuous ΔEtCO2 monitoring could complement conventional intermittent blood pressure measurements in detecting significant hemodynamic changes. ΔEtCO2 thresholds of ≥ 2 and ≤−2 demonstrated excellent predictive value for substantial blood pressure variations (≥ ± 20%), with high sensitivity (> 94%) and specificity (> 97%). However, clinicians should note that these thresholds yielded false positive rates of 2.3% for increases and 2.7% for decreases, where blood pressure remained within acceptable ranges (−10% to + 10%). While this approach offers real-time surveillance using standard anesthesia equipment without additional costs, clinical correlation with blood pressure measurements remains essential. This method could be particularly valuable in settings where invasive arterial monitoring is not indicated, providing an additional layer of hemodynamic surveillance during general anesthesia.

Our results indicated that while changes in EtCO2 showed promising correlation with systolic blood pressure changes, this relationship could be enhanced by incorporating additional clinical parameters for more comprehensive blood pressure prediction. End-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) monitoring serves as a critical tool in intensive care unit management and a surrogate measure for various physiological parameters [33]. In cardiac arrest scenarios, EtCO2-guided cardiopulmonary resuscitation, particularly when combined with diastolic blood pressure (DBP) monitoring, demonstrates superior outcomes compared to conventional fixed-parameter CPR. Porcine models have validated that this EtCO2-DBP approach enhances both hemodynamic parameters and survival rates [34]. Additionally, EtCO2 functions as a biomarker for asthma exacerbation severity, where decreased levels correlate with adverse outcomes [35]. These applications highlight EtCO2’s potential as a key parameter for artificial intelligence model development.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the study population consisted exclusively of patients under general anesthesia with neuromuscular blockade and controlled mechanical ventilation, potentially limiting generalizability to patients with spontaneous breathing, pediatric populations, or those with significant pulmonary pathology. Second, the predictive value of ΔEtCO2 may be compromised in conditions with significant metabolic fluctuations (such as fever or shivering) or varying systemic vascular resistance (SVR), where changes in EtCO2 cannot be attributed solely to alterations in cardiac output and blood pressure. Third, given the observational design of this study, causal relationships between the observed variables cannot be definitively established. Lastly, our prediction analysis focused exclusively on SBP rather than MAP. While SBP was selected for its typically higher sensitivity to immediate hemodynamic fluctuations, future studies comparing the predictive performance of models based on both SBP and MAP would provide additional valuable insights.

Conclusion

The application of ΔEtCO2 thresholds of ± 2 demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance in predicting significant blood pressure changes during general anesthesia. While these thresholds showed limited sensitivity for moderate blood pressure changes (≥ ± 10%), they exhibited superior predictive value for substantial variations (≥ ± 20%). These findings suggest that ΔEtCO2 monitoring could serve as a valuable early warning tool for detecting clinically significant hemodynamic changes in mechanically ventilated patients under general anesthesia. It is particularly noteworthy given the relatively low prevalence of severe blood pressure reductions and elevations in the study population. Although end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) correlates with cardiac output and potentially blood pressure, this prospective observational study demonstrates its potential utility in predicting blood pressure variations. Despite current generalizability limitations, these findings suggest opportunities for future artificial intelligence modeling applications.
