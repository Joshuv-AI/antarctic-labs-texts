Productive engagement patterns and their association with life satisfaction among elderly sandwich generation
Authors: Yang Yu, Liujun Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Little is known about the patterns of productive engagement among elderly sandwich generation. Related, the implication of these patterns for life satisfaction remains unclear. The present study addresses these gaps. The sample comprised 497 Shanghai ESG aged 55 and above. Seven productive activities were included, and latent class analysis was employed to identify prevalent patterns of productive engagement. Regression analysis was then conducted to investigate the relationship between these patterns and life satisfaction. Four patterns of productive engagement among ESG were identified, including Active engagement except for working (46.3%), Family Caring (22.3%), Family Caring-Spiritual growth (21.9%), and Working-Parent caring (9.5%). Compared with the Family Caring -Spiritual growth class, ESG of Working-Parent caring and Active engagement except for working were related to lower life satisfaction. The identification and characterization of those patterns may help community workers and municipalities to provide more appropriate help for different classes of ESG.

Introduction

In China, providing care for elderly parents or parents-in-law remains a common and normative experience for adult children1,2. Similarly, the same social norms and traditional family structures imply that many Chinese grandparents wish to provide care for their young grand children3,4. With the extension of life expectancy, delayed marriage and childbirth, and declining fertility rates, this trend is expected to continue5. An increasing number of older adults can now except to have both one or more surviving parents or parents-in-law and child (ren)4,6, which implies that a growing number of older adults in China face the possibility of being ‘sandwiched’ between the provision of care to their own older parents/parents-in-law and also to their younger children or grandchildren7,8. In our study, this group is referred to as the elderly sandwich generation (abbreviated as ESG). It should be noted that ESG in our study refers to those who have both surviving parents and children and are aged 55 and above1. From this perspective, we focus more on the social roles and relationships of older adults rather than their specific behaviors. Therefore, they may provide care in some areas, or no care at all.

According to recent research across 14 European countries, nearly 30% of grandparents are simultaneously cared for by both their own parents and grandchildren9. In China, recent estimates indicate that over a quarter of grandparents belong to the ESG, with over one-tenth being dual caregivers—providing informal care for both their own parents and grandchildren10. We do not equate the two sets of figures but view them as evidence of multi-generational care emerging under both generous and residual welfare systems. Therefore, the current size of the ESG is considerable, and it is expanding within the demographic trends of China. Understanding the aging issues of the "sandwich generation," especially the multifaceted social participation issues and their psychological effects, is particularly important in the context of China. This is because of China has emphasized the productive engagement of older adults in various domains11. Besides being provided care by family members12,13, there is also a call for older adults to contribute to work, social service activities, lifelong learning, and other forms of engagement, to promote a more meaningful aging process for individuals and society.

Literature review

Research on social participation of the ESG

Currently, a substantial body of gerontology research discusses the multidimensional social participation of older adults and its psychological impacts on the aging process14,15. Among these, numerous studies have distinguished productive engagement from other activities, acknowledging it as an indicator of successful aging and its importance for family and societal welfare16. However, to our knowledge, few studies have explored the type of the productive engagement among ESG. Although evidence is limited, research on senior caregivers suggests that time constraints and role pressures may limit other participation, like formal volunteering. Similar mechanisms may be at play among ESG, who face higher caregiving pressures. Compared to the general elderly population, ESG may face three additional constraints. The first is chronic time pressure due to caregiving responsibilities17,18. The second is economic dependence on adult children, which may reduce disposable resources for social activities19,20. And the third comes to housing pressure from multi-generational living arrangements, which may blur the boundaries between private and public life21. These factors may compress or reshape patterns of productive engagement, making ESG a theoretically unique subgroup whose engagement patterns warrant specialized research.

Interpretation of productive engagement

Considering that the ESG possesses dual attributes as members of the SG and older adults, which implies that, as members of the sandwich generation, they may be involved in more caregiving responsibilities, while as older adults, they seek to achieve more active and meaningful aging through participation in various social activities. From this perspective, the concept of productive aging appears to provide a deeper understanding of our research question. As productive aging emphasizes not only the roles of older adults within the ESG but also focuses on their engagement in multiple productive activities and their impact on individual aging16.

The concept of productive aging emerged initially within developed countries around 1982, a period marked by concurrent pressures of economic growth and escalating social welfare demands. This framework aimed to encourage individuals to take responsibility for their well-being, thus enhancing socioeconomic vitality22–24. As defined by Bass, Caro, & Chen(1993)25, productive aging initially encompassed various activities through which older adults contribute to the production of goods or services, irrespective of compensation. With ongoing research and societal changes, the understanding of productive aging has become increasingly nuanced. Sherraden (2001) subsequently categorized productive activities into six domains: market-based economic activities, economically valuable non-market activities, formal social contributions, informal social assistance, social relationships and activities, and self-improvement26. Further refining measurement approaches, Yoko Sugihara (2008) operationalized productive roles through participation in paid work, volunteer work, and unpaid household labor27.

Overall, scholarly consensus remains limited regarding the scope of activities constituting productive engagement. Within the active aging advocating in China, the discourse on productive engagement emphasizes both macro-level social participation by older adults and micro-level contributions encompassing household labor, family care, lifelong learning, and self-reliance. Synthesizing prior research, we defined productive engagement as the direct or indirect engagement of older adults in activities beneficial to both personal development and societal progress, ultimately aiming to achieve a harmonious integration of personal and societal values. Within this conceptualization28–31]while considering China’s specific context11,32, we examined four key activities: caregiving, employment, and volunteering as productive activities. And alongside seven distinct types of productive activities: (1) caring for parent/parent-in-law, (2) caring for grandchild, (3) paid work, (4) community service, (5) volunteering, (6) lifelong learning, and (7) traveling.

Within the context of Confucian culture in China, family cohesion norms prioritize intergenerational care as a central productive role for older adults15. Filial piety obligations further reinforce reciprocal support expectations across age groups33,34. Regarding economic participation, paid work remains a historically significant productive role tied to economic activity35,36. In the social domain, formal volunteering advances socioeconomic development33,34. Meanwhile, informal community contributions, including material and emotional support to neighbors, reflect traditional expectations for the meaningful community integration of the elderly. These culturally embedded practices collectively sustain social engagement in later life. Spiritual growth through productive engagement constitutes a critical dimension of productive aging. Lifelong learning, through the acquisition of new competencies, helps individuals adapt to life transitions. Concurrently, rising living standards have increased travel prevalence among Chinese elders. Beyond traditional direct contribution paradigms, contemporary scholarship conceptualizes self-investment activities as a process of enhancing human, social, and cultural capital to generate social externalities37,38.Travel exemplifies this when integrated with learning, volunteering, or entrepreneurship: cross-cultural exposure maintains cognitive function (human capital); journey-based networking builds weak ties (social capital); and cultural immersion creates transferable knowledge (cultural capital)39. Our study thus positions travel as a core productive dimension, acknowledging its dual self-investment and social spillover properties. In summary, it makes sense to consider various types of activities including caregiving, employment, and volunteering as productive activities. These investigations represent a comprehensive acknowledgment of economic, familial, and societal perspectives on productive engagement.

Research on productive engagement and its impact on life satisfaction

Over the years, literature has documented the impact of productive engagement on the life satisfaction of the elderly40–43. The early research focused on the positive or negative consequences of engagement in specific activities. For example, extensive studies support a positive relationship between employment and life satisfaction among older adults. These studies indicate that individuals aged 65 and above who remain active in the labor market are more satisfied with their lives compared to their retired peers44,45. However, some research has also found that poor working environments and excessive work negatively impact the quality of life for older adults46. Similarly, numerous studies have examined the effects of family caring, informal help and volunteer work, learning, and traveling on the life satisfaction of older adults.

Furthermore, considering that older adults often engage in multiple productive activities as they age, and their participation in different activities may influence each other47,48, leading to different psychological welfare outcomes. In this context, focusing solely on one activity may be challenging to reflect the actual situation of older adults’ productive engagement and may also result in an oversimplification or underestimation of the impact of single or concurrent productive activities on psychological well-being. Therefore, some composite studies, which investigate the simultaneous participation of older adults in various productive activities and their effects, have become a recent research perspective27,49. For example, simultaneous examination of caregiving and volunteering50,51, volunteering and employment42,52, on the life satisfaction of older adults. More in-depthly, in recent years, some studies have begun to address the patterns of productive engagement among older adults and their psychological effects. Based on the categories and degrees of activities in which older adults participate, these studies merge and classify the productive activities into different participation patterns, such as Low Activity, Family Support, Moderate Working-Volunteering, and Working-Family Support53, or Informal Helper/Carer, Working-Caring Engager, Civic Contributor, and Low/Spousal Carer54, and further explore how these patterns are associated with physical and mental health and well-being. This pattern-oriented research is more helpful in capturing a more comprehensive range of productive activities and their potential interrelationships55, thereby providing important evidence for identifying and assisting different classes of older adults.

However, research on productive engagement specifically focused on ESG individuals is currently lacking. And even fewer studies have addressed the patterns of productive engagement among ESG individuals and their impact on life satisfaction. Therefore, based on the actual situation of ESG individuals in Shanghai, this study aimed to identify distinct patterns of productive engagement among ESG, and then went deeper into to explore how the identified patterns are associated with life satisfaction. For our study, the classification may helpt community workers and municipal authorities to identify the producitive engagement issues of ESG, and thereby develop appropriate social interventions to effectively enhance the life satisfaction of ESG individuals.

Methods

Participants and sampling

In this study, we used data from the Lifelong Learning and Productive Aging Survey of Shanghai (LEAP-SH). This is a large-scale sample survey program in Shanghai launched in 2022 to obtain data on the quality of life and aging needs of the current elderly population through long-term surveys on their daily lives, family lives, social lives, health conditions, etc., so as to provide authentic and reliable basic information for social science research and governmental formulation of public policies on aging. The questionnaire was revised through 30 pilot interviews and two rounds of expert consultation meetings. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of East China University of Political Science and Law in 2021.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Data was collected over a period of October 2022-April 2023. More than 1,707 elderly people aged 55–84 years old in urban Shanghai living in Shanghai were the subject of this study. A questionnaire was produced covering basic personal and family information, socio-economic situation, family structure and intergenerational relationships, work, skills and learning, volunteering, social participation and community facilities, health and living habits, financial literacy, leisure and lifestyle. The survey was conducted through house-to-house interviews by trained enumerators at the homes of the elderly or in their communities, and the question and answer session was conducted on a one-to-one basis, which ensured the response rate of the survey. The survey used a stratified sampling method. Specifically, at the district level, 10 districts were randomly selected from Shanghai. At the street (town) level, 2 streets (towns) were randomly selected from each district. At the residential (village) committee level, 2 residential (village) committees were randomly selected from each street (town), totaling 40 residential (village) committees. At the individual level, more than 40 samples were collected by quota after entering each neighborhood (village) committee.

Based on the characteristics of the study population, sample selection was conducted. According to the features of ESG individuals, The overall sample was divided based on whether they simultaneously had both parents and children, resulting in 498 individuals (29.16%) classified as “sandwich generation” and 1209 individuals (70.84%) classified as non-“sandwich generation”. Subsequently, one case with missing data on multiple important variables was excluded. Finally, 497 ESG individuals were included in our analysis, comprising 211 male ESG individuals (42.45%) and 286 female ESG individuals (57.55%).

Life satisfaction

The dependent variable was life satisfaction. The life satisfaction of ESG was measured using the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) developed by Diener et al. (1985). This scale is composed of five items through which individual was asked to evaluate life satisfaction based on his or her own criteria, including:In most ways my life is close to my ideal.So far I have gotten the important things I want in life.I am satisfied with my life.If I could live my life over, I would change almost nothing.The conditions of my life are excellent.

Participants had to indicate how much they agreed or disagreed with each item using a seven-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).The answers were added to an individual score. Higher scores indicated a higher level of life satisfaction. The reliability and validity of the SWLS Scale has been extensively reviewed as adequate for the elderly56.

Productive engagement

Seven engagement activities were identified for productive aging: caregiving for parents or in-laws, caring for grandchild, paid work, community service, volunteering, lifelong learning, and traveling. Caregiving for parents or in-laws was assessed by inquiring about the frequency of financial support, household assistance, and medical care provided to one’s own or spouse’s parents. Responses were categorized as frequent, occasional, or never. The caregiving variable was dichotomized, with 1 indicating any frequent or occasional involvement in the aforementioned activities, and 0 indicating no involvement.

Grandchild caregiving is assessed by the frequency of assistance provided to one’s children, specifically regarding grandchild care. Responses of frequent or occasional caregiving are scored as 1, while never is scored as 0.

Paid work is determined by the individual’s current employment status, with full-time, part-time, or temporary work indicated by a score of 1, and no work by a score of 0.

Community service involvement is measured by participation in community roles such as building group leader, village group leader, or grid member. Participation in any role is scored as 1, while no participation is scored as 0.

Volunteering is evaluated based on participation in volunteer services within the past year, with affirmative responses scored as 1 and negative responses as 0.

Travelling is gauged by the response to the question, "Do you frequently travel?" with “Yes” coded as 1 and “No” coded as 0.

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of seven socio-demographic variables were included, which include age, gender, education level, marital status, self-rated health status, financial adequacy, and activities of daily living(ADL). These factors have been found to be associated with life satisfaction in previous research56–58. Therefore, they are considered as covariates in our analysis.

Statistical analysis

Mplus 7.4 version and SPSS 26.0 software were used for statistical analysis. In the first step, descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic characteristics of the participants and their data related to productive engagement. In the second step, t-tests and chi-square tests were used to compare the demographic characteristics and other survey variables between male ESGs and female ESGs. In the third step, latent class analysis (LCA) was conducted using Mplus 7.4 version to identify the productive engagement patterns of ESG in Shanghai based on their participation in the seven productive activities mentioned above.

Latent Class Analysis (LCA) identifies unobserved subgroups through patterns of productive engagement. We selected LCA over factor analysis given its theoretical alignment with detecting substantively distinct subgroups rather than continuous dimensions. Compared to cluster analysis, LCA provides model-based posterior membership probabilities and robust fit indices, including AIC, BIC, LMR test, entropy, thereby reducing scaling sensitivity inherent in distance metrics59,60. This human-centered approach classifies individuals, rather than variables, into classes based on activity similarity. The optimal number of classes was determined through iterative model comparisons using multiple fit statistics. LCA outputs primarily include two parameters, one being the latent class probabilities derived from activity co-occurrence patterns to infer class distributions and structure, and the other being the conditional probabilities of participating in specific activities within each class61.

In the fourth step, in view of the validation results, we implemented hierarchical multivariate logistic analysis to assess the individual relationship between productive engagement patterns and life satisfaction of ESG. According to the previous section, we mainly hypothesized the effect of productive engagement patterns on life satisfaction of ESG. While also considering the confounding effects of the individual factors, we therefore treated the demographic characteristics as the control variables in this study. With the life satisfaction of the ESG as the outcome variable, in Model 1, demographic variables were entered as control variables, including age, gender, education level, marital status, self-assessed health status, self-assessed economic status, and suffering from chronic diseases. In Model 2, the productive engagement patterns derived in step 3 was entered. Finally, in Model 3, both demographic variables and productive engagement patterns were added. This hierarchical process provided useful insights to help exclude the interfering factors of the individuals themselves, focusing on the effects of productive engagement patterns on life satisfaction of ESG and their gender differences. We tested for covariance issues and the final VIF showed no covariance issues. Referring to past studies, we considered p-values < 0.1 as statistically significant.

Regression results

Table 5 presents the results of logistic regression to examine the impact of productive engagement patterns on life satisfaction among ESG. Three models were designed, each encompassing socio-demographic variables, productive engagement pattern variables, and all variables, respectively. Additionally, to provide a more intuitive reflection of the role of productive engagement patterns, the Family Caring-Spiritual growth type was taken as the reference class.

Model 1 includes only variables related to respondents’ socio-demographic characteristics to examine their association with life satisfaction. The study shows that self-rated health and financial adequacy have a significant positive impact on ESG life satisfaction, meaning that the higher the self-rated health and the more financially adequate the ESG, the higher their life satisfaction. Other variables, such as gender, age, and marital status, do not have a significant impact on ESG life satisfaction.

Model2 inputs only variables of the four classes of productive engagement to estimate correlations between productive engagement patterns and life satisfaction among ESG. The results indicate that different patterns of productive engagement have varying effects on the life satisfaction among ESG. Specifically, compared to the Family Caring-Spiritual growth,class the other three classes all reported lower life satisfaction among ESG, including Active engagement except for working, Working-Parent caring, and the Family Caring class. Particularly, the Working-Parent caring has a significant impact.

Model 3 simultaneously introduces all variables from the first and second groups to measure their effects on life satisfaction. Clearly, there are some variations in the same variables across different models. Overall, the findings indicate that compared to the Family Caring-Spiritual growth class, both the Active engagement except for working class (β = -0.14, t = -2.57, p < 0.05) and the Family Caring-Spiritual growth class (β = -0.13, t = -2.57, p = 0.05) have a significant negative effect on the life satisfaction of ESG individuals. This implies that, when controlling for socio-demographic variables, the life satisfaction of ESG in the Family Caring-Spiritual growth class is higher than those of the Active engagement except for working class and the Working-Parent caring class. Besides, among the controlled variables, only self-rated health and financial adequacy are significantly correlated with life satisfaction. These results are generally similar to those of Model 1.

Discussion

In the trend of increasing life expectancy and delayed childbirth, ESG are becoming more prevalent as `specialized’ older adults.Therefore, it is necessary to pay attention to their multifaceted productive engagement in the aging process as well as their associated psychological well-being. Taking the perspective of productive aging, we selected four types of activities—seven items, and employed LCA to identify the patterns of productive engagement among ESG in Shanghai, and further explored the association between these patterns and ESG life satisfaction.

Firstly, we revealed four patterns of productive engagement among ESG, namely Family Caring, Family Caring-Spiritual growth, Active engagement except for working, and Working-Parent caring. It was found that all four classes of ESG provided care for their parents, regardless of their engagement in other activities, which is consistent with previous findings1,62. Further, ESG of different classes exhibited different socio-demographic characteristics.

Profiles of 4 -pattern ESG

LCA results portray four patterns that map onto stratified life courses among ESG in Shanghai. The modal “Active engagement except for working” cluster is overwhelmingly female, highly educated, and reports above-average self-rated health and economic security. Drawing on previous findings63–65, we argue that post-retirement financial adequacy lowers the instrumental value of paid employment for these women63,64. Also, due to the earlier retirement age for Chinese women (50–55 years old) and cultural expectation that women prioritize family well-being operate as a gendered timetable that channels them into care-oriented but non-wage activities71–74 Endowed with higher education, these women transform the traditional care role into a repertoire of productive engagements, including family caring, volunteering, and lifelong learning, which simultaneously conform to and subtly expand the feminine script.

The second-largest “Family Caring-Spiritual growth” class exhibits similar health and income advantages, yet its educational ceiling is relatively lower (secondary) and co-residence with a spouse is the highest across classes. Consistent with previous75 finding that marital partnership expands activity repertoires by buffering domestic demands, these ESG are able to pursue self-development without compromising their family responsibilities66,67.

In contrast, the “Family Caring” cluster, disproportionately female ESG with incomplete secondary education and poorer health and financial status, remains tethered to the household. The limited pension replacement and scarce subsidised daycare services78structurally exclude alternatives, reinforcing traditional gender expectations68–71.

Finally, the smallest “Working-Parent caring” profile is dominated by younger, healthier male ESG. Most of them have not yet retired from their jobs, and their parents are in need of care. In China, males tend to retire later than females. And the delaying retirement simultaneously maximises pension entitlements83 and finances eldercare expenses73, illustrating how labour market institutions and filial obligation converge to shape late-life trajectories of male73–75.

Productive engagement patterns on life satisfaction

We found that different classes of ESG exhibited differential life satisfaction. Specifically, ESG in the "Family Caring-Spiritual growth" reported higher life satisfaction compared to those in the "Active engagement except for working" and the "Working-Parent caring." Drawing on the life-course perspective76 and stress process frameworks77–81, we interpret the four classes as distinct constellations of role demands, personal resources, and coping repertoires that jointly shape psychological well-being in later life. A discussion of two obvious effects follows.

By comparing “Family Caring-Spiritual Growth” with “Active Engagement Except for Working”, a double-edged sword effect of social engagement can be observed.These two groups invested similar efforts in family care and both rarely engaged in paid work. However, the latter group, which took on additional responsibilities like volunteer work and learning, reported lower life satisfaction. This underscores the principle that role accumulation is beneficial only when it does not exceed an carrying capacity82,83. Also, consistent with the stress-process emphasis on role overload, the result suggests that older adults who juggle multiple non-economic roles often experience time scarcity and emotional fatigue84,85. A curvilinear relationship between volunteer intensity and depressive symptoms has been documented in Japan86, lending external validity to our interpretation.

The second key contrast involves the “Family Caring-Spiritual growth” class and the “Working-Parents caring” class, both of which provide parental care but differ in the presence (or absence) of paid work and grandchild care. The higher life satisfaction of the former group is consistent with cultural gerontology research that frames grandchild care, lifelong learning and travelling as generative and identity-affirming activities in Chinese society87,88. These roles are embedded in filial piety norms that imbue caregiving with positive meaning and social recognition 89–91. Conversely, remaining employed while caring for aging parents exposes ESG to intersectionality of competing productive activities, especially when age-friendly employment policies are underdeveloped92–95. Our results echo prior findings that older employees who provide family care report higher work-to-family conflict and lower perceived organizational support, both robust predictors of diminished well-being96,97. From an selective optimization with compensation perspective, paid work may be maintained at the cost of optimizing other life domains, thereby eroding the psychological gains typically associated with productive aging98,99.

Policy implications

Firstly, governments should expand time-banking and respite-care schemes that allow grandparent caregivers to pursue self-development without sacrificing family obligations. Secondly, employers hiring or retaining older workers must provide flexible scheduling, caregiver leave, and phased retirement to prevent the documented work-family conflict. Thirdly, community centers might adopt stepped volunteering programs that begin with modest hours and gradually increase commitment, thereby avoiding the overload risk observed in the “Active engagement except for working” class.

Limitations and future research

The first is the issue of causal inference. The cross-sectional nature of our data precluded disentangling whether role configurations shape well-being or whether ESG with higher well-being deliberately opt for less demanding combinations. Longitudinal designs that follow individuals as they transition between latent classes are required to clarify the underlying causal pathways. The second is the trade-off between breadth and depth of measurement. Our indicators captured only engagement in activities but not their intensity, duration, or subjective salience. Within any latent class, small variations in weekly hours or perceived emotional salience may still condition the risk of role overload, effects that remain invisible in the present analysis.

Conclusion

Our study identified four patterns of productive engagement among ESG in Shanghai and its impact on life satisfaction. This suggests that the psychological payoffs of productive engagement among ESG are contingent on how paid work, family care, volunteering, and self-development are bundled rather than on any single activity. Consistent with the life course stress process model, configurations that exceed an individual’s time, energy, or normative resources generate role overload and erode well-being. In contrast, culturally endorsed resources that balance roles enhance well-being. In this process, we move beyond the variable-centric debate of “more is better” or “more is worse” to a person-centered understanding of how role constellations shape psychological outcomes in later life. Moderate, culturally meaningful participation appears to be most ideal. This insight may provide direction for future longitudinal studies examining roleenhancement versus role strain in rapidly aging societies.
