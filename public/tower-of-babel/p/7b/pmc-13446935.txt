Prognosis of Jejunoileal Atresia Treated by Resection and Anastomosis in the Resource-limited Environment
Authors: Salihou Aminou Sadjo, Fossi Kamga Gacelle, Abouamé Palma Haoua, Fadi Salihou, Dikongué Dikongué Eugenie Caroline, Mouafo Tambo Faustin Félicien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Jejunoileal atresia is the most common etiology of intestinal mechanical obstruction. Several surgical techniques have been described in the therapeutic approach to this condition. Treatment by resection and anastomosis with or without gastrointestinal tapering remains a life-saving option in our daily practice. The aim of our study was to report the results of the surgical treatment of jejunoileal atresia by resection and anastomosis in the resource-limited environment.Materials and Methods:We carried out a prospective, descriptive study from November 1st 2021, to October 31st, 2023, equating to 2 years, in the Pediatric Surgery Department. All the records of newborns aged from day 0 to day 21 with jejunoileal atresia, only treated by resection and anastomosis were included. The parameters studied were age at presentation, sex, gestational age, type of atresia, associated malformations, morbidity, mortality, and follow-up.Results:A total of 10 newborns were treated. The median age at the presentation was 2.2 days (1 to 4 days). The male sex was predominant with a sex ratio of 7:3. Prematurity was reported in two cases. No antenatal diagnosis was reported. The type I atresia according to the Martin classification was most commonly found (5 cases). Inguinoscrotal hernia and Ladd bands were associated in 1 case each. Resection and anastomosis with intestinal tapering was performed in all newborns. Surgical site infection was noted in 1 case. We recorded three deaths due to sepsis in our series.Conclusions:In spite of the limitations inherent to low- and middle-income countries region, our current series shows that it is feasible to achieve the acceptable surgical outcomes in neonatal intestinal obstruction due to intestinal atresia. Rigorous adherence to protocols and improvements in the availability of parenteral nutrition are likely to contribute to optimal outcomes in future.

INTRODUCTION

Jejunoileal atresia represents the most common etiology in neonatal intestinal mechanical obstruction. Several surgical techniques have been described in the therapeutic approach to this condition. Treatment by resection and anastomosis with or without intestinal tapering remains a life-saving option in our daily practice.[1] The aim of our study was to report the results of surgical treatment of jejunoileal atresia by resection and anastomosis in the resource-limited environment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We conducted a prospective and descriptive case series from the November 1st, 2021, to the October 31st, 2023, representing a 2-year period, in the pediatric surgery department. We included all newborns between day 0 and day 21 of life with jejunoileal atresia, only treated by resection-anastomosis with tapering of the proximal part of the distal atretic segment (adjusting the opening by approximately 1 cm of distal atretic segment to optimize the connection and shape allowing normal passage). The parameters studied were age at presentation, sex, term, clinical presentation, location of atresia, type, associated malformations, morbidity, mortality, and follow-up. All our patients were classified according to the Martin classification.[2] Surviving was allowed to return home at 3000 g they were systematically seen for a follow-up consultation after 1 and 6 months of discharge. Approval was obtained from the parents and the institutional ethics committee for health research. Data were coded and entered using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Data were summarized using median, minimum, and maximum in quantitative data and using frequency (count) and relative frequency (percentage) for the categorical data.

RESULTS

A total of 10 newborns were treated. The median age at presentation was 2.2 days (1 and 4 days). The predominance was male with a sex ratio of 7:3. Prematurity was reported in three cases. None of our newborns had undergone antenatal diagnosis. The median weight at admission was 2650 g (range 800 and 3800 g). The premature baby weighing 800 g was a male with associated low birth weight. He was presented to us on the fourth day of hospitalization with greenish vomiting and a weight loss of 240 g, whereas his birth weight was 1040 g. Surgical intervention was performed within 24 h. He developed sepsis on the postoperative day 4, which led to his death. The clinical picture was dominated by greenish vomiting noted in six cases. Abdominal distension and delayed meconium passage were noted in 1 case each. Ileal atresia was most commonly encountered in six cases. On the basis of the Martin classification, type I was the most common form in five cases [Figure 1], types II and III in 2 cases each, and type IV in 1 case. Inguino-scrotal hernia and LADD bands were the associated malformations in 1 case each. Resection and anastomosis with digestive remodeling was performed in all newborns [Figures 2 and 3]. Surgical site infection was noted in 1 case. The average time before resuming feeding was 5.5 days. We recorded three deaths due to sepsis in our series. DISCUSSION

The annual frequency of jejunoileal atresia in our series is 3.3 cases per year. It is the third most common cause of neonatal intestinal obstruction in our department after Hirschsprung’s disease and anorectal malformation.[3] The median age at the presentation was 2.2 days (range 1 to 4 days). This delay in consultation reported in our series is comparable to the results of Ekwunife et al. in Nigeria,[4] but far lower than that of Boume et al.[5] in Togo which noted 10 days. It could be explained by the lack of awareness of this clinical entity by some practitioners, more specifically midwives and neonatology nurses, who in the absence of an adequate number of pediatricians, are most often at the forefront of welcoming these newborns. The male predominance observed in our series has been reported by several authors without a specific explanation.[45] Despite the prenatal consultations found in all our patients, none of them had been diagnosed in the antenatal period. This observation is very often reported in African series.[6] Imran et al.[7] in Pakistan also found little prenatal diagnosis in his series which was 3.2%, in contradiction with Western series where prenatal diagnosis of intestinal atresia is made in more than 86.6% of cases.[8] Virgone et al. in England reported prenatal diagnosis in 56.6% of cases.[9] Shakya et al. in Nepal had demonstrated in their series that the absence of prenatal diagnosis was significantly correlated with mortality in these children.[10] Prematurity was recorded in three cases and this prematurity reported by other authors has no correlation with the occurrence of digestive atresia in the newborn but constitutes a significant mortality factor.[45] The median birth weight was 2650 g (800 and 3800 g) in our series. This result is higher than that of du Preez et al. in South Africa which found 2200 g (980 and 3600 g).[11] The correlation between low birth weight in jejunoileal atresia and death has already been reported by several authors.[1213] For Jarkman and Salö in Sweden, this low birth weight is also correlated with a long postoperative hospital stay.[14] The clinical presentation was dominated by bilious vomiting in seven newborns. This early onset of vomiting reflects a high obstruction and constitutes a warning symptom for practitioners and families. On the contrary, Basak in India found cessation of flatus and stool passage as the initial symptom, followed by abdominal distension and bilious vomiting, in respectively 88.9%, 77.8%, and 22.1% of cases.[15] In our study, preoperative sepsis, inguinal hernia, and intestinal malrotation were associated with jejunoileal atresia in 1 case each. Boume et al. in Togo reported four cases of nonrotation.[5] Shakya et al. reported nonrotation in two cases and digestive duplication in one case.[10] The presence of other congenital malformations associated with jejunoileal atresia complicates the course of surgery, thus making the vital prognosis appalling. du Preez et al. in South Africa also demonstrated that sepsis and prematurity are the major risk factors for mortality.[11] The location of the atresia was the ileum in six cases and the jejunum in four cases. This result corroborates those of Shakya et al. in Nepal and Tarek and Abd Elraheem in Egypt who also found the predominance of the ileal location in 60.1% of cases and 61.5% of cases, respectively.[1016] On the other hand, other authors such as Salih et al. had reported a jejunal predominance.[12] Singh et al. in India had noted in his series that the location of the atresia was distributed equally between the jejunum and the ileum.[17] For Shakya et al., the more distal the location, the higher the chances of survival and most authors share this opinion.[10] The predominance of the type I in our series is in conformity with the results of Salih et al. in Iraq and Ekwunife et al. in Nigeria.[412] On the contrary, Singh et al. and Shakya et al. most commonly found type IIIa in their study.[1017] Type II was predominant instead in the series of Salih et al.[12] As for types IIIb and IV which are considered rare and have a poor prognosis, they are often found in the second or third place for many authors.[418] In the treatment of jejunoileal atresia, the approach can be classic or laparoscopic. None of our patients was treated laparoscopically unlike in the series of Rieth et al. in Hungary.[19] Several surgical techniques have been described in the treatment of jejunoileal atresia including resection-anastomosis, the Bishop Koop, Santulli and Mikulicz techniques.[71517] According to the literature, the most commonly used technique is resection-anastomosis, which was performed in all our patients.[2021] The choice of the technique depends on the experience of the teams, the technical platform, and also the state of the intestinal loops. Rieth et al. equally carried out digestive resection and anastomosis in all his patients. For the latter, colostomy was associated only in the case of digestive perforation.[19] The Bishop Koop technique is the safest and was mostly performed in the series of Shakya et al. and Muhammad et al.[1022] On the contrary, Basak et al. found that this technique was a source of anastomotic leakage and sepsis in their series.[15] In his study, the Mikulicz technique was preferred and carried less complications and redo surgeries.[15] The complications were marked by sepsis in all our deceased patients. Some authors noted in their study that postoperative sepsis was caused by anastomotic leakage.[17] Parenteral nutrition and neonatal resuscitation, which have greatly improved the vital prognosis of these patients in developed countries, are not available in our context.[23] All our newborns were systematically put on Dextrose and Ringer Lactate through peripheral venous lines at N/4 concentration while waiting for the resumption of feeding. The unavailability of parenteral nutrition has also been reported by Goodluck et al. in Tanzania and Osuchukwu and Rentea in Nigeria.[2425] The median time before the resumption of oral feeding was 5.5 days (range 5–6 days). This time interval is similar to that of Shakya et al.[10] This early resumption of feeding reduces the risk of postoperative infection, and this has been demonstrated.[152425] The recorded deaths in three cases attributed to the sepsis occurred in the postoperative period on the 1st, 3rd, and 6th day. This mortality is not specific to our series and has been found in all studies, but in varying proportions.[6817] Muhammad et al. recorded a death rate of 36.5% of his series.[22] This poor prognosis recognized for this condition in most series from developing countries is multifactorial and most authors are unanimous on this take.

The limitations of our study were its small sample size which does not allow to establish a cause-and-effect relationship between the complications and deaths. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution.

CONCLUSIONS

In spite of the limitations inherent to a low- and middle-income countries region, our current series shows that it is feasible to achieve the acceptable surgical outcomes in neonatal bowel obstruction due to bowel atresia. Rigorous adherence to protocols and improvements in the availability of parenteral nutrition are likely to contribute to the optimal outcomes in future.
