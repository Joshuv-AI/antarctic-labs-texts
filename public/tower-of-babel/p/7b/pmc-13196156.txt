Commentary on “Intercellular TIMP-1-CD63 signaling directs the evolution of immune escape and metastasis in KRAS-mutated pancreatic cancer cells”
Authors: Biao Zhang, Dong Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We recently carefully read the article titled “Intercellular TIMP-1-CD63 signaling directs the evolution of immune escape and metastasis in KRAS-mutated pancreatic cancer cells” published by Chu-An Wang et al. in Molecular Cancer. While this study provides valuable insights into tumor-immune crosstalk, we raise two points for clarification to enhance its precision. First, the manuscript classifies T2N0M0 samples as stage II pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), which conflicts with the eighth edition of the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual that designates T2N0M0 as stage IB. Second, the authors used pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs) as a surrogate for fibroblasts to model stromal effects, but accumulating evidence indicates that PSCs and fibroblasts are not equivalent. To address this, we integrated single-cell, spatial, and bulk transcriptomic data from multiple cohorts. Our analyses revealed substantial differences between fibroblasts and stellate cells in abundance (fibroblasts enriched in primary pancreatic cancer tumor tissues), prognostic relevance (high fibroblasts associated with poorer survival, high stellate cells with better prognosis), spatial distribution (fibroblasts localized around malignant tumor cells), and intercellular communication (fibroblasts as stronger signal senders to malignant tumor cells). These findings confirm that PSCs cannot accurately represent fibroblasts in PDAC. We emphasize that clarifying these points will not undermine the study’s significance but will strengthen its rigor and comparability. Wang et al.’s work remains a valuable contribution to understanding PDAC progression, and we anticipate these clarifications will further advance stromal-immune crosstalk research in PDAC.

Main body

Dear editor, we read with great interest the article entitled “Intercellular TIMP-1-CD63 signaling directs the evolution of immune escape and metastasis in KRAS-mutated pancreatic cancer cells” published by Chu-An Wang et al. in Molecular Cancer [1]. This study presents a comprehensive and elegant investigation into the role of TIMP-1-CD63-mediated intercellular communication in shaping immune evasion and metastatic behavior in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). By integrating single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) with mechanistic in vitro and in vivo analyses, the authors provide important insights into how tumor-stroma-immune interactions contribute to pancreatic cancer progression.

We would like to particularly commend the authors for their single-cell transcriptomic analysis comparing early and advanced PDAC, which revealed a relative enrichment of macrophages and fibroblast populations in advanced-stage tumors. These findings are highly consistent with the desmoplastic and immunosuppressive nature of PDAC and strongly support the biological relevance of the tumor microenvironment in driving disease aggressiveness.

However, we would like to raise two points for clarification that may further strengthen the interpretation and precision of the study.

First, regarding tumor staging in the single-cell analysis cohort, the manuscript refers to the early-stage samples staged as T2N0M0 as stage II PDAC in the section entitled “Tissue collection, single-cell or bulk RNA sequencing, and digital spatial profiling (DSP)” within the “Materials and Methods” part. According to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Cancer Staging Manual, T2N0M0 PDAC is classified as stage IB rather than stage II [2]. While this discrepancy is unlikely to affect the overall conclusions, clarification of the staging criteria used would be helpful to ensure consistency with current AJCC standards and to facilitate comparison with other contemporary studies.

Second, we noted that the single-cell analysis identified fibroblasts and macrophages as cell populations enriched in advanced PDAC. To functionally model the influence of these stromal and immune components, the authors treated pancreatic cancer cells with conditioned medium derived from pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs), monocytes, and macrophages, and subsequently examined ERK activity and DUSP2 expression. It was found that the conditioned medium of macrophages and the co-culture of monocytes with pancreatic cancer cells, rather than the conditioned medium of PSCs, could lead to an increase in ERK activity and a decrease in DUSP2 levels of pancreatic cancer cells. It was recommended to directly co-culture PSCs with pancreatic cancer cells (similar to the direct co-culture experiment of monocytes and pancreatic cancer cells) to assess the potential effects on ERK activity and DUSP2 levels. Studies have shown that direct co-culture of pancreatic cancer cells and PSCs can lead to changes in the phenotype and function of pancreatic cancer cells, and at the same time, the direct co-culture of these two types of cells may also induce the transformation of stellate cells into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) [3, 4]. Indeed, direct co-culture of PSCs with pancreatic cancer cells can more authentically recapitulate the intercellular crosstalk within the pancreatic cancer microenvironment, in comparison with co-culture using conditioned medium from PSCs. While PSCs are well recognized as a major cellular source contributing to fibrosis in PDAC and are often considered precursors of fibroblasts, they are not entirely equivalent to fibroblasts as defined by single-cell studies. Accumulating evidence indicates that fibroblasts comprise heterogeneous subpopulations with distinct transcriptional programs and functional roles, some of which may not originate directly from PSCs [5, 6]. Helms et al. [7] reported that PSCs produce only a small proportion of CAFs in PDAC, while the remaining CAFs may originate from mesothelial cells, pancreas-resident fibroblast populations, and bone marrow. A study by Huang et al. [8] demonstrated that in PDAC, oxidative stress within the tumor microenvironment can induce the transdifferentiation of monocytes into myofibroblasts. Miyazaki et al. [9] showed that adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells can be induced to differentiate into CAFs when co-cultured with pancreatic cancer cell lines. In this context, using PSCs as a surrogate for fibroblasts in in vitro experiments to illustrate the influence of fibroblasts on pancreatic cancer cells may not be reliable.

To further clarify the distinction between fibroblasts and stellate cells in the PDAC microenvironment, we integrated single-cell, spatial, and bulk transcriptomic data from multiple independent cohorts for comprehensive analysis. Specifically, we obtained single-cell transcriptomic data from 24 pancreatic cancer tumor samples and 11 normal pancreatic samples by downloading the CRA001160 dataset from the GSA database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa/), single-cell transcriptomic data from 5 pancreatic cancer liver metastasis samples by downloading the GSE158356 dataset from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), spatial transcriptomic data from 7 pancreatic cancer tumor samples by downloading the GSE235315 dataset from GEO, and bulk transcriptomic and clinical survival data from 178 pancreatic cancer samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov).

Quality control of scRNA-seq was performed using the following criteria: each gene is expressed in at least three cells, the total RNA count in each cell is greater than 1000, and the proportion of mitochondrial genes is less than 20%. The “DoubletFinder” package was used to detect and exclude double cells. The “NormalizeData” function was employed to standardize scRNA-seq data, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were screened using the “FindVariableFeatures” function. After principal component analysis (PCA) utilizing the first 2000 hypervariable genes, the “Harmony” function was used to eliminate batch effects among samples. The “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions were applied to assess cell similarity and cluster cells, respectively. Dimensionality reduction and visualization were further performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cell subpopulations were annotated based on the expression of marker genes in each cluster [10, 11]. To jointly analyze single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data, deconvolution analysis was performed using the “BayesPrism” package [12]. The inferCNV was used to infer copy number variations (CNV) in epithelial cells to distinguish malignant cells from normal ductal epithelial cells. The Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) algorithm was utilized to deconvolve spatial transcriptomes using scRNA-seq data to generate cell-type-specific gene expression profiles. The CellChat package (version 2.1.2) was employed to systematically analyze intercellular communication networks within the pancreatic cancer microenvironment [13].

Integration of 11 normal pancreatic samples, 24 pancreatic cancer tumor samples, and 5 pancreatic cancer liver metastasis samples yielded transcriptomic data from a total of 106,007 cells. After quality control, dimensionality reduction, and clustering, a total of 10 cell subpopulations were initially annotated, including epithelial cells, endothelial cells, myeloid cells, stellate cells, fibroblasts, T cells, B cells, acinar cells, endocrine cells, and plasma cells (Fig. 1A). The expression and distribution of marker genes for each cell subpopulation in the UMAP plot are shown in Fig. 1B. Overall, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, and stellate cells were the top five most abundant cell subpopulations, accounting for 47.9%, 11.8%, 8.7%, 8.4%, and 7.4% of total cells, respectively (Fig. 1C). Nevertheless, the proportion of cell subpopulations varied significantly across samples, with T cells and myeloid cells showing relatively higher abundance in pancreatic cancer liver metastasis tissues (Fig. 1D). Compared with normal pancreatic tissues and metastatic tissues, fibroblasts were significantly more abundant in primary pancreatic cancer tissues (Fig. 1E). Based on the cell subpopulations identified by single-cell analysis, we used the BayesPrism deconvolution algorithm to infer cell type composition from bulk RNA-seq data in the TCGA database. Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves showed that pancreatic cancer patients with high fibroblast levels had shorter overall survival than those with low fibroblast levels, with a difference approaching statistical significance (Fig. 1F). In contrast, high stellate cell levels were significantly associated with better prognosis in pancreatic cancer patients (Fig. 1G). To further distinguish malignant cells from normal ductal epithelial cells, epithelial cells were isolated and re-clustered. The UMAP plot showed that epithelial cells were further subdivided into cluster 0 and cluster 1 (Fig. 1H). We used inferCNV to infer CNV in cluster 0, cluster 1, and various non-epithelial cell subpopulations. The CNV score of cluster 1 was remarkably higher than that of cluster 0 and all non-epithelial cell subpopulations (Figs. 1I-J). In addition, classic marker genes of ductal cells were significantly highly expressed in cluster 0 (Fig. 1K). Based on these lines of evidence, cluster 0 was annotated as ductal epithelial cells, and cluster 1 was annotated as malignant cells (Fig. 1L). Intercellular communication analysis at the single-cell transcriptomic level showed that fibroblasts were the strongest signal senders to malignant tumor cells, far exceeding stellate cells (Fig. 1M). Subsequently, we integrated single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data using the RCTD method to localize the spatial distribution of different cell subpopulations. The results showed that fibroblasts were largely distributed around malignant tumor cells (Fig. 1N). Furthermore, intercellular communication analysis at the spatial transcriptomic level also revealed the strongest interaction between fibroblasts and malignant tumor cells. While the association between stellate cells and malignant tumor cells was very weak (Fig. 1O). Collectively, fibroblasts and stellate cells exhibit substantial differences in the pancreatic cancer microenvironment. Therefore, employing PSCs as a proxy for fibroblasts to model fibroblast-tumor interactions may not accurately reflect fibroblast-specific effects on pancreatic cancer cells.

Despite these points, we believe that this study represents an important and timely contribution to the field of pancreatic cancer research. The authors convincingly demonstrate how TIMP-1-CD63 signaling integrates oncogenic KRAS signaling with immune modulation and metastatic potential, offering novel mechanistic insights and potential therapeutic implications. We sincerely appreciate the depth and rigor of this work and anticipate that it will stimulate further investigation into stromal-immune crosstalk in PDAC.
