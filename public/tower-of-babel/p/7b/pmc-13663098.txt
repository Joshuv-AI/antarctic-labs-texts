Adherence and continuance intention of biofeedback-assisted pelvic floor muscle training: a theory-driven study among women with pelvic floor disorders
Authors: Ying-Hsuan Chao, Chia-Jung Chiang, Chung-Hung Tsai, Ya-Min Cheng, Li-Chieh Kuo, Fong-Chin Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdherence to pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) remains a major challenge in the conservative management of pelvic floor disorders in women. Although biofeedback-assisted PFMT facilitates motor learning, sustained engagement may be influenced by a range of behavioral and experiential factors. This study investigated clinical and behavioral determinants of engagement with PFMT technologies and examined changes in manometry-derived pelvic floor muscle performance during a supervised biofeedback-assisted PFMT.MethodsThis prospective observational study examined outpatient care for women with pelvic floor disorder. Participants completed six PFMT sessions with manometer-based biofeedback. Based on baseline Pelvic Floor Distress Inventory-20 (PFDI-20) scores, participants were stratified into low- and high-symptom groups. Changes in muscle performance were evaluated using repeated-measures analysis of variance. Adherence determinants were examined using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM).ResultsParticipants with lower baseline symptoms showed higher program adherence. Significant improvements in contraction and peak pressure occurred over time (p < .01), with comparable benefits across symptom groups. Perceived usefulness emerged as the strongest predictor of sustained engagement, followed by self-efficacy, confirmation, and early engagement. Satisfaction and perceived ease of use did not directly predict continuance. Privacy and comfort concerns negatively influenced satisfaction, indirectly reducing continuance intention. Higher baseline quality of life impairment correlated with lower adherence.ConclusionsBiofeedback-assisted PFMT enhances pelvic floor muscle performance across varying symptom severities; however, sustained engagement is primarily driven by perceived functional benefit rather than satisfaction alone. These findings highlight the need to integrate behavioral, experiential, and functional considerations in PFMT interventions.Trial registrationThe Institutional Review Board of National Cheng Kung University Hospital, Tainan, Taiwan, thoroughly reviewed and approved the procedures and consent form (Reference Number AER112385 Registration number NCT07243028 at ClinicalTrials.gov).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04711-9.

Background

Pelvic floor muscle dysfunction is a broader term referring to impaired coordination and function of the pelvic floor muscles, which regulate bladder, bowel, and sexual organ function. Dysfunction may result in sensory disturbances and voiding abnormalities of the lower urinary tract, sexual dysfunction, and chronic pelvic pain syndromes, impacting daily functioning and well-being [1]. Epidemiological evidence indicates that approximately one-quarter of women experience at least one type of pelvic floor disorder (PFD) [2]. Among the various forms of PFDs, urinary incontinence is most prevalent, affecting an estimated 15–17% of women, followed by fecal incontinence, which occurs in approximately 9% of adult women. The prevalence of pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is more difficult to ascertain, with reported estimates ranging from 3% to 8% [2]. Beyond their prevalence, PFDs impose a substantial burden on women’s lives, as these conditions can significantly impair physical functioning, restrict social participation, and diminish overall quality of life [3].

Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) is widely recommended as a first-line, conservative intervention for women with pelvic floor disorders (PFDs) and has been shown to improve symptoms and quality of life across a range of pelvic floor conditions [4, 5], with evidence supporting its effectiveness in symptom reduction and functional improvement [6, 7]. However, clinical success of PFMT depends not only on its physiological efficacy but also on patients’ ability to perform the exercises correctly and to maintain consistent practice over time. Despite professional instruction, more than 30% of women are unable to correctly contract their pelvic floor muscles at initial assessment, often recruiting compensatory muscles or demonstrating inadequate technique [8, 9]. More critically, long-term adherence to PFMT remains suboptimal [10]. In routine clinical care, many women discontinue or inconsistently engage in PFMT programs, limiting therapeutic benefit. Behavioral barriers, including uncertainty regarding exercise technique, absence of real-time feedback, and declining motivation, have been identified as primary contributors to poor adherence, frequently outweighing physiological limitations [11, 12]. These challenges highlight the need for supportive tools and interventions that enhance patient engagement and execution fidelity in PFMT [13].

Biofeedback-assisted PFMT devices address these barriers by providing real-time visual and auditory feedback on pelvic floor muscle contractions [12]. Manometer-based biofeedback systems, such as perineometers, enable patients to observe muscle activation and receive immediate reinforcement during training [14]. Beyond their measurement function, these devices aim to enhance self-efficacy, perceived usefulness, and engagement with PFMT [15]. However, because these systems require intravaginal probe placement, concerns related to comfort, privacy, and perceived intrusiveness may arise and potentially influence patients’ willingness to adopt and continue using the technology [16, 17]. Despite the demonstrated efficacy of biofeedback-assisted PFMT, quantitative research examining the factors that drive adoption and sustained use of these devices remains limited. Existing studies have predominantly focused on treatment effectiveness or have explored adherence-related experiences through qualitative methodologies, including interviews and focus groups [17]. Consequently, structured, theory-driven investigations of technology acceptance and adherence behaviors among women with pelvic floor disorders remain scarce.

Given the barriers to initiating and sustaining biofeedback-assisted PFMT, understanding the determinants of patient acceptance and long-term engagement is essential. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) provide well-established frameworks for understanding technology adoption in healthcare contexts [18]. These models posit that behavioral intention is shaped by key cognitive constructs, including perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, self-efficacy, satisfaction, and confirmation of expectations. In clinical populations, these cognitive determinants are further moderated by contextual and individual factors, such as perceived symptom severity, health-related quality of life, and concerns related to privacy or physical intrusiveness [19, 20].

To address this gap, the present study aimed to investigate the cognitive and clinical determinants of technology acceptance, continuance intention, and adherence in the context of PFMT. Specifically, constructs derived from TAM and UTAUT were examined in relation to patients’ intention to continue using a manometry-based PFMT biofeedback device. In addition, associations between pelvic floor symptom burden, quality-of-life impact, and adherence to PFMT were explored. By integrating behavioral intention with symptom severity and contextual factors, this study seeks to inform strategies for promoting sustained engagement with PFMT technologies in routine clinical care.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a single-arm, prospective, longitudinal design integrated into routine outpatient care for pelvic floor disorder at National Cheng Kung University Hospital, Tainan, Taiwan. Eligible patients were enrolled between February 2024 and January 2026 and were followed throughout a standardized six-session pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) program incorporating biofeedback. The study protocol received Institutional Review Board approval from National Cheng Kung University Hospital, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07243028) in September 2025. This study was conducted as part of a broader registered research program evaluating multiple biofeedback modalities for PFMT, including transabdominal ultrasound, surface electromyography, and vaginal manometry. In addition to physiological assessments, the registered study included patient-centered outcomes related to technology acceptance, adherence, and willingness to continue using PFMT-related technologies. The present manuscript focuses on adherence, continuance intention, technology acceptance, and manometry-derived training outcomes.

Participants and recruitment

Women aged ≥ 21 years with a clinician-diagnosed pelvic floor disorder (e.g., urinary incontinence or pelvic organ prolapse) and a referral for PFMT from an obstetrician or gynecologist were invited to participate. Pelvic organ prolapse severity was assessed by the attending urogynecologist using the Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP-Q) system and classified into Stages I-IV according to standardized criteria. The exclusion criteria were: (1) cognitive impairment hindering informed consent or the accurate completion of questionnaires; and (2) neurologic or neuromuscular conditions that would preclude safe participation in PFMT. Following physician referral, the intervention was delivered by trained physical therapists and nurses. Because the study was conducted as an exploratory investigation within a routine clinical care setting, consecutive eligible patients were recruited during the study period.

Baseline assessments and grouping

During the initial enrollment (Session 1, pre-training), participants completed the 20-item Pelvic Floor Distress Inventory (PFDI-20) and the 7-item Incontinence Impact Questionnaire (IIQ-7) to characterize symptom burden and health-related quality of life, respectively. To facilitate a comparative analysis of adherence, participants were stratified into low- and high-symptom-burden groups based on a median split of the baseline PFDI-20 total scores (Fig. 1).

Intervention: PFMT with biofeedback

Prior to the initiation of training, participants received individualized instruction on correct pelvic floor muscle contraction from a pelvic floor physical therapist. Verbal cues and real-time visual feedback from the manometry system were used to facilitate motor learning and assist participants in performing voluntary pelvic floor muscle contractions. Participants were instructed to avoid compensatory strategies, including breath-holding, Valsalva maneuvers, and excessive activation of accessory muscles during contraction. Participants underwent six supervised PFMT sessions using a perineometric pressure sensor with a clinical biofeedback platform (Solar Uro system, MMS/Laborie; pressure in cmH₂O). An intravaginal pressure probe captured pelvic floor contractions, providing real-time feedback to guide performance. Each session followed a protocol of 5-second contractions and 15-second relaxations over 15 min, with data sampled at 10 Hz.

The training protocol was based on the standardized program incorporated within the Solar Uro biofeedback system and reflected routine clinical practice in our pelvic floor rehabilitation service. Each session consisted of repeated cycles of 5-second pelvic floor muscle contractions followed by 15-second relaxation periods over a 15-minute training duration. Supervised sessions were typically scheduled once weekly, depending on clinical availability and scheduling needs. Participants were encouraged to continue pelvic floor muscle exercises at home between supervised sessions. At each visit, clinicians reviewed participants’ experiences with home practice and addressed any difficulties encountered. However, the frequency and adherence to home-based exercises were not formally recorded or analyzed as part of the study.

To quantify physiological changes, intravaginal pressure was measured using a manometer-based probe during Sessions 1 and 6. The first minute of each recording was excluded to minimize signal instability. Analysis focused on a segment with 10 contraction cycles. Two primary outcomes were derived: (1) mean contraction pressure, the average of all valid peak pressures, and (2) maximum contraction pressure, the highest observed peak pressure. These metrics represent overall contractile output and maximal voluntary contraction. Improvement was assessed by calculating the pressure differential between Session 6 and Session 1 for each participant. For participants who did not complete all six sessions, the last available manometry assessment was used as the post-training measurement.

Questionnaire of intention to use the assistive tool

Perceived acceptance and intention to continue using the manometry-based PFMT biofeedback system were assessed using a theory-driven questionnaire. The questionnaire was specifically developed to evaluate patients’ perceptions, acceptance, and willingness to continue using the manometry-based PFMT biofeedback device. The instrument was developed by integrating constructs derived from the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), expectation–confirmation theory, self-efficacy theory, and involvement theory, and was adapted for the context of manometry-based pelvic floor rehabilitation technologies.

The questionnaire was developed through a systematic process. First, a comprehensive review of empirical studies on PFMT, rehabilitation technologies, and technology acceptance was conducted. Measurement items related to technology acceptance, continuance intention, patient engagement, and self-management behaviors were adapted from validated TAM- and UTAUT-based instruments and PFMT-related questionnaires. The preliminary questionnaire was translated into Chinese using a forward-translation approach.

The translated questionnaire was subsequently reviewed by a multidisciplinary expert panel consisting of two board-certified obstetrician–gynecologists, two professors with expertise in pelvic floor rehabilitation and health technology research, one pelvic floor physical therapist, and one continence nurse. The panel evaluated item relevance, clarity, semantic equivalence, and cultural appropriateness. A pilot test involving approximately 20 women with pelvic floor disorder was conducted to assess item comprehensibility and the feasibility of administration. Based on participant feedback, minor wording revisions were made to improve clarity and comprehensibility; however, no items were removed from the questionnaire.

The final questionnaire included the constructs of perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, self-efficacy, confirmation, satisfaction, involvement, knowledge, privacy concern, and continuance intention. All items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The questionnaire was administered during the second training session, with physical therapists and nurses available to provide clarification when needed.

Adherence outcomes

Individual adherence was quantified based on the number of supervised PFMT sessions completed. For the purposes of analysis, adherence was operationalized as completion of the prescribed six-session PFMT program. Participants who completed all six sessions were classified as adherent, whereas those who did not complete the full program were classified as non-adherent. Adherence was treated as a binary variable (complete vs. incomplete) for between-group comparisons and was analyzed using the chi-square test.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized participant demographics and baseline clinical measures. Continuous variables are reported as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. To examine changes in manometric outcomes after the six-session training protocol, a repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with time (pre- vs. post-training) as the within-subject factor and group (low- vs. high-severity) as the between-subjects factor. Main effects of time and group, and the group × time interaction, were analyzed for mean and maximum peak contraction pressure. Determinants of continuance intention and technology acceptance were investigated using PLS-SEM via SmartPLS (version 4). A two-step procedure was employed: First, the measurement model was assessed for internal consistency and convergent validity using indicator loadings, Cronbach’s alpha, rho_A, composite reliability, and AVE. Second, structural models estimated path coefficients and explained variance (R²). Statistical significance of hypothesized relationships was assessed using bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples, yielding standardized path coefficients, t-values, and p-values. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with significance defined as p < .05.

Results

All 65 participants completed the questionnaire, and no missing questionnaire responses were identified. Therefore, all participants were included in the PLS-SEM analyses. Baseline manometry assessments were available for all participants. For the manometry analyses, post-training values were defined as the final available manometry measurement obtained during the supervised PFMT program. Consequently, participants who did not complete all six supervised PFMT sessions could still contribute to the manometry analyses if a follow-up assessment was available prior to discontinuation.

Participants were stratified into high-severity (n = 33) and low-severity (n = 32) groups based on a median split of baseline PFDI-20 scores. As expected, participants in the high-severity group had significantly higher baseline PFDI-20 scores than those in the low-severity group (102.56 ± 37.30 vs. 35.22 ± 16.02, p < .001). The high-severity group also demonstrated significantly higher IIQ-7 scores (55.65 ± 20.56 vs. 42.05 ± 8.15, p = .001).

No significant between-group differences were observed in age (53.76 ± 10.78 vs. 54.72 ± 10.31 years, p = .715), BMI (24.59 ± 4.78 vs. 23.18 ± 2.69 kg/m², p = .148), educational level (p = .656), symptom classification (p = .88), or number of deliveries (1.91 ± 0.91 vs. 2.06 ± 0.68, p = .441). Delivery type distributions were also comparable between groups.

Adherence rates differed significantly between groups (χ² = 6.81, p = .009). A greater proportion of participants in the low-severity group completed all six PFMT sessions compared with those in the high-severity group (65.62% vs. 33.33%) (Table 1).

Results of repeated measures ANOVA for manometry outcomes

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of time on mean contraction pressure (F(1,63) = 10.76, p = .0017), indicating an increase in average contraction pressure over the six-session training period. Neither the main effect of group (F(1,63) = 2.90, p = .0936) nor the group × time interaction (F(1,63) = 1.55, p = .2183) reached statistical significance, indicating that the pattern of change over time was similar between severity groups. A similar pattern was observed for maximum peak pressure. The main effect of time was significant (F(1,63) = 10.61, p = .0018), whereas the effects of group (F(1,63) = 2.37, p = .1289) and the group × time interaction (F(1,63) = 0.59, p = .4449) were not statistically significant (Table 2).

Self-efficacy, in turn, strongly predicted perceived usefulness (β = 0.637, p < .001). Notably, perceived usefulness did not have a significant effect on satisfaction (β = 0.018, p = .928). However, privacy and physical comfort concerns were negatively associated with satisfaction, indicating that these factors may influence users’ evaluations of the technology. Discussion

This study investigated behavioral and clinical factors influencing patients’ behavioral intention, continuance intention, and adherence to a pressure-based pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) biofeedback device. By integrating symptom severity stratification, device-recorded muscle performance outcomes, and a theory-driven technology acceptance framework, these findings provide a multidimensional perspective on the determinants of engagement with PFMT technologies.

Adherence and symptom severity

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, participants with lower pelvic floor symptom burden, as measured by the PFDI-20, demonstrated significantly higher adherence to the six-session training program (65.6%) than those in the high-severity group (33.3%). Although greater symptom severity might be expected to increase motivation for engagement, this inverse association suggests that individuals with more severe symptoms may follow different care-seeking pathways. Specifically, individuals with a higher symptom burden may have different treatment expectations and care-seeking behaviors, which could influence their willingness to complete a conservative training program. These individuals may be more likely to seek alternative management strategies that are perceived as providing more immediate symptom relief.

Consistent with prior research, adherence to pelvic floor muscle training appears to be shaped by multifactorial determinants that extend beyond symptom severity alone. Recent studies indicate that adherence among women with pelvic floor disorders is generally moderate at best and that factors such as self-efficacy, knowledge, and social support play critical roles in adherence behavior. Symptom severity, in isolation, does not consistently predict adherence in the absence of underlying motivational mechanisms [21]. Similarly, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported substantial variability in PFMT adherence across studies and found no consistent linear association between symptom severity and adherence outcomes, further underscoring the complexity of engagement behaviors in conservative pelvic floor therapies [22].Expert consensus statements have likewise emphasized the importance of behavioral and psychosocial supports in promoting sustained engagement with PFMT, reinforcing the notion that clinical symptom burden interacts with multiple contexture factors in shaping adherence [13].

Moreover, conservative PFMT may pose greater practical and functional challenges for patients with more severe symptoms. Impaired muscle performance or deficient motor control may limit early training success, potentially leading to frustration and premature discontinuation. From a motivational perspective, limited early improvements may negatively affect self-efficacy and perceived benefit, both of which are important factors influencing continued participation in rehabilitation programs [5, 23].

Psychological factors may further contribute to this pattern. Women experiencing pronounced symptom-related impairments in quality of life may also carry heightened emotional burdens, including anxiety, embarrassment, or feelings of hopelessness, which can diminish their capacity to persist with conservative treatment approaches [24]. Collectively, these findings suggest that greater clinical severity does not necessarily translate into higher adherence, highlighting the importance of addressing both functional and psychosocial contexts when designing and implementing PFMT-based interventions [25].

Muscle performance outcomes and clinical considerations of manometer-based biofeedback

Repeated-measures ANOVA demonstrated significant increases in both mean contraction pressure and peak pressure over time following the six-session PFMT program. The absence of significant group-by-time interactions suggests that participants in both the low- and high-severity groups exhibited comparable changes in manometry-derived outcomes throughout the intervention period. This pattern may indicate that manometer-based biofeedback provides a structured training environment that supports motor learning and skill acquisition across individuals with varying levels of symptom severity. Collectively, these findings suggest that manometer-based biofeedback may provide a practical approach for monitoring changes in pelvic floor muscle performance during supervised PFMT.

Previous studies have reported improvements in pelvic floor muscle function and symptom outcomes following biofeedback-assisted PFMT [15]. Consistent with this literature, the present study observed favorable changes in manometry-derived outcomes after six supervised PFMT sessions. However, because of the single-arm design, the observed changes cannot be attributed solely to the intervention. Other factors, including repeated practice, therapist supervision, learning effects, and natural variation, may also have contributed to the observed improvements.

Despite these benefits, several inherent limitations of manometer-based biofeedback warrant consideration when evaluating its broader clinical applicability. First, these devices are invasive by nature, typically requiring vaginal or anal probe insertion, which may reduce acceptability and sustained use among some women, particularly those who experience discomfort, embarrassment, or heightened symptom sensitivity [26, 27]. In addition, pressure-based measures cannot differentiate isolated pelvic floor muscle contractions from increases in intra-abdominal pressure, raising the possibility that compensatory strategies, such as Valsalva maneuvers, may be misinterpreted as improvements in muscle performance. This limitation may affect both clinical decision-making and the accuracy of outcome evaluation, especially in individuals with poor motor control or more severe dysfunction. Consequently, while manometry biofeedback provides accessible and actionable feedback to support motor learning, its use in isolation may not fully capture the quality of pelvic floor muscle activation. These considerations underscore the importance of integrating manometric biofeedback with complementary assessment or training modalities, such as electromyography or image-based ultrasound-assistive tools, to enhance clinical interpretation and optimize individualized intervention strategies [28, 29].

Determinants of continuance intention in PFMT technology use

Structural equation modeling using SmartPLS demonstrated that perceived usefulness (PU) was the strongest predictor of continuance intention, consistent with the core assumptions of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). These frameworks posit that users’ beliefs regarding performance enhancement are central to sustained technology use [30, 31]. Prior research on post-adoption behavior in health technologies similarly indicates that continuance intention is more strongly driven by perceived utility than by initial adoption motives alone, particularly in self-managed or home-based interventions [32]. The present findings extend this theoretical framework to the context of pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), underscoring the critical role of perceived functional value in long-term engagement.

In Model 1, perceived ease of use exerted a significant positive influence on perceived usefulness, aligning with classical TAM propositions that systems perceived as easier to operate are more likely to be evaluated as beneficial [30]. However, perceived ease of use did not directly predict continuance intention, a pattern also reported in previous studies of health-related technologies in which usability functions primarily as an enabling condition rather than a direct driver of sustained behavior [32]. Instead, continued engagement appears contingent on whether ease of use translates into meaningful health-related benefits. Notably, knowledge positively predicted perceived usefulness, suggesting that individuals with greater understanding of pelvic floor health and training principles were more likely to recognize the value of PFMT technologies. This finding is consistent with the PFMT adherence literature, which emphasizes the importance of education and health literacy in shaping engagement with conservative pelvic floor interventions [17, 33].

Model 2 further elucidated the experiential and psychological mechanisms underlying continuance intention. The strong association between confirmation and self-efficacy reflects principles of expectation–confirmation theory, which posits that experiences that align with expectations reinforce confidence and promote continued use [20]. Similar relationships have been reported in rehabilitation and exercise-based interventions, where perceived competence and self-efficacy are important determinants of sustained participation and adherence. In the context of pelvic floor muscle training, previous studies have identified self-efficacy as a key predictor of exercise adherence, suggesting that women who are confident in their ability to correctly perform pelvic floor muscle contractions are more likely to maintain long-term participation in training programs [34]. Our findings are consistent with this literature and further suggest that confirmation may serve as an important mechanism through which self-efficacy is strengthened, thereby promoting continuance intention toward PFMT technologies. The present findings suggest that confirmation is a key contributor to continued engagement with PFMT technologies. Participants whose experiences met or exceeded their expectations were more likely to report greater self-efficacy and stronger continuance intention. Furthermore, involvement exerted a significant indirect effect through confirmation, highlighting the importance of early engagement and personalized interaction in fostering positive usage trajectories among women undergoing PFMT. These findings are consistent with behavioral change frameworks, which emphasize the role of active participation and positive experiences in supporting long-term engagement with self-management interventions.

Interestingly, although perceived usefulness consistently predicted continuance intention, satisfaction did not exert a direct effect, suggesting that affective evaluations alone may be insufficient to sustain engagement in this health behavior context. This finding aligns with prior continuance models indicating that satisfaction often operates in conjunction with, rather than independently of, cognitive appraisals such as perceived usefulness [32]. Nevertheless, affective experiences were not inconsequential. Privacy and comfort concerns demonstrated a significant negative association with satisfaction, indicating that perceived intrusiveness or physical discomfort diminished users’ emotional evaluations of the intervention. Qualitative studies examining women’s experiences with PFMT and biofeedback have similarly identify embarrassment, discomfort, and perceived invasiveness as barriers to satisfaction and sustained participation [35]. Although satisfaction did not directly predict continuance intention in the present model, privacy- and comfort-related concerns indirectly undermined engagement by eroding satisfaction, highlighting a potential pathway through which negative user experiences may affect continued engagement with PFMT technologies.

Taken together, these findings suggest that continuance intention toward PFMT technologies is influenced by a complex interplay of cognitive evaluations, experiential feedback, and contextual acceptability, rather than by symptom severity or affective responses alone [33]. By integrating established technology acceptance theories with clinical and experiential variables, this study contributes to a growing body of women’s health research demonstrating that sustained engagement with pelvic floor technologies is primarily driven by perceived usefulness, supported by early success, self-efficacy, and an acceptable user experience.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the integration of objective physiological measurements with a theory-driven framework of technology acceptance, providing a multidimensional perspective on engagement with PFMT technologies. Furthermore, the prospective design and repeated assessments allowed evaluation of both behavioral and functional outcomes during routine clinical care.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, participants were recruited from a single tertiary medical center and represented women who had been referred for PFMT within a routine clinical care pathway. As a result, the findings may not be generalizable to all women with pelvic floor disorders, particularly those who do not seek treatment or who receive care in different healthcare settings. Second, because this study was conducted as an exploratory investigation within a routine clinical care setting, an a priori sample size calculation was not performed. Although the sample size was sufficient to support the planned analyses, the relatively modest sample may have limited the detection of smaller effects and reduced the generalizability of the findings. Third, participants were stratified into high- and low-symptom groups using a median split of baseline PFDI-20 scores to facilitate exploratory comparisons within the study cohort. Although this approach allowed examination of potential differences according to symptom burden, it may have reduced the variability captured by the original continuous measure and may not fully reflect the spectrum of symptom severity. Fourth, adherence rates differed across symptom-severity groups, leading to variability in the amount of supervised PFMT received during the study period. Consequently, participants may not have received an equivalent training dose prior to the post-training assessment. This variability in training exposure may have influenced the magnitude of the observed changes in manometry-derived outcomes and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Fifth, the intervention period consisted of six supervised PFMT sessions, reflecting routine clinical practice in our outpatient setting. This duration allowed participants to gain experience with the biofeedback system and form perceptions regarding its use. The findings may therefore be more reflective of early technology acceptance and engagement than longer-term behavioral maintenance. Home-based PFMT adherence was not formally monitored. The relationship between continuance intention and sustained long-term adherence remains unclear. Sixth, adherence was defined solely as completion of the supervised PFMT program. Potential contextual factors that may influence treatment adherence, including transportation burden, commuting time, socioeconomic resources, family support, and other access-related barriers, were not formally assessed. Consequently, the reasons underlying incomplete adherence could not be fully evaluated and may have contributed to the observed differences in program completion.

Future studies incorporating larger and more diverse populations, longitudinal follow-up, objective monitoring of home-based PFMT, standardized training exposure, and contextual factors that may influence adherence (e.g., access to care and treatment-related barriers) are warranted to further validate the proposed model and evaluate the persistence of these behavioral mechanisms over time.

Implications

The present findings underscore the importance of tailoring pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) interventions not only to clinical severity but also to users’ behavioral and experiential profiles. The observed differences in continued intention suggest that engagement with PFMT technologies is influenced by a combination of early experience, self-efficacy, and perceived functional value, rather than symptom burden alone. Accordingly, strategies that promote early engagement, such as personalized instruction, confidence-building feedback, and clear performance cues, may help support participation in PFMT programs.

From a practical perspective, the results highlight the importance of emphasizing perceived usefulness, functional gains, and user competence during PFMT implementation. Clinicians and developers may consider incorporating features that reinforce positive experiences and self-efficacy during the early stages of training, when users may be most vulnerable to disengagement. In addition, addressing experiential barriers, including perceived intrusiveness, privacy concerns, and physical discomfort, may improve user satisfaction and support continued engagement with PFMT technologies.

The present findings provide useful insights into factors associated with engagement and continuance intention toward PFMT technologies. Future studies should examine whether the behavioral determinants identified in this study remain applicable in home-based or hybrid delivery settings and should further investigate how longitudinal support strategies, such as reminders, remote feedback, or adaptive coaching, influence continuance intention and long-term adherence over time.

Conclusions

This study found that continuance intention toward PFMT technologies was primarily associated with perceived usefulness and was further supported by positive user experiences, self-efficacy, and confirmation. Although satisfaction did not directly predict continuance intention, privacy and physical comfort concerns were associated with satisfaction and may influence users’ willingness to continue engaging with PFMT technologies. Importantly, symptom severity did not necessarily correlate with greater adherence, suggesting that behavioral and experiential factors should be considered alongside clinical indicators when implementing PFMT programs.

By integrating objective manometry-derived training outcomes with a theoretically grounded model of technology acceptance, this study provides insights into factors associated with patient engagement and continued intention of PFMT technologies. The findings highlight the importance of perceived usefulness, self-efficacy, positive user experiences, and comfort-related factors in supporting continued engagement with PFMT technologies. These factors may represent important considerations when designing and implementing PFMT programs and related rehabilitation technologies.
