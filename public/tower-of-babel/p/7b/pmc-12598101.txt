Prevalence and Associated Factors of Birth Injuries Among Neonates in Ethiopia: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Molla Azmeraw, Amare Kassaw, Dessie Temesgen, Gebremeskel Kibret Abebe, Addis Wondmagegn Alamaw, Alemu Birara Zemariam, Befkad Derese Tilahun, Biruk Beletew Abate
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction and AimsBirth injuries contribute to neonatal morbidity and mortality. The incidence rates of birth injuries vary from 1.9 to 53 per 1000 live births. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of birth injuries in Ethiopian neonates and to identify key associated risk factors. The study addresses inconsistencies in previous prevalence estimates and fills knowledge gaps regarding important contributing factors that were insufficiently explored in earlier reviews.MethodsThe study explored various databases to access articles about the prevalence and associated factors of birth injuries among newborns, which are pertinent only to Ethiopia. The Joanna Briggs Institute quality assessment tool was utilized to evaluate the quality of each study. Data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel and STATA 17.0 statistical software. A random effects model was employed to estimate the pooled prevalence of birth injuries. Additionally, the Cochrane Q‐test statistics and the I² test were applied. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger's statistical tests. Sensitivity analysis was also performed to detect the effects of smaller studies.ResultSix studies involving a total population of 3781 participants were included in the analysis. The pooled prevalence of birth injuries among newborns in Ethiopia was found to be 16.24% (95% CI: 13.34–19.14; I² = 82.67%; p < 0.001). Birth weight greater than 4 kg (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 8.88; 95% CI: 1.79–15.98), prolonged labor (AOR = 4.70; 95% CI: 1.90–7.49), vacuum‐assisted birth (AOR = 14.92; 95% CI: 6.26 to 36.10), and forceps‐assisted birth (AOR = 7.83; 95% CI: 4.71–10.95) were significant predictors.Conclusion and RecommendationThe prevalence of birth injuries is notably high. The identified predictors were preventable. Implementation of routine labor and delivery practice audits and enhancing the skills of healthcare providers are recommended to reduce the risk of birth injuries.

Introduction

Birth injuries are traumatic events that occur during delivery and result in structural or functional impairment in newborns [1]. Regarding the burden of birth injuries, the incidence rate of birth injuries ranges from 6 per 1000 live births in the USA [2] to 22 per 1000 live births in Iran [3]. A 2024 meta‐analysis of 37 studies found a birth trauma incidence of 34 per 1000 live births in low‐ and middle‐income countries, with Africa having the highest rate at 53 per 1000 live births [4]. Another recent review suggests that birth injuries in high‐income countries (HICs) may differ across economic hierarchies due to systemic delays in care, including lack of education, transportation barriers, and limited emergency obstetric teams [5]. In contrast, HICs have standardized protocols, timely cesarean sections, and neonatal intensive care units, resulting in a high burden of birth injuries. Fetal presentation, delivery methods, and maternal factors influence neonatal birth injuries. Fetal risk factors may include macrosomia, fetal malpresentation, shoulder dystocia, prolonged labor, dysfunctional labor, and preterm labor [4, 5, 6].

Once it occurs, birth injuries cause significant functional and anatomical morbidity, including physical impairments and developmental delays [7, 8, 9, 10]. Despite its short and long‐term consequences, birth injuries can be prevented through improved care during labor and delivery. In light of this, one of the targets of Sustainable Development Goal 3 is to eliminate avoidable infant and child mortality by 2030, with all nations striving to reduce these rates to as low as 12 per 1000 live births for neonates [11]. Since 2009, Ethiopia has implemented a human resource strategy aimed at enhancing maternal and child health services [12]. The prevalence of birth injuries in Ethiopia ranges from 12.3% to 24.7%, indicating inconsistency [13, 14]. Although a systematic review and meta‐analysis on birth injuries published by Lera et al. (2023) [15] exhibited a significantly small study effect, ranging from 2.85 to 41. This study also did not include the research conducted by Tolosa et al. (2023), which resulted in considerable heterogeneity (92.6%) [16]. In addition, prolonged labor and birth weight were significantly associated variables in our study, whereas they were not reported in a previously published article by Temesgen Lera (Lera et al. 2023) [16]. Therefore, this systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of birth injuries and the associated factors among live births in Ethiopia and address the gaps that were not covered by previously published articles.

Aim of the Study

This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of birth injuries among neonates in Ethiopia and to identify the associated factors contributing to these injuries. The study addresses the inconsistencies in prevalence estimates reported in previous studies and fills gaps related to important risk factors that were not adequately examined in earlier reviews.

Objective

The objectives of this systematic review and meta‐analysis were to: –Estimate the pooled prevalence of birth injuries among neonates in Ethiopia.–Identify and analyze the key factors associated with birth injuries, including prolonged labor, birth weight, and mode of delivery.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

A systematic review and meta‐analysis study was conducted in Ethiopia. All the studies that have been conducted on birth injuries among neonates in Ethiopia were cross‐sectional in study design. There is an inconsistency in the prevalence report and controversies in reporting the risk factors that expose neonates to birth injuries. In addition, cross‐sectional studies are lower in evidence generation than systematic reviews. Systematic review and meta‐analysis are driven by the evidence‐based medicine movement and the Cochrane Collaboration. They are advantageous in reducing bias, replicability, resolving controversies, and providing a more reliable basis for decision‐making than cross‐sectional studies. Therefore, in this systematic review and meta‐analysis, the authors reviewed studies conducted on birth injuries among neonates in Ethiopia to estimate the pooled prevalence of birth injuries and to resolve the existing controversies between studies. Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions recommends at least 10 studies for publication bias assessment. Only two studies may be enough to do a meta‐analysis study. Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions also recommends at least two studies to run a forest plot [17]. As of August 2023, Ethiopia has a total of 12 regional states (Afar, Amhara, Benishangul‐Gumuz, Gambella, Harari, Oromia, Somali, South Ethiopia, South West Ethiopia, Sidama, Central Ethiopia, and Tigray) and two city administrations (Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa), a federal democratic republic. Its 1,100,000 km2 total territory is divided into zones, which are then further divided into districts, which are then further divided into kebeles, the lowest administrative units [18]. Africa's second most populated nation is Ethiopia, which has about 112 million residents (56,010,000 females and 56,069,000 males in 2019) [19]. In 2021, Ethiopia will have 3.65 million births. That is 10,014 per day, ranking eighth. In terms of urbanization, it is also placed 219th with the urban population of 25.59 million [20].

Searching Strategies

Through the use of various databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, the institutional review and dissemination databases of the national health services, including local university repositories like Addis Ababa University, the available articles were accessed. As a result, the actual search was carried out between December 28, 2022 and January 24, 2023. Google Scholar, PubMed, Web of Science, and Science Direct are among the electronic databases that were looked through. On July 15, 2023, the searching procedure was revised to reduce time‐lag bias. All observational studies with epidemiological data, whether published or unpublished, were the primary focus of our search method. The search term was constructed based on the databases. The key search terms used included “birth injuries”, “neonates” or “infant” or newborn and “Ethiopia” with combinations of their MeSH terms. These key terms were combined using Boolean operators “AND/OR” to narrow the search in the databases. The searching using Boolean operators in PubMed was (“Prevalence” OR “Frequency” OR “proportion” OR “magnitude”) AND (“Associated Factors” OR “determinants” OR “risk factors” OR “causes” OR “correlates”) AND (“birth injuries” OR “trauma”) AND (“neonat**” OR “newborn**” OR “infant**”) AND “Ethiopia”. In Science Direct and Google Scholar, the search engine was “Prevalence” AND (“Associated Factors” OR “determinants”) AND (“birth injur**” OR “ birth trauma”) AND “neonate”) AND “Ethiopia”. In addition, the electronic searching strategy was supplemented by manual searches to identify relevant unpublished studies. The reference lists of included studies were also screened for the presence of additional studies. Three authors (MA, DT and AK) conducted the searching process. References were managed using EndNote software version X8. Prospero registration

We acknowledge the importance of protocol registration for systematic reviews to enhance transparency and reduce reporting bias. However, this review was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO. We have adhered strictly to established methodological guidelines and reported our protocol and methods in detail within the manuscript to ensure rigor and reproducibility.

Exclusion Criteria

Following a thorough analysis of the study abstracts and complete texts, the two reviewers (MA and AK) independently and anonymously selected the studies to be included in the review. Articles with methodological flaws were excluded. These reviewers have experience in systematic review and meta‐analysis. A methodological flaw refers to weaknesses, errors, or limitations in the design, planning, or execution of a research study that undermine the validity, reliability, or credibility of its findings. Any disagreements that arose during the review process were resolved through discussion. Despite discussion, if disagreements persisted, a third party was consulted. Commentaries and review articles were also excluded. Using the PRISMA flow diagram as a guide, the processes for identifying, screening, and including or excluding records were conducted (Figure 1).

Quality Evaluation

Two authors (MA and AK) independently evaluated the caliber of the included studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical assessment technique for cross‐sectional research. For scores of 50, 50–75, and > 75%, respectively. We classified the overall quality scores as low quality (high risk of bias), moderate quality (moderate risk of bias), and high quality (low risk of bias)[23]. The appraisal tool's eight criteria are as follows [1]: Inclusion standards [2], Study subject and setting descriptions [3], Valid and Reliable Exposure Measurement [4], Objective and Standard Criteria Used [5], Confounder Identification [6], strategies to handle confounder [7], outcome measurement, and [8] appropriate statistical analysis. Studies were considered low risk whenever fitted to 50% and/or above in the quality assessment checklist criteria [21, 22].

Data Extraction Process

To ensure systematic and consistent collection of relevant information from each included study, we developed a standardized data extraction tool tailored to the objectives of our review. The data extraction tool captured key study characteristics, including bibliographic details such as author names, publication year, and study title. We also recorded the geographical regional location where each study was conducted to provide contextual background.

Detailed information on study design was extracted to classify studies accurately (e.g., cross‐sectional studies). Participant characteristics were carefully documented, encompassing sample size and response rate specified by the original authors. Outcome data extraction focused on both primary and secondary endpoints, noting the specific outcomes measured. Quantitative results, including prevalence, odds ratio and confidence interval, were systematically collected.

Outcome/Endpoint/

Primary endpoint: The prevalence of birth injuries is the primary endpoint of this study. A neonate was considered to have birth injuries if they experienced accidental scalp lacerations, bruising, caput succedaneum, facial paralysis, subconjunctival hemorrhage, neurological injuries such as phrenic nerve injuries, cephalohematoma, subgaleal hemorrhage, brachial plexus injuries, clavicle fractures, and/or other soft tissue or skeletal trauma occurring during delivery. By aggregating the prevalence of birth injuries reported in each included article, the pooled prevalence of birth injuries was calculated.

Secondary endpoint: the secondary outcome of this study was factors associated with birth injuries, such as instrumental delivery, prolonged labor and birth weight.

Statistical Analysis

The pooled prevalence of birth injuries was estimated using a random‐effects meta‐analysis to account for variability between studies. The Random‐Effects Model in meta‐analysis is a statistical approach that assumes the true effect size varies across the different studies included in the analysis, rather than being exactly the same in all studies. This model acknowledges that each study estimates its own true effect, which is drawn from a distribution of effects rather than a single fixed effect. Unlike the fixed‐effect model, which assumes one true effect size common to all studies, the random‐effects model recognizes that differences in study populations, interventions, settings, or methodologies may cause the true effect size to differ from study to study. These differences introduce between‐study variability or heterogeneity in effect sizes. Prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were combined, and heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q test (p < 0.05 taken into consideration as statistically significant). The I² statistic represents the percentage of total variation across studies in a meta‐analysis that is due to true heterogeneity rather than chance. Values of I² above 75% are generally interpreted as indicating substantial heterogeneity among the included studies. An interpretation of low, medium, considerable and substantial heterogeneity was given to I‐Squared (I2) values of < 25%, 25.01%–50%, 50.01%–75%, and > 75%, respectively. In cases where considerable heterogeneity was detected, subgroup analysis was carefully weighed. For associated factors, adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% CIs were extracted and pooled using a random‐effects model. Forest plots were generated to visualize the effect sizes (prevalence and OR). Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity based on study characteristics such as sample size. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding studies (one study at a time) with a high risk of bias to test the robustness of the findings. Publication bias was assessed through funnel and Doi plots inspection and Egger's and LFK index (Luis Furuya‐Kanamori index) tests. The DOI plot (Difference in Means plotted against Precision) is a graphical tool used in meta‐analyses to visualize asymmetry in the distribution of study effects, which can indicate potential publication bias or small‐study effects. Compared to traditional funnel plots, the DOI plot provides a clearer and more intuitive visual representation of asymmetry by plotting effect sizes against a measure of precision, forming a shape that ideally resembles a symmetrical mountain when no bias is present.

The LFK index (Luis Furuya‐Kanamori index) is a quantitative measure derived from the DOI plot that assesses the degree of asymmetry. It calculates the difference between the cumulative proportions of studies on either side of the mean effect size, standardized by variability. The LFK index values close to zero indicate symmetry (no bias), values between ±1 and ±2 suggest minor asymmetry, and values beyond ±2 indicate major asymmetry, which may reflect publication bias or other small‐study effects.

Together, the DOI plot and LFK index provide complementary visual and statistical methods to detect bias in meta‐analyses. Studies have shown that the LFK index can be more sensitive than traditional tests like Egger's regression in identifying asymmetry, especially in meta‐analyses with a small number of studies. If bias was detected, the trim‐and‐fill method was applied to adjust the pooled estimates. We used Excel for data extraction and STATA version 17 for analysis.

Result

Characteristics of the Studies

All the included studies were cross‐sectional by study design, and the sample size ranged from 272 [23] to 1315 [24]. Two of the included studies were from the Oromia region. The rest 2, 1, and 1 were from Addis Ababa, Amhara and South Nation, Nationalities and peoples of Ethiopia, respectively. The reported prevalence of birth injuries ranged from 12.3% [14] to 24.7% [13] across the included studies (Table 1). In addition, a total of 3781 newborns were included. The quality of each paper secured 60% and above based on the quality tool checklist.

Prevalence of Birth Injuries

Birth injuries were present in 12.3% [14] to 24.7% [13] of live births in Ethiopia. The pooled prevalence of birth injuries in Ethiopia was found to be 16.24% (95% CI; 13.34%–19.14%; I2 = 82.67%; p = 0.001), according to the random‐effects model analysis of the included studies (Figure 2). I2 82.67% showed significant heterogeneity.

Forest plot of the included studies showing the pooled prevalence of birth injury among neonates in Ethiopia.

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analysis was conducted using the median sample size of 552 as a benchmark. In this analysis, no significant difference in heterogeneity was observed (I² = 81.15, p = 0.001). The pooled effect size for studies with a sample size greater than 552 was 18.92% (13.63, 24.21), while the pooled effect size for studies with a sample size less than the median (552) was 16.24% (11.28, 16.98) with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 77.25, p = 0.01).

Sensitivity Analysis

We employed a leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis to identify the potential source of bias. The results of this sensitivity analysis indicated that our findings were not reliant on any single study. Our pooled estimated prevalence of birth injury varied between 14.81% (95% CI, 12.77–16.85) [13] and 17.11 (95% CI, 13.98–20.24) [14] after deletion of a single study once at a time.

Publication Bias

We checked publication bias using the Doi plot and funnel plot. A minor publication bias was detected using these graphs. LFK index and Egger's regression test p‐value were 1.10 and 0.11, respectively, which also indicated the absence of publication bias.

Determinants of Birth Injuries

There was at least one significantly associated factor reported with birth injuries in each of the studies that have been published so far [13, 14, 16, 23, 24, 25]. Gestational diabetic mellitus [13], being preterm (< 34 weeks) [16], fetal mal‐presentation [16], being male [16], being delivered at health facility [16], elective caesarian section (C/S), low birth weight [14], being maternal age within the age range of 25–34 years [23], instrumental delivery [16, 24] and shoulder dystocia [25] were significant predictors of birth injuries. In this study, prolonged labor, birth weight, forceps delivery and vacuum delivery were significantly associated with birth injuries.

The significant variables were thoroughly covered in the following subtopics for more information.

Prolonged Labor

Two studies found a significant association between prolonged labor and birth injuries. Of these, the highest and lowest risk factors for mothers with prolonged labor were AOR = 5.78 (3.0, 11.15) Biset et al. [25] and AOR = 3.74 (1.52, 9.2) Tibebu, et al. [13], respectively, compared to those who had a normal duration of labor. The forest plot combining the results of two studies showed that the overall estimated AOR of prolonged labor was 4.70 (95% CI: 1.90–7.49); I2 = 0.0%; p = 0.001). I2 and p‐value showed the homogeneity of the study. Regarding to publication bias, the funnel and the Doi plot analysis showed symmetrical distribution. The Egger's regression test, and LFK index were p‐value = 0.54 and = 1.78, respectively, which indicates the absence of publication bias (Table 2).

Birth Weight

The other significantly associated variable was birth weight. A significant association between birth weight > 4 kg and birth injuries was reported by four studies. Of these, studies with the highest and lowest AOR were = 25 (14.28, 33.33) Belay et al. [24] and 2.78 (1.01, 7.69) Tibebu et al. [13], respectively, compared to those who had normal birth weight. The forest plot combining the results of four studies showed that the overall pooled estimated AOR of birth weight > 4 kg was 8.88 (95% CI: 1.79–15.98); I2 = 84.95%; p = 0.001) (Table 2). I2 and p‐value showed considerable heterogeneity of the study. The distribution of the studies was visually asymmetric using the funnel and Doi plot. The asymmetry was supported by Egger's regression test‐value and LFK index were 0.002 and 3.69, respectively, which indicates the presence of publication bias (Table 2). A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was also carried out to identify the possible source of bias. The sensitivity analysis's outcomes demonstrated that the conclusions were not reliant on a single study. After excluding one report, our pooled estimated AOR ranged from 3.61 (1.15–6.07) to 12.06 (−0.11–24.23). A study done by Belay et al. [24] demonstrated an impact on the general estimation. A trim and fill analysis was also carried out. There was no study added after a trim and fill analysis on the funnel plot; the combined estimated AOR for the association between birth weight and birth injuries became the same 8.88 (1.79, 15.98).

Vacuum Delivery

A significant association between vacuum delivery and birth injuries was also found by two studies [14, 25]. The corresponding adjusted odds ratios were = 6.74 (2.01, 22.56) Biset et al. [25] and AOR = 29.2 (13.41, 63.709) Yemane et al. [14], respectively, compared to those delivered with cesarean section. The forest plot combining the results of two studies showed that the overall estimated AOR of vacuum delivery was 14.92 (95% CI: 6.26, 36.10); I2 = 61.92%; p‐value < 0.001). I2 and p‐value disclosed the heterogeneity of the studies (Table 2). The distribution was asymmetric, as shown by the LFK index = 3.69. The Egger's regression test value was also 0.11, indicating the absence of publication bias. A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was also carried out to locate the probable source of bias between associated studies. This sensitivity analysis's result demonstrated that the conclusions were not reliant on a single study. After excluding one report, our pooled estimated AOR ranged from 6.74 (−3.53, 17.01) to 29.20 (4.05–54.35).

Forceps Delivery

Furthermore, among the included studies, only Biset et al. [25] and Yemane et al. [14] tested the association between forceps delivery and birth injuries. The degree of association was 7.36 (1.96, 27.58) and 7.9 (2.99, 21.09) via authors Biset et al. [25] and Yemane et al. [14], respectively. According to the results of two studies' forest plot merging, the estimated AOR for the association of live births with forceps delivery was 7.83 (95% CI: 4.71–10.95); I2 = 0%; p = 0.001) (Table 2). I2 and p‐value indicated the study's homogeneity (Table 2). The Doi plot and LFK index suggested asymmetry among the studies; however, the symmetry of the funnel plot and Egger's regression test yielded a value of 0.96, indicating an absence of publication bias. A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was also carried out to identify the potential source of bias among the associated studies. This sensitivity analysis's result demonstrated that the conclusions were not reliant on a single study. We also considered a trim and fill analysis. There was no study added after a trim and fill analysis on the funnel plot; the combined estimated AOR for the association between forceps delivery and birth injuries became the same 7.78 (4.71–10.95).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis study aimed to determine the prevalence of birth injuries and associated factors among neonates in Ethiopia. It included 6 studies to assess the pooled prevalence of birth injuries and associated factors among neonates in Ethiopia, which were published from 2016 to 2023. In this study, the pooled prevalence of birth injuries was 16.24%. The finding is higher than the study finding in Cameroon (1.84%) [26], Nigeria (0.7% to 1.1%) [27, 28], Iran (2.7%) [29], Wisconsin (2.78%) and Chicago (0.82%). The difference might be due to health care service accessibility, health care provider skill competency [30], culture and health‐seeking behavior within the community. The other possible difference could be attributed to the varying geographic locations, study designs, variations over time, as well as the standard of obstetric practices. The finding also suggests that early identification of risk factors for birth injuries is more beneficial in preventing such injuries than taking no action at all [30].

Knowing the obstetric risk factors and prenatal disorders connected to birth injuries is a critical initial step in the management of afflicted neonates because birth injuries requiring rapid, urgent care in the delivery room are infrequent [31].

In this study, prolonged labor, vacuum delivery, forceps delivery, and birth weight > 4 kg were the identified risk factors of birth injuries. The finding was similar to the previous studies on vacuum delivery [32, 33, 34, 35], forceps delivery [32, 34, 35], birth weight [32, 33, 34, 35], and prolonged labor [35].

Neonates born to mothers who experienced prolonged labor were 4.7 times more likely to sustain birth injuries compared to those without prolonged labor. Prolonged labor can lead to fetal distress and an increased likelihood of interventions such as instrumental delivery, which are associated with higher rates of birth trauma. Effective monitoring and timely management of labor progression are therefore critical to reducing the risk of birth injuries associated with prolonged labor [36]. The causes of prolonged labor might be widely ranged. The infant can be positioned improperly or be too big to fit through the mother's birth canal. Besides other factors, weak contractions might cause labor to last longer. Whatever the cause, prolonged labor can be extremely risky. One of the main dangers of prolonged labor is oxygen deprivation of the fetus.

Prolonged labor predisposes to perinatal asphyxia, which itself carries short‐ and long‐term complications, including lifelong brain damage and impairment in the infant.

In addition, newborns with large birth weight (birth weight > 4 kg) were 8.8 times more likely to experience birth injuries than those born with normal weight. The finding was similar to the previous studies [32, 33, 34, 35]. High abdominal to head circumference, high abdominal diameter (AD) and estimated fetal weight (EFW) > 4000 g were all significantly linked with birth injuries [37]. This implies, clinicians must improve prenatal detection of macrosomia, carefully consider delivery methods, and ensure skilled care during childbirth. In addition, pregnant women with gestational weight gain of ≥ 15 kg and a previous history of macrosomia need to be delivered in health care services with specialized delivery instruments/devices.

According to this study's findings, compared to cesarean births, vacuum‐assisted births were associated with a 14.9‐fold increase in the odds of birth injuries. The findings agreed with those of earlier studies [32, 33, 34, 35]. The finding might be due to untrained usage of the vacuum.

Newborns delivered via forceps were 7.8 times more likely to experience birth injuries than their counterparts. The results were consistent with previous studies [32, 34, 35]. To prevent harm, hospitals must ensure strict adherence to forceps guidelines, improve obstetric training, and consider safer alternatives when possible.

Limitations of the Study

This study was conducted with limitations. Although the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions recommends at least 10 studies for publication bias assessment and at least two studies to run a forest plot [38]. According to this recommendation, the bias assessment of this study may not be efficient in detecting it. The other limitation of this study was the uneven distribution of the included studies across the regional states. In addition, the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions also recommended subgroup analysis for moderate to high heterogeneity with at least three articles per group, subgroup analysis by region was not conducted. Furthermore, in the random‐effects model, we calculate an adjusted random‐effects weight for each observation. Since we used the DersSimonian Laird estimator, which is sensitive for extreme variation of sample sizes of the studies, the number of studies, sample size variation from study to study and the size of variance (tau‐square), this study is not free from estimator bias. Collectively, these limitations reduce the validity and generalizability of the study's findings, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation and highlighting the importance of future research with larger, more evenly distributed datasets to confirm and extend these results.

Conclusion and Recommendation

The prevalence of birth injuries was high as compared to the literature. Prolonged labor, birth weight and instrumental delivery (vacuum and forceps) were significant predictors of birth injuries. To lower the burden of birth injuries, routine delivery practice audits, greater caution in excluding cephalopelvic disproportion, and wise use of forceps and vacuum extraction are advisable. Strengthening basic emergency management of obstetrics care is also important. In addition, maternal and perinatal death surveillance and response (MPDSR) provides useful, step‐by‐step instructions for setting up a framework to evaluate the burden of maternal, stillbirth, and neonatal deaths. It also discusses trends in the numbers and causes of deaths, as well as how to connect assessments of maternal and perinatal mortality. To stop similar fatalities, including birth injuries from happening in the future, it is critical to gather information on the modifiable factors that contribute to preventable deaths and to utilize that information to lead action. Auditing the causes of neonatal mortality is important. So, it could be related to the existing MPDSR system and recommended for integration as part of quality improvement measures.

Implication to Policy

The finding of this study strengthens the previous studies' reports on the prevalence of birth injuries. If newborns experienced birth injuries, they are prone to further morbidity like internal bleeding, jaundice, seizure, prolonged hospitalization, and high‐exempted health care costs, even death. Even though all the methods of delivery are recommended by the national obstetrics and gynecology guidelines, the health care providers need to consider instrumental delivery as the last option. They also need to prepare themselves to receive a newborn with birth trauma in an instrumental delivery. Prolonged labor is the cause of birth injuries. This might have indicated poor partograph monitoring and referral system (first delay, second delay and third delay). The government needs to strengthen the refreshment training on partograph monitoring and timely decision‐making on it. In addition, provision of basic obstetrics care training for all newly recruited health care providers who are assigned in labor and delivery ward might be important to reduce birth injuries and other adverse birth outcomes. However, birth injuries prevention and control programs may face several challenges from the side of policy maker(political priority, poor coordination of resources to support multi‐sectorial response and less financial commitment), resource‐human and financial (poor awareness of need for birth injuries prevention and control) and directly the beneficiary‐care givers (lack of education and information about the consequence of labor and delivery like birth injuries and prevention strategies (strict partograph monitoring, birth preparedness and complication readiness, quality of health services, etc.).

Implication to further Research

Since all the included studies were too diverse and cross‐sectional research, they have less power to demonstrate a temporal association between birth injuries and their associated factors. In addition, the measurements of exposure and their effect on the outcome of this study were made at the same time. It is not easy to assess the reasons for existing directions of associations. The study findings may also be prone to mediator and confounding variables effect. As a result, there is a potential for reverse causality of the identified factors. So, prospective studies are needed to assess predictors and outcomes of birth injuries during labor and delivery, with a focus on factors such as the quality of partograph monitoring and healthcare provider decision‐making. These studies should employ stratification or multivariable adjustment to account for potential confounding and mediator variables.
