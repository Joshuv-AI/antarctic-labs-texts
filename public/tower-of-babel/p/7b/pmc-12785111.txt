The new era of bioengineered plant NLR receptors
Authors: Peng Cao, Zhimao Sun, Shijie Wang, Qi Zhang, Yuyan An, Meixiang Zhang
Published: 2025

Main text

Bioengineered plant NLR receptors are advancing toward modular design, enabling accurate and durable detection of diverse pathogens. This commentary highlights emerging strategies for reprogramming NLRs into synthetic immune circuits, introducing a transformative shift from natural co-evolution to programmable immunity. Such approaches have the potential to enhance crop resilience against continuously evolving threats.

Evolutionary bottlenecks in plant immunity

Plants have evolved robust immune responses against fungi, oomycetes, bacteria, nematodes, and viruses. Despite being sessile and continuously exposed to microbial threats, they achieve effective immunity without mobile immune cells, relying instead on a two-tiered defense system. The first layer, pattern-triggered immunity (PTI), is initiated by surface-localized pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) that detect conserved microbial signatures. To bypass PTI, pathogens deliver effectors into host cells, which are recognized by intracellular nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat receptors (NLRs), triggering effector-triggered immunity (ETI), a stronger and more targeted response. This evolutionary arms race—plants evolving receptors to detect effectors and pathogens evading recognition through mutation or effector loss—has shaped immune diversity. However, natural NLR variation is limited by trade-offs, including autoimmunity, epistasis, and narrow recognition spectra. As pathogens mutate or delete effector genes, NLR-mediated resistance often becomes transient (Tamborski and Krasileva, 2020). In contrast, engineered NLRs are not bound by the constraints of natural evolution and can be designed for improved specificity, expanded recognition breadth, and enhanced resilience (Kim et al., 2016; Kourelis et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025a). Leveraging NLR bioengineering will therefore be critical for developing resilient crops in the context of rapidly evolving pathogens and climate change.

Universal key: Bioengineered NLR unlocks broad-spectrum disease resistance

Various engineering strategies have been employed to reprogram plant immunity (Figure 1A). One approach relies on artificial evolution, combining site-directed or random mutagenesis of NLRs with functional screening under pathogen pressure. For example, resistance to Tomato brown rugose fruit virus (ToBRFV)—which has overcome all known tomato resistance genes, including Tm-22—was restored by introducing mutations into the leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain of Tm-22, generating receptor variants capable of recognizing mutated viral movement proteins (Wang et al., 2025b). While effective, this strategy is pathogen specific and inherently reactive, requiring continual adaptation as pathogens evolve.

NLR engineering strategies and a blueprint for programmable plant defense.

(A) Overview of strategies for engineering plant NLR receptors. (a) Artificial evolution applies random or site-directed mutagenesis of the NLR LRR domain (red mark), followed by functional screening under pathogen pressure to generate receptor variants with new effector recognition capabilities. (b) Decoy engineering involves modifying endogenous “decoy” proteins (e.g., PBS1) to contain pathogen-specific protease cleavage sites, such that cleavage by pathogen effectors activates a cognate NLR (e.g., RPS5) and triggers immunity. (c) Integrated domain reprogramming utilizes structure-guided mutagenesis or swapping of native integrated domains (IDs), such as the heavy-metal-associated (HMA) domain, to alter or expand recognition specificity toward non-cognate effectors. (d) Nanobody fusions create customizable receptors by replacing a native integrated domain (e.g., the HMA domain in Pikm-1) with a nanobody targeting a defined antigen (e.g., GFP), where co-expression with the paired NLR signaling partner (e.g., Pikm-2) triggers immune activation. (e) Protease-triggered activation re-engineers autoactive NLRs by inserting a pathogen protease-cleavable blocking linker between functional domains, enabling precise activation exclusively upon pathogen-derived protease cleavage.

(B) Conceptual blueprint for programmable immune systems integrating engineered NLRs and PRRs with machine learning-guided receptor design and CRISPR-based genome editing, establishing a foundation for broad-spectrum, tunable, and climate-resilient immunity in crops.

To overcome this limitation, researchers have turned to decoy engineering. Kim et al. (2016) repurposed endogenous decoy proteins, such as PBS1, by inserting pathogen-specific protease cleavage sites. These engineered decoys activate pre-existing NLRs, such as RPS5, upon protease-mediated cleavage. This “trap-and-trigger” system broadens pathogen detection without modifying the immune receptor itself. However, its effectiveness depends on decoy–NLR compatibility and is not easily generalized across diverse species.

A related strategy involves modifying integrated domains within NLRs. In rice, the NLR RGA5 contains integrated heavy-metal-associated (HMA) domains that directly bind effector proteins. Structure-guided mutagenesis of these domains expanded recognition to non-cognate effectors, such as AVR-Pib (Liu et al., 2021). However, even optimized alleles failed to recognize all effector variants, highlighting the constraints of natural binding scaffolds.

To push beyond effector-based recognition, Kourelis et al. (2023) replaced the native HMA domain of Pikm-1 with nanobody domains that target the fluorescent protein GFP. When co-expressed with Pikm-2 and the corresponding antigen in Nicotiana benthamiana, these chimeras triggered a hypersensitive response, demonstrating that NLRs can be modularly reconfigured to detect synthetic antigens and initiate defense.

Protease-triggered immunity: Turning autoactivity into precision switches

A recent breakthrough has reframed NLR autoactivity from a potential drawback into a design feature. Liu and colleagues engineered NLRs whose activation is controlled by pathogen-derived protease cleavage (Wang et al., 2025a). By fusing autoactive NLR fragments to a protease-sensitive blocking polypeptide, they generated receptors that remain inactive until pathogen proteases cleave the blocking domain. This enables precise, pathogen-dependent activation, preventing unintended immune responses in the absence of infection. Importantly, by targeting conserved viral proteases, these synthetic NLRs can confer resistance against entire virus genera, such as Potyvirus and Potyviridae, achieving broad and durable resistance rarely seen with conventional R genes. In contrast to guard or decoy models, which rely on pathogen-induced perturbations of host proteins, protease-activated NLRs are engineered to directly detect pathogen-derived proteases, enzymes that are typically essential for virulence and host infection. Because these proteases are indispensable, pathogens face strong evolutionary constraints and cannot easily evade recognition without compromising their fitness.

A modular immune system: Toward programmable plant defense

Recent advances—including decoy engineering (Kim et al., 2016), integrated domain swapping (Liu et al., 2021), nanobody fusions (Kourelis et al., 2023), and protease-activated NLRs (Wang et al., 2025a)—collectively demonstrate that plant immunity can now be computationally designed. These developments point to a groundbreaking concept: plant NLRs can function as “plug-and-play sensor scaffolds” for customized pathogen detection. The modularization of NLR structure represents a major milestone in plant immune engineering, enabling fine-tuned control over receptor activation and signaling. Although these strategies remain rooted in the classical PTI–ETI framework, they increasingly blur the boundary between natural and synthetic immunity. Bioengineered NLRs show promise in overcoming evolutionary constraints, enabling tailored and adaptable immune responses. In the future, rational design of both recognition and activation modules will provide a foundation for engineering NLRs that are specific, adjustable, and durable. Optimization of auxiliary components may also be required to achieve the desired immune outcomes. For example, substitution of valine within the MHD motif can induce self-activation of many NLRs, generating a bioengineered “precursor” receptor. The autoactivation can be repressed by N-terminal peptide tags (e.g., FLAG, MYC, HA, or GFP) within the recognition module, effectively converting the receptor into a conditional switch that activates only upon pathogen detection.

While significant attention has focused on NLRs, recent studies highlight the engineering potential of PRRs, which detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) at the cell surface. Li et al. (2025b) and Zhang et al. (2025) demonstrated that the extracellular LRR domain of the PRR FLS2 can be reprogrammed through structure-guided mutagenesis and domain shuffling to recognize previously evasive flg22 variants. These findings extend modular design principles to PRRs, further blurring the line between innate and synthetic plant immunity.

Future perspectives

Plant immunity is entering a synthetic era. While recent advances allow immune receptors to be computationally designed, precisely edited, and rationally engineered, this field remains in an early stage. Key challenges persist, including limited accuracy in predicting receptor–effector interactions, incomplete control over receptor activation thresholds, and insufficient understanding of how engineered components interact with native immune signaling pathways. A major hurdle is ensuring the durability of engineered receptors in field conditions while minimizing fitness penalties (e.g., reduced yield or growth in the absence of infection) and addressing regulatory considerations for the deployment of genetically modified crops. Translating laboratory innovations into agronomically viable solutions will require extensive evaluation across diverse genetic backgrounds and environments. Nevertheless, machine learning approaches are beginning to facilitate receptor design by identifying functional motifs and predicting structure–function relationships, while CRISPR-based tools enable targeted genomic integration (Li et al., 2025a). Moreover, upstream open reading frame-mediated translational control and inducible promoter systems can modulate immune activation intensity (Xu et al., 2017). Together, these advances support the use of engineered NLRs and PRRs as foundational elements for constructing next-generation immune circuits. The next frontier is to engineer synergy between immune layers by integrating modified receptors into synthetic signaling circuits and coupling them to synthetic adaptors or transcriptional modules. Such integrated circuits would enable fine-tuned, spatiotemporal regulation of immune responses, creating systems that are programmable, broad-spectrum, and resilient to evolving pathogens and climate pressures (Figure 1B). This shift reframes immune specificity from a slow, natural co-evolutionary process to a tunable design parameter, marking the beginning of a new era in plant immune engineering.
