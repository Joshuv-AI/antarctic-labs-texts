Functional and Radiological Outcomes of Resurfacing Capitate Pyrocarbon Implantation after Proximal Row Carpectomy in Advanced Wrist Osteoarthritis
Authors: Jordan Bruyneel, Antonia Gkotsi, Afarine Madani, Wissam El Kazzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to assess the functional outcomes of Resurfacing Capitate Pyrocarbon Implant (RCPI) placement and to analyze postoperative radiological progression, with particular focus on its clinical relevance, the discrepancy between imaging findings and patient-reported outcomes, and the differences observed between manual and office workers.Methods:This retrospective study included 18 patients who underwent Resurfacing Capitate Pyrocarbon Implant (RCPI) placement following proximal row carpectomy between 2013 and 2018. All surgeries were performed by the same surgeon through a dorsal approach under general anesthesia or axillary nerve block. Standardized steps included posterior interosseous nerve neurectomy, Berger capsulotomy, and capitate preparation with implant sizing based on the lunate fossa. Postoperatively, patients were immobilized for two weeks and then began physiotherapy. Clinical (VAS, ROM, grip strength, DASH, MMWS) and radiological assessments were performed during a mean follow-up of 34 months.Results:Eighteen wrists were treated with RCPI following proximal row carpectomy. Five cases were excluded due to loss to follow-up, death, or revision surgery. Thirteen patients were clinically reassessed after a mean follow-up of 34 months. Pain scores improved significantly (mean VAS 2.2 ± 1.7, p < 0.05), and patients maintained approximately 70% of the contralateral grip strength. Mean DASH and Modified Mayo Wrist Scores were 21.9 and 67.7, which indicated functional recovery in most cases. Radiological review of 17 wrists demonstrated progression of osteoarthritic changes with increased radiolucency in 11, which stabilized after one year. Only two patients reported persistent pain, and one required implant removal following trauma.Conclusion:Despite progressive osteoarthritic changes observed such as osteophyte formation and widening of the radiolucent line around the implant radiographically, most patients reported satisfactory clinical outcomes. This clinical and radiological discrepancy highlights the functional benefits of the procedure and supports the role of pyrocarbon interposition implants as a motion-preserving alternative in advanced wrist osteoarthritis.Level of Evidence: Level II, Level IV, Therapeutic Study.

Introduction

Proximal row carpectomy (PRC) is an established treatment for wrist arthritis in the absence of lesions of the proximal pole of the capitate (Scapholunate or Scaphoid Non-Union Advanced collapses I and II),1 otherwise eventual friction between an arthritic capitate surface and the distal radius would cause persistent pain, which is why surgeons have historically preferred arthrodesis procedures in such cases. However, today’s patients are very reluctant to sacrifice wrist motion, despite the strength and stability offered by the arthrodesis, when presented with the available therapeutic options.

The Resurfacing Capitate Pyrocarbon Implant (RCPI) is a stemmed pyrocarbon implant developed to expand the indications for PRC by replacing the proximal pole of the capitate.2 Its rotational stability is provided by a 15° angulation between the base and the stem of the implant that is inserted in the capitate. The aim of this study was to evaluate the functional outcomes of RCPI implantation and to analyze the radiological evolution after surgery, with particular attention to its clinical impact, the discordance between imaging and patient-reported results, and the differences observed between manual and office workers.

Material and methods

Approval was obtained from the University of Brussels ethics committee to contact and clinically re-evaluate all patients who had undergone RCPI implantation. (Ref: P2019/090/NA, Date: 15/03/2019)). In addition, authorization was granted to access and review all radiographs acquired during patients’ follow-up after implantation. A detailed study objectives sheet was given to each patient, and patients signed the informed consent form.

Between 2013 and 2018, 18 RCPI implantations for wrist arthritic lesions of various etiologies were performed in 17 patients by the same surgeon. Their demographic data and affection characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Patients were operated either under general anesthesia or axillary nerve block, according to their preference. Through a dorsal approach, after liberation of the extensor pollicis longus and identification of the fourth compartment of extensors, neurectomy of the posterior interosseous nerve was systematically performed as proximally as possible. Berger capsulotomy3 allowed for articular access and the PRC was then carried out, taking care not to be traumatic to the anterior capsule. Evaluation of the capitate took place to confirm the indication of the implantation.

Preparation of the capitate was performed, making use of implants according to the best fitting size. Size choice is based on the size of the equivalent lunate fossa on the radius. Attention was paid to the orientation of the osteotomy of the capitate, which has to be parallel to the lunate fossa of the radius and not distally to the level of the proximal border of the hamatum. The implant’s stem was aligned with the axis of the third metacarpal. Closure of the capsule was frequently accompanied by metallic anchor reinsertion to the radius (micro-corkscrew anchor 2.2 × 4 mm), in case soft tissues were of lower quality for direct suture.

Patients were immobilized for 2 weeks in a palmar cast, followed by rehabilitation by a physical therapist. During the postoperative evaluation, the range of motion of the wrist in all axes was measured bilaterally, both actively and passively. Grip strength (GRASP) was measured using the Jamar dynamometer with the wrist in position 2, elbow against the body, flexed to 90°. Three measurements were taken for each side alternately, and the average of the 3 GRASP measurements was taken into consideration.

Patients were asked to quantify their pain on a visual analog scale (VAS) (0-10), complete a Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand questionnaire (DASH questionnaire), and express themselves in a 3-level post-operative satisfaction scale (very satisfied-satisfied-not satisfied). The Modified MAYO Wrist Score (MMWS) was also calculated. High scores on the DASH questionnaire indicate a high level of disability, and vice versa on the MMWS questionnaire. Radiologic review assessed the osteoarthritic evolution, implant positioning, as well as consistency of the surgical technique.

Friedman and Wilcoxon tests were used to compare preoperative and postoperative VAS. For range of motion (ROM) and strength measurements, the Mann–Whitney U-test was applied to assess differences, primarily between blue-collar and white-collar workers. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare satisfaction. No adjustments for multiple comparisons were applied, as only 2-group analyses were performed and the sample size was limited.

Results

Clinical results

From the 18 implantations, 2 patients were lost from follow-up and 1 patient died. Two patients were excluded from the clinical re-evaluation due to revision surgery. One was converted to total wrist arthrodesis due to persistent pain and functional impairment, whereas the other underwent implant removal due to high energy trauma shortly after surgery, leading to carpal fractures and dislocation of the implant.

Therefore, 13 implants were evaluated clinically, with a mean follow-up of 34 months. They showed a mean flexion of 40.3° (25°-50°), mean extension of 32.5° (25°-55°), mean ulnar deviation of 20.6° (12°-30°), mean radial deviation of 9.3° (2°-20°). Wrist extension and radial deviation were the directions mostly influenced, with an average difference of 43.3% and 45.5%, respectively, between the 2 (Table 2). The mean grip strength was 19.76 kg (4 kg-38 kg) for the operated side and 27.99 kg (13 kg-46 kg) for the “healthy” side. The patients kept 70.6% of their strength compared to the non-operated side (Table 3) knowing that 59% of patients had the surgery on their dominant side.

The mean VAS reported by patients was 2.19 (0-7). Total absence of pain was achieved in 3 patients (23.07%), 6 had tolerable pain (46.15%), and 4 had significant pain (46.15%). A statistically significant difference between preoperative and postoperative VAS (P < .05) was found. The mean postoperative DASH score was 21.92 and the MMWS was 67.69 (Table 3).

When separating the sample into subgroups of blue and white collars, the mean VAS values were 2.92 and 1.33 respectively; the DASH scores were 27.81 and 14.86; and the MMWS values were 63.57 and 72.5, respectively. Their differences were not statistically significant (P > .05). Review of the data according to gender or smoking habits reveals no differences between participants.

Most patients were very satisfied; 45% reported being very satisfied, 33% satisfied, and 22% not satisfied. A better satisfaction rate was present in the white-collar group compared to the blue-collar group, but the difference was not statistically significant. However, all the unsatisfied patients declared not willing to undergo further surgery, accepting their clinical status.

No intraoperative complications were reported. Postoperatively, 3 patients (16.66%) developed pericicatricial hypoesthesia, but symptoms resolved 6 months after the surgery.

Radiologic evaluation

All follow-up radiographs were reviewed, except for the 1 implant removed due to trauma recently after surgery. Therefore, 17 implants were available for analysis. All patients showed progression of osteoarthritic changes, including osteophyte formation, subchondral cysts, and loss of clarity of articular margins.

Rise of the radiolucency around the implant was evident in 11 implants (Figure 1), 8 of which presented a radiolucency measuring more than 1 mm (44%), but that was stable after the first year. However, only 2 of those were symptomatic, describing persistent pain in the wrist with daily activities, and these 2 cases corresponded to the dissatisfied patients among the 13 clinically evaluated.

In 6 out of 17 wrists (64.5%) “contouring,” was observed—an attenuation of the acute angles of the osteotomy—testifying friction with the implant due to its mobility and even migration of the implant and sinking. However, the mean VAS in these 6 patients was only 1.7/10. One distal protrusion was documented, although it remained asymptomatic and the patient considered himself satisfied (Figure 2).

One patient developed erosion of the distal radius surface, with proximal migration of the carpus toward the radius in a form of neoarticulation, remaining asymptomatic presenting the following evaluation: VAS 1, DASH 32, 70° of ROM in the flexion-extension axis and 32° regarding the radio-ulnar deviation (Figure 3).

Neither ulnar translation of the carpus nor rotation of the implant has been noticed.

Discussion

Proximal row carpectomy was an effective solution when it comes to wrist arthritis, but the presence of osteoarthritis of the proximal pole of the capitate is a contraindication for the procedure.2 Different techniques were proposed in the literature to overcome this limitation and allow PRC. Tang and Imbriglia4 were the first to describe osteochondral resurfacing for the capitate, to allow for the PRC to take place. Interposition arthroplasty was also proposed,5,6 with the use of dorsal capsule by Ilyas5 or fascia lata by Eaton.6 However, in the experience, soft tissue interpositions as well as cartilage patches failed with time and pain recurred. The alternative of capitate resurfacing in wrist arthritis by a pyrocarbon implant was introduced in 2011 by Goubier et al7and subsequent publications by Szalay,8 Marcuzzi,9,10 Giacalone11 demonstrated that it was a safe option, offering patients satisfaction and pain-free motion, regardless of the indication of the PRC.

The functional results of this limited series regarding mobility and strength were consistent with those in the literature.8,9,10,11 Notably, reduction of range of motion and strength did not have a major impact on their daily life, on the basis of an average DASH score of 21.9. There is a statistically significant improvement in the postoperative VAS score and high patient satisfaction with the surgery. The discrepancy between patient satisfaction and the objective results could be explained by the fact that the main complaint, pain, was largely resolved. However, systematic posterior interosseous neurotomy may have played an important role in pain reduction. Despite the absence of statistically significant differences between the subgroups, the results confirmed that resection of the proximal carpal row combined with the insertion of an RCPI implant provided only average results for blue-collar workers compared to those of white-collar workers. Inevitably, the lack of stability undermined force transmission, resulting in pain and worse DASH scores.

When interpreting the radiologic evolution in time, it should be kept in mind that pyrocarbon prostheses are interposition implants. They are used to fill the residual space after bone resection, and bone integration is not expected. Consequently, even stemmed pyrocarbon implants, such as the RCPI, exhibited some mobility. In the objective to reduce rotational mobility, the RCPI implant has an angulation between the head and the stem of the implant, but micromotion was inevitably present.

A 0.3 mm radiolucent peri prosthetic border was anticipated, corresponding to the radiolucent pyrolytic carbon layer.12 In their series of 5 patients, Szalay et al,8 reported 2 patients experiencing implant instability, but they were asymptomatic. Marcuzzi et al9 also observed 1 case of implant instability. Ross et al13 described that there is an increase in the radiolucent line associated with peri prosthetic sclerosis of the bone in small joint pyrocarbon implant placements. This radiolucent line did not exceed 1 mm and stabilized within the first postoperative year in 80% of cases, whereas in 15% it continued to increase, exceeding 1 mm before stabilizing after 2 years. This continuous realignment, depending on the use of the joint, would induce a mechanically adequate joint. Only 5% of the pyrocarbon implants demonstrated significant bone fixation without an increase in the radiolucency line. Ross et al13 reported similar findings with radiolucent lines <1 mm in 80% of cases within 1 year, and >1 mm in 15% after several years, as if realignment had induced a mechanically adequate joint. These elements were also observed in the series, but the proportion of radiolucent line measuring more than 1 mm is higher (44%). This high rate may have been explained by the higher number of blue-collar workers still actively working at the time of the surgery. The greater forces passing through the axis of the capitate bone during manual work likely contributed to more frequent and more significant micromovements of the implant that will ultimately create this peri prosthetic osteolysis chamber.

In agreement with the findings of Marcuzzi et al,9 ossification around the head of the prosthetic and the protrusions had no significant clinical implications, and no reoperation was required, despite the discordance between imaging and functional status.

Despite the absence of statistically significant difference between blue and white collars, it was confirmed that manual workers did not perform as well as non-manual workers. Nowadays, patients find it difficult to accept the idea of loss of wrist motion and often refuse arthrodesis procedures. According to the data, manual workers tolerated PRC with RCPI implantation reasonably well, provided that their manual work was not excessively heavy.

In summary, RCPI implantation after PRC provided meaningful functional recovery with acceptable strength and preserved mobility. Most patients reported satisfaction with little or no pain in daily life, even in the presence of radiographic progression such as radiolucent lines, implant migration, or protrusion. This clinical–radiological discordance represents a key finding of the study and supports the role of RCPI as a valuable motion-preserving alternative to wrist arthrodesis in selected patients, particularly those reluctant to sacrifice wrist motion

This study has some limitations. The main ones are the relatively small sample size, the retrospective design, and the absence of a control group, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Nevertheless, the systematic inclusion of all operated patients and the consistent surgical technique strengthen the reliability of the results.
