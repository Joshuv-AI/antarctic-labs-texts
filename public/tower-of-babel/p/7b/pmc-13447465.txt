Eosinophilic Ascites: An Underrecognized Entity in Peritoneal Disorders
Authors: Rishi Chowdhary, Zainab Awan, Param Darpan Sheth, Ibrahim Reyaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEosinophilic ascites (EA) is a rare, under-recognized condition characterized by eosinophil-rich peritoneal fluid, most frequently associated with eosinophilic gastroenteritis (EGE). Its true prevalence is unclear, often misdiagnosed as malignancy, tuberculosis, or cirrhosis. EA typically presents between ages 30 and 50, with slight male predominance. Its clinical importance lies in a favorable response to corticosteroids and the potential for severe complications such as intestinal obstruction if untreated.ObjectiveThis review consolidates current knowledge on EA, focusing on its etiology, pathophysiology, diagnostic challenges, and treatment strategies, while highlighting key knowledge gaps.MethodsA narrative review was conducted using PubMed, SpringerLink, and Web of Science (2000–2025). Search terms included “eosinophilic ascites,” “eosinophilia,” and “ascitic fluid.” Included studies reported histopathologically confirmed EA with ascitic eosinophilia > 10%, imaging data, and treatment outcomes. Case reports lacking confirmatory paracentesis or biopsy were excluded. Data on demographics, clinical presentation, fluid analysis, imaging, etiology, treatment, and outcomes were extracted independently by two reviewers.ResultsEA pathogenesis involves IL-5-driven eosinophilic infiltration of the gastrointestinal serosa. Diagnostic features include exudative ascites (protein > 2.5 g/dL) with eosinophilic predominance (60–90%), peripheral eosinophilia (85%), and CT evidence of bowel wall thickening (72%). Corticosteroids (0.5–1 mg/kg/day) achieve remission in 80% within two weeks; anti-IL-5 agents may benefit refractory cases. Significant gaps include absent diagnostic biomarkers and unknown relapse rates.ConclusionEA demands heightened clinical suspicion in eosinophilic ascites. Future priorities include the development of non-invasive diagnostics, trials comparing biologics with steroids, and the exploration of genetic mutations in refractory disease.

Introduction

Eosinophilic ascites is a rare clinical condition characterized by the accumulation of eosinophil-rich fluid within the peritoneal cavity. This phenomenon is most commonly associated with eosinophilic gastroenteritis (EGE), an uncommon disorder first described by Kaijser in 1937, which involves eosinophilic infiltration of the gastrointestinal tract in the absence of known causes such as parasitic infection, malignancy, or drug reactions [1].

EGE can present across all age groups, with a peak incidence between 30 and 50 years, and may exhibit a slight male predominance [2]. Allergy might contribute to the recruitment of eosinophils to the digestive tract, as multiple studies have found that approximately 50% of patients with EGE have a prior history of atopy. After an initial trigger, activated tissue eosinophils release various chemoattractant cytokines, which in turn attract additional eosinophils to the affected tissues [3]. Although this is not always the case, this syndrome is often associated with elevated serum eosinophil and peripheral blood eosinophilia, as well as elevated serum IgE levels. It is believed that a combination of environmental and genetic factors contribute to the development of eosinophilic gastrointestinal diseases (EGIDs). Patients frequently present with nonspecific symptoms such as abdominal pain, distension, and recurrent ascites, reflecting the heterogeneous clinical spectrum described in observational studies [4, 5]. 10% of EGID patients have a significant family history of the illness [6].

Studies from the Middle East and North Africa have shown that eosinophilic ascites (EA) is often underdiagnosed in both high- and low-resource settings. This highlights the global importance of improving awareness and diagnostic practices. In many low- and middle-income countries, limited knowledge of EA and restricted access to appropriate diagnostic tools may increase the risk of delayed diagnosis, complications, and unnecessary financial burden. Therefore, simple and cost-effective diagnostic approaches that fit local healthcare contexts are essential [3, 7].

Among the 171 published EA cases included in the systematic review, 127 patients (74%) were reported to have eosinophilic gastroenteritis (EGE) as the underlying cause of ascites. In region-specific cohorts, such as those from Egypt, all identified EA cases were ultimately attributed to EGE after comprehensive evaluation and exclusion of alternative etiologies [3]. The key components of treatment are elimination diets and steroids, which in up to 90% of cases result in total remission of symptoms, endoscopic, and radiographic findings; however, some individuals experience a chronic remitting course [8]

Despite its dramatic therapeutic responsiveness and potential for misdiagnosis, EA remains an under-recognized cause of ascites. This review synthesizes current evidence to enhance clinical recognition, clarify diagnostic criteria, and explore evolving management strategies. The purpose of this narrative review is to provide an up-to-date review of eosinophilic ascites, including the etiology, pathophysiology, clinical features, and management of eosinophilic ascites, and to describe gaps in our knowledge and unmet needs.

Pathophysiology

The pathogenesis of eosinophilic ascites involves the infiltration of eosinophils into the serosal layer of the gastrointestinal tract, leading to inflammation and the exudation of eosinophil-rich fluid into the peritoneal cavity. Nearly 74% of reported cases are attributable to eosinophilic gastroenteritis [9]. Patients may present with nonspecific symptoms such as abdominal pain, distension, nausea, vomiting, and weight gain. Peripheral eosinophilia is frequently observed but is not a consistent finding [1]. Endoscopic findings in eosinophilic gastroenteritis (EGE) lack both sensitivity and specificity. Observed abnormalities may include hyperemic mucosa of the stomach and duodenum, esophageal trachealization, thickened gastric folds, friable or nodular mucosa, coarse or rough areas, whitish specks, erosions, and superficial ulcers. However, a normal endoscopic appearance has also been documented in some cases. In a prospective single-center study in Egypt, Medhat et. al. 2024, endoscopic findings revealed gastroduodenitis in 52.9% of cases, duodenitis in 23.5%, and normal mucosa in 17.6%. Additional histopathological features of eosinophilic gastroenteritis include the presence of intraepithelial eosinophils and eosinophilic infiltration within Peyer’s patches. Extracellular deposition of eosinophil major basic proteins (MBPs) has also been observed in some cases, potentially indicating more extensive structural damage. Other documented findings include villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, crypt abscesses, and evidence of epithelial degeneration or regeneration [3].

Clinical Presentation

Eosinophilic ascites present with a heterogeneous array of gastrointestinal and systemic symptoms, as shown in Fig. 1, among which abdominal pain is one of the most frequently reported. Approximately 47% of affected individuals experience pain, which is typically described as cramping or diffuse discomfort, and is often localized to either the upper or lower abdominal quadrants [10]. Approximately 47% of affected individuals experience abdominal pain, yet this percentage is derived from a single prospective cohort study of 17 patients, where 8 reported pain [11]. Several other studies, including small case series and multiple case reports, have also described abdominal pain as a frequent symptom in eosinophilic ascites, but these reports do not provide consistent prevalence figures, making cross-study comparisons challenging [12, 13]. The pain is usually exacerbated by abdominal distension or food intake, and while its severity may vary, it is generally less intense than the pain associated with acute abdominal emergencies such as pancreatitis [13]. On physical examination, mild tenderness may be noted—predominantly in the epigastric or lower abdominal regions—yet the absence of classic peritoneal signs such as guarding or rigidity is suggestive of a non-inflammatory etiology [14]. Signs and symptoms of eosinophilic ascites

As the disease progresses, abdominal distension becomes a prominent clinical feature, reported in approximately 23–29% of cases [4]. Five patients (29%) in the 2024 prospective cohort of 17 individuals exhibited abdominal distension, whereas other small case series reported a lower prevalence (~ 23%), highlighting variability in symptom frequency across studies and suggesting that cohort size, patient selection, and reporting methods may influence observed rates of distension in eosinophilic ascites [15]. Patients frequently describe a progressive sensation of bloating or fullness, which tends to intensify in the supine position. On physical examination, the abdomen often appears visibly distended, and palpation may reveal a firm or tense quality due to the underlying ascitic fluid [16]. Shifting dullness on percussion—a classic diagnostic sign—further corroborates the presence of ascites [17]. The extent of distension generally correlates with the volume of accumulated fluid and serves as a critical indicator of disease progression. Importantly, hepatosplenomegaly is typically absent in eosinophilic ascites, effectively ruling out hepatic or splenic etiologies commonly associated with ascitic presentations, such as cirrhosis or malignancy [9, 16]. Bowel sounds are generally present and audible, though they may be reduced in cases with concurrent intestinal involvement [18]. This finding is in contrast to the hypoactive or absent bowel sounds observed in ascites of hepatic origin or in conditions complicated by mechanical bowel obstruction [7].

Gastrointestinal symptoms are commonly reported and contribute significantly to the overall clinical burden. Nausea and vomiting are frequent complaints; vomiting in these cases is usually non-bilious and non-projectile, suggesting a non-obstructive pathology [6]. Nausea has been reported in 38–52% of patients across multiple studies, including cohorts of 28 patients. Vomiting, typically non-bilious and non-projectile, has been observed in 22–41% of cases, reinforcing its characterization as a non-obstructive manifestation [10, 16]. Diarrhea, often alternating with constipation, reflects variable involvement of different segments of the gastrointestinal tract and adds complexity to the clinical picture [9]. In chronic cases, symptoms such as early satiety and weight loss are notable, often resulting from malabsorption due to eosinophilic infiltration [18]. In some instances, the clinical presentation may resemble that of intestinal obstruction, with a constellation of symptoms including nausea, vomiting, distension, and constipation [8]. However, patients with eosinophilic ascites typically respond well to conservative management, which helps distinguish this condition from true mechanical obstructions or malignancy-associated strictures [9].

Beyond the gastrointestinal system, systemic symptoms may also occur, albeit less frequently. Fever is present in only about 3% of cases and generally reflects underlying inflammation [9]. Fatigue is more commonly reported and is likely attributable to the chronic inflammatory milieu or the effects of nutrient malabsorption [9]. Occasionally, cutaneous manifestations such as rashes may be observed, particularly in patients with a history of allergic diseases or concomitant eosinophilic gastroenteritis [9]. These dermatologic findings, often associated with atopic conditions like asthma or eczema, are relatively rare but can serve as additional diagnostic clues [9]. Mucocutaneous examination is usually unremarkable, although pallor suggestive of anemia may be observed in individuals with chronic gastrointestinal involvement or malabsorptive symptoms [14, 18]. The absence of peripheral stigmata of chronic liver disease such as jaundice or peripheral edema further supports a non-hepatic origin of ascites in these patients and assists in differentiating eosinophilic ascites from hepatic or cardiac causes [14].

Laboratory Findings

Laboratory evaluation in eosinophilic ascites, as shown in Table 1, reveals characteristic findings that support the diagnosis and assist in excluding other etiologies. Peripheral eosinophilia is a common initial finding, with reports documenting marked elevations such as 9,460 eosinophils/mm3 without abnormalities in other cell lines [19], and eosinophil percentages reaching 49.6% [20]. These increases are often accompanied by elevated serum IgE levels ranging from 971 IU/mL [13] to 1,083 IU/mL [18] —suggesting an allergic or immune-mediated mechanism [20]. Paracentesis with detailed ascitic fluid analysis is the essential first diagnostic step in eosinophilic ascites, as it directly demonstrates eosinophil-rich exudative fluid and helps exclude more common causes such as cirrhosis, malignancy, or infection [21, 22]. Laboratory investigations play a supportive but essential role in the diagnostic evaluation of eosinophilic ascites. Peripheral eosinophilia is reported in 60–80% of patients, although this finding is neither universal nor specific, as emphasized in both earlier case series and recent prospective analyses [23, 24]. The fluid typically exhibits eosinophilic predominance, comprising 60–90% of white blood cells [18–20] Additional cellular components may include neutrophils, histiocytes, and mesothelial cells, though cultures are consistently negative for pathogens, and cytological analysis does not reveal malignant cells [19] The protein content of the fluid is elevated, e.g., 4.8 g/dL, indicating an exudative process [27]. ADA levels may be increased but remain below thresholds indicative of tuberculosis [19]. Characteristic laboratory and diagnostic findings in eosinophilic ascites

Bone marrow examination may show eosinophilic hyperplasia, with cases reporting up to 20% eosinophils [33]. Peripheral smears similarly reveal marked eosinophilia up to 60%, without atypical morphology, favoring a reactive over neoplastic etiology [33, 38]. Extensive testing is required to exclude alternative causes. Parasitic infections (e.g., filariasis, giardiasis, amoebiasis) are ruled out via stool and serological tests, which are typically negative. Autoimmune conditions such as celiac disease and vasculitis are excluded by negative serologies (anti-tTG, ANA, ANCA) [26, 31]. Tumor markers (e.g., ACE, CA 19–9) are usually within normal limits, reducing suspicion for malignancy-related hypereosinophilia [26]. Tuberculosis testing, including PCR and cultures, is uniformly negative [31, 39]. Other supportive findings include mild, transient elevations in liver transaminases (ALT, AST) [30], elevated CRP [31], and occasionally a mild restrictive ventilatory pattern on spirometry, reflecting possible allergic involvement [31]. ESR is generally normal or only mildly elevated. Elevated IgE levels, as previously noted, further reinforce the immunologic basis of the condition [31].

Imaging Findings

Abdominal ultrasound is typically the initial imaging modality employed in the evaluation of ascites, as shown in Table 2. In cases of eosinophilic ascites, it commonly reveals moderate to large volumes of free fluid. Notably, ultrasound generally demonstrates an absence of significant organomegaly such as hepatosplenomegaly, which aids in distinguishing eosinophilic ascites from other etiologies, including portal hypertension and cirrhosis. While effective in detecting ascitic fluid, ultrasound provides limited detail regarding gastrointestinal tract involvement or inflammatory changes [32]. Imaging contributes complementary diagnostic value; ultrasound and CT typically reveal free fluid, bowel wall thickening, or mesenteric edema, but these findings lack specificity and mainly serve to rule out cirrhosis, malignancy, or mechanical obstruction (19,29). Computed tomography (CT), particularly with contrast enhancement, serves as a pivotal diagnostic tool in eosinophilic ascites. CT imaging typically demonstrates ascitic fluid accumulation of moderate to large volume [39]. A hallmark feature is diffuse, circumferential bowel wall thickening, especially in the jejunal and ileal segments, suggestive of an inflammatory or infiltrative process consistent with eosinophilic gastroenteritis [26]. Additional findings may include mesenteric lymphadenopathy, supporting diagnoses such as eosinophilic enteritis or gastroenteritis [39]. Peritoneal fat stranding, the presence of halo signs, and enhanced bowel loops further indicate serosal inflammation [27]. Fluid collection in perihepatic and perisplenic regions, as well as between bowel loops, is frequently observed and reinforces the diagnosis [38]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), particularly MRI enterography, is increasingly utilized in instances where CT results are inconclusive or more detailed bowel imaging is required. MRI findings typically parallel those of CT, including bowel wall thickening, ascites, and peritoneal fat stranding [26]. MRI offers superior soft tissue contrast, facilitating more precise visualization of mesenteric structures, which may exhibit inflammatory changes. In some cases, MRI may demonstrate localized inflammation of the terminal ileum, consistent with eosinophilic involvement [26]. Endoscopic procedures, including gastroscopy and colonoscopy, are instrumental in excluding alternative gastrointestinal pathologies. Although direct visualization rarely yields findings specific to eosinophilic ascites, gastroscopy may occasionally reveal mild antral gastritis [17]. Similarly, colonoscopy may show nonspecific features such as mild proctitis or colitis, which are not pathognomonic and may be seen in various inflammatory conditions.[17]As such, endoscopy plays a complementary diagnostic role by ruling out other potential causes of gastrointestinal symptoms. In certain cases, thoraco-abdominopelvic CT imaging may be warranted to assess for systemic involvement. These broader imaging studies can detect associated findings such as pleural effusions, which, although not directly diagnostic of eosinophilic ascites, may support a systemic inflammatory process [28].

Histopathological Findings

Histologically, eosinophilic ascites is defined by the presence of eosinophils in the ascitic fluid, often in conjunction with eosinophilic infiltration of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. In eosinophilic gastroenteritis—commonly associated with eosinophilic ascites—biopsies typically demonstrate eosinophilic infiltration across multiple segments of the GI tract, including the duodenum, ileum, colon, and esophagus [18–20, 26]. In one reported case, endoscopic biopsies revealed dense eosinophilic infiltration in the duodenum and ileum, with counts exceeding 50 eosinophils per high-power field (HPF), while the esophagus and colon showed lower but still notable levels (approximately 10 eosinophils/HPF) [18]. Histological analysis of ascitic fluid confirmed eosinophilic predominance, characterized by typical granules and bilobed nuclei, establishing the eosinophilic nature of the ascites. These findings suggest that eosinophilic involvement extends beyond the mucosa to the peritoneal cavity [27]. In some instances, duodenal biopsies also reveal focal villous atrophy and marked eosinophilic infiltration, with eosinophils forming sheets and penetrating the glandular epithelium, suggesting a role in tissue injury and altered permeability—key features of eosinophilic gastroenteritis and its associated ascites [19].

Additional histological features may include nonspecific chronic inflammation, such as lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates and lamina propria edema. Mild eosinophilic infiltration, particularly in the terminal ileum, has also been reported [25]. When uncertainty persists, histological confirmation through endoscopic or peritoneal biopsy remains critical, especially in patients with suspected eosinophilic gastroenteritis. Across both historical and recent studies, dense eosinophilic infiltration of the gastrointestinal tract remains the hallmark feature distinguishing EA from other exudative ascites [30]. These findings underscore the heterogeneity of eosinophilic infiltration across GI segments and its central role in the pathophysiology of eosinophilic gastrointestinal disorders [29]. Bone marrow biopsy is integral to diagnostic evaluation. In many cases, marrow aspiration reveals elevated eosinophils and precursors, consistent with an eosinophilic disorder [20, 25]. However, eosinophilic leukemia and hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) are typically excluded by the absence of clonal proliferation or overt malignancy [13]. These marrow findings help confirm the diagnosis of eosinophilic gastroenteritis with eosinophilic ascites [20]. Endoscopic findings may be deceptively normal, despite substantial eosinophilic infiltration on histology. This discordance reinforces the necessity of tissue biopsy, as mucosal appearance does not reliably reflect the underlying pathology, particularly in patients with peripheral eosinophilia. Gastric and duodenal biopsies frequently demonstrate eosinophilic infiltration; in one study, 88.24% of patients exhibited such findings. Nevertheless, these are not exclusive to eosinophilic ascites and may occur in other eosinophilic GI disorders [7]. In rare cases, histological examination of the omentum has shown pronounced eosinophilic infiltration, indicating that inflammation may extend beyond the GI mucosa to serosal and peritoneal surfaces, thereby contributing to ascites formation [28].

Diagnosis and Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes a broad range of infectious, inflammatory, neoplastic, and autoimmune disorders, as shown in Fig. 2, all of which need to be carefully excluded through a combination of clinical, laboratory, and histopathological findings. Parasitic infections are often the first consideration in cases of eosinophilic ascites, particularly in endemic regions or for patients with a history of travel or occupational exposure. Although strongyloidiasis was initially suspected given the patient’s occupation as a farmer, it was ruled out via negative serological tests, stool examinations, and the absence of classic symptoms. Similarly, tests for filariasis and toxocara were negative, and the lack of granulomas or larvae in the ascitic fluid effectively excluded parasitic etiology [19, 26, 27]. Malignancy, especially peritoneal carcinomatosis or gastrointestinal lymphoma, was also excluded based on the absence of malignant cells in ascitic fluid, normal tumor markers, and negative gastrointestinal biopsies [13, 14, 18]. Furthermore, autoimmune diseases such as SLE, RA, and vasculitis were ruled out due to negative serologies (ANA, anti-DNA, ANCA) and a lack of systemic manifestations [18, 20, 25]. EGPA (Churg–Strauss syndrome) was also deemed unlikely given the negative ANCA tests and absence of asthma or peripheral neuropathy [14, 29].Fig. 2Differential diagnosis and final diagnosis

Infectious causes were considered, but abdominal tuberculosis was excluded via negative cultures, nonreactive skin tests, and biopsy results [13, 18, 28], while Helicobacter pylori was deemed an incidental finding [27]. Celiac disease was ruled out based on negative antibodies and normal biopsy features [14, 20]. Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) was similarly excluded as ileocolonoscopy showed no typical mucosal ulcers, revealing eosinophilic infiltration instead [18, 26]. Hypereosinophilic Syndrome (HES) was unlikely due to the absence of bone marrow proliferation and organ involvement [14, 26]. While food or drug hypersensitivity can present similarly, the patient’s history and response to therapy supported an immune-mediated condition [7, 27]. Consequently, the clinical presentation of eosinophilia, ascitic fluid eosinophilia, and histopathological findings pointed toward Eosinophilic Gastroenteritis (EG) as the diagnosis, a conclusion solidified by the patient’s favorable response to prednisone therapy [18, 19, 28].

Reaching a Diagnosis

The diagnosis of eosinophilic ascites is complex, as shown in Fig. 2, requiring a thorough process of differential diagnosis. In most cases, parasitic infections, autoimmune disorders, and malignancy are all excluded, and the diagnosis of eosinophilic gastroenteritis is confirmed based on clinical presentation, laboratory findings, imaging studies, and histopathological examination. The patient’s marked eosinophilia in both peripheral blood and ascitic fluid, along with histological evidence of eosinophilic infiltration in the gastrointestinal tract, is a key diagnostic feature. The rapid response to corticosteroid therapy further supports the diagnosis and rules out other potential causes, ultimately confirming the diagnosis of eosinophilic gastroenteritis. Management Strategies

Current Therapeutic Approaches

Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of treatment for idiopathic or autoimmune eosinophilic ascites (EA), as shown in Table 3. Prednisone, administered at 0.5–1 mg/kg/day, induces clinical remission in approximately 70–80% of patients within two weeks [30]. In cases of severe disease, intravenous methylprednisolone at doses of 40–60 mg/day is recommended [32]. Despite their efficacy, prolonged corticosteroid therapy is associated with significant adverse effects, including osteoporosis (17–30%), hyperglycemia (20–50%), and increased risk of infections [35, 36]. To mitigate relapse rates, a gradual tapering schedule over 8–12 weeks is advised [33]. In endemic regions, antiparasitic therapy is crucial. Albendazole (400 mg twice daily for three days) demonstrates up to 95% efficacy against Strongyloides infections [46], while ivermectin (200 µg/kg) is the preferred option in refractory cases [37, 38]. For schistosomiasis, praziquantel (40 mg/kg as a single dose) remains the first-line treatment [38]. Misdiagnosis of parasitic infections as idiopathic EA can result in inappropriate corticosteroid use, potentially exacerbating the underlying parasitic condition [39, 41]. Conclusion

Eosinophilic ascites (EA) is a rare but clinically significant manifestation of eosinophilic gastroenteritis, often under-recognized due to diagnostic challenges and underreporting, particularly in regions with prevalent parasitic or autoimmune conditions. Accurate and timely diagnosis is critical, as EA responds dramatically to corticosteroids, with 70–80% of patients achieving remission within two weeks. Current reliance on invasive paracentesis, limited understanding of pathophysiology, and restricted therapeutic options underscore the need for advancement. Future progress should focus on clarifying true prevalence through comprehensive epidemiological studies, elucidating underlying mechanisms—including cytokine pathways (IL-5, IL-13), genetic predispositions (FIP1L1-PDGFRA), and gut–peritoneal immune interactions—and developing non-invasive diagnostic biomarkers such as serum eosinophil-derived proteins and advanced imaging techniques. Additionally, randomized trials are warranted to optimize corticosteroid regimens and evaluate emerging biologics and novel agents, while standardized severity scoring and advanced monitoring approaches could guide treatment and prognostication. Multidisciplinary collaboration integrating clinical, mechanistic, and epidemiological research will be essential to improve patient outcomes and expand understanding of eosinophilic gastrointestinal and serosal disorders.
