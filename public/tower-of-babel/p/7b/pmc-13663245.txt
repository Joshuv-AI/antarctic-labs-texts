Oncocytic lipoadenoma of the parotid presenting as an unusual neck mass: a case report

Introduction

Oncocytic lipoadenoma is an extremely rare benign tumor of the salivary glands, characterized by a unique admixture of oncocytic epithelial cells and mature adipose tissue [1]. First described in 1998 by Hirokawa et al., fewer than 50 cases have been reported in the literature to date [2, 3]. The vast majority of these tumors occur in the parotid gland, with only rare cases documented in the submandibular gland [4, 5].

Clinically, patients typically present with a slow-growing, painless mass in the salivary gland region [6]. On imaging, oncocytic lipoadenomas generally appear as well-circumscribed lesions with heterogeneous signal intensity on MRI, reflecting the admixture of oncocytic and adipose components [7]. T1-weighted sequences often demonstrate areas of high signal intensity corresponding to the fatty elements, while T2-weighted images may show a more variable appearance depending on the proportion of oncocytic cells [8].

Histologically, these neoplasms are composed of sheets and nests of oncocytes interspersed with mature adipocytes in varying proportions [9, 10]. The oncocytic component resembles that seen in conventional oncocytomas, while the adipose tissue appears similar to normal fat. Additional features may include scattered ductal elements, sebaceous differentiation, and chronic inflammation [11].

The pathogenesis of oncocytic lipoadenoma remains unclear, with some authors proposing it represents an oncocytoma with extensive lipometaplasia, while others suggest it may be more closely related to sialolipoma [1]. Recent molecular studies have identified HMGA2 gene rearrangements in a subset of cases, similar to those seen in some pleomorphic adenomas and soft tissue lipomas [12].

We present a case of a 53-year-old male with an oncocytic lipoadenoma presenting as a large parapharyngeal mass adjacent to the deep lobe of the parotid gland and submandibular gland. This unusual location and size make our case particularly noteworthy, as it expands the known clinicopathological spectrum of this rare entity.

Case description

A 53-year-old Iranian man presented with a large mass-like lesion in the left deep parotid lobe extending to the parapharyngeal space. The patient reported a 7-year history of a slowly enlarging, painless swelling in the neck region. There were associated symptoms such as dysphagia, dyspnea and recent choking sensation.

Physical examination revealed a large mass-like lesion in left mandibular angle extending to submandibular area.

Imaging studies: US revealed a prominent solid mass measuring 9.5 × 1.3 cm.

MRI showed a giant 10 × 7.4 × 3.5 cm heterogeneous enhancing mass in the left deep lobe of parotid with extension in to the parapharyngeal space caused airway compressions (Fig. 1).

A Axial contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging showing a large, well-defined heterogeneous mass arising from the deep lobe of the left parotid gland with extension into the parapharyngeal space and marked airway compression. B Coronal contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging demonstrating the craniocaudal extent of the lesion and its mass effect on the adjacent pharyngeal structures

The patient underwent surgical excision of the mass. Due to the excessive size of the mass in the parapharyngeal space, the chosen surgical approach was through the oropharynx, and access to the parapharyngeal space was achieved after tonsillectomy. Intraoperatively, a well-circumscribed, lobulated mass measuring approximately 10 × 8 × 3.5 cm was identified and completely excised.

Gross examination:

The surgical specimen consisted of a cream brown well-defined multilobulated mass measuring 10 × 8 × 3.5 cm (Fig. 2). On sectioning, the cut surface revealed a soft texture with a light brown to yellow appearance. No areas of necrosis or hemorrhage were identified.

A lobulated well-defined yellow–light brown mass

Microscopic examination:

Histological examination revealed a well-circumscribed neoplasm composed of two distinct cell populations: oncocytes and adipocytes (Fig. 3). The oncocytic component consisted of sheets and nests of large polygonal cells with abundant granular eosinophilic cytoplasm and centrally located nuclei (Fig. 4). These cells were arranged in a trabecular and acinar pattern. Interspersed between the oncocytic areas were mature adipocytes, comprising approximately 60% of the tumor volume.

Oncocytic cells admixed with adipose tissue

High-power view showing trabecular and acinar arrangements of oncocytic cells with interspersed mature adipocytes (hematoxylin and eosin stain)

Based on these histopathological findings, a diagnosis of oncocytic lipoadenoma of the parotid gland with extension into the parapharyngeal space was made.

Discussion

Oncocytic lipoadenoma is an extremely rare benign tumor of the salivary glands, with only a few dozen cases reported in the literature, predominantly occurring in the parotid gland and characterized by a dual cell population of oncocytes and adipocytes. [13]. Our case presents several unique features that contribute to the growing body of knowledge about this uncommon neoplasm.

Firstly, the extension of the tumor in our case—the parapharyngeal space—is noteworthy. While the vast majority of reported oncocytic lipoadenomas are limited to the parotid gland [13–15], our case demonstrates that these tumors can also extend into parapharyngeal space. This atypical presentation causes diagnostic and surgical challenges, as it may mimic other more common parapharyngeal space tumors, such as pleomorphic adenomas or paragangliomas [13, 16].

Our case also contributes to the ongoing discussion about the histogenesis of oncocytic lipoadenoma. The presence of both oncocytic and adipocyte components has led to various theories about its origin. Some authors propose that it represents an oncocytoma with extensive lipometaplasia [17], while others suggest it may be more closely related to sialolipoma [18].

The differential diagnosis of oncocytic lipoadenoma includes several entities. Oncocytoma lacks the significant adipocytic component seen in oncocytic lipoadenoma [19]. Pleomorphic adenoma may show oncocytic change but typically has a more varied histological appearance with myxoid and chondroid areas [3, 20]. Lipomatous pleomorphic adenoma can be distinguished by the presence of myoepithelial cells and its characteristic immunoprofile [3, 20, 21]. Sialolipoma shows a predominance of mature adipose tissue with scattered normal salivary gland elements, lacking the sheets of oncocytes seen in our case. According to Torres-Parlange et al. case report lipoadenoma lacks glandular serous acini versus sialolipoma [22, 23].

Radiologically, oncocytic lipoadenomas present a diagnostic challenge due to their rarity and variable appearance on imaging studies. On CT, they may appear as well-circumscribed, heterogeneous masses with areas of fat attenuation [16]. MRI typically shows a heterogeneous mass with high signal intensity on T1-weighted images in areas corresponding to fat, while the oncocytic components may demonstrate intermediate signal intensity [8].

The treatment of choice for oncocytic lipoadenoma is complete surgical excision [23]. Despite its benign nature, careful followup is recommended due to the limited long-term data available on these rare tumors. In our case, at follow-up, the patient was considered for second surgery to excise the residual mass in parapharyngeal space.

Conclusion

This case of parotid oncocytic lipoadenoma with extension into the parapharyngeal space broadens the clinicopathological spectrum of this rare entity. It highlights the importance of considering this diagnosis in unusual locations and underscores the need for thorough histological examination. Further molecular and genetic studies may shed light on the pathogenesis of this intriguing neoplasm and its relationship to other salivary gland tumors.
