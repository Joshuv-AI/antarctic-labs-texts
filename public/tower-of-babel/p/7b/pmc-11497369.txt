Anaplastic transformation of metastatic papillary thyroid carcinoma lesion histologically mimicking a squamous cell carcinoma: A case report
Authors: Erwin Danil Yulian, Sugiarto, Ryna Zahrotul Martiana, Yuddi Wahyono, Maria Francisca Ham, Faisal Mohammad Rifqi Aqil
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe incidence of anaplastic transformation of thyroid cancer from Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma (PTC) is a rare tumor event, which ranges from 1 to 2 % of all cases of thyroid malignancy. Anaplastic transformation is often reported in primary lesions, but is rarely found in metastatic lesions from Differentiated Thyroid Carcinoma (DTC).Presentation of caseThis case report reports a unique tumor process where a 70-year-old woman had a metastatic lesion in the Mandible from PTC which transformed into ATC with histological features resembling squamous cell carcinoma-type. Immunohistochemical examination was able to prove that the lesion in the Mandible is a type of undifferentiated Thyroid carcinoma (anaplastic) with a squamous pattern originating from PTC.DiscussionThis type of tumor with histological variant images that resemble other types of tumors is a challenge for diagnosis and appropriate management, so understanding the variants of this type of ATC is important for better management.ConclusionAlthough rare, anaplastic transformation can occur in metastatic sites of PTC. This necessitates the need for early accurate diagnosis through IHC assessment and somatic mutation testing so that appropriate treatment can be delivered.Keywords: Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, Papillary thyroid carcinoma, Metastasis, Anaplastic transformation, Squamous differentiation

Introduction

Anaplastic Thyroid Carcinoma (ATC) is a variant of thyroid cancer with a relatively rare incidence but has the highest mortality rate with a one-year overall survival (OS) rate of around 20–50 % [1]. ATC covers only 1–2 % of all thyroid cancers and can develop de novo or from anaplastic transformation of other Thyroid tumors, such as Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma (PTC) [2]. The occurrence of anaplastic transformation is generally more often local to the primary tumor. Anaplastic transformations at the metastatic site are only reported in a few cases of ATC [3]. Anaplastic transformation from PTC to ATC in one patient is a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge for clinicians, especially how to detect the occurrence of such transformation and when to adjust management to handle different types of tumor. Here we present a unique case report, that has been reported in accordance with the SCARE criteria [4], involving a patient with PTC who developed metastasis to the mandible whereby an anaplastic transformation led to the development of ATC with squamous morphology at the metastatic site.

Presentation of case

A 70-year-old woman came with complaints of lumps and swelling in the right lower jaw since 7 months ago (Fig. 1A). Clinically, a 3 × 4 cm lump was found on the right side of the mandible, with a solid consistency, fixed, painless, and indistinct borders. Neck Computed Tomography (CT-Scan) showed a right mandibular mass with discontinuous bone and cortical destruction infiltrating the surrounding muscles (Fig. 1B).

Incisional biopsy of the mass showed histopathology of PTC, follicular variant mixed with columnar cell variant (Fig. 2). Thyroid Ultrasonography (USG) found a nodule of the right lobe measuring 1.3 × 1.4 cm. The mandibular mass was suggested to be a metastatic lesion of the smaller thyroid nodule.

Incisional biopsy of the right mandible shows a picture of papillary carcinoma of the thyroid with follicular variant (a) mixed with columnar cell variant (b) at 20× objective lens magnification.

The patient underwent thyroidectomy and segmental mandibulectomy with plate and screw, but refused the segmental mandibulectomy. Thyroid histopathology results confirmed the presence of Follicular Variant of Papillary Thyroid carcinoma (FVPTC) with mandibular metastasis. Postoperative Whole Body Scan (WBS) (Fig. 3) showed uptake in the Thyroid bed and right Mandible so I-131 radioablation/Radioactive Iodine (RAI) therapy was done for 7 cycles with a total of 780 mCi. However, WBS after cycle 6 and 7 ablation still showed uptake in the thyroid bed and mandible thus the patient was considered a refractory case.

Whole Body Scan (a) post Thyroidectomy (b) post sixth (c) and seventh I-131 therapy.

Due to insurance policy, the patient could not be given Lenvatinib so External Beam Radiation Therapy (EBRT) was performed on the metastatic site of the mandible and vertebral bones with a dose of 300 cGy/fraction, along with bisphosphonate therapy, and TSH suppression.

Since the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient did not show up for 2 years and then returned after new complaints of swelling in the right jaw. Laboratory examinations found an increase in Thyroglobulin (432.60 ng/mL), and Anti-Thyroglobulin (55.46 IU/mL). CT-Scan of the mandible showed tumor mass with destruction of the bone cortex that was larger than before. The CT-Scan also showed a mass inside the maxillary and ethmoidal sinus cavity initially thought to be an extent of the metastatic process. This was given a priority of treatment where the patient was treated by Functional Endoscopic Sinus Surgery (FESS). Biopsy confirmed the sinus mass to be Nonspecific Chronic Sinusitis. By this point the mandibular mass had grown to 10x5x6 cm so the patient was treated with mandibular resection.

Multiple partial sections of the resected mass, consisting of tissue from the center of metastatic lesion, right mentum, thyroid, and border of mandibular bone infiltration, was checked using Hematoxylin and Eosin Stain (H&E Stain) and Immunohistochemistry (IHC). In contrast to the previous findings, the H&E stainof the mandibular resection resembled a moderately to poorly differentiated non-keratinizing Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC), accompanied by structures of FVPTC, and lymphovascular invasion. IHCexamination showed Cytokeratin 5 (CK5) and p63 were only positive in the squamous differentiated component; Thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1) and Paired-box gene 8 (PAX-8) were positive in both the squamous differentiated and undifferentiated components; Thyroglobulin was positive in the well-differentiated area; and the Ki-67 proliferation index showed higher results in the undifferentiated area. The results of the IHC examination supported the histological features of ATCwith a squamous pattern in PTC (Fig. 4). The tissue sample did not show signs of vascular tumor emboli. IHC for BRAF was not routinely performed in this institution.

Follow up

One month post-mandibular resection, the mass grew to an inoperable state thus debulking and EBRT was performed. Despite initial response to EBRT, the mass continues transformation into a bleeding ulcer followed by metastatic lung nodules and pleural effusion (Fig. 5). The patient eventually succumbed to respiratory failure.

Discussion

PTC, a subtype of DTC, is the most prevalent form of thyroid cancer, accounting for approximately 90 % of cases [5]. FVPTC is the most commonly encountered variant, characterized by a lower metastatic potential compared to Follicular Thyroid Cancer (FTC). Overall, FVPTC demonstrates a favorable 10-year survival rate of 99.2 %; however, this rate significantly drops to 58.9 % in cases where metastasis occurs [6].

Although metastasis occurs in only about 2 % of FVPTC cases, its presence can significantly worsen the patient's prognosis. The lungs are the most common site of FVPTC metastasis (53.4 %), followed by bones (28.1 %) [7]. In DTC, bone metastases most frequently involve the spine (34.6 %), pelvis (25.5 %), sternum (18.3 %), and extremities (10.2 %) [8]. Mandibular metastasis of FVPTC is exceedingly rare, with an estimated incidence of 1 % among all bone metastases [9].

The management of this patient adhered to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) which suggests total thyroidectomy for PTC tumors >1 cm, particularly in cases with distant metastasis. The patient was initially planned to undergo a segmental thyroidectomy in hopes of removing the entirety of the mandible tumor but the patient declined. This might have contributed to the remains of the thyroid cell. To accommodate the left out cell, the patient also underwent RAI therapy and EBRT following confirmation of radioiodine uptake in the thyroid bed and metastatic sites [10]. The American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines recommend RAI as an adjuvant therapy after thyroidectomy for patients at high risk of recurrence, as in this case [11]. However, RAI therapy is often not curative in cases of bone metastases. Approximately 5–15 % of DTC cases, and 50 % of metastatic DTC cases, become refractory to RAI therapy [12].

The patient developed RAI-refractory disease, evidenced by the progression of metastatic lesions despite receiving cumulative RAI doses exceeding 600 mCi [13]. Recent studies suggest that genetic mutations, such as BRAF, RAS, NTRK, and RET oncogene mutations, may contribute to RAI resistance [14]. NCCN recommends somatic testing to identify mutations in rapidly progressing tumors, those with poor prognosis, or those that are life-threatening. Somatic mutation testing, such as BRAF via IHC, was not performed in this patient.

Patients with RAI-refractory disease should be managed with a combination of surgical palliation, EBRT, embolization, intravenous bisphosphonates, and systemic therapy [10]. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors, such as lenvatinib, are preferred, especially when molecular testing for mutation identification is unavailable. In the SELECT clinical trial, lenvatinib demonstrated a response rate of 64.8 % and a progression-free survival of 18.3 months, compared to 3.6 months with placebo [15].

A noteworthy finding in this case was the presence of a squamous variant of ATC alongside PTC in the same tissue. Without immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis, the histopathological presence of squamous differentiation could suggest a collision tumor involving SCC and PTC. However, IHC results revealed that the squamous differentiation in this case represented a variant of ATC [16]. Anaplastic transformation of PTC at the primary site has been reported in 14–90 % of DTC cases undergoing surgery, though it remains rare at secondary sites [17]. Reported sites of anaplastic transformation in metastatic locations include the retroperitoneum, lungs, pleura, mandible, and breast [3]. The BRAFV600E mutation is thought to be responsible for anaplastic transformation and is the most commonly identified mutation in PTC (60 %), followed by NRAS (8.5 %) and RET mutations (6.3 %) [18].

Anaplastic transformation in this patient presented a significant challenge for clinicians, as it remains unclear when to shift the management strategy from PTC to ATC. In our case, anaplastic transformation occurred five years after the initial diagnosis of PTC. On average, anaplastic transformation occurs 8.8 years after the diagnosis of PTC, with a range of 6 months to over 30 years [2]. The patient survived for six months after being diagnosed with ATC, ultimately succumbing to respiratory failure. This survival period is slightly shorter than the current literature, which reports an overall survival of approximately 0.67 to 1.31 years in ATC patients [19].

To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the second reported case [20] of squamous-type anaplastic transformation of PTC at a bone metastatic site. Similar to the previous case report, our case showed anaplastic transformation after undergoing post-op RAI. There also seems to be a pattern of clinically finding the metastatic lesion first before the main thyroid cancer in all the previous cases.

Given the rarity of this occurrence, the authors recommend heightened vigilance for atypical ATC presentations in FVPTC cases with metastasis. Early diagnosis of anaplastic transformation can be facilitated by IHC assessment and somatic mutation testing, when available, with the goal of guiding clinicians towards more appropriate mutation-targeted therapies.

Conclusion

While rare, anaplastic transformation can occur with atypical presentation in the metastatic site of FVPTC. IHC assessment and somatic mutation testing should be utilized when available for early diagnosis and prompt treatment.
