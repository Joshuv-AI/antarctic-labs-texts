Unravelling the key factors governing O2 evolution upon charging a reversible LiOH-based nonaqueous Li | |O2 battery
Authors: Linbin Tang, Zechun Lu, Zongyan Gao, Xuechun Lou, Junjian Li, Yujie Wen, Junchao Chen, Zhexuan Zhu, Shijing Luo, Lina Zhou, Guangfeng Wei, Zuofeng Chen, Hongying Zhao, Tong Li, Luming Peng, Fengting Li, Tao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Achieving a reversible four-electron-per-oxygen-molecule oxygen evolution reaction is an essential yet highly challenging task for nonaqueous lithium hydroxide-based Li | |O2 batteries, as the kinetically sluggish oxygen evolution reaction tends to entangle with competing parasitic reactions, whose origins and mitigating strategies remain largely elusive. Here we construct a highly reversible lithium hydroxide-based Li | |O2 battery using iron–cobalt–nickel layered double hydroxide catalysts and tetramethylene sulfone-based electrolytes. Lithium hydroxide decomposition toward oxygen evolution involves key reactive oxygen species of surface-bound hydroxyl and hydroxyl radicals, but no singlet oxygen. These hydroxyl species corrode the electrolyte and carbon support, predominantly accounting for charging irreversibility. While oxidation-resistant solvents and electrical conductors are necessary to reduce hydroxyl-induced side reactions, the synergistic interplay of interfacial water solvation and catalytic surface structures hold the key to steering hydroxyl activity toward the desirable oxygen evolution reaction or by-product formation. This work offers insights into achieving long-life lithium hydroxide-based Li | |O2 batteries.

Introduction

Nonaqueous lithium-air batteries based on lithium hydroxide (LiOH) formation and decomposition have garnered considerable research interest due to their distinct advantages1–6. First, these cells operate via four-electron-per-oxygen-molecule (4e−/O2) oxygen reduction/evolution reactions (ORR/OER), 4Li(s) + 2H2O(l) + O2 (g) ⇆ 4LiOH(s), possessing a high theoretical specific energy (Eo = 3.32 V, 3707 Wh/kg)7,8. The reactants, H2O and O2 ultimately come from the air that dissolve in a largely nonaqueous electrolyte. Second, the electrochemical LiOH formation involves few side reactions, with peroxides (HO2−/H2O2)9 being the key oxygen intermediates. This was largely ascribed to the preferential H-donor role of H2O to the reactive LiO2·/O2−· species, thereby circumventing their nucleophilic attack to organic solvents that is otherwise often observed in Li2O2-based systems. Thirdly, the LiOH electrochemistry exhibits high resistance to moisture and CO2 contamination from air10. This stability is a prerequisite for fully achieving the high energy in open lithium-air systems, as it eliminates the need for cumbersome O2 cylinders and purification auxiliaries. Despite these advantages, the development of long-life LiOH-based batteries has been seriously hindered by the poor reversibility of the LiOH decomposition reaction during recharging, typically evidenced by low OER Faradaic efficiencies (FE)11–13. This irreversibility is mainly caused by the highly insulating nature of bulk LiOH solid product and the kinetic barriers associated with activating the 4e−/O2 OER7,8,14.

Two notable strategies have been adopted to tackle these challenges. One involves using soluble catalysts or mediators in the electrolytes. These soluble species undergo redox processes and shuttle between the insulating LiOH product and conductive positive electrodes2,15–17, thereby providing effective electron/hole transfer pathways through electrolytes and circumventing the kinetically prohibited charge transfer via the LiOH bulk. O2 recovery rates, as high as 99.5% in the first cycle, have been reported1,2,17. Nevertheless, the O2 recovery rates tend to fade rapidly in the subsequent cycles, resulting in limited cycle life1,2,17. The other strategy employs solid-state catalysts5,12,13,18 (Fig. 1a). In this case, water pre-existing in the wet electrolyte partially dissolves LiOH. Effectively, water-aided mass transport paths for LiOH are created in electrolytes so that the sluggish decomposition of the solid crystals is turned into catalyzed oxidation of solvated OH− species10. Indeed, the reported catalytic effect on LiOH decomposition is enormous, which could decrease its charging voltages from 4.5 V19 to as low as 3.3 V12,20. Notwithstanding the complete removal of LiOH during recharge, the corresponding O2 evolution often falls far below the ideal level for 4e−/O2 stoichiometry12,13,20, and in some cases no O2 evolution occurs at all. Clearly, regardless of the strategy in use, the issue of sluggish charge kinetics often intertwines with complex parasitic reactions that degrade the cell reversibility. Researchers have suggested that Li2CO3 is a major by-product during the charging of LiOH-based systems4,13,17. However, the origins of these side reactions remain largely elusive, not to mention quantitative analysis. For instance (Fig. 1a), what are the key reactive oxygen species (ROS) involved in the recharge of a reversible LiOH system? How do these species impact the battery chemistry, and how to better design the electrochemical interface to mitigate the deterioration connected to them? The lack of this fundamental knowledge severely limits the understanding and rational design of highly reversible battery systems7,8,22.

Here, we use oxyhydroxides, well-established efficient catalysts for the 4e−/O2 OER in alkaline systems23–26, as model catalysts, alongside an optimized electrolyte to construct a highly reversible nonaqueous Li | |O2 battery, and study the fundamental factors governing the reversibility of the charge reaction. Through in situ spectroscopies, we unravel that during LiOH decomposition, hydroxyl, *OH and ∙OH, act as the key reactive species, and singlet oxygen (1O2) is not generated, unlike that in Li2O2 decomposition process27–29. Using a multitude of quantitative methods assisted by isotopic labeling, we have unequivocally demonstrated that electrolyte oxidation and carbon corrosion by hydroxyl species (*OH and ∙OH) are the primary sources of parasitic reactions during recharge. We highlight that the suppression of hydroxyl reactivity that closely connects to the OER FE is governed by the interplay of interfacial properties: catalyst surfaces and water activity-tuned solvation serve as the dominant regulatory factors, with solvent type and carbon support as complementary protective strategies.

Results

Pathways toward more reversible systems

Before addressing the poor reversibility during charge, we first clarify the fundamental pathway of LiOH formation during discharge. How the typical Li2O2 formation via 2e−/O2 ORR in neat nonaqueous electrolytes is converted to LiOH formation via 4e−/O2 ORR in water-organic mixed electrolyte has been systematically studied8,14,30. In contrast to LiO2/O2− formation (Li+ + O2 + e− → LiO2), followed by chemical disproportionation to form Li2O2 (2LiO2 → Li2O2 + O2) in neat aprotic electrolyte, the presence of water molecules leads to formation of peroxide intermediates, HO2−/H2O2 (O2 + 2e− + 2H2O → H2O2 + 2OH− ↔ HO2− + H2O + OH−), in a water-organic mixed electrolyte9,31. An appropriate catalyst20 then promotes the chemical disproportionation of the peroxide intermediates into OH−/H2O and O2 (2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2; 2HO2− ↔ 2OH− + O2), completing the nominal 4e−/O2 ORR to generate LiOH. It is this synergy of both water and catalyst at the interface that realizes the LiOH-based electrochemistry. The absence of either factor would revert to the typical Li2O2-dominated formation. Since water is a reactant during discharge, its concentration in organic electrolyte should not be too low, which would limit cell capacity. The water content used here in the electrolyte is typically 5 vol%, unless specified otherwise.

Next, we move on to discuss cell recharge. To demonstrate the overall effects of cell components on the charge reversibility, a series of catalysts (carbon-supported ruthenium (Ru/SP) and layered double hydroxides (LDH) (Supplementary Figs. 1–5, Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Note 1, 2)), electrolytes (ethers, dimethyl sulfoxide, tetramethylene sulfone), water concentrations, and electrical conductors (amorphous Super P (SP) carbon, graphitic carbon, gold (Au)) have been investigated. Online electrochemical mass spectrometry (OEMS) was the first tool used, as it efficiently quantifies O2 consumption/evolution, thus directly revealing the reversibility. It was evident (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 3–5) that the aforementioned cell components can all impact on the O2 evolution during recharge. For example, in cells using a tetramethylene sulfone (TMS) electrolyte (Fig. 1b), switching the catalysts from Ru/SP to FeCoNi-LDH/SP resulted in an increase in the OER FE by 44%, while replacing the SP carbon with gold conductors led to another leap by 31%. In addition, we found that electrolyte solvent and water content are also important parameters need optimization. Overall, these results show that the OER FE can be significantly boosted from nearly zero to 75% by judiciously designing the cell components.

Quantifying side reactions

To understand the cell electrochemistry in more details, we then focused on a representative system made of FeCoNi-LDH/SP catalyst and TMS-based electrolytes. The discharge exhibited (Fig. 2a) a single plateau (4.1e−/O2) as expected for LiOH formation, whereas the recharge was more complex, having 3 distinct plateaus at ~3.65, 3.8 and 4.2 V. In situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Fig. 2b) suggested that LiOH was the only crystalline phase formed on discharge, which then decomposed at the first two charge plateaus. Along with LiOH decomposition, O2 evolution occurred (Fig. 2a), although it only started from the second plateau and ceased at the third, where CO2 evolved instead. OEMS assisted with 18O-isotope labeling by H218O (Fig. 2g) further confirmed that the released O2 (16O18O, 16O2, 18O2) during the second plateau at 3.8 V indeed stemmed from decomposition of LiOH (18O/16O scrambled). In addition, linear sweep voltammogram (LSV) coupled to OEMS measured on cells without preformed LiOH showed no O2 evolution below 4.5 V, but with electrolytic water splitting occurring at >5 V (Supplementary Fig. 6b). This observation further confirms that LiOH decomposition and water splitting are two distinct processes occurring at different potentials in the organic-water mixed electrolyte (Supplementary Note 2). Of note, the plateau at 3.65 V only emerged in the first charge, and was likely a result of surface reconstruction, typically observed during activation of LDH to form amorphous surface oxyhydroxides, FeCoNi(OH)2 + OH− → FeCoNi(O)OH + H2O + e− 32. This surface reconstruction was found irreversible. In contrast to the favored O2 evolution, undesirable CO2 evolved at the 3rd plateau at 4.2 V (Fig. 2a), indicating the decomposition of carbon-containing byproducts33, most commonly Li2CO334. To characterize other amorphous phases undetectable by XRD, we further performed chemical titration (Supplementary Figs. 8–10) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements at different states of charge (Supplementary Fig. 11a–d). The titration results (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 2) quantitatively showed that Li2CO3 gradually accumulated alongside with LiOH removal, and decomposed at the 3rd plateau. Consistent with the Li2CO3 accumulation, LiOH consumption during charging exceeds the theoretical stoichiometry (LiOH/e− = 1) (Fig. 2d). The extra LiOH demand is attributed to additional parasitic reactions that consumes LiOH beyond the desirable OER process. Other minor by-products, such as lithium sulfite (Li2SO3) and lithium oxalate (Li2C2O4) have also been detected by XPS and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) (Supplementary Figs. 11e, f). Nevertheless, in a heavily cycled cell, Li2CO3 was a primary component of the passivating film on positive electrodes that caused the cell failure (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Note 4). It is thus worth further investigating the origins of Li2CO3 formation.

In the current system, the potential carbon sources for Li2CO3 are SP carbon, the TMS electrolyte and perfluorinated hydrocarbon binders (PTFE), with the former two likely being more dominant (Supplementary Fig. 13). To unambiguously differentiate carbon corrosion from TMS decomposition, the carbon substrate was 13C-labeled (99% 13C-enrichment), while the TMS electrolyte remained 12C-labeled. The resulting 13CO2 signal due to carbon corrosion accounted for 65% of the total (Fig. 2e), whereas 12CO2 originated from the decomposition of electrolyte-derived Li2CO3 was just 35%. Replacing SP carbon with gold conductors, the amount of CO2 evolution was also suppressed by ~65% (Supplementary Fig. 14), highly consistent with 13C-labeled experiments. These results strongly suggest that carbon corrosion represents a major contributor to the overall side reactions during charge.

Furthermore, stepwise titrating the 13C carbon-labeled electrodes at different states of charge, we observed that Li212CO3 rapidly accumulated in the first plateau but then slowed down considerably at the second plateau (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 15). On the contrary, Li213CO3, being a secondary by-product during the first plateau, overtook Li212CO3 to become the prevailing by-product during the second plateau. Several important implications from these results could be derived. During catalyst reconstruction stage, significant electrolyte decomposition has concurrently occurred. But when O2 evolution becomes activated at the second plateau, the electrolyte decomposition is considerably suppressed. On the contrary, the carbon corrosion rate seems less affected by the different reactions and operation conditions during the first two plateaus.

At the second cycle, the discharge was still dominated by 4e−/O2 ORR (Supplementary Fig. 7e), and the charge typically had more O2 evolutions at ~50%. This increased O2 release versus the 1st charge was attributed to the more activated catalytic surfaces of FeCoNi-LDH, evidenced by the disappearance of the first charge plateau. Meanwhile, we also observed a slightly decreased electrolyte decomposition, i.e., reduced 12CO2 evolution (Supplementary Fig. 16), consistent with the rise in O2 evolution. However, 13CO2 evolution from 13C-carbon corrosion increased further during the 2nd charge (Supplementary Fig. 16). As the cycling continued, the 3rd plateau became elongated and increasingly more CO2 evolution occurred, reinforcing the point that carbon corrosion is a major factor worsening the rechargeability.

Identifying the key reactive oxygen species during recharge

Parasitic reactions often involve actions of ROS generated at the electrode-electrolyte interface27–29,35. Next, we unravel the key ROS during LiOH decomposition. Considering the OER in alkaline media, the decomposition of LiOH could potentially involve the following ROS: *OH, ∙OH, H2O2, *OOH and 1O2. To detect them, we employed in situ ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) methodologies10 aided with indicators specific to a particular ROS (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 17) and in situ infrared spectroscopy (IR) spectroscopy; the specificity of UV-vis spectroscopy has been experimentally verified (Supplementary Fig. 18 and 19a–c, Supplementary Note 5) and well-documented for these indicators in the literature36. For instance, p-nitrosodimethylaniline (RNO) possessing an ultrahigh reaction rate constant to ∙OH (Supplementary Table 3)37, has been used to detect hydroxyl radicals, where the decaying signals of RNO upon charging were proportionally linked to the generation of ∙OH during electrochemical LiOH decomposition (Fig. 3a). Hydroxyl species (∙OH/*OH) is considered more aggressive36 than 1O2 and H2O2, so its potential presence and behavior in the system deserve particular attention. To determine whether ∙OH forms, we potentiostatically charged a LiOH-preloaded FeCoNi-LDH/SP electrode at different states of the first two charge plateaus. Without exception, the RNO signals dropped at these measured potentials (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 20), but when the preloaded LiOH was absent, the RNO signal remained stable at the corresponding potentials, suggesting that ∙OH was indeed generated during LiOH decomposition. To trace the origin of the observed ∙OH, we have also used in situ attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) to investigate the decomposition of fully 18O-labeled Li18OH; the results revealed that vO–H of *18OH and *H218O (Supplementary Fig. 21e and Supplementary Note 7) gradually emerged as the potential increased beyond 3.6 V38—the similar potential where RNO first detected ∙OH (Supplementary Fig. 20). This synchronous appearance of *OH and ∙OH is notable, as 3.65 V is far below the ~4.0 V potential threshold required for direct electrochemical oxidation of OH− to ∙OH (a well-established constraint in aqueous/quasi-organic systems)39,40. Thus, LiOH-derived OH− cannot directly form ∙OH at 3.65 V; instead, it might form via secondary processes involving the transformation of OER intermediates, i.e., *OH, *O or *OOH. Three possible pathways have been evaluated using density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Supplementary Fig. 22, 23a, c and d), and *O + H2O → ·OH + *OH is identified as the most likely one (Supplementary Note 8). We also note that the formation energy of ·OH is highly dependently upon the catalyst composition and the water content (Supplementary Fig. 23b) in electrolytes, as discussed later. Previously, using in situ electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) and FTIR spectroscopy, You et al.41 and Qiao et al.38 independently showed that *OH and ∙OH was anodically generated on oxide surfaces in aqueous media, indicating the similarity in the formed ROS between alkaline and this mixed organic-aqueous systems.

As the most reactive ROS, *OH42,43 and ∙OH44 are known to be corrosive to the carbon substrate in alkaline conditions. In our case, when an electrode with partially 18O-enriched LiOH (Fig. 2g) was charged, significant scrambled CO2 signals (C18O2, C16O18O and C16O2) from Li2CO3 decomposition were indeed observed, strongly suggesting that majority of oxygen in Li2CO3 originates from *18OH (18OH− → *18OH + e−) formed upon charging. Furthermore, Fenton reaction experiments assisted with 13C-carbon labeling confirmed that ∙OH can also corrode carbon to generate 13CO2, although the corresponding signal (Supplementary Fig. 24a) was more than an order of magnitude lower than that generated upon cell charging (Fig. 2e), indicating that ∙OH is not primary driver for carbon corrosion. This stark contrast in 13CO2 signals is consistent with ∙OH being short-lived and solution-dispersed, making it inefficient for corroding carbon, but effective for electrolyte oxidation. Furthermore, merely mixing LiOH with carbon, even for a prolonged period, did not lead to appreciable carbon corrosion (Supplementary Fig. 24b and Supplementary Note 9). Together, these observations thus suggest that carbon corrosion is primarily driven by electrochemically generated *OH during LiOH decomposition, which aligns with previous reports42,43 suggesting that *OH plays a key role in oxidizing carbon (*C + 6OH− → CO32− + 3H2O + 4e−)42 in alkaline conditions. In addition to carbon corrosion, these hydroxyl species were also found to oxidize TMS, forming SO2, Li2SO3, Li2CO3 and Li2C2O4, as confirmed by NMR, XPS (Supplementary Fig. 11e, f) and titrations (Fig. 2f). The generation of SO2 by ∙OH was further verified by mass spectrometry in a simulated Fenton reaction in TMS electrolytes (Supplementary Fig. 25 and Supplementary Note 10).

Furthermore, if *OH carries on to evolve O2, O–O reformation must occur at some stage. We thus carried out LiOH decomposition potentiostatically at 3.75 V in the presence of Ti4+, so as to detect the possible H2O2 species. The in situ UV-vis results clearly showed that an weak, broad absorbance peak at 462 nm gradually increased with capacity (Fig. 3c), leading to a green-colored solution (Supplementary Fig. 19d–f), a signature of peroxotitanium complexes formed by Ti4+ and H2O2 in slightly alkaline conditions45,46. Notably, this H2O2 generation is closely linked to *H2O2 formation. In situ ATR-FTIR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 21b) revealed that peaks at 787 and 854 cm−1, corresponding to the ¹⁸O–¹⁸O stretch of adsorbed *HO2− and *H2¹⁸O236,47, gradually emerged as the potential exceeded 3.7 V. This adsorbed *H2O2 appeared shortly before the detection of free H2O2, supporting that H2O2 originates from the desorption of surface-bound *H2O2. As for the potential superoxide species *OOH, its signatures are typically expected in the range of 1100–1200 cm−1 in FTIR spectra48,49. Unfortunately, its signal in this region was highly obscured by the background interference from the electrolyte. The shoulder component at 1157 cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 21d) might be related to its presence.

It is important to note that no 1O2 formation was detected during LiOH decomposition (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 26). This absence of 1O2 is consistent with prior in situ EPR observations by Hu et al.50, and stands in stark contrast to Li2O2 decomposition, where 1O2 is well-documented as a major destructive ROS29,35. This distinction points to fundamentally different reaction pathways between the two processes, as discussed later.

The key processes during recharge can be schematically summarized (Fig. 3d). At the first plateau, the catalyst undergoes surface reconstruction to form active oxyhydroxide phases, electrochemically consuming part of the LiOH product. This process does not evolve O2; instead, part of the generated *OH participate in corrosion of carbon. At the second plateau, LiOH continues to decompose and O2 starts to evolve with *H2O2 and potentially *OOH being the reaction intermediate. Concurrently, corrosions of the electrolyte (predominantly by ∙OH) and the carbon substrate by hydroxyl species (predominantly by *OH) result in increased Li2CO3 accumulation. At the third plateau, accumulated Li2CO3 are decomposed, releasing a large amount of CO2 and 1O251, the latter causing further corrosion of cell components, incomplete removal of by-products and larger cell polarizations.

Key factors governing OER Faradaic efficiency

As shown earlier, electrolyte decomposition and carbon corrosion by hydroxyl species are the major side reactions during recharge. Suppressing these processes is thus critical for lifting the OER FE. A straightforward strategy is to select solvents with enhanced resistant to hydroxyl radical oxidation. Hydroxyl radicals are known to oxidize organic solvents via a variety of routes52,53, including single-electron transfer (SET), hydrogen atom abstraction (HAA) and radical adduct formation52. Mechanistic analysis (Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Note 11) reveals that the initiation of hydroxyl radical attack often occurs via electrophilic (SET and RAF) or radical (HAA) reactions. To assess solvent susceptibility to these pathways, we employed Fukui index calculations, i.e., f− and f0, to respectively assess the likelihood of an organic solvent (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data 1) to undergo electrophilic and radical reactions. Our calculation confirmed that TMS shows higher resistance to ∙OH oxidation than several common solvents, including N,N-dimethylacetamide, tetraglyme, 1-butyl-1-methylpyrrolidinium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide, and dimethyl sulfoxide. This result aligns with both our experimental results (Fig. 1b) and previous kinetic studies on solvent oxidation against hydroxyl radicals (Supplementary Table 3). This consistency between theoretical and experimental data validates Fukui index as a reliable tool for preliminary solvent screening in LiOH-based Li | |O2 batteries. Notably, our calculations also predicted decent ∙OH resistance for hexamethyl phosphoramide (HMPA). We thus tested HMPA as an electrolyte solvent in a LiOH-based Li | |O2 cell: OEMS measurements showed an OER FE of 21% (Supplementary Fig. 27), the second-highest among all solvents evaluated. Another strategy to enhance OER FE is to suppress hydroxyl species’ activity toward side reactions while promoting their participation in OER. Since both *OH and ∙OH form at catalyst surfaces, their reactivities and the resulting balance between OER and side reactions should be highly sensitive to the water-induced solvation effects and the chemical nature of catalytic surfaces. To testify this hypothesis, we conducted OEMS measurements on a series of Co, CoFe and FeCoNi-LDH catalysts in the TMS-based electrolytes with different water contents. Results (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 28–30) show that each catalyst exhibits a maximal FE. However, the water content required for the optimal FE and the corresponding charging polarization differ across these cases. At the same water content, different catalysts also exhibit varied maximal FEs. This volcano-type behavior (Fig. 4b) being prevalent in all cases thus confirms that the synergistic interplay between water activity and catalyst surfaces exert strong regulatory effects on OER FE.

To understand this dependence of OER activity on water content, we conducted systematic DFT calculations to evaluate the free energy landscape of the reaction at varying water contents. To accurately describe the solvation environment at the interface, we employ a combined explicit and implicit solvation model, where the long-range and short-range solvation effects are modeled by a continuous solvation method and explicitly introduced water molecules, respectively (see Computational Methods and Supplementary Note 12, 13). We select Co-LDH as the surface model to eliminate the complexity introduced by multi-metal sites, allowing us to focus solely on the impact of water content. Calculations (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Data 2) reveal that water content regulates the OER rate-determining step (RDS): specifically, at 30 vol% H2O, the formation of *O intermediate (*OH + OH− → H2O + *O + e−) is the RDS, presenting a free energy change (ΔGRDS) of 0.86 eV, whereas at 10 vol% H2O, the RDS shifts to the subsequent *OOH formation step (*O + OH− → *OOH + e−), and the ΔGRDS decreases to 0.79 eV. Notably, this RDS switching is mainly attributed to the sensitivity of *O stability to water content (Fig. 4d), whereas those of *OOH and *OH appears comparatively less affected. This ultimately resulted in the lowest ΔGRDS at 10 vol% H2O, effectively promoting the OER activity, which is well consistent with the experimentally observed maximum OER FE through optimized water activity (Supplementary Fig. 28).

To elucidate the origin of this *O stability modulation, we further calculated the adsorption free energies of water molecules in the first solvation shell of key reaction intermediates (*OH, *O, and *OOH). Results (Fig. 4d, e and Supplementary Fig. 31) indicate that *O, in particular, undergoes a distinct hydrophilic-hydrophobic transition as water content varies. This manifest in two scenarios. When the water content is low, the polar *O site acts as a favorable hydrophilic anchor for the scarce water molecules, leading to a negative water adsorption free energy and a promoted hydrogen-bond (HB) stabilization of the *O intermediate. Conversely, at high water concentrations (>12 vol% H2O), the calculated water adsorption free energy on *O is positive, suggesting that interfacial water molecules are more stable within the electrolyte than those adsorbed through hydrogen bonding with *O. Consequently, the *O intermediate exhibits an apparent hydrophobicity, repelling water from the compact interfacial layer and leading to *O destabilization. This hydrophilic-to-hydrophobic transition of *O is further evidenced by the number of adsorbed water molecules in its first solvation shell, which decreases to zero as the water content increases (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 31). In essence, this transition reflects free energy change associated with water molecules partitioning from the bulk continuum into the compact interfacial layer as a function of water content. This phenomenon explains the volcano-type FE trend: overly stabilized *O (at very low water content) raises the energy cost for the subsequent *O → *OOH formation step, while overly destabilized *O intermediate (at high water content) penalizes its own formation. Therefore, optimal catalytic activity is achieved when the water content positions the key *O species exactly at this hydrophilic-hydrophobic transition, balancing the energetics of both critical OER steps.

Beyond interfacial HB effects, water content variations also alter the bulk electrolyte properties, specifically OH− transport and LiOH solubility. We investigated these effects via molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of TMS-based electrolytes (Supplementary Table 5). Electrolyte snapshots (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Data 3) show that as water content increases, the HB networks clearly become increasingly extended and continuous, with no aqueous/nonaqueous phase separation. This phenomenon is distinct from the formation of isolated water domains in ethereal systems31,54, which exhibited inferior OER FE (Fig. 1b) than the TMS-based electrolyte. The above contrast in the forms of HB networks can be rationalized by the stronger dipolar interactions (S=O…H–OH) between H2O and TMS molecules in these mixed electrolytes55,56, and underscores TMS’s multi-fold roles: beyond the merit of antioxidation and reducing water activity, another critical function lies in mediating the formation of uniform HB network that in turn regulates interfacial solvation properties. In addition, high water contents enhance LiOH solubility in the mixed electrolyte, as reflected by the increased Li+−O (H2O) coordination numbers with rising water contents (Supplementary Fig. 32). This higher OH− concentration, combined with more interconnected HB networks, significantly facilitate OH− mobility. Strong evidence comes from the significantly increased mean square displacements of OH− (Fig. 4g) and OH− diffusion coefficients (more than an order of magnitude), when the water content rises from 1 to 30 vol% water. The enhanced OH− transport at higher water contents is a critical prerequisite for fast LiOH decomposition kinetics; without it, more severe polarization and thus parasitic reactions would occur upon charging, as evidenced by the observation at 1 vol% H2O (Supplementary Fig. 28b). Nevertheless, high water contents do not necessarily guarantee high FEs (Supplementary Fig. 28g), partly due to the disfavored reaction energetics (Fig. 4c) and the more likely ∙OH formation (Supplementary Fig. 23), further emphasizing the need for a balanced water activity for improved OER performance.

The intrinsic chemical structure of catalysts is another dominant factor (Fig. 4b) playing in concert with interfacial solvation for regulating OER FE. OEMS measurements of Li | |O2 cells at a fixed water content but using various LDH-based catalysts (Fig. 5a) clearly show that by modulating the composition of catalysts, the OER FE is gradually increased, while CO2 evolution signifying hydroxyl-induced side reaction is concomitantly suppressed. It also shows that multi-metal LDHs outperform single-metal counterparts, following the activity sequence of FeCoNi-LDH > CoFe-LDH > NiFe-LDH > Co-LDH > Fe-LDH > Ni-LDH. This trend clearly suggests that Co is a more active metal site for OER than Fe and Ni on LDH catalysts, i.e., with Co-centered sites likely better at steering *OH away from parasitic pathways. This suggestion is further validated by DFT calculations of OER energetics on Co-, Fe- and Ni-LDH surfaces (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 33). Among these single-metal LDHs, Co-LDH (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$11\bar{1}$$\end{document}111¯) surface exhibits the lowest ΔGRDS (0.79 eV), indicating the favorable role of Co in promoting OER (Supplementary Note 14). Extending this analysis to the ternary FeCoNi-LDH (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$11\bar{1}$$\end{document}111¯) surface, we went on to calculate free energy profiles for distinct local metal configurations to identify the most active site (Figs. 5c and Supplementary Data 4, 5). These calculations reveal that the FeCoCo site—a central surface Co atom adjacent to both surface Fe and another Co atoms—as the most active site, exhibiting the lowest ΔGRDS of 0.76 eV. To elucidate the fundamental origin of this improved catalytic activity, we then investigated the electronic structure of FeCoNi-LDH surface. The calculated total and projected density of states (TDOS and PDOS, Fig. 5e) reveal that the conduction band minimum (CBM)—the critical electron-accepting state for the oxidative OER process—is predominantly composed of Co orbitals. In contrast, Fe and Ni orbitals contribute mainly to states at higher energies, suggesting their primary role is to electronically modulate the active Co centers. Crucially, the PDOS of the central Co atom within the most active FeCoCo site is shown to dominate the CBM of the system (red line, Fig. 5e). This indicates that the lowest unoccupied states are spatially localized on these specific Co atoms, enabling efficient acceptance of electrons from OER intermediates. This localization is visually confirmed by the wavefunction isosurface of the CBM (inset, Fig. 5e), which is concentrated around the central Co atom of the FeCoCo site. Therefore, this electronic structure analysis provides a mechanistic explanation for our energetic calculations: the high activity of FeCoCo and similar Co-centric sites is associated with their favorably positioned electronic states at the CBM, which facilitate the critical electron-transfer steps of the OER.

Achieving high OER FE requires catalysts to be not only active but also highly selective. To this end, we further investigated their capacity to inhibit the formation of destructive ∙OH. We calculated the formation energy of ∙OH on the full suite of active sites and uncovered a notable trend, i.e., the trend in ∙OH formation energy (Fig. 5f) aligns exactly with the site-specific OER activity trend (Fig. 5b, c). Specifically, sites that are more active for OER (i.e., have a lower ΔGRDS) exhibit a higher free energy change to form ∙OH. This resistance to ∙OH formation decreases in the following order: FeCoCo > FeCoNi > Co > NiCoCo > CoFeCo > CoNiCo > Fe > Ni. This correlation underscores a crucial dual functionality: the most effective catalytic sites simultaneously promote the desired OER pathway while inhibiting the side reaction. The influence of catalyst composition on ∙OH formation (variations up to 1.3 eV) far outweighs that of water activity (≈ 0.2 eV), highlighting intrinsic catalyst design as the more effective lever for suppressing hydroxyl-driven parasitic reactions. Taken together (Fig. 5h), the lower ΔGRDS for OER and the higher ∙OH formation energy collectively contribute to the high OER activity of the FeCoCo site relative to the other sites and account for the high OER FE observed for the FeCoNi-LDH catalyst.

Finally, stabilizing the interface between the electronic conductor and LiOH is another critical prerequisite for achieving long-cycle LiOH-based Li | |O2 batteries. Due to the severe carbon corrosion, the capacity of the cells using SP (Fig. 5g) faded after only 11 cycles. By contrast, when the amorphous SP carbon was replaced by more ordered graphitic carbon or gold conductor, the rechargeability of the system was further improved (Fig. 5g). Notably, the cells with gold conductors exhibited high OER FE of 75% and stable cycling for over 300 cycles at 0.5 mAh/cm2 (Fig. 5a, g), with fairly stable terminal voltages and no sign of capacity decay. Titration, XRD and scanning electron microscopy confirmed that the discharge-charge over multiple cycles was still based on LiOH formation/decomposition (Supplementary Fig. 35 and Supplementary Table 6). These results reaffirm the critical role of the interface between the electronic conductor and LiOH in determining cell reversibility.

Discussion

This work shows that the key destructive ROS involved in LiOH decomposition are *OH and ∙OH in TMS-H2O mixed electrolytes. No 1O2 formation was detected, in contrast to the case for Li2O2 decomposition. This distinction points to fundamentally different reaction pathways between the two processes. For Li2O2 decomposition, superoxide species (LiO2, O2−) are generated as key intermediates, whose solution-phase self-disproportionation directly produces 1O229,35. In contrast, LiOH decomposition proceeds most exclusively via surface-bound intermediates (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Fig. 34): *OH, *O and *OOH, limiting superoxide desorption into the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 36). This surface-confined pathway may circumvent the solution-phase disproportionation of superoxide—thus precluding 1O2 formation. An additional contributor to the lack of 1O2 may be the direct decomposition of H2O2 on the catalyst surface via the minor OER path (Supplementary Fig. 34), which generates H2O and O2 without 1O2 as a byproduct, as further verified by UV-vis measurements (Supplementary Fig. 19c).

Furthermore, our results unambiguously demonstrate that oxidation of electrolytes and carbon support by hydroxyl species (*OH and ∙OH) represents the major sources of parasitic reactions. Designing a reversible LiOH-based Li | |O2 battery relies on an integrated regulatory framework, featuring dual dominant regulation of catalyst composition and water activity, paired with complementary protection via solvent screening and electric conductor optimization (Fig. 5h). While oxidation-resistant solvents and electrical conductors are necessary to reduce hydroxyl-induced side reactions, the interfacial water solvation and most importantly, its synergy with the catalytic surfaces hold the key to effectively control hydroxyl actions toward O2 evolution or by-products formation. As a result, considerably improved OER FE (75%) and cell cycle-life (more than 300 cycles) have been achieved. These results validate the feasibility of overcoming the stability bottleneck in organic-dominant LiOH-based Li | |O2 batteries, and provides a transferrable interface design model for ROS-sensitive secondary batteries.

Moving forward, our results suggest that sulfone-based electrolytes are worth further investigation, and that Fukui index calculations could be used as a preliminary screening method to aid the discovery of more stable solvents. Alternatively, other than seeking for a solvent being absolutely inert to ∙OH, the parasitic reactions involving solvents could also be reduced by diluting the fraction of solvents, using the approaches of high-concentration electrolyte or localized high-concentration electrolyte57,58. In these approaches, comparatively stable lithium salt and fluorinated diluent molecules could effectively shield solvents susceptible to ∙OH oxidation. These directions are worth exploration.

Notably, *OH/∙OH being species generated at surfaces renders its properties highly susceptible to the chemical environment and structure of the catalytic interface. Ideally, future catalyst designs should simultaneously enable facile OER kinetics, suppressed ∙OH formation and quenched ∙OH activity. These multi-faceted abilities of catalytic interfaces have indeed been confirmed in our results. Moreover, our results based on a series of LDH catalysts further suggests that better catalytic sites may be constructed by increasing the compositional complexity of catalytic surfaces, such as in high-entropy oxides/oxyhydroxides. In these materials, the diversity of surface chemical configurations may provide sites that favor OER kinetics and direct *OH toward O2 evolution rather than parasitic reaction pathways. This work delves deeply into the key molecular species, factors and strategies that could modulate cell reversibility, offering insights into achieving a long-life LiOH-based Li | |O2 battery.

Materials synthesis

Synthesis of FeCoNi-LDH: FeCoNi-LDH was synthesized by metal ion hydrolysis and hydroxide co-precipitation59. Briefly, 3.49 g Co(NO3)2·6H2O and 3.94 g 2-methylimidazole were dissolved in 300 mL methanol, respectively. The 2-methylimidazole solution was poured into the Co(NO3)2·6H2O solution with stirring, and then kept at 25 ± 2 °C for 24 h. Then the reaction product was separated by centrifugation, washed several times with methanol and dried in vacuum at 60 oC to obtain the purple power Co-ZIF. Subsequently, the obtained Co-ZIF (about 0.60 g) was ultrasonically dispersed in 150 mL ethanol for about 10 min, and a Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (1.10 g) solution (dissolved in 50 mL ethanol) was added to the above dispersion and stirred for 90 min. The separation and drying processes were the same as for Co-ZIF. The obtained Co-ZIF@CoNi-LDH product (about 0.38 g) was then dispersed in 10 mL ethanol. Finally, the FeCl2·xH2O (about 1.05 g) (dissolved in 10 mL ethanol) solution was poured into the Co-ZIF@CoNi -LDH solution, and then stirred for 2.5 h. The final FeCoNi-LDH product was collected after centrifugation, washed with ethanol/H2O for several times and then dried in vacuum at 60 °C.

Synthesis of Co-LDH: Co-ZIF powder (0.20 g) and Co(NO3)2 (0.45 g) were firstly dispersed in 100 mL and 25 mL methanol, respectively. The above two solutions were homogeneously mixed and stirred at 25 ± 2 °C for 2 h. The resulting products were then centrifuged three times with ethanol and dried under vacuum at 60 °C. The dry powder was then ultrasonically dispersed in 75 mL of ethanol and followed by an addition of 25 mL of ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm, Millipore). The mixtures were stirred at 25 ± 2 °C for 3 days. At last, the product was centrifuged twice with ethanol and subjected to vacuum drying at 60 °C.

Synthesis of CoNi-LDH: CoNi-LDH was synthesized by using similar method. Another precursor Ni(NO3)2 solution (0.45 g dissolved in 25 mL methanol) was added and the final mixtures were stirred at 25 ± 2 °C for 1 day.

Synthesis of CoFe-LDH: FeCo-LDH was synthesized by using similar method. Another precursor FeCl2 solution (0.31 g dissolved in 25 mL methanol) was added and the final mixtures were stirred at 25 ± 2 °C for 1 day.

Synthesis of Ni-LDH: Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (3.49 g) and 2-methylimidazole (3.94 g) were separately dissolved in 300 mL of methanol. The 2-methylimidazole solution was then added dropwise to the Ni(NO3)2·6H2O solution under continuous stirring and kept at 25 ± 2 °C for 24 h. The reaction product was separated by centrifugation, washed several times with methanol, and dried under vacuum at 60 °C to obtain a dry yellow powder. The dried powder was then ultrasonically dispersed in 75 mL of ethanol, followed by the addition of 25 mL of ultrapure water. The mixture was stirred at 25 ± 2 °C for 1 day. Finally, the product was centrifuged twice with ethanol and vacuum dried at 60 °C. The resulting powder was light green in color.

Synthesis of NiFe-LDH: The yellow powder (0.20 g) from the previous step and 0.04 g FeSO4·7H2O were separately dispersed in 100 mL and 25 mL of methanol, respectively. The two solutions were then mixed and stirred at 25 ± 2 °C for 1.5 h. The resulting product was centrifuged three times with ethanol and dried under vacuum at 60 °C. The dried powder was ultrasonically dispersed in 75 mL of ethanol, followed by the addition of 25 mL of ultrapure water. The mixture was stirred at 25 ± 2 °C for 1 day. The product was then centrifuged twice with ethanol and vacuum dried at 60 °C.

Electrode and electrolyte preparation

For FeCoNi-LDH/SP positive electrode preparation, the FeCoNi-LDH powder, Super P (SP) and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) with a mass ratio of 1:2:0.5 were homogeneously mixed in a mortar. The mixture was manually kneaded and rolled into a thin film using a cylindrical stainless-steel rod. The film was subsequently punched into circular electrode disks with a diameter of 15 mm using a stainless-steel punch. The areal loading of electrode was ~1 mg cm−2. The FeCoNi-LDH/13C positive electrode was prepared in the same way as FeCoNi-LDH/SP. For FeCoNi-LDH/Au positive electrode preparation, the FeCoNi-LDH powder, gold powder (Au) and PTFE with a mass ratio of 1:4:0.5 were homogeneously mixed and prepared into positive electrode films (about 1 cm2), which was then pressed on a stainless-steel mesh with a diameter of 15 mm. The preparation procedure for positive electrodes using other catalysts follows the same protocol as described above.

For counter electrode preparation10, the lithium iron phosphate (LFP), ferric phosphate (FP), SP and PTFE with a mass ratio of 2:1:1:1 were homogeneously mixed in a mortar and prepared into counter electrode films with 20 mm diameter (about 40 mg/cm2)

These films were transferred into an Ar-filled glovebox (Mikrouna, H2O < 0.1 ppm, O2 < 0.1 ppm) after dried under vacuum at 160 °C for 12 h.

For electrolyte preparation, 0.287 g Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) was dissolved in 1 mL mixed solvent consisting of ultrapure water and single organic solvent, with water content controlled within 0–30 vol%. The obtained electrolyte was 1 mol L−1 (M) LiTFSI/organic solvent-water mixed electrolyte. The adopted organic solvents were sulfone (TMS), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), tetraglyme (G4) and hexamethyl phosphoramide (HMPA), respectively. All organic solvents were dried over freshly activated 4 Å molecular sieves for several weeks. For electrolyte with 18O-enriched water, 18O-enriched water was used as purchased. The electrolyte preparation was conducted in an Ar-filled glovebox.

Battery assembly and electrochemical measurements

All Li | |O2 cells were prepared using a Swagelok-type design. Specifically, the FeCoNi-LDH/SP positive electrode (15 mm diameter) was utilized together with a lithium iron phosphate/ferric phosphate/SP counter electrode (20 mm diameter), a glassy fiber separator (22 mm diameter), electrolytes (120 μL) and 120-mesh stainless-steel mesh current collector (20 mm diameter, average pore size and areal density are about 0.13 mm and 0.38 kg m−2, respectively) for the assembly of a cell in an Ar-filled glovebox. The Li | |O2 cell was cycled galvanostatically on a Neware battery test system (Neware, CT-4000) at 25 ± 2 °C (constant-temperature room). The electrolyte used in Li | |O2 cell was 1 mol L−1 LiTFSI/single organic solvent with 5 vol% H2O or 18O-enriched H218O addition. The water content in mixed electrolytes was generally fixed at 5 vol%, unless noted otherwise. The Li | |O2 cells were tested under a gas atmosphere of 20% 16O2 in Ar or 20% 18O2 in Ar. Typically, the catalyst/carbon-based Li | |O2 cells were cycled galvanostatically at 0.25 mA. The catalyst/Au-based Li | |O2 cells were discharged at 0.1 mA and charged at 0.25 mA. The voltage was ranged from 2.0 V to 5.5 V, unless noted otherwise.

The resistances of the Swagelok-type cells at the initial state and the 7th fully charged state were determined from electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements performed using a CHI 650E electrochemical workstation (Shanghai CHI Instrument Co., Ltd., China). The EIS spectra were acquired in potentiostatic mode at open-circuit voltage (OCV) by applying a sinusoidal voltage perturbation with an AC amplitude of 5 mV over a frequency range from 10 kHz to 0.1 Hz, with 10 points per decade of frequency. Before each EIS measurement, the cells were rested at OCV for 1 h to reach a quasi-stationary state. All measurements were conducted at 25 ± 2 °C.

Characterization

Characterization of the catalysts and the discharge-charge products

The structure of the synthesized catalysts and the discharge-charge products were characterized by ex situ/in situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a DX-2700B powder diffractometer (Haoyuan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). For in situ XRD, firstly, the Swagelok-type cell using FeCoNi LDH/SP as positive electrode was discharged to 2 mAh at a current of 0.25 mA in a pure oxygen environment. The cell components were then removed from the cell and reassembled into the in situ XRD device in an argon-filled glovebox. The XRD measurement parameters were set to a 2θ range of 19–39°, a step width of 0.02° and 4 seconds per step. The cell was then charged to 5.2 V at 0.25 mA.

For the electrodes subjected to ex situ measurements, after the electrochemical tests, the cells were immediately transferred into an Ar-filled glovebox. The positive electrodes at different electrochemical states were harvested from the cells, soaked in anhydrous acetonitrile to remove residual electrolyte, washed three times, and dried under vacuum at 25 ± 2 °C. The processed positive electrodes were stored in an Ar-filled glovebox and then used for XRD, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Raman characterizations. For XRD measurements, the dried electrode was mounted in a homemade X-ray sample holder sealed with Kapton polyimide films to avoid air exposure. For SEM measurements, the dried positive electrodes were taken to the SEM sample loading chamber in a sealed sample holder to minimize air exposure. The time from opening the sealed sample holder to finishing sample loading into electron microscopes was < 10 seconds. For Raman measurements, the dried electrodes were loaded into a sealed Raman cell equipped with a quartz window in an Ar-filled glovebox to avoid air exposure.

The morphology and composition of the products on the electrode surface were studied by SEM (Hitachi JSM-7900F microscope) and Raman. Raman Spectra were acquired on a Renishaw laser microscopy confocal excited with a 514 nm wavelength laser. 13C nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (13C NMR) was performed to determine the minor byproduct during charge using a Bruker ADVANCE III 600 MHz instrument.

In situ spectroscopy measurements

To detect reactive oxygen species (ROS), in situ ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) measurement was conducted using UV-2800 equipment (Shanghai Yixin Scientific Instrument) with wavelength ranging from 190–1100 nm. The assembly procedure of the in situ three-electrode ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) cell was as follows: A slurry of LiOH-preloaded FeCoNi-LDH/SP was prepared by homogeneously mixing LiOH (10–15 mg), FeCoNi-LDH (10 mg), SP (20 mg), and polyacrylic acid binder (0.15 g, PAA). Additionally, a slurry of lithium iron phosphate, ferric phosphate, SP, and PAA (LFP/FP/SP) was prepared in a mass ratio of 2:1:1:1. A specified amount of the LiOH-preloaded FeCoNi-LDH/SP slurry was then dropped onto an Au disk working electrode, and the Au wire counter/reference electrodes were coated with the LFP/FP/SP slurry. The three electrodes were dried at 25 ± 2 °C under an Ar atmosphere. The electrolyte used was 0.1 mol/L LiTFSI/TMS with 5 vol% H2O and a ROS-specific indicator. The electrodes were then inserted into a UV cell filled with a certain volume of electrolyte, sealed in an Ar-filled glovebox. The thickness of the electrolyte is about 1 mm (Supplementary Fig. 5), which means the optical path length is 1 mm. The in situ UV-vis testing process did not involve stirring. The detection procedures for hydroxyl radicals (·OH), singlet oxygen (1O2) and hydroperoxides (H2O2) can be found in the Supplementary Note 6.

To verify the presence of *OH and *H2O2/*HO2−, in situ attenuated total reflectance-fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) was performed on a Nicolet 6700 spectrometer instrument and a single-reflection monolithic diamond attenuated total reflection sampling cell. Firstly, a Li | |O2 cell with FeCoNi LDH/SP as positive electrode and TMS added with 5 vol% H218O as electrolyte, was discharged to 0.5 mAh under an 18O-enriched 18O2 atmosphere and the discharge product is Li18OH. Then, all the components inside the Swagelok-type cell were transferred to the in situ infrared cell in an Ar-filled glovebox. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurement of the cell was carried out on a Hukuto Denko battery system (HJ series, Japan) in the potential range of 3.0–5.2 V at a scan rate of 0.3 mV s−1.

Online electrochemical mass spectrometry

The OEMS system was composed of Swagelok-type cell, mass spectrometer (QMG250M1, Pfeiffer, Linglu Instrument Co., Ltd) and electronic mass flow controllers (Bronkhorst)10. In the quantitative OEMS mode, to ensure accurate quantification of O2 and CO2, their MS signals have been calibrated using the high-purity gases and accurately controlled flow by electronic mass flow controllers. The flow velocity of the working gas (20% O2 and 80% Ar) and carrier gas (Ar) was controlled at 0.09 mL min−1 and 2 mL min−1 via two mass flow controllers, respectively. All cells using LDH catalyst/SP as the positive electrode were discharged to 0.5 mAh (~283 mAh/g) at 0.25 mA (0.14 mA/cm2), followed by charging at 0.14 mA/cm2. For the FeCoNi-LDH/Au positive electrode-based cells, the discharge capacity was 0.5 mAh (~100 mAh/g) at a discharge current of 0.1 mA, with the charge current set to 0.25 mA.

In order to perform quantitative operations on the obtained mass spectrometry signals of mixed isotopes, we attempted a semiquantitative calibration by a simple assumption. That is, the mass spectrometric signals for the same moles of gas molecules with different isotopic labeling are identical, e.g., passing in the mass spectrometer of 1 nmol/s of 16O2 and 1 nmol/s of 18O2 leads to identical signals of m/z = 32 and 36. In this way, the calibrated relationship (i.e., the ratio of their integrated peak areas) of MS current signals to nmol/s signals for 16O2 could then be transferrable to that of 18O2 and 16O18O, and their MS signals can thus be quantified. Likewise, the MS signals of 13C16O2, 12C16O18O, 12C18O2 can be quantified based on the calibrated signals of 12C16O2.

Based on the above semiquantitative method, we have established a calibration protocol enabling approximate conversion of MS ion current signals to quantitative gas fluxes (nmol/s). Therefore, absolute quantitative values may be seen with this precaution.

Ex situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy

The four FeCoNi-LDH/SP-based Swagelok-type Li | |O2 cells were discharged to 1 mAh. One of the cells stopped discharging, and the other three cells were continuously charged to 3.7, 3.9 and 5.0 V at a current of 0.25 mA, respectively. Subsequently, the positive electrodes in different states were extracted from these cells, soaked in acetonitrile solvent and washed three times, and then dried under vacuum at 25 ± 2 °C. These processed positive electrodes were used for XPS testing.

XPS was conducted on a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer equipped with monochromatic Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV). The working voltage and filament current of the equipment were set to 12 kV and 6 mA, respectively. For ex situ XPS measurement, in order to accurately analyse the composition of the products on the electrode surface, a portable transfer vessel was used to process samples in a glovebox and loaded into the XPS without exposure to air.

Titration experiments

The FeCoNi-LDH/SP positive electrodes or FeCoNi-LDH/13C-carbon positive electrodes and glass fiber separators were extracted from cells at different discharge/charge states at 0.25 mA with a capacity limit of 1.0 mAh at 25 ± 2 °C. The collected electrodes and glass fiber separators were dried in an Ar-filled glovebox and then completely immersed in 20 mL of ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm, Millipore) to ensure complete dissolution of the reaction products. After soaking for ~3 min, the positive electrodes and glass fiber separators were removed from the solution. The obtained solution was used for the quantification of Li2CO3, Li2O2 and LiOH. By combining acid-base titration with ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy and mass spectrometry, the discharge/charge by-products, including Li2CO3, Li2O2 and LiOH, were quantified. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in Supplementary Note 16.

Computational methods

Density functional theory

The spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)60. Electron-ion interactions were described using the projector-augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotentials61,62, and the exchange-correlation energy was treated with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerh of (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)63. A plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV was applied throughout. Long-range van der Waals interactions were incorporated using Grimme’s DFT-D3(BJ) correction scheme64,65. Strong electron correlations in the 3 d orbitals of Fe, Co, and Ni were treated using a Hubbard-U correction66 with effective U values of 4.3, 3.52, and 3.8 eV, respectively. The solid/liquid interface was modeled using a hybrid approach that combines implicit and explicit solvation67. A periodic continuum solvation model captured long-range electrostatic effects from the bulk electrolyte, while explicit water molecules were placed in the compact interfacial layer to accurately describe short-range interactions with reaction intermediates. The first Brillouin zone was sampled using a Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh68 with a separation length of 0.05 Å−1. All atomic positions were fully relaxed using the quasi-Newton l-BFGS algorithm until the residual forces on all atoms were less than 0.05 eV·Å−1. Free energy reaction profiles were constructed based on our previously established methodology69, incorporating zero-point energy (ZPE) corrections from vibrational frequency calculations and thermal contributions from standard thermodynamic data70. More details can be found in the Supplementary Note 12 and 13.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to investigate the coordination structures of solvents, cations, and anions in distinct electrolyte systems. The molecular compositions of all simulated systems are summarized in Supplementary Table 5. Atom charges of TFSI−, TMS and OH− molecule were calculated using Gaussian 16 code71 and the B3LYP/6-311 g (d,p) functional and basis set were applied72. All atoms were parametrized employing the OPLS-AA force field73 and Auxiliary Tools of Force Field (AuToFF), such as the bond parameters, angle parameters and the dihedral angles, and so on. Notably, parameters for TFSI⁻ were directly adopted from the OPLS-2009IL force field74.

The MD simulations were performed using the GROMACS 2021 software package75–77. Prior to the MD calculations, the steepest descent method was employed to minimize the initial energy of each system, with a force tolerance of 1 kJ (mol−1 nm−1) and a maximum step size of 0.001 ps78. Periodic boundary conditions were imposed in all three directions. The Leapfrog algorithm was utilized to integrate the Newtonian equation of motion. The MD simulation was conducted in an NPT ensemble, with a total simulation time of 20 ns.

In NPT simulations, the pressure was maintained at 1 bar in an isotropic manner using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat79. The temperature was controlled at 298.15 K via the V-rescale thermostat. The LINCS algorithm80 was employed to constrain the bond lengths of hydrogen atoms. The Particle-Mesh-Ewald (PME) with a fourth-order interpolation was used to evaluate the electrostatic interactions and a cutoff of 1.0 nm was employed to calculate the short-range van der Waals interactions81.
