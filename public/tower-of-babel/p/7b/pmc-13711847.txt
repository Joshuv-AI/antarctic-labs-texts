Assessing vulnerability of diarrheal diseases associated with climate variability effects in four coastal districts of Bangladesh
Authors: Golam Kibria, Md. Raisul Islam, Hashinur Rahman Pavel, Nadia Sharmin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate change has intensified public health challenges in coastal Bangladesh, particularly increasing the prevalence of diarrheal diseases. This study assesses the vulnerability to diarrheal diseases in four coastal districts-Satkhira, Jhalakathi, Barguna, and Gopalganj-by examining the relationship between climate variability and disease occurrences. A mixed-methods approach was used, integrating hospital admission records (n = 46,741 diarrheal cases identified from 361,265 patients) from five government health facilities between January 2017 and December 2022 with meteorological data from the Bangladesh Meteorological Department. Key Informant Interviews (KIIs) and Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) were conducted in all four districts. A total of 28 KIIs and 9 FGDs were conducted across the selected upazilas to collect field-level information. Findings indicate a significant positive correlation between maximum temperature and diarrheal incidence (r = 0.228, p = 0.0012). Diarrhea cases were more prevalent at maximum temperatures of 30–35 °C. In contrast, rainfall showed no statistically significant association with diarrheal trends (r = 0.094, p = 0.235), although descriptively fewer cases were observed at rainfall levels above 60 mm. Seasonal analysis revealed peak diarrheal cases between March and May, coinciding with the hottest months of the year. Age-specific analysis showed that children aged 0–3 years accounted for 37.6% of total cases, and females represented 52.7% of reported cases (χ2 test, p < 0.001). Qualitative findings from KIIs and FGDs highlighted salinity intrusion, flooding, waterlogging, inadequate sanitation, and limited healthcare resources as key drivers of vulnerability in the study districts. Vulnerable groups, including children under five and women, are disproportionately affected due to exposure to contaminated water and healthcare access constraints. To mitigate climate-induced diarrheal diseases, the study recommends improvements in water and sanitation infrastructure, enhanced healthcare services, and community-based awareness programs. Introduction

Diarrhea is one of the most prominent public health concerns in Bangladesh which is influenced by the climate change impacts. Temperatures rise, changes in patterns of rainfall, recurrent floods and cyclones are highlighted to favor diseases having waterborne causes [1, 2]. Elderly people, pregnant women, children below five years of age are the most vulnerable to suffer circumstances of unbearable health crisis which is caused by unavailability to clean water for drinking and domestic use purposes [3, 4]. Globally, diarrheal diseases remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, accounting for nearly half a million deaths annually among children under five, with the highest burden in South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa 5. In Bangladesh, diarrheal diseases are among the top causes of child mortality and hospitalization, with recurrent outbreaks reported during seasonal changes and extreme weather events [6, 7]. This underscore both the national and global urgency of addressing diarrhea as a climate-sensitive health challenge. Against this global backdrop, Bangladesh’s coastal districts present a particularly acute case: a convergence of climatic stressors, ecological fragility, and socioeconomic deprivation creates conditions under which diarrheal disease burden is intensified and unevenly distributed.

Bangladesh is one of the most vulnerable countries in the world in terms of climate change. The mean temperature of the country has risen 0.16 °C between 1970–2020 and is projected to increase by 0.41 °C for the period of 2020–2039 and 0.89 °C for the period 2040–2059 under SSP3-7.0 scenario [8]. Consequently, the number of days with a Heat Index > 35 °C will be increased to 99.66 days over the period of 2020–2039 and to 133.47 days over the period of 2040–2060 in a calendar year compared to the reference period 1995–2014 under SSP3-7.0 scenario. In addition, Bangladesh experienced variation in its seasonal rainfall and a significant decrease in rainfall per decade in eastern divisions with a significant increase in rainfall per decade in western divisions over the period of 1971–2020. Consequently, sea level rise, salinity intrusion, more extreme events, unusual floods, drought, and waterlogging have been more intense due to climate change in Bangladesh [9, 10].

Climate change is getting priority in the global public health agenda as a significant global health issue as it can directly and indirectly harm human health [11–13]. Direct effects are injury or illness related to climate-induced hazards and disasters like cyclones, floods, heat waves, and saltwater intrusion whereas indirect effects are more complex to identify that might occur as a result of changes in natural systems, seasonal shifting and abrupt changes in the microbiome structures [13, 14]. Changes in the earth’s ecosystems result in increasing trends of water and vector-borne diseases, heat-related diseases, diseases related to air pollution, and extreme events [15]. Climate change can impact human health in a number of ways, including extreme heat, poor air quality, extreme weather events, lack of food and safe drinking water, poor sanitation, and favorable conditions for disease occurrences [15, 16]. Due to its geographical location and highly populated areas, Bangladesh is more vulnerable to climate change and already started experiencing climate-induced public health impacts [17, 18]. To understand these dynamics, this study adopts an exposure–vulnerability conceptual framework, which posits that diarrheal disease risk is jointly determined by the degree of climate exposure (e.g., temperature extremes, flood frequency) and the adaptive capacity of local populations, shaped by sanitation infrastructure, healthcare access, and socioeconomic status [19, 20].

Waterborne diseases, particularly diarrhea, have surged in coastal Bangladesh due to increasing contamination of drinking water sources by floodwaters and saline intrusion [21, 22]. More than 50% of people in coastal areas report experiencing climate-induced diarrheal diseases, highlighting the urgent need for mitigation and adaptation measures [23]. Extreme rainfall and flooding lead to the overflow of sanitation facilities, mixing human waste with drinking water supplies and increasing the transmission of diarrheal pathogens, including Vibrio cholerae, Escherichia coli, and rotaviruses 24.

Heat stress and extreme weather events also contribute to the rise in diarrheal diseases by compromising food safety and sanitation infrastructure. Warmer temperatures accelerate bacterial growth in food and water sources, increasing the risk of foodborne illnesses [15, 16]. Additionally, malnutrition caused by climate-induced crop failures weakens immune systems, making individuals more susceptible to diarrheal infections [17].

Despite the growing recognition of climate-induced health risks, research on the relationship between climate change and diarrheal disease occurrence in Bangladesh remains limited. Specifically, it remains unclear how district-level climatic variability-including temperature thresholds, rainfall seasonality, and saline intrusion-differentially drives diarrheal disease burden across the four coastal districts of Satkhira, Jhalakathi, Barguna, and Gopalganj. Moreover, the extent to which socio-environmental factors such as inadequate sanitation, limited healthcare access, and population vulnerability interact with climate stressors to amplify disease risk in this deltaic context has not been systematically quantified. Existing studies primarily focus on coastal areas, leaving gaps in understanding regional variations in disease patterns. Furthermore, few studies utilize hospital-registered patient data to analyze long-term trends in climate-sensitive diseases. No prior study has simultaneously combined six-year hospital MIS records with meteorological data and community-level qualitative evidence to map the climate–diarrhea nexus at the sub-district level in coastal Bangladesh. This study aims to characterise facility-recorded diarrheal disease patterns in five government health facilities across four coastal districts of Bangladesh, using hospital MIS data as a sentinel indicator of climate-related health vulnerability, and to integrate these findings with qualitative community evidence to inform context-specific adaptation strategies. Guided by an exposure–vulnerability framework, this study specifically seeks to: (i) quantify the association between temperature and rainfall variability and diarrheal case counts using six years of hospital MIS data (2017–2022); (ii) identify high-risk demographic groups and seasonal peaks across study districts; and (iii) integrate qualitative community evidence to contextualize the structural and environmental drivers of disease vulnerability in coastal Bangladesh.

Study design and sites

This research adopted a mixed-methods approach, combining qualitative and quantitative data collection methods. Specifically, this study employs a Convergent Parallel Design [25], in which quantitative data (hospital MIS records and meteorological data,QUAN) and qualitative data (Key Informant Interviews and Focus Group Discussions; qual) were collected concurrently and independently, then merged at the interpretation stage to develop a more complete understanding of climate–diarrhea vulnerability. The Convergent Parallel Design (QUAN + qual → Merge) was operationalised in three phases. Phase 1 (Quantitative Strand): Six years of hospital MIS data (n = 46,741 diarrheal cases from 361,265 patients, January 2017-December 2022) were analysed alongside meteorological records from the Bangladesh Meteorological Department to quantify associations between maximum temperature, rainfall, and monthly diarrheal case counts across five health facilities in four coastal districts. Bivariate correlations and chi-square tests were used to identify statistically significant relationships with demographic variables (age, sex). Phase 2 (Qualitative Strand): Concurrently and independently, 28 KIIs and 9 FGDs were conducted across the four study districts. KII participants included doctors, health assistants, midwives, and community leaders; FGD participants were recruited from local communities. Thematic analysis of transcripts was used to identify dominant patterns related to water security, healthcare access, and climate-related vulnerability. Phase 3 (Merging): The two strands were integrated at the interpretation stage using a joint display approach [26], in which quantitative findings were systematically compared with qualitative themes to assess convergence, complementarity, or divergence. The two strands were weighted equally (QUAN = qual). Neither strand informed data collection in the other prior to the merging phase. This design was chosen because it enables robust statistical pattern identification alongside contextually grounded community perspectives, producing a more complete understanding of climate-induced diarrheal vulnerability than either strand could provide alone. The study was focused on the heavily impacted coastal regions of Bangladesh, including Satkhira, Jhalakathi, Barguna, and Gopalganj, to assess the scope and drivers of climate variability. The study sites were purposively selected based on the area's vulnerability and the fact that consultation workshops for NAP formulation were conducted in these locations [27]. The selection of these four districts was guided by three converging criteria: (i) high documented vulnerability to climate-sensitive diseases as identified in Bangladesh’s National Adaptation Plan [27], (ii) diversity of dominant climate hazards,Satkhira is characterized by frequent tidal surges and saline intrusion, Barguna by cyclone-driven coastal flooding, Jhalakathi by riverine waterlogging, and Gopalganj by erratic rainfall and seasonal inundation,and (iii) availability of complete, continuous MIS hospital records for the full six-year study period (2017–2022). Geographically, all four districts occupy the low-lying Bengal Delta, characterized by deltaic and riverine terrain, dense rural populations, and livelihoods centred on agriculture, fisheries, and daily wage labor. Access to improved sanitation remains below the national average across all four districts, with reliance on shared or unimproved latrines widespread in rural and char areas, directly compounding waterborne disease risk. The districts differ markedly in climate exposure and health infrastructure: Satkhira and Barguna experience the most severe salinity-related drinking water contamination and operate comparatively under-resourced upazila-level health facilities; Jhalakathi and Gopalganj have modestly better healthcare access but experience acute seasonal diarrheal spikes associated with waterlogging and inadequate drainage systems. A mixed-method approach was employed, utilizing both secondary and primary data. Secondary data were collected from hospital visit records to analyze trends in diarrhea outbreaks. Primary data were gathered through interviews and focus group discussion with healthcare professionals, community leaders, and local residents to identify contributing factors, systemic healthcare challenges, and potential mitigation strategies [28].

Primary Data

Key Informant Interviews (KII)

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with key informants to gather qualitative data on their perceptions of climate change impacts on health in their respective coastal areas. Key informants were selected using a purposive sampling strategy. Interviewees included doctors, nurses, midwives, health assistants, high school teachers, union council members and chairman, NGO workers. The key informants assessed their observations of climate-related health issues, patient trends, and healthcare challenges. Insights on disease patterns, treatment practices, and mitigation efforts were explored. A total of 28 Key Informant Interviews (KIIs) were conducted in Shyamnagar, Kathalia, Jhalakathi Sadar, Gopalganj Sadar, and Patharghata with 14 doctors, 5 health assistants/CHCPs, 5 midwives, and 4 teachers.

Focus Group Discussions (FGDs)

FGDs were conducted with local community members to gather qualitative data on their experiences and perceptions of climate change impacts on health. FGD participants were recruited through purposive community sampling. Discussions focused on identifying health hazards, coping mechanisms, and community resilience strategies. A total of nine FGDs were held three in Jhalakathi Sadar and two each in Kathalia, Patharghata, and Gopalganj Sadar.

Quality assurance in qualitative data collection

A range of quality assurance steps was taken to strengthen the rigour of both data collection and analysis. The KII and FGD guides were first piloted in one upazila before fieldwork began and then refined based on feedback from two independent public health researchers. With participants’ consent, all interviews and discussions were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim in Bengali. These transcripts were subsequently back-translated into English by a bilingual research assistant and carefully reviewed by the lead researcher to ensure accuracy.

Credibility was further reinforced through investigator triangulation: themes emerging from FGDs were systematically compared with findings from KIIs and reconciled before being integrated with the quantitative results. In addition, negative case analysis was used to identify perspectives that diverged from dominant patterns. This approach ensured that minority viewpoints particularly those from remote char areas in Barguna were preserved in the final interpretation rather than overshadowed by more prevalent narratives.

Triangulation strategy

Triangulation in this mixed-methods study was applied at multiple levels to strengthen the validity of findings and ensure that interpretations were not unduly reliant on any single data source. The following triangulation strategies were employed:Data Triangulation (between quantitative and qualitative strands): Findings from the QUAN strand (hospital MIS records and meteorological correlations) were systematically compared with findings from the qual strand (KIIs and FGDs) to assess whether the two independent data sources converged on the same conclusions or revealed complementary dimensions of the research questions. For example, the quantitative evidence of a temperature-diarrhea association peaking in March–May was triangulated against qualitative accounts of increasing disease burden during the hot season from KII participants and FGD communities. Similarly, the high discharge-on-request rate at Jhalokathi District Hospital (34.01%) identified quantitatively was triangulated against KII reports of prohibitive transport costs in flood-prone areas, confirming that the administrative pattern reflects access barriers rather than clinical recovery. Multiple research team members reviewed transcripts independently and compared thematic interpretations to reduce individual interpretive bias.Methodological Triangulation (within the quantitative strand): Multiple meteorological variables (maximum temperature, rainfall) were examined in relation to diarrheal case counts to provide a richer characterization of the climate-disease relationship than any single variable could provide. Results

Focus Group Discussions (FGD) on diarrhea and climate change impacts

Diarrhea has emerged as a critical health concern in Bangladesh, particularly in areas impacted by climate change. Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) conducted in Satkhira, Jhalakathi, Barguna, and Gopalganj highlighted the ways in which rising temperatures, erratic rainfall, and frequent floods have exacerbated the prevalence of waterborne diseases. These discussions offered valuable insights into the challenges faced by local communities and proposed actionable solutions.

Climate vulnerability and disease prevalence

Participants across all FGDs emphasized the direct link between climate change and the rise in diarrhea cases. Rising temperatures create conditions favorable for bacterial growth, while floods and cyclones often contaminate water supplies, increasing the risk of outbreaks. Poor drainage systems and irregular rainfall further expose communities to unsafe water. Over the last two decades, diarrhea has become more frequent, severely affecting vulnerable populations.

Regional Observations

In Satkhira (Munshiganj, Shyamnagar, February 2, 2022), participants identified frequent flooding and salinity intrusion as primary causes of diarrhea outbreaks. Children under five and women were reported as the most vulnerable groups due to limited access to clean drinking water. While community clinics are present, they often lack essential medicines like oral saline, forcing residents to travel to Upazila Health Complexes for treatment. Participants stressed the need for improved water management, including the installation of deep tube wells, and the expansion of hygiene awareness programs.

In Jhalakathi (Kewra Union, February 13, 2022), recurring floods and rising temperatures were cited as major contributors to diarrhea. Vulnerable groups, including children and elderly individuals, are most affected. The lack of adequate medical supplies and the frequent absence of doctors at community clinics exacerbate the issue. Participants recommended enhancing drainage systems, providing emergency supplies during floods, and conducting community-level campaigns on sanitation practices.

In Barguna (BFDC Ghat, Patharghata, February 15, 2022), tidal surges and salinity intrusion have worsened water quality, leading to year-round diarrhea outbreaks. Healthcare facilities in this region face significant challenges, including shortages of experienced doctors and essential medicines. Many participants reported that divisional hospitals are often the only viable option for severe cases, although travel to these facilities is expensive and time-consuming. To address these issues, participants advocated for modernizing local healthcare infrastructure and implementing rainwater harvesting systems to ensure a consistent supply of safe drinking water.

In Gopalganj (Char Manikdah Community Clinic, February 18, 2022), erratic rainfall and waterlogging were identified as significant contributors to diarrhea. While healthcare access is better than in coastal areas, seasonal spikes in diarrhea persist, particularly among pregnant women and children. Participants suggested expanding awareness campaigns to educate the public on the importance of hygiene and ensuring year-round access to clean water through infrastructure improvements.

Contrasting pathways and common challenges

Findings from the FGDs across the four districts point to both shared experiences and important local differences in how communities face climate-related diarrheal risks. Across all sites, participants described rising temperatures and shifting seasonal patterns as key factors behind increasing disease burden-an observation that aligns with the study’s quantitative evidence of a significant temperature–diarrhea relationship (r = 0.228, p = 0.0012). Limited access to healthcare also emerged as a common concern, often reflected in medicine shortages and a heavy reliance on distant referral hospitals. In every district, young children and women were consistently identified as the most vulnerable groups.

At the same time, the underlying environmental drivers varied notably from place to place. In Satkhira and Barguna, communities described salinity intrusion as a persistent, year-round challenge that contaminates tube wells and limits the use of surface water for long periods. Many participants felt this problem has worsened over time, driven by sea-level rise and stronger tidal influences. In contrast, participants in Jhalakathi and Gopalganj pointed to episodic flooding and waterlogging as the primary concern, with contamination risks peaking during the monsoon season.

Differences also emerged in how communities experience and respond to healthcare challenges. In Satkhira and Barguna, shortages of trained medical staff were particularly acute, whereas in Jhalakathi and Gopalganj, participants highlighted poor drainage systems and the lack of timely early-warning information as major gaps.

These distinctions have clear implications for adaptation planning. Chronic salinity exposure in Satkhira and Barguna calls for long-term infrastructure solutions such as deep tube wells and rainwater harvesting. Meanwhile, in Jhalakathi and Gopalganj, where risks are more episodic, improving drainage systems and strengthening community-level early-warning mechanisms may offer more effective responses.

Healthcare challenges

The FGDs revealed systemic gaps in healthcare delivery. Many community clinics lack essential medicines and trained staff, leaving rural populations underserved. Severe cases are frequently referred to divisional hospitals, which are often inaccessible for low-income families due to high travel costs. Moreover, awareness programs focusing on hygiene and the health impacts of climate change are insufficient, leaving communities ill-equipped to prevent diarrhea outbreaks.

Thematic analysis of FGD findings

Thematic analysis of FGD data across the four districts identified three cross-cutting themes shaping community experiences of climate-induced diarrheal vulnerability. First, water insecurity emerged as a key driver, though manifested differently: salinity intrusion contaminated tube wells in Satkhira and Barguna (a chronic, sea-level related hazard), while waterlogging affected shallow wells and pit latrines in Jhalakathi and Gopalganj (an episodic rainfall-related hazard). Communities linked both conditions to perceived climate changes over the past decade, aligning with the quantitative temperature-diarrhea association. Second, vulnerability varied by gender and age, driven primarily by women’s water and food roles and children’s peri-domestic exposure, highlighting social pathways rather than biological susceptibility. Third, healthcare inaccessibility acted as an amplifier, as shortages of oral saline during the March–May peak period coincided with increased climate-related disease demand, intensifying diarrheal burden beyond climate exposure alone.

The FGDs highlighted diarrhea as a growing climate-induced health crisis in regions like Satkhira, Jhalakathi, Barguna, and Gopalganj. Rising temperatures, floods, and inadequate sanitation systems are driving the spread of waterborne diseases, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations. Addressing these challenges requires a multi-faceted approach that combines improved infrastructure, enhanced healthcare services, and robust public education. By implementing these strategies, the health impacts of climate change can be mitigated, improving the well-being of at-risk communities.

Key Informant Interviews (KII) on diarrhea and climate change impacts

Diarrhea continues to be a significant public health challenge in Bangladesh, particularly in regions most affected by climate change. Key Informant Interviews (KII) conducted with health officials, community leaders, and environmental experts in Satkhira, Jhalakathi, Barguna, and Gopalganj revealed critical insights into how rising temperatures, erratic rainfall, and frequent floods exacerbate diarrhea outbreaks. Specific accounts from informants like Dr. Mahmuda Akter, Mr. Abdur Rahman, and Ms. Farida Begum provided valuable perspectives on the systemic challenges and potential interventions. It should be noted that the named informants (Dr. Akter, Mr. Rahman, Ms. Begum, Dr. Hossain) are cited as illustrative examples representing the views of their respective district informant groups; they do not constitute the sole basis for any interpretive claim. Each thematic finding reported in this section is grounded in convergent perspectives across multiple KII participants. Across the 28 KIIs conducted in five upazilas (Shyamnagar, Kathalia, Jhalakathi Sadar, Gopalganj Sadar, and Patharghata), comprising 14 doctors, 5 health assistants/CHCPs, 5 midwives, and 4 teachers, thematic saturation was reached for the three principal themes identified: (i) climate change as an accelerant of existing vulnerabilities; (ii) health system fragility as a structural mediator; and (iii) a knowledge–action gap at the community level. Individual quotes and accounts are used to illustrate these convergent themes, not to stand in for independent thematic claims.

Climate change and diarrhea prevalence

The KIIs highlighted a direct relationship between climate change and the rising incidence of diarrhea. Informants noted that higher temperatures facilitate bacterial growth in water sources, while floods and cyclones contaminate drinking water, making diarrhea outbreaks more frequent. Poor drainage systems and irregular rainfall patterns further expose communities to unsafe water. Over the past two decades, the prevalence of diarrhea has increased significantly, with vulnerable populations such as children under five and pregnant women being disproportionately affected.

Regional Observations

In Satkhira (Shyamnagar, February 5, 2022), Dr. Mahmuda Akter, a local healthcare provider, emphasized that frequent flooding and salinity intrusion are major contributors to diarrhea outbreaks. Children and women are particularly vulnerable due to their dependence on contaminated surface water. Community clinics in the area often lack essential medicines such as oral saline and antibiotics. Dr. Akter recommended installing deep tube wells and expanding rainwater harvesting systems to improve access to clean water. She also stressed the need for hygiene education campaigns targeting rural households.

In Jhalakathi (Kathalia, February 12, 2022), Mr. Abdur Rahman, a community leader, described how rising temperatures and recurrent cyclones exacerbate diarrhea cases in the region. He noted that the elderly and children are the most affected, particularly during the rainy season. Limited medical supplies and inadequate healthcare infrastructure were identified as significant barriers to treatment. Mr. Rahman advocated for upgrading drainage systems, providing emergency medical supplies during floods, and conducting community awareness programs to improve hygiene and sanitation.

In Barguna (Patharghata, February 16, 2022), Ms. Farida Begum, a health worker, highlighted the impact of tidal surges and waterlogging on the year-round prevalence of diarrhea. Different risks and shared vulnerabilities

A comparison of KII accounts across the four districts reveals both important overlaps and clear regional differences, each with direct relevance to the research questions. In Satkhira and Barguna, key informants consistently pointed to salinity intrusion as the main chronic stressor-a persistent, year-round issue that makes tube well water unsafe for drinking and cannot be resolved through seasonal measures alone.

In contrast, informants in Jhalakathi and Gopalganj linked peaks in diarrheal disease to short-term, episodic events such as recurring cyclones, seasonal river overflow, and inadequate drainage systems that periodically overwhelm sanitation infrastructure. These differing exposure patterns are mirrored in the responses they consider most effective. Informants from Satkhira and Barguna emphasized long-term infrastructure solutions, including deep tube wells, desalination, and rainwater harvesting. Meanwhile, those from Jhalakathi and Gopalganj focused on improving drainage and ensuring the timely supply of emergency medical resources during flood periods.

Despite these differences, a strong point of convergence emerged across all districts. Informants uniformly highlighted weaknesses in the healthcare system-particularly shortages of oral rehydration salts and trained personnel during the March–May peak season-as a critical gap that intensifies the disease burden, regardless of the specific environmental drivers involved.

Taken together, these findings sharpen the study’s understanding of district-specific vulnerability and underscore the need for tailored, context-sensitive adaptation strategies rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

Healthcare challenges

Key informants across all regions pointed to systemic challenges in healthcare delivery. Many community clinics lack essential medicines and trained medical personnel, leaving rural populations without adequate care. Severe cases are often referred to divisional hospitals, but the high cost of transportation and treatment makes this option inaccessible for many families. Furthermore, there is a lack of targeted public health initiatives focusing on hygiene education and the prevention of climate-induced diseases.

Thematic analysis of KII findings

Thematic analysis of KII data identified three themes that complement and deepen the FGD findings. First, climate change was widely perceived as accelerating existing vulnerabilities rather than creating new risks. Key informants across sectors described diarrheal disease as worsening over the past 10–15 years due to increased flooding, prolonged dry periods, and salinity intrusion—trends consistent with regional climate evidence. This alignment between professional observation and environmental data strengthens the study’s climate–health interpretation. Second, health system fragility emerged as a structural mediator linking climate exposure to disease burden. Informants highlighted medicine shortages, staff constraints, and referral costs as systemic barriers that limit effective response during peak disease periods. While FGDs reflected community experiences of inaccessibility, KIIs explained the institutional causes behind these gaps. Third, a clear knowledge–action gap was observed. Although communities understood the risks of contaminated water, limited resources and institutional support prevented preventive action, underscoring the need for climate-adaptive public health interventions.

Etic and emic perspectives on recommendations

The emic perspective from FGDs highlights immediate, practical needs shaped by lived experience access to oral saline, nearby safe water sources, and drainage systems that prevent flood-related contamination. Though not framed as climate adaptation, these solutions function as everyday adaptive responses. By contrast, the etic perspective from KIIs presents recommendations at the systemic level, emphasizing supply-chain strengthening, clinical protocol improvement, behavior-change communication, and infrastructure investment. These translate community vulnerabilities into scalable policy actions. Rather than conflicting, the two perspectives are complementary: emic views identify where support is needed, while etic views explain how systems must change to deliver it. The gap in framing shows that community knowledge remains underrepresented in policy design. Integrating both perspectives is therefore essential for effective and sustainable climate-adaptive public health planning in coastal Bangladesh.

Climate change vulnerability and diarrheal disease: analytical interpretation of quantitative findings

This study interprets diarrheal disease vulnerability in coastal Bangladesh through a climate-change lens. The significant temperature–diarrhea association (r = 0.228, p = 0.0012) suggests that rising temperatures are increasing exposure risk, as peak incidence occurs within the 30–35 °C range already common across the study districts. Continued warming is therefore likely to expand high-risk periods even without changes in sanitation or healthcare access. The consistent March–May peak aligns with pre-monsoon conditions, when high temperatures and low water tables concentrate pathogens in shallow water sources. The non-significant rainfall relationship reflects opposing effects: moderate rainfall increases contamination, while heavy rainfall spreads but may dilute pathogens. Increasing climate variability may intensify both processes, meaning current findings could underestimate future risk. Higher incidence among young children and females further indicates exposure shaped by caregiving roles and water-use practices rather than biology alone. Overall, the results point to a climate-sensitive vulnerability profile in which temperature rise, rainfall variability, and coastal environmental change jointly influence diarrheal disease risk.

To anchor these interpretations in the study’s own evidence, three clear links between the quantitative results and climate change vulnerability stand out.

First, the six-year MIS dataset (2017–2022; n = 46,741) shows a consistent seasonal pattern: diarrheal admissions across all five facilities rise sharply from March to May-the hottest months of the year. In April 2019 and April 2022, admissions exceeded 600 cases per month, noticeably higher than the average during the cooler October–February period (around 648 cases per month). This pattern, reinforced by a statistically significant association with temperature (r = 0.228, p = 0.0012), provides direct, study-specific evidence that temperature-a climate-sensitive variable-plays a meaningful role in shaping diarrheal disease burden in this context.

Second, facility-level differences offer further insight. Shyamnagar Upazila Health Complex in Satkhira, which serves populations heavily exposed to chronic salinity intrusion, recorded the highest improve-and-discharge rate (99.01%). While this suggests that acute cases are being effectively treated, it also points to a deeper issue: the underlying driver-saline-contaminated water-remains unaddressed between episodes. This aligns with a climate exposure framework in which vulnerability persists despite successful short-term clinical outcomes.

Third, the notably high discharge-on-request rate at Jhalakathi District Hospital (34.01%), compared with other facilities, hints at barriers to healthcare access. Factors such as travel distance and transportation costs-especially in flood-prone areas-likely discourage patients from completing treatment. These constraints can intensify disease burden by interrupting care and increasing the risk of recurrence or complications.

Taken together, these observations ground the analysis firmly in the dataset, linking quantitative patterns to the exposure vulnerability framework while also reflecting district-specific realities. This approach avoids broad generalizations and instead builds a more nuanced, evidence-based understanding of climate-related health risks.

Discussion

The findings of this study illuminate the patterns and drivers of diarrheal disease vulnerability across four coastal districts of Bangladesh in the context of climatic variability. They provide empirical insight into how seasonal temperature shifts, changing rainfall patterns, and location-specific environmental pressures interact with social and infrastructural conditions to influence disease risk. The evidence from focus group discussions (FGDs), key informant interviews (KIIs), and secondary data analysis highlights a strong correlation between rising temperatures, extreme weather events, and increased incidence of diarrheal diseases. These results align with previous studies that have established climate change as a critical determinant of public health [1, 3, 20]. Beyond confirming alignment with prior literature, the following subsections interpret the specific mechanisms, contextual differences, and mixed-methods linkages that shape the observed patterns in this deltaic coastal setting.

The discussion is structured around the Convergent Parallel Design (QUAN + qual → Merge) used in this study. Results from the quantitative strand (hospital MIS records, meteorological correlations) and the qualitative strand (KIIs and FGDs) are first presented separately in the Results section, then converged here through triangulation. This approach follows the integration typology proposed by Fetters, Curry, and Creswell 26.

Temperature and diarrheal disease prevalence

The study findings revealed that diarrheal cases are more prevalent in months with higher temperatures, particularly from March to May. This trend supports existing literature suggesting that higher temperatures create favorable conditions for bacterial growth in food and water sources, thereby increasing the risk of diarrheal outbreaks (Rylander, Odland, & Sandanger, [15, 30]. The positive association between maximum temperature and diarrhea occurrences is consistent with research indicating that prolonged heatwaves exacerbate microbial contamination of drinking water [14]. In this setting, the mechanism operates through two local pathways: accelerated pathogen growth in surface water during the pre-monsoon heat, and increased reliance on unprotected sources when tube wells are saline-intruded—a pattern corroborated by KII accounts from Satkhira and Barguna. The correlation (r = 0.228, R2 = 0.052) is nonetheless weak and likely confounded by humidity, sanitation access, and care-seeking behaviour,the association should be treated as indicative rather than causal.

Seasonality versus long-term climate variability effect

A central interpretive issue in this study concerns the distinction between intra-annual seasonality and long-term climate change as drivers of diarrheal disease patterns. The observed March–May peak in diarrheal admissions reflects a recurrent seasonal pattern tied to the pre-monsoon temperature cycle, which is a regular feature of Bangladesh’s climate regardless of long-term change. It is therefore important not to conflate seasonal variation with climate change effects, as the two operate at different temporal scales and through partially distinct mechanisms.

Seasonality refers to the predictable intra-annual cycle in which diarrheal cases rise during the hot pre-monsoon months (March–May) and decline during the cooler post-monsoon period (October–February). This pattern is documented in the study’s six-year MIS data and is consistent across all five health facilities. In contrast, long-term climate change refers to multi-decadal trends such as progressively rising mean temperatures, altered monsoon onset and intensity, increased frequency of extreme heat days, and accelerating sea-level rise that are modifying the baseline conditions within which seasonal cycles operate. The World Bank Group [8] projects that Bangladesh will experience an increase from 99.66 to 133.47 days annually with a Heat Index above 35℃ by mid-century under the SSP3-7.0 scenario, which implies that the “high-risk temperature window” of 30–35℃ identified in this study will become wider and more prolonged over time.

The present study’s six-year observational window (2017–2022) is insufficient to detect multi-decadal climate trends statistically; it can only document the current expression of seasonal temperature effects on diarrheal burden. The association (r = 0.228, p = 0.0012) captures how temperature variation within and across years relates to case counts, but cannot isolate long-term anthropogenic warming from natural inter-annual variability. To attribute observed changes in diarrheal burden to climate change rather than seasonality, longer time-series analyses with detrending of seasonal cycles, or comparison against historical baselines that predate the current warming trajectory, would be required. Nevertheless, the significance of the temperature-diarrhea relationship within this study period, combined with the projected widening of extreme heat exposure under climate change scenarios, provides a sound scientific basis for framing this study within a climate-change vulnerability framework. The implication is not that seasonality and climate change are synonymous, but rather that climate change is progressively amplifying the existing seasonal risk-extending the duration and intensity of the high-risk pre-monsoon window and increasing the frequency of conditions that trigger diarrheal outbreaks. This distinction should be explicitly acknowledged in the framing and interpretation of the results.

Rainfall patterns and disease incidence

A sharp decline in diarrheal cases was observed with increased rainfall, particularly beyond 60 mm. This finding suggests that excessive rainfall may help dilute contaminants in water sources, reducing the risk of infection. However, erratic rainfall and flooding events contribute to waterborne disease outbreaks by overwhelming sanitation infrastructure and mixing human waste with drinking water sources [21, 22, 31]. The results corroborate previous studies demonstrating that climate-induced floods heighten exposure to waterborne pathogens such as Vibrio cholerae and Escherichia coli [23, 24]. This narrative must be reconciled with the non-significant result (r = 0.094, p = 0.235). The decline likely reflects seasonal confounding, as low rainfall months are also cooler. In this context, moderate rainfall can contaminate shallow wells by overflowing pit latrines (reported in Satkhira and Jhalakathi FGDs), while heavy rainfall may dilute pathogens-offsetting effects that produce the null result.

Regional variations and healthcare challenges

Findings from FGDs and KIIs indicate that different coastal districts experience varying levels of vulnerability to diarrheal diseases. For instance, Satkhira and Barguna reported higher disease burdens due to frequent tidal surges and salinity intrusion, limiting access to safe drinking water. In contrast, Jhalakathi and Gopalganj, despite better healthcare access, still faced seasonal spikes in diarrhea cases due to waterlogging and inadequate sanitation [17]. These regional disparities highlight the need for localized adaptation strategies to combat climate-induced health risks [19]. These differences reflect distinct mechanisms: Satkhira and Barguna face chronic salinity-driven water insecurity requiring structural infrastructure solutions, while Jhalakathi and Gopalganj experience episodic flooding-driven contamination more amenable to drainage and early-warning interventions. Treating all districts uniformly in adaptation planning risks misallocating resources.

A major concern raised in the study is the inadequacy of healthcare infrastructure in affected regions. Many community clinics lack essential medicines and trained personnel, compelling patients to travel long distances for treatment [18, 32]. High discharge rates from hospitals indicate that while patients may recover with medical intervention, preventive measures such as hygiene awareness campaigns and improved sanitation infrastructure are still insufficient. Integrating both datasets reveals that oral saline shortages reported in FGDs coincide with the March–May case spike in the quantitative data, indicating that healthcare bottlenecks amplify climate-driven disease burden. Similarly, the high discharge-on-request rate at Jhalokathi (34.01%) aligns with KII reports of prohibitive transport costs, showing that administrative discharge patterns reflect access barriers rather than clinical recovery. These convergences confirm that the QUAN and qual strands yield consistent conclusions across the core findings, satisfying the integration criterion of the Convergent Parallel Design and lending greater credibility to the study’s overall interpretation.

Gender and age-related vulnerabilities

The study also found that females had a slightly higher prevalence of diarrheal diseases compared to males (52.7% vs. 47.3%). This aligns with findings that suggest women, particularly pregnant women, face greater risks due to nutritional deficiencies and increased exposure to unsafe water sources [16, 33]. Additionally, children under five were identified as the most vulnerable age group, a pattern widely reported in previous research on climate-sensitive diseases [12, 34]. In this context, women’s elevated risk is driven by their roles in water collection and food preparation, which involve prolonged exposure to contaminated sources, compounded by mobility constraints that delay care-seeking. For children under five, immature gut immunity combined with peri-domestic open-defecation exposure explains the disproportionate burden.

Policy implications and recommendations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The bivariate analysis does not control for confounders such as humidity, sanitation coverage, or healthcare access, so temperature associations cannot be treated as causal. Raw case counts lack population denominators, limiting cross-district comparisons. Facility-based data also underrepresent true community burden, particularly in access-constrained districts. Future studies should employ multivariable modelling and community-based surveillance to address these gaps.

Conclusion

The study confirms that climate change plays a pivotal role in the increasing prevalence of diarrheal diseases in coastal Bangladesh. Rising temperatures, erratic rainfall, and extreme weather events exacerbate public health challenges, particularly in vulnerable communities. This underscores the pressing need for policy interventions that integrate climate resilience with healthcare planning and infrastructure improvements. Without urgent action, the health burden on coastal populations will continue to rise, leading to increased morbidity and economic strain on affected households. Critically, this study advances understanding beyond prior work by demonstrating that the climate–diarrhea relationship in coastal Bangladesh is not uniform but shaped by district-specific exposure pathways—chronic salinity intrusion in Satkhira and Barguna versus episodic flood-driven contamination in Jhalakathi and Gopalganj-a distinction absent from previous national-level analyses. This distinction is supported by converging evidence from both quantitative and qualitative sources. Data from Shyamnagar Upazila Health Complex in Satkhira show a consistent, year-round pattern with the highest improve-and-discharge rate (99.01%), suggesting chronic exposure to contaminated water, while Jhalakathi District Hospital’s high discharge-on-request rate (34.01%) points to episodic access barriers, likely linked to flooding. Qualitative findings mirror this contrast: participants in Satkhira described permanent salinity damage to tube wells, whereas those in Jhalakathi and Gopalganj associated diarrheal risk with floods and the monsoon. Together, these aligned patterns provide a clear, data-driven basis for the district-specific characterization. It also establishes, for the first time using six years of sub-district MIS data, that temperature is a statistically significant but contextually weak predictor (R2 = 0.052), and that rainfall exerts no independent linear effect, resolving an ambiguity in the existing literature that had conflated seasonal confounding with protective rainfall effects.

Furthermore, the findings highlight the necessity for a multi-sectoral approach involving government agencies, healthcare providers, and community stakeholders. Strengthening healthcare services, expanding access to clean water, and enhancing public awareness campaigns should be central to future adaptation strategies. Long-term epidemiological studies focusing on climate-health linkages will also be critical in designing effective mitigation plans. Addressing climate-induced diarrheal diseases requires immediate, coordinated efforts at local, national, and global levels. By implementing proactive strategies, such as investing in resilient healthcare systems and ensuring equitable access to sanitation facilities, policymakers can mitigate health risks and build climate-resilient communities. Future research should aim to refine intervention strategies by incorporating real-time climate monitoring data and disease surveillance to provide more targeted responses to emerging health threats. Specifically, future studies should: (i) apply distributed lag non-linear models to disentangle temperature and rainfall effects across the 0–4 week exposure window; (ii) normalise case counts by catchment population to enable valid inter-district burden comparisons; (iii) incorporate sanitation coverage and healthcare access indices as covariates to test whether infrastructure moderates climate sensitivity; and (iv) conduct community-based surveillance in Satkhira and Barguna to capture the true burden missed by facility-only MIS data. These steps would transform the descriptive associations reported here into a causal evidence base capable of informing district-specific climate-health adaptation plans.
