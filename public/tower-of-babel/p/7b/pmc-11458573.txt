The presence of a significant endophytic fungus in mycobiome of rice seed compartments
Authors: Eunji Jeong, Najib Abdellaoui, Jae Yun Lim, Jeong-Ah Seo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Seed microbial communities have been known to have a crucial role in the life cycle of a plant. In this study, we examined the distribution of the fungal communities in three compartments (husk, brown rice, and milled rice) of the fourteen rice seed samples. Ten fungal genera distributed throughout the three compartments of the rice seeds were identified as the core mycobiome of the rice seeds, regardless of collecting regions or cultivars. Based on the diversity analysis, the distribution of the fungal community in milled rice was found to be more diversified, evenly distributed, and differently clustered from the other two compartments. Among the core mycobiome, Moesziomyces dominated almost 80% of the fungal communities in the outer compartments of rice seeds, whereas the abundances of other endophytic pathogenic fungi declined. Our results provide that antagonistic yeast Moesziomyces may be able to control the endogenous pathogenic fungal communities in rice seeds, hence maintaining the quality of rice seeds. In addition, the distribution of fungal communities differs depending on the rice seed’s compartment, indicating that the compartment can affect the distribution of the seed microbial community.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73550-1.

Introduction

The plant-associated microbial community performs a variety of important functions related to the crop development1,2, productivity3, and adaptation to abiotic stresses4,5. Recent studies have shown that microorganisms living on the surface of plants (epiphytes) and inside plants (endophytes) protect against pathogens and improve plant growth and productivity6–8. And much research has been conducted to elucidate the plant microbiome of various plants9,10. Among the plant microbiome, the rhizosphere, which harbors diverse microbial species from the surrounding soil, is a major focus of research within the plant microbiome11. In addition, seed microbiomes have attracted attention because they can be passed on to the next generation and influence the development of the microbiome throughout the plant life cycle12–14.

Rice (Oryza sativa) is a staple crop for a large population, particularly in Asia15,16, and rice seeds are directly consumed after processing into brown rice and milled rice17. Previous studies on the microbial communities of rice seeds have shown that the rice seed microbiome can promote crop growth and productivity, but may also compromise rice quality and safety18,19. Many bacterial genera commonly found in rice seeds, such as Curtobacterium, Methylobacterium, and Pantoea, have been shown to promote plant growth20,21. Meanwhile, the fungal communities reported in rice are known to mainly consist of Alternatia, Cladosporium, and Fusarium, which are rice pathogens that threaten rice quality and safety11,22,23. In addition, previous study has shown that the rice microbial communities can be distributed differently depending on the compartments of rice seeds, regardless of the collection areas or cultivars24. Therefore, if epiphytic and endophytic microorganisms existing in each compartment of rice seeds are identified at the species level, it is expected that more in-depth information on the rice microbiome can be obtained25,26.

In this study, we separated rice seeds collected from rice producing areas in Korea into husk, brown rice, and milled rice, and investigated the fungal composition of epiphytic or endophytic fungi from the three compartments. Based on the metagenome analysis, we attempted to find meaningful proportions and diversity of fungal communities in three separate compartments of rice seeds. Fundamentally, if we better understand the nature of the microbial community distribution and community dynamics in rice seeds, we may be able to get ideas for protecting important crops from fungal pathogens in a more natural way.

Results

Fungal metagenome analysis of three compartments of rice seed: husk, brown rice, and milled rice

The fungal communities of the rice seed were profiled in three distinct compartments of fourteen rice seed samples, namely the husk, brown rice, and milled rice (Fig. 1). High-quality ITS1 reads were generated from 77,258 to 148,666 and a total of 894 fungal operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were determined at 97% sequence identity (Table 1). We classified a total of 894 fungal OTUs into 3 phyla, 21 classes, 68 orders, 141 families, 211 genera, and 265 species, excluding the unknown and unclassified ones (Supplementary Table S1). The average number of fungal OTUs found in husk, brown rice, and milled rice was 97, 141, and 121, respectively. The rarefaction curves for richness in all three compartments of rice seeds meet at a point between 3500 and 5000 reads (Fig. 2), showing that the sequencing depths of all samples were good enough. There was a noticeable difference in reaching the asymptote point between milled rice and the other two groups. The fungal rarefaction curve of milled rice reached the asymptote faster than that of husk and brown rice, indicating that the fungal richness of milled rice is higher than that of the other two groups.

Diversity of the fungal communities of three compartments of the rice seed

The alpha diversity was measured in three compartments of rice seeds using the fungal OTU richness, Chao1 index, Shannon index, and Simpson index (Fig. 3). The number of fungal OTUs observed in husk, brown rice, and milled rice ranged from 26 to 38, and increased in the inner compartment of rice seed (Fig. 3a). There was a significant difference in the number of fungal OTUs observed in husk and milled rice among the three compartments of rice seeds (P = 0.009). The Chao1 index was also increased in the inner compartments of rice seeds, being significantly higher in milled rice than in husk (P = 0.005). The fungal OTU richness and Chao1 index increased in milled rice than in husk, indicating that milled rice harbors a considerable fungal community. Similarly, the Shannon index results showed that there was no significant difference in the fungal community diversity between husk and brown rice (P = 0.270), but milled rice showed a greater diversity of fungal communities than husk (P = 9e−10) and brown rice (P = 6e−10) (Fig. 3b). In addition, the results showed that the fungal diversity of milled rice was more evenly distributed than husk (P = 1e−8) and brown rice (P = 3e−12). In summary, the metagenome analysis indicated that the diversity and evenness of the interior fungal community of rice seeds were greater than those of the externa compartments, brown rice and husk.

The beta-diversity was measured in three compartments of rice seeds using PCoA with the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix, and the internal relationships between husk and brown rice were confirmed (Fig. 4). The PCoA matrix shows that fungal communities are clustered by the three compartments of the rice seed. The fungal PCoA for the two compartments, husk and brown rice, overlapped, indicating that fungal communities associated with husk and brown rice were similar. In contrast, the fungal PCoA of the milled rice clustered very distinctly with husk and brown rice (Fig. 4). These results indicate that there is a significant statistical difference in the fungal community diversity between the milled rice and the outer compartments surrounding it.

Taxon composition of the fungal communities of three compartments of rice seeds

We primarily detected 894 fungal OTUs, identifying 3 phylum, 211 genera, and 265 species (Supplementary Table S1). The two main phyla detected in rice seed were Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, with other minor taxa including Mucoromycota, and unknown or unclassified fungi (Supplementary Table S2). The Basidiomycota phylum has the highest percentages in husk and brown rice with 79.8 ± 12.8% and 78.3 ± 9.2%, respectively, followed by Ascomycota with 19.3 ± 12.8% and 21.1 ± 8.9% (Fig. 5a). On the other hand, the Ascomycota phylum has the highest percentages in milled rice, with 62.9 ± 14.6%, followed by Basidiomycota with 36.7 ± 14.4%.

Among the 211 genera, ten had an abundance greater than 1% in at least one sample (Fig. 5b; Table 2, Supplementary Table S3). Among the 10 genera, Moesziomyces was the most abundant genus in all rice seed compartments, with an overall abundance ranging from 17.6% (milled rice of SW1 rice seed sample) to 90.3% (husk of CJ1 rice seed sample). In husk and brown rice, Moesziomyces was the predominant genus, accounting for 69.8 ± 12.3% and 76.6 ± 9.6%, respectively. In contrast, the abundance of Moesziomyces in milled rice was significantly lower, accounting for 35.8 ± 14.2%, than in husk (P = 4e−07) and brown rice (P = 7e−09). We identified two Moesziomyces species (M. antarcticus and M. aphidis), of which M. antarcticus was the most abundant species in all compartments, with abundances ranging from 14.8 to 90.0% (Supplementary Table S3). Besides Moesziomyces, four genera showed significant differences in abundance between milled rice and the other two compartments of the rice seed (Table 2). The mean relative abundance of Alternaria, Epicoccum, Nigrospora, and Phaeosphaeria in milled rice were 15.2%, 11.3%, 6.9%, and 3.5, respectively, which were approximately three to five times higher than those in husk and brown rice (P < 0.01). The abundance of Neosetophoma was less than 5% in all three compartments, but there was a significant difference in abundance between husk and milled rice (P = 0.001). Cladosporium and Fusarium were abundant in all three compartments of rice seeds (> 3.5%), but there was no statistically significant difference (Table 2). Cladosporium showed the highest abundance in husk, accounting for approximately 7.7%, and showed the greatest difference from the milled rice (P = 0.030). Fusarium species were most abundant in milled rice, accounting for about 8.9%, and the difference from the husk was the largest (P = 0.045) (Table 2, Supplementary Table S3).

The Spearman correlation coefficients identified 7, 13, and 5 significant correlations (|Rho| > 0.5) among the 10 fungal genera in husk, brown rice, and milled rice, respectively (Fig. 6). Discussion

It is known that various environmental conditions have a great influence on the composition of the microbial community of plants, including rice. In particular, since rice is a food crop that must be healthy and highly productive, the most interesting topic is how the microbial community in rice, including endophytic fungi, is formed and how it affects the crop. Microorganisms associated with rice seeds have attracted much attention because they affect not only the quality and safety of the seeds but also the growth and productivity of the host plant25,27,28. Previous study has reported that compartments within rice seeds have a great influence on the formation of fungal communities24. In this study, we carefully considered how to divide these important compartments and decided to divide them according to the actual rice consumption pattern. In Korea, rice seeds are harvested and the husk is removed to produce brown rice, which is then milled to make milled rice for consumption. Therefore, each rice seed sample was divided into three compartments (Fig. 1): husk, brown rice, and milled rice, and the fungal communities were analyzed in the three compartments of each 14 sample. Numerous studies have suggested that rice seeds, regardless of genotype or sampling location, contain a core microbiota and that this microbiota plays a role in shaping the plant microbiome via vertical transmission28–30. Our study revealed significant taxonomic overlap in the fungal communities of three compartments of rice seed, and we identified the core mycobiome of ten genera, distributed with high abundances across the three compartments (Table 2). We also analyzed differences in fungal distribution across seeds by region or cultivar, but similar to previous studies, we did not find statistically significant differences (data not shown)11,24. Consequently, we focused only on the differences in fungal distribution across the three compartments of the rice seeds.

The outer compartment of the seed protects the seed while also acting as a channel for transmitting environmental signals into the seed inside, promoting embryo and endosperm development23,31,32. Previous studies have shown that bacterial and fungal communities differ in different parts of the seed, suggesting that the seed compartment may affect the growth of the microbial community24. Our results also indicated that the diversity and evenness of the fungal distribution vary depending on the compartment of the rice seeds (Figs. 3 and 4). The analysis revealed that the high abundance, diversity, and evenness of the fungal communities identified in milled rice, were higher than those in the outermost layer (husk) and brown rice (Fig. 3). The milled rice had more fungal richness than the other two compartments, as shown by the total number of fungal OTUs and the Chao1 diversity index (Fig. 3a). Additionally, the milled rice exhibited a notably higher Shannon index and Simpson index compared to the other two compartments, indicating a more diverse fungal communities were distributed evenly in the milled rice (Fig. 3b). The high alpha diversity index of milled rice suggests that the diverse fungal communities are evenly distributed in the inner compartment of the rice seed, while a small number of fungal communities dominate husk and brown rice. Our results contradict a previous report that the diversity of the husk is higher than that of the grain at the fungal genus level24. Moreover, the beta diversity index showed that the milled rice had a distinct clustering pattern, while husk and brown rice samples exhibited some overlap (Fig. 4). Our results suggest that seed compartments significantly influenced the formation of rice seed fungal microbiome27,33,34.

Results showed that Basidiomycota dominated the fungal communities of husk and brown rice, but not of milled rice, the innermost compartment of the rice seed (Fig. 5; Table 2). Basidiomycota accounted for approximately 80% of all fungi found in husk and brown rice (Fig. 5a), which was similar to its distribution in rice phyllosphere (77–100%) reported in previous studies24,35. In this study, most of the Basidiomycetes found in husk and brown rice were identified as Moesziomyces, which constitutes the majority of the fungal community in the outer compartment of rice seeds, so the fungal distributions in husk and brown rice were less diverse (Figs. 3 and 5b). In contrast, the abundance of Moesziomyces decreased to 35% in milled rice, whereas the abundance of Ascomycota increased to about 63% (Supplementary Table S2). Moesziomyces has been known as a genus of fungi widely distributed in cereal crops, Poacea, especially in rice (wild and domestic) in East Asia, and has also been reported to be commonly distributed in the phyllosphere of many other plants22,23,36–40. Ascomycetes, such as Alternaria, Epicoccum, Nigrospora, and Phaeosphaeria, generally contain the most common plant pathogenic fungi, and the previous report showed that Ascomycetes fungi similar to our results were present in the rice seed compartment, but Moesziomyceswas not found21,24,41–43. The milled rice, the innermost compartment of the rice seed, is relatively rich in plant pathogenic fungi, suggesting the possibility of seed-borne diseases. Seeds can act as a carrier of plant pathogenic fungi that affect plant growth and productivity, although this can vary depending on environmental conditions and agricultural practices44,45. Therefore, pathogenic fungi found in seeds are one of the potential threats to seed quality and crop productivity46,47. Our results suggest that the distribution of plant pathogenic fungi varies depending on the compartment of the rice seed and shows a negative correlation with the distribution of Moesziomyces (Figs. 5 and 6).

Moesziomyces, formerly known as Pseudozyma, is an endophytic fungus found in rice plant’s tissues21,22,38 and some Moesziomycesstrains have been known to have antagonistic activity against several plant pathogenic fungi48,49. Previous studies have shown that certain stains of Pseudozymacan inhibit the growth of fungal pathogens through antimicrobial and ectoparasitism50 and may also protect crops from bacterial and viral infections40,48,51. Additionally, Moesziomyces is known to be an antagonistic yeast that inhibits the growth of Fusariumstrains causing FHB in cereal crops52,53. We found a negative correlation between the distribution of plant pathogenic fungi like Alternaria, Epicoccum, and Fusarium and the antagonistic yeast Moesziomyces in rice seeds (Fig. 6), indicating a decrease in plant pathogenic fungi with an increase in Moesziomyces. Our results and previous studies suggest that Moesziomyces may influence the development of the rice seed mycobiome, including pathogenic fungi, by dominating its distribution or interacting with other fungi that have not been previously discovered.

Regardless of genotype, rice seeds harbor a wide range of microorganisms, and these microorganisms can transmit to growing plants through seeds. Therefore, seed microbes can significantly affect seed quality and the occurrence of seed-borne plant diseases. In this study, the core mycobiome of rice seeds was relatively rich in pathogenic fungi that could act as potential pathogens. However, Moesziomyces, an antagonistic yeast, dominated the fungal community outside rice seeds (Table 2; Fig. 5b). This important endophytic fungus may influence the development of the rice mycobiome and seed safety51. Furthermore, our findings reveal that the composition and distribution of the mycobiome vary throughout the compartments of rice seeds, regardless of the collecting region and cultivars. The major factors that determine the plant microbial community are generally known to be environmental conditions and genetic variation within the host plant54. However, our study showed that the compartments of rice seeds may be a more important factor in shaping the microbial community.

Materials and methods

Collection of rice seed samples and separation of three compartments of rice seeds

Fourteen rice seed samples harvested in 2018 from eight provinces of South Korea were collected by the National Agricultural Products Quality Management Service (NAQS) in Korea with prior permission. For this study, each rice seed was separated into husk and brown rice through the dehulling process, and into milled rice through a milling process that removes bran and aleurone layers of brown rice, helped by the NAQS of South Korea (Fig. 1a)17. After separating compartments, a total of forty-two samples including husk, brown rice (hulled rice or unmilled rice), and milled rice (white rice) of fourteen rice seeds (Fig. 1b) were obtained. Each sample was stored at − 20 °C until DNA extraction.

The use of rice seed samples was in accordance with all relevant guidelines and regulations. We received permission from NAQS before collecting rice seed.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Fifty grams of three compartments of the rice seed samples were soaked in 200 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), incubated at 4 °C for 24 h, and incubated at 25 °C with 180 rpm shaking for 1 h. The culture was harvested and stored at − 20 °C until DNA extraction. The fungal DNA was extracted from the pellet using the PowerSoil gDNA extraction kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, the concentration and quality of gDNA were assessed using Nanodrop (Thermofisher, USA). In order to identify the fungal community, the ITS1 region from the rDNA gene was amplified using the universal primers ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2 (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′)55,56. The PCR was performed under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, then 25 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final elongation at 72 °C for 5 min. The amplicons were sequenced by MiSeq (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) in accordance with the service manuals provided by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea).

Metagenome data processing and analysis

The raw reads were first demultiplexed, and FastQC Version 0.11.9 was used to assess the quality of the reads. The LotuS pipeline57 was used for filtering and clustering the fungal ITS sequences based on the UNITE database58. The clustered sequences were denoised at the 97% identity level to generate operational taxonomic units (OTUs). The fungal ITS databases from NCBI were used to identify the OTUs, and the taxonomy was determined using the ETE 3 toolkit59. The level of taxonomic assignment was determined by the Lotus pipeline’s default parameters if the best hit similarity was less than 97%. These settings were 97% for species, 95% for genus, 93% for family, 91% for order, 78% for class, and 97% for phylum. To identify the distribution of fungal taxa across the three compartments of rice seeds, that accounted for at least % of the total reads in at least one sample were analyzed. And the data was processed using phyloseq60 and microbiomeutilities in the R package (https://microsud.github.io/microbiomeutilities/). The rarefaction curves for samples were calculated to a depth of 5000 reads using the Rarefy in R package (https://arts.units.it/handle/11368/2966451). The richness and diversity were calculated based on the species count table, and the differences in the alpha-diversity indicators (Observed, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson) between samples were estimated using Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric analysis of variance followed by Dunn’s posthoc test. The principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was used for the analysis of beta-diversity through the coordination of Bray-Curtis distances. The spearman correlation coefficient between the relative abundances of the top 10 fungal genera in three compartments of rice seed was calculated using pspearman in R package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=pspearman) and plotted using heatmap package in R program.
