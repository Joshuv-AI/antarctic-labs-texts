Association between gestational diabetes mellitus and maternal and neonatal outcomes in twin pregnancies: a retrospective cohort study in Guangdong, China
Authors: Liting Huang, Weipeng Xu, Wei Li, Ya Jin, Sui Zhu, Guosheng Liu, Chuan Nie, Yueqin Ding, Bingxiao Li, Shasha Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundIn singleton pregnancies, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) causes maternal and neonatal complications. However, this association remains unclear in twin pregnancies. This study aimed to investigate the association between GDM and obstetric outcomes specifically in twin pregnancies.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted among women with twin pregnancies from January 2018 to July 2022. Maternal and neonatal outcomes were compared between women with and without GDM. Adjusted ORs, 95% CIs and SEs were estimated using logistic regression, adjusting for maternal age, prepregnancy body mass index and chorionicity.ResultsA total of 808 women (1616 neonates) were included in the study, of whom 178 had GDM, while 630 did not. GDM was found to be positively associated with (adjusted OR, (95% CI)) preterm birth (1.73 (1.17 to 2.54)), premature rupture of membranes (1.70 (1.03 to 2.79)) and hypothyroidism (2.58 (1.46 to 4.56)) in women with twin pregnancies. In regard to neonatal outcomes, GDM was positively associated with admission to neonatal ward (1.49 (1.13 to 1.96)), oxygen therapy (1.91 (1.45 to 2.52)), hyperbilirubinaemia (1.44 (1.08 to 1.90)), total use and prophylactic use of antibiotics (1.98 (1.17 to 3.33) and 1.88 (1.00 to 3.54), respectively). When stratified by gestational age, GDM remained positively associated with certain adverse outcomes exclusively in twin preterm infants.ConclusionsOur study demonstrated that GDM is significantly associated with several adverse pregnancy outcomes in twin pregnancies, including hypothyroidism in pregnant women, preterm birth, admission to neonatal ward, oxygen therapy, hyperbilirubinaemia, the total and prophylactic use of antibiotics in twins. Specifically, in twin pregnancies, GDM may be more strongly associated with adverse outcomes in preterm infants than in term infants.Keywords: Diabetes in pregnancy, NEONATOLOGY, OBSTETRICS

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as a glucose tolerance disorder leading to hyperglycaemia that is first diagnosed or develops during pregnancy with the exclusion of patients meeting criteria for type 2 diabetes mellitus.1 2 The incidence of GDM is increasing due to advanced maternal age and the rising prevalence of obesity among women of reproductive age.3 With advancements in fertility treatments, the rate of twin pregnancies has also been on the rise, particularly in China following the implementation of the second-child policy. Twin pregnancy is an established independent risk factor for GDM.4

In singleton pregnancies, GDM is associated with accelerated fetal growth and various maternal and neonatal complications.5 6 However, in twin pregnancies, these associations may differ due to inherently slower fetal growth in the third trimester7 8 and a baseline elevated risk for complications.9 10

The literature on the association between GDM and obstetric outcomes in twin pregnancies remains inconsistent. Some studies have demonstrated that GDM is linked to an elevated risk of preterm birth, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, neonatal respiratory distress syndrome and admission to the neonatal intensive care unit in twin pregnancies.11,13 Conversely, other studies have reported that GDM does not significantly increase the risk of adverse outcomes in women carrying twins.14 15 Additionally, some research suggests that the risks of very preterm delivery, being small for gestational age (SGA) and low birth weight are actually reduced in twin pregnancies affected by GDM.16 Therefore, the aim of our study was to investigate the association between GDM and obstetric outcomes specifically in twin pregnancies.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a historical cohort study that included all women with twin pregnancies who delivered live-born twins in the first affiliated hospital of Jinan University and Dongguan People’s Hospital between January 2018 and July 2022. Women were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: (1) pre-existing type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus; (2) first diagnosed as hyperglycaemia during pregnancy but meeting criteria for type 2 diabetes mellitus; (3) incomplete or missing oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) results and (4) the presence of twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS).

Diagnosis of GDM

The diagnostic criteria for GDM were based on the 2010 International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups guidelines17 and the WHO criteria.2 A 75 g OGTT was performed between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation. GDM is diagnosed if one or more of the following criteria are met: fasting plasma glucose 5.1–6.9 mmol/L (92–125 mg/dL); 1-hour plasma glucose ≥10.0 mmol/L (180 mg/dL) following a 75 g oral glucose load; 2-hour plasma glucose 8.5–11.0 mmol/L (153–199 mg/dL) following a 75 g oral glucose load.

In this study, pregnant women diagnosed with GDM were referred to a dedicated GDM clinic jointly staffed by certified nutritionists, nurses with specialised training in maternal–fetal medicine and obstetric endocrinologists. Management was followed the Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Gestational Hyperglycaemia (2022),18 encompassing individualised medical nutrition therapy, structured physical activity counselling and pharmacologic intervention when indicated. Insulin therapy was initiated if glycaemic targets were not achieved (fasting plasma glucose remained ≥5.3 mmol/L, 1-hour postprandial plasma glucose ≥7.8 mmol/L or 2-hour postprandial plasma glucose ≥6.7 mmol/L) after ≥2 weeks of optimised lifestyle intervention.

Outcomes and definitions

Data on the study subjects were obtained by accessing the hospital’s electronic medical record (EMR) system. An in-house standardised questionnaire was developed for data collection, encompassing demographics as well as maternal and neonatal outcomes. To ensure data accuracy, a double-person verification and entry process was implemented. Data were accessed for research purposes from 1 January 2018 to 1 August 2022. The authors did not have access to any information that could identify individual participants during or after the data collection process.

Maternal and neonatal demographic variables included maternal age, height, prepregnancy weight, predelivery weight, gravidity, parity, in vitro fertilisation status, chorionicity, gestational age, birth weight, birth head circumference and birth length.

Maternal outcomes comprised hypothyroidism, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, premature rupture of membranes, preterm birth, chorioamnionitis and caesarean delivery.

Neonatal outcomes included admission to neonatal ward, neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, oxygen therapy, box oxygenation, nasal continuous positive airway pressure (nCPAP), invasive ventilation, use of pulmonary surfactant, hypoglycaemia, hyperbilirubinaemia, patent ductus arteriosus, hypothyroidism, infection, total and prophylactic use of antibiotics and birth defects.

In this study, the criteria for TTTS are made by ultrasound identification of both polyhydramnios for the recipient twin by a maximum vertical pocket (MVP) of >8 cm and oligohydramnios for the donor twin with an MVP <2 cm.19 Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) prior to pregnancy.20 Preterm birth was defined as a gestational age of less than 37 weeks. When hypothyroidism is discussed as a maternal outcome, it is diagnosed or develops during pregnancy, not before pregnancy. Box oxygenation refers to a medical intervention in which an infant experiencing respiratory distress is placed within an incubator designed to deliver precise and controlled levels of oxygen. Large for gestational age (LGA) and SGA were defined as neonatal birth weights greater than the 90th percentile and less than the 10th percentile for gestational age, respectively. Birth weight discordance in twins (BWDT) was defined as the percentage difference in birth weight between twin fetuses being ≥20%0.21 The criteria for percentiles of birth weight, birth head circumference, and birth length were based on the third version of Fenton growth chart.22

Statistical analyses

The subjects were categorised into the GDM group and the non-GDM group, and maternal and neonatal outcomes were compared between these two groups. Given the influence of gestational age on neonatal outcomes, our study performed a stratified analysis of neonatal outcomes based on gestational age.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V.28.0 for Windows. Continuous variables are presented as means±SD for normally distributed data or as medians with IQRs (P25, P75) for highly skewed data, and were analysed using t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, respectively. Categorical variables are expressed as percentages and were analysed using χ² tests. Logistic regression analysis was employed to examine the association between GDM and perinatal outcomes. A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing data were handled using exclusion.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. This approach was consistent with the retrospective nature of the study, which used de-identified data from the hospital’s EMR system.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

During the study period, 52 429 women with singleton or twin pregnancies delivered live-born newborns at the two hospitals. Of these, 833 had twin pregnancies and 51 596 had singleton pregnancies. All women underwent a 75 g OGTT between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation. The prevalence of GDM in the twin and singleton groups was 22.1% (n=184) and 8.1% (n=4179), respectively. Among the 833 women with twin pregnancies, 25 were excluded per protocol: 8 with pre-existing diabetes, 2 newly diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus during pregnancy, and 15 with TTTS. Thus, 808 women (1616 neonates) were included in the study: 178 women (356 neonates) with GDM and 630 women (1260 neonates) without GDM (figure 1). Of the 178 women with GDM, 12 required insulin therapy, while the remainder were treated by diet alone. The insulin utilisation rate among women with GDM complicated by twin pregnancy was 6.7%.

The characteristics of the women included in the study are presented in online supplemental table 1. Compared with women without GDM, women with GDM were older, had a higher BMI and experienced less weight gain during pregnancy. Mothers in the GDM group showed a significantly higher proportion of advanced maternal age, obesity and dichorionic villi (p<0.05).

Association of GDM with maternal outcomes in twin pregnancies

The risk of maternal outcomes in women carrying twins with and without GDM is presented in online supplemental table 2. Women with twin pregnancies who had GDM exhibited a higher risk of preterm birth, premature rupture of membranes and hypothyroidism compared with women without GDM. However, there was no significant difference in the risks of caesarean delivery, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy or chorioamnionitis between the two groups.

Association of GDM with neonatal outcomes in twin pregnancies

The risk of neonatal outcomes in women carrying twins with and without GDM is presented in online supplemental table 3. Twin infants of women with GDM were at a higher risk of admission to neonatal ward, oxygen therapy, nCPAP, box oxygenation, hyperbilirubinaemia and both total and prophylactic use of antibiotics compared with infants of women without GDM. However, there was no significant difference in the risks of invasive ventilation, pulmonary surfactant use, neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, birth defects, hypoglycaemia, patent ductus arteriosus, hypothyroidism, anaemia or infection between the two groups.

When stratified by gestational age at birth, only in the preterm group were infants of women with twins and GDM more likely to require admission to the neonatal ward, oxygen therapy, box oxygenation, hyperbilirubinaemia and prophylactic antibiotic use compared with infants of women without GDM (figure 2).

Association of GDM with physical growth of twin neonates

The risk of physical growth of twin neonates born to mothers with and without GDM is presented in online supplemental table 3. In twin pregnancies, percentiles of birth weight, birth length and birth head circumference were similar between newborns with and without GDM. Twin neonates of women with GDM were not at a higher risk of being LGA or SGA compared with those without GDM. After adjustment, there was no significant association between GDM and BWDT.

Discussion

Our findings revealed that twin gestations affected by GDM were significantly associated with several adverse pregnancy outcomes compared with twin gestations not affected by this disease. Additionally, we observed that in twin pregnancies, preterm neonates born to mothers with GDM were more likely to suffer adverse outcomes compared with those without GDM. In singleton pregnancies, GDM is known to accelerate fetal growth; however, in our study, no significant association was found between GDM and percentiles of birth weight, birth length or birth head circumference in twin pregnancies. In our study, women with GDM experienced less weight gain during pregnancy. This observation may be attributable to improved prenatal care adherence among pregnant women, coupled with the implementation of a standardised glycaemic management protocol for GDM at both participating hospitals.

The relationship between GDM and adverse outcomes, such as hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, preterm birth, premature rupture of membranes, caesarean delivery, infection, respiratory distress syndrome, hypoglycaemia and hyperbilirubinaemia, has been well established in singleton pregnancies.5 6 Several studies exploring the association between GDM and adverse complications in twin gestations have reported an increased risk of preterm birth,23 24 accelerated fetal growth,13 caesarean delivery,23 hypertensive disorders of pregnancy,12 neonatal respiratory complications25 and neonatal ward admission.24

Our study demonstrated that GDM in twin pregnancies was associated with premature rupture of membranes, preterm birth, admission to neonatal ward and hyperbilirubinaemia, consistent with its effects in singleton pregnancies. Furthermore, our findings indicated that GDM increased the risk of noninvasive respiratory support (including nCPAP and box oxygenation) in twin infants rather than invasive respiratory support.

However, we did not observe any significant association between GDM in twin pregnancies and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, caesarean delivery, respiratory distress syndrome or hypoglycaemia. One possible explanation for this lack of association may be the inherently higher baseline risk of prematurity and hypertensive complications in twin gestations,9 which could overshadow the relatively smaller effect of GDM observed in singleton pregnancies.

We also identified a novel association between GDM and hypothyroidism in women with twin pregnancies. Thyroid dysfunction and diabetes mellitus are closely linked. Previous studies26 27 have shown that type 1 diabetes results from autoimmune β-cell destruction, leading to absolute insulin deficiency. This condition is clinically associated with autoimmune-induced thyroid dysfunction due to shared pathophysiological mechanisms. Both conditions share an autoimmune predisposition, and certain genetic factors may contribute to the co-occurrence of autoimmune thyroid disease and type 1 diabetes. Although several studies have documented an increased prevalence of thyroid disorders in patients with diabetes mellitus,28 29 limited data exist regarding the mechanism by which GDM affects thyroid function. Some studies have reported an association between GDM and hypothyroidism in singleton pregnancies.30 31 The persistent hyperglycaemic state in pregnant women with GDM may damage the thyroid gland, potentially leading to thyroid dysfunction. Data on the association between thyroid function and GDM in twin pregnancies remain scarce, warranting further investigation into their intricate interaction.

Current evidence indicates that in singleton pregnancies, poor glycaemic control in pregnant women increases the risk of maternal and neonatal infections,5 6 resulting in higher antibiotic usage among infants born to mothers with diabetes. However, existing studies on GDM and twin pregnancies pay little attention to infection-related outcomes.11 In our study, GDM was associated with both total and prophylactic antibiotic use in twins, despite no significant association between GDM and infection in twin pregnancies. This observation indicated that there might be an excessive use of antibiotic prophylaxis at these hospitals. These findings raise questions about whether GDM management should be individualised for twin pregnancies and whether additional research is needed to guide antibiotic use in twins with GDM.

It is important to note that we stratified the analysis of the association between GDM and neonatal outcomes by gestational age at birth. We found that GDM was associated with admission to neonatal ward, oxygen therapy, box oxygenation, hyperbilirubinaemia and prophylactic use of antibiotics only in preterm twins. This suggests that GDM may exert differential effects on twin newborns depending on gestational age. A possible explanation for this observation is that as gestational age increases, unfavourable factors such as restricted growth space and inadequate nutritional intake become more pronounced,32 33 thereby diminishing the influence of insulin resistance on twin fetuses.

We found no significant association between GDM and accelerated fetal growth in twin pregnancies, consistent with some previous studies.12 34 Conversely, other studies have reported an increased risk of accelerated fetal growth in twin pregnancies complicated by GDM.23 35 Notably, nearly all these studies used birth weight as the sole measure of fetal growth, which has limitations due to the wide variation in absolute birth weight values across different gestational ages. Birth length and birth head circumference are also critical biometric indices for evaluating fetal growth. Therefore, in our study, we analysed percentiles of birth weight, birth length and birth head circumference but found no significant differences. The slower growth of twins during the third trimester36 and the elevated risk of fetal growth restriction in twins37 may explain why twin pregnancies complicated by GDM are less likely to exhibit accelerated fetal growth. The incidence of BWDT was lower in the GDM group (10.2% vs 16.4%). However, after adjustment, we found no significant difference between GDM and BWDT (p=0.141). A potential reason may be insufficient statistical power to detect a strong association.

The primary strength of our study lies in adjusting for important confounding variables (eg, maternal age, prepregnancy BMI and chorionicity), which are significant risk factors for adverse outcomes in this context. Other potential confounders, such as in vitro fertilisation, gravidity and parity, were not included because there were no significant differences in the risks associated with these confounders between the two groups in our study. Another key point is that our study encompassed a broader range of outcomes compared with prior studies. Lastly, we used percentiles of birth weight instead of absolute birth weight values to assess fetal growth.

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective, two-centre cohort study, which may limit the generalisability of our findings to other settings with different patient populations, clinical practices or healthcare systems. Second, the retrospective design is subject to selection bias and residual confounding despite our multivariable adjustment. Third, unmeasured variables (eg, placental weight, degree of glycaemic control and medication requirements) could have influenced outcomes. Finally, the relatively small sample size may restrict the applicability of our results to subgroups not adequately represented in this cohort.

Conclusions

Our study revealed that GDM was significantly linked to certain adverse pregnancy outcomes in twin pregnancies, including hypothyroidism in pregnant women, preterm birth, admission to neonatal ward, oxygen therapy, hyperbilirubinaemia, the total and prophylactic use of antibiotics in twins. Additionally, GDM appeared to have a more pronounced association with preterm infants and was less likely to be associated with accelerated fetal growth in twin pregnancies. Our findings challenge the applicability of current GDM management guidelines to twin pregnancies and highlight the need for further investigation. More prospective studies are warranted to clarify this issue.
