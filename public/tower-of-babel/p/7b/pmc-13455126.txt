Near-Field Radio-Frequency for Real-Time Facial Expression Recognition
Authors: AAKASH KAPOOR, UPEKHA DELAY, KAPIL GANGWAR, EDWIN C KAN
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe advent of smart wearable computing platforms in the form of VR/AR enhanced devices has unlocked new strides in mental wellness (through tele-health counseling), personalized spatial computing, and interactive content consumption. However, most current devices rely upon vision and audio based user inputs that pose privacy risks and often consume high amounts of power. In this work, we introduce a novel near-field radio-frequency (NFRF) based facial expression recognition (FER) system that is personal, privacy-respecting, and may be extended to commercially available wearable form-factors. The proposed system demonstrated high discriminability across common facial expressions, including reciprocal expressions occurring across individual facial hemispheres, achieving a test accuracy of 83.01% with sub-millisecond inference time, making it a suitable candidate for wearable computing as well as innovative healthcare devices, such as for objective measurement of post-stroke palsy rehabilitation.INDEX TERMS: Facial expression recognition, near-field radio-frequency, wearable computing, human-computer interface

INTRODUCTION

Facial expressions represent one of the most fundamental non-verbal communication channels in human interaction, serving as windows into our emotional states. Although the universality of expressions between cultures remains an area of scholarly debates [1], [2], [3], robust evidence indicates that facial expressions function as reliable biomarkers of emotional and psychological states between various age groups [4]. These natural indicators of affective states offer a uniquely accessible pathway for monitoring mental well-being, potentially enabling early detection of conditions such as depression and anxiety disorder when integrated into appropriate sensing technologies. This capability becomes particularly valuable for vulnerable populations, such as older adults in long-term care facilities, who may experience neglect or inadequate mental health monitoring. The growing global mental health crisis, exacerbated by the widespread social, economic, and psychological impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic [5], underscores the urgent need for unobtrusive, continuous affective monitoring solutions.

VR and AR systems, typically deployed as head-mounted devices, have emerged as promising platforms for mental health interventions by providing immersive therapeutic environments that can enhance traditional behavioral therapy approaches when used alongside tele-health consulting [6], [7]. These wearable platforms present a unique opportunity for integrating affective sensing technologies that can continuously monitor users’ emotional states through facial expression analysis. Beyond therapeutic applications, they also hold potential for assistive devices that can transform the lives of individuals with severe disabilities, including tetraplegia [8], [9]. By embedding emotion recognition capabilities within wearable VR and AR systems, we can enable both passive monitoring of mental well-being as well as create novel emotion-responsive interfaces that adapt to users’ affective states, offering a more natural and empathetic computing experience [10].

Computer vision has historically dominated facial expression recognition (FER) approaches, evolving from early heuristic methods to contemporary deep learning systems. Heuristic pattern recognition algorithms for facial expression recognition typically employ geometric feature-based approaches that track displacement of facial landmarks and appearance-based methods like local binary patterns and Gabor wavelets that capture texture information correlated with specific expressions. Many of these approaches operate within the framework of the facial action coding system, which decomposes expressions into action units corresponding to specific muscle movements. More recently, vision systems have largely transitioned to deep neural networks [11] and attention-aware transformer models [12], leveraging large annotated datasets to achieve state-of-the-art performance. Deep learning approaches offer significant accuracy gains over heuristic methods but also present significant limitations in wearable contexts, particularly for VR/AR applications. These systems require unobstructed views of the face, are sensitive to ambient lighting conditions, and raise substantial privacy concerns that may limit their acceptability across diverse user populations and regulatory environments.

Privacy-preserving alternatives for facial expression recognition address these limitations through modalities that capture facial muscle activity without requiring visual data. These include photoplethysmography (PPG), acoustic sensing, inertial measurement units (IMU), and surface electromyography (sEMG), each offering distinct advantages for wearable deployment [13]. The inherently anatomical nature of these sensing modalities eliminates the need for face detection algorithms and their associated privacy concerns. Moreover, the continuous time-series data produced by these sensors requires significantly lower memory resources compared to image or video streams, enabling faster inference, reduced computational overhead, and extended battery life - all critical requirements for practical wearable implementations. These efficiency advantages strongly position non-visual sensing approaches as preferred solutions for designing emotion-aware wearable devices.

PPG is an optical sensing modality that leverages the differential absorption of low-intensity infrared light between oxygenated blood and deoxygenated hemoglobin to track hemodynamic changes beneath the skin. While traditionally deployed for cardiovascular monitoring applications such as blood oxygen saturation (SpO2) measurement and pulse tracking, PPG has recently demonstrated promise for affective computing. This includes PPG sensors embedded within earphone interfaces which establish a relationship between facial muscle activity and localized blood flow patterns to distinguish common human facial expressions without direct visual observation, offering an integration path into existing consumer wearables while maintaining strong privacy protections [14]. However, to the best of our knowledge, facial expressions corresponding with weaker eyelid motion have not been explored using PPG. Further studies are also required to assess the impact of subject-specific variations, such as skin color, on the accuracy of PPG-based FER systems [15].

Acoustic sensors, on the other hand, leverage ultrasonic band-limited signals to detect morphological changes in the ear canal arising from facial muscle movements. A large number of generally available consumer earphones, especially smart earbuds, already contain the necessary hardware for transmitting and receiving ultrasonic sound waves, which presents a pathway to rapid, widespread adoption through updates to existing devices rather than requiring new hardware purchases [16]. Acoustic sensors demonstrate promising accuracy rates around 80% [17], but struggle in detecting subtle movements, such as those involving eyelids, which induce weaker ear canal deformations [18].

Inertial measurement units (IMUs) provide high-precision tracking of three-dimensional body motion through an integrated system of gyroscopes, accelerometers, and temperature sensors. While primarily deployed for spatial positioning and trajectory tracking applications, IMUs have been demonstrated as viable alternatives for human-computer interface (HCI) contexts, including prosthetic control through hand-gesture recognition and head-gesture detection [19], [20]. While their compact form factor, low power requirements, and wide commercial availability makes IMUs suitable for wearable integration, isolation of faint signals pertaining to facial muscle activation from overall head movements presents a significant challenge, often requiring multi-modal deployments in conjunction with sEMG systems [21].

In this paper, we introduce a near-field radio-frequency (NFRF) system for continuous facial expression recognition that may be deployed standalone or in conjunction with the above sensors as part of a multi-modal system. Electromagnetic fields generated by a transmitting (Tx) antenna are perturbed by motion of dielectric bodies in the antenna near-field, allowing for coupling of dielectric body motion with incident EM signal [23]. A suitably placed receiving (Rx) antenna can measure the resulting amplitude and phase modulated signal, which can be demodulated for non-invasive sensing of dielectric body motion. Carrier frequencies of transmitted signal are typically selected to be in the UHF band, which provides a robust balance between antenna size, coupling depth, and isolation from environmental interference. A key advantage of the NFRF sensing method is the ability to use multiplexing schemes such as code-division multiplexing (CDM) and orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM), which allow for efficient implementation of independent data channels, such as from sensors placed at different spatial locations. NFRF systems also allow for rapid industrial uptake due to the wide availability and mature ecosystem of miniaturized RF components operating at Industrial, Scientific, and Medical (ISM) frequency bands, significantly reducing barriers to commercialization. While prior research has explored NFRF sensing for physiological monitoring [24], vital sign sensing [25], [26] and cardiopulmonary assessment in humans [27], [28], its application for human-computer interface applications has been largely unexplored. This work explores the application of an NFRF system towards continuous FER with the intended aim of providing a versatile, privacy-preserving human-computer interface. The proposed system was assessed on a human study with 9 facial expressions, including a reference expression to evaluate system robustness against false positives. Further, two pairs of hemi-facial expressions were assessed to evaluate the potential of the system for future applications such as objective measurement of rehabilitation from stroke-induced facial palsy. Generalizability was tested by hold-out cross validation. Our system demonstrated a test accuracy of 83.01%, competitive with other sensing modalities, making it an ideal choice for a private, low-power, wearable sensor that may be seamlessly integrated into existing VR/AR form factors.

NFRF SENSING

Dielectric bodies placed in the near-field of a Tx antenna modulate impinging electromagnetic radiation by their motion. Rx antennas may thus be placed, in either a back-scattering or forward-scattering configuration with respect to a Tx antenna, to measure motion of dielectric bodies located in the antenna near-field, shown in Fig. 1. The strength of back-scattered NFRF signals, assuming spherical dielectric bodies of radii lying within the Mie scattering regime, is inversely proportional to the cube of the distance between the dielectric center and Tx-Rx antennas [29]. Although Equation 1 only strictly applies for spherical bodies, the underlying principle has been applied to more general non-spherical bodies under suitable assumptions [27]. For our present analysis, we did not make use of an exact analytical relation for the received signal strength since relative strengths of backscattered signal across sensors located at different positions, shown in Fig. 2(a), were sufficient for the purposes of FER. It is important to note, however, that relative signal strengths may only be able to recognize hemi-facial motion but not provide continuous tracking over long periods, such as would be required for rehabilitation monitoring. For the present study, we chose to place Tx/Rx antennas at a short distance from the subject’s face since sensing superficial facial motion was sufficient for FER. Applications pertaining to sensing of deeper muscular motion, however, would require skin contact to ensure sufficient near-field coupling with underlying tissue. In practical implementations, Tx and Rx antennas are typically deployed as pairs positioned at approximately equal distances from the tissue surface of interest. Frequency-division multiplexing (FDM), implemented using a superheterodyne scheme, provided a multi-input multi-output (MIMO) system. The resulting measurement grid was visualized as a fully-connected graph, shown in Fig. 2(c), with vertices representing Tx-Rx pairs and edges corresponding to MIMO channels. Since graph edges vary as 𝒪(V2), where V is the number of vertices, spatial data acquired by the proposed system is highly scalable, allowing relatively high spatial diversity to increase system reliability and sensitivity.

SYSTEM EVALUATION

EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

The NFRF sensing system was integrated into a face shield to simulate realistic deployment conditions while maintaining consistent positioning of sensors and stability during the experimental procedure. Future iterations and commercial deployments may include miniaturized antennae for seamless integration into more ergonomic form factors, including VR/AR headsets, smart glasses, or specialized medical monitoring devices, that maintain the advantages of non-contact sensing while improving user comfort and aesthetic appeal. Taoglas TG.19.0112 antennas were chosen as the Tx-Rx sensors and were secured in custom-designed, radio-transparent 3D-printed enclosures for non-contact signal acquisition, as shown in Fig. 2(b). This configuration ensured that no antenna hardware directly touched participants’ skin. To maximize spatial awareness and expression detection capabilities, we strategically positioned sensor pairs proximal to four key facial regions of interest, which were near the eyes and cheeks, shown in Fig. 2(a). The arrangement remained fixed throughout the study, with no participant-specific adjustments made to prevent introduction of positioning bias. Since the current experimental setup used coaxial cables for connecting to the USRP devices, we ensured that cables were secured to prevent in-band interference induced by mechanical perturbations.

Data were acquired using two synchronized Ettus B210 Universal Software Radio Peripheral (USRP) devices, each connected to two Tx-Rx pairs. USRP devices were controlled using a custom LabView program that stored sensor data, at a sampling rate of 10 kilo-samples per second (kSps), and provided audio instructions to study participants during data collection. We implemented a frequency-division multiple access scheme using four distinct intermediate frequencies, resulting in 16 distinctive channels, each corresponding to a unique Tx-Rx pair. A carrier signal of 1.8 GHz was used for signal transmission and reception and each Tx antenna was restricted to a maximum power output of −10 dBm (0.1 mW), significantly below the safety standards for radio-frequency radiation limits. DATA COLLECTION AND PROCESSING

We performed a human study involving 20 participants, balanced across male (N = 11) and female (N = 9) participants to avoid gender biases. The human study was approved by the Cornell Institutional Review Board (IRB) for Human Participants under Protocol ID #1812008488 and conducted with written informed consent from all participants. As studying localized facial surface motion was a key consideration of the experiment design, participants were screened to ensure their ability to perform all the required expressions. The experiment protocol consisted of 9 distinct facial expressions, illustrated in Fig. 3, which were selected either because of their direct connection to an emotional state or because they had hemi-facial counterparts, allowing us to study surface motion arising from muscle groups of all parts of the face. The chosen expressions were sub-divided into two broad categories: expressions restricted to either the left or the right facial hemisphere, including cheek pulls and winks, and expressions focused on either the upper-half or lower-half of the face with reciprocal counterparts, such as eyebrow raise and furrow. The system design mainly focuses on two use cases of localized sensing: 1) to monitor subtle expression changes in order to provide a comprehensive suite of facial expression interactions that can realize an emotion-aware VR/AR system, and 2) to assess feasibility of quantifiable hemi-facial assessment for rehabilitation progress monitoring of stroke patients with partial facial paralysis. To better assess classification performance, we introduced a reference class, blinking, as a neutral non-expressive involuntary action that occurs simultaneously and continuously regardless of other expressions performed, allowing us to measure systematic bias towards positive detection of expressions.

Expression selection for human study:

Facial expressions corresponding to common emotional states were selected and organized into two broad categories - those involving muscles across the face with reciprocal surface motion, and expressions performed along one hemisphere of the face. Expressions were chosen to span both upper and lower halves of the face, with blinking behaving as a reference.

Participants were prompted by computer-generated audio instructions to perform each facial expressions five times per experimental run, with 3 seconds allowed for performing each expression. This timing allowed participants to perform expressions at a comfortable pace while providing sufficient separation between consecutive expressions for clear data segmentation. We conducted 8 runs per participant, yielding a dataset of 40 repetitions per expression per participant. Across the study, facial expression durations ranged approximately from 0.2 s to 1.5 s, corresponding to fundamental frequencies from 0.67 Hz to 5 Hz. The large relative distance between the NFRF sensors and torso minimized artifacts induced as a result of respiratory and cardiac events. Due to the present limitation of wired connection to USRP devices, instances with excessive fidgeting causing strong cable motion were excluded from the dataset and replaced with additional data collected. Real-world deployments of RF sensors typically involve fabricated planar antennas without loose hanging SMA cables and are thus not expected to be impacted by this cable motion.

Algorithm 1 highlights the data preparation and classification pipeline. An equiripple band-pass filter from 0.05 Hz to 15 Hz was applied to the acquired signal. Since the fundamental frequencies of performed expressions were as high as 5 Hz, the upper pass-band limit ensured that filtered data included at least 3 harmonics for the signal of interest while removing high-frequency line noise.

The lower pass-band frequency was chosen to remove slow baseline drifts, that occurred over the duration of the data collection, while keeping filter order low. Polynomial trends with duration longer than 20 seconds, which typically resulted from subtle shifts in the wearable system’s position relative to the face during extended sessions, could not be removed using the equiripple filter. We performed cubic polynomial detrending which effectively eliminated gradual trends without distorting the desirable data. The filtered and detrended data was then normalized. A representative example of the raw data and processed data is shown in Fig. 4. Normalization was performed across each experimental run instead of per-segment to ensure relative strengths of hemi-facial expressions were effectively captured in the segmented dataset. Individual instances of each facial expression were obtained by segmenting the processed data into 3-second windows that aligned with the experimental protocol timing. The importance of per-run normalization over per-segment normalization is highlighted by visualizing hemi-facial expressions of a participant in Fig. 5. The resulting dataset consisted of 7,380 such segments, each consisting of 16 channels of position-specific time-series, with corresponding labels of participants, expressions, and experimental runs.

EXPRESSION RECOGNITION

The multi-variate time series from the proposed NFRF sensing system contain correlated perturbations corresponding to spatially separated facial motion. Acquired data, thus, exhibits not only temporal alignment of sampling points but also spatial correlation across measurement channels, thereby precluding us from assuming mutual independence. Limited pairwise independence may be observed for expressions involving surface perturbations that are not within the near-field coupling region of Tx-Rx pairs, such as for idealized hemi-facial expressions. In practice, however, since most individuals are unable to reliably perturb only one half of their face, any general emotion-aware sensing system must not assume pairwise spatial independence across channels. We are thus left with highly correlated spatial-temporal data whose high dimensionality may present significant challenges for robust generalizable classification performance, especially when using data-driven deep-learning methods. Non-data-driven methods can be broadly classified into the following four types: 1) distance-based methods such as nearest-neighbors, 2) feature-based approaches, 3) morphology-based approaches such as shapelets, and 4) grammar-based approaches such as sequence modeling [30]. For our experiment, we chose a feature-based approach that provided superior classification by keeping data dimensionality low. We then used nearest-neighbors as a comparative baseline.

Time-series representing underlying dynamics are prone not only to time warping, depending upon the speed of underlying action, but also upon amplitude scaling and corruption from noise. Correspondingly, this requires distance-based implementations to make use of metrics that are independent of scaling in time such as dynamic time-warping (DTW). Generic DTW computation has an intensive computational time complexity of 𝒪(N2), making it a poor candidate for long time-series. Approximate methods, such as Fast-DTW, can improve computational complexity, but often suffer from degraded accuracy and may not necessarily provide a significant real-world speed-up [31], [32]. Prior meta-analyses have demonstrated the superiority of k-Nearest Neighbors (NN) using DTW metric for time-series classification [33], [34]. Thus, we performed classification using 1-NN with DTW to establish a baseline of system performance. As modern CNN-based architectures such as ConvNext [35] are popular in edge device computing, we adopted a 1-D ConvNext with tunable depth as a baseline for performance comparison.

Deep learning methods require caution when used with small datasets such as ours. The generalization of a system for small datasets is usually characterized using one of the many available cross-validation (CV) strategies. While k-Fold CV is commonly used to split datasets for evaluating generalized validation performance, this strategy is sensitive to the choice of k as the number of folds, as well as the size of elements within each fold [36]. Another popular method involves an additional testing split to the dataset comprised of held-out data samples, thereby better representing real-world usage [37]. Since there remain debates on the superiority of the above strategies, we adopted a nested CV strategy, as shown in Algorithm 2. Each subject’s data were partitioned by individual runs, leaving one run out (LORO) as a test split. The remaining data was used for training and validation using 4-Fold cross-validation while classifier scores were evaluated on the held-out test split. In order to minimize variance caused by extremely poor or good experiment runs, we iteratively chose each run as our test split and presented the average classification results across the iterations.

For each NFRF time series, 12 features that are commonly adopted for multivariate biomedical time series were used, yielding a 192-element flattened feature vector [38]. These features included Hjorth parameters that capture signal variance and bandwidth [39], statistical measures, and morphological features of time series waveform such as the number of peaks. Hand-crafted features were chosen owing to their computational efficiency, generalizability, as well as interpretability. Representations learned using neural networks were not selected since they tend to perform worse in comparison with hand-crafted features when testing on out-of-distribution data, resulting in poor generalizability of performance, especially in data-constrained environments [40]. Multivariate NFRF time-series represent correlated spatiotemporal information, leading to redundancy in extracted features. Redundant features contribute to a high-dimensional input space which can adversely affect classification speed as well as generalizability. Therefore, we chose the minimal-redundancy-maximal-relevance (mRmR) criterion to prune redundant features by selecting the top P = 20% of the most relevant features [41]. Relative importance of all extracted features, across all subjects in the dataset, is visualized in Fig. 6. The visualization lends support to our choice of the hyper-parameter P, which roughly corresponded to selecting features with a relative importance greater than 50%. For the final classification stage, we experimented with interpretable classifiers including random forests, bagged decision-trees, adaptively boosted decision-trees (AdaBoost), and linear discriminant analysis (LDA). We finally selected AdaBoost as the classifier of choice, owing to its ability to better capture complex decision boundaries [42]. A grid-search was subsequently performed to choose optimal hyper-parameters that did not overfit the selected classifier.

RESULTS

EXPRESSION RECOGNITION

The NFRF sensing system was evaluated using the method proposed in Algorithm 2, as well as the two baseline classifiers: 1-NN with DTW and ConvNext. All classification tasks were run on a computer with an NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU with 24GB graphics memory and an Intel i9-13900K CPU with 128GB RAM. Validation was performed using 4-fold CV and testing was performed by LORO in order to simulate real-world use and generalizable performance.

Fig. 7 presents the overall confusion matrix for facial expression detection, averaged across all participants. As shown in Table 1, while similar validation performance was observed across all three tested models, the proposed method had a superior test accuracy in comparison with both baseline methods. The drop off in performance among validation and test accuracy is primarily attributed to the small dataset and is not expected to translate to potential future studies with larger datasets. The proposed method is significantly faster than both baselines, taking 0.09 ms per inference in comparison with 152.95 ms for 1-NN with DTW and 0.81 ms for ConvNext.

The highest classification accuracies were observed for expressions that were not as significantly correlated with other expressions, such as pucker, eyebrow furrow and raise. While the poorest recognition accuracies were observed for hemi-facial expressions, including both winks and cheek pulls, we observed that the primary confusion was not among the corresponding hemi-facial expression pairs but rather among the hemi-facial expression and the corresponding whole-face expression. The choice of classifier, while important for overall classification accuracy, did not significantly impact the relative accuracies of different expressions. This may point towards underlying biological or physiological factors, such as poor hemisphere-independent muscle control, as an underlying factor in the lower accuracies of hemi-facial expressions. Another potential explanation includes asymmetric facial expressions being induced as a result of asymmetric emotional expression across the brain hemispheres, with studies suggesting a bias for more visible emotion expression on the left side of the face [43], [44]. On the other hand, we observed highly confident expression recognition for reciprocal whole-face expressions such as smiling, lip pucker and eyebrow raise/furrow. This may be attributed to individuals having greater practice to perform expressions across facial hemispheres, leading to more pronounced surface motion arising from better developed muscles.

CONSISTENCY ANALYSIS

Facial expression recognition systems, regardless of sensing modality, usually exhibit variable performance across different use cases. These variations may be caused by environmental factors, such as the impact of lighting on vision-based systems, differences across individuals, and action-specific variables, such as weak muscular actuation for certain expressions. While the proposed sensing system was resilient to most environmental factors in our testing, performance was affected by intra-subject and inter-subject factors. Intra-subject factors usually arise from differential muscular actuation across different regions of the face, causing certain expressions to be more prominent than others. Inter-subject factors arise from uniqueness in expression performance across different individuals. We evaluated the range of performance of the proposed sensor by analyzing consistency across inter-subject and intra-subject factors. While facial expression recognition performance was consistent across most individuals, we observed a few participants whose performance was severely degraded. Individual analysis of Subject 14 revealed a poor accuracy of 46% for both right wink and right smile, which may stem from either poor fitting of sensors on the individual or the individual’s inability to perform strong right-side expressions. While Fig. 7 highlights the average recognition accuracy per expression, it does not provide insights into the variance of performance, so the expression accuracies across all subjects were visualized in Fig. 9. Two clear trends can be observed: 1) Hemi-facial expressions had the highest variance in performance, and 2) left-sided facial expressions had lower variance than their right-side counterparts, which may be an important biological systematic bias to be noted for future studies.

TRANSFER LEARNING

Transfer learning is a powerful tool for adapting models trained on larger, more-general datasets towards smaller, more-specific tasks [45]. Transfer learning can be particularly useful in reducing training requirements for enrolling new individual subjects in HCI systems. Since pre-existing vision-based FER systems have been observed to benefit greatly from the use of transfer learning, we investigated the applicability of transfer learning as well [46]. For each of the 20 subjects, we trained classifiers on data corresponding to all other subjects and tested on the selected individual. Subject enrolment was simulated by gradual augmentation of training dataset using examples from the test subject, in steps of 5% of the held-out subject dataset. We observed a higher test accuracy for the transfer learning model in comparison with no transfer learning. However, the transfer learning model did not perform uniformly across different subjects, with the best-case scenario of 82.1% accuracy with only 10% mixing and the worst-case scenario showing 47% accuracy after 45% mixing, shown in Fig. 10. This implies that although transfer learning can be overall beneficial to performance, certain individual difference cannot be well captured by the generalized model in the worst case, where individual training will still need to be extensive to meet the required performance.

DISCUSSION

CURRENT LIMITATIONS

The proposed system and algorithms were able to perform localized sensing of facial surface perturbations, including across lateral hemispheres. It must be noted that while the classification algorithm executes at real-time inference speeds, the current signal processing in Algorithm 1 relies upon non-causal zero-phase filters. While the observed test accuracy of 83.01% across all expressions was acceptable, motion arising from smaller muscle groups, such as around the eyes, exhibited higher variance in performance, as shown in Fig. 9. A slight bias towards better accuracy for hemi-facial expressions on the left side of the face was also observed in the above analysis. Further investigations across a more diverse pool of participants are required to be performed before any conclusions about fundamental biases, such as differences across hemi-facial control from a neurophysical context, may be claimed. There are two major limitations that exist within the present study.

SELF-CALIBRATION AND LONGITUDINAL ASSESSMENT

In the present study, tracking relative motion across facial hemispheres was found to be sufficient for FER. However, long-term deployments for clinical applications in facial muscle rehabilitation require quantitative measurements of absolute motion. Furthermore, establishing accurate quantitative tracking is a fundamental prerequisite for advancing the system toward micro-expression recognition. Micro-expressions are spontaneous, involuntary facial movements characterized by their extreme subtlety and brief duration [47], [48]. Because their physical amplitudes are significantly smaller than voluntary macro-expressions, reliable detection of these features requires tracking absolute local displacements against rigorously calibrated baselines. Transitioning from measuring relative motion to absolute motion requires long-term longitudinal studies to test the self-calibration protocols, such as transceiving known reference sequences to estimate link budget and channel variability [49], [50]. Furthermore, non-linearities induced by near-field effects may be corrected for by injecting known pilot signals within the channel band [51].

LIMITED DATASET SIZE AND HETEROGENEITY

Variability in backscattered NFRF signals primarily arise from tissue dielectric properties [29]. For instance, differences in skin thickness and muscle density affect near-field dielectric coupling and NFRF amplitude scaling [52]. Biological age may also introduce baseline variations, as tissue permittivity and conductivity systematically decrease over time due to reduced tissue water content [53]. In contrast with optical sensing modalities, which require extensive validation against diverse skin tones due to melanin absorption [15], NFRF sensing is inherently invariant to skin pigmentation [54]. Similarly, physical surface conditions such as beard density, sweating, or moisturizing cosmetics have minimal effects on the signal integrity from their finite thickness. The present dataset consisted of a relatively small number of subjects (n = 20) primarily comprising younger adults, posing two major restrictions that require further study before broader generalizability can be ascertained. First, smaller datasets inherently have high variance in test accuracy. Second, dynamics observed within the present participants may not scale universally across all demographics. Future studies with a larger and more diverse pool of participants, similar to those conducted by in-ear PPG [55] and body-worn cameras [56], would be essential for minimizing variance in expression recognition, improving transfer learning performance, and identifying demographic variability.

PATH TO MINIATURIZATION

Commercial deployments of the proposed NFRF system will require transitioning to miniaturized, low-profile designs. VR headsets are typically larger than smart glasses and integration with custom-made slot antennas is a promising direction for overall facial motion detection, including lower-jaw and cheek shaping [57]. Smart glasses, typically focused around AR applications, are much more constrained in their form factors and require much more creative productization. Planar inverted-F antennas are a promising candidate for direct integration within the temples and side-arms of smart glasses [58], [59]. While lateral motion can be clearly measured with these positions, sensing antennas may be too distant for effective near-field coupling with chin and mouth motion. Optically transparent antennas that may be integrated directly into smart glass lenses can be an effective alternative due to their higher proximity to the mouth [60], [61]. Finally, conformal slot antennas are another promising candidate due to their ability to be integrated within the frame around the lens, allowing higher proximity to the entire face as well [62]. While all of the above are promising candidates for commercial integration within sleek form-factors, future studies are essential for quantifying the impact of increased distance, especially when detecting weaker motion such as for micro-expressions. Furthermore, since clinical applications have stricter requirements on accuracy than human-computer interfaces, a comparative feasibility analysis on the different form factors for each use-case would be crucial. The FER system can be controlled using ultra-low-power compact wireless microcontrollers such as TI SimpleLink 3200MOD and Nordic nRF52840 based on ARM Cortex-M4 microcontroller architecture, leveraging a single-channel transceiver design scaled to an 8 SoC implementation for parallel processing [26]. The proposed algorithm used 16 channels of 32-bit floating-point data, each containing 300 samples, corresponding to 18.75 kB of raw data, well within the data buffer constraints of an M4 MCU, and a model storage size of 275 kB, well within typically available flash memory. Based on a linear clock-rate scaling from our benchmark, the estimated inference time for classifying individual expressions on the MCU is 7 ms, demonstrating the system’s viability for low-power, real-time edge execution.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

While recent wearable human-computer interfaces highlight advances including IMUs, MEMS, and piezoelectric sensors, each sensing modality exhibits limitations [63]. Owing to the low-power consumption of wearable sensors, sensor fusion is a practical strategy that leverages individual sensor strengths for superior ensemble performance [64]. The proposed NFRF sensing system can be a valuable addition in multi-modal sensing systems for enhancing wearable FER. The current experimental setup relied upon omni-directional antennas that are prone to external interference and motion artifacts. Real-world deployments can utilize custom compact directional antennas that exhibit stronger coupling to the facial motion with higher ambient motion tolerance [65]. An analysis on the robustness vs. computational complexity and resource consumption of the proposed method can be useful. Moreover, studies need to be conducted to assess the suitability of the NFRF system for clinical applications which often have strong requirements on maximum allowed error.

CONCLUSION

In this work, we have proposed a novel sensing system for measuring facial expression as proxies of underlying emotional states in a personal and privacy-preserving fashion. We also proposed an efficient recognition algorithm which not only provided a high test accuracy of 83.01% among common emotive facial expressions but was also able to distinguish among the facial hemispheres actuated. Although the current system is limited to measuring surface motion owing to its distance from the wearer, observed localization capability can be used as a proxy for underlying muscular actuation. This has important implications for future clinical applications in rehabilitation therapy, particularly for stroke patients suffering from partial facial paralysis, where quantitative metrics of continuous recovery are not available in convenient, wearable form factors today. While current experiments were limited to controlled lab environments, they lay the groundwork towards more sophisticated studies involving larger participant pools and greater environmental variations. With wide availability and interest in miniaturized antennas, cost-effective integrations compatible with our experimental design may help transform existing wearable gadgets such as VR/AR headsets into emotionally-aware human-computer interfaces.
