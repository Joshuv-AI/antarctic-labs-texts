MRI features of primary pancreatic lymphoma in a young child: a case report
Authors: Tsega Hagos Mehari, Misiker Gebremariam Waktola
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary pancreatic lymphoma is a rare type of extranodal lymphoma that originates within the pancreas. It is exceptionally uncommon in the pediatric population and requires a high index of suspicion for timely diagnosis. Differentiating Primary pancreatic lymphoma from other pancreatic tumors is crucial, as it is primarily managed with chemotherapy rather than surgical intervention. Magnetic resonance imaging, with its superior soft-tissue contrast and multiplanar capabilities, plays a pivotal role in identifying characteristic features of lymphoma. These include a well-defined, homogenous hypo-enhancing mass, evidence of diffusion restriction, and the absence of pancreatic ductal dilatation or parenchymal atrophy, all of which can support early and accurate diagnosis. We present a case of pathologically confirmed primary pancreatic lymphoma in a 3-year-old girl who initially presented with a dry cough of 3 weeks' duration. The pancreatic mass was incidentally discovered on a chest CT scan performed to investigate her respiratory symptoms, and an MRI further characterized the lesion before histologic confirmation.Keywords: Child, Lymphoma, Magnetic resonance imaging, Pancreas, Primary pancreatic lymphoma

Introduction

Primary pancreatic lymphoma (PPL) is a rare form of extranodal non-Hodgkin lymphoma involving the pancreas, accounting for approximately 0.2 % of all pancreatic tumors [1]. Although the gastrointestinal tract is the most common site for extra-nodal lymphomas, pancreatic involvement is uncommon, particularly when the disease is confined to the pancreas without systemic spread [2]. In pediatric patients, PPL is exceptionally rare, and due to the limited number of cases, the clinical and radiologic spectrum remains poorly defined.

Imaging plays a pivotal role in the detection and differential diagnosis of pancreatic masses. Recognizing features suggestive of lymphoma is essential, as PPL is managed primarily with chemotherapy, unlike most pancreatic neoplasms that often require surgical intervention [3]. Radiologic assessment using computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can help identify characteristics such as a well-circumscribed, homogenous, hypovascular mass with minimal or no pancreatic ductal dilatation, findings that may support the diagnosis and guide appropriate tissue sampling for confirmation [4].

Case presentation

A 3-year-old girl presented with an intermittent dry cough lasting for approximately 2 weeks. Her initial chest X-ray was unremarkable; however, due to persistent symptoms, a chest computed tomography (CT) scan was performed for further evaluation. While no pulmonary pathology was identified, the scan incidentally revealed a soft tissue mass in the region of the pancreatic head, within the upper abdominal portion included in the imaging field. This unexpected finding led to further abdominal imaging and clinical investigation. On postcontrast imaging, the lesion appeared as a hypo-enhancing mass (Fig. 1).

Abdominal ultrasonography (USG) showed a homogeneously hypoechoic mass in the pancreatic head. The common bile duct appeared normal, and the main pancreatic duct was mildly dilated. The gallbladder was normal, and there was no significant intrahepatic biliary ductal dilatation.

Suspicion of a pancreatic head tumor prompted a multiphasic, multisequence, gadolinium-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study of the abdomen. On noncontrast T1-weighted imaging (Fig. 2A), a well-demarcated, lobulated mass involving the head, uncinate process, and neck of the pancreas was seen, measuring 58 × 54 × 47 mm in craniocaudal, mediolateral, and anteroposterior dimensions. The mass showed homogeneously hypointense signal intensity relative to the surrounding pancreatic parenchyma. On T2-weighted imaging (Fig. 2B), it appeared slightly hyperintense.

It appears as a Homogenous hypo-intense lesion in T1WI (A) and as a slightly hyper-intense lesion on T2WI (B) compared to the remaining tissue of the pancreas (white fat arrows in A and B). In the postcontrast study, there is a mild enhancement of the tumor(C, D, and E). Mild and smooth prominence of the pancreatic duct is noted (thin white arrows in C, D, and E).

On diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) (Fig. 3B), the mass demonstrated high signal intensity, corresponding to low signal on the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map (Fig. 3A), indicating restricted diffusion. No significant enhancement was observed during the arterial and venous phases (Fig. 2C and D), while mild, inhomogeneous enhancement was noted in the delayed phase (Fig. 2E). The pancreatic duct was mildly dilated to 3 mm at the body. The superior mesenteric vessels, splenic vein, and portal vein were preserved. No significant peripancreatic, retroperitoneal, or mesenteric lymphadenopathy was identified. The liver and spleen appeared normal with no focal lesions.

Laboratory findings were largely unremarkable. The white blood cell count was 6,100/µL, with a differential of 48% neutrophils and 26% lymphocytes. Serum lipase was within normal limits at 12 IU/L. Liver enzymes were also within normal range, with aspartate transaminase (AST) at 28 IU/L and alanine transaminase (ALT) at 20 IU/L. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) was mildly elevated at 290 IU/L. Renal function was normal, with a serum creatinine level of 0.6 mg/dL.

Given the imaging findings of a large, lobulated, homogeneously hypo-intense mass in the pancreatic head with restricted diffusion, and the absence of significant mass effect or invasion into adjacent structures, the possibility of primary pancreatic lymphoma was strongly considered.

Ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration (FNA) was subsequently performed at our institution, and cytological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of lymphoma. Although the histologic images and detailed pathology report are no longer available due to loss of the original patient chart, the diagnosis was documented at the time and guided appropriate treatment.

The patient was referred to pediatric oncology and started on standard multiagent chemotherapy. She showed significant clinical improvement with resolution of her initial respiratory symptoms and normalization of serum alkaline phosphatase level. She remains under outpatient surveillance with no signs of recurrence to date.

The dry, nonproductive cough at presentation was unusual given the absence of findings on chest imaging. A possible explanation is a para-neoplastic phenomenon, in which systemic effects of the tumor, such as cytokine release or immune-mediated inflammation, may have caused transient airway irritation. Although speculative, the resolution of symptoms after chemotherapy supports a potential indirect link between the malignancy and the respiratory presentation. This case highlights the importance of considering broad differential diagnoses when evaluating persistent, unexplained symptoms in pediatric patients.

Discussion

Primary pancreatic lymphoma remains a diagnostic challenge due to its rarity and nonspecific clinical presentation. In adults, common symptoms include abdominal pain, a palpable mass, weight loss, and occasionally obstructive jaundice or acute pancreatitis [5]. However, in pediatric patients, the presentation is variable and often subtle. Most reported pediatric cases describe abdominal pain or jaundice as initial symptoms [[6], [7], [8]]. In our case, the patient presented atypically with a dry, nonproductive cough, which may represent a paraneoplastic phenomenon related to cytokine-mediated inflammation rather than direct thoracic involvement.

The diagnostic criteria for PPL include a mass predominantly involving the pancreas, lymph node enlargement limited to the peripancreatic region, absence of palpable superficial lymphadenopathy, no hepatic or splenic involvement, no mediastinal lymphadenopathy on chest radiography, and a normal white blood cell count [4]. In our case, all of these criteria were fulfilled. The mass was confined to the pancreatic head and neck, with no evidence of distant organ involvement or lymphadenopathy beyond the pancreas. There were no palpable peripheral lymph nodes, and imaging revealed no mediastinal, hepatic, or splenic abnormalities. Laboratory evaluation showed a normal white blood cell count. Notably, there was also no peripancreatic lymphadenopathy. These findings are consistent with a diagnosis of primary pancreatic lymphoma and highlight the critical role of imaging in narrowing the differential and guiding further diagnostic workup.

Radiologically, 2 main morphologic patterns of PPL have been described [9]. The more common focal form appears as a large, well-defined mass, often located in the pancreatic head, while the diffuse form presents with glandular enlargement and poorly defined margins, occasionally mimicking pancreatitis [9]. MRI findings in our case demonstrated a large, homogeneous mass with low signal intensity on T1-weighted images, mild hyperintensity on T2-weighted images, and restricted diffusion, with no significant signs of pancreatic ductal obstruction or adjacent organ invasion. These imaging features are consistent with previously reported cases of PPL.

The differential diagnosis of PPL in pediatric patients includes solid pseudo-papillary tumor, pancreatoblastoma, and inflammatory masses such as autoimmune pancreatitis. Solid pseudo-papillary tumor typically occurs in adolescent females and often displays a mixed solid and cystic appearance due to hemorrhagic transformation [10]. Pancreatoblastoma tends to be heterogeneous with both solid and necrotic components and often occurs in younger children [11]. Although pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is a common malignancy in adults, it is rare in children, typically presenting in late adolescence, with a slight male predominance [10]. Autoimmune or chronic pancreatitis may also appear as focal mass-like lesions mimicking PPL but generally demonstrate higher apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values compared to lymphomas. The marked diffusion restriction seen in lymphoma is a distinguishing feature that aids in radiologic differentiation.

Treatment of PPL is primarily medical, involving chemotherapy, with or without immunotherapy or radiation. Surgery is typically avoided unless needed for diagnosis or to address complications. In this case, the patient responded favorably to chemotherapy with resolution of clinical symptoms and no signs of recurrence during a 6-month follow-up. The role of imaging in guiding diagnosis, monitoring response, and avoiding unnecessary surgical intervention remains central in the management of PPL.

Conclusion

In this case, Primary pancreatic lymphoma presented in a 3-year-old girl with an unusual respiratory symptom and no abdominal complaints, highlighting the diagnostic challenge in pediatric patients. Imaging revealed a large, well-defined, homogeneous mass in the pancreatic head without significant ductal obstruction or adjacent invasion. Magnetic resonance imaging helped raise suspicion of lymphoma, particularly through the demonstration of marked diffusion restriction and low signal intensity on the ADC map. These findings, in the absence of systemic involvement, supported the diagnosis and guided appropriate management without delay.
