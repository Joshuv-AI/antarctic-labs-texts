Exploring the impact of collagen-I genetic variants on intestinal structure and gut microbiota in mouse models of osteogenesis imperfecta
Authors: Yuan K. Zhang, Michèle M. Iskandar, Stan Kubow, Svetlana V. Komarova, Josephine T. Tauer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI), an inherited connective tissue disorder, primarily results from genetic variants that alter collagen-I. Collagen-I also plays a crucial role in intestinal structure and function. A recent large cohort reported an increased risk of gastrointestinal diseases in OI patients, including peptic ulcer disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and constipation. However, the extent of intestinal phenotypic alterations and gut microbiota shifts in OI remains uncertain. Using OI mouse models, Col1a1Jrt/+ and Oim-/-, we explored the effects of collagen-I genetic variants on intestinal morphology, function, and microbiota. Regular chow-fed female and male OI mice and respective wildtype littermates (WT) were studied at 4, 8, and 12 weeks of age. OI mice were significantly smaller than WT. They exhibited reduced intestinal expression and quantity of collagen-I; additionally, expression of collagen-III and elastin was reduced in male Oim-/- mice. Structurally, shorter crypts were observed in OI small intestine, suggesting potential structural atrophy secondary to disrupted intestinal collagen content. OI mice maintained gut integrity and demonstrated a greater gut length to body mass ratio, suggesting a morphological adaptive strategy for greater energy demands in OI. Microbiome profiling revealed generally similar microbial composition in OI mice and WT; however, reduced levels of Lactobacillus were consistently observed in female Col1a1Jrt/+ and Oim-/- mice. In keeping, we observed reduced cecal short-chain fatty acid concentrations in female Col1a1Jrt/+ and Oim-/- mice. This study unveils a previously unexplored sex-dependent intestinal phenotype in OI mice, highlighting gut health as a critical consideration in OI management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47221-2.

Introduction

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a rare heritable connective tissue disorder, primarily characterized by fragile bones prone to fractures, skeletal deformities, and short stature1. OI mainly results from pathogenic variants in genes encoding the alpha chains of collagen-I, COL1A1 or COL1A21. Collagen-I, a fibrillar triple-helical protein assembled from two procollagen alpha 1 chains and one procollagen alpha 2 chain2, is the primary component of the extracellular matrix (ECM) in bones. Clinically, impaired bone matrix formation and mineralization lead to pronounced skeletal fragility, which remains the central focus of pediatric OI management1. While skeletal manifestations of OI are well-documented, the impact on other organ systems, particularly the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, has received far less attention. So far, GI symptoms have often been attributed secondarily to skeletal deformities such as scoliosis, hip abnormalities, or acetabular protrusions that may affect bowel mobility3. However, collagen-I is also essential for intestinal integrity and function4, and emerging clinical evidence shows that individuals with OI have a markedly increased risk of GI disorders, including peptic ulcer disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and constipation5. Moreover, a recent survey assessing the broader impact of OI on daily life reported a high prevalence of “bowel and stomach issues” among patients6, underscoring the need to better understand GI involvement in OI.

The intestine plays a central role in nutrient absorption, and functions as a multilayered barrier that prevents harmful pathogens from entering systemic circulation7. Effective absorption relies on continuous epithelial turnover, a process in which collagen-I influences cellular differentiation and homeostasis7. Epithelial integrity, arguably the most important component of the gut barrier, also depends on the structural support of collagen-I within the intestinal ECM8. Moreover, the intestine harbors a diverse gut microbiota, especially in the cecum and colon, dominated by bacterial communities9. These microbes ferment unabsorbed carbohydrates and amino acids to produce metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and branched-chain fatty acids (BCFAs), which are critical for maintaining epithelial integrity, modulating nutrient absorption, and regulating systemic metabolism10–12.

In addition to bone fragility, metabolic alteration is another characteristic of OI. Young OI patients and juvenile OI mice demonstrate hypermetabolism, reduced adiposity, increased energy expenditure, and elevated body temperature13–15, indicating higher nutritional demands during prepuberty. This hypermetabolic phenotype tends to resolve in adulthood and appears to shift toward an increased body fat-to-muscle ratio16. Dietary intervention studies further showed that male mice with moderate-to-severe OI are protected from high-fat diet–induced obesity but not from glucose intolerance17. Because the intestine plays a pivotal role in systemic energy metabolism, and collagen-I is the predominant collagen in the gut, intestinal involvement may contribute to OI pathophysiology; yet this remains largely unexplored.

Given the high prevalence of GI symptoms in OI patients5,6, and, additionally, the substantial contribution of GI problems to OI mortality rates18, this study aimed to explore GI alterations in mouse models of OI caused by distinct collagen-I variants. Two genetically different OI mouse models were examined: Col1a1Jrt/+, which carries a Col1a1 variant and models moderate-to-severe OI19, and Oim−/−, which harbors a Col1a2 variant and models a severe OI phenotype20. Our objective was to characterize the intestinal consequences of these collagen-I mutations by assessing intestinal morphology, function, and microbiota composition, thereby advancing the understanding of GI involvement in OI pathophysiology.

Results

OI mice exhibit a greater relative length of the small and large intestine

Previous studies have established that Col1a1Jrt/+ mice (Jrt) and Oim−/− mice (Oim) exhibit hallmark features of OI, including smaller body size, reduced bone mass and strength, and spinal curvature1,19,20. Consistent with these findings, we observed that both Jrt (Fig. 1A,B, Supplemental Table 3) and Oim (Fig. 2A,B, Supplemental Table 3) mice were smaller in size across sexes and ages compared with their respective WT littermates (genotype effect p < 0.001, three-way ANOVA). In all genotypes and sexes, body mass increased over time (p < 0.001), and male mice demonstrated greater body mass than female mice (p < 0.001).

During intestinal isolation, we observed a profound fragility, particularly the duodenum, in intestines from Jrt (Fig. 1C,D) and Oim (Fig. 2C,D) mice compared to their corresponding WT littermates. In Jrt, 50% of intestines had at least one duodenal rupture compared to 12.5% in WT (Fig. 1D, arrow, Supplementary Table 4). Similarly, 69% of Oim intestines had at least one duodenal breakage, whereas only 23.3% of WT intestines were affected (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table 4).

Raw intestinal lengths did not differ significantly across genotypes in either Jrt or Oim mouse models (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 5). However, since OI mice display substantially lower body mass than their WT counterparts, we also examined relative intestinal lengths (normalized to body weight), given that gut length, particularly the small intestine, strongly correlates with body mass21. After normalizing, both Jrt (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Table 6) and Oim (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Table 6) mice demonstrated significantly greater relative small intestine lengths compared to their corresponding WT (p < 0.001), with significant overall effect of sex (greater in females, p < 0.001) and age (p < 0.001). Relative colon length was also greater in Jrt (Fig. 1F) and Oim (Fig. 2F) mice (p < 0.001), again with significant main effects of sex (greater in females, p < 0.001) and age (p < 0.05 for Jrt, p < 0.001 for Oim).

Overall, OI mice showed increased intestinal fragility and greater body-mass-adjusted small- and large-intestine lengths despite having similar absolute gut lengths as WT controls.

OI mice exhibit altered intestinal extracellular matrix composition and structure

Next, we investigated how collagen-I genetic variants may influence intestinal ECM composition and structure in OI mice. As the duodenum was identified as the most fragile intestinal segment, we focused on duodenal expression of key ECM structural genes, including Col1a1 and Col1a2 (coding for collagen-I α1 and α2 chains, respectively), Col2a1 (collagen-II α1), Col3a1 (collagen-III α1), and Eln (Tropoelastin). In Jrt mice, Col1a1 expression was significantly lower (p < 0.05) in male OI mice and Eln expression significantly increased (p > 0.05) in female OI mice, while other genes (Col1a2, Col2a1, Col3a1, Eln) were not affected (Fig. 3A). Immunoblotting, conducted on a subset of samples (n = 5–6 per genotype), confirmed lower collagen-I protein levels in Jrt duodenum compared to WT (Fig. 3C,D), with insufficient power to compare between sex groups statistically. In Oim mice, Col1a2 expression was significantly lower in males and showed a decreasing trend in females (p = 0.08) compared to sex-matched WT (Fig. 3B). Male Oim mice also showed significantly decreased expression of Col3a1 (p < 0.01) and Eln (p < 0.01), along with a trend toward lower Col1a1 expression (p = 0.06). However, these genotype-related decreases were not observed in female Oim mice. At the protein level, collagen-I α1 levels were similar in Oim and WT mice, although collagen-III α1 showed a decreasing trend (p = 0.15) (Fig. 3E,F).

To validate gene expression data, we quantified collagen and non-collagen content using Fast green/Sirius red staining in histological sections from Jrt and WT mice (Fig. 4). Consistent with the transcript findings, Jrt mice displayed lower collagen content than WT, with a significant main genotype effect in the duodenum (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4C left), but not in jejunum, ileum (Supplementary Table 7), or colon (Fig. 4C right). Notably, female mice of both genotypes (Jrt and WT) had higher collagen levels than males, with a significant sex effect observed in the duodenum (p < 0.05) and jejunum (p < 0.01).

Next, we examined the villus-crypt structures in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum (Fig. 4B,D,E, Supplementary Table 8). The absolute crypt depth was significantly shorter in the duodenum (p < 0.05) and jejunum (p < 0.01) of OI mice (Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, male WT and OI mice demonstrated greater villus height than females (p < 0.05) in the duodenum (Supplementary Fig. 2). The villus height to crypt depth ratio (V/C) was significantly higher in the duodenum (Fig. 4D left, p < 0.001), jejunum (p < 0.05), and ileum (p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 2) of Jrt mice compared to WT. However, the cross-sectional villus surface area was similar in duodenum (Fig. 4D right) and lower in the jejunum (Supplementary Fig. 2, p < 0.05) of Jrt mice compared to WT. A modest decrease in duodenal villus surface area was observed in female WT and OI mice relative to males across age groups (p < 0.05).

Overall, both Jrt and Oim mice exhibited altered expression of intestinal ECM proteins, along with lower intestinal collagen content and shorter crypt length across the small intestine.

OI mice exhibit distinct intestinal permeability profiles

To assess gut permeability in OI mice, a small cohort of 12-week-old mice was subjected to an oral gavage FITC-dextran assay. In this assay, blood levels of FITC-dextran were measured 1 h after a single oral dose. Oral gavage was performed by trained personnel, and all mice tolerated the procedure well, showing no signs of distress during or after the 1h post-gavage period. In Jrt mice, plasma FITC-dextran levels were generally similar to WT, but with a trend toward lower permeability in Jrt mice (Fig. 4E, p = 0.16). Notably, two Jrt mice displayed extremely high plasma FITC-dextran levels, approximately 100-fold higher than the others, and were excluded from statistical analysis; however, for transparency, these values are indicated as red stars in the graph. Oim mice, in contrast, exhibited significantly higher plasma FITC-dextran levels compared to WT (p < 0.05), suggesting increased gut permeability (Fig. 4G). However, similar to Jrt mice, two male Oim mice showed extremely high plasma FITC-dextran levels after oral delivery, potentially reflecting a higher risk of GI leakage in OI mice.

To assess a potential “gastrointestinal leakage”, we next assessed the expression of (i) cellular tight junction markers: tight junction protein 1 (Tjp1) and occludin (Ocln), and (ii) inflammatory cytokine Tnf-α. Female Jrt mice exhibited higher Tjp1 expression compared with female WT (Fig. 4F), while Ocln was not affected. In contrast, Tjp1 expression was not affected in female Oim mice, whereas Ocln expression was significantly reduced compared to female WT (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4H). Duodenal expression of Tnf-α was unaffected in Jrt mice (Fig. 4F), but was significantly decreased in male and female Oim mice (Fig. 4H).

Overall, these results reveal distinct intestinal adaptations in OI models: Jrt mice seem to preserve gut integrity despite altered collagen, whereas Oim mice demonstrate increased gut permeability without the anticipated inflammatory response.

OI and WT mice exhibit similar microbial profiles, albeit reduced abundance of specific genera and microbial activities in female OI mice.

To explore gut microbial profiles, 16S rRNA sequencing was performed in cecal samples of 4-week-old OI and WT mice. Alpha diversity, measuring diversity within individuals, was assessed using Chao1 index for species richness and the Shannon index for both richness and evenness. Alpha diversity was similar in OI and WT mice of both Jrt (Supplementary Fig. 3A) and Oim (Supplementary Fig. 4A) models. For beta diversity, which examines similarities in microbial profiles between groups, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) demonstrated comparable cecal microbiota compositions in male and female OI and WT mice in Jrt (Supplementary Fig. 3B) and Oim (Supplementary Fig. 4B) models.

The relative abundance of the top ten most dominant genera was generally comparable between OI and WT both in Jrt (Fig. 5A) and Oim (Fig. 6A) models. However, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) identified three genera with differential abundance (LDA > 2.0) in Jrt mice compared to their WT: Lactobacillus, Lachnospiraceae_FCS020, and Desulfovibrio (Fig. 5B). Specifically, female OI mice demonstrated lower abundance of these genera compared to their WT counterparts. In Oim mice, LEfSe (LDA > 2.0, p < 0.05) identified four differentially abundant genera, Lactobacillus, Anaerosporobacter, Turicibacter, and Leuconostoc (Fig. 6B). Specifically, low levels of Lactobacillus were found in female Oim mice compared to their corresponding WT. Thus, female mice in both Jrt and Oim OI mouse models demonstrated reduced levels of Lactobacillus.

Lactobacillus is known as a key producer of SCFAs and BCFAs, which are crucial for metabolism and skeletal turnover9,22. Thus, we further quantified SCFAs and BCFAs in cecal samples of OI mice and their WT littermates. In Jrt model, total SCFA levels exhibited a significant genotype effect (p < 0.01) and a genotype-sex interaction (p < 0.05), with female Jrt mice producing less total SCFAs than WT (Fig. 5C, left, Supplementary Table 9). A sex effect was observed in WT mice (p < 0.05), where females produced more SCFAs than males, but this difference was absent in Jrt mice. Total BCFA production was similar in Jrt and their corresponding WT mice (Fig. 5C, right, Supplementary Table 10). The proportions of dominant SCFAs were similar across all groups (Fig. 5D), suggesting a general reduction in SCFA production in female Jrt mice rather than a specific SCFA deficiency. Among individual fatty acids, Jrt mice produced significantly lower levels of acetic (genotype effect p = 0.01), butyric (p = 0.005), propionic (p = 0.02) (Fig. 5E), caproic (p = 0.004), and heptanoic (p = 0.005) acids, as well as isovaleric (p = 0.008) and isocaproic (p = 0.009) acids compared to WT (Supplementary Fig. 3C, Supplementary Tables 9, 10).

In Oim model, a significant genotype effect with lower production in female Oim mice was observed for both SCFAs (Fig. 6C, left, Supplemental Table 9) and BCFAs (Fig. 6C, right, Supplemental Table 10). The proportions of dominant SCFAs were similar across groups in the Oim model (Fig. 6D), confirming a general reduction in SCFA production in female Oim mice rather than a specific SCFA deficiency. Among individual fatty acids, Oim mice had significantly lower cecal concentrations of acetic (genotype effect p = 0.03), butyric (p = 0.04), propionic (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6E), valeric (p = 0.06), isobutyric (p = 0.01) and isovaleric (p = 0.02) acids compared to their WT (Supplementary Fig. 4C, Supplementary Tables 9, 10).

These data support lower bacterial fermentation activities in female OI mice compared to female WT mice, both in Jrt and Oim models.

Cross-model comparison of intestinal phenotypes between Jrt and Oim mice

To compare intestinal phenotypes in Jrt and Oim mice on different mouse strain backgrounds, we analyzed fold-changes relative to their respective WT controls. Examination of small intestinal length (Fig. 7A) revealed significant effects of OI model (p < 0.05), sex (p < 0.01), and age (p < 0.05) by three-way ANOVA. Specifically, Jrt males exhibited longer intestinal lengths than Oim males, particularly at older ages, while females showed relatively consistent lengths across colonies (supported by a significant model × sex interaction (p < 0.05)). A significant model × age interaction (p < 0.001) revealed that the difference between the two models was age-dependent, with Jrt mice showing increased intestinal length with age, while Oim mice displayed minimal age-related change. For the relative colon length (Fig. 7B), only a significant main effect of age (p < 0.001) was observed along with a significant model × age interaction (p < 0.001). Specifically, the relative colon length increased with age among Jrt mice, while such trend was not observed in Oim model (Fig. 7B). The comparison of gene expression changes between Jrt and Oim mice demonstrated that the duodenal ECM expression was more affected in Oim mice than in Jrt mice (Fig. 7C). Specifically, male Oim mice showed greater decreases in the expression of Col1a1, Col1a2, and Col3a1, while female Oim mice displayed more suppressed Eln expression than the female Jrt mice. A significant model difference (p < 0.05) was observed in the intestinal permeability assessed via oral delivery of FITC dextran (Fig. 7D). Male Oim mice demonstrated a more prominent increase in gut permeability compared to Jrt mice, while in female mice similar trend was noted but did not reach statistical significance. Finally, we compared the total cecal SCFAs (Fig. 7E) and BCFAs (Fig. 7F) levels in Jrt and Oim mice. For both SCFAs and BCFAs, significant main effects of model (p < 0.01) demonstrated a higher reduction in SCFAs and BCFAs in Oim mice compared to Jrt.

Discussion

This study is the first to demonstrate that OI, a collagen-I-related disorder primarily linked to bone fragility, is accompanied by measurable alterations in intestinal structure and function in mouse models of OI. We demonstrate that male and female OI mice from two distinct mouse models both had disproportionately larger intestinal tracts for their body size, together with decreased intestinal collagen-I content. In the more severe OI model, Oim mice, intestinal expression of collagen-III and elastin were also reduced, together with higher gut permeability. While microbial diversities were generally comparable between OI mice and their corresponding WT groups in both models, certain genera were found to be less abundant in both OI colonies. In particular, lower levels of Lactobacillus were observed in female OI mice of both models, coupled with lower bacterial fermentation activity as evidenced by reduced cecal SCFAs in both Jrt and Oim mice, and reduced cecal BCFAs in Oim mice. Overall, our data indicate that pathological variants of collagen-I are associated with changes in intestinal structure and microbiota, warranting further research on GI health in OI pathophysiology and treatment.

Since collagen-I is an integral part of the GIT ECM8, Col1a1 variants could be expected to affect intestinal ECM, altering intestinal structure and absorption. We found lower intestinal collagen-I expression in Jrt and Oim mice compared to their WT counterparts, consistent with the widespread collagen dysregulation seen in OI23. Lower expression of intestinal Col1a1, Col3a1, and Eln were observed in male OI mice, with a suppression level being greater in the Oim model, which represents a more severe OI phenotype. This suggests male OI mice may have more compromised intestinal structural protein expression compared to female mice. Interestingly, female Jrt mice showed a significant increase in Eln expression, suggesting a compensatory mechanism that warrants further research. While the pattern in Jrt and Oim mice may reflect differences in phenotypic severity, it is also important to consider that the two models harbor mutations in distinct collagen chains (Col1a1 in Jrt and Col1a2 in Oim). Type I collagen consists of two α1(I) chains and one α2(I) chain, and mutations affecting either component may differentially influence collagen assembly, fibril formation, and extracellular matrix organization. In particular, the absence of functional α2 chains in Oim mice results in homotrimeric collagen-I formation, which may alter tissue biomechanics and cell–matrix signaling in ways that are distinct from the structural alterations caused by mutant α1 chains in Jrt mice. Therefore, both mutation-specific effects and overall disease severity likely contribute to the observed intestinal differences.

Further, OI mice demonstrated altered villus-crypt structure in the small intestine histologically, with shortened crypts observed in both the duodenum and jejunum. Crypt morphology has been proven to shift with physiological demands. While deeper crypts can be associated with increased cellular proliferation on the epithelial layer24, the observed shorter crypts in OI may suggest mild structural atrophy, which might decrease the enterocytic turnover on the absorptive villus units25. Despite similar absolute gut lengths, OI mice possess disproportionately larger intestinal tracts for their body size, suggesting possible compensatory structural adaptations that may help accommodate their elevated energy turnover13. These exploratory intestinal phenotypic findings warrant further investigation into how these intestinal modifications may impact gut functionality and nutritional well-being in OI mice.

Although we did not perform quantitative biomechanical testing of intestinal tissue, we observed a higher frequency of duodenal ruptures in OI mice during tissue isolation. Outliers in gut permeability also suggest a greater risk for leaky GI tract, similar to findings in Ehlers-Danlos syndrome mouse models, which also show brittle connective tissue due to collagen variants26,27. Although collagen-I is known for providing tensile strength4, and recent publication showed that tendons from Jrt mice exhibit reduced mechanical properties28, gut permeability assessments in 12-week-old OI mice indicated intact intestinal integrity in Jrt, but increased gut permeability in Oim mice. Overall, the impact of OI-causing collagen-I variants on intestinal biomechanics remains poorly understood, and further research is needed to determine how the OI genotype influences intestinal mechanical properties that are essential for gut integrity and digestion29,30.

In addition to structural alterations of collagen-I, cellular stress pathways have emerged as important contributors to OI pathophysiology31,32. Intracellular retention of misfolded collagen-I chains can induce endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, activate the unfolded protein response (UPR), and promote oxidative and inflammatory stress signaling. These mechanisms have been increasingly recognized in OI bone pathology31,33 and may extend beyond the skeletal system. In the intestine, chronic ER stress and oxidative stress are known to impair epithelial barrier integrity, alter epithelial cell differentiation, and influence mucosal immune responses34,35. Therefore, it is plausible that collagen-related cellular stress in OI may contribute to altered intestinal metabolism, barrier function, and microbiome interactions. However, future studies are needed to examine ER stress markers, redox balance, and inflammatory signaling in intestinal tissue to provide important mechanistic insight into genotype-specific GI manifestations.

Interestingly, unlike previous findings of increased TNF pathway activity in male bone marrow cells of certain OI models (Col1a1+/-365, Col1a2Oim/+)36, we found lower intestinal Tnfα expression in Oim mice, especially in males. This discrepancy may be due to the tissue microenvironment, age, or genetic background of the models used. Inflammatory signaling is highly tissue-specific37, and pathway enrichment does not mean Tnfα is upregulated everywhere. Broader immune-response signatures seen in earlier analyses indicate complex transcriptional patterns36, not uniform changes in Tnfα. Overall, these results suggest that inflammatory signaling in OI varies between organs and may depend on local extracellular matrix conditions and metabolic needs, highlighting the importance of tissue-specific studies.

The 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed significant differences in gut microbiota between OI and WT mice. Female Jrt mice showed lower abundances of Lactobacillus, Lachnospiraceae_FCS020, and Desulfovibrio. Similarly, Oim mice also had lower abundances of several fermentative genera, including Lactobacillus, along with Anaerosporobacter and Turicibacter. Lactobacillus is a vital probiotic genus, essential for gut integrity and metabolic health38. The observed differences in gut microbiome composition may be driven by potential altered gut transit time39 or variations in absorption efficiency40, as food intake and diet quality were consistent between both genotypes. Regarding the observed sex-dependency in gut microbial differences, cecal samples for 16S were taken from prepubertal mice, suggesting that these sex-dependent differences are not solely influenced by sex hormones, consistent with prior reports of metabolic differences in prepubertal OI mice13. Other factors, such as diet-microbiome interactions and colonic transit time, may also contribute to this sex-dependent variation39.

The changes in gut microbial composition were accompanied by lower cecal SCFA concentrations in 4-week-old Jrt and Oim mice, as well as lower BCFAs in Oim mice. SCFAs, known to protect against GI diseases40 commonly reported in OI patients5,6, also regulate energy homeostasis through the gut-adipose tissue axis, influencing thermogenesis and fat storage41. Reduced SCFA production in OI mice may be associated with the hypermetabolic status previously observed in the Jrt model13, as their gut microbiota may have decreased energy-harvesting capacity, contributing to reduced fat storage41. More importantly, SCFAs, including propionate and butyrate, have shown promise in supporting colonocyte function, reducing intestinal inflammation, maintaining the gut barrier42, enhancing skeletal health by inhibiting osteoclastogenesis43 and stimulating the production of insulin-like growth factor 1 to promote skeletal anabolism44. Taken together, these findings highlight the need to further explore gut microbial involvement in OI, supporting novel research directions in terms of clinical applications, such as pro- and prebiotic supplementation in OI patients.

However, our study has limitations. First, due to the modest sample size of this exploratory study, the study may be underpowered to detect higher-order interaction effects. This limits statistical power and constrains the ability to detect subtle differences in parameters such as intestinal length, and therefore, we interpret nonsignificant results with caution. Therefore, the absence of interaction should not be interpreted as evidence of independence between factors. Second, while both Jrt and Oim mouse models demonstrated similar metabolic13,15 and skeletal1 phenotypes with OI patients, findings in this exploratory animal study need to be further validated in future preclinical studies before making translational implications in clinical settings. Third, the OI mouse models exhibit low breeding efficiency in terms of litter size and genotype distribution, resulting in pups being produced in different batches. Although we standardized our procedures to minimize batch-related variation, we cannot fully exclude a cage and/or batch effect in our microbiome analyses. To assess whether batch effects may have influenced our colonies, we examined Helicobacter pylori abundance in our dataset. Prior studies have shown that cage effects can account for up to 31.7% of microbial differences, in part due to Helicobacter pylori infection45. In our assessment, Helicobacter abundance was minor and remained consistent across all study groups of both OI colonies, suggesting our data is not impacted by batch-related contamination effects.

Clinically, pediatric OI patients experience altered metabolism and body composition14,16, and, with age, develop GI issues, including unspecified abdominal pain, inflammatory bowel disease, hernia, and functional constipation5,6. Further, it was shown that these GI issues in OI further contribute to increased mortality rates of OI patients18. Gut-derived SCFAs are crucial for maintaining gut epithelial integrity and motility12. Low Lactobacillus abundance and the reduction in cecal SCFA levels in OI mice may be linked not only to the increased severity of gastroesophageal reflux disease and constipation in OI patients46, but also to the heightened risk of cardiovascular diseases in OI47. Currently, GI symptoms in OI are managed symptomatically, largely following general gastroenterological guidelines, such as constipation is treated with laxatives, stool softeners, and dietary fiber. However, these approaches target symptoms rather than underlying mechanisms. Given that collagen-related structural fragility, altered biomechanics, and inflammatory pathways may contribute to GI dysfunction in OI, and that microbiota-derived metabolites appear reduced, future strategies could include microbiome-targeted therapies, nutritional optimization, and modulation of cellular stress pathways. Evaluating GI-specific outcomes in OI clinical trials may clarify whether disease-modifying treatments also improve GI health, while mechanistic studies into microbial metabolites could identify new therapeutic avenues to mitigate both GI and systemic metabolic complications in OI.

In conclusion, although nutrition counselling has been revised in OI care16, most treatment modalities and research have been focused on skeletal health, often overlooking the metabolic challenges and the effects of mutant collagen-I on GI function. Limited data on nutrition and GI health in OI leave the specific metabolic needs of these patients largely unclear48. Our study shows for the first time that GI alterations in OI may depend on the specific collagen mutation, indicating that previously assumed GI problems are not solely a consequence of skeletal deformities. Rather, they likely arise from a combination of intrinsic structural effects of collagen mutations, skeletal deformities, and possibly general nutritional behavior. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating nutritional and GI support into OI care. Further research is needed to define the unique metabolic and nutritional requirements of OI patients across different ages and to identify strategies for managing OI-specific metabolic and GI complications.

Methods

Animal model and study design

The study was approved by the McGill University Animal Care Committee (Protocol #7127). All procedures followed guidelines and policies of the Canadian Council on Animal Care and McGill University. This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.049. The ARRIVE checklist has been completed and is available in the supplementary material. Mice were bred and housed at the Shriner’s Hospital for Children, following McGill guidelines. Animals were kept in a controlled environment with a 12-hour light-dark cycle (6 AM − 6 PM) at 21 °C, housed with sex- and genotype-matched siblings (up to 5 per cage). Mice were fed with Teklad Global 18% Protein Rodent Diet (#2918, Envigo) and had ad libitum water. All mice used in this study were bred at the Shriner’s Hospital for Children Montreal, with a low breeding rate due to OI skeletal phenotypes and the low occurrence of targeted genotypes. To minimize background and batch effects, all mice were separated by sex and genotype immediately after weaning and genotyping, and the maternal and paternal genotypes were controlled to be consistent across all pups used in this study. All the breeding and mice separations were handled by the same animal facility technician. Sample collection procedures were performed under specific pathogen–free (SPF) conditions using aseptic techniques, with autoclaved cages and supplies, sterile tools, and biosafety cabinet handling to minimize microbial contamination and prevent microbiota transfer between cages. This study utilized two OI mouse models, the Col1a1Jrt/+ and Oim−/− mouse models, provided by Jane Aubin (University of Toronto, Toronto, Canada) and Bettina M. Willie (Shriners Hospital for Children, Montreal, Canada), respectively. The Col1a1Jrt/+ mouse model, on the FVB background strain, carries a T to C transition in the Col1a1 gene19. The Oim−/− mouse model, on the C57BL6 background, carries a spontaneous single-base deletion in the Col1a2 gene20. Male and female Col1a1Jrt/+ and Oim−/− mice and their corresponding age- and sex-matched wildtype littermates (WT) of 4, 8, and 12 weeks of age were studied.

In-vivo gut permeability assessment

Male and female 12-week-old WT and OI mice were fasted from 6 am to 10 am and given a single oral dose of 4-kDa fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran (#60842-46-8, Sigma-Aldrich, dose: 600 mg/kg body mass, solution: 80 mg/mL phosphate-buffered saline; PBS) by a trained animal technician. Mice were closely monitored during the 1-hour post-gavage observation period. Submandibular blood samples were taken before and 1 h after administration into heparin-coated capillary tubes (#22260950, ThermoFisher Scientific). Plasma fluorescence (1:8 dilution in PBS) was determined at 530 nm (excitation at 485 nm) with a microplate reader (Infinite F200, Tecan Group Ltd.). A standard curve (0.025 to 25 mg/mL FITC-dextran in PBS) was used, and intestinal permeability was determined by subtracting pre-gavage FITC-dextran levels from post-gavage readings.

Tissue sample collection and intestinal length

Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation and terminated by an intracardiac puncture to collect blood, followed by cervical dislocation. The intestines were carefully isolated and photographed for length determination using ImageJ (version 1.53, https://imagej.net/). The small intestine was measured from the pyloric sphincter to the ileum, and the colon from the cecal junction to the rectum. Afterwards, the intestines were rinsed with PBS, and 5-mm segments of the superior duodenum segment, middle point of the jejunum, end point of the ileum, and middle point of the colon were fixed in 10% buffered formalin for histology. A 1-cm piece from each segment was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further processing.

Histological tissue processing

Following 24-hour fixation, samples were dehydrated through graded ethanol solutions (80%, 95%, 100%), followed by three 20-minute xylene washes (#218545, Fisher Chemical). Samples were infiltrated with paraffin (#2207140, Leica) at 60 °C for 90 min and embedded into blocks. Transverse intestinal Sect. (5-µm) were de-paraffinized in two changes of xylene (5 min each) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 70%, and 50%; 5 min each) followed by distilled water (5 min). Rehydrated slides were stained with Fast green and Sirius red (#9046, Chrondrex) at room temperature for 30 min, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sirius Red is a strong anionic sulfonated dye that binds with high affinity to the densely packed amino acids lining the triple-helical collagen backbone, giving collagen a red signal. In contrast, Fast Green preferentially associates with the remaining non-collagenous proteins as it does not interact with the tightly ordered collagen helix, thereby creating a clear two-color separation of protein classes on the slides50. Of note, the collagen/non-collagen staining analysis was performed in the Jrt model only because tissue processing in Oim mice proved technically challenging due to reduced stability of the intestine. During sectioning and staining procedures, Oim tissues frequently exhibited tearing, folding, or detachment, compromising reproducibility and quantitative evaluation. To ensure methodological consistency and reliable histological quantification, we therefore restricted this analysis to the Jrt model, where tissue integrity allowed standardized processing.

Histomorphometry

Histological sections were scanned using a 20x brightfield microscope, and the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and colon were analyzed using Fiji software51. For each stained section, 10 well-oriented villus–crypt units were randomly selected, and villus height (from tip to crypt mouth), villus width (measured at the midpoint), and crypt depth (from crypt mouth to base) were manually recorded. To enhance accuracy and reproducibility, measurements were performed in duplicate, yielding 20 data points per sample. The averaged values for each mouse were further used to calculate the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (V/C), a common parameter used to quantitatively assess the intestinal morphological disease response52,53. The small intestinal surface area was estimated using the Eq. (2 π) (villi width/2) (villus height)54. For collagen quantification, purple staining was isolated using Fiji’s color deconvolution, and the percentage of purple-stained area from the total tissue area was quantified.

Intestinal gene expression

From snap-frozen samples, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol™ (#15596026, ThermoFisher Scientific), and reverse transcription was performed using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (#4387406, Applied Biosystem), according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Gene expression of the intestinal ECM (Col1a1 and Col1a2 (coding for collagen-I alpha chains), Col2a1 (collagen-II), Col3a1 (collagen-III), and Eln (Tropoelastin)), intestinal barrier integrity (Tjp1 and Ocln), and inflammatory status (Tnf-a) was assessed using real-time PCR (primer details: Supplementary Table 1). The TATA-binding protein (Tbp) and hydroxymethylbilane synthase (Hmbs) were used as housekeeping genes55. The 2−ΔΔCT method was applied, where the means of OI male and female groups were normalized to their sex- and age-matched WT counterparts.

Immunoblotting

Total protein was extracted using TRIzol™, following the manufacturer’s instructions, and quantified using the Qubit Protein Assay Kit (#Q33211, ThermoFisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (19.68 mg/sample) were separated on 8% SDS-PAGE (Mini-PROTEAN® Tetra Cell, BioRad) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (#162 − 0115, BioRad). Transfer efficiency was assessed by Ponceau S staining (#P7170, Sigma Life Science). Following blocking with 5% non-fat milk in tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 2), washed 3 times with TBST for 15 min each, and incubated with secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table 2) for 35 min at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence substrate kit (#32106, ThermoFisher Scientific), and β-actin was used as a loading control. Densitometry was performed using ImageJ (version 1.53, https://imagej.net/).

Intestinal microbial activity

Cecal luminal contents were weighed and snap-frozen during animal dissection. Thawed samples were vortexed with 1 mL sterile PBS and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm nylon syringe-driven filter into a glass vial. Microbial-derived SCFAs (acetic, butyric, propionic, valeric, caproic, heptanoic acids) and BCFAs (isobutyric, isovaleric, isocaproic acids) were quantified using gas chromatography (6890 A series, Agilent Technologies) with a flammable ionization detector (GC-FID). Helium was used as a carrier gas (1 mL/min), the inlet temperature was 220 °C, and the detector temperature was 230 °C. The oven temperature started at 100 °C (held for 1 min), then increased to 220 °C by 10 °C/min (14-minute method). A standard curve was generated using pure free volatile fatty acid standard mix (#46975-U, Sigma-Aldrich), with a detection threshold of 0.15 µmol/mL56.

Microbial DNA extraction

Microbial DNA was extracted using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro (#51804, Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA purity was assessed by measuring absorbance at 260 nm and at 280 nm using NanoDrop™ One (ThermoFisher Scientific). Samples were stored at − 80 °C until 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Microbial DNA samples were amplified and sequenced at the McGill Genome Center, Montreal, Canada. Briefly, the V4 region of 16S bacterial rRNA was amplified using the primer pair 515 F (GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA) and 806R (GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT). Amplification was performed using the Kapa HiFi Hotstar Ready Mix (Roche). Samples were pooled and normalized before quantification with qPCR and the LabChip. The pooled samples were loaded for sequencing on the MiSeq Illumina platform (Argonne National Laboratory, Institute for Genomics and Systems Biology, Next Generation Sequencing Core). The sequencing data was processed with DADA2 using the GenPipes Amplicon Sequencing pipeline, with SILVA taxonomy base. All the 16S amplicon sequence variants (ASV) table analysis and visualization were performed using MicrobiomeAnalyst (https://www.microbiomeanalyst.ca). Data was filtered by default, followed by rarefication to the minimum library size (n = 127,464) and Good’s coverage check (> 99.98%). Alpha-diversity, including Chao1 and Shannon index, was compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Beta-diversity (Bray-Curtis index) was compared with permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) and visualized by Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA). LEfSe was applied to identify differentially abundant genera between OI and WT groups (LDA > 2.0).

Statistical analyses

Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and outliers were determined by the ROUT method and excluded from analysis. Factorial ANOVA was applied within a balanced experimental design to evaluate the effects of multiple factors simultaneously. Three-way ANOVA was used to assess the effects of age, genotype, and sex, including all main effects and interactions; two-way ANOVA was used to assess the effects of genotype and sex. Given the modest sample size, the analysis was intended to identify consistent directional effects, and interpretation was performed conservatively without assuming absence of interaction where not detected. ANOVAs were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) with post-hoc comparisons conducted using the Bonferroni correction. Independent t-test/Mann-Whitney U test was used to consider genotype effect only. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Additionally, Bayesian model comparison was performed using JASP (JASP Team 2024, Version 0.19.3) to evaluate the relative plausibility of models containing different combinations of main effects and interactions, interpreted based on posterior model probabilities and Bayes Factors. The findings of classical and Bayesian approaches were consistent, supporting the robustness of the conclusions. Only classical ANOVA results are shown in the figures for clarity.
