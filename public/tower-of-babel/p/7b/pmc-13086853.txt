Land-use types shape soil bacterial communities, co-occurrence networks, and predicted functions in karst ecosystems
Authors: Danliang Fang, Dan Chen, Jianbing Zhang, Cong Wang, Shijie Dou, Weiqun Luo, Yanmei Zhu, Wei Zhou, Shenglin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how different land-use types shape soil bacterial communities and functions is critical for promoting sustainable land management and ecological restoration in fragile karst regions. This study evaluated the effects of different land-use types (pitaya [HF], maize [MF], sugarcane [SF], and paddy [PF] fields, forestland [FL], and grassland [AF]) on soil bacterial diversity, community composition, functional potentials, and co-occurrence networks in karst regions. The results showed that HF, MF, and PF exhibited the highest bacterial diversity (Shannon index), whereas SF had the lowest possibly because of continuous cropping and fertilization. Variance partitioning analysis revealed that soil bacterial community composition and functional potential were largely explained by the combined effects of land-use type and soil properties (34.8% for composition and 59.7% for functional potential), suggesting that land-use effects on bacterial communities are mainly mediated through changes in soil physicochemical properties. Hierarchical partitioning analysis further identified that soil texture, particularly silt and sand, and pH were the primary factors shaping bacterial community composition, whereas pH was the dominant driver of functional potential. Functional analysis indicated that HF enhanced nitrogen cycling processes, such as nitrification, aerobic nitrite oxidation, and aerobic ammonia oxidation; SF promoted carbon-related functions, including chemoheterotrophy, aerobic chemoheterotrophy, and cellulolysis; and PF favored iron and sulfur respiration. Co-occurrence network analysis demonstrated that HF had the most complex microbial interactions, likely attributable to no-tillage practices and favorable soil conditions, whereas PF showed the lowest network connectivity. Core bacterial groups such as Acidobacteria, Proteobacteria, Chloroflexi, and Actinobacteria, dominated in abundance but also functioned as keystone taxa, suggesting their critical roles in driving nutrient cycling and maintaining ecological stability. These findings suggest that no-till pitaya cultivation may help improve soil microbial diversity, functional potential, and ecological stability in fragile karst ecosystems, indicating its potential as a sustainable agricultural land-use practice. Introduction

Karst landscapes are widely distributed, covering approximately 18% of the Earth’s terrestrial surface. One of the world’s largest karst regions is located in southwest China, occupying approximately 5.1 × 107 ha1,2. Owing to slow pedogenesis, shallow soils, and discontinuous spatial distribution, the karst peak-cluster depression regions of Guangxi are highly sensitive and vulnerable to erosion and degradation caused by agricultural activities2–4, and have long been recognized as ecologically fragile areas and key targets for rocky desertification control5. With support from the “Grain for Green” project, substantial ecological restoration has been achieved in these regions, primarily converting degraded lands into grasslands or forests3,6. Despite its ecological benefits7, such restoration may limit local agricultural livelihoods and economic development8. To balance ecological protection with economic demands, various agricultural land-use types have been maintained or integrated alongside restoration areas9. Consequently, Guangxi’s karst landscapes now exhibit a dual land-use pattern: (1) ecological restoration areas dominated by forests and grasslands, and (2) agricultural systems characterized by regionally important crops such as sugarcane, maize, paddy rice, and pitaya. Nevertheless, the comparative effects of these distinct land-use types on soil health under the environmental constraints characteristic of karst systems remain poorly understood.

Soil bacteria, the most abundant and functionally diverse components of the soil microbiota, are fundamental drivers of biogeochemical cycling and play pivotal roles in maintaining soil health, fertility, and ecosystem stability10. Moreover, they serve as sensitive biological indicators of environmental changes in resource-limited and fragile karst ecosystems4. Importantly, soil bacterial communities are highly sensitive to land-use changes, which can significantly alter soil physicochemical properties (e.g., soil pH and organic matter content), thereby reshaping bacterial community structure and assembly processes11. To date, most studies have focused on comparing soil bacterial traits between individual agricultural land-use types and restored ecosystems, typically considering single, pairwise land-use contrasts, such as cropland versus forests or grasslands12–14. For instance, within karst regions, previous studies have demonstrated that bacterial diversity and community composition differ markedly between forestland or grassland and single cropland types15–17. Across both karst and non-karst ecosystems, higher bacterial diversity has been reported in individual agricultural soils than in individual restored ecosystems12,16. However, whether these patterns are consistent across different cropland types and various vegetation restoration practices, particularly in ecologically fragile karst ecosystems, remains unclear.

In general, the relatively higher bacterial diversity observed in croplands has been associated with more complex microbial interaction networks than those in artificially restored forests or naturally recovered shrublands in karst region15. Nevertheless, agricultural management practices, particularly intensive disturbances such as tillage, may disrupt microbial interactions and reduce the complexity and stability of microbial networks18,19, and are recognized as key drivers shaping the differences in microbial communities between intensively managed agricultural and natural soils20–22. In contrast, in karst ecosystems, comparative studies that integrate soil physicochemical properties, bacterial community composition, and microbial interactions across diverse crop-specific agricultural systems and ecological restoration types (e.g., forests and grasslands) remain limited, leaving their differential effects on soil bacterial communities largely unexplored.

Bacterial communities form complex interaction networks in soils that are essential for soil functioning and ecosystem stability23. However, focusing solely on shifts in bacterial diversity and community composition provides limited insight into the mechanisms by which different land-use types regulate soil microbial functioning in karst ecosystems. Increasing evidence suggests that land-use practices not only alter community composition but also profoundly influence microbial interactions, community organization, and functional potential24,25. Therefore, assessing bacterial network complexity is crucial for understanding microbial interactions under different land-use conditions. Co-occurrence network analysis offers an effective approach to characterize microbial interaction patterns and network structure, providing deeper insights into community stability and ecological organization15,26,27. Meanwhile, functional prediction based on amplicon (16 S rRNA) datasets enables the inference of microbial functional potential and links land-use–driven shifts in bacterial communities to potential alterations in biogeochemical processes28,29. Therefore, integrating bacterial community composition, co-occurrence network structure, and predicted functional attributes thus provides a comprehensive framework for elucidating how different agricultural systems and vegetation restoration practices shape soil microbial ecosystems in fragile karst regions and for revealing the ecological mechanisms underlying land-use effects.

In this study, four predominant agricultural systems—pitaya orchards (HF), maize fields (MF), paddy fields (PF), and sugarcane fields (SF)—and two vegetation restoration approaches, namely forestland (FL) and grassland (AF), were investigated. Considering the generally shallow soil characteristics of karst landscapes, analyses were performed in two topsoil layers (0–10 and 10–20 cm). By integrating analyses of soil physicochemical properties, bacterial diversity and community composition, co-occurrence networks, and predicted functions, this study aimed to: (1) elucidate how land-use types, interacting with karst-specific constraints, shape bacterial community assembly and potential ecological functions across topsoil layers; and (2) identify sustainable agricultural land management practices that promote soil health and conservation in karst ecosystems. We hypothesized that (1) soil bacterial communities, co-occurrence networks, and functional potential would exhibit significant stratification patterns influenced by land-use type, soil depth and their interactions; and (2) among agricultural land-use patterns, pitaya field under conservation tillage practice would enhance soil bacterial diversity and improve network stability. These results are expected to provide critical insights for developing sustainable land-use strategies and establishing depth-specific indicators for soil health assessments in fragile karst ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Site description

The study site was located at the Guohua Karst Ecological Experimental Station (107°22′ E, 23°24′N) in Guangxi Province, China. The area has a southern subtropical monsoon climate, with an average annual precipitation of 1400–1550 mm, an average annual temperature of 21.5 ℃, an annual sunshine duration of 1,682 h, and a frost-free period exceeding 345 d. The landscape is characterized by a typical karst peak-cluster depression, developed on Upper Carboniferous strata consisting mainly of pure limestone. The predominant soil type is brown limestone. The study area represents a typical karst agricultural zone with high population density and intensive land use. Historically, the region has experienced severe ecological challenges, including a fragile eco-environment, low vegetation coverage (less than 10%), and severe rocky desertification. A series of rocky desertification control measures, including hillside closure for afforestation, the “Grain for Green” project, land consolidation, and soil and water conservation practices, have significantly improved the situation. Consequently, the vegetation coverage has increased to approximately 75%. Although large areas have been restored, depression zones and some sloping farmlands remain suitable for agricultural cultivation. In addition to traditional crops, such as maize, sugarcane, and rice, introduced crops such as pitaya are now widely cultivated.

In this study, four typical agricultural land-use types (HF, MF, PF, and SF) and two vegetation restoration types (FL and AF) were selected for comparison. The six land-use types are described below:

Sample collection

Soil samples were collected from different land-use plots using a soil drill (5 cm diameter) at two depth intervals (0–10 and 10–20 cm) in April 2018. The Guohua Karst Ecological Experimental Station, a National Field Scientific Observation and Research Station and core platform for karst ecosystem and rocky desertification control research, has granted us permission to collect soil samples for this study. All sampling procedures adhered strictly to the station’s ecological protection guidelines to minimize disturbance to local ecosystems. The experiment used a completely randomized design with six treatments (four agricultural land uses, FL, and AF). For each land-use type, three plots (20 m × 20 m) were randomly established. Within each plot, five soil samples from soil depths (0–10 and 10–20 cm) were randomly collected and mixed into a single composite sample, resulting in a total of 36 soil samples. After removal of fine roots, plant residues, and large rocks, each composite soil sample was divided into two parts: one part was immediately frozen at – 80 ℃ for microbial analysis, and the other part was air-dried for the analysis of soil physicochemical properties.

Analysis of soil physicochemical properties

Soil physicochemical properties, including pH, electrical conductivity (EC), soil organic matter (SOM), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), and soil texture, were analyzed for each composite soil sample following the methods described by Bao30. Soil pH and EC were determined in a soil-to-water suspension (1:2.5 ratio) using pH (Metro-pH320; Mettler-Toledo Instruments Shanghai Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China) and EC meters (Cond-meter DS-51, Horiba, Kyoto, Japan), respectively. The SOM was determined by wet digestion with H2SO4-K2Cr2O7. The TN was analyzed by digestion with K2SO4, CuSO4, and Se catalysts. The TP concentration was determined via acid digestion using H2SO4 and HClO4 solutions. Soil texture was determined using a particle size analyzer (Malvern Masterizer 2000, UK).

DNA extraction and sequencing

Genomic DNA from each sample (0.5 g fresh weight) was extracted using an E.Z.N.A. DNA kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.). The extracted DNA was detected using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The primers targeting bacterial 16 S rRNA were 515 F (5’-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGG-3’) and 907R (5’-CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3’). PCR amplification was conducted using Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix with GC Buffer High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase. The PCR amplification conditions were as follows: pre-denaturation at 94 ℃ for 3 min; 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 ℃ for 30 s, annealing at 56 ℃ for 60 s, and extension at 72 ℃ for 30 s; and final extension at 72 ℃ for 10 min. The PCR products were pooled in equimolar ratios and sequenced on a HiSeq2500 PE250.

Raw sequencing data were processed and analyzed using the UPARSE pipeline in USEARCH and R. Specifically, paired-end reads were assembled into composite reads using PEAR, resulting in 4,515,396 sequences. The sequences were filtered using the Mothur software package and clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity using UPARSE.

Statistical analysis

Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess the effects of land-use type, soil depth, and their interaction on soil physicochemical properties. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the least significant difference test, was employed to compare differences in soil physicochemical properties, Shannon index, and functional groups among the different land-use types. Independent sample t-tests were used to compare these parameters between the soil layers. The Shannon index was calculated using QIIME (V1.9.1) software. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was used to determine differences in bacterial community composition under various land-use types and soil depths. Permutational MANOVA (Adonis) was conducted using the “vegan” package in R to test the differences in bacterial communities (n = 999 permutations). The Mothur method and SSUrRNA database of SILVA were used for taxonomic classification and analysis of bacterial community composition. Functional prediction of bacterial communities was performed using Functional Annotation of Prokaryotic Taxa (FAPROTAX) software31. Variance Partitioning Analysis (VPA) was used to quantify the individual and shared contributions of land-use type and soil physicochemical properties to variations in bacterial community composition and putative functions32. Redundancy Analysis (RDA) was performed on Hellinger-transformed species and functional data using the “vegan” package to examine associations between environmental factors and bacterial community composition, functional groups, and network topological properties33. Hierarchical partitioning was conducted using the “rdacca.hp” package in R to quantify the contributions of individual environmental factors to the total variation in bacterial community composition, predicted functions, and key network topological properties34. Co-occurrence networks were constructed to assess microbial network complexity across land-use types and soil depths. Spearman’s rank correlations among microbial OTUs were calculated, and statistically significant correlations (|r| > 0.6, p < 0.01) were retained to generate weighted undirected networks using the “WGCNA” and “igraph” R packages35,36. The topological properties of the networks were computed to quantify the complexity of the soil microbial networks, and the networks were visualized using Gephi software37. The topological roles of nodes in the co-occurrence networks were defined by their within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi), metrics computed with the microeco package in the R environment.

Results

Soil physicochemical properties across land-use types and soil depths

Soil pH, EC, SOM, TN, clay, silt, sand, and C/N ratio were all significantly affected by both land-use type and soil depth, and the C/N ratio also showed a significant interaction between the two factors (p < 0.05; Fig. 1). Soil pH ranged from 4.10 to 7.97 across the six land-use types, with the highest value observed in PF and the lowest in SF (p < 0.05). Soil EC in PF, SF, and AF was significantly higher than in HF and MF (p < 0.05). The SOM and TN contents in the AF were markedly higher than those in the HF, MF, and SF. In the 0–10 cm soil layer, vegetation restoration patterns showed markedly higher SOM and TN content than compared to HF and MF. FL significantly reduced TP in comparison with HF, PF, SF, and AF, but significantly elevated N/P ratio relative to HF, MF, PF, and SF (p < 0.05). Among the six treatments, HF had the highest clay content, whereas PF had the highest sand content and the lowest silt content at a soil depth of 10–20 cm.

A total of 4,188,920 high-quality bacterial sequences were obtained from all the samples and clustered into 11,520 OTUs with 97% similarity. Taxonomic annotations classified these sequences into 57 phyla, 71 classes, 139 orders, 257 families, 573 genera, and 340 species.

At depths of 0–20 cm, HF, MF, and PF showed the highest Shannon indices, whereas SF exhibited the lowest values (p < 0.05; Fig. 2a). The vegetation restoration treatments (FL and AF) had significantly higher Shannon indices than SF (p < 0.05; Fig. 2a). MF and PF exhibited higher Shannon indices at both 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm depths. In the 0–10 cm layer, the Shannon index of PF was significantly higher than that of HF (p < 0.05; Fig. 2a). Both PF and SF showed a significant decrease in Shannon index from 0 to 10 cm to 10–20 cm (p < 0.05; Fig. 2a).

The PCoA and Adonis tests based on the Bray-Curtis distance demonstrated significant differences in the bacterial community structure across various land-use types (p < 0.001, Fig. 2b). The first two principal coordinate axes explained 46.2% of the variation in the bacterial communities (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, soil bacterial community structures differed clearly between soil depths, with the first two axes accounting for 54.9% and 45.7% of the variation at soil depths of 0–10 cm and 0–20 cm, respectively (Fig. 2b).

Soil bacterial community composition

Among the six treatments, the dominant bacterial phyla (relative abundance > 7%) were Acidobacteria (16.86–32.62%), Proteobacteria (21.58–32.28%), Actinobacteria (7.65–21.11%), and Chloroflexi (10.60–20.44%), together accounting for 77.96% of the total sequences (Fig. 3a). Among these treatments, SF had the highest relative abundance of Acidobacteria; PF exhibited the highest relative abundances of Proteobacteria and Chloroflexi, and the lowest relative abundance of Actinobacteria (p < 0.05; Figs. 3a, S1). Additionally, PF had the lowest relative abundance of Acidobacteria at both 0–10 cm and 0–20 cm soil depths (p < 0.05), whereas there was no significant difference in Acidobacteria abundance among PF, MF, and AF at the 10–20 cm soil depth (p > 0.05). No significant differences were observed in the relative abundance of Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Chloroflexi, Oxyphotobacteria, Rokubacteria, Gemmatimonadetes, and Latescibacteria between FL and AF (p > 0.05). However, FL significantly increased the relative abundances of Acidobacteria and Nitrospirae compared with AF (p < 0.05; Figs. 3a, S1). Furthermore, vegetation restoration significantly enhanced the relative abundance of Rokubacteria compared to SF, while significantly decreasing the relative abundance of Nitrospirae compared to HF, MF, and PF (p < 0.05; Figs. 3a, S1). AF significantly increased the relative abundance of Actinobacteria compared to HF, MF, and PF (p < 0.05; Figs. 3a, S1). In the 0–10 cm soil layer, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria in SF was significantly higher than that in the 10–20 cm layer. HF and PF had significantly higher relative abundances of Gemmatimonadetes in the 0–10 cm soil layer than in the 10–20 cm layer, while FL showed the opposite trend. Additionally, HF and MF exhibited significantly greater Nitrospirae abundance in the 10–20 cm soil layer than in the 0–10 cm layer (p < 0.05).

Soil bacterial functional characteristics

The top 10 bacterial functional groups included chemoheterotrophy, aerobic chemoheterotrophy, cellulolysis, nitrification, aerobic nitrite oxidation, aerobic ammonia oxidation, iron respiration, respiration of sulfur compounds, predatory or exoparasitic, and sulfate respiration (Figs. 3b, S2). Among the six treatments, SF had the highest relative abundances of chemoheterotrophy, aerobic chemoheterotrophy, and cellulolysis, but the lowest abundances of respiration of sulfur compounds, predatory or exoparasitic, and sulfate respiration (p < 0.05; Figs. 3b, S2). The relative abundances of nitrification, aerobic nitrite oxidation, and aerobic ammonia oxidation were the highest in HF among all the treatments (p < 0.05). The relative abundances of iron respiration, respiration of sulfur compounds, and sulfate respiration were the highest in PF among all treatments (p < 0.05). PF and AF had significantly higher relative abundances of chemoheterotrophy and aerobic chemoheterotrophy in the 0–10 cm soil layer than in the 10–20 cm layer. MF, PF, and FL exhibited significantly greater nitrification abundance in the 10–20 cm soil layer than in the 0–10 cm layer (p < 0.05). In the 0–10 cm soil layer, the relative abundance of aerobic nitrite oxidation in HF, MF, and PF was significantly higher than that in the 10–20 cm layer.

Co-occurrence patterns in networks

Co-occurrence networks were constructed to characterize interaction patterns within bacterial communities across various land-use types and soil depths (Fig. 4). Positive links (green lines) accounted for 85.46–92.51% of all links, substantially exceeding negative links (red lines) across all treatments (Fig. 4). FL exhibited a higher number of positive interactions, and PF exhibited a higher number of negative interactions at the 0–20 cm soil depth, compared with the other treatments (Fig. 4). At the 0–20 cm soil depth, HF exhibited the highest number of nodes and edges among all land-use types, whereas PF contained fewer nodes and connections (Fig. 4; Table S1). Compared to the vegetation restoration treatments (FL and AF), HF and MF showed significantly more network edges at the 0–20 cm soil depth, whereas PF and SF displayed significantly fewer nodes. FL and AF exhibited the longest average path length. In the 0–10 cm soil layer, AF exhibited the highest number of nodes and edges compared to the other land-use types (Fig. 4; Table S1).

Associations between soil bacterial communities, functional groups, and networks with environmental factors

For bacterial communities, at the 0–20 cm soil depth, VPA revealed that a total of 63.8% of the variation in bacterial community composition was explained by land-use types and soil physicochemical properties, with 23.2% explained independently by land use, 5.8% explained independently by soil properties, and 34.8% jointly by both factors (Fig. 6a). The RDA showed that the first two ordination axes explained 73.70% of the total variation in the dominant bacterial phyla at this depth (Fig. 6c). Specifically, the relative abundances of Acidobacteria and Planctomycetes showed positive correlations with silt, but negative correlations with pH, sand, TN, and N/P ratio. The relative abundance of Actinobacteria was positively correlated with silt, TN, and N/P ratio, but negatively correlated with pH and sand. The relative abundances of Proteobacteria and Chloroflexi showed positive correlations with pH and sand, but negative correlations with silt, TN, and N/P ratio. The relative abundances of Oxyphotobacteria and Rokubacteri showed positive correlations with pH, silt, TN and N/P ratio, but negative correlations with sand. The relative abundances of Gemmatimonadetes, Latescibacteria, and Nitrospirae showed positive correlations with pH, sand and TN, but negative correlations with silt (Fig. 6c). Hierarchical partitioning indicated that soil pH was the predominant independent driver (20.02%), followed by sand (15.75%) and silt (13.21%) (Fig. 6e). In addition, at the 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm soil depths, VPA showed that the shared contribution of land-use types and soil properties explained 44.3% and 35.7% of the variation, respectively, whereas land use independently explained 26.2% and 18.8%, and soil properties alone explained 6.5% and 14.3% (Fig. 6a). The RDA indicated that the first two ordination axes collectively explained 78.63% and 75.17% of the variation in dominant bacterial phyla at the 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm soil depths, respectively (Fig. 6c). Notably, the correlation between the relative abundance of Proteobacteria and pH shifted from negative at the 0–10 cm to positive at the 10–20 cm soil depth. Furthermore, at the 0–10 cm soil depth, the relative abundances of Proteobacteria, Chloroflexi, Oxyphotobacteria, and Nitrospirae were positively correlated with EC, whereas those of Acidobacteria, Actinobacteria, Rokubacteria, Gemmatimonadetes, Latescibacteria, and Planctomycetes were negatively correlated (Fig. 6c). Hierarchical partitioning analysis revealed that the independent drivers identified at the 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm depths were consistent with those observed at the 0–20 cm depth, with pH and soil texture components (sand and silt) remaining the dominant factors regulating the bacterial community composition (Fig. 6e).

The RDA showed that the first two ordination axes explained 86.75% of the variation under different land uses (Fig. 6d). The relative abundances of chemoheterotrophy and aerobic chemoheterotrophy showed positive correlations with TN, SOM, and EC, but negative correlations with pH, sand, and N/P ratio. The relative abundances of cellulolysis, nitrification, aerobic nitrite oxidation, and aerobic ammonia oxidation were positively correlated with clay, but negatively correlated with TN, SOM, sand, and EC. The relative abundances of iron respiration, respiration of sulfur compounds, predatory or exoparasitic, and sulfate respiration showed positive correlations with pH, TN, SOM, N/P ratio, and sand, but negative correlations with EC (Fig. 6d). Furthermore, hierarchical partitioning identified soil pH as the overwhelmingly dominant independent driver of bacterial community function at this depth, explaining 40.91% of the total variation (Fig. 6f). In addition, at the 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm depths, VPA showed that the shared contribution of land-use types and soil properties explained 66.3% and 71.8% of the variation, respectively, whereas land use alone explained 11.4% and 6.3%, and soil properties exhibited no independent explanatory power (Fig. 6b). Moreover, the RDA indicated that the first two ordination axes explained 83.61% and 86.30% of the variation in the dominant bacterial functional groups at the 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm soil depths, respectively (Fig. 6d). At the 0–10 cm soil depth, the relative abundances of chemoheterotrophy and aerobic chemoheterotrophy showed negatively correlated with clay and sand. The relative abundances of nitrification and aerobic nitrite oxidation were positively correlated with clay and sand, but negatively correlated with SOM. The relative abundances of respiration of sulfur compounds, predatory or exoparasitic, and sulfate respiration were positively correlated with SOM and sand, but negatively correlated with clay (Fig. 6d). Meanwhile, at 10–20 cm soil depth, the relative abundances of nitrification, aerobic ammonia oxidation, respiration of sulfur compounds, predatory or exoparasitic, and sulfate respiration were positively correlated with N/P ratio, but negatively correlated with C/N ratio, whereas aerobic chemoheterotrophy, cellulolysis, and iron respiration were negatively correlated with N/P ratio but positively correlated with C/N ratio. The relative abundance of chemoheterotrophy was positively correlated with N/P ratioand C/N ratio, whereas aerobic nitrite oxidation was negatively correlated with both (Fig. 6d). Consistent with the 0–20 cm results, hierarchical partitioning confirmed that soil pH remained the dominant independent driver across depths, explaining 24.46% and 35.7% of the functional variation at 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm soil depths, respectively (Fig. 6f).

Regarding topological properties of bacterial network, the RDA1 and RDA2 axes together explained 87.99% of the variation under different land uses (Fig. 7a). The average path length and network diameter were positively correlated with pH, TN, SOM, N/P ratio, sand, and EC, whereas the number of edges, average degree, average clustering coefficient, and network density exhibited negative correlations with pH, TN, SOM, N/P ratio, sand, and EC. The number of nodes was positively associated with pH, TN, SOM, and N/P ratio, but negatively correlated with sand and EC. Modularity showed positive correlations with pH, TN, SOM, N/P ratio, and sand, but negatively correlated with EC (Fig. 7a). Hierarchical partitioning further revealed that soil pH was the dominant independent driver of bacterial network topological properties, explaining 32.63% of the variation in the 0–20 cm soil layer (p < 0.05), followed by EC (18.15%) (Fig. 7b).

Discussion

Effects of land use on soil bacterial diversity

Land-use change-induced alterations in cultivation practices, vegetation cover, and soil properties are the key drivers of microbial diversity38. SF had the lowest Shannon index among the six land-use types (Fig. 2a), likely as a result of long-term continuous cultivation and fertilization, which have been shown to degrade soil health and reduce microbial diversity39. Compared to forestland and grassland, agricultural lands (HF, MF, and PF) exhibited higher bacterial diversity (Fig. 2a), possibly for several reasons. First, croplands generally exhibit greater exposure than forestland and grassland, increasing opportunities for the dispersal of wind- and animal- borne bacteria into agricultural systems40. Second, the tillage practices in PF and MF improved soil permeability, which might benefit the movement and transformation of soil moisture, heat, and nutrients, thereby promoting bacterial survival and proliferation. Moreover, tillage disrupted soil aggregate structures, releasing organic matter that bacteria can utilize, thereby increasing the labile nutrient content, creating additional ecological niches, and stimulating opportunistic bacterial populations41. In contrast, forest soils experienced fewer disturbances, facilitating the development of specialized and well-adapted dominant bacterial populations40. Soil Shannon index in PF and SF was significantly higher in the top 0–10 cm layer than in the 10–20 cm layer (Fig. 2a), which might be partly attributed to surface-applied fertilization practices that increased nutrient availability for surface microbes42. In particular, PF exhibited lower Shannon indices in the 10–20 cm layer, likely due to reduced oxygen availability caused by flooding, which limited nutrient and carbon availability and favored the proliferation of specialized oligotrophic bacterial phyla43. Additionally, long-term return of sugarcane straw to the topsoil was found to increase surface soil bacterial diversity44. These findings indicated that different land-use types associated with different management practices shaped distinct biogeochemical conditions in the top 0–10 cm and lower 10–20 cm layers, ultimately regulating microbial diversity patterns.

Effects of land use on soil microbial community composition and functional potential

The separation observed in the PCoA ordination indicates that land-use type and soil depth exert strong controls over soil bacterial community composition (Fig. 2b). These compositional differences are likely driven by land-use-related variations in cultivation practices (such as tillage and fertilization), vegetation cover, and soil properties. These changes alter microbial habitats and resource availability, thereby shaping the composition of bacterial communities45,46. The VPA results revealed that soil bacterial community composition and functional potentials were predominantly influenced by the shared influence of land-use types and soil properties (Fig. 6a and b), indicating that the influence of land use on soil microbial communities is largely mediated through alterations in soil properties.

At the phylum level, Acidobacteria was particularly prominent under SF (Figs. 3a, S1), which may be associated with the long-term effects of continuous monocropping and fertilization. Such management practices could contribute to decreased soil pH and reduced nutrient availability (Fig. 1), conditions under which acidophilic and oligotrophic Acidobacteria are more likely to be favored47. This explanation was further supported by the RDA, which showed negative correlations between the relative abundance of Acidobacteria and both soil pH and TN (Figs. 1 and 6c). In contrast, PF was characterized by a relatively high soil pH, which may partly explain the lower abundance of Acidobacteria (Figs. 1 and 3a). Similarly, Acidobacteria appeared to be more abundant in FL than in AF, which may also be related to the lower soil pH in FL (Figs. 1 and 3a, S1). In addition, Proteobacteria, Chloroflexi, and Nitrospirae were particularly enriched in PF (Figs. 3a, S1), which may be related to the higher EC and sand content (Fig. 1), as supported by the RDA results (Fig. 6c). Consistent with previous studies48,49, these taxa appear to be well adapted to the anaerobic conditions of long-term flooded paddy soils, and may also be favored in soils with higher salinity and coarser texture. As noted by Xia et al.50, soil texture was the second most significant factor influencing microbial community after soil pH. Together, these observations highlighted the important role of soil physicochemical properties, such as salinity and texture, in shaping microbial community composition. The lowest abundance of Actinobacteria in PF might be attributed to their poor tolerance to anaerobic and saturated conditions (Figs. 3a, S1)51, whereas they are generally considered to have a competitive advantage in nutrient-rich environments52. Accordingly, AF had a significantly higher relative abundance of Actinobacteria compared to HF and MF (Figs. 3a, S1), potentially influenced by higher TN levels (Fig. 1), as suggested by the positive correlations observed in the RDA (Fig. 6a). The elevated TN in AF could be linked to increased inputs of aboveground litterfall and root residues, providing a more favorable nutrient environment for these bacteria53. No statistically significant differences were detected in the relative abundances of key bacterial phyla, including Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Chloroflexi, Oxyphotobacteria, Rokubacteria, Gemmatimonadetes, and Latescibacteria, implying that these two systems may share similar community structures (Fig. S1). The higher relative abundance of Nitrospirae in FL than in AF (Figs. 3a, S1) was likely related to differences in root structure and microbial interactions, as these nitrate-oxidizing autotrophs typically inhabit non-rhizosphere soils54. In grasslands, denser fine roots may promote heterotrophic microbes that outcompete and suppress autotrophs like Nitrospirae55,56, whereas forests with deeper root systems could shift microbial competition to deeper layers, potentially reducing inhibition of Nitrospirae in surface soils. FL and AF exhibited significantly lower relative abundance of Nitrospirae than HF, MF, and PF (Figs. 3a, S1), likely due to reduced nitrogen input and less frequent disturbance under vegetation restoration, whereas fertilized agricultural soils tend to favor their proliferation57. Furthermore, both HF and MF showed a markedly increased abundance of Nitrospirae in the 10–20 cm soil layer compared to the 0–10 cm layer (Figs. 3a, S1), likely due to less competition in deeper soil and their unique ability to oxidize ammonia and nitrite for energy for growth58. Vegetation restoration significantly enhanced the relative abundance of Rokubacteria compared to that in SF (Figs. 3a, S1), which may be due to the elevated soil pH and TN levels that provide more favorable growth conditions for this bacterial phylum59. These results were further confirmed by RDA (Fig. 6c).

Putative functional potential, as predicted by FAPROTAX, suggested that HF exhibited a higher metabolic potential for N cycling, such as nitrification, aerobic nitrite oxidation, and aerobic ammonia oxidation, than the other treatments (Fig. 3b), possibly due to higher clay content (Fig. 1). The large surface area and strong chemical binding capacity of clay can enhance nutrients and moisture retention, thereby creating favorable conditions for the growth and metabolic activities of nitrogen cycling microorganisms60,61. Additionally, higher clay content may contribute to improving soil aggregate stability, thereby promoting the formation of stable microenvironments that protect microorganisms from environmental stress and enhance their metabolic potential in nitrogen cycling62. RDA further supported this finding, revealing a positive correlation between these functional groups and clay content (Fig. 6d). SF significantly enhanced carbon-related functions, including chemoheterotrophy, aerobic chemoheterotrophy, and cellulolysis, compared to other treatments (Fig. 3b), which may be partly explained by straw return practices in the sugarcane field. Straw incorporation introduces additional organic carbon into soil, providing substrates that may stimulate the growth and activity of chemoheterotrophic and aerobic heterotrophic bacteria63. As these exogenous inputs are rich in complex organic materials such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, they may also boost functional groups involved in organic matter decomposition and transformation, especially those related to cellulolysis64. Such organic inputs may also stimulate carbon related enzyme activities, thereby facilitating the degradation of cellulose65. Additionally, the increased relative abundance of Acidobacteria in SF (Figs. 3a, S1), a group widely implicated in soil organic matter decomposition, may have contributed to the observed carbon cycling potential66. PF showed a relatively higher metabolic potential for iron respiration, sulfur compounds respiration, and sulfate respiration (Fig. 3b), which may be partly attributed to the anaerobic conditions in paddy fields. Such conditions may decrease microbial oxidation of substrates and promote accumulation of reduced organic compounds, which may facilitate the proliferation of specific bacteria mediating these processes67,68. In addition, the relatively high soil pH in PF (Fig. 1) may create a favorable ecological niche for Fe-reducing and sulfate-reducing bacteria69. RDA further supported this finding, revealing a positive correlation between these functional groups and pH (Fig. 6d). In contrast, SF had the lowest soil pH (Fig. 1), along with the lowest abundances of respiration of sulfur compounds, predatory or exoparasitic, and sulfate respiration (Fig. 3b), suggesting that acidic conditions might constrain the activity or proliferation of these microbial groups. PF and AF had higher levels of chemoheterotrophy and aerobic chemoheterotrophy in the 0–10 cm soil layer than in the 10–20 cm layer (Fig. 3b), which may have been caused by more active organic matter decomposition in surface soils70. MF and PF showed enrichment of nitrification-related guilds in 10–20 cm soil layer, whereas aerobic nitrite oxidation was more enriched in 0–10 cm soil layer (Fig. 3b), likely due to oxygen gradients and differences in substrate availability between soil layers that influenced soil nitrifier activity.

Although FAPROTAX provides a rapid and promising approach for predicting ecologically relevant functions of bacterial taxa based on 16 S rRNA amplicon data, it has several limitations71. Its predictions are based on known functions in the limited reference database and may not fully capture the functional diversity of uncultured or poorly characterized taxa. Moreover, FAPROTAX infers potential functions rather than measuring actual microbial activity, and cannot account for gene expression or regulation. Therefore, the functional results presented in this study primarily represent the functional potential of soil bacterial communities in current databases, rather than capturing all soil microbial functions or actual microbial activity in situ. Future studies involving updated reference databases and the integration of enzyme activity measurements, metagenomics, and functional gene assays will be helpful for validating these predictions.

Effects of land use on soil microbial co-occurrence networks and keystone taxa

In co-occurrence networks, positive correlations generally indicate cooperation and niche differentiation among microbial communities, whereas negative correlations typically reflect competition due to niche overlap72. At the 0–20 cm soil depth, FL exhibited more positive associations (Fig. 4), suggesting that FL had lower competitive pressure and greater potential for facilitation. This was likely due to higher resource availability, which may alleviate resource limitation and thus weaken competition73. In contrast, the higher number of negative interactions observed in PF compared to other land use types (Fig. 4) may be attributed to the environmental stress induced by the alternating wet–dry conditions74, which likely promote more competition among microbial communities.

HF demonstrated the greatest number of nodes and edges, and exhibited relatively high modularity, network density, and average degree among all land-use types in the 0–20 cm layer (Fig. 4; Table S1), indicating the most complex microbial co-occurrence network. This complexity is likely linked to the optimal soil conditions observed in HF, which are characterized by lower EC (reducing salt stress), neutral pH, and higher clay content (Fig. 1). These conditions may promote microbial survival and interaction by: (1) alleviating competitive pressure through reduced salinity75; (2) broadening ecological niches to enhance network complexity (evidenced by negative EC-node correlations in Fig. 7); and (3) supporting diverse metabolic processes through pH stability76 (positively correlated with modularity, Fig. 7). Furthermore, the clay-rich soil in HF may mitigate environmental fluctuations by retaining water and nutrient77, maintaining microenvironmental stability that enhanced microbial resilience and network modularity. In addition, the enhanced stability of the microbial co-occurrence network in HF was possibly attributed to the environmental stability induced by no-till practices, which helped preserve soil structure and microbial habitats78. In contrast, PF showed the lowest network complexity (Fig. 4; Table S1), which may be attributed to conventional tillage disrupting the complex connections between rhizosphere and bulk soil bacterial communities79, resulting in a less interconnected microbial community (Fig. 4).

In the present study, Acidobacteria, Proteobacteria, Chloroflexi, and Actinobacteria were identified as the most prominent keystone taxa in networks, consistently detected across land-use types and most soil depths, with most links to other microbial taxa, suggesting their central role in shaping microbial communities (Table S2; Figs. 5 and 6). In addition, Acidobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Chloroflexi belonged to the dominant bacterial groups with relatively high abundance, indicating most microbial interactions were mediated by specific bacterial phyla. These keystone taxa have strong adaptability through habitat selection79, and play crucial roles in nutrient cycling80, likely governing ecological functions across land uses and soil layers. Our findings highlighted the dual importance of these taxa as both dominant and keystone species in maintaining soil quality and regulating key ecosystem functions. Therefore, targeting these critical bacterial groups in ecological restoration efforts could therefore strengthen the microbial community stability and enhance the resilience of fragile karst agricultural systems.

Overall, HF, MF, and PF showed the highest soil bacterial diversity, with HF also exhibiting the most stable bacterial co-occurrence network. These results indicate that no-till farming practices promote robust and interconnected microbial communities. Therefore, in ecologically fragile and degraded karst regions, no-till pitaya cultivation may enhance soil fertility and microbial network stability, thereby supporting long-term agricultural ecological sustainability. However, potential long-term limitations should be considered, including the risks associated with monoculture systems, increased reliance on pesticides, and possible adverse impacts on soil health, microbial diversity, and ecosystem resilience.

Conclusions

This study comprehensively assessed the effects of different land-use types, including agricultural systems (pitaya, maize, paddy, and sugarcane fields) and vegetation restoration approaches (forestland and grassland), on soil bacterial diversity, community composition, functional potentials, and co-occurrence networks in shallow karst soils. Our results demonstrated that land-use types strongly influence bacterial community structure, functional potential, and networks, largely mediated through modifications of soil physicochemical properties, particularly pH, soil texture, and nutrient availability. Among the treatments, pitaya fields under no-till management exhibited relatively higher bacterial diversity, more complex and stable co-occurrence networks, and enhanced nitrogen cycling functions, suggesting that conservation-oriented agricultural practices may contribute to microbial stability and ecosystem function. In contrast, sugarcane fields showed lower bacterial diversity and simpler network structures, likely due to continuous cropping, intensive fertilization, and altered soil chemical conditions. Vegetation restoration (forestland and grassland) contributed to the enrichment of specific keystone taxa and network connectors, emphasizing the role of undisturbed or low-intensity management in maintaining microbial functional redundancy and soil health. Overall, these findings indicate that no-till pitaya cultivation and targeted vegetation restoration could potentially contribute to enhanced microbial diversity, functional potential, and network resilience, with implications for sustainable soil management in fragile karst ecosystems. Future studies integrating metagenomic and functional gene analyses, together with long-term monitoring, are necessary to validate these functional predictions and optimize land-use strategies for long-term soil health and ecosystem sustainability.
