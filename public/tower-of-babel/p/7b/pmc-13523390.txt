Long-Term Heart Failure Stabilization and Autonomic Trends After Bilateral Cardiac Sympathetic Denervation
Authors: Yuko Miki, Shingo Yoshimura, Takehito Sasaki, Kenichi Kaseno, Kohki Nakamura, Nogiku Niwamae, Mitsuhiro Kamiyoshihara, Shigeto Naito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBilateral cardiac sympathetic denervation (CSD) is used for refractory ventricular tachycardia (VT); however, its effects on heart failure (HF) progression and left ventricular (LV) remodeling are unclear.Case SummaryA 64-year-old man with ischemic cardiomyopathy was admitted for refractory monomorphic VT after 4 HF-related hospitalizations in the preceding year. Bilateral CSD for refractory VT eliminated HF-related hospitalizations for 32 months and was associated with LV reverse remodeling, with improved ejection fraction (20% → 30%) and reduced LV end-systolic volume index (66 → 50 mL/m2). 123I-metaiodobenzylguanidine scintigraphy demonstrated a reduced myocardial washout rate (62% → 52%). Beginning 28 months post procedure, implantable cardioverter-defibrillator diagnostics revealed a declining atrial pacing burden, suggesting attenuation of the sympatholytic effect and a preceding late VT storm with rapid HF deterioration.DiscussionCSD exerted a time-limited stabilizing effect on HF and VT.Take-Home MessageCSD may suppress HF progression and promote LV reverse remodeling. Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator–derived autonomic trends may indicate waning benefit.

History of Presentation

A 64-year-old man with ischemic cardiomyopathy presented with chest discomfort while gardening. Although he was hemodynamically stable, interrogation of his implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) revealed multiple episodes of monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT) requiring antitachycardia pacing and ICD shocks.

Past Medical History

His medical history included prior inferior myocardial infarction, chronic ischemic cardiomyopathy with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 20%, and 4 hospitalizations for acute decompensated heart failure (HF) in the preceding year. An ICD had been implanted for secondary prevention. Medical therapy included beta blockers, amiodarone, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and diuretics.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnoses included scar-related monomorphic VT, sympathetically triggered VT storm, myocardial ischemia, and failure of antiarrhythmic drug therapy. Given the presence of a chronic inferoposterior scar and recurrent monomorphic VT, scar-related VT with autonomic contribution was considered most likely.

Investigations

Baseline electrocardiography revealed sinus rhythm with inferior Q waves. Clinical VT demonstrated a superior axis and left bundle branch block morphology with a cycle length of 468 ms. Laboratory testing revealed mildly elevated brain natriuretic peptide levels, without electrolyte disturbances. Chest radiography revealed no pulmonary congestion or pleural effusion.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a dilated left ventricle with akinesis of the inferior and lateral walls, and an ejection fraction of 20%. An electrophysiological study revealed noninducibility of clinical VT under deep sedation, induction of 4 nonclinical VTs, extensive low-voltage substrate in the inferior wall and inferoseptum, and abundant fractionated potentials. Despite substrate modification guided by pace mapping and the elimination of local abnormal ventricular activities, VT recurred.

Management

Because VT was refractory to antiarrhythmic therapy and catheter ablation, and was consistently suppressed under sedation, suggesting a strong sympathetic contribution, bilateral cardiac sympathetic denervation (BCSD) was pursued.1,2 Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) was performed under general anesthesia with 1-lung ventilation via two 7-mm ipsilateral subaxillary incisions. After lung deflation, the sympathetic chain was identified, and the T2-4 ganglia were resected while sparing the stellate ganglion (SG). Bipolar electrocautery was applied along the superior border of the second rib to ablate the Kuntz's nerve. The procedure was repeated on the contralateral side (right and left). However, electrical VT storm developed the day after cardiac sympathetic denervation (CSD) and was refractory to intravenous landiolol and amiodarone, requiring deep sedation with propofol and midazolam for suppression. On postoperative day 9, additional bilateral CSD, including resection of the lower half of the SG and T5, was performed, after which the VT storm was suppressed without sedation.

Right ventricular pacing-triggered VT episodes occurred during the early postoperative period and were terminated by antitachycardia pacing. After reprogramming the ICD to AAI mode, no further VT episodes were observed.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was followed with electrocardiograph monitoring, regular ICD interrogations, and remote monitoring. Before CSD, bisoprolol (5 mg) and amiodarone (200 mg) were administered daily. Sotalol (80 mg) was initiated at the time of CSD. At 22 months, antitachycardia pacing for VT occurred, and the sotalol dose was increased to 160 mg. No spontaneous VT or ICD therapy occurred during the first 22 months after BCSD. Following CSD, HF hospitalizations without VT recurrence occurred at 2 and 4 months, with no further HF hospitalizations during the subsequent 32 months. B-type natriuretic peptide levels, which had previously fluctuated between 40 and 500 pg/mL, stabilized to 30 to 300 pg/mL during this 32-month period. The NYHA functional class improved from III to I.

ICD diagnostics demonstrated a stable atrial pacing burden during the initial 28 months after the CSD (Figure 1). Beginning at 28 months, a progressive decline in atrial pacing percentage was observed, accompanied by a gradual increase in the intrinsic sinus rate. Discussion

BCSD achieved sustained VT suppression and HF stabilization, followed by late recurrence associated with apparent attenuation of its sympatholytic effect. Portions of the antiarrhythmic effect of BCSD in this patient have been previously reported as part of a single-center case series (primarily focusing on arrhythmia suppression), with limited longitudinal follow-up.2 In contrast, this case integrates myocardial sympathetic imaging and longitudinal ICD-derived autonomic trends, allowing assessment of both the establishment and attenuation of the sympatholytic effect of BCSD, together with reverse ventricular remodeling and the temporal relationship to late VT recurrence.

Conventional CSD includes resection of the lower half of the SG with the T2-T4 ganglia. Given the risk of Horner syndrome and injury to adjacent structures (brachial plexus, subclavian vessels) with SG resection, and the technical difficulty and limited routine use of SG intervention in VATS, SG-sparing BCSD (T2-4) via VATS was initially selected to minimize invasiveness in this case. However, persistence of electrical VT storm requiring deep sedation for suppression suggests insufficient sympathetic denervation. A recent meta-analysis showed that CSD is well tolerated, with no procedure-related mortality, with VT freedom achieved in approximately 60% of patients at a mean follow-up of 15.0 ± 10.7 months. However, outcomes varied across studies, likely due to differences in denervation extent and technique, with a substantial proportion of nonresponders.3 Prior studies indicate that BCSD efficacy depends on the completeness of denervation, particularly SG involvement and laterality. In SG-sparing BCSD, VT freedom is reported in only 40% at a median follow-up of 15 months.4 Inclusion of the SG may improve arrhythmia suppression via more complete denervation. Consistently, in this case, additional VATS-based BCSD including resection of the lower half of the SG and T5 achieved effective suppression, likely reflecting more extensive denervation. In addition to procedural factors, anatomical variation may contribute to incomplete denervation. The Kuntz nerve represents an accessory sympathetic pathway, typically arising from the second thoracic nerve and connecting to the first thoracic nerve, the first intercostal nerve, or the ramus communicans between the SG and the T1 nerve; if not identified or ablated, it can provide residual sympathetic input.5,6 Accordingly, cauterization of the Kuntz nerve during BCSD has been associated with improved efficacy. In our practice, bipolar electrocautery is applied circumferentially along the superior border of the second rib to ensure adequate ablation along its presumed course, irrespective of visualization. Residual or recurrent ventricular arrhythmias after CSD may partly reflect Kuntz nerve–related incomplete denervation.

Current guidelines and expert consensus statements describe CSD as a rescue or adjunctive therapy for drug- and ablation-refractory VT, particularly in the setting of an electrical storm, and generally position BCSD as a Class IIb recommendation based on limited evidence.7,8 In contrast, the effects of CSD on HF progression and ventricular remodeling remain poorly characterized, and guideline recommendations are lacking.9,10 This case demonstrates that BCSD provides potent but time-limited stabilization of both refractory monomorphic VT and HF progression in advanced ischemic cardiomyopathy. Notably, early HF hospitalizations at 2 and 4 months after CSD may reflect residual sympathetic activity due to neuraxial activation and delayed depletion of distal neurotransmitter stores.3 The patient experienced nearly 3 years of arrhythmia suppression and more than 2 years free from HF-related hospitalization, accompanied by reverse ventricular remodeling and favorable sympathetic imaging findings. Detailed longitudinal autonomic and device-based data further illustrated the temporal attenuation of the sympatholytic effect preceding late VT recurrence. A notable observation was the decline in the atrial pacing burden beginning approximately 28 months after CSD, suggesting progressive sympathetic reactivation or partial functional reinnervation. This autonomic shift preceded clinical VT recurrence, highlighting the potential value of ICD-derived autonomic markers as early indicators of waning efficacy of CSD.

Although CSD is often described as an “ultimate beta-blocker,” its long-term durability varies. In patients with advanced structural heart disease, the suppression of ventricular arrhythmias does not necessarily translate into sustained HF stabilization, underscoring the importance of longitudinal surveillance and timely consideration of advanced HF therapies.

Conclusions

In this patient with advanced ischemic cardiomyopathy, BCSD achieved durable suppression of spontaneous VT and prolonged stabilization of HF.
