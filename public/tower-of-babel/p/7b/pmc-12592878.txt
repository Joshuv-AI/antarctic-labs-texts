Development and Characterization of an Oral Mucoadhesive Patch Containing Amla (Phyllanthus emblica) Bark and Fruit Extracts to Promote Healing of Oral Ulcers: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Lalitha S Jairam, R Deveswaran, Faizan Ali Khan, Eshwari D Naik, Fathima Tanzeem, S Dineshkumar, Hiba Haleema
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral ulcers are a common problem across most age-groups, having limited treatment modalities such as gels, solutions, and creams. Mucoadhesive patches containing chemical agents have been studied. However, there is scanty literature on the incorporation of a natural agent into the drug delivery system for the treatment of oral ulcers. Amla (Phyllanthus emblica), a medicinal plant well known for its healing properties, shows immense potential for the treatment of oral ulcers.The aim of this study is to develop and characterize a mucoadhesive patch containing Amla bark and fruit extracts and assess its physicochemical properties and wound healing efficacy. The phytochemical analysis revealed the presence of phytocompounds having antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiulcerogenic, and various other medicinal properties. The mucoadhesive patches were formulated using the solvent casting method. The patches were then subjected to in vitro tests such as folding endurance, swelling index (SI), mucoadhesive strength, tensile strength, moisture absorption (MA), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and revealed optimal results. Assessment of the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay of the extracts revealed that 25 µg/mL was the maximum safest therapeutic dose for human oral fibroblasts. A wound healing assay revealed that in a 12-hour period, the ethanolic extracts of P. emblica bark and fruit at 10 µg/mL concentration demonstrated an enhanced wound closure rate with values of 50.12% and 67.44%, respectively. Both extracts exhibited 100% wound healing after 24 hours.This study supports the ethnopharmacological relevance of P. emblica in oral ulcer treatment, highlighting its potential as a wound healing agent. The findings justify further development and clinical evaluation of P. emblica mucoadhesive patches for the treatment of oral ulcers.How to cite this articleJairam LS, Deveswaran R, Khan FA, et al. Introduction

Oral ulcers or recurrent aphthous stomatitis is one of the most common oral diseases affecting about 25% of the population.1 The development of painful, recurrent, solitary, or numerous shallow ulcerations on the oral mucosa is the hallmark of this condition. Patients may also experience problems with chewing and brushing, irritation to salty, spicy foods, and a loss of appetite. The etiology of oral ulcers still remains elusive. However, it has been postulated that certain systemic diseases, hormonal changes, trauma, medications, food hypersensitivity, tobacco, and stress could be the etiological factors.2

Currently, anesthetic gels, creams, oxygenating agents, dissolvable and nondissolvable patches, film-forming gels, and mouth rinses are the available treatment modalities for treatment of this condition, but each of these is associated with specific constraints and shortcomings. Mouth rinses and topical anesthetic gels mainly focus on pain relief without significant improvement in wound healing. Creams and gels tend to get diluted due to saliva, and thus, there is a reduced efficacy.3 This leaves the lesion exposed, rendering it vulnerable to exacerbation from trauma caused by food particles, brushing, or just mere contact with teeth. This led to the development of mucoadhesive delivery systems, including particulates,4 tablets,5 films, and patches.6–8 Various mucoadhesive patches containing steroidal drugs have also been developed for the management of oral ulcers.9 Mucoadhesive patches are better tolerated by patients and have greater efficacy than oral solutions. The advantages are a longer residence time, localized drug delivery, and consistent drug release, ensuring an enhanced therapeutic effect.10 However, majority of the medicated patches containing synthetic drugs such as dexamethasone and ornidazole are often unpalatable and may exhibit cross reactions with other medications,11 stimulating an allergic reaction.12 Thus, there is a quest for an alternative natural derivative. The advantages of herbal pharmaceuticals over synthetic drugs include a reduction in side effects, cost-effectiveness, patient compatibility, and its ease of availability.13

Phyllanthus emblica is a well-known medicinal plant with an array of applications in the medical field. It owes its nutritious qualities to its high tannin, vitamin C, and vitamin E content.14 It is also well known for its high levels of polyphenols, flavones, and other bioactive substances, which exhibit regenerative, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, analgesic, anticancer, antioxidant, hypolipidemic, and hypoglycemic effects, among others, which have been exhibited in vitro and animal studies.15,16 The properties of the fruit of P. emblica have been extensively studied, and its beneficial effects are proven.17 It has been found that a 50% methanolic extract of P. emblica fruit has a considerable wound closure rate of 62% when compared to natural healing, exhibiting a higher efficacy than the widely employed Betadine.18 To date, there is no natural agent incorporated into a mucoadhesive patch for healing of oral ulcers. Hence, this gave an impetus to incorporate P. emblica into a mucoadhesive patch for promoting wound healing. The aim of the study was to develop and characterize a mucoadhesive patch containing P. emblica bark and fruit extracts and assess its physicochemical and wound healing properties.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Amla (P. emblica) bark and fruit samples were sourced locally from Kaiwara (Chikkaballapur, Karnataka). Distilled water, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, ethanol, 70% Sorbitol, diethyl phthalate, propylene glycol, and aluminum foil. All the chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification.

The wound healing assay was performed at CellKraft Biotech Pvt Ltd. (Bengaluru, Karnataka), using mouse fibroblast cell lines L929 sourced from the National Center for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune.

Formulation and Phytochemical Analysis of P. emblica Bark Extract (PBE) and P. emblica Fruit Extract (PFE)

Preparation of the Extract

Fresh bark and fruit were collected from a P. emblica tree, and these samples were authenticated at the Foundation for Revitalization of Local Health Traditions (FRLHT), with Authentication no. FRLHT Acc. No. 5817. The bark and fruit were cleaned, cut into small pieces, and dried. A homogenized powder of bark and fruit was obtained after grinding them separately in a sterilized mixer. The homogenized bark and fruit powders, 5 gm each, were weighed separately and dissolved in 25 mL of ethanol in separate beakers. The beakers were covered with aluminum foil and kept for soaking overnight. The solvents were filtered to get filtrates. Both the filtrates were dried using an electric water bath at 50°C. The residues that were obtained from the dried filtrates of bark and fruit were powdered and collected as bark and fruit extracts, respectively.

Phytochemical Analysis

The phytochemical analysis of the ethanolic extracts of P. emblica bark and P. emblica fruit was carried out at The South India Textile Research Association (SITRA) Chemical Laboratory, Coimbatore, using a gas chromatograph mass spectrometer–triple quad (GC-MS/MS) with Purge & Trap (VOC Analyzer).

Preparation of P. emblica Bark Extract (PBE) and P. emblica Fruit Extract (PFE) Patches and Their Characterization

Preparation of PBE and PFE Buccal Patches

Synthesis of the P. emblica bark patch was carried out by taking 800 mg of Hydroxypropyl Methylcellulose (HPMC) in a beaker and soaking it in 5 mL of ethanol. In a separate beaker, 100 mg of P. emblica bark extract was dissolved in 5 mL of Ethanol along with 1 mL of 70% Sorbitol solution and 1 mL of Diethyl phthalate, after which this solution was added to the HPMC solution and combined.

The combined solution obtained was poured into a Petri dish lined by an aluminum foil and covered by an inverted funnel. This was left to air dry for 3 days.

Similarly, fabrication of the P. emblica fruit patch was carried out by taking 1200 mg of HPMC in a beaker and soaking it in 10 mL of ethanol. In a separate beaker, 200 mg of P. emblica fruit extract was dissolved in 3 mL of propylene glycol, and this was added to the HPMC solution and combined. The final solution obtained was poured into a Petri dish lined by aluminum foil and covered by an inverted funnel. This was left to air dry for 7 days. Write how the patches were cut into uniform sizes and used for further testing.

Characterization of Developed PBE and PFE Buccal Patches

The bark and fruit patches that were obtained were assessed for the thickness, weight, surface pH, folding endurance, mucoadhesive strength, and tensile strength. The surface morphology and active functional groups were assessed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Zeiss, Germany) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis, respectively.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

The surface morphology and pore size of the patches were assessed using an SEM (Zeiss, Germany). SEM was used to investigate the surface morphology of both bark and fruit patches using SEM (Zeiss, Germany).

Thickness and Weight Uniformity

The thickness of the bark and fruit patch 2 × 2 cm2 was measured separately at four different points using a standard screw gauge. For weight uniformity, a square of length 2 cm was cut from a bark and a fruit patch, and they were weighed individually on an electronic weighing machine.

Surface pH

A 1 × 1 cm2 was obtained from both the bark and fruit patches and kept in separate Petri dishes, each containing 1 mL of distilled water. After 30 minutes, the surface pH of each of the square patches was measured using an electronic pH meter.

Folding Endurance

Folding endurance of both bark and fruit patches was determined by manual folding. Each patch was folded at the same place till it broke. The value of folding endurance was recorded by the number of times that the patch was folded without breaking at the same place and substituted in the formula to obtain folding endurance, where d is the number of double folds. F = log10d

Swelling Index (SI)

A 2 × 2 cm2 sample was cut from the patch and weighed on the glass slide (W1). The slide was then kept in a Petri dish with 50 mL of distilled water in it. Now, the weight of this glass slide was noted at 15-minute intervals for 2 hours and marked as (W2) for respective time intervals, and the results were recorded. The acquired results were substituted in the following formula to obtain the SI: SI=W2-W1W1×10

Mucoadhesive Strength

The P. emblica fruit, P. emblica bark, and control patches were cut into 1 × 1 cm samples each. The control patches were plain HPMC patches. Ten samples each for bark, fruit, and control patches were tested for mucoadhesive strength using a Universal Testing Machine, INSTRON E3000, using the method described by Patel and Poddar.19 The prepared samples were attached to the clamps of the testing machine, and a preload of 0.5 N was applied, and maximum force of detachment was recorded in Newton.

Tensile Strength

The P. emblica bark and fruit patches were cut into 1 × 1 cm samples. Ten samples each of bark and fruit patches were tested for tensile strength using Universal Testing Machine, INSTRON E3000, using the method described by Patel and Poddar.19 The patches (1 × 1 cm2) were mounted between the upper and lower clamps. The upper clamp was moved upward with speeds 1 or 2 mm/s until the patch broke. The tensile strength of the patches was directly recorded from the display of the Testing Machine.

Moisture Absorption (MA)

A 1 × 1 cm2 patch was cut from the sample and weighed (w1). This patch was left undisturbed in a Petri plate for 3 days, following which it was weighed again (w2). The acquired values were substituted in the formula as follows: MA=W2-W1W1×100

Fourier-transform Infrared Spectroscopy

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy analysis was performed on the formulated P. emblica patches and powders, respectively, to identify their functional groups. The spectrum was recorded over a wavenumber range of 500 to 3500 cm−1 to reveal characteristic absorption bands corresponding to various molecular vibrations.

Antimicrobial Test Using Disc Diffusion Study

The microorganisms used for antimicrobial analysis were purchased from Microbial Type Culture Collection and Gene Bank, Chandigarh. The bacterial strains were maintained on nutrient agar. pure cultures of anaerobic bacteria from the plate were inoculated into a nutrient agar plate and subcultured at 37°C for 24 hours. Inoculum was prepared by aseptically adding the fresh culture into 2 mL of sterile 0.145 mol/L saline tube, and the cell density was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard to yield a bacterial suspension of 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL. Standardized inoculum was used for the antimicrobial test. The medium was prepared by dissolving 38 gm of Muller Hinton Agar Medium (Hi Media) in 1000 mL of distilled water. The dissolved medium was autoclaved at 15 lbs pressure at 121°C for 15 min at a pH of 7.3. The autoclaved medium was cooled, mixed well, and poured into Petri plates (25 mL/plate). The plates were swabbed with pathogenic bacteria and incubated for 24 hours. Four wells of 6 mm were bored in the inoculated media with the help of a sterile cork-borer (6 mm). About 1 mg of the specimen was weighed and dissolved in 1 mL of Milli-Q water to achieve the 1 mg/mL concentration. The sample-loaded discs with 1 mg/mL were then placed on the surface of Muller–Hinton medium and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. At the end of the incubation, inhibition zones were examined around the disc and measured with a transparent ruler in millimeters. The size of the zone of inhibition (ZOI) was measured in millimeters. 10 µg/mL of Norfloxacin was used as a standard control against the Streptococcus mutans.

3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide (MTT) Assay

The HOrF (human oral fibroblasts) was purchased from ScienCell Research Labs, California, United States. The HOrF cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium with high glucose supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) along with the 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution in the atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 18–20% O2 at 37°C temperature in the CO2 incubator and subcultured every 2–3 days. Passage number 12 was used for the present study. A medium control (medium without cells), a negative control (medium with cells but without the experimental drug/compound), and a positive control (medium with cells treated with 20 µM/mL of Camptothecin) were used in the assay.

A 200 μL cell suspension was seeded in a 96-well plate at the required cell density (20,000 cells per well) without the test agent and allowed to grow for 24 hours. Appropriate concentrations of the test agent were added and incubated for 24 hours at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. After the incubation period, the spent media was removed and the MTT reagent was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL of total volume. The plate was wrapped with aluminum foil to avoid exposure to light and returned to the incubator for 3 hours. Following this, the MTT reagent was removed, and 100 μL of Dimethyl Sulfoxide solubilization solution was added. Gentle stirring was carried out in a gyratory shaker to enhance dissolution. The absorbance was read on a spectrophotometer or an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader at 570 nm wavelength, and the percentage of cell viability was calculated using the formula given below: %cell viability = Mean abs of treated cellsMean abs of untreated cells×100

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value, which is the concentration of a drug that causes 50% cell viability, was determined by using a linear regression equation: Y=Mx+C

Here, Y = 50, M, and C values were derived from the viability graph.

In vitro Testing of Patches Using a Wound Healing Assay

The ethanol extracts of P. emblica fruit and P. emblica bark incorporated in the patches were assessed at CellKraft Biotech Pvt Ltd., Bangalore, for the wound healing assay. Mouse fibroblast cell line L929 was obtained from the National Center for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune, and cell culture media—Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium with high glucose supplemented with 10% FBS was obtained.

Cell lines were cultured in 6-well plates to form a monolayer. As the cells reached around 70% confluence, a scratch was made to form a wound. The monolayer was washed with 1 mL of Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline—HiMedia—twice. About 2 mL of the medium was added to each well plate; two wells containing 10 µg/mL of PFE, two wells containing 10 µg/mL of PBE, and two wells kept as controls without any drug additives. All the plates were incubated for 24 hours. Images of the cell growth were taken at regular time intervals (0 hour, 12 hours, and 24 hours) using an inverted phase contrast microscope. The percentage of cell migration/wound healing was calculated by comparing the final gap (12 hours or 24 hours) area to the initial gap area (0 hour).

Discussion

Various studies have been undertaken to demonstrate P. emblica's wound healing abilities. A study has demonstrated that P. emblica improves immunity to illnesses and has positive effects on memory, diabetes, and helps in the prevention of ulcers.20 In another study done by Singh et al., P. emblica is a commonly used medicinal plant in Ayurveda containing components such as ascorbic acid, gallic acid, and ellagic acid, which account for its antimicrobial and antifungal properties that help in wound healing as well as protection from infections.21 Another research demonstrated that phenolic extracts of P. emblica showed higher levels of antioxidant activity, in which gallic acid and tannic acid were identified as the major antioxidant components contributing to wound healing.22

The APT Research Foundation, a Subsidiary of the National Toxicology Center on rats, stated that the bark extract of P. emblica may have therapeutic effects for wound healing, especially in dermatological cuts, wounds, and burns.23

A review done on mucoadhesive drug delivery systems concluded that topical and local systems based on mucoadhesives have demonstrated improved and quick absorption, which was attributed to their large surface area. Drug distribution over the mucosa avoids first-pass hepatic metabolism and gastrointestinal enzyme degradation and therefore shows good bioavailability.24 Fibroblast and procollagen proliferation were enhanced by P. emblica extract in a concentration- and time-dependent manner.25P. emblica provided a practical and effective way to enhance cutaneous wound healing by increasing cellular proliferation, extracellular signal-regulated kinases, collagen cross-linking, and antioxidant activity at the wound site.26 In comparison to recognized herbal collagen boosters, emblica extract induced 7.75 times higher type I procollagen.15

In the present study, GC-MS analysis revealed the presence of phytocompounds that have been documented to have antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiulcerogenic, and other medicinal properties.27 Both extracts showed the presence of phenolic anylates (ellagic acid, gallic acids, and corilagin), ascorbic acid, and tannins. Some of the important bioactive compounds with peak incidence in the extracts such as benzoic acid, 3,4,5-trihydroxy; 1,2,3-benzenetriol; myristamide; and N-ethyl-5-alpha-aminoethyl tetrazole. This was similar to the findings of Balasubramanian et al.28 The thickness of the bark and fruit patches measured at multiple locations revealed minimum variations and were similar to previously fabricated buccal patches.29,30 To ascertain in vivo compatibility, a further assessment of the weight of the patches is required.

The surface pH of P. emblica fruit patch was neutral,7 and may not cause any irritation to the oral mucosa. The surface pH of P. emblica bark patch was alkaline10 which might cause irritation to the oral mucosa.31 Folding endurance test demonstrated good durability and resistance to breakage in both the bark and fruit patches. This indicates that the patches show good flexibility and can withstand mechanical load. The SI of both bark and fruit patches showed a significant increase in the first 15 minutes, followed by a gradual increase for both up to 120 minutes. The MA of the patches is vital as it determines the amount of water uptake from the oral cavity and hence the amount of diffusion of the drug. The MA of the bark and fruit patches being 50% and 29.4%, respectively, is found to be satisfactory.31

The P. emblica fruit patches could withstand a maximum force of 5.85 MPa and demonstrated higher mucoadhesive strength, which was less than that of the control, as depicted in Table 1. This was an expected result as the incorporation of an external drug will reduce the strength of the prepared patches. The P. emblica bark patches showed the least value of 1.62 MPa.

The P. emblica fruit patch showed a lower tensile strength of 7.03 N before detachment, as seen in Figure 1A, whereas the P. emblica Bark patches exhibited excellent resistance to tensile test, demonstrating 13.8 N of force at peak tension, as depicted in Figure 1B. This finding can be attributed to the anatomical location of bark in the P. emblica tree, which shows inherent strength compared to other parts of the plant.23

The SEM analysis shows that the P. emblica fruit patch is more homogenously distributed and shows less porosities when compared to the bark patch, as seen in Figures 2 and 3. This may be accredited to the inherit nature of the bark itself, which is more crystalline. The P. emblica fruit patches appeared smoother when compared to the bark patch and hence may be more suitable for oral use. The nonhomogeneous nature of the bark patch may potentially harbor debris and microorganisms owing to the surface roughness. The patches had pore size ranging from 45 to 55 μm and 8 to 25 μm for bark and fruit patches, respectively. This was within the acceptable range for the diffusion of vital components from P. emblica for functional activity.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy analysis of P. emblica bark and fruit powders revealed presence of hydroxyl groups, alkyl groups, carbonyl groups, aromatic rings, alcohols, ethers, and esters, indicating the presence of complex organic molecules, which are found in plants. These findings are consistent with the known phytochemical constituents of P. emblica fruit, such as phenolic compounds, tannins, and organic acids, which highlight its therapeutic efficacy, particularly in applications aimed at healing and protecting oral health. FTIR analysis of the fabricated polymeric patches shows the presence of broad O–H stretching bands, indicating significant hydroxyl group content from the phenolic compounds in the P. emblica extracts. The C–H stretching vibrations confirm the structural integrity of the polymer, while the distinct C=O stretching band points to interactions between the polymer and the extract. Additionally, the aromatic C=C stretching vibrations and C–O stretching peaks further substantiate the presence of P. emblica's complex organic molecules within the patch. On comparing the FTIR spectra of the P. emblica powders and patches, consistent structural compositions were noted, suggesting that the chemical structures of both P. emblica bark and fruit remain intact when incorporated into the polymeric patch. The similarity in FTIR results validates the effectiveness of the formulation process in maintaining the integrity of P. emblica bioactive compounds, therefore making it suitable for the intended use in healing oral ulcers.

The P. emblica bark and fruit extracts showed an average ZOI of 14 mm and 10 mm, respectively, while the standard positive control, 10 µg/mL Norfloxacin showed a 25 mm ZOI. Hence, we can conclude that both extracts showed potential antimicrobial properties against the bacterial strain tested, S. mutans, similar to the standard control used for the study.

The MTT assay results suggest that the given test compounds, P. emblica bark and Amla fruit extracts, were nontoxic till the maximum concentration of 25 µg/mL, with greater than 80% cell viability after the incubation period of 24 hours. In summary, both P. emblica bark and P. emblica fruit extracts proved to be safe against the human oral fibroblasts till the maximum dose of 25 µg/mL, with greater than 80% cell viability after 24 hours of incubation. In summary, it can be confirmed that both the fruit and bark were safe to use on nonmalignant human oral cells till the maximum dose of 25 µg/mL without any adverse or side effects.

The wound healing assay demonstrated desirable healing abilities, showing 67.44% wound approximation for the fruit extract and 50.12% wound approximation for the bark extract, which was significantly better when compared to the controls, which showed <50% wound closure as depicted in Figure 8. In this aspect, the fruit patches demonstrated excellent wound healing ability, while the bark patches showed acceptable results. At 50 μg/mL concentration of both extracts, the cell growth was inhibited. This difference may be attributed to the fact that the P. emblica extract is inducing an effect on the fibroblast growth in a dose-dependent relationship, due to which, at concentrations of 50 μg/mL, cell growth was inhibited.32 At 24 hours, all the samples showed 100% wound closure. Further evaluation is required to assess the concentrations of the P. emblica extracts at which maximum growth is possible.33

Conclusion

This study underscores the potential of P. emblica bark and fruit extracts in wound healing and drug delivery in the form of mucoadhesive patches. Our research delved into the composition and characteristics of P. emblica extracts. Both P. emblica bark and fruit extracts contained abundant beneficial phytocompounds, further reinforcing their therapeutic potential.

From the results, we can conclude that P. emblica fruit extract will be more effective in healing wounds than P. emblica bark extract, as it showed faster wound closure rates in the wound healing assay (mention the percentage). In vitro testing of various physical and mechanical properties revealed that the P. emblica fruit patch has exceptional tensile strength, surface pH, and folding endurance, while the bark patches also showed acceptable results.

This study on P. emblica-based wound care and drug delivery formulations offers potential for more effective and patient-friendly treatments. Further research is needed to optimize concentration levels and assess the in vivo applicability of the patches.
