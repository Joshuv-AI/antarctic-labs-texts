Extreme heat triggers first-time acute myocardial infarction: Evidence from a case-crossover study in Tianjin, China
Authors: Zinuo Wang, Rui Hu, Jinglin Li, Jingting Chen, Yifan Liu, Hongyue Sun, Yishu Yang, Yiqing Wang, Suqin Han, Yuming Guo, Congchao Lu, Shilu Tong, Nai-jun Tang, Xi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess whether extreme high temperatures (EHT) and heat waves (HW) are associated with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) risk, and to determine whether these effects differ between first-time and recurrent events.MethodsWe performed a time-stratified case-crossover study of 7573 AMI admissions in Tianjin, China (2021–2023). Meteorological exposures were derived from ground-based monitoring and satellite products. Conditional logistic regression with distributed lag nonlinear models quantified immediate and delayed associations between temperature and AMI, stratified by event type (first-time vs recurrent). Effect modification was examined by age, sex, and ST-segment elevation.ResultsBoth EHT and HW were associated with increased AMI risk, with effects concentrated among first-time events. The strongest single-day associations for first-time AMI were observed 4–5 days after exposure (peaking at lag 4 for EHT_90TH: OR = 1.1594; 95 % CI: 1.0622–1.2655), while cumulative risks peaked across lag windows of 4–6 days (peaking at lag 0–6 for EHT_90TH: OR = 1.1564; 95 % CI: 1.0558–1.2665). Among first-time AMI, higher risks were seen in men, individuals <65 years, and those with non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.ConclusionsShort-term exposure to extreme heat significantly increases the risk of first-time AMI, but not recurrent events. Risk persists for several days after exposure, particularly among younger adults, men, and NSTEMI patients. These findings highlight the need for targeted heat-health messaging, early recognition of symptoms, and preventive strategies tailored to individuals at risk of a first AMI in the era of climate change.Keywords: Acute myocardial infarction, Extreme high temperatures, Heat waves, First-time events, Preventive cardiology

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of death worldwide, with ischemic heart disease (IHD) accounting for a substantial and growing share of premature mortality [1]. Acute myocardial infarction (AMI), a severe manifestation of IHD, continues to impose a major clinical and public health burden despite advances in prevention and care [[2], [3], [4]]. The global incidence of AMI remains elevated, with the United States reporting an overall incidence rate of 3 %. A similar trend has been observed in China, where both the incidence and mortality rates of AMI are on the rise [[5], [6], [7]]. In China and globally, demographic shifts and changing risk profiles underscore the urgency of refining strategies for AMI prevention and early warning.

Concurrently, climate change is intensifying the frequency, duration, and severity of extreme heat. Recent assessments indicate record-high global temperatures and a rapid increase in population-level exposure to heat [[8], [9], [10]]. Extreme high temperatures (EHT) and heat waves (HW) can exacerbate cardiovascular stress via thermoregulatory strain, hemoconcentration, endothelial dysfunction, and pro-arrhythmic and pro-thrombotic pathways, potentially precipitating acute coronary events [11,12]. Epidemiologic studies have linked non-optimal temperatures—particularly heat—to excess cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [13,14]. It is noteworthy that extreme heat not only produces acute, immediate effects but also exhibits delayed effects [13,15]. Nevertheless, most previous studies have emphasized the impact of extreme temperatures on mortality rather than morbidity. The few studies that have used morbidity as an outcome have failed to distinguish between first-time and recurrent AMI, leaving certain research gaps and simultaneously limiting insights into the timing and dynamics of heat-triggered AMI onset.

To address these gaps, we investigated the association between ambient heat and AMI in Tianjin, China, from 2021 to 2023 using a time-stratified case-crossover design and distributed lag nonlinear models. We evaluated immediate and lagged effects of EHT and HW on AMI, focusing on first-time events, and assessed effect modification by age, sex, and ST-segment elevation. Our objective was to generate evidence directly relevant to preventive cardiology—informing heat-health alerts, clinical counseling, and resource planning during periods of sustained high temperature.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a time-stratified case-crossover study to evaluate short-term associations of ambient heat with AMI in Tianjin, China. Patients admitted to a tertiary cardiovascular hospital from January 1, 2021 through December 31, 2023 with a physician-confirmed AMI diagnosis (ICD-10 I21) were eligible. Standardized clinical evaluation included electrocardiography and cardiac biomarkers. AMI events were classified as ST-segment elevation (STEMI) or non-ST-segment elevation (NSTEMI). After excluding unclassifiable events, 7573 AMI admissions were included. Demographics (age, sex) and admission dates were abstracted from the electronic medical record. The study protocol was approved by the Tianjin TEDA International Cardiovascular Hospital Medical Ethics Committee (2022–0714–5); written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Exposure assessment

Hourly meteorological fields were obtained from ERA5 reanalysis (10×10 km, 1 h resolution) and aggregated to daily metrics to align with date-stamped admissions [16]. Relative humidity and heat index were calculated, with detailed methods provided in the supplementary methods. Air pollution was sourced from the National Urban Air Quality Real-Time Distribution Platform (China Environmental Monitoring General Station). Daily mean concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, and CO were computed across city monitoring stations; ozone (O3) was summarized as the daily 8-h average.

Definitions of extreme heat and heat waves

We defined season-specific relative temperature thresholds from Tianjin’s daily mean temperature distribution [17]. EHT days were those with daily mean temperature ≥ the 90th (P90) or 95th (P95) seasonal percentile [18]. HW combined threshold and duration: days with mean temperature ≥ P90 or ≥ P95 sustained for ≥ 2, ≥ 3, or ≥ 4 consecutive days, yielding six HW definitions [19].

Case-crossover sampling

For each admission (“case day”), control days were selected as the same day of week in the same month and year (3–4 control days per case), ensuring control for seasonality, long-term trend, and day-of-week effects while holding time-invariant individual factors constant [20,21]. This yielded 7573 case days and 25,671 control days.

Statistical analysis

We summarized meteorological and air-pollution variables using the mean, SD, range, and interquartile range. Primary associations were estimated with conditional logistic regression, expressing effects as odds ratios (ORs) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). To capture nonlinear temperature–response and delayed effects, we embedded a distributed lag nonlinear model (DLNM) cross-basis for daily mean temperature within the conditional logistic framework. The exposure–response was modeled with a natural cubic spline (internal knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of the temperature distribution); the lag–response used a natural cubic spline (three internal knots equally spaced on the log scale), with a maximum lag of 7 days. We estimated single-day lags (lag0–lag7) and cumulative lags (lag0–1 through lag0–7).

EHT and HW were analyzed as categorical exposures to estimate event-time risk relative to non-EHT/non-HW days. We evaluated total AMI and, a priori, first-time vs recurrent AMI. Subgroup analyses were prespecified by sex and age (< 65 vs ≥ 65 years), and subtype analyses by STEMI vs NSTEMI among first-time AMI.

To address multipollutant confounding, we derived a composite air-pollution index via principal component analysis (PCA) on standardized PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, O3, and CO; components with eigenvalues >1 and cumulative variance >80 % were retained and entered as covariates.

Sensitivity analyses included: (1) additional adjustment for the PCA-derived air-pollution components; (2) alternative EHT (≥ P97.5) and HW definitions (≥ P97.5 for ≥ 2/ ≥ 3/ ≥ 4 days); and (3) evaluation of robustness across single-day and cumulative lag structures.

Post hoc power calculations, based on the observed sample and two-sided α = 0.05, indicated ≥ 80 % power to detect effect sizes of interest for first-time AMI and sufficient power for nonlinear temperature effects. Analyses used R 4.2.3; two-sided p < 0.05 denoted statistical significance.

Primary associations

Three-dimensional exposure–response relationship indicates that AMI risk varies across different lag days at high temperatures, with an increased AMI risk incidence for both total AMI and first-time AMI to varying degrees (Fig. 2). In preliminary models, higher daily maximum/mean temperature, skin temperature, and heat index were positively associated with total and first-time AMI, whereas total precipitation showed inverse associations (Table S3).

Subsequently, we categorized temperature into binary variables based on EHT and HW definitions for further analysis. In EHT analyses, first-time AMI risk rose significantly, peaking at lag4 for both EHT_90TH (OR = 1.1594; 95 % CI: 1.0622–1.2655) and EHT_95TH (OR = 1.1713; 95 % CI: 1.0464–1.3110). Across six HW definitions, risk elevations were observed for total and first-time AMI at single-day lags (peaking at lag4–lag5) and cumulative windows (lag0–4 or lag0–5). Specifically, first-time AMI risk for HW_90TH_2D peaked at lag 4 (OR = 1.1558; 95 % CI: 1.0546–1.2667). No significant associations were found for recurrent AMI. No material associations were detected for recurrent AMI (Fig. 3; Fig. S3).

Subtype and subgroup findings

Among first-time AMI, NSTEMI showed greater heat sensitivity than STEMI. Under EHT_90TH, NSTEMI risk was elevated at lag2 (OR = 1.1690) and across lag0–7 (OR = 1.1742), with additional increases observed under multiple HW definitions at short lags (lag1–lag2) and cumulative windows (lag0–1 to lag0–5) (Fig. 4; Tables S4–S6).

Odds ratios (ORs) and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) are shown for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). The vertical reference line indicates OR = 1.

Sex- and age-stratified analyses showed stronger heat effects in males and in individuals < 65 years. For example, under EHT_90TH, first-time AMI risk in those ≤ 65 years was increased from lag0 through lag3, peaking at lag1 (OR = 1.210; 95 % CI: 1.063–1.379) (Figs. 5,S4–S7; Tables S7–S8).

Sensitivity analyses

After adjusting for the PCA-derived air-pollution index, EHT and HW were still found to be associated with an increased incidence of first-time AMI. For example, the odds ratio for EHT_90TH was 1.1293 (95 % CI: 1.0341–1.2333) (Fig. S8; Tables S9–S11). Results were broadly consistent under alternative EHT/HW thresholds (Fig. S9).

Discussion

In this time-stratified case-crossover study, short-term exposure to EHT and HW was associated with an increased risk of AMI. Effects were most evident within a lag window of up to seven days, with risk concentrated among first-time AMI, particularly in men, adults younger than 65 years, and patients presenting with NSTEMI. Although the effect size was modest to moderate, these findings have clear public health relevance, as even small relative increases in risk may translate into considerable population burdens under ongoing climate change.

Comparison with prior studies

Our findings corroborate prior epidemiologic evidence linking extreme heat with cardiovascular morbidity [22,23]. Studies in the United States and Vietnam reported elevated risks of AMI or cardiovascular admissions following high-temperature days, with risk often peaking several days after exposure [24,25]. Similarly, a nationwide Swedish study observed short-term associations between ambient temperature and AMI admissions [26]. Although the effect size was modest, this aligns with previous evidence showing that even small individual-level risks can translate into substantial population burdens due to the high baseline incidence of AMI [27,28].

However, null or inverse associations have also been reported, including studies from Worcester (USA) and southern China. Discrepancies between research findings may arise from multiple factors. First, studies have employed different definitions of extreme heat. Our study used percentile-based thresholds adapted to local climates (e.g., the 90th or 95th percentile), which more accurately reflect regional variations and actual risk [19,29]. Second, we utilized high-resolution ERA5 reanalysis data, allowing more precise characterization of short-term climatic variations [30]. Furthermore, differences in regional climate characteristics, population vulnerability, disease management, exposure assessment, and confounder control may also contribute to inconsistencies across studies [31].

Importantly, this heterogeneity is not merely methodological but clinically meaningful. The variation in research findings suggests that preventive measures should be tailored to regional and population-specific temperature sensitivities [32]. At the clinical level, identifying high-risk individuals can help physicians more accurately assess heat exposure risks and optimize treatment strategies. At the public health level, understanding these differences supports the development of stratified interventions and the rational allocation of healthcare resources [33,34]. Overall, a deeper understanding of population- and region-specific temperature responses is essential for reducing heat-related cardiovascular risks and enhancing the resilience of healthcare systems.

Biological plausibility

The associations observed are supported by established pathophysiological mechanisms. Heat exposure challenges thermoregulatory homeostasis, leading to cutaneous vasodilation, increased cardiac workload, and higher myocardial oxygen demand. Concomitant dehydration, hemoconcentration, and electrolyte imbalance can increase blood viscosity and thrombogenic potential, thereby precipitating acute coronary syndromes [35,36]. Recent meta-analyses of controlled heat-exposure studies confirm heat-induced impairments in cardiac output and endothelial function [27], providing strong mechanistic plausibility for our findings.

Differential vulnerability

A novel contribution of this study is the differentiation of first-time and recurrent AMI. We found that first-time AMI events were more strongly associated with heat exposure, whereas recurrent AMI showed no clear association, which may be attributable to insufficient statistical power for recurrent cases. This divergence may reflect heightened health awareness, behavioral adaptation, and cardioprotective medication use among recurrent patients, which collectively buffer heat effects [37]. By contrast, individuals at risk of a first AMI may lack such protective factors, making them more vulnerable during periods of extreme heat.

Sex- and age-stratified analyses indicated stronger effects in men and adults younger than 65 years. Occupational exposures, later retirement age, and higher likelihood of outdoor work may increase men’s vulnerability in China, whereas younger patients may be less adapted or less likely to restrict activity during extreme heat [38]. Our finding of greater sensitivity among NSTEMI than STEMI aligns with prior research in Germany and South Korea [39,40], suggesting that subacute pathophysiological pathways such as inflammation or vascular dysfunction may be particularly relevant to NSTEMI onset during heat stress [26].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of a large hospital-based registry of clinically confirmed AMI, application of a time-stratified case-crossover design that controls for fixed confounders and seasonal trends, and the incorporation of multiple EHT and HW definitions with distributed lag nonlinear models. Subtype and subgroup analyses further strengthen interpretability and translational relevance.

Limitations warrant consideration. First, the small number of recurrent AMI cases may have limited statistical power, and survivor bias cannot be excluded, which may partly explain the null association observed for recurrent AMI. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Second, potential exposure misclassification cannot be ruled out, as ambient meteorological data may not accurately capture individual-level exposures influenced by housing conditions or adaptive behaviors. Third, admission date was used as a proxy for symptom onset, introducing potential temporal misclassification. Finally, findings from a single city may limit generalizability, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded; thus, future multi-center studies or meta-analyses are needed to better evaluate the impact of extreme heat on AMI.

Clinical and public health implications

These findings have important implications for preventive cardiology. Extreme heat should be recognized as a short-term trigger for first-time AMI, with risk persisting several days after exposure. Preventive messaging and clinical counseling should therefore extend beyond the heat event itself. Based on our findings, we propose the following actionable strategies to mitigate the heat-related cardiovascular risks:(1) Extended Heat-Health Alerts [41]: Implement a warning system that covers the current day and the next 3–5 days, enabling high-risk groups to take proactive protective measures during and after heatwave events; (2) Occupational Health Policies [42]: Implement health protection measures for outdoor and high-risk workers, including shaded rest areas, flexible working hours, and regular health check-ups; (3) Cardiovascular Risk Counseling [43]: Integrate heat-related cardiovascular risks into routine consultations. Clinical practitioners should strengthen health education for high-risk populations (e.g., young males) by advising on preventive measures during standard check-ups; (4) Clinical Awareness of NSTEMI [44]: Enhance clinical recognition of heat sensitivity in NSTEMI patients, with intensified monitoring and patient education during heatwaves.

Strengthening early-warning systems, tailoring occupational health guidelines, and integrating climate resilience into cardiovascular prevention frameworks are urgent priorities as global warming accelerates.

Conclusion

Short-term exposure to extreme heat significantly increases the risk of AMI, with risk concentrated among first-time cases and particularly in men, younger adults, and NSTEMI patients. In the era of climate change, preventive strategies in cardiology should explicitly incorporate heat-related risk, emphasizing targeted health communication, early recognition of symptoms, and timely medical evaluation during and after periods of sustained high temperature.
