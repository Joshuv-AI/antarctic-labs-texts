Intermittent fasting alleviates obesity-associated impairments in bone fracture healing: Exploring the role of gut microbiome
Authors: Honey Hendesi, Dana A Godfrey, Ana Ferreira Ruble, Aaron M Tran, David A Villani, Samantha H Landgrave, Nur A Hasan, Douglas J Adams, Michael J Zuscik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntermittent Fasting (IF) is a dietary strategy with metabolic benefits that can reverse certain obesity-related pathologies. This study aimed to investigate whether IF can mitigate delayed bone fracture healing associated with obesity. Using cohorts of mice on high-fat or control diets, we applied either an ad libitum feeding or an alternate-day fasting regimen to animals from both diet groups. We assessed bone healing outcomes by evaluating callus mineralization and adipocyte accumulation within the callus through micro computed tomography (micro-CT), histology, and immunohistochemical analyses. Since IF is known to modulate gut microbiome composition, often associated with improvement in various metabolic and inflammatory processes, particularly in high-fat-fed mice, we also explored the microbial community changes in IF mice through 16S rRNA sequencing of cecal samples. Metabolically, IF led to reduced body weight and improved glucose tolerance in obese mice. Regarding fracture healing outcomes, reduced/delayed mineralization and adipocyte accumulation in fracture callus tissue in the high-fat-fed cohort were significantly attenuated when the high-fat-fed mice were subjected to alternate-day fasting. These benefits of IF were not observed in lean mice fed a control diet. Furthermore, IF significantly altered the gut microbiota of mice on a high-fat diet, including an increased abundance of short-chain fatty acid producing bacteria, known for their positive effect on bone density, and a reduction in various pro-inflammatory taxa. While the mechanistic role remains unknown, these findings suggest that the improved fracture healing observed in obese mice following IF may be associated with alterations in gut microbiome composition and function.Keywords: Bone fracture healing, Nonunion, Diet-induced obesity, Intermittent-fasting, Gut-microbiome, Short chain fatty acid

Introduction

Bone fracture nonunion occurs in approximately 5–10 % of all fractures, resulting in increased hospitalization time, medical expenditures, and disability (Lerner et al., 1993; Mills et al., 2017; Zura et al., 2016a). Obesity and associated type 2 diabetes (T2D) are risk factors for both delayed fracture healing and development of nonunion in both weight-bearing and non-weight-bearing bones (Zura et al., 2016a; Hernandez et al., 2012; Zura et al., 2016b). With the prevalence of obesity growing (Dietz, 2015), the burden of delayed healing and fracture nonunion in obese patients and the healthcare system will persist.

Our understanding of tissue-level changes that occur in obesity-related fracture healing is limited to studies in experimental animal models. Specifically, long bone fracture healing in high-fat-diet (HFD)-fed obese mice leads to distinct healing tissue phenotypes that include diminished callus size, delayed callus mineralization, increased adiposity within callus structure, and delayed recovery of mechanical strength of the fractured bone (Brown et al., 2014; Ross et al., 2021). Impaired differentiation of skeletal progenitor cells is characterized by commitment of a subset of cells to the adipo-lineage that may underlie the preponderance of adipocytes in the callus leading to bone formation deficits (Figeac et al., 2022).

Gut microbiome dysbiosis is a known feature of diet-induced obesity and is one of the mechanisms thought to contribute to obesity related metabolic dysregulations (Le Chatelier et al., 2013; Turnbaugh et al., 2006). Thus, strategies have been developed that mitigate gut dysbiosis to improve host metabolism, reduce inflammation systemically, and mitigate obesity related pathological conditions (Favazzo et al., 2020; Schott et al., 2018). A crosstalk between gut microbiome and bone, particularly through short-chain fatty-acids (SCFAs) produced by microbiota, has been previously investigated and identified (Seely et al., 2021; Sjogren et al., 2012). The SCFAs butyrate, propionate, and acetate are biproducts of dietary fiber fermentation driven by gut bacteria. It follows that pro- and/or prebiotic interventions that induce shifts in the microbial community and promote SCFA production could increase bone density in bone loss models via mechanisms that involve both osteoblast and osteoclast function (Garcia-Vieyra et al., 2014; Kondo et al., 2022; Lawenius et al., 2022; Li et al., 2016; Lucas et al., 2018; Montalvany-Antonucci et al., 2019; Roberfroid et al., 2002; Tyagi et al., 2018; Wallimann et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2022). Beyond bone remodeling, probiotics have also been shown to positively influence bone repair. Supplementation with Akkermansia muciniphila has improved gut barrier function, angiogenesis, and callus mineralization in mice (Liu et al., 2020a). Similarly, species of Bifidobacterium, alone or in combination with Lactobacillus, enhanced gut barrier integrity, callus mineralization, and mechanical strength of the callus (Roberts et al., 2023a; Roberts et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023). Nevertheless, the underlying mechanisms linking gut to the site of fracture repair remain to be elucidated.

Intermittent fasting (IF), characterized by repeating alternating cycles of fasting and eating, is a non-pharmacological strategy associated with several metabolic, anti-inflammatory, and cardiovascular benefits that are independent of its potential weight loss effects (Di Francesco et al., 2018; Sutton et al., 2018; Trepanowski et al., 2017; Varady et al., 2009; Varady et al., 2021). Several eating patterns fall under the description of IF, including alternate-day fasting, the 5:2 diet, and time restricted eating. While alternate-day fasting is the most effective approach, all these methods provide weight loss and cardiometabolic benefits (Elortegui Pascual et al., 2023). IF has a significant impact on gut microbiome composition and function in both humans and mice (Perez-Gerdel et al., 2023; Stanislawski et al., 2021), with several studies providing evidence linking IF to induced shifts in the gut microbiome that resolve dysbiosis and lead to mitigation of obesity-related pathologies in various contexts (Li et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2020b; Yang et al., 2023). Currently, information about the influence of IF on the human skeleton is limited, with available data collected secondary to other primary outcomes in IF clinical studies (Bredella et al., 2021; Bredella et al., 2022; Lobene et al., 2021; Papageorgiou et al., 2023).

In the present study, based on evidence that IF can alleviate obesity-related gut dysbiosis and can mitigate many of the systemic effects of obesity, we hypothesize that obesity-associated delays in bone fracture healing can be reversed by IF, and that this reversal is associated with a shift in the gut microbiome that is characterized by loss of proinflammatory taxa and increased abundance of SCFA producers. To address this hypothesis, we examined callus tissue structure using micro-CT imaging and histology-based methods at 21-days post-fracture, the time point that has been identified previously to exhibit the most notable disparities between obese and lean mice (Brown et al., 2014). Further, we study changes in the microbial community caused by obesity and IF via 16S rRNA sequencing on cecal samples to examine gut microbiome community structure. Here we report that IF mitigates obesity-associated impairments in fracture healing in parallel to correction of gut microbiome dysbiosis. Our findings identify potential key microbial players modulating the IF effect, setting the stage for further mechanistic study of the causal relationship between IF effects on the gut microbiome and mitigation of delayed fracture healing in obesity.

Materials and methods

Animals

Four-week-old C57BL/6J male mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. Mice were housed in groups of 4 per cage, in autoclaved wood chip covered cages, a controlled 14/10-h light/dark cycle, and at a temperature of 23 °C. All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the University Committee on Animal Resources (IACUC) at the University of Colorado.

Diet, intermittent fasting, experimental time line

After two weeks acclimation in vivarium, six-week-old mice were introduced to HFD (60 % kcal from fat, Open Source Diets, D12492) or the matched control diet (10 % kcal from fat, Open Source Diets, D12450B). The diets were sterilized by gamma irradiation and were used throughout the study. After inducing obesity for eighteen weeks (>3 standard deviation of average weight of control group), until the mice were 24 weeks of age, we divided the obese and control mice into two groups: 1) mice maintained on ad libitum access to HFD or Control diet, and 2) mice intermittently fasted on HFD or Control diet. For the IF regimen, we put mice on alternating cycles of 24-h ad libitum access to the designated diet and 24-h of fasting. Fasting cycles began at noon, while access to water was unrestricted. At the beginning of each fasting cycle, mice were transferred to clean cages to limit the influence of coprophagia. Mice were routinely monitored for body weight and food intake was quantified by measuring the weight of the added and remaining food at the beginning and end of each cycle. Measurements were taken per cage and normalized to number of mice housed in the cage. Fracture surgery was performed at 28 weeks of age, and mice were euthanized at the week 31. Tibia fracture surgery

Open tibia fracture was performed on 28-week-old mice using a previously described surgical method (Brown et al., 2014). Briefly, under isoflurane anesthesia, a 15-mm skin incision was made over the anterior aspect of the right lower limb. The mid-diaphysis of the right tibia was identified and cut using a Dremel tool and a circular fine-toothed blade. A 23 gauge needle was inserted through the anteromedial tibial plateau to provide intramedullary fixation (Fig. 1B). The incision was sutured, and X-ray imaging was conducted using a Faxitron X-ray cabinet (Hologic, Marlborough, MA) to evaluate the fracture and fixation. To manage post-operative pain for the first 72 h, subcutaneous injection of Buprenorphine SR (extended release, 1 mg/kg) was administered.

Glucose tolerance test (GTT)

After four weeks of IF, fasting glucose levels were measured in blood samples collected from the tail vein, using a commercially available glucometer (One Touch Ultra; Lifescan). All mice were fasted for 6 h prior to the test. Subsequently, an intraperitoneal glucose bolus (200 mg/kg) was administered, and blood glucose levels were measured at 20, 40, 60, and 120-min post-injection. The GTT was performed prior to the induction of tibial fractures. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated using GraphPad Prism software based on the curve of glucose levels of the GTT in each cohort.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA)

Following four weeks of IF and before the fracture surgery, DXA scans were performed on anesthetized mice to measure body composition (Hologic Faxitron Bioptics UltraFocusDXA). The VisionDXA software was used to measure body fat percentage and bone mineral density. The region of interest included the entire body minus skull.

Micro computed tomography (micro-CT) scan

High-resolution micro-CT scanning of the excised fractured tibiae was performed at 21 days post-fracture (μCT50, Scanco Medical AG, Basserdorf, Switzerland). The bone volume within the fracture callus was determined using previously described methods (Brown et al., 2014). Briefly, samples were scanned using the following parameters: 70 kV and 200 μA (14 W), 2000 projections/revolution and 500 millisecond integration time per projection, providing 10 μm isometric resolution (1,000,000 voxels/mm3). Two contour lines were created for each slice; one outlining the perimeter of the external fracture callus, and the other outlining the residual cortical bone that was present within the callus area and was not a product of the callus mineralization process. Subtracting the residual (extant) cortical bone from the total mineralized tissue in the callus defined all mineralized tissues above the pre-set threshold, thereby encompassing mineralized tissue volume in the fracture callus. Bone mineral was calibrated to a discrete-step hydroxyapatite phantom and segmented from marrow and soft tissue in conjunction with a constrained Gaussian filter to reduce noise, applying a mineral density threshold of 200 mg HA/cm3.

Histology, histomorphometry, immunohistochemistry

Fracture callus tissues were collected, fixed, processed, and paraffin embedded using methods we have previously employed (Brown et al., 2014). Briefly, following euthanasia, fractured tibiae were collected and fixed at room temperature in 10 % neutral buffered formalin for three days. Intramedullary fixation needles were removed, and tibiae were decalcified in 14 % EDTA +1.8 % acetic acid solution (PH 7.4) for fourteen days. Following this, tibiae were processed using a Sakura Tissue Tek VIP 6 vacuum infiltration processor (Torrance, CA) and embedded in paraffin in an orientation that facilitated sagittal sectioning. Starting on the lateral aspect of individual tibiae, 5 μm thick sagittal sections were cut at 3 levels spanning the center of the callus, with each level being 100 μm from the previous level (i.e., mid-sagittal and ± 100 μm). Sections were stained with Alcian blue/Hematoxylin Orange G for histomorphometric analysis. Stained slides were scanned at high resolution using a Zeiss Axioscan Slide scanner (Obrkochen, Germany). A blinded observer segmented the callus and quantified fracture callus area. Adipocytes were segmented, and their number and total area were measured and normalized to the callus area. For cartilaginous tissue, Alcian Blue–stained regions within the callus containing chondrocytes were segmented, and the total area was calculated and normalized to callus area (Malhan et al., 2018) using Zeiss ZenLite software. Area measurements and cell counts were averaged from three separate stained sections collected from each tibia.

Expression of PPARγ was assessed via immunohistochemistry (IHC) as we have described previously (Brown et al., 2014). Briefly, an avidin-biotin-peroxidase system which included a goat anti rabbit secondary antibody (Vector Lab, Burlingame, CA, USA) was used to detect the primary mouse-anti-PPARγ rabbit monoclonal antibody (1∶100: Cat. #2435: Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Danvers, MA). Reactions were then visualized with diaminobenzidine (DAB) as a substrate (Vector Lab, Burlingame, CA, USA) and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. For immunograding, two slides from each mouse were scored and averaged by 4 blinded observers using the Intensity of Staining component of the established Immunoreactive Score, an approach which involves a scale of 0–3, where 0 = no color reaction, 1 = mild positive reaction, 2 is moderate positive reaction, and 3 is an intense positive reaction (Fedchenko and Reifenrath, 2014).

Cecal sample collection and 16S rRNA sequencing

Immediately after euthanasia, cecal content was collected into sterile DNase free tubes and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. For each diet group, cecal samples were collected from mice housed in different cages. Samples were shipped on dry ice to EZBiome (Gaithersburg, MD, USA) for DNA extraction and sequencing. For DNA extraction, frozen cecal samples were thawed at room temperature, and 250 mg aliquots of cecal material were lysed and homogenized in a bead-beating tube containing 0.1 mm and 0.5 mm beads and ZymoBiomics lysis buffer using Omni's Bead Ruptor 96 (Perkin Elmer, USA) following manufacturer's instructions. Total DNA was then isolated using the ZymoBiomics DNA extraction mini kit (Zymo Research, USA) with a final elution volume of 50 μL. The concentration of genomic dsDNA was measured for each sample using Qubit® dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on an Invitrogen Qubit® 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

16S amplicon sequencing was performed by EzBiome (Gaithersburg, MD, USA). The 16S rRNA V3-V4 regions within the ribosomal transcript were amplified using the following primer pair: Illumina F: TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG, and Illumina-R: GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC. These primers contain the gene-specific sequences and Illumina adapter overhang nucleotide sequences. Amplicon PCR was performed to amplify template out of input DNA samples as previously described (Brumfield et al., 2021). Briefly, each 25 μL of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) solution contained 12.5 ng of sample DNA as input, 12.5 μL 2× KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA), and 5 μL of each primer (1 μM). PCR reactions were carried out using the following protocol: an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 3 min followed by 25 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 30 s), annealing (55 °C, 30 s) and extension (72 °C, 30 s), and a final elongation of 5 min at 72 °C. PCR products were cleaned up from the reaction mix with Mag-Bind RxnPure Plus magnetic beads (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA). A second index PCR amplification, used to incorporate barcodes and sequencing adapters into the final PCR product, was performed in 25 μL reactions, using the same master mix conditions as described above. Cycling conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 8 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 30 s. A final, 5-min elongation step was performed at 72 °C. The libraries were then normalized and pooled and the pooled library was checked using an Agilent 2200 TapeStation and sequenced on the MiSeq (Illumina, San Diego, CA) on a 500 cycle (2 × 250 bp paired end) run.

16S amplicon taxonomic assignment, functional prediction and statistical analyses

Taxonomic profiling of 16S sequencing data was carried out by directly uploading the paired end reads to the EzBioCloud microbiome taxonomy profiling platform (www.ezbiocloud.net) as described elsewhere (Yoon et al., 2017). Briefly, the cloud app of the EzBioCloud detects and filters out sequences of low quality regarding read length (<80 bp or >2,000 bp) and averaged Q values less than 25. Denoising and extraction of non-redundant reads was carried out using DUDE-Seq software. The UCHIME algorithm was applied against the EzBioCloud 16S chimera-free database to check and remove chimera sequencing. Taxonomic assignment was performed using the USEARCH program to detect and calculate the sequence similarities of the query single-end reads against the EzBioCloud 16S database. Sequencing reads were clustered into OTUs at 97 % sequence similarity using the UPARSE algorithm. Reads from each sample were clustered into many OTUs using the UCLUST tool with the above-noted cutoff values. For the EzBioCloud 16S-based MTP pipeline, the PICRUST algorithm was used to estimate the functional profiles of the microbiome identified using 16S rRNA sequencing. The raw sequencing reads were computed using the EzBioCloud 16S microbiome pipeline with default parameters and discriminating reads that were encountered in the reference database. The functional abundance profiles of the microbiome were annotated based on bioinformatics analyses, specifically by multiplying the vector of gene counts for each OTU by the abundance of that OTU in each sample, using the KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) orthology and pathway database. Subsampling, generation of taxonomy plots/tables and rarefaction curves, and calculation of species richness, coverage, and alpha and beta diversity indices were carried out in the EzBioCloud App (Yoon et al., 2017). Briefly, microbial richness was measured by the number of OTUs found in the microbiome taxonomic profile (MTP) index. The Shannon and Phylogenetic α-diversity indices were applied to estimate the diversity for each group using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Beta diversity was calculated with Generalized UniFrac distances based on the taxonomic abundance profiles. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) were applied to measure the statistical significances of β-diversity. Different groups were clustered with principal component analysis (PCoA) based on generalized the Unifrac distance metric. Kruskal-Wallis H test, LEfSe and Taxon XOR analysis were performed to determine enrichment in the assigned taxonomic and functional profiles between groups.

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as mean ± SEM (parametric tests) or as median with interquartile range (non-parametric tests). GraphPad Prism software (version 10) was used for statistical analyses. Unpaired Student's t-tests were used to analyze the differences between two groups and two-way ANOVA with the Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used for comparisons between four groups. For non-parametric comparisons between four groups, we performed multiple Mann-Whitney U tests and reported adjusted p-values. For all experiments, differences between groups were considered significant if p values were < 0.05.

Results

Intermittent fasting reduces body mass in obese mice

Monitoring daily food intake revealed that both Control-IF and HFD-IF mice increased their food intake on feeding days to compensate for the lack of energy intake on fasting days. While HFD-IF mice consumed an average of 0.1 g on their feeding days, compared to the 0.07 g average daily intake in HFD-Ad lib mice, Control-IF mice consumed double the daily food intake of Control-Ad lib mice on feeding days (0.18 g vs 0.09 g, Fig. 1C). Changes in body mass were monitored from start of the IF intervention until fracture surgery. While Control-IF mice did not exhibit a significant decrease in body weight compared to the Control-Ad lib mice, HFD-IF showed significant weight loss relative to HFD-Ad lib, with differences detectable as early as two weeks into the IF regimen (Fig. 1D). Despite this weight loss, IF mice remained significantly heavier than mice on control diet (Fig. 1D).

To evaluate changes in bone mineral density and body fat percentage following four weeks of IF, we performed DXA scans prior to fracture surgery. Consistent with previous reports, overall the obese mice on HFD had lower BMD compared to lean mice (Parhami et al., 2001; Patsch et al., 2011); however, 4 weeks of IF did not cause a significant change in BMD in either of the diet groups (Fig. 1E). Similarly, body fat percentage was not significantly altered in either of the diet groups following four weeks of IF (Fig. 1F). These findings indicate that a modest decrease in energy intake and body weight of HFD-IF mice was discernable, but there were no significant changes in BMD or body fat percentage prior to the induction of the fracture.

Intermittent fasting improves glucose metabolism in obese mice

It is known that the HFD-fed obese mice become diabetic (Winzell and Ahren, 2004). To investigate the impact of IF regimen on glucose metabolism, we measured fasting glucose levels and conducted glucose tolerance tests (GTT) after four weeks of IF, prior to fracture surgeries. As expected, HFD-Ad lib mice exhibited elevated fasting glucose levels and impaired glucose tolerance (Fig. 2A–C). Notably, IF lowered the fasting glucose in HFD-IF mice to levels comparable to mice on control diet (Fig. 2A). Glucose tolerance, measured by the area under the curve (AUC), also improved significantly in HFD-IF mice, reverting to the metabolic phenotype seen in mice on control diet (Fig. 2B and C). IF did not affect fasting glucose levels and glucose tolerance of mice on control diet (Fig. 2A–C). These findings indicate that 4 weeks of IF was sufficient to restore glucose metabolism in obese mice in the period prior to induction of tibial fractures.

Intermittent fasting increases bone volume fraction of the fracture callus in obese mice

To assess new bone formation within the fracture callus, we performed micro-CT scans at day 21 post-fracture. Representative sagittal and transverse sections of fractured tibiae depict the size and mineralization at the fracture site in various diet groups (Fig. 3A). Evaluation of the density of the mineralized tissue within the callus showed no difference between diet groups (Fig. 3B). Assessment of the callus volume showed that although mice on the control and HFD-IF diets had on average larger calluses compared to the HFD-Ad lib, the differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 3C). The bone volume within the callus of the control mice was significantly greater than the HFD-Ad lib mice, while HFD-IF mice showed a trend towards higher BV that did not reach significance (p = 0.06) (Fig. 3D). The callus bone volume fraction (BV/TV) of HFD-Ad lib mice was significantly lower compared to Control-Ad lib mice, which is consistent with previous studies (Fig. 3E) (Brown et al., 2014). Notably, in HFD-IF mice, callus BV/TV was increased to levels comparable to the control-Ad lib group (Fig. 3E). In mice on the control diet, IF did not have a significant effect on callus volume, bone volume or the BV/TV (Fig. 3C–E). These results suggest that reduced fracture callus BV/TV caused by ad lib consumption of the high fat diet is to some extent reversed in high fat-fed fractured mice that undergo IF.

Intermittent fasting reduces adiposity and increases cartilage content in obese callus

To investigate whether IF could mitigate previously reported increases in fracture callus adiposity in obese mice (Brown et al., 2014), we conducted histomorphometric analysis on sections of callus at the 21-day post-fracture time-point (Fig. 4A–C). Representative histology images from the four diet groups illustrate boundaries of the callus and the regions of adipocyte accumulation which are more prominent in HFD-Ad lib group (Fig. 4A). When quantified, as expected, we detected an increase in both the number of adipocytes and overall adipocyte-covered area in the callus of HFD-Ad lib group compared to the control-Ad lib group (Fig. 4B, C). Comparatively, both measures were markedly reduced in HFD-IF mice (Fig. 4B, C). IF had no significant effect on adipocyte number or adipocyte area in callus of mice on control diet (Fig. 4B, C). To examine the potential molecular basis for this observation, we conducted immunohistochemical staining for PPARγ, a key regulator of fatty acid storage which promotes differentiation of skeletal progenitors into adipocytes (Li et al., 2018; Tontonoz et al., 1994). Our analysis revealed elevated PPARγ expression in the callus of the HFD-Ad lib group compared to the Control-Ad lib group, and this molecular phenotype was significantly attenuated by IF (Fig. 4D, E). Assessment of the cartilage area of the callus, showed no significant difference between control- Ad lib and HFD- Adlib, which is expected at day 21 (Brown et al., 2014). Notably, IF significantly increased the cartilage area in the HFD-IF mice compared to HFD-Ad lib (Fig. 4F). A trend towards increased average cartilage area was observed in Control-IF mice, although it did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that IF reduces the adiposity phenotype in the fracture callus of obese mice, potentially through modulation of major signaling pathways, and promotes cartilage tissue in the callus through mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Obesity induces a distinct gut microbiome dysbiosis

At 21 days post-fracture, the microbial profile of cecal samples from HFD-Ad lib and Control-Ad lib mice were assessed to characterize the impact of obesity with overlay of bone fracture. Principle coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on generalized Unifrac dissimilarity (β-Diversity) revealed two distinct clusters, with significant distance between diet groups (PERMANOVA, p = 0.01) (Fig. 5A). However, the Shannon index of α-diversity, which measures taxa richness and evenness within each sample, did not differ significantly between the two groups (Fig. 5B). At the phylum level, compositional differences were evident with HFD-Ad lib showing an increased abundance of Firmicutes (90 % vs 69 %) and the emergence of Proteobacteria, both known to be associated with gut dysbiosis (Ley et al., 2006; Shin et al., 2015). This was in parallel to decreases in beneficial phyla, including Actinobacteria and Verrucomicrobia (Binda et al., 2018; Yan et al., 2021) compared to the Control-Ad lib group (Fig. 5C). At the family level, HFD-Ad lib mice exhibited increased relative abundances of several taxa including Peptostreptococcaceae, Streptococcaceae, Coriobacteriaceae, and Desulfovibrionaceae, while Akkermansiaceae decreased and Bifidibacteriaceae and Clostridiaceae dissipated (Fig. 5D). These results indicate shifts in HFD-Ad lib mice that are consistent with a pro-inflammatory gut dysbiosis induced by the high fat diet that has been previously reported (He et al., 2018).

Intermittent fasting alters the composition of the obese gut microbiome

The composition of the gut microbiome in HFD-Ad lib and HFD-IF mice was compared to reveal the effects of IF regimen in obese mice with fractures. β-diversity evaluation of the compositional dissimilarities, illustrated by a PCoA plot, revealed significant alterations in the structure of the gut microbiome due to IF (PERMENOVA, p = 0.045, Fig. 6A). In contrast, α-diversity did not show significant differences following IF (Fig. 6B). At the phylum level, HFD-IF mice exhibited a marginal decrease in the relative abundance of Firmicutes (84 % vs. 90 %), a complete loss of Proteobacteria, and an increase in Actinobacteria and Verrucomicrobia compared to HFD-Ad lib group (Fig. 6C). At the genus level, IF leads to an increase in the abundance of short chain fatty acid producers, such as Acetatifacor, an unclassified genus of Akkermansia (p = 0.0508), and Alistipes (Yan et al., 2021; Dao et al., 2016; de la Cuesta-Zuluaga et al., 2017; Pfeiffer et al., 2012). In contrast, Dorea, particularly Dorea formicigenerance, which has been directly linked to obesity (Companys et al., 2021), was reduced by IF. Similarly, Turicibacter, a genus involved in lipid metabolism and bile acid modifying ability (Lynch et al., 2023), and an unclassified genus in Muribaculaceae (S24-7) family, known for species with both pro and anti-inflammatory effects (Roberts et al., 2023b; Zhao et al., 2024), were diminished in HFD-IF mice (Fig. 6D).

Differences in microbial abundances in the HFD-IF and HFD-Ad lib groups were further examined by linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe), with the cutoff of p < 0.05 and LDA scores >2.0. This analysis identified several taxa and phylotypes (i.e., a tentatively assigned taxa that represents a unique 16S rRNA gene sequence) that were significantly enriched in either HFD-IF or HFD-Ad lib cohorts (Fig. 6E). Despite the inherent limitations of 16S rRNA sequencing in identifying species and strains, our results suggest that several unidentified taxa belonging to the Lachnospiracea family were enriched in the HFD-IF group, whereas an unidentified species of Bifidobacterium was more abundant in the HFD-Ad lib group (Fig. 6E). Predicted pathway analysis using PICRUST (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States) indicated significant enrichment of bacterial taxa associated with the propanoate metabolism and fatty acid metabolism in HFD-IF mice (Fig. 6F). Collectively, these findings suggest that IF induces significant shifts in gut microbiome composition in HFD-fed mice, potentially alleviating obesity-related dysbiosis. Furthermore, detected microbial alterations could potentially lead to increase in SCFAs production.

Discussion

Fracture nonunion and delayed fracture healing are orthopedic comorbidities associated with obesity and obesity-related metabolic disturbances, imposing significant burden on both individuals and health care systems (Zura et al., 2016a; Hernandez et al., 2012; Zura et al., 2016b). Current strategies for addressing delayed fracture healing include surgical interventions sometimes involving bone grafting to enhance bone fixation (Cho et al., 2021). Non-surgical interventions include electromagnetic or ultrasonic bone stimulator devices, which have variable success (Griffin et al., 2011; Zura et al., 2015), and may not have been fully investigated in the context of obesity-impaired bone healing.

In recent years, intermittent fasting has generated attention for its benefits, including weight loss and improved metabolic and cardiovascular parameters. Because of its simplicity, due to requiring minimal calorie counting and no drastic changes in macronutrient composition, IF has become a more attractive option than conventional calorie-restriction diets (Varady et al., 2021). While studies have explored the impact of IF on varying clinical situations, investigation into its effects on bone health have been limited. Clinical studies following three to six months of IF have reported increased bone mineral content, measured by DXA scans (Lobene et al., 2021), and reduced serum marker of bone resorption (Papageorgiou et al., 2023). Additionally, a pair of studies comparing the effect of over-eating and fasting on bone microstructure and marrow adiposity using High-Resolution Peripheral Quantitative CT-Scan (HR-pQCT) and single-voxel proton MR spectroscopy have revealed that in contrast to effects of over eating, fasting increases bone density and reduces marrow adiposity (Bredella et al., 2021; Bredella et al., 2022). These studies suggest that in contrast to the negative effect of conventional calorie restriction regimens on bone density (Devlin et al., 2010; Villareal et al., 2016; Villareal et al., 2006), IF, which is not calorie restrictive over long periods of intervention, are potentially positive. This suggests a distinct and unknown mechanism of action on bone that warrants in-depth exploration.

To examine potential IF benefits for compromised fracture healing in the context of obesity, we conducted this proof-of-concept study, incorporating the IF regimen into a well-characterized diet-induced obese mouse model of delayed healing. Our model used a combination of dietary and surgical interventions, which was well-tolerated. We observed reduced body weight and improved glucose tolerance in obese mice that continued to consume the high fat diet but were fasted intermittently. This aligned with the reported benefits of IF in translational and clinical studies (Varady et al., 2021; Li et al., 2017). While the four-week duration of IF did not yield significant reductions in body fat percentage, as measured by DXA scan, the trend towards lower body fat suggests that longer durations of the IF regimen may result in more pronounced changes in body fat. This also applies to changes in BMD following four weeks of IF regimen. Although no systemic effect on BMD at the time of fracture healing was detected, a trend towards increased BMD suggests that long term IF may have a positive effect on bone homeostasis which itself requires to be investigated (Fig. 1E). Unfortunately, with our DXA approach, we could not isolate skeletal muscle mass from other compartments to determine whether observed weight loss in HFD-IF regimen is partly due to the muscle loss.

Studies have linked impaired fracture healing in obese mice to a decrease in osteoblastic differentiation of the progenitor cells, leading to reduced callus bone content and ultimately decreased mechanical strength of the callus (Brown et al., 2014; Figeac et al., 2022). This impaired osteogenesis is attributed to the reciprocal relationship between adipogenic and osteogenic differentiation of skeletal progenitors and shifts in the marrow microenvironment, which supports adipogenesis (Tencerova et al., 2018). PPARγ is the key transcriptional regulator of adipogenesis, which plays a pivotal role in determining the fate of skeletal progenitors towards adipocytes and away from osteoblasts (Li et al., 2018; Tontonoz et al., 1994). The evidence of this fate-switching is manifested in PPARγ loss of function mice, which have higher bone density due to enhanced osteoblastogenesis (Akune et al., 2004; Cao et al., 2020). Our findings in HFD-Ad lib mice demonstrated concomitant reduction in bone formation in the callus with increased adiposity and elevated PPARγ expression (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Although IF reversed these phenotypes, whether the fate-switching of skeletal progenitors in favor of osteogenesis underlies the impact of IF on bone healing, or other mechanisms such as lipolysis are involved, remains an open question. Investigation into cartilaginous tissue in the callus, revealed an increase in cartilage area in HFD-IF mice compared to the HFD-Ad lib. We acknowledge that day 21 is not an ideal time-point to assess cartilage in fracture calluses and considering the transient nature of cartilage during the endochondral repair process, it is unclear whether the higher cartilage content reflects increased formation or delayed resorption (Fig. 4). Given the complex nature of long bone fracture repair, a multi-time-point assessment of the healing process would be instrumental in fully capturing the influence of IF on the key cellular and molecular events of fracture repair, particularly early immune cells responses which have been implicated in the delay of bone healing in obesity (Khajuria et al., 2023). μ-CT analysis of the fracture callus revealed that no defects in density of the newly formed bone in obese callus. However, the bone quantity per volume of the callus was reduced in HFD-Ad lib compared to control-Ad libitum, a defect that was restored in HFD-IF mice (Fig. 3). This suggests involvement of mechanisms involving bone formation and resorption by osteoblasts and osteoclasts which warrants further investigation. Notably, enhanced new bone formation in the callus was not observed in lean mice, indicating that the benefits of IF in fracture repair are limited to when the obese and diabetic microenvironment is present (Fig. 3).

Several studies have linked the health benefits of IF to shifts in gut microbiota composition and function (Li et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2020b; Beli et al., 2018; Cignarella et al., 2018). Given that both obesity and bone fractures alter gut microbiome composition (Roberts et al., 2023b), we first characterized their combined effect in HFD-Ad lib and Control-Ad lib mice (Fig. 5). Our analysis revealed that the gut microbiome of HFD-Ad lib mice exhibits features consistent with enhanced dysbiosis, including an enrichment of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria, and a decline in Actinobacteria and Verrucomicrobia phyla (Fig. 5). Several families within Firmicutes, including Peptostreptococcaceae, Lactobacillaceae, and Streptococcaceae were increased in HFD-Ad lib mice. These taxa have been associated with gut dysbiosis, increased intestinal permeability, and inflammatory disease (Dahal et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2022). In contrast, Ruminococcaceae, a family that produces SCFAs and is associated with a healthy gut microbiome, was decreased in HFD-Ad lib mice (Jiang et al., 2015). Desulfovibrionaceae, a Proteobacteria family linked to various pro-inflammatory and metabolic diseases, was also enriched in HFD-Ad lib mice (Singh et al., 2023). Conversely, Actinobacteria, a phylum associated with the lean phenotype, and Bifidobacteriaceae, a family within Actinobacteria known to support skeletal health (Schott et al., 2018), were not detected in HFD-Ad lib mice.

Remarkably, applying the IF regimen in obese mice reversed some of the signatures of gut dysbiosis mentioned above. HFD-IF mice had a reduced abundance of Firmicutes, a complete loss of Proteobacteria, and increased Actinobacteria and Verrucomicrobia (Fig. 6C). The most striking finding in HFD-IF mice was an increase in the relative abundance of SCFA-producing genera, including Acetatifactor, Akkermansia, Alistipes, and a genus within Ruminococcaceae family (Yan et al., 2021; Pfeiffer et al., 2012; Parker et al., 2020) (Fig. 6D and E). Predictive pathway analysis confirmed that these shifts in the microbial community would be supportive of propionate production (Fig. 6F).

SCFAs not only improve gut barrier, glucose metabolism, and systemic inflammation (Dao et al., 2016), but a growing body of evidence recognizes them as key mediators of a gut-bone connection (Zaiss et al., 2019). It has been shown that in HFD-fed mice, supplementation with acetate, propionate, and butyrate leads to increased tibia BV/TV and reduced markers of bone resorption and osteoclast number, while osteoblast number remained unchanged (Lucas et al., 2018). This suggests that anti-resorptive effects of SCFAs occur through a direct impact on osteoclasts. The anabolic effects of SCFAs on bone have been suggested to be mediated by increased expansion of regulatory T-cells and CD8+ cells, leading to upregulation of Wnt10b in osteoblasts (Tyagi et al., 2018). It is worth noting that in obese mice, supplementation with SCFAs replicates some of the observed benefits of IF, including reduced body weight, improved glucose metabolism, reduced PPARγ expression, and increased fatty acid oxidation (den Besten et al., 2015). Whether SCFAs can rectify the obesity-related impairments in fracture callus and their mechanism of action warrants further investigation.

Despite our promising findings pointing to SCFAs, the influence of other microbial metabolites cannot be excluded. Notably, the limitations of 16S rRNA sequencing in identifying bacterial species and strains provide only information on community structure, not its function, which would be important to examine in the obese and IF contexts. Our sequencing data, showing many uncharacterized taxa at the species level, mitigates our ability to draw firm conclusions about key mediators of the effects that we observed in this study (Fig. 6E). Thus, future studies should approach assessment of the functional capacity of the microbial community either using metagenome and/or metatranscriptome analyses in conjunction with metabolomic study of the community. These approaches would support analysis of changes in major pathways that lead to the production of SFCAs or other metabolites that may have direct host effects. Correlating multi-omic microbial datasets with changes in callus phenotype could uncover potential contributors connecting the gut community to fracture repair. Additionally, we acknowledge that IF may influence fracture repair through mechanisms independent of the gut microbiome, such as host-generated metabolites or changes in somatic gene expression. Investigation into these alternative mechanisms would be important to fully comprehend the impact of IF on fracture repair.

Although HFD-induced obese mice have been a valuable model for studying the mechanism of obesity-associated impairments in fracture healing, this model has important limitations when applied to female mice. Previous studies have shown variability, and in some cases, a lack of response, to HFD in females. Unlike males, female mice on HFD often fail to gain weight or develop glucose metabolism dysregulations (e.g., hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia) that consistently occur in males. As a result, female mice have frequently been excluded from murine studies of obesity and fracture healing. Our study is also subject to this limitation. Obesity is not sex-specific in humans, and it is critical that using alternative animal models negative influence of obesity on fracture healing in females be investigated (Casimiro et al., 2021; Fajardo et al., 2014; Pettersson et al., 2012).

With these limitations in mind, we report here, for the first time, a protective effect of IF on fracture healing in the HFD-induced obese context that parallel effects on the gut microbiome which indicate mitigation of obesity related dysbiosis. Our findings support the safety and feasibility of alternate-day fasting as a possible intervention to protect against the negative influence of the high fat diet and the obese context on fracture healing.
