Early Childhood Education and Care and Developmental Vulnerability at School Entry
Authors: Kendalem Asmare Atalell, Gavin Pereira, Bereket Duko, Sylvester Dodzi Nyadanu, Sarah Cherian, Gizachew A. Tessema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionIs attendance in early childhood education and care before school entry associated with developmental vulnerability?FindingsIn this population-based cohort of 123 256 children, attendance in early childhood education and care, particularly preschool, was significantly associated with lower developmental vulnerability. Daycare attendance was associated with higher developmental vulnerability in social competence and emotional maturity, but lower developmental vulnerability in language and cognitive skills (school-based), and in communication skills and general knowledge.MeaningThese findings suggest that early childhood education, particularly preschool, is associated with reduced developmental vulnerability at school entry by strengthening socioemotional learning and ensuring equitable access to effective early childhood education programs.

Introduction

Early childhood education and care (ECEC) programs, including daycare, kindergarten, and preschool, are widely recognized for their role in supporting early childhood development, educational attainment, and long-term health.1,2,3 The transition to formal schooling is a major developmental milestone, and skills established in the first 5 years of life shape cognitive, emotional, social, and physical development across the life course. Suboptimal development during this period is associated with poorer academic achievement, reduced earning potential, and increased risk of adverse health outcomes.4,5,6

In Australia, more than 1 in 5 children start school with developmental vulnerability, defined as the risk of not reaching their full developmental potential across 5 domains: physical health and well-being, social competence, emotional maturity, language and cognitive skills (school-based), and communication skills and general knowledge.7,8,9 Developmental vulnerability is particularly common among children from socioeconomically disadvantaged and geographically remote communities, with consistently higher rates across all domains.10,11,12 Previous Australian and international studies suggest that participation in ECEC in the year before starting formal school can improve school readiness and longer-term educational outcomes.13,14,15

Prior research has not adequately differentiated between ECEC settings, particularly preschool and daycare, despite important differences in their structure and intended function. In the Australian context, preschool programs are typically curriculum based and explicitly designed to support school readiness through early cognitive and socioemotional development.14,16 Conversely, daycare often provides more flexible, extended-hour care, with a broader emphasis on supervision and daily care alongside early learning opportunities.17 Differences in timing, duration, and opportunities for structured interaction may, therefore, influence developmental domains differently, particularly socioemotional outcomes. Existing evidence suggests that structured early learning environments are more often associated with cognitive development, whereas care-based settings show more heterogeneous associations with socioemotional outcomes.18

Much of the current literature relies on cross-sectional or area-level analyses, limiting the ability to account for individual-level confounding and to directly compare different ECEC settings within the same population. Furthermore, population-based evidence specific to Western Australia (WA) also remains limited. Although ECEC participation may be particularly beneficial for children experiencing socioeconomic disadvantage,19,20,21 important gaps remain in understanding whether associations differ between preschool and daycare and across developmental domains.1,22,23

To address these gaps, we used a population-based, person-level linked cohort of WA children across 5 Australian Early Development Census (AEDC) waves from 2009 to 2021. The primary aim was to examine the association between attendance in ECEC and developmental vulnerability at school entry. A secondary aim was to assess whether associations differed by ECEC setting, preschool vs daycare.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

We conducted a population-based, retrospective cohort study of children in WA who participated in the AEDC waves collected between 2009 and 2021. AEDC records were linked with the Midwives Notification System, Birth Registrations, Hospital Morbidity Data Collection, Emergency Department Data Collection, WA Notifiable Infectious Diseases Database, WA Cancer Registry, and the WA Register of Developmental Anomalies–Birth Defects. Detailed descriptions of these datasets are available from the WA Data Linkage Services,24 with additional information provided in eAppendix 1 in Supplement 1. This study followed the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data (RECORD) guidelines.25

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the WA Department of Health (PRN: RGS0000005148) and Curtin University Human Research Ethics Committee (HRE2024-0039). Because the study used linked, deidentified administrative and retrospective cohort data of large samples, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Participants

The study population included 141 303 children with AEDC records between 2009 and 2021. Exclusions were applied sequentially for duplicate identifiers (165 children), multiple births (4141 children), missing Midwives Notification System records (55 children), children identified as having developmental disability (termed special needs in the data sources; 5214 children), developmental anomalies (7052 children), and invalid AEDC scores (1420 children). Invalid AEDC scores were defined as records with incomplete assessment data preventing calculation of 1 or more developmental domains. We further excluded 367 children with missing covariate data. Additional exclusions were applied separately for each exposure due to missing exposure information: ECEC attendance (5198 children), preschool attendance (5393 children), and daycare attendance (37 345 children) (Figure 1).

Exposure and Intervention

The primary exposure was participation in ECEC, defined as attendance at either preschool or daycare in the year before commencing full-time school. Consistent with the literature,26 children were classified as exposed if they attended any formal ECEC program and unexposed if they did not attend either preschool or daycare before school entry. In WA, preschool (kindergarten) is a structured early childhood education program delivered in the year before compulsory full-time schooling. Preschool programs are primarily education focused and aim to promote children’s cognitive, language, social, emotional, and physical development in preparation for school. In contrast, daycare refers to center-based early childhood education and care services that provide regular care and educational activities for children, typically operating for longer hours and throughout the year to support both child development and family care needs. All exposures were coded as binary indicators (yes or no) using AEDC data.

Outcome

The outcome of this study was developmental vulnerability, assessed using the AEDC, a validated national teacher-completed census conducted every 3 years at school entry. It measures 5 domains: physical health and well-being, social competence, emotional maturity, language and cognitive skills (school-based), and communication skills and general knowledge. Children were classified as developmentally vulnerable if their score fell below the 10th percentile of the national AEDC reference distribution for a developmental domain. This threshold is the standard AEDC-defined cut-point for developmental vulnerability and is used in national reporting and AEDC-based research to identify children who may benefit from additional support.27,28,29,30,31 In addition, 2 summary indicators were examined: developmentally vulnerable in 1 or more domains (DV1) and developmentally vulnerable in 2 or more domains (DV2).32

Covariates

Covariates were selected on the basis of previous literature and data availability.14,15,33 Child characteristics included sex at birth, age at AEDC assessment, English as a second language, AEDC census year, remoteness, and area-level socioeconomic disadvantage measured using the Socioeconomic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA), specifically the Index of Relative Socioeconomic Disadvantage, categorized into national quintiles. Perinatal factors included maternal age, marital status, ethnicity, parity, maternal smoking, gestational age, birth weight, birth weight for gestational age, gestational diabetes, and preeclampsia. Ethnicity was self-reported and obtained from linked administrative datasets, classified according to data custodian record. Child health history included hospitalizations, emergency department visits, infectious diseases, and childhood cancer. Additional operational definitions are provided in eAppendix 2 in Supplement 1. All covariates were included to account for potential confounding.

Statistical Analysis

Given the low level of missingness across covariates (0.30%) and the large sample size, complete-case analysis was used. Characteristics of excluded children were summarized to assess patterns of missingness (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). Participant characteristics were described using descriptive statistics by ECEC, preschool, and daycare attendance. The percentage of children classified as developmentally vulnerable was calculated separately for exposed and unexposed groups across AEDC waves. Associations between exposures (ECEC, preschool, and daycare) and outcomes (DV1, DV2, and each AEDC domain) were estimated using mixed-effects modified Poisson regression with a log link function, a random intercept for school to account for clustering, and robust SEs. Results are reported as relative risk (RR) and 95% CI. Statistical significance is defined as a 95% CI that does not include the null value of 1. Absolute risk differences were calculated to estimate differences in predicted probabilities between exposure groups. E-values were estimated to assess the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would require to fully explain the observed associations.34 Analyses were conducted between November 2025 and January 2026 using Stata statistical software version 19 (StataCorp).

Additional Analysis

Additional analyses examined overlapping ECEC exposure patterns, including children who attended both preschool and daycare. Restricted analyses were also conducted among preschool-only (excluding daycare attendees) and daycare-only (excluding preschool attendees) groups to evaluate the potential influence of overlapping exposure categories on the primary findings.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses using modified Poisson regression without random effects assessed the robustness of estimates. Subgroup analyses evaluated effect modification by socioeconomic disadvantage (SEIFA <40th vs ≥40th percentile), geographic remoteness (remote vs not remote), and sex (male vs female). All subgroup models applied the same covariate adjustments and are reported adjusted RRs (aRRs) with 95% CIs.

Results

Participant Characteristics

In this population-based study of 123 256 children, complete data were available for 117 691 children in the ECEC analysis (59 335 [50.4%] male; 87 216 [74.1%] aged 5 years 1 month to 5 years 10 months), 117 496 in the preschool analysis (59 234 [50.4%] male; 87 089 [74.1%] aged 5 years 1 month to 5 years 10 months), and 85 544 in the daycare analysis (43 289 [50.6%] male; 63 155 [73.8%] aged 5 years 1 month to 5 years 10 months) (Table 1). The distribution across socioeconomic quintiles was relatively even, with 17 476 children (14.9%) in the ECEC cohort, 17 489 (14.9%) in the preschool cohort, and 13 232 (15.5%) in the daycare cohort living in the most disadvantaged areas, and 24 137 children (20.5%) in the ECEC cohort, 24 040 (20.5%) in the preschool cohort, and 17 753 (20.8%) in the daycare cohort living in the least disadvantaged areas.

Preschool attendance was 95.0% (111 650 children) among children with complete preschool data, whereas daycare attendance increased from 23.1% (4362 children) in 2009 to 37.1% (6478 children) in 2021. Attendance in both preschool and daycare was lower among children from socioeconomically disadvantaged areas (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). For example, ECEC attendance was 94.0% (16 427 children) among children in the most disadvantaged SEIFA group compared with 97.1% (23 446 children) in the least disadvantaged group, and daycare attendance was 22.7% (3000 children) in the most disadvantaged SEIFA group vs 32.5% (5762 children) in the least disadvantaged group.

Developmental Vulnerability by Early Childhood Education Programs

Among children included in the ECEC analysis, the prevalence of DV1 was 20.0% (95% CI, 19.8%-20.3%) among ECEC attenders compared with 33.1% (95% CI, 31.7%-34.5%) among nonattenders; the corresponding proportions for DV2 were 9.8% (95% CI, 9.6%-9.9%) and 19.6% (95% CI, 18.5%-20.8%), respectively. A similar pattern was observed for preschool attendance, with 19.9% (95% CI, 19.7%-20.1%) of preschool attenders for DV1 vs 33.0% (95% CI, 31.8%-34.2%) of nonattenders, with corresponding numbers of 9.7% (95% CI, 9.5%-9.8%) vs 19.2% (95% CI, 18.3%-20.3%) for DV2.

Across all 5 AEDC domains and census waves, children who attended ECEC or preschool had lower developmental vulnerability than nonattenders (Figure 2). In contrast, daycare attendance showed a mixed pattern: children attending daycare had higher developmental vulnerability in social competence (8.8% [95% CI, 8.5%-9.2%] vs 7.3% [95% CI, 7.1%-7.5%]) and emotional maturity (10.5% [95% CI, 10.2%-10.9%] vs 7.7% [95% CI, 7.5%-7.9%]), but lower developmental vulnerability in language and cognitive skills (school-based) (5.6% [95% CI, 5.3%-5.9%] vs 8.8% [95% CI, 8.6%-9.1%]) and communication skills and general knowledge (5.5% [95% CI, 5.2%-5.8%] vs 7.9% [95% CI, 7.7%-8.1%]) compared with children who did not attend daycare (eTable 3 in Supplement 1).

Association Between ECEC and Developmental Vulnerability

In adjusted analyses, ECEC attendance before school entry was associated with a lower risk of developmental vulnerability, including DV1 (aRR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.74-0.82; absolute risk difference, −5.6 percentage points) and DV2 (aRR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.62-0.72; absolute risk difference, −4.5 percentage points). ECEC attendance was associated with lower developmental vulnerability across all 5 developmental domains, including physical health and well-being (aRR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.65-0.76), social competence (aRR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.68-0.83), emotional maturity (aRR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.83-0.99), language and cognitive skills (school-based) (aRR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.53-0.61), and communication skills and general knowledge (aRR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.56-0.66) (Figure 3 and eTable 4 in Supplement 1).

Association of Preschool and Daycare With Developmental Vulnerability

Preschool attendance was consistently associated with lower risk of DV1 (aRR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.72-0.79) and DV2 (aRR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.62-0.70). Preschool attendance was also associated with lower developmental vulnerability across all 5 AEDC domains: physical health and well-being (aRR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.65-0.74), social competence (aRR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.65-0.76), emotional maturity (aRR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.78-0.91), language and cognitive skills (school-based) (aRR, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.51-0.59), and communication skills and general knowledge (aRR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.57-0.66) (Table 2).

Additional Analyses of Overlapping Exposure

In supplementary analyses that examined overlapping and restricted exposure patterns, attendance at both preschool and daycare was associated with higher risk of DV1 (aRR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.03-1.09) and DV2 (aRR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.05-1.15), as well as higher developmental vulnerability in social competence (aRR, 1.23; 95% CI, 1.17-1.29) and emotional maturity (aRR, 1.44; 95% CI, 1.38-1.50). In contrast, preschool-only attendance remained consistently protective across all outcomes, whereas daycare-only attendance was not associated with DV1 (aRR, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.89-1.15) or DV2 (aRR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.88-1.28), with wide 95% CIs suggesting limited precision (eTable 5 in Supplement 1).

Robustness to Unmeasured Confounding

E-values showed moderate robustness to unmeasured confounding. For DV1, the E-value was 1.90 (lower confidence limit, 1.75), increasing to 2.35 (lower confidence limit, 2.13) for DV2. Across developmental domains, E-values ranged from 1.44 to 2.94, with largest values observed for language and cognitive skills (school-based) (2.94; lower confidence limit, 2.69) and communication skills and general knowledge (2.67; lower confidence limit, 2.40). In contrast, emotional maturity showed lower E-values 1.44 (lower confidence limit, 1.09), indicating greater susceptibility to residual confounding (eTable 4 in Supplement 1).

Sensitivity and Subgroup Analysis

The nonclustered modified Poisson models used in sensitivity analyses produced results similar to those of the primary mixed-effects models (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). Subgroup analyses indicated that the protective association of ECEC was slightly greater among female children compared with male children. This protective association remained consistent across socioeconomic disadvantage (SEIFA) and geographic remoteness categories (eTables 7-9 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this population-based cohort study of WA children, attendance at ECEC programs, particularly preschool, was associated with lower developmental vulnerability across all domains at school entry. These findings extend prior Australian and international evidence by demonstrating consistent associations at the population level and by differentiating between preschool and daycare settings.14,15,35 The results indicate the importance of early educational experiences during a critical developmental period for school readiness.

ECEC attendance was associated with lower developmental vulnerability across all 5 AEDC domains. Preschool attendance showed consistent associations with lower developmental vulnerability across all domains, supporting evidence that participation in preschool programs in the year before school entry is associated with improved developmental outcomes. These findings are consistent with prior population-based studies and reinforce the role of preschool as a key component of early childhood development and school readiness.33,36,37

The protective association of ECEC was higher among female than male children. This difference may reflect gender-related variations in early childhood development, particularly in social and emotional domains. Evidence suggests that female children may benefit more from structured early learning environments because of differences in early language acquisition, self-regulation, and responsiveness to social interactions.38 In contrast, male children are more likely to exhibit externalizing behaviors and may require more targeted or supportive environments to achieve comparable developmental gains.39

In contrast, daycare attendance was associated with lower developmental vulnerability in language and cognitive skills (school-based) and communication skills and general knowledge, but higher vulnerability in social competence and emotional maturity. Several factors may explain this pattern. Differences in age at entry, duration and intensity of care, and variation in family and contextual factors may influence socioemotional development. Earlier and prolonged exposure to group care environments may affect opportunities for stable caregiver interactions and responsive social engagement during sensitive developmental periods.28,40 In addition, underlying differences in family circumstances may contribute to the observed associations, independently of daycare attendance itself.41 Within the AEDC framework, social competence and emotional maturity are assessed as separate domains, although they are often interpreted together as part of broader socioemotional development.

Despite policy initiatives aimed at improving universal preschool access, participation gaps persist, particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged and geographically remote populations.42 Our findings align with previous evidence showing lower participation among disadvantaged groups and persistent developmental inequalities.10,43 Subgroup analyses further indicated that ECEC attendance was associated with lower developmental vulnerability across all socioeconomic groups, with slightly greater protective associations among children from disadvantaged areas, reinforcing the role of ECEC as an equity-promoting intervention. Although socioeconomic disadvantage was adjusted in the models, these patterns highlight underlying structural inequities in both ECEC participation and child development. Although our analyses did not directly measure program characteristics, the observed associations suggest that differences in patterns of ECEC participation may contribute to disparities in school readiness.4,44

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, including its large, population-based design and the use of linked administrative datasets spanning multiple census waves. The analysis accounted for a wide range of child, maternal, and contextual factors and used mixed-effects modified Poisson regression models to estimate RRs. The inclusion of multiple developmental domains allowed for a nuanced assessment of heterogeneity across outcomes.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, although we adjusted for a broad range of child, maternal, and health-related factors, residual confounding from unmeasured family-level characteristics, including parental education, household income, home learning environment, and parental mental health, remains possible. Such factors may influence both ECEC participation and developmental outcomes. Although E-value analyses suggested moderate robustness to unmeasured confounding, the protective associations observed for preschool attendance and the higher developmental vulnerability observed among daycare attenders should be interpreted cautiously. Second, preschool and daycare attendance were not mutually exclusive exposures, and some children attended both settings before school entry. Although supplementary analyses produced findings consistent with the primary results, residual confounding related to overlapping participation patterns cannot be excluded. Third, information on ECEC quality, intensity, duration, and timing of attendance was unavailable, limiting our ability to evaluate dose-response relationships or differences according to program characteristics. Fourth, developmental outcomes were assessed using teacher-completed AEDC measures at a single time point at school entry, which may be subject to reporting variability and do not capture developmental trajectories over time.

Conclusions

In this population-based cohort study, attendance at early childhood education programs, particularly preschool, was associated with lower developmental vulnerability at school entry. Daycare attendance showed heterogeneous associations across developmental domains. These findings highlight the importance of equitable access to early childhood education to support school readiness and child development. Future research using longitudinal and quasi-experimental designs, incorporating measures of exposure quality, duration, and timing alongside detailed family-level factors and advanced predictive modeling approaches, is needed to clarify causal pathways and identify key drivers of developmental vulnerability.
