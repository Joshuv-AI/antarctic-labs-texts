Clinical implications of platelet reactivity after antiplatelet initiation in patients with acute ischaemic stroke
Authors: Teck Long King, Shirley Siang Ning Tan, Lee Len Tiong, Irene Yee Yew Chieng, Wan Chung Law
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh on-treatment platelet reactivity (HOTPR) may undermine the efficacy of antiplatelet therapy in acute stroke. This study aimed to assess platelet reactivity and HOTPR status during the acute phase of ischaemic stroke following antiplatelet initiation, and to determine their associations with stroke outcomes in a Malaysian cohort.MethodsThis prospective, observational study enrolled patients with acute ischaemic stroke at Sarawak General Hospital. Platelet reactivity was measured at baseline and on Days 1 and 3 post-antiplatelet therapy using Multiplate® Analyser. HOTPR was defined by ASPItest (> 30 U) or ADPtest (> 46 U) values. The primary outcome was recurrent stroke at one year, while secondary outcomes included early neurological deterioration (END), poor functional outcome (mRS ≥ 2) at three months, and all-cause mortality at one year. Cox and logistic regression models adjusted for clinical covariates were used, with Firth’s correction applied for rare events and model non-convergence.ResultsA total of 198 participants were enrolled, with a mean age of 59.8 years; 66.7% were male. At one year, 9.1% experienced recurrent stroke, 9.6% had died, 9.2% experienced END during admission, and 21.6% had mRS ≥ 2 at three months. HOTPR rates decreased by Day 3 (aspirin: Day 1 20.0% to Day 3 6.6%; clopidogrel: 50.5–28.0%), with persistent HOTPR in 11.8% (aspirin) and 39.2% (clopidogrel). Some initially normal responders developed HOTPR by Day 3 (aspirin 5.4%, clopidogrel 16.3%). Platelet reactivity and HOTPR status on Days 1 and 3 were not significantly associated with recurrent stroke at one year or END. However, higher ADPtest values on Day 1 [OR 1.05 (95% CI 1.00, 1.09), p = 0.037], clopidogrel HOTPR on Days 1 and 3 [OR 20.99 (95% CI 1.53, 7089.91), p = 0.017; OR 16.58 (95% CI 1.57, 24326.82), p = 0.017], and persistent clopidogrel HOTPR on both days [OR 45.25 (95% CI 2.34, 21861.76), p = 0.007] were significantly associated with poor functional outcomes.ConclusionPlatelet reactivity and HOTPR status were not associated with recurrent stroke and END. However, higher ADPtest values and clopidogrel-related HOTPR were associated with poor functional outcomes. Further studies are needed to evaluate the clinical utility of platelet reactivity monitoring in acute stroke management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04396-x.Keywords: Acute ischemic stroke, Antiplatelet therapy, Platelet reactivity, High on-treatment platelet reactivity, Stroke outcomes, Recurrent stroke, Early neurological deterioration, Mortality

Introduction

Stroke remains a major cause of mortality and morbidity globally [1]. In Malaysia, it is the third leading cause of death [2], with approximately half of stroke survivors discharged with varying degrees of disability, and readmission rates within the first month post-stroke ranging between 11% and 13% [3–5].

Antiplatelet therapy is the cornerstone of treatment for non-cardioembolic acute stroke, significantly reducing the risk of recurrent stroke and mortality post stroke [6–8]. Recently, dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), combining aspirin with a P2Y12 Inhibitor such as clopidogrel or ticagrelor, has been recommended for short-term management following acute stroke. Landmark trials like CHANCE, POINT, THALES, and INSPIRES have demonstrated that DAPT reduces the risk of secondary stroke more effectively than aspirin alone in patients with acute minor stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA) [9–12]. However, studies showed that 5.7–17.7% of stroke patients continued to have recurrent event within the first-year post-stroke [13]. This may be attributed, in part, to antiplatelet resistance, also known as high on-treatment platelet reactivity (HOTPR), where platelet aggregation is inadequately reduced despite antiplatelet therapy [14].

The prevalence of antiplatelet HOTPR varies widely, depending on assays used and the populations studied [15, 16]. A Malaysian study reported a HOTPR rate of 46.7% among 90 healthy volunteers given a dose of 300 mg clopidogrel dose [17], while a study in Mainland China found a 40% rate of clopidogrel resistance in ischaemic stroke patients [18].

Early initiation of antiplatelet therapy, often with a loading dose followed by a maintenance dose, is recommended in acute ischaemic stroke [19]. However, a significant proportion of acute stroke patients continue to have high residual platelet reactivity or HOTPR [20, 21], which is associated with an increased risk of vascular events [21]. Moreover, variability in on-treatment platelet reactivity during acute stroke may correlate with clinical events. For instance, increasing reactivity to aspirin has been associated with early neurological deterioration, while persistent HOTPR is linked to a higher risk of vascular events [21, 22].

The role of on-treatment platelet reactivity in acute stroke patients in determining clinical outcomes post-stroke has been an area of active research. While Multiplate® Analyser has proven useful in predicting bleeding and stent thrombosis in patients undergoing cardiac procedures [23, 24], its roles in ischaemic stroke outcomes remains uncertain. A literature search identified two prospective studies that were unable to establish the clinical utility of platelet reactivity assessments using Multiplate® Analyser in predicting stroke outcomes due to low event rates [25, 26].

This study aimed to assess platelet reactivity measurements, HOTPR status and their temporal changes, as assessed by Multiplate® analyser, and their associations with recurrent events, early neurological deteriorations (END), poor functional outcome, and mortality following the initiation of antiplatelet therapy in patients with acute ischaemic stroke in a Malaysian patient cohort.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a prospective, observational study conducted on patients with acute ischemic stroke who were admitted to Sarawak General Hospital (SGH). The primary objective was to determine associations of platelet reactivity or HOTPR status during acute stroke with recurrent events at one year. Secondary outcomes, including END, poor functional outcome at three months, and all-cause mortality at one year, were also evaluated.

The study was approved by the Medical Research & Ethics Committee, Ministry of Health Malaysia (NMRR-16-2206-33140) and conducted in compliance with the Malaysian Guideline for Good Clinical Practice [27] and the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorised representatives.

Patients were enrolled within 24 h of admission to the emergency department (ED). Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, a diagnosis of acute ischaemic stroke, and symptom onset within 48 h. Exclusion criteria included brain haemorrhage, cardioembolic stroke (as per the Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment [TOAST] classification), recent thrombolysis (within 30 days), concomitant anticoagulant use, long-term nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, platelet or bleeding disorders, chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²), chronic liver disease, thrombocytopenia (< 90 × 10⁹ platelets/L), and low haematocrit (< 29%).

Data collection

Data were collected on demographics, cardiovascular risk factors (i.e., hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes mellitus, smoking, alcohol consumption, history of stroke or TIA, and acute coronary syndrome), stroke classification based on the Oxfordshire Community Stroke Project (OCSP), aetiology classification based on TOAST, and antiplatelet therapy details from patient medical records. Stroke severity was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) at baseline and daily during hospitalisation, up to Day 5 or discharge, whichever occurred first [28].

Antiplatelet therapy

Patients admitted with acute ischaemic stroke were prescribed an antiplatelet regimen in accordance with standard practice at SGH. At SGH, patients diagnosed with acute ischaemic stroke with NIHSS ≤ 3 were routinely initiated on short-term DAPT, followed by long-term single antiplatelet therapy. These patients received a loading dose of 300 mg aspirin and 300 mg clopidogrel, followed by maintenance dose of 75 mg of both agents for three weeks. The patients were then maintained with life-long antiplatelet therapy of either clopidogrel or aspirin monotherapy.

For patients with more severe stroke i.e., NIHSS ≥ 4, those who were aspirin-naïve were given 300 mg aspirin once daily for two weeks, then 150 mg once daily for two weeks, followed by lifelong 75 mg once daily. Patients already on aspirin prior to their stroke with an NIHSS ≥ 4 received a 300 mg loading dose of clopidogrel, followed by lifelong 75 mg clopidogrel once daily.

Platelet function test

Platelet reactivity was assessed using Multiplate® Analyser, a point-of-care platelet function test based on whole blood impedance aggregometry. Tests were performed at baseline (upon presentation at ED), and on Days 1 and 3 of antiplatelet therapy during hospitalisation. The ASPItest was used to measure the effect of aspirin on platelet reactivity via arachidonic acid-induced platelet activation, while the ADPtest assessed clopidogrel’s efficacy through adenosine diphosphate receptor activation. High on-treatment platelet reactivity (HOTPR) was defined as an ASPItest value > 30 units (U) while on aspirin or an ADPtest value > 46 U while on clopidogrel, based on established cut-off consensus [29, 30].

To examine HOTPR changes during acute phase of stroke, patients were categorised into four groups based on their HOTPR status on Days 1 and 3. Group 1 had normal platelet reactivity (ASPItest ≤ 30 U or ADPtest ≤ 46 U) on both days; Group 2 had normal platelet reactivity on Day 1 but HOTPR on Day 3; Group 3 had HOTPR on Day 1 but normal platelet reactivity on Day 3; Group 4 had HOTPR on both days.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint of the study was recurrent stroke within one year. Secondary endpoints included END within five days of admission, poor functional outcome at three months, and all-cause mortality at one year. END was defined as an increase in NIHSS of ≥ 4 points within the first five days of hospitalisation. Functional outcomes were graded using the modified Rankin scale (mRS) [31], with a score > 2 indicating poor functional outcomes. Recurrent stroke and all-cause mortality were recorded during one-year follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages, and continuous variables are expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR). Within-group difference of platelet reactivity at different time points were analysed using paired t-test, and between-group differences were assessed using independent t-test. Proportions of HOTPR at Days 1 and 3 were compared using the McNemar’s test.

Cox regression analysis was used to estimate hazard ratios for recurrent stroke and all-cause mortality at one year. Logistic regression was used to assess the association of platelet reactivity and HOTPR status with END and poor functional outcomes (mRS > 2). The multivariable models were adjusted for clinically significant covariates, including age, sex, prior stroke or TIA, initial NIHSS, prior antiplatelet use, and DAPT after stroke. Statistical analysis was performed in R version 4.4.1 [32]. The significance level was set at p < 0.05 for all statistical tests.

Firth’s bias-reduced correction was applied to both Cox proportional hazard and logistic regression models using the R packages “coxphf” (version 1.13.4) [33] and “logistf” (version 1.26.0) [34]. This correction is used to mitigate the problem of separation, which can lead to infinite or extreme estimates in certain models. Firth’s method reduces bias in the estimation of parameters based on the profile penalised likelihood method [35].

In addition, sensitivity analysis was conducted using a reduced model that included only age, sex, initial NIHSS, and ASPItest/ADPtest variables.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total 198 patients with acute ischaemic stroke were enrolled between May 2017 and August 2019. The majority had lacunar infarcts (67.0%), with a mean age of 59.8 years (SD 12.8), and most were male (66.7%). Hypertension was prevalent (61.1%), while about 12% had a history of stroke or TIA. Prior to admission, 17% were on antiplatelet therapy (25 on aspirin alone, six on clopidogrel alone, and two on DAPT). The median NIHSS on arrival was 4 (IQR 5) (Table 1).

Platelet reactivity and HOTPR status during acute stroke

At ED, 96 patients (48.5%) were given aspirin only, 9 patients (4.5%) received clopidogrel only, and 92 patients (47.0%) were treated with DAPT (Table 1). At baseline, ASPItest was performed in 189 patients who had received aspirin, and ADPtest in 103 patients who had received clopidogrel. By Day 3, ASPItest was performed in 183 patients on aspirin, and ADPtest in 104 patients on clopidogrel. However, the Day 3 ADPtest results of four patients who were switched from aspirin monotherapy to clopidogrel or DAPT during hospitalisation were excluded from analysis. These patients had received only one or two doses of clopidogrel prior to testing, resulting in suboptimal drug exposure. Their inclusion would likely have led to overestimation of platelet reactivity and the proportion of clopidogrel HOTPR. To minimise this potential bias, their results were excluded. Further details on platelet function testing and missing data are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Following antiplatelet initiation, mean platelet reactivity levels on Day 1 were significantly reduced to 23.4 (15.2) U for ASPItest (p < 0.001) and 50.5 (21.6) U for ADPtest (p < 0.001). Further reductions were observed by Day 3, with ASPItest levels decreasing to 19.8 (8.9) U (p = 0.002) and ADPtest levels to 37.6 (18.0) U (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

On Day 1, approximately 20% of patients exhibited aspirin HOTPR, while 50.5% had clopidogrel HOTPR. By Day 3, these rates significantly decreased to 6.6% (p < 0.001) for aspirin and 28.0% for clopidogrel (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Among patients with normal on-treatment platelet reactivity on Day 1, 5.4% of those taking aspirin and 16.3% of those on clopidogrel developed HOTPR by Day 3. Conversely, of those with HOTPR on Day 1, 88.2% on aspirin and 60.8% on clopidogrel converted to normal on-treatment platelet reactivity by Day 3 (Table 2).

Changes in high on-treatment platelet reactivity (HOTPR) status from Day 1 to Day 3

When comparing between antiplatelet monotherapy and DAPT, patients on aspirin monotherapy had significantly higher ASPItest values on Day 3 than those on DAPT [21.4 (8.6) U vs. 18.3 (9.0) U, p = 0.018]. However, no significant differences were found for ADPtest values or HOTPR status for either aspirin or clopidogrel on Days 1 and 3 between the monotherapy and DAPT groups (Supplementary Table S3).

Stroke outcomes

At one-year follow-up, among 198 patients enrolled, 18 patients (9.1%) were readmitted for recurrent stroke, and 19 patients (9.6%) had died. For functional outcomes, 37 of 171 patients (21.6%) had an mRS score ≥ 2 at three-month. In terms of acute outcomes, 18 of 196 patients (9.2%) showed END within the first five days of admission.

For the primary endpoint, neither platelet reactivity levels nor HOTPR status on Days 1 and 3 were significantly associated with recurrent stroke at one year. Changes in HOTPR status also did not significantly impact the recurrence rate. (Table 3).

For secondary endpoints, higher ADPtest levels on Day 1 [OR 1.05 (95% CI 1.01, 1.10), p = 0.037] and HOTPR while on clopidogrel on Days 1 and 3 [OR 20.99 (95% CI 1.53, 7089.91), p = 0.017; OR 16.58 (95% CI 1.57, 24326.82), p = 0.017] were significantly associated with a poor functional outcome (mRS > 2) at three months (Table 4). In addition, compared to patients with normal platelet reactivity on both Days 1 and 3, those whose HOTPR persisted across both days had a significantly higher risk of poor functional outcomes [OR 45.25 (95% CI 2.34, 21861.76), p = 0.037, Table 4]. Furthermore, patients with HOTPR on Day 1 who converted to normal on-treatment ASPItest results by Day 3 had a lower risk of one-year all-cause mortality [OR 0.09 (95% CI 0.001, 0.85), p = 0.031, Table 5]. No significant associations were found between platelet reactivity levels, HOTPR status, or their variability and END (Table 6). Sensitivity analyses using a reduced model yielded consistent results with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

The current study demonstrated a significant reduction in platelet reactivity following antiplatelet therapy initiation after the loading dose, with further reductions observed after subsequent doses. Despite these reductions, a notable high rate of HOTPR persisted, particularly among patients on clopidogrel. The HOTPR rate for clopidogrel was consistent previous findings in acute ischaemic stroke and TIA patients using Multiplate® Analyser [36]. In contrast, the HOTPR rate for aspirin (6.6%) in our cohort was lower than previously reported rates of 11.1–11.6% using the same assay [20, 37]. This could be attributed to the higher initial aspirin doses used in our centre (300 mg for two weeks) among patients on aspirin monotherapy, as well as a high proportion of patients on DAPT. A study has shown that poor response to aspirin or clopidogrel can be overcome by increasing the antiplatelet dose [38]. We also found that patients on DAPT in the current study had a significantly lower ASPItest value compared to those taking aspirin only (Supplementary Table S3). Studies have reported that the addition of clopidogrel to aspirin in the dual therapy provides superior efficacy in reducing stroke recurrence, albeit at the expense of an increased bleeding risk [7], suggesting an overall increase in platelet inhibition. However, aspirin and clopidogrel target different platelet aggregation pathways. One study demonstrated that while clopidogrel reduces ADP-induced and collagen-induced platelet aggregation, it does not significantly enhance arachidonic acid-induced platelet inhibition in dual therapy. Other contributing factors to these variations could include the timing of testing, genetic polymorphisms, and drug interactions [39].

High platelet reactivity in patients on antiplatelet therapy has been associated with outcomes after stroke, such as increased risk of recurrent vascular events, END, and more severe stroke [40, 41]. Elevated platelet reactivity in the acute phase, especially within the first five days following aspirin initiation, correlates with an increased risk of END [22]. Similarly, patients with HOTPR following antiplatelet initiation for stroke have been shown to had higher risk of subsequent vascular events post-stroke [21, 42, 43]. Despite these previous findings, our study did not find a significant association between platelet reactivity or HOTPR status and recurrent stroke or END at one year. It is worth noting that most of these studies used platelet function assays such as optical aggregometry, PFA-100, or VerifyNow®, which differ from the Multiplate® Analyser used in our study. The Multiplate® system is based on whole blood impedance aggregometry. However, we did observe that higher ADPtest values and HOTPR status on clopidogrel were signficantly associated with poorer functional outcome at three months, but not with ASPItest and aspirin HOTPR. This is consistent with the findings of Yi et al., who reported that clopidogrel non-responders had significantly higher rates of ischaemic vascular events and worse mRS scores at six months [18]. While we also noted that patients who experienced recurrent events tended to have higher ADPtest values during acute stroke after clopidogrel administration, these differences did not reach statistical significance.

Kim et al., demonstrated that persistent HOTPR in patients on aspirin by the fifth day, had a significantly higher rate of composite outcome, including stroke, myocardial infarction, and vascular death [22]. In contrast, in our study, we found that persistent HOTPR from Day 1 to Day 3 while on clopidogrel—but not aspirin—was significantly associated with poor functional outcomes at three months. Interestingly, we noted a potential protective effect in patients who showed transient HOTPR on Day 1 but achieved normal platelet reactivity by Day 3 while on aspirin, as this was associated with a reduced risk of one-year post-stroke mortality. However, given the wide confidence intervals, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

This study has several strengths, including its prospective design and real-world setting within a Malaysian cohort. In addition, the study offers insights into the clinical utility of Multiplate® analyser as a platelet function assay in acute ischaemic stroke patients. The use of platelet function testing at multiple time points provides data into the temporal changes in platelet reactivity and its potential impact on clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, there are several potential limitations of the study. First, this was a single-centre study, with a relatively small sample size and low event rates, particularly in the subgroup of patients taking clopidogrel. This may have limited the power to detect significant associations, especially given the heterogeneity of stroke patients and the presence of various comorbidities that may have confounded clinical outcomes. Although adjustments were made for several covariates, other confounders such as genetic polymorphisms that influence drug metabolism (e.g., CYP2C19 for clopidogrel) and potential drug interactions (e.g. proton pump inhibitors) were not accounted for in the study. Lastly, while the cut-off values used for defining HOTPR were based on consensus [30, 44], they may not perfectly align with clinical outcomes across all populations. Current evidence from randomised trials is mixed, and the clinical utility of platelet function testing to guide antiplatelet therapy is yet to be clearly established [45, 46].

In conclusion, this prospective study provides valuable insights into platelet reactivity and its clinical implications in acute ischaemic stroke patients in Malaysia. While platelet reactivity significantly decreased following antiplatelet initiation, a considerable proportion of patients still had HOTPR, particularly with clopidogrel. Platelet reactivity and HOTPR status measured using Multiplate® Analyser did not predict the recurrent event or END. However, higher ADPtest values and persistent clopidogrel HOTPR were associated with poor functional outcomes, while transient aspirin HOTPR followed by normalisation showed a potential protective effect against one-year all-cause mortality. The wide confidence intervals in our results, however, underscore the need for larger, multi-centre studies to further elucidate these associations. Further investigations are warranted to establish the role of platelet function testing in guiding antiplatelet therapy in acute ischaemic stroke patients.
