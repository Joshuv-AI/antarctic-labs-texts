Impact of total neoadjuvant therapy regimens on radiation-induced lymphopenia in locally advanced rectal cancer: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Rie Sasaki, Senzo Taguchi, Hikaru Miyauchi, Yasuo Yoshioka, Eiji Shinozaki, Kensei Yamaguchi, Tomohiro Yamaguchi, Takashi Akiyoshi, Seno Satoshi, Takeaki Ishihara, Daisuke Miyawaki, Ryohei Sasaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTotal neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) improves oncological outcomes in locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC); however, treatment-induced lymphopenia remains a concern. We analyzed 74 patients undergoing three TNT regimens: long-course chemoradiotherapy with consolidation chemotherapy (LCCRT–CNCT), short-course radiotherapy with CNCT (SCRT–CNCT), and induction chemotherapy with LCCRT (INCT–LCCRT). Severe radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL, Grade ≥ 3) occurred in 48%, 24%, and 54%, respectively (P = 0.126). In the LCCRT–CNCT group, large bowel irradiation (V35 Gy > 46 cc) was significantly associated with severe RIL in univariable analysis but not in multivariable models (P = 0.227), and in an exploratory combined analysis of LCCRT–CNCT and INCT–LCCRT, this showed a trend (P = 0.093). Pre-TNT absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) was an independent predictor of RIL. Small bowel irradiation (V15 Gy > 104 cc) predicted severe lymphopenia during chemotherapy in the univariable analysis; but multivariable analysis suggested pre-TNT ALC as the main factor, showing a trend toward significance (P = 0.051). In the SCRT–CNCT group, pre-TNT ALC was the only significant factor for severe lymphopenia in both the RT and chemotherapy phases in univariable analysis. Severe RIL significantly prolonged lymphocyte recovery time (median, 283 vs. 76 days, P < 0.001), whereas immune recovery did not differ according to the TNT regimen. The median ALC at the last follow-up was 86% of the baseline value, indicating incomplete recovery. While pre-TNT ALC correlated with lymphopenia risk, minimizing bowel irradiation may help mitigate treatment-induced immunosuppression. Prospective studies are required to validate these findings.Keywords: total neoadjuvant therapy, locally advanced rectal cancer, radiation-induced lymphopenia, bowel radiation dose, lymphocyte recovery, immunosuppression

INTRODUCTION

Total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) has emerged as a promising treatment strategy for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC), with significant improvements in oncological outcomes. Trials on Rectal cancer And Preoperative Induction therapy followed by dedicated surgery (RAPIDO) and Organ Preservation for Rectal Adenocarcinoma (OPRA) have provided robust evidence supporting the benefits of TNT [1, 2]. In the RAPIDO trial, short-course radiotherapy (SCRT) followed by consolidation chemotherapy (CNCT) and total mesorectal excision (TME) was compared with conventional long-course chemoradiotherapy (LCCRT), demonstrating a significant reduction in distant metastasis risk and improved DFS [1]. Similarly, the OPRA trial evaluated the optimal sequencing of systemic chemotherapy and LCCRT and highlighted that LCCRT followed by CNCT led to higher clinical complete response rates, thus enabling a greater proportion of patients to pursue nonoperative management [2].

Despite these promising results, adoption of TNT in Japan remains limited. One possible reason for this is the lack of large-scale clinical trials that have evaluated the feasibility and oncological outcomes in the Japanese population. Furthermore, the 2024 Japanese treatment guidelines acknowledge that data on TNT from Japan are limited and provide a weak recommendation against its use. Japanese guidelines have traditionally emphasized TME with lateral pelvic lymph node dissection and adjuvant chemotherapy [3].

Additionally, it is crucial to examine the biological effects of TNT, particularly its effects on immune function and treatment-induced lymphopenia.

Radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL) is associated with poor outcomes in rectal cancer. The nadir absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) during neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CRT) has been proposed as a predictor of tumor response and survival, highlighting the critical role of immune status in treatment outcomes [4]. Moreover, persistent RIL following radiotherapy (RT) has been associated with prolonged immune suppression, which may compromise long-term tumor control and contribute to decreased overall survival [5]. Dose-volume parameters of the bone marrow have been shown to predict hematological toxicity in patients undergoing CRT [6]. However, the effect of bowel irradiation on lymphopenia remains unclear. The gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) plays a crucial role in immune regulation by hosting a significant proportion of lymphocytes in the body [7]. Further research is required to clarify whether bowel irradiation influences lymphopenia and its possible effects on immune function in patients with rectal cancer.

Although RIL have been studied extensively, their interaction with systemic chemotherapy in TNT regimens remains poorly understood. Considering that TNT may exert stronger immunosuppressive effects than RT alone, further research is required to clarify its effects on immune recovery. These findings may inform future research on the potential role of immune-based strategies in rectal cancer treatment [8]. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of lymphopenia on the incidence and recovery of TNT regimens, with the goal of minimizing treatment-related hematologic toxicity and optimizing radiation dose planning.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Design and patient selection

This retrospective study included Japanese patients with rectal cancer who underwent TNT at the Cancer Institute Hospital, Japanese Foundation for Cancer Research, between May 1, 2020 and January 31, 2023. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (approval number: 2024-GB-053), with informed consent waived due to its retrospective nature. The eligibility and exclusion criteria are summarized in Fig. 1. Of 132 Japanese patients who underwent TNT, 58 were excluded due to participation in clinical trials, treatment at other hospitals, limitations in RT technique, or receipt of non-standard chemotherapy regimens. Although focusing on LARC (stages II–III), the study included seven patients with stage I disease who were selected for organ-preservation and treated with TNT using a protocol similar to that used for patients with LARC, with minor variations in radiation dose. Since the study evaluated treatment-related adverse events rather than oncological outcomes, stage I cases were considered clinically equivalent regarding immunosuppressive effects and included in the analysis. The final analysis included 74 patients categorized into three TNT regimens: LCCRT followed by CNCT (LCCRT–CNCT, n = 33), SCRT followed by CNCT (SCRT–CNCT, n = 17), and induction chemotherapy (INCT) followed by LCCRT (INCT–LCCRT, n = 24). This classification facilitated comparison of outcomes across TNT regimens.

Treatment protocols

Radiotherapy

Patients underwent volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT) in prone position using a bellyboard and full bladder to minimize normal tissue exposure. Immobilization techniques and image-guided verification ensured accurate RT delivery. Prescribed doses and fractionation schemes varied by treatment group. In the LCCRT–CNCT group, patients received 50 Gy in 25 fractions (2 Gy/fraction). A total dose of 54 Gy in 27 fractions (2 Gy/fraction) was given to seven patients (six with T2N0 and one with T2N1) for organ preservation, with a simultaneous integrated boost (SIB) to the primary tumor. In these patients, 50 Gy was administered to the prophylactic area in 27 fractions (1.85 Gy/fraction). An example of an SIB-VMAT treatment plan is shown in Fig. 2.

In the SCRT–CNCT group, patients received 25 Gy in five fractions (5 Gy/fraction). In the INCT–LCCRT group, patients received 50 Gy in 25 fractions (2 Gy/fraction). Gross tumor volumes (GTVs), including primary tumor (GTV primary) and metastatic lymph nodes (GTV node), were determined using computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging. Clinical target volumes (CTVs) were defined as: a 15 mm cranial margin, 5–15 mm caudal margin, and 5 mm anterior–posterior and lateral margins around GTV primary (CTV primary); 5 mm margins around GTV node (CTV node), and inclusion of mesorectal, presacral, and internal iliac lymph nodes for prophylactic coverage (CTV subclinical). The superior border of CTVs was set at the sacral promontory, except in eight patients where it was placed at L4–L5 intervertebral space due to high lymph node metastasis risk, as determined by the physician. Planning target volumes (PTVs) were defined as 5–6 mm margins around CTV primary (PTV primary), 5 mm margins around CTV node (PTV node), and 4–5 mm margins around CTV subclinical (PTV subclinical). For organs at risk, Bone marrow (BM) was defined as three anatomical regions per Mell et al. [9]: (i) ilium, including iliac crests to superior border of femoral heads, (ii) lower pelvis, consisting of pubic bones, ischial bones, acetabulums, and proximal femurs, from superior border of femoral heads to inferior border of ischial tuberosities, and (iii) lumbosacral spine, from most superior vertebral body in PTV to entire sacrum. Small and large bowel volumes were contoured according to Gay et al. [10] guidelines. Small bowel is a noncircular tubular structure within the peritoneal cavity, typically delineated with oral contrast. Large bowel includes bowel loops superior to rectum, with lower border typically 15 cm above anal verge.

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy regimens were administered according to each TNT regimen. In the LCCRT–CNCT group, patients received oral capecitabine during LCCRT (825 mg/m2 twice daily), then six cycles of capecitabine (1000 mg/m2) and oxaliplatin (130 mg/m2 on Day 1 of a 21-day cycle). In the SCRT–CNCT group, patients received six capecitabine and oxaliplatin (CAPOX) cycles after SCRT. In the INCT–LCCRT group, patients received six CAPOX cycles, with bevacizumab as needed, followed by LCCRT with oral capecitabine. Bevacizumab use was based on favorable results from our institutional phase II trial on neoadjuvant chemotherapy, CRT, and laparoscopic surgery with selective lateral node dissection for poor-risk lower rectal cancer [11].

Blood sampling and timing of ALC assessments

The baseline absolute ALC before radiotherapy (RT) was defined as the value measured on RT initiation day or within the preceding period (median, 6 days; interquartile range [IQR], 6–21 days). The baseline ALC before chemotherapy was defined as the value measured on chemotherapy initiation day (median, 0 days; IQR, 0–1 days). During LCCRT, blood samples were collected once during RT, typically in weeks 2–3 (median, 2 weeks from RT start; IQR, 2–3 weeks), and again at the end of RT. During short-course RT (SCRT), blood sampling was not performed during the 5-day treatment course. Post-RT blood samples were first collected ~2 weeks after completion. As this was a retrospective study, the timing and frequency of blood tests during RT and chemotherapy varied based on clinical judgment. To standardize toxicity assessment, ALC measurements obtained within 30 days of RT or chemotherapy completion were considered treatment-related.

Endpoints and study variables

Primary Endpoint: The primary endpoint was RIL incidence (Grade ≥ 3), assessed using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v5.0. RIL was defined as lymphopenia developing during RT; however, in accordance with the blood sampling schedule, ALC measurements obtained within 30 days after RT completion were also included in the evaluation. Secondary endpoints included grade ≥ 3 lymphopenia incidence during chemotherapy. Risk factors for RIL grade ≥ 3 and lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 in the chemotherapy phase were assessed, including pre-TNT ALC and dose-volume parameters of BM, small bowel, and large bowel.

The influence of dose-volume parameters on RIL was assessed across groups. In the INCT–LCCRT group, where chemotherapy preceded RT, analysis examined whether chemotherapy adherence influenced RIL grade ≥ 3 occurrence. For dose-volume parameters showing significant association with RIL grade ≥ 3 and lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 during the chemotherapy phase, ROC analysis was used to determine the optimal dose threshold (Gy) for risk stratification in radiation dose planning. For dose-volume parameters (cc) without clear thresholds, the median value was used as cutoff for balanced statistical analysis. Chemotherapy dose adherence was evaluated during the chemotherapy phase, with assessments for capecitabine (≥ 90% or ≥ 75% of planned dose) and oxaliplatin (≥ 90% or ≥ 75% of planned dose) following OPRA trial definition [12].

In the LCCRT–CNCT and SCRT–CNCT groups, where RT preceded chemotherapy, an additional analysis was performed to determine whether RT dose-volume parameters influenced the occurrence of lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 during the chemotherapy phase. Additional endpoints included recovery time from RIL and percentage changes in ALC from pre-TNT to last follow-up. RIL recovery was defined as achieving the same lymphocyte grade as pre-TNT level regardless of ALC. Follow-up period was defined as the interval from RT end to the last blood test, either at routine follow-up, before chemotherapy initiation due to recurrence or progression, or before death.

Dose calculation and evaluation

Treatment plans were generated using Eclipse treatment planning system (version 15.6; Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). Dose calculations were performed using an anisotropic analytical algorithm (AAA, version 15.6) with a 2.5 mm calculation grid and heterogeneity correction. DVHs for target volumes and OARs were generated using the planning system.

For each OAR (bone marrow, small bowel, and large bowel), the following dose-volume parameters were extracted:

– For LCCRT–CNCT and INCT–LCCRT regimens: bone marrow was evaluated using relative volumes receiving 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, and 50 Gy (V5–V50, %), whereas the small and large bowel were assessed using absolute volumes (V5–V50, cc). In addition, the mean dose (Dmean, Gy) and total contoured volume (cc) were recorded.

– For SCRT–CNCT regimens: given the prescribed dose of 25 Gy, parameters included V5–V25 (% for bone marrow, cc for bowel), along with Dmean (Gy) and contoured volume (cc).

Target volume analysis and multivariable Modeling

In addition to OAR parameters, the clinical target volume (CTV) and planning target volume (PTV) were measured for each patient and analyzed as continuous variables. The pre-TNT ALC was also included as a candidate predictor.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at P-value <0.05. Methods were selected based on data type. For continuous variables, Mann–Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons, while Kruskal–Wallis test was used for three groups, followed by Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc test when significant. For categorical variables, two-group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test when expected cell counts were ≥ 5, and Fisher’s exact test when <5. For three-group comparisons, chi-square test evaluated overall differences, and Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test was used for multicategory comparisons when necessary. When statistical significance was observed, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using Cramér’s V test to measure effect sizes.

For correlated dose–volume parameters (e.g. multiple thresholds for the same organ), ROC analysis was performed to compare predictive ability, and the parameter with highest AUC was selected as the representative variable for inclusion in the main model. Other parameters were evaluated by sensitivity analysis. Dose-volume parameters were entered as continuous variables, with ROC-derived binary cutoffs used only for stratified analyses and figures. Variance inflation factors (all <2) confirmed no problematic collinearity.

For multivariable analysis, variables with P < 0.05 in univariable analysis were entered into a logistic regression model to identify independent predictors. Due to limited events, variables were restricted to avoid overfitting. Primary models incorporated variables based on clinical relevance and statistical significance. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess robustness by substituting variables. To improve statistical power for identifying RIL risk factors during LCCRT, exploratory multivariable analysis was also performed by combining patients from LCCRT–CNCT and INCT–LCCRT regimens. This approach requires cautious interpretation due to treatment sequence differences.

Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed to evaluate the time to recovery from RIL. Log-rank test was used to compare recovery time between groups categorized by RIL severity (Grade ≥ 3 vs. < 3) and across different TNT regimens (LCCRT–CNCT, SCRT–CNCT, INCT–LCCRT). Censored data were defined as patients who had not yet achieved recovery from RIL by the last available follow-up.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval number: 2024-GB-053). The requirement for patient consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study. All patient data were anonymized before the analysis to maintain confidentiality.

This chronological presentation allowed visualization of ALC changes, showing persistent lymphopenia after LCCRT–CNCT and marked recovery before LCCRT in the INCT–LCCRT regimen (exact values are provided in Supplementary Table S1). In the RT phase, RIL grade ≥ 3 frequency was 48% in LCCRT–CNCT, 24% in SCRT–CNCT, and 54% in INCT–LCCRT groups, though not statistically significant (P = 0.126). Factors associated with lymphopenia

Lymphopenia in the RT phase

Table 4 summarizes associations between pre-TNT ALC, radiation dose-volume parameters, and lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 in RT or chemotherapy phase. Results for all evaluated dose–volume parameters, including those without statistically significant associations, are presented in Supplementary Tables S2–S8. In the RT phase, lower pre-TNT ALC (P = 0.001), small bowel Dmean (P = 0.023) and higher large bowel radiation doses (V25–45 Gy, P = 0.023–0.037) were significantly associated with increased risk of RIL grade ≥ 3 in the LCCRT–CNCT group. To determine the optimal large bowel radiation dose threshold associated with an increased risk of RIL grade ≥ 3 in the LCCRT–CNCT group, ROC analysis was performed for large bowel V25, V30, V35, V40, and V45 Gy. Among these, V35 Gy showed the highest predictive ability (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.732), followed by V30 Gy (AUC = 0.728), V25 Gy (AUC = 0.724), V40 Gy (AUC = 0.724), and V45 Gy (AUC = 0.713), which indicated that these dose levels had moderate predictive accuracy for RIL grade 3. Given its superior predictive ability, V35 Gy was identified as the most suitable candidate for determining optimal dose-volume cutoff. Patients were divided into two groups based on median large bowel V35 Gy volume (46 cc), and RIL grade ≥ 3 incidence was compared. Results showed patients with V35 Gy > 46 cc had significantly higher RIL grade ≥ 3 incidence (71%) compared to those with V35 Gy ≤ 46 cc (25%) (OR = 7.2, 95% CI: 1.545–33.556, P = 0.009).

Lymphopenia in the chemotherapy phase

In the chemotherapy phase, a lower pre-TNT ALC (P = 0.001) and small bowel radiation doses (Dmean, V10–25 Gy, P = 0.020–0.044) were significantly associated with an increased risk of lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 in the LCCRT–CNCT group. As shown in Supplementary Table S1, ALC remained low after LCCRT and before CNCT, whereas in the INCT–LCCRT regimen, they recovered before LCCRT. To determine the optimal small bowel radiation dose threshold associated with an increased risk of lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 in the chemotherapy phase for the LCCRT–CNCT group, a ROC analysis was performed for small bowel V10, V15, V20, and V25 Gy. Among these thresholds, V15 and V20 Gy exhibited the highest predictive ability (AUC = 0.746), followed by V10 Gy (AUC = 0.734), Dmean (AUC = 0.722), and V25 Gy (AUC = 0.714), indicating moderate predictive accuracy for lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 in the chemotherapy phase. As V15 Gy and V20 Gy had identical AUC values, further testing determined the optimal dose-volume cutoff for risk stratification. Results showed patients with V15 Gy > 104 cc had a higher incidence of lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 (56%) compared to those with V15 Gy ≤ 104 cc (18%) (odds ratio [OR] = 6.0, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.223–29.445, P = 0.021). Similarly, patients with V20 Gy > 70 cc had a higher incidence of lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 (53%) compared to those with V20 Gy ≤ 70 cc (19%) (OR = 4.875, 95% CI: 1.008–23.568, P = 0.041). Although V15 Gy and V20 Gy had similar predictive accuracies, V15 Gy was chosen for further multivariable analysis as it provided a clinically conservative dose threshold and slightly stronger significance in univariate testing.

In multivariable analysis including pre-TNT ALC and small bowel V15 Gy, neither reached statistical significance (P = 0.051 and P = 0.252, respectively), though pre-TNT ALC showed a trend toward association. Sensitivity analyses replacing V15 Gy with V10, V20, and V25 Gy, or Dmean yielded consistent results; pre-TNT ALC remained a significant predictor (P = 0.040, 0.042, 0.034, and 0.045, respectively), whereas small-bowel dose parameters were not significant after adjustment. The variance inflation factors for all variables were < 2, indicating no problematic collinearity.

In the LCCRT–CNCT group, adherence to ≥90% of the planned dose of oxaliplatin (P = 0.047) or capecitabine (P = 0.041) was associated with a significantly lower incidence of lymphopenia grade ≥ 3. However, these factors were excluded from multivariable analyses due to potential confounding and selection bias, as treatment intensity and adverse events were inversely correlated in our cohort. In contrast, no significant associations were observed at ≥75% of the planned dose for either agent. Additionally, no significant associations were found for bone marrow or large bowel dose-volume parameters.

In the SCRT–CNCT group, a lower pre-TNT ALC was significantly associated with lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 in the chemotherapy phase (P = 0.010). Additionally, adherence to ≥90% of the planned dose of oxaliplatin (P = 0.029) or capecitabine (P = 0.015) was associated with a significantly lower incidence of lymphopenia grade ≥ 3; however, these factors were excluded from multivariable analyses due to confounding and selection bias. No significant associations were observed at ≥75% adherence or dose-volume parameters of BM, small bowel, or large bowel.

In the INCT–LCCRT group, no significant associations were observed between lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 in the chemotherapy phase and adherence to ≥90% or ≥ 75% of the planned doses of capecitabine or oxaliplatin. Additionally, no significant associations were found for pre-TNT ALC.

Time to recovery from RIL

Among the 68 patients who developed RIL, the median follow-up period was 958 days (interquartile range [IQR], 710–1192 days). As shown in Fig. 3, a Kaplan—Meier analysis was conducted for time to recovery from RIL. Patients with RIL grade ≥ 3 had a significantly longer median recovery time (283 days, 95% CI: 224–342) compared to those with grade < 3 RIL (76 days, 95% CI: 20–132) (P < 0.001). A subgroup analysis of TNT regimens showed no significant differences in median recovery time among the LCCRT–CNCT, SCRT–CNCT, and INCT–LCCRT groups (P = 0.620). The median time to recovery was 168 days (95% CI: 149–187) in the LCCRT–CNCT group, 200 days (95% CI: 0–453) in the SCRT–CNCT group, and 119 days (95% CI: 0–245) in the INCT–LCCRT group.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the impact of different TNT regimens on the subsequent immune recovery from RIL in patients with rectal cancer. In the RT phase, severe RIL frequency varied among TNT regimens, though differences were not statistically significant. While this study primarily focused on RIL during radiotherapy, RIL often persists in the chemotherapy phase, affecting immune status beyond RT. The lymphopenia during chemotherapy reflects combined radiation- and chemotherapy-induced immunosuppression. We included chemotherapy-phase lymphopenia analysis to capture these cumulative effects, providing clarity on how RT-induced immune injury affects recovery and toxicity across TNT regimens. During chemotherapy, severe lymphopenia occurred less frequently in the INCT–LCCRT group than in the LCCRT–CNCT group, indicating that the sequence of TNT regimens may influence immune status during the chemotherapy phase.

TNT may exert stronger immunosuppressive effects than CRT alone. In a previous study involving 33 patients with rectal cancer who underwent long-course CRT, grades 0, 1, 2, and 3 lymphopenia were observed in 1 (3%), 6 (18%), 12 (36%), and 14 (43%) patients, respectively [13]. Another study of 57 patients treated with long-course CRT showed grade ≥ 3 lymphopenia in 35% of patients [14]. In our study, the incidence of grade ≥ 3 RIL after LCCRT was 48% in the LCCRT–CNCT group and 54% in the INCT–LCCRT group, which were slightly higher but not markedly different from previous reports. As LCCRT–CNCT begins with CRT, toxicity after LCCRT reflects CRT phase effects. In the INCT–LCCRT regimen, while chemotherapy precedes CRT, most patients recovered before starting CRT despite initial lymphopenia. After INCT, grades 0, 1, 2, and 3 lymphopenia were observed in 11 (46%), 7 (29%), 5 (21%), and 1 (4%) patients, respectively. Before LCCRT, grades 0 and 1 lymphopenia were observed in 22 (92%) and 2 (8%) patients, respectively, showing marked recovery. This suggests that in TNT regimens with induction chemotherapy, lymphocyte impact is transient and does not worsen CRT-induced toxicity. In the LCCRT–CNCT group, grades 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 lymphopenia after LCCRT occurred in 1 (3%), 3 (9%), 13 (39%),15(46%), and 3 (9%) patients, respectively. Before CNCT, grades 0, 1, 2, and 3 lymphopenia were observed in 8 (24%), 6 (18%), 16 (49%), and 3 (9%) patients, respectively, indicating persistent lymphopenia after LCCRT (Supplementary Table S1). This persistence may have led to higher incidence of lymphopenia during CNCT. The incidence of grade ≥ 3 lymphopenia during chemotherapy was significantly higher in the LCCRT–CNCT group (36%) compared with the INCT–LCCRT group (4%) (P = 0.004). A similar pattern was observed in the SCRT–CNCT group, in which persistent lymphopenia was also noted before CNCT initiation (Supplementary Table S1). Although the shorter RT course and lower total dose in SCRT may result in less pronounced immune suppression compared with LCCRT, these findings suggest that RT-induced lymphopenia can persist regardless of fractionation schedule and should be considered when planning subsequent chemotherapy. Taken together, these findings indicate that in TNT regimens with induction chemotherapy (INCT–LCCRT), the risk of exacerbating subsequent CRT-induced lymphopenia is low. However, in regimens with CRT followed by consolidation chemotherapy (LCCRT–CNCT), CRT-induced lymphopenia often persists and may cause severe lymphopenia during subsequent chemotherapy, warranting careful monitoring.

Although previous studies have primarily examined the systemic effects of lymphopenia, limited research has explored how localized radiation exposure to specific organs influences immunosuppression. The association between bowel radiation dose and RIL identified in this study aligns with the findings in cervical cancer, where a higher bowel irradiation volume (V45) was a significant predictor of severe lymphopenia [15]. However, the effects of bowel irradiation on systemic immune function remain poorly understood, particularly in patients with rectal cancer.

Our study provides novel insights into these relationships. In the LCCRT–CNCT group, large bowel irradiation was strongly associated with severe RIL in univariate analysis, with large bowel V35 Gy > 46 cc identified as the optimal threshold (OR = 7.2, P = 0.009). However, multivariable logistic regression revealed that pre-TNT ALC was the only independent predictor of severe RIL (P = 0.047), while large bowel V35 Gy showed no significance (P = 0.227). To improve statistical power, an exploratory analysis combining the LCCRT–CNCT and INCT–LCCRT groups confirmed the significance of pre-TNT ALC (P = 0.004) and a non-significant trend for large bowel V35 Gy (P = 0.093).

In contrast, small bowel irradiation was significantly associated with severe lymphopenia in the chemotherapy phase under persistent RIL in univariate analysis; small bowel V15 Gy > 104 cc conferred increased risk (OR = 6.0, P = 0.021). Although this association was not consistently retained in multivariable analysis, pre-TNT ALC showed a strong trend toward significance (P = 0.051) at the V15 threshold and achieved statistical significance in sensitivity analyses. These findings suggest that baseline immune status plays a critical role in lymphopenia risk alongside organ-specific radiation exposure, though some uncertainty remains regarding the independent predictive power of pre-TNT ALC in this context.

Moreover, our findings expand on those reported by Nanos et al. who demonstrated that BM irradiation significantly contributes to treatment-induced lymphopenia [16]. While their study highlighted the role of the BM as a critical organ in hematologic toxicity, our results suggest that bowel irradiation, particularly of the large and small bowels, also plays a key role in the development of RIL. This further underscores the necessity of optimizing RT planning to minimize the immunosuppressive effects.

Additionally, pelvic irradiation may reduce circulating ALC through direct irradiation of major pelvic vessels such as the internal and external iliac arteries and veins, where a substantial proportion of the body’s lymphocytes recirculate [7]. This mechanism could contribute to systemic lymphopenia, independent of or in combination with radiation exposure to the lymphoid organs and the gastrointestinal tract.

A previous study in patients with non-small cell lung cancer treated with concurrent CRT demonstrated that pre-RT ALC was significantly associated with severe lymphopenia during RT [17]. Similarly, our study found a lower pre-TNT ALC significantly associated with lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 during RT and chemotherapy phases in LCCRT–CNCT and SCRT–CNCT groups. However, no significant association was found in the INCT–LCCRT group. This difference highlights varying immune response patterns across TNT regimens and suggests other factors may predominantly influence lymphopenia risk in the INCT–LCCRT group.

Contrary to the general assumption that higher chemotherapy intensity increases the risk of lymphopenia, our results indicated that patients who received ≥90% of the planned dose of capecitabine or oxaliplatin had a lower incidence of lymphopenia grade ≥ 3 in the chemotherapy phase. Because this finding is counterintuitive, an unknown selection bias may have influenced the results. Further investigations are necessary to confirm this observation.

A previous study in rectal cancer patients reported that those who developed severe RIL (Grade ≥ 3) exhibited persistent lymphopenia throughout one year of observation after CRT [14]. Similarly, our study demonstrated that patients with RIL grade ≥ 3 had significantly prolonged recovery compared with those with RIL grade < 3. This confirms that RIL severity plays a crucial role in determining the duration of immune recovery. Moreover, our study assessed the percentage change in the ALC in the entire patient cohort following TNT. At the last follow-up, the median ALC remained at 86% (IQR: 68%–103%) of the pre-TNT levels, indicating incomplete recovery in a substantial proportion of patients despite prolonged follow-up. This raises concerns about the potential long-term immunosuppressive effects of TNT. In the LCCRT–CNCT group, we hypothesized that small-bowel irradiation during RT phase may cause subclinical immune damage, contributing to lymphopenia during subsequent chemotherapy. This is supported by our finding that small bowel V15 Gy > 104 cc was significantly associated with severe lymphopenia during chemotherapy in univariate analysis. Although this association was not retained in multivariable analysis, the delayed immunological effects of bowel irradiation merit further investigation. Persistent RIL may sensitize lymphoid tissues in the gastrointestinal tract, including GALT, to additional cytotoxic insults during chemotherapy.

In the LCCRT group, ALC was measured only twice (during RT and at the end of RT), and in the SCRT group, ALC was first assessed ~2 weeks after RT. These limited and heterogeneous sampling schedules may not have fully captured lymphopenia occurring during RT, and thus could have led to an underestimation or misclassification of RIL in both groups.

Despite these insights, the small sample size and retrospective design may limit the generalizability of our findings. Therefore, prospective studies are required to validate these findings.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that differences in TNT regimens influenced the incidence of severe lymphopenia during the chemotherapy phase, although no significant difference was observed in severe RIL incidence. In the LCCRT–CNCT group, higher large bowel radiation doses were strongly associated with increased severe RIL risk in univariable analysis (large bowel V35 Gy > 46 cc; OR = 7.2, P = 0.009), while multivariable analysis showed a non-significant trend. Small bowel radiation doses (V15 Gy > 104 cc) were significantly associated with severe lymphopenia during chemotherapy in univariable analysis, though this association was not maintained in multivariable models. Additionally, pre-TNT ALC was identified as a significant predictor of severe lymphopenia in both the RT and chemotherapy phases in the LCCRT–CNCT and SCRT–CNCT groups, highlighting baseline immune assessment importance. While this study provides insights into bowel-sparing radiotherapy, limitations of our retrospective design require cautious interpretation. Continued research, particularly prospective trials, is essential to fully elucidate its clinical impact.
