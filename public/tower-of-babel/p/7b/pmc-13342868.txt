Effect of Music on Pain, Embarrassment, and Urodynamic Outcomes During Urodynamic Testing: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Yasemin Özhanlı, Havva Bozdemir, Züleyha Şimşek Yaban, Nagihan Sarı, Cüneyd Özkürkçügil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study investigated whether music affected pain, embarrassment, and urodynamic outcomes during invasive urodynamic testing.MethodsIn this randomized controlled trial, 94 patients were assigned to a music group (n=49) or a control group (n=45). Patients in the music group listened to slow-tempo classical instrumental music at 40–60 dB, whereas those in the control group received standard care. The primary outcome was procedural pain, which was measured using a visual analogue scale at 3 stages: uroflowmetry, positioning on the examination table, and catheter insertion. Secondary outcomes included embarrassment and objective urodynamic parameters. Nonparametric methods were used because of the data distribution.ResultsThe music group reported lower pain during uroflowmetry (T1; P=0.022) and positioning on the examination table (T2; P=0.041), although the absolute reductions were small. Pain did not differ significantly between groups during catheter insertion (T3). No significant between-group differences were found in embarrassment or urodynamic parameters (P>0.05).ConclusionsMusic may modestly reduce pain during some stages of invasive urodynamic testing, but it did not affect embarrassment or urodynamic outcomes. Because it is simple, safe, and low cost, music may be a useful supportive nursing intervention to improve patient comfort, although its clinical effect appears limited.

INTRODUCTION

Urodynamic testing (UDT) is a commonly performed outpatient procedure used to evaluate bladder and voiding function in patients with lower urinary tract dysfunction [1, 2]. Despite its diagnostic value, UDT is invasive and may cause pain, embarrassment, and anxiety because it involves urethral catheterization and exposure of intimate body areas [1, 3-5]. These sources of discomfort are clinically relevant because anxiety and distress may interfere with voiding dynamics and potentially affect pressure-flow measurements [6, 7]. Patient satisfaction and willingness to undergo repeat testing are also important considerations in clinical practice [8].

Music has been widely used as a nonpharmacological intervention to reduce anxiety and procedural discomfort. Previous studies have shown that music can reduce pain and anxiety during several invasive procedures, including cystoscopy and colonoscopy [9-12]. However, findings have not been consistent across urological interventions; some studies have reported no significant benefit during lithotripsy or prostate biopsy [13, 14]. This variability suggests that the effect of music may depend on the procedure.

In urodynamics, limited evidence is available regarding the effect of music not only on subjective outcomes such as pain and embarrassment but also on objective urodynamic parameters. Because anxiety may influence voiding function, it remains unclear whether music can modify pressure-flow outcomes in addition to improving patient comfort. Therefore, this randomized controlled trial evaluated the effect of music on procedural pain, the primary outcome, as well as embarrassment and urodynamic parameters during invasive UDT.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This prospective, parallel-group randomized controlled trial was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT072077 46). The study evaluated the effect of music during invasive UDT.

Objectives

The primary objective of this study was to determine whether listening to music reduced procedural pain during invasive UDT. The secondary objectives were to determine whether music exposure affected procedural embarrassment and urodynamic parameters.

Study Hypotheses

Primary hypothesis

H0: Procedural pain scores did not differ between the music and control groups during invasive UDT.

H1: Procedural pain scores differed between the music and control groups during invasive UDT.

Secondary hypothesis

H0: Embarrassment scores and urodynamic parameters did not differ between the music and control groups.

H1: Embarrassment scores and/or urodynamic parameters differed between the music and control groups.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this randomized controlled trial was procedural pain, measured using the visual analogue scale (VAS). Secondary outcomes included embarrassment, measured using the VAS for embarrassment, and objective urodynamic parameters, including filling volume, voiding volume, detrusor pressure at maximum flow, maximum flow rate, and postvoid residual volume.

Sample Size

The study population consisted of patients scheduled for invasive UDT at the Urodynamics Center of a university hospital between February 2024 and June 2025.

The primary outcome was procedural pain, assessed using the VAS. The sample size calculation was therefore based on between-group differences in VAS pain scores reported in a prior urodynamic study [15]. Power analysis was performed using G*Power version 3.1.9.2 (Kiel University, Germany), with a 2-tailed alpha of 0.05, 95% power, and an anticipated effect size of Cohen d=0.79. Based on these assumptions, 43 participants per group were required. To account for potential attrition and incomplete data, 50 patients were enrolled in each group.

Embarrassment scores and urodynamic parameters were predefined secondary outcomes. Because reliable effect-size estimates for these parameters are limited in the literature, the study was not specifically powered to detect small physiological differences. Analyses of these outcomes were therefore considered exploratory.

Eligibility Criteria

Participants were adults aged 18 years or older who were literate and scheduled for UDT. Patients with visual, auditory, or cognitive impairment or chronic pain were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria were sedation during the procedure, pregnancy, permanent urinary catheterization, recurrent or active urinary tract infection, an indication for urodynamics because of urinary retention, and prior UDT.

Randomization and Study Flow Diagram

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the music or control group using a computer-generated simple randomization sequence (Research Randomizer, www.randomizer.org).

The randomization list was generated in advance by an independent statistician who was not involved in recruitment or data collection.

Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes prepared by the statistician. After eligibility assessment and written informed consent, each participant received the next envelope in sequence. The procedural nurse opened the envelope immediately before the intervention, and group assignment was recorded in the study log. Allocation remained concealed from investigators involved in recruitment and outcome assessment until the intervention began.

Because of the nature of the intervention, participants and the procedural nurse were not blinded. However, the statistician who performed the data analysis remained blinded to group allocation until the analyses were completed.

A total of 106 patients were assessed for eligibility; 6 were excluded because 4 did not meet the inclusion criteria and 2 declined to participate. One hundred patients were randomized equally to the music group (n=50) or control group (n=50). During follow-up, 6 participants were excluded because of incomplete data: 1 in the music group and 5 in the control group. The final analysis included 49 patients in the music group and 45 in the control group (Fig. 1).

Procedure

After written informed consent was obtained, demographic and clinical data were collected, and baseline anxiety was assessed using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI). All participants underwent routine urinalysis before the procedure.

Both groups underwent standardized multichannel UDT in accordance with the International Continence Society Good Urodynamic Practice guidelines [16, 17]. The protocol began with uroflowmetry, followed by filling cystometry and voiding cystometry. Patients were positioned semirecumbently on the urodynamic chair. After local disinfection, an 8F cystometry catheter was inserted into the bladder to measure intravesical pressure, and a 4.5F rectal catheter was inserted to measure intra-abdominal pressure. Urodynamic parameters, including filling volume, voiding volume, detrusor pressure at maximum flow, maximum flow rate, and postvoid residual volume, were recorded.

Participants in the music group listened to standardized classical instrumental music at 60–80 beats per minute and 40–60 dB throughout the procedure, which lasted approximately 30 minutes [5, 8]. Participants in the control group received standard care without music. Because the intervention was audible, participant blinding was not feasible.

Procedural pain, the primary outcome, and embarrassment, the secondary outcome, were assessed using a VAS at 3 predefined stages: during uroflowmetry (first time point, T1), while the patient was positioned on the examination table (second time point, T2), and during catheter insertion (third time point, T3). These time points were selected on the basis of previous evidence indicating higher discomfort and embarrassment during these procedural stages [3-5].

Data Collection Forms

Data were collected using a structured information form, the BAI, and the VAS.

Personal information form

This form was prepared by the researchers in accordance with the literature [1, 2] and included questions on patients’ sociodemographic characteristics and problems related to urination.

Visual analogue scale

The VAS was used to quantify subjective variables that could not be measured directly. Pain and embarrassment were assessed using a 100-mm VAS, on which 0 indicated the absence of the assessed parameter and 10 indicated the highest level of that parameter. In this study, patients’ pain and embarrassment levels were recorded as VAS_pain (VAS-P) and VAS_embarrassment (VAS-E) scores, respectively.

Beck Anxiety Inventory

The BAI measures anxiety symptoms. The scale is a 21-item Likert-type instrument, with each item scored from 0 to 3. A higher total score indicates more severe anxiety symptoms. The BAI was developed by Beck et al. [18], and its Turkish validity and reliability were evaluated by Ulusoy et al. [19]. In the original scale, anxiety scores were categorized as normal, low, moderate, and severe. In this study, anxiety was categorized as ≤moderate or ≥severe by combining categories to improve group homogeneity. The Cronbach alpha internal consistency coefficient was 0.93.

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 25.0 (IBM Co., USA). Continuous variables were summarized as mean±standard deviation or median and interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Analyses were performed using complete-case data. Six participants, 1 in the music group and 5 in the control group, were excluded from the final analyses because of incomplete outcome measurements. No imputation was performed because the proportion of missing data was low and missingness was not related to group assignment. Normality of continuous dependent variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, with P>0.05 indicating a normal distribution, and was confirmed by visual inspection of histograms and boxplots. Because most continuous variables did not meet the normality assumption, nonparametric tests were used. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate, for categorical variables. Within-group comparisons across repeated measurements were performed using the Friedman test. Effect sizes for Mann-Whitney U analyses were reported as r in accordance with current reporting guidelines [20]. Because the primary analyses relied on nonparametric tests, namely the Mann-Whitney U and Friedman tests, confidence intervals were not calculated. Instead, effect sizes were reported to improve the interpretability of between-group differences. All statistical tests were 2-tailed, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Aspects of the Study

Ethical approval was obtained from the Kocaeli University Ethics Committee for Interventional Research (Project No: 2023/385; Decision No: KÜ GOKAEK-2024/01.03; Date: 18 January 2024). All patients received detailed information about the study before participation, and verbal and written consent was obtained. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [21].

RESULTS

The demographic characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between the music and control groups in age, body mass index, sex, marital status, education level, or baseline anxiety, as measured using the BAI (P>0.05). These findings indicate that the groups were comparable at baseline (Table 1).

Levels of Pain (VAS-P)

Between-group comparisons showed that the music group reported significantly lower pain scores than the control group during uroflowmetry (T1; Z=2.296, P=0.022) and positioning on the examination table (T2; Z=2.041, P=0.041). No significant between-group difference was observed during catheter insertion (T3; P>0.05). Although the differences at T1 and T2 were statistically significant, the absolute differences in VAS pain scores were small, suggesting a limited clinical magnitude of effect. Within both groups, pain increased significantly across the 3 assessment points, with the highest scores recorded at T3 (music group: χ²=32.021, P<0.001; control group: χ²=29.167, P<0.001) (Table 2).

Levels of Embarrassment (VAS-E)

Embarrassment scores did not differ significantly between the groups at any measurement point (P>0.05). Within-group analysis showed no significant change in the music group (χ²=0.974, P=0.615). In contrast, the control group had higher embarrassment scores at T2 and T3 than at T1 (χ²=10.920, P=0.004) (Table 2).

Urodynamic Parameters

No significant between-group differences were observed in filling volume, voiding volume, detrusor pressure at maximum flow (PdetQmax), maximum flow rate (Qmax), or post-void residual volume (P>0.05) (Table 3). These findings indicate that music did not meaningfully alter objective urodynamic measures during the procedure.

DISCUSSION

This randomized controlled trial evaluated the effects of music during invasive UDT on patients’ pain, embarrassment, and urodynamic outcomes. Many patients experience discomfort and anxiety during UDT because of limited privacy and insufficient preprocedural information [22]. Previous studies have indicated that anxiety, pain, and discomfort can interfere with the reliability of urodynamic assessments by potentially affecting detrusor function and delaying initiation of the voiding re-flex [6, 7]. Diri et al. [5] reported that music reduced pain and anxiety and improved patient satisfaction during UDT, and Durgun and Yaman Aktaş [23] observed similar benefits during cystoscopy. Evidence from other invasive procedures, including colposcopy, cystoscopy, and hysteroscopy, also suggests that music interventions can reduce pain and anxiety [10, 24-26]. Solomon and Ridgeway [1] similarly showed that nonpharmacological interventions, including music, could reduce discomfort during UDT.

Consistent with these studies, our findings indicate that music reduced pain during uroflowmetry and positioning on the examination table. However, the reductions were small in magnitude despite being statistically significant, and their clinical importance should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Anglin et al. [15] found that music reduced pain during outpatient urological procedures, with sex-specific differences, and Çelebi et al. [9] reported similar findings during colonoscopy. Rather than supporting a strong analgesic effect, our results are consistent with the broader literature suggesting that music provides modest, context-dependent reductions in procedural discomfort.

In contrast to its effect on pain, music did not significantly reduce embarrassment in this study. This finding suggests that embarrassment may be driven less by immediate physiological arousal than by psychosocial and cultural factors, including exposure of intimate body areas, perceived loss of dignity, and the presence of healthcare providers of a different sex. Previous studies have emphasized that embarrassment is among the most frequently reported negative experiences during urodynamic and cystoscopic procedures and may not be readily reduced by distraction techniques alone [27, 28]. From a nursing perspective, strategies such as ensuring sex-concordant staff when feasible, using appropriate draping techniques, limiting the number of personnel in the room, and providing clear preprocedural education may help reduce embarrassment [28-30]. These findings support the view that embarrassment is a multidimensional emotional response that is unlikely to be improved by a single-component intervention such as music.

Although we hypothesized that music might positively influence detrusor function by reducing anxiety, no significant between-group differences were observed in pressure-flow parameters or voiding-phase measures. These findings are consistent with previous trials evaluating the effect of music on objective urodynamic outcomes. Diri et al. [5] reported that although music significantly reduced anxiety and improved patient comfort, it did not produce meaningful changes in pressure-flow parameters such as Qmax or pdetQmax. Similarly, the systematic review and meta-analysis by Rahman et al. [8] concluded that music interventions improved subjective experiences but did not significantly modify bladder capacity, detrusor pressure, or voiding efficiency across randomized trials. Çulha et al. [6] also found that auditory interventions, such as running-water sounds, produced only minimal, nonsignificant improvements in uroflow metrics. Taken together, these findings suggest that music may improve subjective comfort, but its ability to alter the physiological mechanisms underlying voiding appears limited.

The present trial was powered for the primary outcome of procedural pain, whereas urodynamic parameters were analyzed as secondary exploratory outcomes. Therefore, the study may not have had sufficient power to detect small physiological differences in pressure-flow measures. These findings should be interpreted in light of this limitation.

This study has several limitations. First, baseline anxiety was analyzed using categorical groupings, namely moderate or lower and severe, which may have reduced sensitivity for detecting subtle anxiety-related differences in perception. Second, embarrassment was assessed using a single-item VAS, which may not fully capture the complexity of this construct. Third, only classical instrumental music was used; responses may differ with other genres or with patient-selected music. Fourth, the absence of blinding may have introduced perception-related bias in subjective outcomes. Finally, the single-center design limits generalizability. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the modest, primarily subjective benefits observed in this trial.

In conclusion, this randomized controlled trial showed that music reduced pain during uroflowmetry and positioning on the examination table during UDT, but it did not affect embarrassment or urodynamic parameters. Given the modest size of the pain reductions and the absence of changes in objective measures, music should be considered a supportive adjunct rather than a standalone intervention for improving patient comfort. Future studies should evaluate multimodal approaches, such as personalized music, patient education, relaxation strategies, and sex-concordant staffing, to better address embarrassment and improve patient-centered care during urodynamic procedures.
