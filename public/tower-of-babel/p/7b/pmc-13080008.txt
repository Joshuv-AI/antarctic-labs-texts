Elucidation of the aragonite nanofiber formation mechanism of LICP contained in the hinge ligament of Pinctada fucata
Authors: Kei Futagawa, Yuto Namikawa, Taichi Morioka, Haruki Meguro, Akira Shida, Yuki Nagano, Kazuo Furihata, Hiroyuki Watanabe, Fabio Nudelman, Taiga Okumura, Toshihiro Kogure, Teppei Ikeya, Yutaka Ito, Hidekazu Katayama, Koji Nagata, Michio Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceUnderstanding how proteins control crystal growth on solid surfaces is essential in biomineralization, materials science and protein sciences. Ligament intracrystalline peptide (LICP) controls the crystal orientation and growth in the aragonite nanofiber. The structural relationship between LICP and the aragonite surface provides key insights into nonclassical organic–inorganic interactions, as it reveals detailed structural dynamics of the peptide, including side-chain orientations, on the mineral surface. In this study, we used the synthetic peptide with the full-length including a posttranslational modification. The specific binding of LICP to {hk0}—all planes parallel to the c-axis—and elongation of the c-axis provide design principles for peptide-based control of aragonite nanofibers and other inorganic crystal in materials science and protein sciences.

Minerals formed by living organisms are referred to as biominerals, and the processes leading to their formation are known as biomineralization (1, 2). Calcium carbonate (CaCO3) is the most abundant biomineral, found in corals, mollusk shells, calcified algae, and sea urchin exoskeletons (3–7). CaCO3 exists in three crystal polymorphs: calcite, aragonite, and vaterite (Fig. 1A). Calcite is the most thermodynamically stable form under ambient conditions (8–11), while aragonite forms preferentially under high-pressure or in the presence of magnesium ions (8, 12–14) and vaterite is the least stable. CaCO3 also exists as a metastable amorphous form, referred to as amorphous CaCO3 (ACC), that rapidly transforms to more stable forms such as calcite and aragonite (9–11). Calcite and aragonite are the predominant polymorphs found in biominerals, and the selection of these forms is meticulously controlled by the organisms, largely owing to the biological function of the tissue.

In bivalves, the two shell valves are connected by the hinge ligament which is a flexible tissue that is mainly made from aragonite crystals in the form of nanofibers approximately 50 nm in diameter organized in parallel arrays and surrounded by fibrous organic matrices secreted from the mantle isthmus (Fig. 1 B–E) (15–18). The aragonitic nanofibers form at the calcification front in an extracellular process, where granules and vesicles derived from the Golgi apparatus migrate, encapsulating the crystals within organic matrices, promoting fiber alignment (19). The growth direction of the aragonite nanofibers in the ligament is perpendicular to the surface of the shell and mantle isthmus. The long axis of aragonite nanofibers are parallel to the c-axis, while the orientation of a- and b-axes are not controlled (20).

Several specific proteins have been identified from the hinge ligament from the pearl oyster Pinctada fucata such as tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase (PfTIMP), ligament methionine-rich protein (LMP), and ligament intracrystalline peptide (LICP) (20–23). PfTIMP and LMP are framework proteins that constitute the fibrous organic matrix, while LICP is intracrystalline and consists of 10 amino acid residues with N-terminal pyroglutamate (pQ) (sequence: pQPDHEGTYDY) (Fig. 1F). pQ is a common modification in secreted proteins that stabilizes the N terminus against aminopeptidases and proteolytic degradation because proteolytic cleavage often disrupts the three-dimensional structure or removes functional motifs (24). The pQ has been reported in the prismatic layer protein Prismalin-14 and the nacre-associated lectin PPL3 (25, 26), suggesting that the pQ of LICP contributes to its stability in the biomineralization environment. In vitro functional analyses revealed that LICP affects the formation of aragonite crystals (23), the detailed underlying mechanisms however remain unclear.

Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) lack a well-defined three-dimensional structure under physiological conditions (27, 28), yet they play critical roles in diverse biological processes, including signal transduction, molecular recognition, and biomineralization. Understanding the structure–function relationship of IDPs remains a central challenge in structural biology, as their conformational flexibility complicates traditional structural analysis. Among the various systems that provide insights into IDP function, biomineralization offers a unique context in which protein conformation directly impacts crystal growth and morphology. In biomineralization, short peptides can strongly modulate mineral nucleation and growth; examples include N-terminal fragments of statherin and osteocalcin for hydroxyapatite (29, 30), the R5 motif of silaffin-1A1 for silica (31), and acidic segments of the magnetosome peptide Mms6 for magnetite (32). Metal-binding peptides identified by phage display can bind specifically to metal and metal-oxide surfaces such as gold and titanium particles (33, 34). Low-molecular-weight disordered peptides embedded within biogenic minerals, such as the LICP found in the hinge ligament of Pinctada fucata, serve as powerful model systems to explore how disordered proteins interact with inorganic surfaces. These systems allow us to address fundamental questions regarding how IDPs modulate nucleation and crystal orientation at the molecular level.

Despite the growing interest in IDPs, understanding the molecular mechanisms that underpin the control over mineralization by proteins and peptides has been a major challenge. It requires elucidating the role of their structure and molecular conformation in interacting with crystal nuclei and surfaces. Such analyses, however, face several limitations. X-ray crystallography, a widely used technique to determine protein structure at the atomic level, cannot be employed in this context because of the challenges in crystallizing proteins while adsorbed on the surface of inorganic crystals. While cryotransmission electron microscopy enables protein structure determination without requiring crystallization, in samples containing both proteins and mineral the scattering of electrons by the latter will dominate the imaging, precluding the analysis of the conformation of biomineral proteins involved in organic–inorganic interactions.

Here, we developed a NMR methodology to elucidate the molecular mechanism of the organic–inorganic interactions between LICP and aragonite nanofibers (35). Conventional solid-state NMR analyses of biomineral proteins have been applied to CaCO3–protein systems (36–38). However, solid-state NMR generally requires relatively large amounts of protein loading on the mineral and has low spectral resolution. To overcome the lack of sufficient resolution of solid-state NMR that prevents the determination of the detailed conformation of proteins and the need of solution NMR to maintain both the proteins and the mineral particles uniformly dispersed, we designed the following experimental system using a suspension of CaCO3 dispersive particles (DPs) (39–41). Unlike bulk minerals, CaCO3 DPs can rotate and disperse within the solution, thereby reducing the anisotropic effects on the spectrum and producing sharper signals (42–44). Therefore, this system enables the analyses of conformational changes in biomineral proteins on solid surfaces using solution NMR, offering a method for studying organic–inorganic interactions (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

Previous studies have typically used the partial domains or sequences derived from full-length biomineral proteins to examine the changes in mineral crystal habit or surface imaging with a few atomistic discussion of the protein conformation (45–48). In contrast, LICP is a very short, intrinsically disordered peptide (10 residues), that can be synthesized by chemical methods with exactly the same structure as the natural product, including its posttranslational modifications. For these reasons, we chose to use LICP for this study using special techniques of NMR.

Results

Effects of LICP on CaCO3 Polymorph Selection and Crystal Morphology.

To optimize the conditions for CaCO3 crystallization, we first varied the Mg/Ca ratio from 0 to 5 in the presence of LICP (3 µM) for 2 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). At Mg/Ca = 0, XRD and SEM analyses showed only calcite and vaterite. At Mg/Ca ratios of 1 and 2, mixtures of calcite, aragonite, and vaterite were obtained. The calcite ratio appeared lower in the presence of LICP than in the negative control (NC, without LICP). At Mg/Ca ratios = 3 to 5, the precipitates primarily consisted of acicular aragonite. Based on these results, we used Mg/Ca ratios of 0, 1, 2 to evaluate the effects of LICP on nucleation in dose-dependent and time course experiments.

We synthesized CaCO3 at Mg/Ca = 0 in the presence of LICP. XRD measurements showed that high ratio of spherical vaterite was observed at the initial phase of CaCO3 crystal growth in the presence of LICP (e.g., ~4 h at 0.03 to 0.3 µM and ~24 h at 3 µM) (SI Appendix, Figs. S3 and S4 and Supplementary Note 1). Some vaterite particles may be derived from ACC precursor, indicating that the metastable vaterite/ACC phase was stabilized by LICP (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). LICP did not induce the aragonite formation after 168 h and the formed calcite crystals were typical rhombohedral crystals, suggesting that LICP had little effect on calcite morphology.

We also synthesized CaCO3 at Mg/Ca = 1 and 2. At Mg/Ca = 1, SEM images at 4 h showed a high abundance of spherical vaterite in the presence of LICP (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Semiquantitative analyses using XRD revealed that CaCO3 precipitates synthesized using LICP contained more aragonite and less calcite than those of NC after 16 h (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). SEM images at 16 h also indicated that almost all precipitates were acicular aragonite, in the presence of LICP, whereas some rhombohedral particles were observed in the NC (Fig. 2A). The aspect ratio of the acicular aragonite tended to be higher in the presence of LICP than in the NC. At Mg/Ca = 2, LICP similarly increased the ratio of acicular aragonite with high aspect ratio compared with the NC (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Taken together, these results suggested that LICP interacted with the ACC and vaterite, thereby inhibiting calcite formation and promoting the growth of elongated acicular aragonite crystals.

To investigate the detailed effect of LICP on aragonite morphology, we synthesized aragonite crystals in vitro using LICP at Mg/Ca = 5, which is close to the condition of seawater (49, 50). The formation of aragonite in the NC and in the presence of LICP was confirmed by XRD (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). The presence of LICP in the crystallization solution resulted in acicular crystals with high aspect ratio compared with the NC (Fig. 2 C and D). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) showed that hexagonal-shaped aragonite was observed in synthesized aragonite using LICP (Fig. 2 E and F). The lateral face and bottom face of hexagonal prism in synthesized aragonite using LICP were {110} and {001}, respectively (Fig. 2G). These results suggested that the LICP may contribute to the formation of thin hexagonal-shaped aragonite crystal exposed {110} of lateral faces (Fig. 2G).

Binding Assay of FITC-LICP to Aragonite Plane.

To reveal the interaction between LICP and {110} of aragonite crystal, we performed a binding assay using fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled LICP as the fluorescent probe. FITC-LICP showed the same activity for the aspect ratio of acicular aragonite as native LICP (Fig. 2 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). The fluorescent peptide was tested on aragonite slices that specifically exposed {001} or {110}. (Fig. 2H and SI Appendix, Fig. S9). FITC-LICP showed high fluorescence intensity on {110} compared to {001} (Fig. 2 I and J), demonstrating the specificity of the binding of FITC-LICP to {110} of aragonite. Fluorescence intensity of FITC alone on {110} and {001} was significantly low. No autofluorescence was observed in both {001} and {110} (SI Appendix, Fig. S10).

Solution NMR with CaCO3 DPs.

To investigate the conformational changes of LICP on CaCO3 surfaces, we employed a solution NMR method optimized for biomineral-bound peptides. This method used CaCO3 DPs suspended in solution, allowing for uniform dispersion and rotation of the mineral phase. This setup overcomes the limitations of conventional solid-state NMR, which often suffers from spectral broadening due to anisotropy, and enables high-resolution conformational analysis of proteins adsorbed onto solid surfaces. Using this system, we analyzed the conformational change of LICP in the presence of different CaCO3 polymorphs and inorganic ions. SI Appendix, Supplementary Note 2 showed synthesis and characterization of the CaCO3 DPs (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). The ACC DPs were prepared in the presence of phosphate ions. According to ICP–MS (Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry), the Ca/P molar ratio of ACC was 0.21. The experimental procedures and analyses about NMR were described in SI Appendix, Supplementary Note 3 (NMR assignments) (SI Appendix, Figs. S12–S26, Table S1, and Supplementary Note 4), (CYANA structure calculations) (SI Appendix, Fig. S27, Tables S2 and S3, and Supplementary Note 5 and Datasets S1–S4), (ROESY-based analyses of conformational changes in the addition of CaCO3 DPs) (SI Appendix, Fig. S28).

Relative Distance from Aragonite DPs to Each Residue in LICP.

Saturation transfer difference (STD) is commonly used to detect correlations between ligands and receptors. Here, the water molecules within the hydration layers around the aragonite DPs were used as the receptor, and LICP as the ligand. The STD 1H NMR spectra were measured by varying the saturation time (tsat; 0.02 to 10 s) (SI Appendix, Fig. S29). The relative distance between each residue in LICP and the water molecules within the hydration layers were analyzed by peak intensity changes. The STD amplification factors (Astd) were plotted against the saturation time (tsat) to obtain the STD build-up curve (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, using the least-squares curve fitting, we determined the saturation time constant (ksat) and maximum of intensity (Amax) in equations [1] and [2] of Material and Methods. The results of 1Hβ from each residue are listed in SI Appendix, Table S4. The residues of STD0 values with more than 60% were E5 Hβ2, E5 Hβ3, Y8 Hβ2, Y8 Hβ3, D9 Hβ3, Y10 Hβ2, and Y10 Hβ3 in LICP (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Table S4). These residues were relatively close to the water molecules within the hydration layers.

Conformational Analyses of LICP with the Addition of Various CaCO3 DPs Polymorphs or Inorganic Ions.

The three carboxyl groups in the side chains of the acidic residues D3, E5, and D9 were arranged in a straight line and on the same plane of the aromatic ring of the Y8 and Y10 residues in both LICP/water and LICP/Aragonite (Fig. 3C). The conformations of LICP/water showed the round shape with straight line of acidic residues and narrow plane consisting of D3, E5, D9, Y8, and Y10 (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S27). On the other hand, LICP/Aragonite was elongated with similar straight line of acidic residues and wide plane consisting of D3, E5, D9, Y8, and Y10 (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S27). The area of plane of LICP/water was smaller than that of LICP/Aragonite, because the backbone of LICP/Aragonite was more extended than that of LICP/water. This linear and wide planar arrangement of LICP/Aragonite was likely favorable for binding to the mineral surface, matching the localization of calcium ions in the aragonite lattice.

To investigate whether the change in conformation of LICP was specifically induced by binding to the surface of aragonite, we analyzed the peptide conformation in the presence of other CaCO3 DPs polymorphs (ACC, calcite, and vaterite) or inorganic ions. A chemical shift change indicates that the surrounding electronic environment has changed due to the added CaCO3 DPs or the conformational changes. In comparison with LICP/water (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S30), the chemical shifts of acidic residues and aromatic amino acids showed significant upfield shifts only in the addition of CaCO3 (ACC, aragonite, calcite, and vaterite) DPs. The straight line of acidic side chains and wide plane of acidic and aromatic side chains were not observed except for LICP/Aragonite, LICP/Aragonite+MgCa, and LICP/ACC (Fig. 3E).

We analyzed the orientation and spread of aromatic rings in Tyr by measuring the angles of ∠D3CβD9CβY8Cγ (∠Y8) and ∠D3CβD9CβY10Cγ (∠Y10) and the distance ratio between the N terminus and the Tyr residues (pQ1N-Y8Cγ/pQ1N-Y10Cγ) in 200 calculated conformations (SI Appendix, Fig. S31 and Supplementary Note 6). We observed a larger spread of the aromatic ring orientations in the LICP/Aragonite, LICP/Aragonite+MgCa, and LICP/ACC compared with other polymorphs (Fig. 3F). The large spread of Tyr aromatic rings in LICP enables adaptation to chemical inhomogeneities on aragonite surfaces, such as variations in hydration, ions, and pH. This flexibility may facilitate specific and stable binding of LICP to aragonite.

Tyr typically exhibits fluorescence with an excitation wavelength around 280 nm and an emission wavelength of approximately 315 nm. In this study, we examined how the fluorescence intensity of Tyr in LICP varies in solution with different CaCO3 DPs polymorphs. Interactions with molecules can serve as a transferred pathway for excited energy near a fluorescent Tyr. Due to this interaction, the energy absorbed by the LICP can be partially transferred to CaCO3 DPs with fixed water molecules, resulting in a quenching effect that reduces fluorescence intensity. As a result, the fluorescence intensity of Tyr significantly decreased in the binding of LICP to crystalline CaCO3 (aragonite, calcite, and vaterite) with the largest effect on aragonite (Fig. 3G).

We measured the average distances between three carboxyl groups of acidic residues (D3-E5 and E5-D9) under all LICP conditions (Fig. 3 H and I and SI Appendix, Fig. S32A). The average distances in LICP/water were 9.94 Å (D3-E5) and 13.29 Å (E5-D9), yielding a distance ratio (D3–E5/E5–D9) of about 0.6 (Fig. 3 H and I). The numbers of residues for D3-E5 and E5-D9 were 3 and 5, respectively, giving the distance ratio of 0.6, which was energetically favorable for the LICP backbone. In LICP/Aragonite, D3–E5 increased to 10.39 Å, while E5–D9 decreased to 11.47 Å, resulting in a distance ratio of about 1.0. The ratio of 1.0 was normally energetically unfavorable for the LICP backbone, indicating that this conformation was stabilized by interactions with the aragonite DPs. No significant changes of distance ratio from LICP/water were observed in other CaCO3 DPs or inorganic ions except for LICP/Aragonite and LICP/Aragonite+MgCa. The convergence of the distance ratio toward 1.0 suggests that the carboxyl groups may bind in alignment with the evenly spaced calcium ions on the aragonite surface. The calcium ion spacing of 11.48 Å on the {110} closely matched the about 11 Å distance between carboxyl groups in LICP/Aragonite, suggesting preferential binding to planes parallel to the c-axis (SI Appendix, Fig. S33). This 11 Å spacing was absent on the {001} plane. These results imply that LICP aligns along the {110}, potentially promoting c-axis-oriented aragonite growth.

Dose-Dependent Conformational Changes of LICP Binding to Aragonite and ACC DPs.

To strongly support the interaction between LICP and solid aragonite, we measured NMR spectra and analyzed the three-dimensional structure of LICP in different amounts of aragonite DPs to investigate whether conformational change of LICP does not depend on the saturated calcium ion in the solution, but also on the solid CaCO3 in the solution. The concentration of saturated calcium ions was stable in solution with CaCO3 DPs. When the amount of the CaCO3 DPs is changed, the change of NMR spectra and conformation must be observed.

At first, we analyzed the 1H chemical shifts of the acidic residues (D3, E5, and D9) and of the aromatic ring of Y8 and Y10 in concentrations of aragonite DPs ranging from 0.36 to 7.29 mg/mL. In comparison with LICP/water, the 1H chemical shifts progressively shifted upfield with increasing amounts of aragonite DPs (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S34A). Additionally, the average distance between D3-E5 and E5-D9 gradually approached 11.48 Å and the D3-E5/E5-D9 ratio approached 1.0 (Fig. 4 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S32B).

Next, we compared the conformational parameter of Tyr in 200 calculated conformations. The ROESY signals related to aromatic ring of Tyr gradually disappeared with the addition of aragonite DPs indicating that structural calculation could not fix the position of Tyr, while signals corresponding to His remained (Fig. 4D). The distance ratio (pQ1N-Y8Cγ/pQ1 N-Y10Cγ) and the angles of ∠Y8 and ∠Y10 were measured (SI Appendix, Fig. S35 and Supplementary Note 7). As the amount of aragonite DPs increased in a dose-dependent manner, a significant change in the orientation of the Tyr aromatic ring and its random spreading from the backbone were observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S35).

Same as LICP/Aragonite, we also compared the conformations of LICP/ACC in different amounts of ACC DPs in SI Appendix, Figs. S32C, S34B, and S36 and Supplementary Note 8. The aromatic rings in Tyr spread randomly from the backbone of LICP with the addition of ACC DPs equivalent as LICP/Aragonite.

Structural and Functional Analyses Using LICP Mutants.

We designed the following LICP mutants by substituting Tyr with Phe or Ser and acidic residues with Ala: LICPYF (Y→F), LICPYS (Y→S), and LICPDEA (D and E→A) (SI Appendix, Table S5). We investigated the effect of LICP mutants by in vitro aragonite formation. The mean aspect ratio of acicular aragonite was about seven with LICP, but decreased to about 5 with LICPYF and LICPYS and to about two with LICPDEA (Fig. 5 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S37).

Conformational analyses of LICP mutants using NMR revealed that the addition of aragonite DPs resulted in significant upfield changes in the chemical shifts of Asp, Glu, and Phe or Ser in LICPYF and LICPYS (SI Appendix, Fig. S38). In LICPDEA, the chemical shift of Tyr shifted upfield in the addition of aragonite DPs, whereas the chemical shift of Ala showed no significant change.

Based on 1H chemical shift assignments, conformational changes of LICP mutants were examined using ROESY spectra with or without aragonite DPs. Focusing on the direction of side chains, the conformations of LICPYF/Aragonite and LICPYS/Aragonite did not maintain the linear and planar arrangement (Fig. 5C). On the other hand, the conformation of LICPDEA/Aragonite that has the side chains of the three Ala residues replacing D3, E5, and D9, kept the linear and planar arrangement, indicating that two Tyr residues are important for the stabilization of both linearity and planarity (Fig. 5C). These results suggested that aromatic rings in Tyr were indispensable for maintaining both linearity and planarity. Consistent with the results of LICP mutants in the addition of aragonite DPs, the structural calculations in the addition of ACC DPs also showed that Tyr were indispensable for maintaining both linearity and planarity (SI Appendix, Figs. S38 and S39 and Supplementary Note 9).

The distribution of the histogram for the pQ1N-Y8Cγ/pQ1N-Y10Cγ (SI Appendix, Fig. S31A and Supplementary Note 6) was broader for LICPDEA than LICPYF and LICPYS (Fig. 5D), suggesting that the acidic residues were not the primary determinant of the distribution, whereas two C-terminal Tyr residues (Y8 and Y10) were a key structural factor of the distribution. LICP mutants showed the distance ratio (D3-E5/E5-D9) was less than 1.0 (Fig. 5 E and F), indicating that all acidic and Tyr residues were important for the conformational change on the surface of aragonite DPs.

Stability of Attached LICP on the {110} and {001} of Aragonite Surface by MD Simulations.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted to analyze binding modes of LICP on the surface of {001} and the {110} (SI Appendix, Fig. S40A and Supplementary Note 10). For each surface, five independent 200 ns trajectories were generated, and backbone rmsd, Rg (radius of gyration), and per-residue RMSF (Rms fluctuation) were calculated (SI Appendix, Figs. S41 and S42). In all replicates, LICP remained bound to both the {001} and the {110} surfaces throughout the simulations. (SI Appendix, Fig. S43). To evaluate the binding stability of LICP to the aragonite surface, we measured the distance distribution between the oxygen atoms of the carboxyl groups in LICP and the calcium atoms in the {001} and the {110}, shown in Fig. 6A and SI Appendix, Fig. S43. D3 and Y10 indicated over 70% binding to both the surface of the {001} or the {110}. E5 and D9 did not bind at all to the {001}, whereas they bound 18.0% and 35.9% to the {110}. Unlike on the {001} surface, where only D3 and Y10 were involved in binding, LICP was able to interact with the {110} through all of its amino acid residues.

Binding Process of LICP from the Solution to the {110} Aragonite Surface by MD Simulations.

To investigate the structural dynamics of how LICP in solution binds to the aragonite surface, three repeats of 200 ns MD simulations were conducted, where an initial configuration of LICP was placed 30 Å away from the {110} surface (SI Appendix, Supplementary Note 10). Three independent 200 ns trajectories were generated, and backbone rmsd, Rg, per-residue RMSF, and SASA (solvent accessible surface area) were calculated (SI Appendix, Figs. S44–S47). In one of the three simulations (Repeat 2), LICP did not bind to the aragonite surface within 200 ns and remained solvated in water molecules, whereas in the other two simulations (Repeat 1 and 3), LICP bound to the surface by carboxy groups of LICP approaching calcium atoms on the {110} in several tens of ns (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S44). After LICP bound to the aragonite surface, LICP did not dissociate from the surface. Y10 and D9 in Repeat 1 and D3 and D9 in Repeat 3 bound to the {110}.

Discussion

The crystallization experiments (Fig. 2 A and B) indicated that LICP stabilized spherical vaterite and increased the ratio of aragonite relative to calcite, thereby biasing CaCO3 polymorph selection toward aragonite at low-Mg/Ca conditions (Mg/Ca = 0-2). LICP maintained nanoscale crystal size and significantly increased the aspect ratio of acicular aragonite at a ratio of Mg/Ca = 5, suggesting it promotes elongation along the c-axis (Fig. 2 C and D). TEM-SAED revealed that LICP induces the formation of thin, hexagonal aragonite with lateral {110} and basal {001} (Fig. 2 E–G). A binding assay using FITC-LICP showed significantly stronger binding to {110} than {001} (Fig. 2 H–J), indicating specific interaction. These results suggested that LICP bound preferentially to the {110} and facilitated the formation of elongated hexagonal aragonite crystals through organic–inorganic interactions.

To investigate the function of LICP in forming aragonite nanofiber at the molecular level, we analyzed the conformational changes of LICP on solid CaCO3 surfaces using solution NMR with CaCO3 DPs. According to the results of conformational analysis, LICP adopted compact conformations in aqueous solution (LICP/water), stabilized by hydrogen bonds, intramolecular electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions (51, 52). The acidic residues (D3, E5, D9) promoted electrostatic interaction between calcium ions on CaCO3 surfaces, leading to overcome intramolecular interactions and elongate the backbone of the peptide. The loss or weakening of intramolecular ROESY signals between sequence-distant residues supported these conformational changes (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S36F). In LICP/Aragonite, the peptide further adopted an extended and planar state in which the acidic residues (D3, E5, D9) and Tyr residues (Y8, Y10) were aligned in a straight line and lay within the same plane (Fig. 3C). This linear and planar arrangement is likely favorable for binding, suggesting that LICP interacts with the aragonite surface through three functional groups (carboxyl groups) or up to five (including aromatic rings). This arrangement was specific for LICP/Aragonite, LICP/Aragonite+MgCa, and LICP/ACC and was not observed in other CaCO3 polymorphs, inorganic ions, and LICP mutants. These results indicated that LICP changed a conformation that enables its carboxyl groups and aromatic rings to interact not only with aragonite DPs but also with ACC. Although the arrangement of calcium ions and carbonate ions in bulk ACC is disordered, the surface of bulk ACC partially makes the planar layer with calcium ions, carbonate ions, and the water molecules within the hydration layers. As a result, three carboxylate groups can align approximately along the surface and interact with bulk ACC, because of the flexibility of calcium ion localization in bulk ACC. Our crystallization experiments also showed that LICP stabilizes vaterite or ACC spherical precursors and delayed their phase transformation (Fig. 2 A and B and SI Appendix, Figs. S4–S7).

LICP formed the elongated backbone on the surface of calcite and vaterite, and its fluorescence was clearly quenched (Fig. 3 E and G), suggesting that LICP interacts with calcite and vaterite surfaces. However, these interactions may be weaker than those with aragonite, because the acidic side chains and Tyr aromatic rings in LICP/calcite or LICP/vaterite did not show both linearity and planarity (Fig. 3E). The fluorescence measurements also revealed a lower quenching effect for calcite and vaterite than for aragonite (Fig. 3G). These observations suggested that LICP can still interact with calcite and vaterite mainly through nonspecific electrostatic contacts whose interaction is weaker and less specific than on aragonite.

The fluorescence emission intensity of Tyr in LICP/MgCa was very similar to LICP/water, LICP/Ca, and LICP/CaCO3sat (Fig. 3G). The elongated conformation of LICP in the presence of magnesium ions closely resembled that of Mg-free controls (e.g., LICP/Ca2+, LICP/CaCO3-saturated) (Fig. 3E). We also added magnesium ions to aragonite DPs (LICP/Aragonite+MgCa) for NMR measurements. The calculated conformation of LICP/Aragonite+MgCa was essentially the same as that calculated for LICP/Aragonite in Mg-free buffer. These results demonstrated that magnesium ions did not materially alter the conformation and function of LICP on aragonite.

From the result of the conformational changes in Tyr aromatic rings with the different amount of aragonite DPs (SI Appendix, Fig. S35 and Supplementary Note 7), schematic diagram of conformational change about aromatic rings of Tyr were suggested as follows (Fig. 4E). Aromatic rings of Tyr were folded near the backbone in LICP/water, while the aromatic rings in Tyr gradually spread randomly from the backbone and a remarkable change in the orientation of the Y10 aromatic ring was detected in the addition of aragonite DPs.

Mineral surfaces, such as aragonite, are influenced by a variety of chemical factors including hydration state, surrounding inorganic ions, and solution pH, which alter the chemical homogeneity of the surface (53). These changes can affect the surface’s charge density and chemical activity, potentially impacting the adsorption behavior of proteins. The flexibility of Tyr aromatic rings to orient in various directions serves as an adaptation mechanism to these surface inhomogeneities. By orienting in different directions, the aromatic rings can form optimal interactions with the surface’s varied chemical properties, enhancing effective protein adsorption. This flexibility may be crucial for the specific binding and functional expression of LICP on the surfaces of aragonite.

MD simulations have revealed the structural dynamics and adsorption properties of biomineral protein or peptides: dentin phosphoprotein on hydroxyapatites surfaces (54), barnacle cement protein and aspartate derivatives on calcite surfaces (55, 56), and CaCO3 binding peptide on aragonite surfaces (57). MD simulations supported that LICP preferably bound to the {110} through all carboxyl groups rather than to the {001} (Fig. 6A), consistent with the binding assay (Fig. 2 H–J). As these simulations were performed with explicit solvent, the carboxyl groups of LICP penetrated the hydration shell to bind the aragonite surface. The distance distribution between the carboxyl oxygens and the surface calcium ions showed a population around 2.4 Å (Fig. 6A). This value is highly consistent with the typical Ca–O coordinate bond length of 2.36 Å (58), indicating that water molecules in the first hydration layer are stripped off to form direct bonding with the crystal surface. Furthermore, LICP could bind to the {110} through carboxy groups even though an initial configuration of LICP was placed 30 Å distance from the {110}. Terminal residues tend to exhibit higher conformational freedom than internal residues because terminal residues are less sterically constrained and have fewer intramolecular interaction (59). Y10 had a relatively low RMSF value and the highest SASA value (SI Appendix, Figs. S45 and S46 and Supplementary Note 10), suggesting that Y10 played a crucial role in the initial phase of the neighborhood process of LICP binding to the {110}. From the conformational dynamics of the binding process of LICP to the {110} surface, the hypothesized binding process was as follows: When LICP approaches the aragonite surface in a random conformation, the carboxyl group of Y10 at the C-terminus of the backbone enhanced the probability of forming electrostatic bonds between LICP and the aragonite surface. LICP binds to the aragonite surface by adopting the elongated conformation, consistent with the results of structural calculations by NMR.

Previous studies showed that carboxyl groups of acidic residues interacted with calcium ions in CaCO3 and influenced nucleation and crystal growth (2, 49, 60). In LICP/Aragonite, the average E5-D9 distance significantly changed, to about 11 Å and the D3-E5/E5-D9 approached approximately 1.0 (Fig. 3 H and I). These results suggested that distances of carboxyl groups could be adjusted to match the positions of calcium ions in {110} of aragonite. The average E5–D9 distance of 11 Å closely matches the 11.48 Å spacing between calcium ions on the crystal faces parallel to the c-axis of aragonite in the {XY0} planes—including the {110} plane and all other planes oriented along the c-axis (SI Appendix, Fig. S33). As a result, LICP induced radially oriented acicular aragonite elongated along the c-axis direction (Fig. 6 C and D).

The schematic diagram of aragonite nanofiber formation is shown in Fig. 6E. In the absence of LICP, aragonite crystals grew freely and became thick acicular fibers. According to our proposed model, in the presence of LICP, lateral growth of the crystals along the a- and b-axes is inhibited by the binding of LICP to the crystal faces parallel to the c-axis of aragonite. The hinge ligament is suggested to be formed by the cooperative mechanism of LICP and organic framework such as LMP. LMP with huge spiral conformation for resistance of pressure is methionine-rich protein to make the organic framework in the ligament (20, 41). A previous study showed that small needles of aragonite were secreted from the tissue and aligned for same direction in the initial phase (19). LMP has a function to make the organic framework between nanofibers for the alignments (20).

Overall, this study provides a valuable contribution to the emerging field of disordered protein–mineral interactions. By focusing on a short, disordered peptide (LICP) embedded in a biologically mineralized structure, we have demonstrated how structural disorder can enable specific recognition and functional control of crystal growth through conformational adaptability. These findings extend the current understanding of intrinsically disordered proteins beyond classical signaling roles and highlight their relevance in rigid, ordered environments such as biominerals. Furthermore, our integrative approach—combining solution NMR, in vitro crystallization, and MD simulations—offers a powerful platform for resolving the elusive structure–function relationships of IDPs at the organic–inorganic interface. We anticipate that the conceptual and methodological advances presented here will facilitate broader exploration of IDPs in biomineralization, materials science and protein sciences.

Materials and Methods

This study involved multiple experimental methods, with a detailed Materials and Methods section provided in SI Appendix.

Synthetic LICP (identical in structure to the natural product) and LICP mutants were prepared. CaCO3 crystallization assays were performed under controlled supersaturation while varying the Mg/Ca ratio, peptide concentration, and reaction time. Polymorph fractions were quantified by XRD, and crystal morphologies were characterized by SEM, TEM, and SAED. Plane-selective binding was evaluated by incubating FITC-LICP with aragonite slices specifically exposing the {110} or the {001} plane and quantifying fluorescence images.

To analyze peptide conformations on CaCO3 surfaces, CaCO3 DPs were prepared and used for solution NMR. Based on the 1H assignments using 2D-NMR spectra, the conformation of LICP was calculated. All-atom MD simulations were performed on the surface of the {110} and {001}.
