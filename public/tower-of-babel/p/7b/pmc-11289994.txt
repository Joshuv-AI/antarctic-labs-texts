Establishment and verification of novel TNM staging system for lung mucinous adenocarcinoma
Authors: Qi-Yue Ge, Chao Zheng, Guo-Chao Zhang, Zhuang-Zhuang Cong, Jing Luo, Yang Xu, Chang-Yong Wang, Chao Luo, Wei Wei, Zhi-Hui Yang, Ming-Ze Li, Yu-Heng Wu, Yu-Yang Wang, Qi Xue, Yi Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung adenocarcinoma is a high-mortality rate cancer. Within this category, Lung mucinous adenocarcinoma (LMAC) is a rare and distinct subtype of lung adenocarcinoma necessitating further investigation. The study was launched to compare the difference of survival features between LMAC and lung non-mucinous adenocarcinoma (LNMAC) and to investigate the significance and demand for developing a new staging system tailored to LMAC.MethodsThis retrospective study assessed the suitableness of the current staging system for LMAC. It compared the overall survival (OS) between LMAC and LNMAC from 2004 to 2020 (LNMAC: 160,387; LMAC: 6,341) and instituted a novel classification framework for LMAC based on US population. Verification group consisting of patients from two Chinese medical centers from 2010 to 2018 (n = 392) was set to ascertain the applicability of this novel system. The primary endpoint was OS. To minimize the bias, propensity score match (PSM) was employed. Survival analysis and Log-rank test were executed to explore the survival features of LMAC.ResultsThe results indicated that the existed staging system was not suitable for LMAC. Patients diagnosed with LMAC exhibited a superior OS compared to those with LNMAC in stage IA2 (P < 0.0001), IA3 (P < 0.0001), IB (P = 0.0062), IIA (P = 0.0090), IIB (P = 0.0005). In contrast, a worse OS in stage IVA (P = 0.0103) was found in LMAC patients. The novel classification system proposed for LMAC proved to be highly applicable and demonstrated substantial efficacy, as confirmed by the verification group.ConclusionThe newly established classification system was more effective for LMAC, but it necessitates large-scale verification to confirm its applicability and reliability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-12714-8.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with approximately 2.2 million new cases reported annually, accounting for 11.4% of all cancer cases, and 1.8 million deaths, accounting for 18.0% [1]. LMAC is a rare subtype of lung adenocarcinoma, characterized by the presence of goblet and/or columnar tumor cells containing abundant intracytoplasmic mucin vacuoles [2]. With the increasing number of LMAC cases and development in pathology, surgeons and physicians are finding LMAC to be unique in terms of prognosis, tumor metastasis, and recurrence. It appears that patients who undergo LMAC treatment tend to have longer survival times compared to other types of NSCLC [3], though there are instances where this may starkly contrast [4–6]. Hence, it is crucial to reach a conclusion of the survival characteristics associated with LMAC.

The 8th edition of the IASLC classification is the existed most recent cancer staging system. According to the IASLC 8th edition NSCLC TNM classification system, LMAC, as a sub-histological type of NSCLC, is staged by uniform classification criteria. Current studies indicated that the IASLC classification system holds stronger prognostic significance in LNMAC [7]. However, there is still controversy surrounding the suitability of the 8th edition IASLC TNM classification system for LMAC. Accurate classification of lung adenocarcinoma plays a decisive role in treatment selection, as an inappropriate classification could negatively impact survival benefits.

In order to comprehensively assess the survival characteristics of LMAC and develop an effective staging strategy, our study was designed to investigate the survival rates at each stage for both LMAC and LNMAC, with the aim of highlighting the limitations of the current classification system. Subsequently, we proposed a new staging strategy for LMAC based on the comparative analysis, and validated its applicability through Chinese population investigation.

Methods and materials

Patients

US population (via SEER database). Lung adenocarcinoma cases from 2004 to 2020 were reviewed from Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database (http://seer.cancer.gov). The eligible criteria of cases in SEER database were: (1) patients suffered from lung adenocarcinoma from 2004 to 2020; (2) patients with positive pathological diagnosis. The excluded criteria were: (1) cases without sufficient information for staging (8th edition); (2) cases lacking of survival information.

We revised all the stage of patients from 2004 to 2020 into 8th edition. As there is no sufficient stage information for the cases from 2004 to 2017 in stage IV to be transformed into 8th edition, cases staged IVA or IVB were originated from 2018 to 2020 in comparison between LMAC and LNMAC.

Chinese population (double-centered). LMAC cases from 2010 to 2018 in Nanjing Jinling Hospital and Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences Affiliated Cancer Hospital were selected. The included criteria were: (1) LMAC patients from 2010 to 2018; (2) patients with positive pathological diagnosis. The excluded criteria were the same as SEER-database cases.

Ethic

We were authorized to access the SEER database (16289-Nov2021). The project was approved by Ethic Committee of National Cancer Center/Cancer Hospital, Chinses Academy of Medical Science and Peking Union Medical College and the Institutional Review Board of the Jinling Hospital. Owing to the retrospective nature, the study was dispensed from obtaining individual inform consents, which was also approved by the two ethics committees mentioned above.

Data collection

Cases from SEER database was extracted by SEER*Stat software. Patients from two centers was collected by Excel sheet. The baseline characteristics included were list as follows: age, gender (male and female), race (American Indian/Alaska native, Asian or Pacific Islander, black, white and unknown), married status (divorced, married, separated, single, unmarried or domestic partner, widowed and unknown), tumor location (main bronchus, upper lobe, middle lobe, lower lobe, overlapping lesion of lung, unknown), laterality [left, right, bilateral (single primary), one side (unspecific) and no information], Surgical Procedure (none, performed and unknown), chemotherapy (no/unknown and yes), radiotherapy (no/unknown and yes). Overall survival (OS) to was the outcome of this study. OS was calculated by the date of diagnosis to the date of death or the last follow-up date. Any missing of the baseline characteristics or outcome information would be excluded.

Statistical analysis

SEER*Stat software (version 8.4.1.2) was used to convert the data from SEER database into txt. file. Stata/SE 16.0 software (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA) and R 4.3.2 software (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.r-project.org) were employed to analyze the data. Normally-distributed continuous variables were analyzed by t-test and the non-normal distributed variables were compared by Kruskal-Wallis H test. We used Chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test to compare categorical variables. Kaplan-Meier estimates were used to compare the difference of OS between stages. The survival difference between among groups were evaluated by log-rank test. To reduce the bias of baseline characteristics, PSM was performed and the standardized difference figure was used to evaluate the effect of PSM. The following baseline characteristics were included: age, gender, race, married status, tumor location, laterality, surgical procedure, chemotherapy and radiotherapy. P-value < 0.05 was considered to be significant different.

To investigate the OS feature of LMAC more accurately, we compared the stages with positive results to the different stages in LNMAC. The results of PSM were shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. The survival estimates results revealed that the OS of LMAC in stage IA2 was longer than that of LNMAC in stage IA1 (P = 0.0155). The IA3-staged LMAC had a better OS with IA2-staged LNMAC (P = 0.0241) and a close OS with IA1-staged (P = 0.3280). The OS of IB-staged LMAC was somewhere between IA3-staged (P = 0.0284) and IA2-staged (P = 0.0298) LNMAC. LMAC in stage IIA had a similar survival rate to LNMAC in stage IB (P = 0.2565). The survival rate of IIB-staged LMAC was somewhere between IB-staged (P = 0.0007) and IIA-staged LNMAC (P = 0.0061). On the contrary, IVA-staged LMAC presented a similar survival rate to IVB-staged LNMAC (P = 0.1415). The plots of survival analyses were shown in Supplementary Fig. 3.

According to the results above, the 8th edition of NSCLC classification was proved to be unsuitable for LMAC. Thus, we tried to establish a new TNM staging suitable for LMAC based on IASLC 8th edition NSCLC classification. We analyzed the difference between each T, N, M category. For T category, stage T1a and stage T1b had a similar survival rate (P = 0.2164) and we combined them as T1a in the new classification system for LMAC. The stage T1c in 8th edition staging was renamed as T1b and the rest of T stage was kept the same. No change was made in N stage. In M stage, M1b and M1c showed a close OS (P = 0.8869) and we redefined the M1b and M1c into M1b in the new staging for LMAC. The PSM results and survival analyses of T, N and M category could attain in Supplementary Figs. 4–9. The new TNM classification system was shown in Table 2.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the stages of LMAC through both US population (via SEER database) and Chinese population. The US population was taken to reveal that the current 8th edition IASLC staging for NSCLC was not suitable for LMAC. LMAC presented a different survival characteristic to LNMAC in the survival analysis. The results indicated that LMAC showed a better survival than LNMAC in early stages, probably owing to the unique pathological features of LMAC. As we know, LMAC had a lower pleural invasion rate than LNMAC [3]. A study in 2016 revealed that mixed LMAC and LNMAC had a worse disease-free survival than pure LMAC in stage I [6]. But there was no significant difference between LMAC and LNMAC in stage IA1. The possible reason for it could be the persistent increased and improved survival rate of stage I lung cancer [8]. However, in stage IVA, it presented a worse survival probability. Current study found that non-TKI chemotherapy might not be beneficial for stage IV LMAC [9], which could illustrate the reason of the terrible prognosis in stage IV.

The results effectively explained why LMAC needed a new classification criterion and we redefined a new TNM classification system for LMAC based on 8th edition IASLC TNM classification. Stage T1a and T1b were combined as T1a in the new T classification system and stage T1c was redefined as T1b. No change was made in N classification. In M classification, M1b and M1c was turned to have no significant difference and were combined as M1b in the new M classification. We re-analyzed the LMAC cases from SEER databases. The LMAC cases from US population showed a good classification and the analysis of Chinese population verified the new staging system to some extent.

The treatment of LMAC was not discussed in our study. However, due to its unique prognostic features, the treatment of LMAC should be considered separately from other subtypes of NSCLC. Stage IA was parallel with stage IA1 and IA2 in 8th edition classification. Similarly, stage IB was equivalent to stage IA3 in the original classification. According to the guidelines, initial therapy for operable NSCLC patients in Stage IA involves surgical exploration, resection, and mediastinal lymph node dissection, or systematic lymph node sampling [10]. Besides, it was widely accepted that surgical resection was preferred therapy to these early-staged NSCLCs and that postoperative follow-up without any adjuvant treatment was also suggested in the study [11]. As Saji et al. [12]. reported in a high-quality randomized controlled trial, lobectomy could be the standard surgical procedure for NSCLC in stage IA (by 8th edition classification). Since LMAC had a better OS than LNMAC, the lobectomy or even wedge resection could be the suitable therapy for LMAC in stage I [13–16]. In our study, the stage IIA of the newly proposed classification aligned with stage IB from the 8th edition, while the new stage IIB encompassed both the previous stage IIA and a portion of stage IIB. For stage IB and IIA in the 8th edition classification, postsurgical observation without other therapy was permitted if surgical margins were negative and no high-risk factors were presented [10]. Given that LMAC was demonstrated a superior OS compared to LNMAC, it is conceivable that stage II in the novel classification might share the same treatment as stage IB and IIA in the 8th edition. Future studies could pay attention to this potential alignment.

Radiotherapy was widely endorsed for early stage NSCLC patients who could not undertake surgical procedure, as recommended by several researches [17, 18]. However, previous study indicated that the advantages of post-operation radiotherapy in locally advanced LMAC was limited, with the quantity of positive lymph nodes being a critical determinant [5, 19]. Whereas, ablation could be an alternative therapy for those early stage LMAC patients who cannot tolerate surgical procedures [20]. Moreover, chemotherapy was also advocated for LMAC in the guideline [21]. Yet, a study from Cha et al. [9]. noted that the non-TKI chemotherapy could not benefit LMAC patients in stage IV and the TKI chemotherapy performed even worse. Consequently, alternative therapies were necessity for LMAC which could not be managed surgically. The current studies revealed that NRG1 was an important therapeutic target so that some second-generation EGFR-TKIs like Afatinib could be a potential effective drug for LMAC [22–24]. Immunotherapy also emerged as a potential alternative therapy for LMAC [25].

To more precisely diagnose LMAC, molecular subtyping should be taken into consideration. It was demonstrated that mutations in EGFR, KRAS, NKX2-1, and other genes were associated with LMAC, which might reveal specific tumorigenic pathways [26, 27]. The previous study showed that KRAS-mutation was common in LMAC and was associated with a worse prognosis [28]. Molecular subtype, as a predictor of prognosis, held promising potential for further staging. However, only a limited number of researches focused on molecular subtype of LMAC and the sample sizes of these studies were usually small. As a result, there was no sufficient evidence to establish its value in TNM staging.

The potential integration of the novel system into the existing system should also be taken into consideration. Integration implies regarding the mucous component as a significant risk factor rather than an isolated lung cancer system. However, as our study mentioned above, LMAC demonstrated variations across multiple dimensions. Many scholars argued that it was essential to classify LMAC as a distinct form of lung cancer at its core. Combining the new system for LMAC with the current staging system forcefully could potentially lead to confusion.

Furthermore, the novel system exhibited distinct advantages over the existing TNM staging system for LMAC. The novel TNM stage allowed for tailored treatment decisions. For example, patients in stage I might be recommended to take surgical procedure without postoperative adjuvant therapy, while stage II patients should carefully consider postoperative adjuvant therapy. Specifically, patients in stage IA could undergo lobectomy resection or wedge resection. Moreover, the novel staging system still held promising potential in unearthing further significance, warranting additional research for exploration.

To our knowledge, it was the first study to establish a new TNM classification system specifically tailored for LMAC. We wish to highlight three major points. Initially, we conducted a comprehensive comparison of all stages between LMAC and LNMAC. Further, we summarized the survival rate characteristics of each stage in contrast with LNMAC. Subsequently, although the results initially attained from the analysis of SEER database, we validated the new classification system using cases from two independent centers. Nevertheless, as the targeted therapy and immunotherapy applied widely in the recent years, we had to acknowledge that there might be a potential improvement in OS. We hoped more studies could continue to refine and update the results in the future.

Several limitations existed in our study. Firstly, our sample predominantly comprised U.S. residents, with only a minimal representation from the Chinese population, which might result in bias. The verification was not strong enough owing its lack of diversity so that the results might not be generalizable to other demographic groups. Future research would benefit from a larger, more diverse sample to substantiate our conclusions. Secondarily, the new TNM classification was developed based on the 8th edition IASLC staging system. As a result, this approach resulted in recombined T, N, or M categories based on the existing stages within the 8th edition framework. Although the results showed a great classification, the cut-off point of tumor size between stages needed more consideration in the future. Thirdly, our study was confined to the staging of LMAC. Several events for LMAC, such as treatment, was not mentioned in our study. Future studies could explore the impact of treatments at different stages, particularly as the current 8th edition staging system may prove inadequate for LMAC. Additionally, due to its retrospective nature, some unavoidable bias existed. More prospective studies were needed to verify the results.

Conclusion

The existing classification system for lung adenocarcinoma is not adequately tailored to LMAC. The newly established staging system presents a more suitable option for categorizing LMAC. Nonetheless, broader and more extensive population studies are crucial to substantiate its validity in the future.
