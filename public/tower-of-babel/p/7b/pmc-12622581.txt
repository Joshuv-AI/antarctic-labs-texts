Associations between metabolic score for visceral fat and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) in US adults: A cross-sectional study based on NHANES 2017–2020
Authors: Juan Li, Shenglan Dai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nowadays, obesity is an independent risk factor for nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), metabolic score for visceral fat (METS-VF) can assess obesity more accurately than other obesity index. However, the research assessing the relationships between METS-VF and NAFLD is limited. We utilized data from 3025 participants in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2017 to March 2020 for this cross-sectional study. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between METS-VF and NAFLD in US adults. Weighted multivariable-adjusted logistic regression was used to investigate the association between METS-VF and NAFLD. We used generalized additive model and threshold effect model in order to allow for potential nonlinear associations. Last, the subgroup analysis can be useful for identifying susceptible subgroups of populations. This population-based study included a total of 3025 adults. In our investigation, with an increase in METS-VF quartile, the prevalence of NAFLD increased gradually (P < .001). After adjusting for potential confounders, the odds ratios for NAFLD patients in the fourth METS-VF quartile were 6.28 (95% confidence interval: 2.94–13.38) (P < .05) in comparison with those in the first quartile. Using the generalized additive models and smooth curve fitting, we observed an S-shaped association between METS-VF and NAFLD, suggesting a critical inflection point at 5.17. On the right side of the inflection point, METS-VF was positively associated with the prevalence of NAFLD. In subgroup analyses, no significant interaction was observed. Further analysis showed that almost all subgroups were positively correlated with the prevalence of NAFLD. Our results show that the METS-VF was nonlinearly and positively associated with the prevalence of NAFLD. We observed that with the level of METS-VF decreases the probability of the odds of NAFLD decreases. METS-VF may be a potential early predictor of NAFLD.

1. Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), initially introduced by Schaffner and Thaler in 1986[1] has since evolved into a significant global health burden. According to a meta-analysis, the worldwide prevalence of NAFLD is 32.4% and the prevalence of NAFLD in the general adult has increased by 37.8% from 2005 to 2016. Meanwhile, they found the NAFLD incidence was higher in males than in females.[2] The liver serves as a central organ for maintaining metabolic homeostasis. Metabolic disorders, including obesity, insulin resistance (IR), type 2 diabetes mellitu, dyslipidemia, and NAFLD, are interconnected via complex molecular, biochemical, and immunological mechanisms within the liver.[3,4] Although NAFLD is usually found in obesity, many epidemiologic data showed that NAFLD may also be present in nonobese individuals, suggesting that, besides obesity, other factors may contribute to development of NAFLD.[5] Several studies reported that among factors related to obesity, visceral fat or visceral fat obesity is more associated with NAFLD than body mass index (BMI).[6–8] The higher visceral or visceral to subcutaneous fat ratio was shown in individuals with NAFLD compared to those without NAFLD.[9] While clinical assessment remains limited by the expensive, time-consuming nature and restricted availability of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) machines, metabolic evaluation of visceral fat (METS-VF) emerges as a convenient index for evaluating metabolic visceral fat.[10] Previous studies have shown an association between NAFLD and some metabolic disease,such as visceral adiposity index,[11] fat mass index,[12] waist-to-height ratio (WHtR)[13], insulin resistance metabolic score metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR).[14] In the Kuang et al study emphasis was placed on age significantly affected both the association between METS-VF and NAFLD odds, and METS-VF’s diagnostic performance for NAFLD in males and females.[15] However, to date, the relationship between METS-VF and NAFLD was very limited. In order to explore the relationship between the 2 and provide new ideas for the monitoring of NAFLD, we conducted this study. As a simple and low-cost test, METS-VF may serve as a simple and reliable index to evaluate the disease in low-income and middle-income countries.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

All data collected in this study was derived from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), in the period 2017 to 2020. The data are published in every 2-year survey cycle and are available online at https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/default.aspx.The components of NHANES include demographic data, questionnaire information, examination data laboratory data and other relevant information. The NHANES survey was reviewed and approved by The National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Committee, and all participants provided their written informed consent. Because the NHANES database is open to the public, this study was exempt from ethics review. Figure 1 illustrates the participants exclusion process. Specific exclusion criteria were applied as follows: (1) Missing controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) values, (2) Missing METS-VF values, (3) Age <20 years, (4) Positive hepatitis B virus surface antigen or positive hepatitis C virus antibody, (5) Excessive alcohol consumers (ever have 4/5 or more drinks every day). A total of 15,560 participants were initially enrolled from the NHANES database. After excluding cases meeting the aforementioned conditions, a total of 3025 participants remained and an initial statistical analysis was performed on this sample.

Flow chart for inclusion of participants in this study. CAP = controlled attenuation parameter, HBV = hepatitis B virus, HCV = hepatitis C virus, METS-VF = metabolic score for visceral fat, NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.2. Diagnostic criteria for NAFLD

NHANES staff use FibroScan 502 Touch (Echosens, Paris, France) device to assess liver stiffness and hepatic steatosis, which uses ultrasound and the vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) to derive liver stiffness. The device also simultaneously measures the ultrasound attenuation related to the presence of hepatic steatosis and records the controlled attenuation parameter (CAPTM) as the indicator for the fatness in the liver. In accordance with previous studies, CAP ≥ 248 dB/m defined as hepatic steatosis. NAFLD is defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis in the absence of excessive alcohol consumption or other chronic liver disease.

2.3. Definition of METS-VF

Metabolic Score for Visceral Fat as a comprehensive indicator for assessing visceral fat can be calculated using the following formula: METS-VF = 3.239 × [Ln (WHtR)]³ + 0.011 × [Ln (METS-IR)]³ + 0.319 × gender (female = 0, male = 1) + 4.466 + [Ln (Age) (year)] × 0.594; METS-IR = BMI (kg/m²) × Ln [TG (mg/dL) + 2 × FPG (mg/dL)]/ Ln [HDL-C (mg/dL)]; BMI = Weight (kg)/ Height2 (m2); WHtR = Waist circumference (cm)/ Height (cm).

2.4. Covariates

Demographic characteristics, such as age (year), gender (male or female), race (Non-Hispanic White or Non-Hispanic Black or Mexican American or other), education level (less than high school and high school, greater than high school), marital status (Married or living with partner or never married or other), income to poverty ratio. The enrolled clinical variables comprised Serum cotinine (ng/mL), moderate work activity (yes or no), BMI (kg/m2), diabetes (yes or no) and hypertension (yes or no). Finally, Laboratory variables included Triglycerides (TG, mmol/L), Total Cholesterol (TC, mg/dL), Aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L), Uric acid (UA, mg/dL), C-reactive protein (CPR, mg/L).

2.5. Statistical analysis

Based on NHANES complex sampling design, the sample weighting and complex study design were considered in all analyses to ensure national estimates were calculated to represent he entire population in the United States. Weighted Student t tests for continuous variables and weighted chi-squared tests for categorical variables were used to assess differences between METS-VF quartiles. Continuous variables were presented as the means ± standard deviations, providing a measure of central tendency and dispersion, while categorical variables were expressed as percentages to illustrate the distribution of subjects in various categories. We used multiple imputation to account for missing data for the Income to poverty ratio (13% missing). Only variables with <1% missing values were retained. We evaluated various combinations of covariates, subsequently using covariates assessed using 3 models in the analysis to control for confounding. Model 1 included no adjustments for covariates, and thus, served as the baseline model to determine the unadjusted association between METS-VF and NAFLD. Model 2 included basic demographic information such as age, gender, race, education level, Income to poverty ratio and marital status. Model 3 was further adjusted for the BMI, TG, TC, UA, diabetes and hypertension. The significance persisted in Model 3 after multivariable adjustment. To test the nonlinear association between METS-VF and NAFLD, a weighted generalized additive model regression and the penalized spline approach were utilized. A generalized additive model was applied to find whether METS-VF and NAFLD were in a nonlinear relationship. Piece-wise regression was used for dividing METS-VF into segments and fitting a separate regression model to each segment and we employed the log-likelihood ratio test to assess which model provided a better fit. The analysis can provide a more comprehensive and precise evaluation of the association between METS-VF and NAFLD across different levels of the exposure variable. Additionally, a multivariate regression analysis that was stratified by gender, age, education level, BMI, diabetes and hypertension was performed in the subgroup analyses and we visualizd the results as a forest map. Data were analyzed with the use of EmpowerStats software (X&Y Solutions Inc., Boston, MA, USA).

Compared to the other 3 groups, participants in quartiles 4 displayed a propensity for mostly male, higher BMI, Higher TG levels and a higher prevalence of diabetes, hypertension. 3.2. The association between METS-VF and NAFLD

We applied 3 linear regression models to investigate the association between METS-VF and NAFLD. All models indicated that METS-VF and NAFLD were positively correlated (Table 2). Grouping the METS-VF ratios into quartiles revealed a consistent correlation, the association between METS-VF and NAFLD was observed to strengthen as the ratio increased (P < .0001). Participants in Q2, Q3, and Q4 exhibited NAFLD odds ratios (ORs) of 6.32 (95% CI, 4.21–9.47), 12.28 (95% CI, 8.05–18.74), and 20.36 (95% CI, 12.97–31.98), respectively (Table 2, Model 1). All comparisons between Q1 and the 3 other groups were statistically significant (all P < .0001). Moreover, following adjustments for additional clinical variables, the corresponding NAFLD ORs for Q2, Q3, and Q4 were 3.04 (95% CI, 1.99–4.65), 4.52 (95% CI, 2.35–8.70), and 6.28 (95% CI, 2.94–13.38), respectively (Table 2, Model 3), in comparison with the METS-VF of the Q1 participants (all P < .05). The incidence of NAFLD increased by 434% for every unit rise in METS-VF after considering for all relevant variables (95% CI: 2.79–6.75, P < .01). (see Table 2)

3.3. Relationship between METS-VF and NAFLD

We explored the potential nonlinear association between METS-VF and NAFLD using generalized additive models and smooth curve fitting. According to Figure 2, our findings indicated a nonlinear positive correlation between the METS-VF and NAFLD and we found an S-shaped relationship between METS-VF and NAFLD with an inflection point of 5.17. The linear relationship between the two was degraded by 2-piecewise regression (P for log-likelihood ratio < 0.05, the 2-segment linear regression providing a better fit), as indicated in Table 3 and Figure 2.

3.4. Subgroup analysis

To assess the overall population’s consistency in the association between METS-VF and NAFLD, subgroup analyses were conducted in terms of gender, age, education level, BMI, diabetes and hypertension. After adjustment for confounding factors, β coefficients of METS-VF and NAFLD remained consistent across all subgroups (all β > 0), notwithstanding that this positive correlation exhibits some degree of instability in diabetic patients (95% CI lower limit < 0 or broader 95% CI). 4. Discussion

METS-VF was used to estimate VAT by combining an IR component METS-IR, an anthropometric component WHtR and a demographic component (age, sex).[10] some studies have found that resistance insulin is the key factor in the pathogenesis of NAFD.[16,17] The metabolic score for insulin resistance METS-IR is a reliable marker for IR, which has also been shown to have good predictive value for NAFLD.[18,19] While BMI is widely used for general obesity assessment, waist circumference and WHtR serve as more accurate indicators of central adiposity, which have also been associated with NAFLD.[20] As a synthesized metric based on these indicators, METS-VF may be a sensitive indicator for assessing NAFLD.[15] Shi et al compared IR indices and abdominal obesity indices, and found that both categories of indices exhibit a significant positive correlation with NAFLD. Further investigations by the same research group revealed that METS-IR presents a “smooth semi-bell-shaped” nonlinear positive correlation with NAFLD, with no obvious threshold effect observed.[5] In our study, threshold effect analysis identified 5.17 as a critical risk inflection point for METS-VF. Compared with single-component indices, this inflection point enables more effective identification of NAFLD risk intervals in U.S. adults, further enhancing the indicator’s application value in clinical risk stratification for NAFLD. Although NAFLD is more prevalent in obese individuals, many epidemiologic data show that nonobese individuals may also suffer from NAFLD.[5] Comparing the results from BMI and other total body fat content, visceral fat or VFO accumulation accompanied is an important disease background of NAFLD.[6–8] Individuals with NAFLD exhibit significantly higher VAT accumulation[6,7] and visceral to subcutaneous fat ratio compared to individuals without NAFLD.[9] Mitochondrial respiration was reduced in VAT of obese humans, but remained unchanged in subcutaneous adipose tissue compared to that in obesity individuals of NAFLD.[21] A mendelian randomization study provided genetic evidence that elevated VAT is causally associated with a higher risk of NAFLD.[22] The METS-VF score performed satisfactorily (AUC of 0.78 (95% CI 0.72–0.85)) for predicting an increased VAT area ≥ 163 cm 2 as detected by DXA.[23] MRI, computed tomography (CT), and DXA are a good method for assessing visceral obesity. Meanwhile, MRI and CT have been shown to accurately quantify the total amount of VAT.[24,25] However, it is difficult to use MRI and CT as a conventional screening tool due to its high cost. The limitations of this imageological examination prompted us to explore easier and more cost-effective evaluation scheme. This study is the first to assess the potential association between the METS-VF and NAFLD in U.S. adults. A significant correlation between elevated METS-VF and increased risk of NAFLD was reported among 3025 subjects. This relationship persisted even when the participants were stratified into subgroups based on age, gender, education level, BMI, diabetes and hypertension. Through threshold effect analysis and smoothing curve fitting, we identified a nonlinear association between METS-VF and NAFLD, which confirmed the robustness of our findings. A METS-VF value of 5.17 was determined to be a significant turning point. Increased frequency of NAFLD was positively associated with increased score of METS-VF above this tipping threshold. Conversely, below this threshold, the correlation between METS-VF and NAFLD was not significant. Studies in U.S. adolescents have shown that METS-VF is positively associated with NAFLD, with an OR 15.74 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 10.44–23.72); the studies also identified a METS-VF threshold of 5.75 for NAFLD.[26] In contrast, we identified a lower METS-VF risk threshold in our study. This finding suggests that the critical threshold of METS-VF contributing to NAFLD may trend downward with increasing age. Kuang et al’s study on Asian populations focused on investigating the effects of age and sex on the association between METS-VF and NAFLD. Results showed that the strength of this association was significantly enhanced in women aged 40 to 60 years, while NAFLD risk increased significantly in men with METS-VF exceeding 6.2.[15] In our subgroup analysis, we found that after stratification by age and sex, the association between METS-VF and NAFLD remained consistently stable, with no significant differences observed across different age or sex groups. This discrepancy in study results may be attributed to ethnic differences among participants. Given its simplicity, accessibility, reliability, and cost-effectiveness, the indicator could be widely implemented in lower middle-income countries.

5. Study strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study was the use of a large, nationally representative data from the United States which minimized the deviation error of the results and permitted application of results to American adults nationwide. Liver biopsy is the gold standard invasive procedure for diagnosis of NAFLD. However, the disadvantage of Liver biopsy is that it is invasive and expensive.[27] Hepatic steatosis was assessed using VCTE, since VCTE is more accurate than ultrasound for diagnosis.[28] Meanwhile, the study has some limitations. To start with, due to the nature of the cross-sectional study design, it was not possible to establish a causal association between METS-VF and NAFLD. Next, VCTE has shown good diagnostic performance but its performance requires additional verification against liver biopsy. Moreover, Although, we considered and adjusted many confounders, the possibility of other potential confounders cannot be completely ruled out. At last, the participants included in this study were of United States, thus, the results may not be equally applicable to other populations. Future studies need to validate and extend our result to other populations and racial.

6. Conclusions

In the American adult population, the METS-VF was nonlinearly and positively associated with the prevalence of NAFLD. Therefore, the control of METS-VF may help reduce the incidence of NAFLD. METS-VF may be an effective marker for the diagnosis of NAFLD and monitoring the efficacy of treatment.
