Shifts in Incident Glucose Lowering Drug Initiation in Type 2 Diabetes From 2014 to 2024: A Population‐Based Study
Authors: Peter S Hamblin, Stella Talic, Harvey J W Koh, Arul Earnest, Anthony W Russell, Sophia Zoungas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis study aimed to analyse incident glucose lowering drug (GLD) utilisation in Australia between the period 2014 and 2024.Materials and MethodsAustralian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) data (10% PBS dataset) were analysed to assess the proportions of incident monotherapy, dual therapy and triple therapy and the proportion of prescriptions dispensed for each GLD class. Annual changes in age at incident GLD initiation were also quantified.Results49 129 individuals were included. The proportion of individuals prescribed incident GLD as monotherapy decreased from 90.4% in 2014 to 77.2% in 2024, while incident dual therapy rose from 9.3% to 21.8% and incident triple therapy from 0.2% to 1.0%. The overall proportion of incident metformin therapy (monotherapy or in combination with other GLDs) decreased from 83.9% in 2014 to 40.1% in 2024, with incident metformin as monotherapy falling from 82.7% to 29.1% over the same period. Proportions of incident SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA (monotherapy or in combination with other GLDs) were 35.7% and 11.2%, respectively, in 2024. Over the study period, age at the time of incident prescribing increased for all GLD classes, except GLP‐1 RA, which decreased.ConclusionsOver the past decade, substantial changes in incident GLD prescribing have occurred in Australia. While metformin continues to play a central role as initial treatment, major shifts toward use of initial dual therapy, including SGLT2i and GLP‐1RA, have occurred.Keywords: antidiabetic drug, database research, pharmaco‐epidemiology, population study, real‐world evidence, type 2 diabetes

Introduction

The past decade has witnessed a paradigm shift in the management of type 2 diabetes (T2D), driven by landmark cardiovascular outcome trials demonstrating that certain glucose lowering drugs (GLDs) confer benefits beyond glycaemic control, including reductions in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), heart failure (HF) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1, 2]. These findings have prompted guideline updates recommending sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) for patients with T2D and high cardiometabolic risk [3]. Consequently, prescribing patterns have evolved substantially, with newer agents being more frequently prescribed in clinical practice.

Recent real‐world data indicate a marked rise in SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA utilisation, accompanied by a relative decline in older drug classes such as sulphonylureas and insulin. For example, in US cohorts, SGLT2i use increased from 2.8% in 2018 to 12.2% in 2022, while GLP‐1 RA use rose from 5.2% to 9.9% [4], with similar trends observed in Australia [5]. Similarly, tirzepatide, a dual GIP (glucose‐dependent isulinotropic polypeptide)/GLP‐1 agonist, rapidly gained market share following its approval, accounting for 12.3% of GLD prescriptions by late 2023 [6]. These trends have coincided with a decline in metformin initiation rates in the United States from 30% to 19% between 2021 and 2023, as GLP‐1 therapies increasingly dominate first‐line prescribing [6].

Despite these shifts, metformin continues to be commonly prescribed and remains the first‐line therapy recommended by many guidelines for glucose lowering, particularly for people without ASCVD, HF or CKD. For example, a Canadian study reported that 89% of patients initiating GLD treatment were prescribed metformin monotherapy [7]. Similarly, studies from the United Kingdom and Australia suggest that while newer agents are increasingly used as add‐on therapies, sulphonylureas, dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP4is) and insulin persist in routine practice, particularly among older adults and in settings where access to newer drugs is restricted [8, 9, 10]. However, contemporary data on incident first‐line GLD utilisation remain limited, as much of the existing literature focuses on prevalent GLD use or second‐line treatment intensification rather than incident first‐line prescribing. As guideline recommendations have increasingly endorsed SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA earlier in the treatment pathway for patients with cardiorenal comorbidities, understanding how these recommendations have influenced initial prescribing decisions has become critical for evaluating the real‐world impact of evidence‐based recommendations and identifying gaps in implementation.

The aim of this study was to assess initial GLD utilisation in the last decade and to describe changes in prescribing patterns with the introduction of newer cardioprotective therapies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a population‐based retrospective cohort study using routinely collected dispensing data from the Australian Government's 10% Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) sample. The PBS dataset includes person‐level records of all government‐subsidised medicines dispensed in community pharmacies, private hospitals and public hospitals where PBS medicines are supplied to outpatients. The 10% sample is generated through a random selection of individuals based on the last digit of their Medicare number, ensuring representativeness of the national population. The dataset contains detailed information on dispensing dates, medicine strength and quantity, prescriber type and patient demographic characteristics, but does not include clinical diagnoses, laboratory results or inpatient prescribing. To protect anonymity, dates of birth and death are offset, and data are suppressed if fewer than three people are dispensed a medicine and data for people over the age of 99 years. Authors had full access to the 10% PBS dataset.

Population

The study population consisted of adults (> 18 years of age) with T2D, identified using dispensing records for GLD captured in the 10% PBS sample. Medication use was used as the primary method for defining diabetes, consistent with pharmacoepidemiologic practice in administrative datasets where clinical diagnoses are not available. Relevant diabetes‐related PBS item codes were mapped to the World Health Organization (WHO) Anatomic therapeutic chemical (ATC) classification for drugs used in diabetes (Table S1). In Australia, prescriptions for use of SGLT2i for diabetes require a specific PBS streamlined Authority item code, while a separate code applies for non‐diabetes indications. To ensure our analysis focused on GLD use among people with diabetes, we identified SGLT2i prescriptions using PBS item codes specific to diabetes rather than the ATC code, which does not differentiate between diabetes and non‐diabetes indications, given that SGLT2is are often prescribed for non‐diabetes indications such as HF and CKD. After noting that only 2.1% of people living with T2D registered with the Australian National Diabetes Services Scheme were under the age of 40 [11] and in line with other studies that excluded individuals younger than 40 years, to further ensure we captured the T2D population only, we excluded incident insulin therapy users (to exclude those with type 1 DM) and individuals younger than 40 years (to exclude those with polycystic ovarian syndrome and gestational diabetes for which metformin is frequently prescribed) [12].

Exposure and Outcome

The term ‘glucose lowering drugs’ has been used throughout because the WHO ATC codes refer to SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RAs only as GLDs, despite their well‐known glucose‐independent cardiovascular and renal effects. The 10% PBS dataset was used to access data on all incident dispensing of the first non‐insulin GLD in the period from 2014 to 2024 inclusive. The index date was defined as the date of the first recorded GLD dispensing within the study year. Incident use was assessed at two levels. Overall incident GLD initiation was defined as the first‐ever dispensing of any GLD following the 1‐year lookback period, including instances where multiple classes were initiated on the same day. Class‐specific incident use was then identified for each GLD class (metformin, sulphonylureas, DPP4is, SGLT2is, GLP‐1 RAs, peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma [PPARγ], acarbose), whereby individuals were classified as incident users of a given class only if they had no prior exposure to that class. This approach ensured that true treatment initiation was distinguished from subsequent therapeutic switching or intensification.

The numbers of GLDs dispensed as monotherapy, fixed dose combinations (FDC) and variable dose combinations (two or more individual GLDs or a FDC together with one or more individual GLDs dispensed on the same day) were analysed. Individual components of FDCs were treated as separate prescriptions for analysis. Age (mean [SD] and age categories 40–49; 50–59; 60–69; 70–79; 80–89; 90–99 years), sex (male, female), concessional beneficiary status (general, concessional) and prescriber types (general practitioner, endocrinologist, cardiologist, nephrologist, internal medicine specialist, other [e.g., respiratory physicians, rheumatologists, gastroenterologists, neurologists, surgeons, nurse practitioners]) were also obtained (Table S2).

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the data. Categorical variables are reported as counts and proportions, while continuous variables are reported as means ± standard deviations [SD]. Incident GLD use was defined using a new‐user design to minimise bias related to prior treatment exposure. Changes in the types of FDC dispensed over time were assessed. The relative proportions of each GLD class were calculated for each year as were the relative proportions of monotherapy versus dual or triple incident therapy. Yearly trends in the age at the time of first GLD use from 2014 to 2024 were assessed. Trends in incident GLD utilisation were assessed using the Joinpoint Regression Program, Version 5.4.0‐April 2025 (Statistical Methodology and Applications Branch, Surveillance Research Program, NCI) [13]. Joinpoint software uses permutation tests to identify time points where linear trends change significantly. The annual percentage change for each linear trend segment was calculated. Joinpoint analysis assumed homoscedasticity (constant variance). Data were log transformed to minimise variance in the series. Weighted Bayesian information criterion was applied and empirical quantiles were used for confidence intervals. Temporal trends of the mean age of starting each incident GLD as monotherapy were assessed using weighted linear regression, with weights based on the variable annual sample sizes of prescriptions across the study period. Linear trends in monotherapy, dual therapy and triple therapy were assessed by the Cochran‐Armitage test.

To reduce misclassification arising from treatment cycling or re‐initiation, individuals could contribute only one incident episode during the study period. Some degree of misclassification may persist, particularly in later study years as treatment sequencing becomes more complex and as individuals re‐initiate therapy after extended treatment gaps. However, the use of a new‐user design, class‐specific definitions and sensitivity analyses was intended to minimise bias and improve the validity of observed temporal trends.

To ensure robustness and validate our findings, two sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, a sensitivity analysis using a two‐year lookback period was performed to accurately capture incident users. Second, to address the potential for non‐diabetes SGLT2i use, despite excluding HF and CKD indications in the selection criteria, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis of GLD utilisation that excluded data from cardiology and nephrology prescribers.

Data analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 SE (StataCorp, College Station, TX), with level of significance 5%.

Ethics Approvals

Ethics approval was granted by Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee (Project ID: 39870) and access to the 10% PBS dataset was approved by the External Request Evaluation Committee of Services Australia (RMS4083).

Results

In total, 49 129 people (mean age 60.8 years [SD 12.1]; 56.0% male) were identified after applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure S1) with 60 177 GLD scripts dispensed. Demographic details of people treated with monotherapy or multiple incident GLDs are shown in Table S3. In a sensitivity analysis using a two‐year lookback, the number of incident GLD users did not change.

Number of GLDs Utilised

Table 1 shows the proportion of individuals who were treated with incident monotherapy, dual therapy or triple therapy. The proportion treated with monotherapy fell from 90.4% in 2014 to 77.2% in 2024 (p < 0.001). Over the same period, the proportion treated with dual therapy increased from 9.3% in 2014 to 21.8% in 2024 (p < 0.001). Incident treatment with three drugs, dispensed on the same day, increased from 0.2% in 2014 to 1.0% in 2024 (p < 0.001). There was a higher proportion of males treated with initial dual or triple therapy compared with monotherapy (Table S3). In a sensitivity analysis excluding cardiologist and nephrologist prescribers, GLD monotherapy fell from 90.4% to 75.0% between 2014 and 2024, dual therapy increased from 9.3% to 24.0%, and triple therapy increased from 0.2% to 1.1% (Table S4).

Type of GLD Utilised

Metformin (composite of monotherapy, component of FDC and variable dose combinations) was the dominant incident GLD every year from 2014 to 2024 (Figure 1). It comprised 83.9% of all incident GLD prescriptions in 2014, but this fell to 40.1% in 2024 (Figure 2; Tables S5 and S6). Metformin monotherapy showed a steeper decline (Figure S2, Tables S5 and S6), falling from 82.7% of all individuals initiating a GLD in 2014 to 29.1% in 2024 (Figure S3).

Incident total SGLT2i utilisation (as monotherapy or a component of FDC and variable dose combinations) increased rapidly after 2021 and by 2024 was close to that of metformin (2729 metformin prescriptions vs. 2427 SGLT2i) (Figure 1). The major change was an increase in the use of SGLT2i as monotherapy. SGLT2i monotherapy utilisation was stable between 2016 and 2020, but increased by 82.0% per year (95% CI 45.5, 199.8, p < 0.001) from 2020 to 2024 (Tables S5 and S6). Incident SGLT2i as monotherapy surpassed that of incident metformin monotherapy in 2024 for the first time (Figure S2).

Incident total GLP‐1 RA utilisation (as monotherapy or a component of variable dose combinations) increased by 60.2% per year (95% CI 47.4, 74.3, p < 0.001) from 2014 to 2024. GLP‐1 RA monotherapy use increased by 72.7% per year (95% CI 44.9, 106.0, p < 0.001) from 2014 (Tables S5 and S6).

Incident total DPP4i utilisation (as monotherapy or as a component of FDC and variable dose combinations) increased by 23.7% per year (95% CI 19.1, 29.5, p < 0.001) between 2014 and 2020 and plateaued thereafter with a non‐significant change of −6.2% per year (95% CI −13.9, +0.2, p = 0.059) to 2024. DPP4i monotherapy use fell significantly by 11.3% per year (95% CI −17.7, −5.7, p < 0.001) from 2020, having increased by 32.8% per year (95% CI 28.5, 37.9, p < 0.001) from 2014 to 2020 (Figure 1, Tables S5 and S6).

Incident total sulphonylurea utilisation (as monotherapy or a component of FDC and variable dose combinations) increased by 35.6% per year (95% CI 2.20, 89.8, p = 0.036) from 2014 to 2016, then plateaued (Figure 1, Tables S5 and S6). Sulphonylurea monotherapy use did not change over the study period (Tables S5 and S6).

Prescription numbers for acarbose and PPARγ, as monotherapy or in variable combinations with other GLDs, were very low (less than 30 prescriptions annually for each drug).

Until 2022, the leading incident FDC was DPP4i with metformin. From 2023, SGLT2i with metformin was the most used (Figure S4). Although the use of the FDC SGLT2i with DPP4i steadily increased from its introduction in 2018, it only constituted 7.1% of all FDCs by 2024.

In a sensitivity analysis which excluded SGLT2i monotherapy prescriptions by cardiologist and nephrologist prescribers, the number of SGLT2i prescriptions fell from 2427 to 1943 in 2024. As a result, the proportion of total prescriptions due to metformin increased to 43.2% (compared with 40.1% with cardiologist and nephrologist prescribers included). Although moderated, a large rise in total SGLT2i prescriptions persisted (Figures S5 and S6).

Age and GLD Utilisation

The mean age and change in age of individuals starting their first GLD prescription as monotherapy are shown in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. Individuals receiving metformin monotherapy were younger than those receiving the other GLD classes for most of the study period (Table 2), with DPP4i recipients the oldest. There was a small steady increase in the age of those receiving their first prescription of metformin and a large increase in the age of SGLT2i treated individuals. There were modest increases in age of initiation among those treated with sulphonylurea and DPP4i, while those prescribed GLP‐1 RA showed a gradual decrease in age of initiation.

In 2024, of those receiving incident monotherapy, the predominant age categories were the 70–79 age group for SGLT2i (n = 518, 30.6%); 60–69 age group for DPP4i (n = 35, 26.1%) and sulphonylurea (n = 39, 34.8%); and 50–59 age group for metformin (n = 419, 26.4%) and GLP‐1 RA (n = 223, 32.5%) (Table S7).

Prescriber Type and GLD Utilisation

In 2024, primary care doctors were the predominant prescribers of all incident monotherapy GLDs, with a maximum of 87.4% of metformin prescriptions and a minimum of 74.6% of DPP4i prescriptions (Table S8). After primary care, cardiologists were the next highest prescribers of SGLT2i monotherapy (6.2%) and endocrinologists the next highest prescribers of GLP‐1 RA monotherapy (6.4%).

Discussion

In this large Australian population‐based study of GLD utilisation over an 11‐year period, we found substantial changes in both the number and types of GLD initiated for the management of T2D. First, the dominance of metformin as initial therapy progressively declined over the study period, with a halving of the proportion of metformin initiations in 2024 compared with 2014 (83.9%–40.1% of all GLD initiations). Second, the proportion of people starting dual therapy as initial treatment more than doubled (9.3%–21.8%) and initial triple therapy increased fivefold from 0.2% to 1.0%. Third, incident SGLT2i monotherapy increased by 82.0% per year from 2020 and incident monotherapy with GLP‐1 RA by 72.7% per year from 2014. The decline in GLP‐1 RA initiations from 2023 to 2024 likely reflects the Australian Government campaign to prescribers in late 2022 and during 2023 reminding them that under PBS rules, metformin and SGLT2i should be prescribed before GLP‐1 RAs (the reason being the significantly higher cost of GLP‐1 RAs). Tirzepatide is not available on the PBS. Fourth, the age at which GLDs were initiated increased for all classes, except for GLP‐1 RA, which declined. The increase in age was particularly evident for people initiating SGLT2i after 2020.

Our findings show that over the past decade, there has been a marked change in incident subsidised GLD dispensing patterns, increasingly consistent with cardiorenal‐oriented prescribing. The increase in initial dual and triple therapy may reflect earlier intensification, but may also reflect the availability of FDC, reimbursement changes, non‐diabetes use or growing treatment of older, comorbid patients. The shift toward the use of GLDs with cardiovascular and renal benefits and away from metformin and DPP4i has occurred despite Australian guidelines not yet recommending first‐line use of SGLT2i or GLP‐1 RAs [14]. It should be noted that metformin remains the dominant GLD numerically. The decline in proportionate use of metformin in part reflects the increasing use of FDC. Incident sulphonylurea use has remained low and stable. The annual number of new GLD initiations in our study is consistent with the reported incidence of T2D in Australia [15].

These incident prescribing trends are consistent with data from Denmark and Australia which also found significant increases in GLP‐1 RA and SGLT2i incident use [16, 17] and a trend toward earlier T2D treatment intensification [16]. Another Danish study found that most GLD initiations were by general practitioners but that while GLP‐1 RA tended to be prescribed by general practitioners, specialists were more likely to initiate SGLT2i [18]. An Australian study of add‐on GLD therapy after metformin (second‐line treatment) also reported an increasing proportion of SGLT2i and GLP1 RA use between 2018 and 2022 and slightly earlier treatment intensification each year [11]. A case can be made for a focus on initial weight loss with lifestyle, medications or weight loss surgery for everyone newly diagnosed with T2D to try to achieve remission.

Despite our attempt to restrict this study to the use of SGLT2i for diabetes, a proportion of prescriptions are likely to have been for people without diabetes who have HF or CKD. The marked rise in prescriptions by cardiologists compared with historical prescribing patterns is suggestive of this. However, even after excluding prescriptions by cardiologists and nephrologists, the rise in SGLT2i therapy was still marked. However, excluding cardiology and nephrology prescribers removes only part of the potential non‐diabetes prescribing signal and residual uncertainty regarding the indication for SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA by other prescriber groups remains. The PBS restricts GLP‐1 RA use only to people living with T2D. Despite audits of prescribers to ensure compliance with government subsidisation criteria, it is possible that a proportion of monotherapy with GLP‐1 RAs was for the treatment of obesity rather than T2D.

The increase in age of incident SGLT2i users over the study period is likely due to prescribers preferencing this GLD class in people with co‐existing HF or CKD. People with these comorbidities are more likely to be older [19, 20]. The reason for the increases in the age at which metformin, DPP4i and sulphonylurea were initiated requires further study. It is possible that it relates to the ageing of the Australian population and more proactive detection of T2D in older people. A systematic review of factors influencing first‐line prescribing for T2D around the world found that incident metformin prescribing was strongly and positively associated with younger age [21]. In our study, the age of metformin users was the lowest of all classes until 2020 after which the mean age of GLP‐1 RA users dropped below that of metformin users. This is consistent with the development of a more nuanced approach to GLD prescribing for younger people. The reducing age for GLP‐1 RA initiation may also potentially relate to use in a proportion of people living with obesity without co‐existing T2D. As a descriptive study, the data cannot distinguish between diabetes‐specific prescribing, obesity treatment, and comorbidity‐driven off‐label or quasi‐indication use.

The observed shift toward GLDs with established cardiovascular and renal benefits, alongside reduced initiation of metformin and DPP4is, indicates increasing prioritisation of outcomes beyond glycaemic control. This pattern may suggest that prescribers are incorporating cardiometabolic risk into initial treatment decisions, rather than relying on glucocentric or historically entrenched prescribing paradigms. The persistently low and stable incidence of sulphonylurea initiation is consistent with heightened awareness of their adverse risk profile, particularly hypoglycaemia and weight gain. This finding may support the notion that safety considerations increasingly influence prescribing decisions in contemporary T2D care.

Changes in initial therapy are likely driven by broader system‐level factors, including evolving clinical guidelines, reimbursement policies, and diffusion of cardiovascular outcomes evidence, rather than individual prescriber behaviour alone. Additionally, nearly a quarter of patients are starting with dual therapy rather than initial monotherapy. The gradual nature of the shifts observed underscores the complexity of translating trial evidence into routine clinical practice. While earlier use of combination therapy may offer potential clinical benefits, it also raises important questions regarding treatment complexity, adherence, equity of access and long‐term real‐world effectiveness.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strength of this study relates to the comprehensiveness of the 10% PBS dataset and that it is representative of all incident GLD initiations in Australia. There is negligible T2D private prescribing in Australia. Limitations include the likelihood that some of the data for SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA use is for people who do not have T2D. Therefore, the magnitude of the changes described needs to be treated with caution. If a material fraction of prescribing is for non‐diabetes indications, which is minimised by government access restrictions and auditing, the changes described would over‐estimate the described shifts in initial diabetes management. In addition, a proportion of metformin use may be used for treatment of pre‐diabetes or for polycystic ovarian syndrome in women older than 39 years of age.

Conclusion

Incident dispensing patterns have markedly changed and appear increasingly consistent with a cardiorenal orientation. Use of dual and triple GLD therapy as initial treatment has more than doubled. Future studies using linked clinical and outcomes data are needed to evaluate whether these evolving prescribing patterns translate into improved patient outcomes and identify populations who may be left behind.
