Blood bag rupture due to centrifuge bucket damage: A call for routine equipment maintenance
Authors: Rajat Bansal, Pandeep Kaur, Amit Kumar Chatterjee
Published: 2026

Sir,

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) preparation relies on standardized centrifugation protocols, but maintenance often focuses on calibration rather than the structural integrity of centrifuge buckets. Polycarbonate buckets subjected to repeated cycles can develop surface deformities that risk blood bag rupture and product failure.[12] We report a blood bag rupture attributable to bucket damage during PRP preparation.

A 45-year-old male with Kellgren–Lawrence Grade III knee osteoarthritis underwent PRP therapy. Eighty milliliters of whole blood was collected into a 100 mL Citrate Phosphate Dextrose Adenine (CPDA) blood bag and processed in a Thermo Scientific Heraeus Cryofuge 6000i using a soft-spin protocol (850 rpm; 118 × g; 9 min at 22°C). After centrifugation, blood was found spilled inside the bucket. Inspection revealed a 3-mm inward indentation on the inner wall of the polycarbonate bucket, corresponding to the laceration site on the bag. The bucket had been in continuous use for over 7 years, with an estimated 12,000 cycles, and had not undergone any structured surface integrity assessment.

Polycarbonate, though transparent and impact resistant, undergoes fatigue-related crazing and crack formation under cyclic loading, especially at stress concentrators.[23] These microdefects may remain unnoticed until they create sharp projections that compromise bag integrity at high g-forces. Existing maintenance schedules for bench-top centrifuges typically mandate periodic verification of speed and temperature but seldom specify systematic inspection or retirement criteria for aging buckets.[4]

This case highlights the need to formally incorporate bucket integrity into centrifuge maintenance programs. We suggest routine visual and tactile inspection of buckets before use, detailed surface checks during preventive maintenance, and documentation of cumulative spin cycles with predefined retirement thresholds derived from manufacturer recommendations and fatigue data.[34] Staff should be trained to recognize early signs of wear such as roughness, ridges, or whitening of polycarbonate surfaces and to promptly withdraw suspicious buckets from service.

Manufacturers should clearly state the expected service life and maximum permissible cycle count for buckets and include inspection guidance in user manuals. Professional societies and regulatory bodies may consider including centrifuge bucket integrity in quality and safety standards for PRP and blood component preparation. Early recognition of mechanical fatigue in centrifuge buckets can prevent avoidable product loss and enhance patient safety.
