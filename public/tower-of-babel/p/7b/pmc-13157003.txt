Assessing plague risk across seven decades of environmental change in Inner Mongolia, China: An interpretable machine learning
Authors: Xiaoxu Wang, Meng Shang, Chunchun Zhao, Yelin Sun, Lianfang Feng, Lianxu Xia, Qiyong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPlague, caused by Yersinia pestis, persists as a public health threat, particularly in natural foci such as China’s Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region. Understanding the distinct environmental drivers of pathogen transmission from wildlife reservoirs to humans is critical for targeted control. We integrated long-term human case data (1950–2023) with extensive rodent serological surveillance (2005–2023) to model these dynamics. Using a Generalized Additive Model and interpretable machine learning, we found that human cases were concentrated in specific foci and nonlinearly linked to proximity to railways and precipitation, but not rivers. In contrast, enzootic maintenance was predominantly driven by a negative correlation with nighttime light, nonlinear climatic effects, and positive associations with specific land uses (water, forest, residential). Our framework reveals that spillover and enzootic cycles are governed by differing environmental factors. This approach enables spatially refined risk assessment, guiding surveillance to incorporate the distinct anthropogenic and climatic drivers of plague transmission.

Introduction

Plague is a serious infectious disease caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, for which rodents are the main host and fleas serve as the vector.1 Transmission to humans can lead to sporadic cases, outbreaks, or large-scale epidemics, manifesting as bubonic, pneumonic, or septicemic plague.1 Historically, plague caused three devastating pandemics, resulting in over 100 million deaths.1,2,3 Recent decades have witnessed a marked recrudescence of plague cases globally, raising significant public health concerns.4

China maintains 12 documented natural plague foci, with the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region representing an epidemiologically critical zone harboring four major enzootic foci: Hulunbuir Plateau Natural Plague Focus, Song-Liao Plain Natural Plague Focus, Ordos Plateau Natural Plague Focus, and Xilingol Plateau Natural Plague Focus.5,6 Since 2019, this region has emerged as an active transmission epicenter. A notable outbreak occurred in November 2019 when two pneumonic plague cases from the Ordos Plateau Natural Plague Focus led to the quarantine of 447 contacts in Beijing, generating substantial public alarm.7 Neighboring Mongolia and adjacent Chinese regions have also reported numerous cases in recent years,8,9,10,11 underscoring the widespread enzootic circulation of the pathogen.12 Given plague’s high transmissibility and mortality, the risk of outbreaks necessitates sustained epidemiological vigilance and research.

Global environmental change is reshaping the distribution of zoonotic pathogens. Scientific consensus indicates that climate change, land-use transformation, and expanding human-wildlife interfaces collectively escalate spillover risks.13 Research on environmental drivers of plague has predominantly focused on three frameworks: (1) climate-driven models linking meteorological factors (e.g., temperature and humidity) to host-vector dynamics14,15; (2) landscape ecological models emphasizing the role of altitude, vegetation, and soil conditions in shaping host habitat suitability16,17,18; and (3) socioeconomic models examining how expanded transport networks facilitate pathogen diffusion.19 However, significant limitations persist. Many existing studies rely on single data sources and lack integrated long-term wildlife surveillance, thereby preventing comprehensive multi-scale spatiotemporal analyses that link long-term trends with local ecological contexts.13 Furthermore, holistic analyses that simultaneously quantify climatic, landscape, and socioeconomic drivers remain scarce, limiting both predictive accuracy and the development of targeted interventions.18

Machine learning algorithms, with their superior capacity to model complex nonlinear relationships and interaction effects, show great promise in infectious disease prediction, as evidenced in studies of dengue and malaria.20,21,22 Their application to plague ecology, however, remains underexplored. Equally importantly, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), a game theory-based interpretability framework, identifies key predictors and local interaction patterns in complex systems.22 By combining machine learning, this approach extracts actionable insights from noisy data, enhancing trust in data-driven interventions while clarifying disease transmission mechanisms. Its application improves the detection of plague spread patterns.

To address these research gaps, this study integrates long-term human case data and wildlife serological surveillance to develop a multidimensional analytical framework for the plague foci in Inner Mongolia (Figure 1). We hypothesize that: (1) The spatial distribution of human plague cases is predominantly determined by proximity to specific linear landscape features (e.g., railways) that structure the human-animal interface, rather than by broad regional climatic gradients. (2) The enzootic maintenance of Y. pestis in wildlife reservoirs is driven by a distinct set of local environmental factors (Table 1) related to host habitat suitability, which can be effectively identified and interpreted using machine learning approaches. By testing these hypotheses, our study aims to provide an integrated perspective on the environmental drivers of plague transmission across different epidemiological stages—from wildlife reservoir maintenance to human spillover. The findings are expected to enhance the theoretical understanding of plague eco-epidemiology and offer practical, evidence-based insights for improving spatial risk assessment and guiding targeted surveillance and prevention strategies in high-risk regions.

Results

The distribution of plague cases in Inner Mongolia

During the period from 1950 to 2023, a total of 277 human plague cases with 136 fatalities were reported in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region (Table 2; Figure 2). Among these, the natural focus of plague in S. dauricus accounted for 246 human cases and 127 human deaths, yielding a human case fatality rate (CFR) of 51.84%. In contrast, the M. unguiculatus natural focus reported 31 human cases and 9 human deaths, corresponding to a human CFR of 29.03%. Bubonic plague was the predominant clinical form across both natural foci, comprising 242 cases (87.4%), followed by pneumonic plague with 28 cases (10.1%); other forms were rare (Tables 2, S1, and S2). Notably, the proportion of pneumonic plague cases was significantly higher in the M. unguiculatus natural focus (10/31, 32.3%) than in the S. dauricus focus (18/246, 7.3%). Plague pathogen positivity is influenced by multiple environmental factors

Spearman correlation analysis revealed statistically significant correlations among a subset of the 22 variables associated with plague positivity rates (Figure S7). Subsequent elimination of variables with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.8 resulted in the retention of 16 variables, including the NTL index, climatic factors (mean temperature, mean precipitation, and wind speed), vegetation cover, land surface characteristics, railway density, grazing intensity, elevation, and land use types. Multicollinearity diagnostics confirmed the absence of significant multicollinearity among the retained variables. The ROC curves for random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and AdaBoost (AB) models are presented in Figure 6. The AUC values of these models were 0.84, 0.64, and 0.62, respectively. To conduct a more comprehensive assessment, we carried out a comparative analysis of their evaluation indices, as presented in Table 5. The RF algorithm exhibited the optimal performance, achieving 81% accuracy, a precision of 0.82, a recall of 0.90, and an F1 score of 0.85. Discussion

Our study demonstrates that human cases exhibited distinct regional clustering and seasonal variation, with spatial distribution significantly coupled with railway networks and disease risk showing an inverse S-shaped association with annual precipitation. Rodent plague seroprevalence was primarily influenced by NTL index, climatic factors, and land use types, including water areas, forest areas, and residential areas. These findings support two mechanisms: 1) trophic cascade effects, where extreme temperatures and moderate precipitation amplify plague prevalence through ecosystem productivity-mediated effects on rodent-flea population dynamics, and 2) socioeconomic-ecological coupling effects, where railway infrastructure directly shapes human case distribution, while rodent plague seroprevalence was driven by NTL exposure and land use patterns.

Previous studies have reported inconsistent or even contradictory associations between plague and precipitation.23,24 These discrepancies may stem from variations in temporal and spatial scales across different regions and studies. Our research reveals an inverted S-shaped relationship between rainfall and both human cases and the rodent plague model. On multiple continents, moderate precipitation has been shown to enhance rodent and flea populations by increasing food availability.25 This pattern aligns with the trophic cascade hypothesis.26,27 Population growth may lead to heightened human-animal contact, thereby increasing human cases.28 In Mongolia’s semi-arid steppes, preliminary studies suggest a positive correlation between plague risk and high precipitation when marmot densities exceed a threshold level.29 However, Xu et al. also highlight regional differences in the impact of rainfall on plague intensity between northern and southern China.24 In northern regions, humid conditions favor rodents, but excessive rainfall may flood their burrows, directly killing plague-carrying rodents. A study in Uganda supports this observation, finding a positive correlation between plague risk and discontinuous rainfall in February, October, and November, but a negative correlation with continuous rainfall in June.25 This phenomenon may help explain the inverted S-shaped effect of annual precipitation on plague intensity in Inner Mongolia.

Similar to the effects of precipitation, our results also suggested nonlinear associations between temperature and plague positivity. Our study revealed a U-shaped relationship between the plague positivity rate and mean temperature in the rodent model. We hypothesize that this pattern stems from the multifactorial effects of temperature on pathogen viability, vector competence, host ecology, and human behavior. For example, in Madagascar, the developmental rates of the two primary flea vectors (Synopsyllus fonquerniei and Xenopsylla cheopis) exhibited a positive correlation with temperature, with a marked divergence occurring around 21°C.30 In the U.S., fleas maintained at 10°C and 15°C demonstrated significantly higher plague transmission efficiency compared to those at 23°C.31 These findings suggest that temperature exerts region-specific, nonlinear effects on flea-mediated transmission dynamics. In contrast, human spillover events were most likely to occur during wet and cold years, consistent with prior observations in other studies.13 This phenomenon has been attributed to an alternative tropical cascade mechanism: when adverse seasonal fluctuations follow a rodent population peak, the subsequent population decline leads to flea aggregation on the remaining rodents. This not only enhances intraspecific plague transmission but also increases flea host-seeking behavior for alternative blood meals, thereby elevating spillover risk to humans.32

Although human transportation systems have long been implicated as critical facilitators of disease transmission, their impacts are rarely tested. Historical analyses have revealed potential associations between railway networks and plague transmission: An early investigation in 1911 demonstrated higher plague incidence in villages adjacent to railways compared to other regions,33 hypothesizing that railways enhanced interpersonal connectivity between villagers and frequent infection hotspots while concurrently promoting cargo transportation—particularly grain shipments—that could disseminate infected rodents or fleas.34 Our study reveals clustered human plague cases in Inner Mongolia, with distributions strongly linked to railways. Case density peaked 12.5–50 km from railways, yet railway density did not affect infection rates in wild rodents. These findings resonate with Xu et al.'s research on the Third Pandemic, which demonstrated positive correlations between plague propagation velocity and proximity to roads.19 Synthesizing these results suggests that railway systems may predominantly facilitate human plague transmission through intensified population mobility and material circulation, while exerting limited influence on the population ecology of animal reservoirs or disease maintenance mechanisms within natural foci.

The NTL index serves as a proxy for human landscape modification.35 Its negative association with rodent plague positivity likely reflects reduced habitat suitability for key sylvatic reservoirs (e.g., S. dauricus and M. unguiculatus) in intensively urban or cultivated areas. Additionally, artificial nighttime light may influence enzootic dynamics through biological pathways, such as disrupting rodent circadian rhythms and immune function.36,37 However, as plague in Inner Mongolia is maintained in a sylvatic cycle and the studied rodents are wild species, the observed correlation may partly reflect the natural absence of both reservoirs and plague in urban centers. Thus, while NTL broadly signals lower enzootic transmission in highly anthropized landscapes, it remains a coarse proxy that conflates urban, rural, and interface environments.38 Future studies should incorporate more direct metrics—such as human/livestock density and fine-scale land cover—to better distinguish the socio-ecological drivers of plague persistence in peri-urban, pastoral, and natural interface habitats.

The negative correlation between near-surface wind speed and plague outbreaks primarily stems from its dual inhibitory effects on host behavior and vector transmission. High wind speeds reduce the duration of ground activity in rodents, such as the Arctic ground squirrel (Urocitellus parryii), which decreases its surface activity by 16.3 min at wind speeds exceeding 4 m/s. This behavioral suppression limits contact between hosts and fleas, disrupting the transmission chain. Additionally, strong winds significantly shorten flea survival times in the air, reducing their dispersal capacity.32

Research on plague ecoepidemiology often overlooks the impact of land-use changes on disease transmission, typically focusing on climate, environment, presence of large mammals, and host abundance. Our investigation revealed that expansions in water, forest, and residential land increase the likelihood of animal plague occurrence. The expansion of water and forest areas can create more favorable habitats for disease vectors such as rodents and fleas, consequently heightening the risk of plague transmission. Historical analysis of the Black Death and landscape dynamics in Europe indicated that the arable contraction and forest succession in certain regions may have contributed to plague outbreaks.17 Similarly, a study in Tanzania demonstrated that fallow land and forests serve as conducive shelters and food sources for rodents, promoting their population growth while mitigating predation risks.39 Notably, our study reveals for the first time the relationship between land-use area changes and plague risk. It has been observed that once residential areas surpass a certain threshold, there is a notable escalation in plague risk. This phenomenon is likely attributed to the fragmentation of natural habitats resulting from residential expansion, thereby heightening the probability of interactions between rodents and humans. Furthermore, the proliferation of residential areas can lead to an accumulation of waste and food remnants, which in turn attracts rodents, thereby amplifying the risk of plague dissemination.40 Consequently, forthcoming research endeavors should delve deeper into elucidating the causal link between changes in land utilization and the transmission of plague. Thoughtful urban planning and enhanced surveillance efforts are imperative for the effective prevention and management of plague outbreaks.

Our findings provide actionable inputs for public health strategy while highlighting the broader relevance of our framework. The identified link between human cases and railways defines transportation corridors as priority surveillance zones, especially during seasons of moderate precipitation (250–550 mm) and lower temperatures. Similarly, expansions in water and forest areas elevate enzootic risk, underscoring the need to integrate zoonotic assessment into land-use management. These insights advocate for strengthening integrated One Health surveillance systems. Beyond regional application, our study carries significant international relevance: while core plague ecology is universal, the dominant socio-ecological drivers are context-specific. In contrast to African foci, where domestic rodent populations and household storage drive transmission, or to South American hotspots with distinct sylvatic cycles, our work identifies infrastructure and large-scale landscape change as pivotal in semi-arid, developing regions. This comparison emphasizes that effective global preparedness requires evidence-based, regionally tailored models. Our analytical framework offers a transferable approach for assessing plague risk across diverse settings, reinforcing the need for context-specific, data-informed policies worldwide.

Limitations of the study

This study has several methodological and data limitations. Firstly, our modeling results demonstrate correlational relationships, with causality difficult to establish. This challenge is compounded by the fact that seropositivity indicates past exposure rather than concurrent infection. Furthermore, individual-level demographic data such as age and sex were not available in the de-identified surveillance dataset used for this spatial-temporal analysis, underscoring the need for more detailed field studies. Secondly, inherent uncertainties exist in key input datasets. The NTL data, derived from two different satellite sensors (DMSP-OLS and NPP/VIIRS), may contain inconsistencies that affect temporal comparability. Similarly, the land use data were interpolated to a monthly resolution, which may not fully capture short-term changes. Finally, the analytical framework was developed and calibrated using historical data from Inner Mongolia. While the integrated methodology (multi-source data fusion, machine learning, and SHAP interpretation) is designed to be generalizable, the specific model parameters and risk thresholds are likely region-dependent. External validation of this framework in other plague foci (e.g., the Tibetan Plateau) is therefore essential to confirm its broader applicability and to distinguish universal drivers from context-specific factors.

Study area

The Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, situated in northern China, spans longitudes 97°11′ E to 126°02′ E and latitudes 37°24′ N to 53°23′ N. This region is an integral part of the Eurasian continental interior (Figure 1). As of 2023, its administrative structure comprises 12 prefecture-level divisions, which include 9 cities and 3 leagues, further subdivided into 103 counties. Occupying a vast territorial expanse of roughly 1.183 million square kilometers, the region constitutes 12.3% of China’s total land area, thereby ranking it as the third most extensive province or region in China.Figure 1Location of the study area in Inner Mongolia, China

Human plague case data

In Inner Mongolia, plague cases exhibited a clustered distribution from 1950 to 1960, whereas from 1961 to 2023, human cases of plague have occurred sporadically but consistently. The human plague case data were extracted from the National Notifiable Disease Reporting System maintained by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. This was a retrospective descriptive study, including all reported cases that met the national confirmation criteria in China. These criteria require a combination of clinical symptoms, epidemiological linkage, and/or laboratory confirmation (e.g., bacterial culture, PCR, or serological testing), and no allocation to experimental groups was performed. Geographic coordinates were primarily assigned based on the patient's residential address or specific location of exposure as documented in the epidemiological investigation. When such precise information was unavailable, the centroid of the smallest available administrative unit (township or county) of residence was used as a proxy. A total of 277 plague cases is recorded over 1950-2023, across 26 counties in Inner Mongolia, China. For each identified natural focus, the CFR was calculated as the number of reported human deaths from plague divided by the total number of reported human cases attributed to that focus, multiplied by 100.

Rodent serological data

As one of the natural foci of plague, the Inner Mongolia region of China harbors rodents as the primary natural reservoirs of Yersinia pestis. These animals often overlap with human activity areas, such as pastures and tourist sites, thereby increasing the risk of zoonotic spillover. Consequently, since 2005, the National Plague Surveillance Program of China (http://www.gov.cn/yjgl/2005-08/30/content_28245.htm) and the Inner Mongolia Plague Surveillance Program (https://www.nmg.gov.cn/zwgk/zfgb/2020n/202016/202009/t20200903_307068.html) have routinely included serological monitoring of rodent populations to assess the subclinical infection rate of plague among these animals and the activity level of the plague foci. Serological testing primarily employs indirect hemagglutination assay (IHA) and reverse indirect hemagglutination assay (RIHA) methods. Overall, the dataset utilized spans from 2005 to 2023, comprising 44,219 records, among which 571 rodents tested positive. Most positive records were from M. unguiculatus, S. dauricus, and Lasiopodomys brandtii (Table S3). The spatial and seasonal distribution patterns of the seropositivity rate are detailed in Figures S5 and S6, respectively. The fluctuation in the plague positivity rate reflects a transition between active and dormant states of plague foci.41 A positivity rate exceeding 10 is defined as characterizing an active plague focus, while a rate below 10 is classified as a dormant plague focus.

Method details

Environmental covariates

Environmental variables corresponding to human case data

Due to the extensive temporal span of human case data, it is challenging to obtain corresponding economic, social, and anthropogenic factors influencing plague occurrence. Therefore, we investigated the effects of climatic factors, rivers, and railways on plague cases. The river and water system data were extracted from the Chinese River and Lake Dataset (data.tpdc.ac.cn, accessed on January 9, 2025), where first-, third-, and fourth-order rivers were merged. Historical railway data were obtained from the OpenStreetMap (OSM) open-source mapping project (https://www.openstreetmap.org, accessed on January 10, 2025). To assess the influence of rivers and railways, the proximity analysis tool in ArcGIS 10.7 was used to determine the distance from case points to railways and rivers. Subsequently, the significant variables were incorporated into the analysis of the rodent serological dataset.

Environmental variables corresponding to rodent serology data

The independent variables comprise 22 elements across nine categories: economy, climate, vegetation cover, surface properties, soil moisture, railway density, grazing, topography, and land use. This a priori selection was designed to capture key environmental and socio-economic determinants known or hypothesized to influence the enzootic maintenance and spillover risk of Yersinia pestis. Specifically, variables were chosen to represent: (i) climatic conditions affecting flea vector survival and development; (ii) habitat suitability for primary rodent reservoir species, as indicated by vegetation, soil moisture, and land cover; and (iii) anthropogenic factors that may modulate host distribution, human-wildlife contact rates, or pathogen dispersal. Basic datasets and data preprocessing

The dataset, in its original format, is bifurcated into spatial and tabular data categories. All spatial data utilized in this study were in TIFF format and referenced to the World Geodetic System 1984 (WGS84) coordinate system, wherein the vector data is delineated at the county level, and the raster data maintains a spatial resolution of 1 km. Utilizing ArcGIS's connectivity feature for public fields—such as place names or administrative division codes—enables the integration of attribute tables with spatial vector data.

The primary source of nighttime light (NTL) data originates from the Earth Observation Group (EOG) under the National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), specifically from the Defense Meteorological Satellite Program’s Operational Linescan System (DMSP-OLS) and the Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership’s Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (NPP/VIIRS). The DMSP-OLS dataset (https://ngdc.noaa.gov/eog/dmsp.html), spans the period from 2005 to 2012, with quarterly data being converted to monthly data to compute the monthly average NTL brightness for each county. The NPP/VIIRS dataset (https://ngdc.noaa.gov/eog/viirs/download_dnb_composites.html) covers the years 2012 to 2023 and provides monthly data. The acquired NTL data, formatted as TIFF, are processed using Geographic Information System (ArcGIS) software to clip the global NTL data according to the administrative boundaries of Inner Mongolia, thereby extracting the monthly average NTL data for each county in Inner Mongolia. Owing to the differing resolutions of the two datasets, a bilinear interpolation method is employed to resample the NPP/VIIRS data.

Meteorological data were obtained from the National Tibetan Plateau Scientific Data Center (https://doi.org/10.11888/Hydro.tpdc.270302, accessed on 16 November, 2024), with climate data sourced from the China 1 Ckm resolution month-by-month dataset spanning 1901 to 2023.42 Specifically, precipitation data, average temperature data, maximum temperature data, and minimum temperature data were extracted from China’s monthly dataset over 2005-2023 years.

The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) data (2005–2023) were obtained from NASA (https://doi.org/10.5067/MODIS/MOD13A3.006, accessed 24 September, 2024) with 1 km spatial resolution.43 Derived from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) on NASA's Terra satellite, the dataset was processed and calibrated to ensure quality. Image preprocessing (geometric correction, projection, and format conversion) was implemented in ArcGIS and R software. Mean NDVI values were calculated for each county using administrative boundaries as the spatial analysis units.

The soil moisture, near-surface wind speed, subsurface runoff, and surface runoff data used in this study were obtained from the FLDAS (FEWS NET Land Data Assimilation System) global monthly land surface attributes dataset provided by NASA's Earth Science Data website (https://disc.gsfc.nasa.gov/datasets/FLDAS_NOAH01_C_GL_M_001/summary?keywords=FLDAS, accessed 24 February, 2025). This dataset, developed by the Hydrological Sciences Laboratory at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center, comprises monthly data spanning the period from 1982 to 2024.44 The FLDAS dataset is specifically designed for monitoring and evaluating global hydroclimatic conditions. The monthly average data for each county in Inner Mongolia were extracted and processed using ArcGIS and R software for spatial clipping and analysis.

Historical railway data were derived from the OpenStreetMap (OSM) open-source mapping project (https://www.openstreetmap.org, accessed on 10 January, 2025). To assess the factors of railways, a fishnet grid was created to calculate the densities of the railway networks. The density was obtained by dividing the length of the railways by the area of the fishnet grid within the statistical region.45

In this study, grazing intensity data were obtained from the high-resolution livestock grazing intensity dataset (LHGI) developed by Wang et al. This dataset comprises grazing information for seven pastoral regions at a 1-km resolution, with validation at 73 sites demonstrating a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.78.46 Using established administrative boundaries as reference points, the average grazing intensity for each county was calculated.

The Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data were sourced from the SRTM dataset available on the Geospatial Data Cloud website (https://www.gscloud.cn, accessed on 9 August, 2024), with a spatial resolution of 90 m. The mean elevation of each county was computed using geostatistical functions in R, with elevation values extracted and averaged according to official administrative boundaries.

The Inner Mongolia land use data (https://zenodo.org/records/8176941, accessed on 29 March, 2025) spans the period from 1985 to 2023, with a spatial resolution of 30 meters and a data format of TIFF. This dataset was derived from Landsat series satellite remote sensing imagery, enabling high-precision land use classification, including cultivated land, forest land, grassland, water bodies, urban and industrial land, residential areas, and unused land. The overall classification accuracy reached 79.31%.47 The data was processed using ArcGIS software, and the area of various land types at the county level was calculated based on administrative division data.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Analysis of plague cases

We conducted a descriptive analysis of plague cases in Inner Mongolia from 1950 to 2023, visualizing geographical distribution patterns using doughnut charts and temporal trends through heatmaps. The seasonal-trend decomposition using LOESS (STL) model was employed to characterize meteorological factor distributions during the study period. Considering potential nonlinear relationships between case density and distances to rivers/railways, as well as associations between meteorological factors and case counts, we implemented generalized additive models (GAM) with a quasi-Poisson distribution as the link function. This analytical framework specifically examined the effects of distance to rivers, proximity to railways, and meteorological variables on plague cases. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.3) with the “mgcv” package.

Analysis of rodent serology data

To mitigate multicollinearity, Spearman correlation analysis was first performed to exclude variables with a correlation coefficient exceeding 0.8.48 Subsequently, a Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) assessment was conducted, removing variables with a VIF greater than 10. This process identified pivotal variables for plague forecasting and enhanced the predictive precision of the model.

Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and AdaBoost (AB) were employed to capture complex, nonlinear associations between multi-source environmental factors and seropositivity rates of plague. RF integrates multiple decision trees via bagging and majority voting, enhancing robustness against overfitting when handling high-dimensional environmental predictors.49,50 SVM maps predictors into a higher-dimensional space to identify an optimal separating hyperplane, effectively classifying nonlinearly separable data through kernel-based transformations.51 AB sequentially improves prediction by focusing on misclassified samples through weighted additive voting.52 Collectively, these methods offer superior flexibility in modeling intricate interaction effects and nonlinear patterns prevalent in multi-source environmental data, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of plague seropositivity drivers.

The dataset was stratified and randomly divided into training and testing subsets at a ratio of 7:3. Using the training data, three machine learning models were constructed to predict plague risk. Model hyperparameters were systematically optimized via grid search (Table S4). Performance was evaluated using metrics including the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score, which guided the optimization process. The final model configurations were selected through 10-fold cross-validation combined with parallelized grid search iterations.

Among the evaluated machine learning models, RF was selected as the optimal model based on comprehensive performance assessment. RF consistently demonstrated superior predictive ability, with AUC > 0.8 and maintained Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score above 0.7–0.8. Its adoption was further supported by its capability for both discriminative classification and intrinsic feature importance ranking.53 The Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), derived from cooperative game theory, enables local and global interpretation of machine learning models.54,55 SHAP values represent the average marginal contribution of a feature across all possible feature combinations.56 Global feature importance was visualized using SHAP summary plots, while SHAP dependence plots quantified the impact of individual features on model predictions. All analyses were carried out using Python (v.3.6.6) and R (v. 4.2.3). Two-tailed P values <0.05 were labeled as significant.
