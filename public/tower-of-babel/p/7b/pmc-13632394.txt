Characterization of secondary bacterial infections in Buruli ulcer disease in Ghana: a focus on antimicrobial resistant Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Abigail Opoku Boadi, Charity Wiafe Akenten, Thorsten Thye, Nancy Ackam, Michael Nkrumah-Appau, Morrah Oppong, Dzifa Kofi Ahiatrogah, Victoria Sogbo, Evelyn Natashia Ayinpoka Ayinlooya, Cynthia Kyerewaa Adu-Asiamah, Bernadette Agbavor, Abigail Agbanyo, Wibke Loag, Neyaz Ahmed Khan, Stefan Berg, Augustina Angelina Sylverken, Jürgen May, Yaw Ampem Amoako, Denise Dekker, Richard Odame Phillips
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBuruli ulcer (BU), caused by Mycobacterium ulcerans, is a chronic necrotizing skin disease often complicated by secondary bacterial infections that may delay healing and worsen clinical outcomes. Among these, Staphylococcus aureus is frequently implicated; however, the broader spectrum of bacterial colonizers and their antimicrobial resistance patterns in BU lesions remain inadequately characterized. This study aimed to characterize secondary bacterial infections in BU lesions, with a particular focus on the frequency, antimicrobial resistance profile, and methicillin resistance determinants of S. aureus.MethodsFrom October 2023 to March 2025, wound swabs were collected from PCR-confirmed BU patients across three endemic districts in Ghana before the start of BU-specific antibiotic treatment. Bacterial isolates were cultured and identified using standard microbiological techniques and the BioMérieux VITEK 2 compact system. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was interpreted according to European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines. Molecular detection of the mecA gene was performed using conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with agarose gel electrophoresis confirmation.ResultsA total of 83 bacterial isolates representing 18 genera were recovered from 31 of 40 BU patients. Gram-positive bacteria accounted for 56.6% (47/83) of isolates, while Gram-negative organisms constituted 43.4% (36/83). Staphylococcus aureus was the predominant species (17/83; 20.4%). The isolates exhibited high resistance to benzylpenicillin (94%) and tetracycline (82%), but retained complete susceptibility (100%) to linezolid, moxifloxacin, and quinupristin/dalfopristin. The mecA gene was detected in 94.1% of S. aureus isolates, indicating a high prevalence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA). Notably, PCR-based detection identified a higher proportion of MRSA compared with phenotypic methods (94.1% vs. 58.8%).ConclusionSecondary bacterial infections in BU lesions are microbiologically diverse, with S. aureus emerging as a predominant and clinically significant pathogen. The high prevalence of MRSA highlights the need for integrated management strategies addressing both M. ulcerans and co-infecting bacteria. Furthermore, reliance on phenotypic methods alone may underestimate MRSA burden, underscoring the importance of incorporating molecular diagnostics into routine surveillance and clinical care in endemic settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Buruli ulcer (BU) is a neglected tropical disease caused by the environmental pathogen, Mycobacterium ulcerans [1, 2]. It primarily affects the skin and soft tissues, leading to painless nodules, plaques, or oedema that can progress to large ulcers with undermined edges if left untreated [3]. The disease has been reported in more than thirty countries around the world [4, 5], with a notable burden in rural areas of West and Central Africa [4, 6]. Although the overall prevalence has declined over the past decade [7], the disease continues to disproportionately affect populations in resource-limited settings, where access to timely diagnosis and effective treatment remains constrained. In Ghana, cases are frequently observed in children under the age of 15 years [8].

While the transmission route of BU in Africa remains uncertain, it has been associated with exposure to slow-moving or stagnant water [9]. BU significantly compromises the quality of life of affected individuals and their caregivers, particularly in resource-limited settings [10–12]. Early diagnosis and initiation of appropriate BU-specific treatment are crucial to prevent disability, scarring and long-term complications.

Currently, treatment involves administering daily oral rifampicin and clarithromycin for 8 weeks, good wound care, and physiotherapy especially for lesions that are located close to joints [13]. In some cases, adjunctive surgical procedures such as debridement and skin grafting may be required to facilitate wound healing [13, 14].

Mycobacterium ulcerans produces mycolactone, a polyketide derived macrolide-like toxin which disrupts skin barriers and suppresses local immune responses [14], thereby creating an ideal environment for other pathogens to colonise BU lesions. Studies from Ghana, Benin, and Nigeria have consistently reported substantial secondary bacterial colonisation [15–18]. Despite this, secondary infections in BU remain relatively underexplored, with only a few studies addressing pathogen identification, characterization, and clinical management [2].

Previous studies have documented diverse bacterial species including Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacterales from BU lesions [15, 16]. This microbial diversity in BU is similar to that observed in other chronic wounds, such as diabetic foot ulcers and lymphatic filarial wounds [19, 20], underscoring the potential clinical significance of secondary colonisers in BU pathogenesis and treatment outcomes.

Among these organisms, Staphylococcus aureus is frequently recovered from BU lesions [15, 17, 21]. Its persistence in wound environments is facilitated by virulence factors and biofilm-forming capacity [22]. Importantly, the emergence of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), driven by acquisition of resistance determinants such as mecA, poses significant therapeutic challenges [23–25]. The presence of MRSA in chronic wounds is particularly concerning in resource-limited settings, where empirical antibiotic use is common and laboratory confirmation may be limited. Moreover, discrepancies between phenotypic and genotypic detection of methicillin-resistant S. aureus have been reported [26], suggesting that standard susceptibility testing may underestimate mecA-mediated resistance in some isolates.

Despite this burden, few studies have assessed the frequency, antimicrobial susceptibility, and antimicrobial resistance genes of S. aureus in BU lesions. This gap may undermine effective management and contribute to treatment failure. This study therefore aimed to determine the frequency and antibiotic resistance patterns of S. aureus in BU lesions, and to detect the presence of the antimicrobial resistance gene, mecA in BU patients.

Methods

Study settings

The study was carried out in three Buruli ulcer endemic districts in Ghana. The study sites included Tepa Government Hospital in the Ahafo Ano North district of the Ashanti Region, Agogo Presbyterian Hospital in the Asante Akim North district of the Ashanti Region, and Dunkwa Government Hospital in the Upper Denkyira East district of the Central Region. These sites have established treatment centres staffed with trained healthcare professionals for BU management within those districts and neighbouring communities.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Committee for Human Research, Publication and Ethics at the School of Medical Sciences, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, in Kumasi, Ghana (approval number, CHRPE/AP/837/23) and the German Ärztekammer in Hamburg (No: 2021-100726-BO-ff). Permission was also obtained from all three district hospitals involved in the study. Written informed consent was voluntarily obtained from all study participants, before their enrolment in the study. For participants aged 11–18 years, assent was obtained in addition to consent from the parental or legal guardian.

Study design and participant recruitment

This was a cross-sectional study conducted between October 2023 and March 2025. The data analysed in this report were derived from a larger longitudinal cohort, of which this study formed a component. Recruitment was by consecutive sampling of participants. The study included persons presenting with clinically suspected BU lesions who voluntarily consented to participate. Recruitment was not restricted by age, and all clinically suspected lesions required laboratory confirmation before initiation of BU specific treatment. Individuals who were later confirmed PCR negative and those who refused to give their consent were excluded from the study. BU confirmation was performed using the IS2404 PCR method, as previously described [27, 28]. Clinical and demographic information was collected using the standard Skin NTD BU01 form.

Sample collection and transport

Swabs were collected at baseline from PCR confirmed ulcerative and pre-ulcerative BU lesions that had begun ulcerating at the time of sampling before the commencement of BU-specific antibiotic treatment. Samples were obtained with a sterile cotton swab pre-moistened with 1X Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) by applying pressure for 5 s over a 1cm2 area of the wound bed. The samples were then transported at 4o–8 °C in a cool box to the Kumasi Centre for Collaborative Research in Tropical Medicine (KCCR) for microbial analysis. The mean time for transportation of samples from the field to the laboratory was 3.5 h, after which samples were processed without delay.

Sample processing and bacterial culture

On arrival at the microbiology laboratory, samples were pre-enriched in 0.5 ml Brain-heart infusion broth (BHI, Oxoid, Thermofisher Scientific Inc., Massachusetts, USA) and incubated at 35–37◦C for 18–24 h in normal atmosphere. Pre-enriched wound swabs were inoculated onto Blood agar (CM0331B, Oxoid) containing 5% sheep blood and MacConkey agar (CM0007B, Oxoid) using the streak method, then incubated at 35–37 °C for 18–24 h in normal atmosphere. After incubation, visible growth was observed, followed by sub-culturing on blood agar to obtain pure cultures.

Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Confirmation of positive bacterial cultures was based on colony morphology and microscopic examination following Gram-staining. Preliminary bacterial identification was done by standard biochemical tests. Isolates were confirmed and antibiotic susceptibility (AST) tested using the VITEK-2 Compact system (Biomerieux, Marcy-l’étoile, France), according to the manufacturer’s instructions [29]. AST results were interpreted using the Advanced Expert System (AES) according to the 2021 recommendations of the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) version 11.0 (https://www.eucast.org).

Detection of mecA gene

Genomic DNA was extracted from S. aureus isolates using the QIAamp DNA investigator kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions [30]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays targeting the mecA gene were performed using specific primer sets and cycling conditions as previously described [31], on an Applied Biosystems Veriti thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, USA). Briefly, the cycling conditions comprised an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of final denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s and annealing at 55 °C for 30 s. This was followed by an extension step at 68 °C for 10 min. Amplicon sizes of 162 bp (bp) were analysed on 1.5% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide. S. aureus ATCC43300 was used as positive control and nuclease-free water as a negative control. MRSA was defined as mecA positive S. aureus.

Assessment of secondary infection

All participants underwent baseline clinical evaluation to assess for clinical signs of secondary bacterial infection using predefined clinical criteria selected based on commonly reported clinical features of wound infection [32]. Each lesion was evaluated under aseptic conditions for the presence of the following five clinical signs: differential warmth, pus exudation, pain, perilesional redness and swelling. Photographic documentation of lesions was performed.

Data analysis

Data generated was first entered in RedCap version 15.5.28 and cleaned. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 15 (StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas, USA) and R version 4.4.2 (2024-10-31) (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, and results were presented in tabular format. Associations between sociodemographic and clinical parameters of participants and the likelihood of isolating S. aureus were assessed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Associations were assessed at the participants level (presence or absence of S. aureus isolation). Antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of the S. aureus isolates were expressed as percentages indicating resistance and visualized in histograms. The frequencies of mecA antimicrobial resistance genes were reported as proportions of the total number of tests conducted. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants

A total of 40 participants were recruited into this study. The majority (n/N = 21/40; 52.5%) were females and the median age was 31 (14–43.5) years. Most participants (n/N = 26/40; 65%) presented with ulcerative lesions; predominantly on the lower limbs. Category I and II lesions were the most frequently observed lesion categories among study participants. Additionally, 14 (35%) participants reported prior self-medication including antibiotics and herbal remedies before reporting to the BU clinic (Table 1).

Antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of S. aureus isolated from study participants

Seventeen [17] S. aureus isolates were recovered from the lesions of 15 participants (including two participants with multiple isolates). Ten of the 17 (58.8%) S. aureus isolates showed resistance to cefoxitin and oxacillin and were classified phenotypically as MRSA. All 17 S. aureus isolates were fully susceptible to linezolid, moxifloxacin, and quinupristin/dalfopristin (17/17; 100%). A susceptibility rate of 94% (16/17) was observed for ciprofloxacin, erythromycin, levofloxacin and tigecycline. Furthermore, 88% (15/17) of the isolates were susceptible to gentamicin, clindamycin, vancomycin, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (Fig. 2). In contrast, the isolates showed high resistance to benzylpenicillin (n/N = 16/17, 94%) and tetracycline (n/N = 14/17, 82%). One of 2 (50%) isolates tested showed resistance to rifampicin.

Discussion

This study reveals a high frequency of secondary bacterial colonisation, particularly MRSA in Buruli ulcer (BU) lesions in the studied Ghanaian population. Despite only 20% of patients showing clinical signs consistent with secondary infection, laboratory culture results showed 77.5% of patients harboured secondary bacteria. S. aureus was the most common pathogen, and the majority of these isolates carried the mecA gene (94.1%), consistent with MRSA, that can lead to difficult-to-treat infections and indicate a serious antimicrobial resistance concern. S. aureus isolation tended to be more frequent among participants from Tepa, suggesting possible local or environmental influences on infection risk; however, causality cannot be inferred from this cross-sectional design. Even though it is unclear what may have accounted for this, possible explanations may include differences in local healthcare practices across the study sites, environmental and community factors which may have favoured the proliferation and frequency of S. aureus within different geographical areas as well as laboratory capacity for early detection and management of S. aureus infections within the communities.

Buruli ulcer is typically characterized by painless skin necrosis before treatment initiation. However, the presence of pain, pus, erythema, swelling, and increased local warmth observed in some patients may suggest secondary infection. A number of participants in this study presented with clinical evidence of ongoing secondary infection, notably pain and pus. According to recommendations from the World Health Organization (WHO), secondary infection should be suspected when BU lesions develop cellulitis or become painful [33]. The disparity between clinical assessment (20% infection rate) and laboratory isolation (77.5% isolation rate) suggests that clinical signs alone may underestimate the true prevalence of secondary bacterial colonization in BU lesions. Clinicians should utilise clinical indicators of infection together with laboratory confirmation to guide decisions regarding diagnosis of secondary infection to reduce the risk of inappropriate antimicrobial use [34]. Laboratory diagnosis should be used to confirm any clinical suspicion to promote effective antimicrobial stewardship, reduce misdiagnosis, and support proper wound management [35].

In this BU cohort, diverse bacterial species were identified, with S. aureus being the most frequently recovered species. The predominance of S. aureus observed in BU lesions reflects its strong colonisation potential and adaptability within chronic wound environments. Virulence factors such as toxins and immune evasion mechanisms [22] enable S. aureus to often outcompete other bacteria and persist, explaining its frequent dominance in BU lesions [15, 17, 18]. It must be noted that the presence of S. aureus has been associated with delayed wound healing and increased morbidity in other chronic wound settings [36–38]. This organism is known to contribute to sustained inflammation, increased tissue damage, and impaired re-epithelialization, thereby prolonging the healing process. Furthermore, S. aureus can produce a range of virulence factors, including toxins and biofilm-forming components, which enhance its persistence within the wound environment and reduce the effectiveness of both host immune responses and antimicrobial therapy. Collectively, these characteristics have been linked to poorer clinical outcomes, including prolonged disease duration, increased risk of complications, and greater healthcare burden in patients with chronic wounds [39, 40].

Antimicrobial resistance testing of S. aureus isolates from BU lesions demonstrated high resistance to commonly used first line antibiotics, consistent with reports from other chronic wound studies within similar tropical settings [17, 21, 41, 42]. Resistance to benzylpenicillin (94%) and tetracycline (82%) may reflect their widespread and often unregulated use, where accessibility contributes to selective antimicrobial pressure [43, 44]. Such resistance increases the risk for treatment failure, prolonged hospitalization and higher healthcare costs. In resource limited settings, restricted access to second line antibiotics and diagnostic facilities further constrains effective wound management and may exacerbate morbidity. While resistance to older antibiotics was high, the 100% susceptibility to last-line agents like linezolid, moxifloxacin, and quinupristin/dalfopristin provides guidance for treating severe MRSA co-infections. The high level of susceptibility observed to the anti-MRSA agents - vancomycin, linezolid, and quinupristin–dalfopristin - is consistent with previous reports indicating that these drugs remain effective treatment options against MRSA, particularly for severe or complicated infections [45]. Vancomycin remains a cornerstone in the treatment of serious MRSA infections due to its reliable activity against S. aureus; however, emerging reduced susceptibility and concerns about toxicity necessitate careful use and monitoring. Linezolid, with its excellent oral bioavailability and good tissue penetration, provides an important alternative for the treatment of skin and soft tissue infections, including wound infections, although prolonged use may be associated with haematological toxicity. Quinupristin-dalfopristin is also active against MRSA but is generally reserved for multidrug-resistant infections due to limitations related to cost, availability, and potential adverse effects [45]. These findings indicate that these last-line anti-MRSA agents remain effective and underscores the importance of antimicrobial stewardship to preserve their efficacy and minimize the inappropriate use of these critical antibiotics. Treatment strategies should incorporate availability and cost considerations in the decision making.

For technical reasons, rifampicin susceptibility testing was performed on only two S. aureus isolates, one of which was resistant. Although Mycobacterium ulcerans susceptibility to rifampicin was not assessed in this study, the detection of rifampicin-resistant S. aureus isolates at baseline is noteworthy given that rifampicin is a cornerstone of WHO-recommended Buruli ulcer treatment. Future studies evaluating rifampicin susceptibility in Mycobacterium ulcerans may be needed to monitor the continued effectiveness of standard treatment regimens in this setting.

The high frequency of MRSA among S. aureus isolates poses a serious challenge for BU management, exceeding rates reported in other hospital-based studies, which typically range from approximately 4.2%-52.8% [46–48]. The finding that 94.1% of S. aureus isolates were MRSA is notable and warrants attention with implications for BU management, as MRSA complicates wound care, increases the risk of treatment failure, and may require changes to empirical antibiotic protocols. This elevated burden may be driven by excessive antibiotic use, weak stewardship practices, and limited routine susceptibility testing [49]. Empirical treatment without culture confirmation promotes persistence of resistant strains, complicating outcomes and increasing risks of systemic infection and surgical intervention. These findings may underscore a need for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship, routine resistance surveillance, and improved infection prevention and control in BU-endemic regions. The significant association between study site and S. aureus infection might be due to local hygiene practices, environmental contamination, or healthcare-seeking behaviours. There was a lack of association with other factors like age, lesion type, or prior antibiotic use.

Importantly, the use of molecular techniques revealed a higher MRSA prevalence compared to the phenotypic testing, suggesting that reliance on phenotypic testing alone may fail to detect certain mecA-positive isolates. The observed discrepancy between phenotypic and genotypic MRSA detection in this study may be attributed to several biological and methodological factors. Although detection of the mecA gene remains the gold standard for confirming methicillin resistance, phenotypic expression of resistance may vary. For example, S. aureus may exhibit heterogeneous resistance, where only a subpopulation of cells expresses resistance despite the presence of the mecA gene, leading to false susceptibility in phenotypic assays [26]. Furthermore, differences in test performance can arise from the choice of antimicrobial agents and testing conditions, as cefoxitin is a stronger inducer of mecA expression and provides clearer phenotypic endpoints compared to oxacillin. Environmental and methodological factors - such as inoculum size, incubation conditions, and duration - may also influence phenotypic outcomes and contribute to discordant results [50]. In addition, certain isolates may harbour alternative mechanisms of resistance. For instance, mecC-positive strains or isolates with genetic variations in the SCCmec element may complicate molecular detection and interpretation. Conversely, borderline oxacillin-resistant S. aureus (BORSA), which lack mecA/mecC but exhibit resistance due to β-lactamase hyperproduction or other mechanisms, may appear phenotypically resistant while testing negative genotypically [51, 52]. These discrepancies underscore the importance of integrating both phenotypic and molecular diagnostic approaches for MRSA detection into routine practice to improve accuracy, guide appropriate antibiotic use, and ultimately enhance patient outcomes.

This study has some limitations. Although the parent study was longitudinal, the present report is based on a cross-sectional analysis at a single time point, restricting insights into the temporal evolution of the frequency of S. aureus and resistance. The relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of the findings. In addition, molecular detection was restricted to the mecA gene, without assessment of other methicillin resistance determinants such as mecC. Virulence factors, including Panton–Valentine leukocidin (PVL), were also not evaluated. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs with larger sample sizes, validate the impact of pre-enrichment by comparing recovery rates with direct culture methods, and undertake comprehensive molecular characterisation of isolates to provide a clearer understanding of the dynamics and pathogenic potential of S. aureus.

Conclusion

Overall, this study has demonstrated S. aureus as a frequent secondary coloniser of Buruli ulcer lesions, underscoring the need for comprehensive wound care strategies that integrates appropriate antibiotic therapy with strict infection control and hygiene measures. The higher detection rate of MRSA using genotypic methods highlights the need to incorporate molecular diagnostics into routine practice to ensure accurate identification and guide effective treatment decisions. Our findings support ongoing efforts to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship in the management of Buruli ulcer and warrants further research to understand the drivers of MRSA colonisation in BU wounds and its impact on healing outcomes.
