Evolutionary modularity of the primate vestibular system: Morphological distinction of otolithic and canalicular organs
Authors: Christopher M Smith, Jeffrey T Laitman, Zhonghe Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mammalian inner ear is traditionally divided into two systems: the peripheral vestibular system (for balance) and the cochlea (for hearing). This bipartite model has shaped our understanding of inner ear evolution, function, and pathology. However, it groups the semicircular ducts (which detect angular velocity) and otolithic organs (which detect linear acceleration, vibration, and head tilt) into a single peripheral vestibular system, overlooking key differences in their structure and function. Using advances in imaging technology, we investigate whether the otolithic system is evolutionarily and structurally distinct from the semicircular ducts, with a focus on primates. Specifically, we assess modularity and phylogenetic signal in the inner ear across 14 primate species using landmark-based 3D geometric morphometrics within a phylogenetic framework. Our results reveal previously unrecognized modularity in the primate peripheral vestibular system. The semicircular canals and otolithic organs show stronger integration within their own structures rather than with each other. Furthermore, these two systems show divergent evolutionary trajectories in both size and shape, particularly among hominoids. These findings challenge the traditional bipartite model of the inner ear. Instead, a tripartite framework comprising distinct cochlear, canalicular, and otolithic systems better reflects the structural, functional, and evolutionary complexity of the primate inner ear. This revised model reshapes perspectives on vestibular system organization and has implications for studies on balance, sensory adaptation, and inner ear disorders.Keywords: evolution, inner ear, vestibular system

This study uncovers a previously unrecognized level of organization in the primate vestibular system, overturning the traditional view that the inner ear is divided into two main functional parts. Instead, our findings support a three-part (tripartite) model, with distinct canalicular, otolithic, and cochlear systems—better reflecting the intricate structure and function of the primate balance apparatus. By redefining how we understand the evolution and organization of the vestibular system, this work establishes an expanded framework to investigate its roles in movement, perception, and sensory adaptation across species.

Introduction

The mammalian inner ear consists of fluid-filled membranous sensory organs responsible for balance and hearing, enclosed within an endosseous (bony) labyrinth inside the petrous region of the temporal bone. The vestibular system, which controls balance, is the most evolutionarily ancient part of the inner ear and is vital for perceiving spatial orientation in three dimensions (3D). The structures responsible for this function, the otolithic organs and semicircular ducts, are collectively called the peripheral vestibular system, distinguishing them from the central vestibular system (1). The central vestibular system comprises the vestibular nuclear complex in the rostral medulla and caudal pons, which projects to the ocular motor nuclei via the medial longitudinal fasciculus for gaze stabilization, to the cerebellum, reticular formation, and descending vestibulocollic and vestibulospinal tracts for head and body posture and to the cerebral cortex via thalamic relays, where vestibular information integrates with visual, somatosensory, and proprioceptive inputs (for a review, see (2)).

The peripheral vestibular system consists of five paired sensory organs: three semicircular canals and their associated ducts, which detect angular head movements, and two otolithic organs, the utricle and saccule, located within the vestibule, which detect linear acceleration, vibration, and head tilt relative to gravity ((3, 4), Fig. 1). These structures form the foundation for movement and gravitational perception. As a result, they play a crucial role in various physiological systems, including postural control (5–8), gaze stabilization (9), and cognitive functions ranging from reflexive responses to higher-order consciousness (8, 10, 11).

The otolithic organs and semicircular canals are often subsumed into a single peripheral vestibular system, while most research focuses on the canals. This is especially true in evolutionary studies of the vestibular system, which favor assessment of canal morphology (12–18). This grouping may not be structurally or physiologically accurate, however, as the canals differ considerably from the otolithic organs in structure, function, and evolutionary history. For example, hair cells within the crista ampullaris are stimulated by movement of the gelatinous cupula generated through inertia of the endolymph during head rotation, while hair cells in the otolithic organs are stimulated by shearing forces caused by inertia of otoconia superficial to the hair cells themselves. Furthermore, growing evidence indicates that the otolithic organs in mammals can detect both airborne and bone-conducted soundwaves (19, 20); a retention of some of their original function as vibration detectors in fish (21). Understanding the distinction between the otolithic organs and semicircular canal systems is thus critical to better assess the functional morphology and evolution of the inner ear. As a first step toward clarifying these potential differences, this study tests the hypothesis that the peripheral vestibular system comprises two distinct structural complexes: (i) the semicircular canals: housing their respective ducts and cristae ampullares and (ii) the vestibule: housing the otolithic system of the utricle and saccule. If supported, this modular view of the peripheral vestibular system would challenge current conceptions of inner ear organization. We then take this one step further to examine trends in phylogenetic signal and rates of morphological change throughout primate evolution.

Our intent in testing modularity, however, is not to reestablish the “anatomical separateness” of the semicircular canals and vestibule, which is well recognized, but rather to evaluate whether these regions represent statistically independent modules in terms of shape covariance in primates. In the context of geometric morphometrics, modularity is a quantitative property that describes how tightly integrated subsets of traits covary relative to one another, reflecting potential developmental, functional, or evolutionary compartmentalization of form (22–24). Importantly, the presence of anatomical boundaries does not necessarily imply modular independence: components may still exhibit high degrees of covariation if they share developmental pathways, functional loading regimes, or evolutionary constraints. By testing for modularity, we therefore assess whether the semicircular canals and vestibule, and, by extension, the sensory systems housed inside, evolve as semi-independent units of morphological variation rather than as a single, integrated structure.

Although modularity is often examined at the intraspecific level to infer developmental or genetic associations, interspecific datasets provide complementary insight into the evolutionary realization of these relationships. Patterns of covariation that persist across phylogenetic scales suggest that underlying developmental linkages have been maintained or canalized through evolutionary time. Our comparative, cross-species framework thus evaluates whether the hypothesized modules retain this signal throughout evolutionary time (in our case, measured as “phylogenetic signal”). Methodologically, we implement this by quantifying the morphological covariance structure among landmarks using phylogenetically informed modularity tests, which allow us to compare within- versus between-module shape integration while accounting for shared ancestry.

Our results indicate significant morphological distinction between the bony structure of the canals and otolithic organs, with a modular structural and evolutionary pattern among primates. These findings support our hypothesis, suggesting that the semicircular canals and vestibule function as statistically, and potentially evolutionarily, distinct morphological modules.

Results

Morphological modularity within the primate endosseous labyrinth

We tested the hypothesis of morphological modularity among the systems of the inner ear (i.e. the semicircular canals, vestibule, and cochlea) by quantifying the degree of morphological modularity among the three using Procrustes shape variables derived from 3D landmark coordinates placed on the endosseous labyrinth of each respective region (Fig. 2). This test was run in a phylogenetic context (under a Brownian motion model of evolution) to account for nonindependence of data points due to shared ancestry. Results indicate a significant degree of modularity (covariance ratio [CR] = 0.727, effect size = −11.072, P = 0.001), indicating greater levels of shape integration within rather than between modules. We then explored the degree of morphological modularity within the peripheral vestibular system (semicircular canals and vestibule) and between the cochlea and vestibule. In both cases, we recovered a similarly strong signal of modularity (CR = 0.709, effect size = −12.228, P = 0.001; CR = 0.699, effect size = −10.563, P = 0.001, respectively; Fig. 3).

Evolutionary properties of the primate peripheral vestibular system

We find that size and shape of the semicircular canals and vestibule exhibit differential phylogenetic signals. Variance in absolute semicircular canal size exhibits a strong phylogenetic signal (Pagel's λ = 1) and matches well with a Brownian motion model of evolution (i.e. close relatives resembling each other as expected under the model; Blomberg's K = 1.069, P = 0.02), while variance in absolute vestibule size lacks a significant signal (K = 0.151, P = 0.956), despite having Pagel's λ estimated at 1. Relative to body mass, semicircular canal and vestibule size share a similar pattern, with a low estimated Pagel's λ (0.1) and low, nonsignificant phylogenetic signal (semicircular canals: K = 0.419, P = 0.288; vestibule: K = 0.255, P = 0.822). We did not recover a significant signal in canal shape variance, despite a moderate signal (λ = 0.58, Kmult = 0.334, P = 0.2035). Shape variance in the vestibule exhibited no evidence of a phylogenetic signal (λ = 0; see Table 1 for a summary of the above results).

Results from our analyses of size and shape evolution in the primate vestibular labyrinth indicate a slight difference between the semicircular canals and vestibule in the rate of size evolution relative to body mass, with the semicircular canals exhibiting a higher evolutionary rate (log10-transformed: 1.23E−04) compared with that of the vestibule (log10-transformed: 8.80E−05) (Fig. 4). Across primates, semicircular canal and vestibule shapes both evolve at higher rates compared to size (0.005 and 0.001, respectively). Permutation tests indicate that these differences in overall evolutionary rates are not statistically significant, either for log10-transformed relative size (P = 1) or for shape (P = 0.06) (Table S1). Despite this, clade-specific patterns of size evolution differ markedly across primate phylogeny. In hominoids, the vestibule shows an overall relative expansion, whereas the semicircular canals vary in size across the clade. Nonhominoid primates also show differences in relative size evolution, most notably in Papio, where the vestibule shows a relative reduction in size compared to the semicircular canals.

Discussion

Morphological integration of the inner ear has been a topic of considerable interest to evolutionary biologists and paleontologists for decades (for a developmental perspective, see (25)). However, few studies have employed statistical methods to quantify these patterns and assess their evolutionary implications, particularly in terms of “evolvability” (but, for example, see (26, 27)). Addressing these questions is crucial for understanding covariance in the structure and function of these sensory organs, which in turn helps clarify major evolutionary transitions of sensory biology in the fossil record.

Our results provide evidence for differential structural and evolutionary constraints in the primate vestibular labyrinth. In particular, we find that the size and shape of the peripheral vestibular system exhibit complex relationships with phylogeny. More specifically, the shape of the otolithic organs does not conform to a Brownian motion model of evolution, and the relative sizes of the semicircular canals and vestibule follow distinct evolutionary trajectories, suggesting that the organs housed within (semicircular ducts and otolithic organs) are shaped by different evolutionary pressures. Such divergent evolutionary trajectories are expected for systems that must respond somewhat independently to selective pressures. Together, these distinctions have likely influenced the evolution of these sensory complexes in primates in concert with broader transformations in related sensory systems (8, 28–30).

Based on our results, we can infer that the evolutionary trajectories of the semicircular canals and otolithic organs have diverged across primates, with pronounced differences among hominoids (Fig. 4). In hylobatids, both systems show an overall increase in relative size, consistent with their highly suspensory locomotor repertoire and reliance on rapid, 3D body movements. However, the pattern in hominids reveals a different evolutionary dynamic. Whereas the semicircular canals show modest, lineage-specific differences, the otolithic organs exhibit a large-scale increase in relative size across the entire clade.

These contrasting patterns suggest that the two systems are subject to distinct evolutionary pressures during hominoid evolution. The overall expansion of the vestibule, coupled with more clade-specific, nuanced change in semicircular canal size among great apes implies shifts in how different components of movement sensing were integrated within the inner ear. This could potentially be linked to changes in the degree and breadth of positional behaviors employed across hominoids. Taken together, these differing evolutionary patterns, paired with the above evidence for morphological modularity, support our hypothesis of morphological differentiation between these two regions of the vestibular labyrinth and ultimately point to a tripartite rather than bipartite conceptualization of the primate inner ear (Fig. 5).

These findings align with recent visualizations of vestibular membranous structure, which indicate that the utricle and saccule share a distinct membranous network separate from the semicircular canals. For example, the membranous utricle is suspended in the perilymphatic space by a delicate connective tissue meshwork known as the membrana limitans (31–33). Similarly, the membranous saccule is structurally isolated from the canal and cochlear systems via the ductus reuniens and saccular and endolymphatic ducts (34, 35). In addition, our modularity analyses indicate that the saccule and utricle are more strongly integrated with each other than with the semicircular canals, further supporting their functional and structural cohesion.

Recognizing the otolithic organs as distinct from the semicircular canals and cochlea has important implications for both vestibular medicine and evolutionary studies. Independent modeling of the utricle and saccule (along with fibrous support structures such as the membrana limitans), rather than grouping them with the canals or cochlea, could enhance the accuracy of diagnosing inner ear lesions and better pinpoint their impact on vestibular function. Indeed, prior studies have highlighted the distinctive functional properties of the otolithic organs in sensitivity to airborne sound and bone-conducted vibration (19) and in modulating semicircular canal signals (36, 37). Our findings provide morphological insights into the physiological distinctions that make the otolithic system functionally unique.

Considering the utricle, saccule, and accessory support structures (e.g. the membrana limitans) as a distinct third system of the inner ear also carries important evolutionary implications. Evolutionary studies of the mammalian vestibular system often focus on the semicircular canals or treat the vestibule as a secondary component. Our results suggest that this approach overlooks a critical system of the ear and that analyzing the otolithic system alongside, rather than subordinate to, the semicircular canals could reshape current understandings of inner ear evolution in primates and mammals more broadly. This is particularly relevant for catarrhine primates (monkeys, apes, and humans), where an enlarged vestibule has been recognized as a hallmark of the hominid lineage (38) but has traditionally been interpreted as a byproduct of semicircular canal enlargement. A tripartite perspective on the primate inner ear could yield further insights into evolutionary patterns and postural behavior shifts with unparalleled resolution. While our study focuses on primates, further research is needed to determine whether similar patterns extend across other mammalian groups and how they might translate across vertebrates with more diverse assemblages of peripheral vestibular organs.

In this study, we assess morphological patterns in the primate peripheral vestibular system. However, we want to highlight two limitations in the current study. First, we assess only bony morphology and not the membranous or functional components of the semicircular ducts or otolithic organs themselves. This was done as a first step to examine morphological differences in these systems, as membranous data on the semicircular ducts and otolithic system are rare. Work is ongoing to image such membranous tissue across vertebrates and will be added to future work.

Second, variation in the number of landmarks between structures may influence estimates of modularity. The semicircular canals and cochlea necessitate a greater number of landmarks due to their continuous, curved geometry, whereas the vestibule is more globular in shape. While these differences in metrics may contribute to the observed modular signal, landmark counts for the canals and cochlea were kept to a minimum to minimize any potential bias introduced by this disparity.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate a clear morphological distinction between the bony architecture of the semicircular canals and that of the otolithic system. This divergence reflects deeply rooted modular organization within the peripheral vestibular system and supports a tripartite evolutionary model of the primate inner ear. By revealing the otolithic system as an evolutionarily distinct component of primate balance, our results shift the traditional paradigm of inner ear structure, emphasizing that the otolithic organs should be considered an independent axis of vestibular evolution.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and reconstruction

Our sample includes virtual 3D meshes of 68 right or left inner ears derived from micro-computed tomography (μCT) scans of 14 extant primate species, including a representative sample of modern Homo sapiens (Table S2). The H. sapiens data represent a geographically varied sample of individuals from North America (not subject to the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act), South America, Africa, and Asia. Data from this sample have been previously published in (39). New existing μCTs were downloaded from MorphoSource (www.morphosource.org) while other crania were selected from the American Museum of Natural History (AMNH) collections in the Division of Anthropology and scanned using a GE Phoenix Vtome Xs μCT scanner housed within the AMNH Microscopy and Imaging Facility. Isometric voxel size ranged from 33 to 116 μm. Each μCT stack was exported as a series of DCM or TIFF images.

In most individuals, only the left ear was used. However, some individuals only presented with a viable right ear (e.g. meshes downloaded from online repositories). We tested for possible bilateral asymmetry in inner ear morphology using a Procrustes ANOVA on specimens in which both ears were available using the “bilat.symmetry” function of the “geomorph” package of R (version 3.5.3 (40)). Results indicated no significant bilateral asymmetry present in the morphology of the semicircular canals, vestibule, or cochlea (R2 = 0.0016; F = 0.8068; Z-score = −0.332; P = 0.635). Any potential effects of bilateral asymmetry in the labyrinth, therefore, have no significant impact on our results.

μCT stacks were imported into the software package 3D Slicer, v. 4.10.1 (41) to perform 3D segmentation and reconstruction of each endosseous labyrinth. Segmentation of each labyrinth was carried out by the author (C.M.S.) using a combination of manual and semi-automated thresholding to select the air-filled space of the labyrinth and delineate it from the surrounding bone. Surface meshes were exported from 3D Slicer and were processed using the software Geomagic Wrap 2017 (42) to check for any potential artifacts/holes in the mesh and refinement of the topology using the “mesh doctor” function.

We used three subsets of single landmarks, curve semilandmarks, and surface semilandmarks placed along the midline of the semicircular canals, ampullae, vestibule, and cochlea (see Fig. S1 for a visualization of landmark placement; see Table S3 for a summary and definitions of landmarks). Landmarks were placed using the software Avizo v. 9.0 (FEI Visualization Sciences Group). Curve semilandmarks on the semicircular canals were placed using the “autoskeleton” module in Avizo and placed along the outer curvature of the cochlea from the anteroinferiormost point of the round window to the apex of the cochlea. Initially, arbitrary counts of curve semilandmarks were resampled to equal counts of equally spaced landmarks using the mean numbers of landmarks used across specimens. Landmark coordinates along curves were then slid prior to analyses.

To capture the bony morphology of the utricle, a modified set of landmarks was chosen (landmarks 1–5, see Table S3, Fig. S1). To capture the bony morphology of the saccule, 25 sliding semilandmarks were placed on the borders of the spherical recess and along its surface using the software Landmark Editor (Table S3, Fig. S1) (43–45). These landmarks approximate otolithic system morphology and orientation of the maculae using the morphology of the bony vestibule (46). A generalized Procrustes analysis (GPA) was performed on landmarks of the semicircular canals, vestibule, and cochlea separately using the “gpagen” function of the R package “geomorph,” v. 4.0.6 (47). The GPA aligns specimens by translating all to the origin, scaling them to unit-centroid size, and rotating them (using a least-squares criterion) until all of the coordinates of corresponding points align as closely as possible. This process separates shape (the Procrustes coordinates) and size (centroid size) in the initial landmark configurations (46, 48).

Intraobserver error was assessed by repeating landmark placement on one H. sapiens labyrinth (voxel resolution = 89 μm3) ten consecutive times. In this error sample, we recovered a mean Procrustes distance of 0.056, whereas the mean distance for the human sample (n = 29) was 0.13. Given the limited overlap between Procrustes distances in the error sample and that of the entire human sample, we are confident that intraobserver error had no significant effect on landmark placement.

Morphological modularity

Observations of species’ traits are not independent of each other and have unequal covariances due to the different durations of shared evolutionary history between different taxon pairs (49). This necessitates the use of phylogenetic comparative methods (50–52) to evaluate morphological integration and relationships between inner ear traits and phylogeny (53, 54). For these analyses, we used 100 trees of primate phylogeny from the phylogenetic framework of Upham et al. (55) downloaded from VertLife (http://vertlife.org/phylosubsets/) to generate a consensus tree that was then implemented into the analyses.

We evaluated the degree of modularity in labyrinth shape (i.e. Procrustes coordinates) using phylogenetic modularity tests (50, 56) implemented via the “phylo.modularity” function in the R package “geomorph” (57, 58). For each test, we generated species shape means for each taxon from the total sample using the “mshape” function in the “geomorph” package of R, resulting in a final “n” of 14 ears.

Evolutionary properties

Tests were carried out to determine the level of phylogenetic signal present in the shape and size of the semicircular canals, vestibule, and cochlea. We examined the degree of phylogenetic signal present in Procrustes shape variables (shape) and centroid size (size) of the semicircular canals, vestibule, and cochlea on the consensus tree generated above. These tests were based on the standardized location of the log-likelihood in a distribution generated from randomization of residuals in a permutation procedure using the “physignal.z” function in the “geomorph” package of R. This approach allowed us to optimize branch length transformations (Pagel's lambda) while also reporting Blomberg's K-value along with an estimate of the effect size (Z) (59). In cases where phylogenetic signal in shape variance was assessed, a multivariate version of the Bloomberg's K statistic (Kmult) was used, which estimates the degree of phylogenetic signal present in a multivariate dataset (i.e. Procrustes shape variables); for a review, see (60).

We also examined the evolutionary rates of change in relative size and shape of the semicircular canals, vestibule, and cochlea using the “compare.evo.rates” function from the “geomorph” package of R. Relative size measures were taken and standardized for body mass, which were taken from averaged male and female values from (61, 62).
