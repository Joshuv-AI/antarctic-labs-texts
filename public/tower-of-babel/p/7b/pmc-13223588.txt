Long Noncoding RNA function prediction via multiview cross-contrastive learning combined with multiscale semantic adaptive optimization
Authors: Zhixia Teng, Qingqi Li, Di Liu, Chunyu Wang, Guohua Wang, Yuming Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) function is essential for revealing molecular mechanisms and developing effective therapies for complex diseases, as lncRNAs play important regulatory roles in many disease-related biological processes. However, existing lncRNA function predictors struggle to extract discriminative features from multimodal omics data and to model the semantic and topological structure of the gene ontology (GO), which severely limits their ability to achieve biologically meaningful and functionally informative predictions. To address these challenges, we propose a novel framework for lncRNA function prediction, namely MiCLSAO. Firstly, MiCLSAO utilizes multiview cross-contrastive learning with attention mechanisms to extract highly discriminative lncRNA features from diverse omics similarity networks. Secondly, graph convolutional networks are applied to learn initial features of GO terms, while multiscale topological and semantic relationships are incorporated to adaptively refine term representations. Finally, an lncRNA function predictor is developed by dynamically integrating the representations of lncRNAs and GO terms using a Kolmogorov–Arnold network. Extensive experiments demonstrate that MiCLSAO consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods across multiple metrics, with significant capability to recover known functions and uncover novel ones. Moreover, MiCLSAO demonstrates remarkable practical utility and potential value by providing more functionally informative annotations for lncRNAs.

Introduction

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) play a crucial regulatory role in key biological processes such as neural development, cellular regulation, tumorigenesis, and metastasis in mammals [1], exhibiting remarkable functional diversity [2]. Moreover, increasing evidence suggests that abnormal lncRNA expression is closely associated with the onset and progression of several complex human diseases [3]. Therefore, understanding lncRNA functions is essential for elucidating the mechanisms underlying these diseases. Since uncovering lncRNA functions through wet experiments is time-consuming and labor-intensive, developing efficient computational predictors for lncRNA functions is significant for accelerating related biomedical research.

In recent years, many efforts have been made on lncRNA function prediction. For example, Guttman et al. [4] extracted co-expression patterns of lncRNAs and mRNAs and applied biclustering combined with gene enrichment analysis to predict lncRNA functions. Liao et al. [5] constructed a co-expression network of lncRNAs and proteins, using gene ontology (GO) annotations of the neighboring proteins to infer lncRNA functions. LncRNA2Function [6] inferred the potential functions of lncRNAs by identifying enriched functional terms through the hypergeometric test on co-expression profiles of lncRNAs and proteins. However, these methods primarily focused on analyzing expression profiles rather than integrating diverse biological information, restricting their ability to fully predict lncRNA functions.

To address this issue, several approaches integrated diverse biological data into lncRNA–protein networks to associate lncRNAs with GO terms mediated by proteins and use various metrics to identify the most relevant terms for functional annotation. For example, lnc-GFP [7] predicted lncRNA functions using a global network propagation algorithm on an lncRNA–protein network, which integrated their co-expression profiles with protein–protein interactions. KATZLGO [8] first constructed an lncRNA–protein network and then calculated correlation scores between lncRNAs and proteins using the KATZ measure. Based on these scores, the GO terms of highly correlated proteins were used to annotate the functions of lncRNAs. Similarly, BiRWLGO [9] performed a bi-random walk algorithm on lncRNA–protein network to estimate probability scores for all lncRNA–protein associations and used GO terms of proteins with high probability scores to annotate the functions of lncRNAs. NeuraNetL2GO [10] extracted features of lncRNAs using diffusion component analysis from their expression similarity network and then trained a multilayer perceptron to predict lncRNA functions based on the annotations of their interacting proteins. PLNRGO [11] employed a Support Vector Machine (SVM) to predict lncRNA functions using features learned from the lncRNA–protein network via random walks. HLPFN [12] integrated GO annotations of lncRNAs and proteins, lncRNA co-expression profiles, lncRNA–protein interactions, and protein–protein interactions into a heterogeneous network. Then, lncRNA functions were inferred based on the proximity between lncRNA and GO term, which was measured by the meta-path-based AvgSim method [13] within the network. DNGRGO [14] employed random walks on a heterogeneous network of lncRNAs, proteins, and miRNAs to extract node features, obtained their low-dimensional feature vectors using stacked denoising autoencoders, and predicted lncRNA functions with SVM. However, these methods mainly focused on lncRNA–protein associations within heterogeneous networks while neglecting the intrinsic properties of lncRNAs. Consequently, lncRNAs with identical topological positions were assigned the same functions. Due to the sparse network structure and data noise, many lncRNAs occupied identical topological positions, making it hard to distinguish their unique functions. Thus, these approaches provided low-specific functional predictions, severely limiting their ability to extract meaningful insights into lncRNA functions.

Moreover, existing methods treated terms as independent functional labels, ignoring their semantic and topological relationships, thereby failing to incorporate the true path rule (TPR) in prediction. However, these methods applied TPR to extend the predicted functions of lncRNAs, which assumed that if a term annotates a specific lncRNA, its ancestor terms should also annotate the same lncRNA. As a result, shallow and false positive predictions spread and accumulate widely, severely limiting the accuracy and reliability of the predictions. In conclusion, lncRNA function prediction still faces several challenges: (i) How can we effectively extract discriminative features of lncRNAs from multi-modal prior knowledge? (ii) How can we learn representations of GO terms that retain intrinsic semantics and topological relationships? (iii) How can we accurately associate lncRNAs with relevant GO terms?

To overcome these limitations, we propose MiCLSAO, a novel method for lncRNA function prediction based on multiview cross-contrastive learning and multiscale semantic adaptive optimization, as shown in Fig. 1. First, by constructing similarity networks based on multiple perspectives (such as expression profiles, lncRNA–protein interactions, and lncRNA–miRNA interactions), we can integrate different prior knowledge to improve discrimination, thereby better handling network sparsity and noise issues, and helping to distinguish different lncRNAs with similar topological positions. In addition, previous studies have shown that graph convolutional networks (GCNs) and attention mechanisms can be used to capture prior information of the graph structure of lncRNA in the multiview scenarios [15, 16]. Contrastive learning has also been introduced into lncRNA-related computational tasks and has shown strong generalization ability in multiview heterogeneous graphs [17–19]. Therefore, GCNs are applied to the GO hierarchy graphs, where nodes represent terms and edges capture their topological relationships, to learn the initial term features. Then, these features are refined at multiple scales by considering the semantic relationships between parent, child, ancestor, and descendant terms. This process ensures compliance with the TPR, thereby enhancing the consistency and relevance of the feature representations. A cross-contrastive learning strategy is employed to effectively learn the features of lncRNAs, while the attention mechanism is incorporated to capture their most distinctive intrinsic characteristics. Finally, lncRNAs are associated with relevant GO terms using the Kolmogorov–Arnold Network (KAN) [20], which dynamically adapts the features of both to precisely characterize their relationships. Extensive experiments demonstrate that MiCLSAO consistently surpasses state-of-the-art approaches and exhibits significant promise in uncovering informative lncRNA functions.

Overall framework of MiCLSAO, which consists of three main parts: (A) discriminative lncRNA features are learned from expression data and interactions with miRNA and protein using multiview cross-contrastive learning with attention mechanism; (B) informative GO term features are obtained using GCN and enhanced through multiscale semantic adaptive optimization; (C) lncRNA functions are predicted by dynamically integrating the above features using KAN.

Informative gene ontology term feature learning

Initial gene ontology term features learning

To learn term features, a hierarchy graph is first constructed based on the GO data released on 8 September 2024 [24], with terms as nodes and edges representing their relationships. Then, similar to GOGCN [25], GCN is applied to the GO hierarchy graph, which is regarded as a knowledge graph consisting of triplets (head term, relationship, and tail term). The representations of these triplets are updated under the guidance of the ConvE scoring function [26], thus obtaining the initial embeddings of the terms. Multiscale semantic adaptive optimization

To capture more informative term embeddings, we propose a novel multiscale semantic adaptive optimization method that refines initial term representations within the GO hierarchy. In this hierarchy, terms are semantically related to their ancestors, parents, children, and descendants in distinct ways. Specifically, ancestors and parents convey inherited semantics, shaping the core meaning of the term, while children and descendants introduce semantic variation, enriching its diversity. Among these, parent and child terms capture the local semantic context, whereas ancestors and descendants reflect broader global semantics. Therefore, effective embedding optimization is expected to model these multiscale relationships accordingly.

In this mechanism, the nodes associated with the target term are first categorized into four types, namely ancestors, parents, children, and descendants, which helps distinguish the sources and propagation patterns of semantics across different levels, thereby enhancing the interpretability of term representations. Similarly, we further project it onto the direction of the initial feature of the term through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mathcal{P}(CE^{r}_{t} \rightarrow E_{t})$\end{document} to reinforce the intrinsic semantic expression of the term and prevent it from being shifted by upstream or downstream trends. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mathcal{P}(*\rightarrow *)$\end{document} denotes the projection of the former to the direction of the latter, and the notation ||.|| refers to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $L2$\end{document} norm of embeddings.

Finally, according to Equation (6), we fuse the projected features with the original features to preserve the core semantics while incorporating contextual hierarchical information, thereby obtaining the final term representations that balance consistency and distinctiveness.

For example, in the GO part of Fig. 1B, the ancestor nodes of the term GO:0007049 (cell cycle), including GO:0008150 (biological process) and GO:0009987 (cellular process), describe macroscopic biological activity trends, while its descendant nodes, such as GO:0022402 (cell cycle process), GO:0045786 (negative regulation of cell cycle), and GO:1903047 (mitotic cell cycle process), represent finer grained mechanisms of the cell cycle. According to the above method, Equation (3) extracts the trend features of the ancestors and descendants, which reflect the macro and micro semantic directions, respectively. The dual projection mechanism in Equations (5) and (6) aligns these hierarchical trends while preserving the semantic focus of cell cycle, resulting in a representation for the term that integrates global consistency and local specificity. Through multiscale alignment and a feature fusion mechanism, the MiCLSAO method achieves adaptive differentiated modeling of term representations while maintaining compliance with the TPR, thereby providing a finer grained and semantically consistent representation foundation for downstream function prediction.

Given that ancestors and parents shape the core semantics of a term better, and that parents and children provide more specific semantic distinctions than other relatives, the optimization is carried out in the following order: parent, child, ancestor, and descendant scales. This order is designed to incorporate local and essential semantic information first, followed by the integration of broader contextual knowledge. By iteratively refining embeddings across these four scales until convergence, MiCLSAO ensures that each term obtains a semantically consistent and context-sensitive representation with respect to its neighbors in both local and global GO structures. This ultimately enables more accurate downstream lncRNA function prediction.

Long noncoding RNA function prediction with Kolmogorov–Arnold Network

To accurately predict lncRNA functions, this study utilizes KAN [20] to capture the complex relationships between lncRNAs and terms through their representations learned in the previous steps. KAN applies learnable nonlinear activation functions on the network edges to perform GO term-specific nonlinear transformations on each dimension of the lncRNA representation, based on which the prediction score of each term is computed and aggregated. This mechanism enables the model to simultaneously capture both the consistency and distinctiveness of the same lncRNA representations across different GO terms. Based on this mechanism, the model can generate discriminative representations for each lncRNA that are consistent with the multi-level semantics of GO terms, thereby effectively improving the accuracy of lncRNA function prediction. This is essential for elucidating the intricate annotation relationships between lncRNAs and the complex GO terms.

Implementation details

The software and hardware environment was Python 3.12 (Ubuntu 22.04), PyTorch 2.3.0, and CUDA 12.1; the end-to-end runtime was 6955.99 s. The training configuration employed a two-layer GCN, with the embedding dimension of lncRNA representations set to 128 and the learning rate set to 0.005. The complete implementation and usage examples are available in the GitHub repository at https://github.com/BioMLab/MiCLSAO.

Results

Datasets and performance metrics

In our study, we conducted independent testing of the related methods using the publicly available lncRNA2GO-55 and lncRNA2GO-68 datasets obtained from the literature [10, 14]. Moreover, the human lncRNA functional annotations were collected from the NONCODE database [21], with any annotations overlapping with the lncRNA2GO-55 dataset removed. After this filtering process, a total of 26 500 valid annotations were retained. Meanwhile, we downloaded and parsed the latest GO data from the official GO website and constructed the GO directed acyclic graph. Based on the TPR, we further completed the annotation labels by propagating them through the GO hierarchy. The resulting dataset, constructed through the aforementioned procedures, is referred to as NCAGO-25 in this article. The methods were first trained on the NCAGO-25 dataset and then evaluated on the lncRNA2GO-55 and lncRNA2GO-68 datasets, respectively.

Ablation study

In this section, we systematically evaluated the contributions of the main components of MiCLSAO through ablation experiments. These components include multiview lncRNA similarity networks, cross-attention mechanism, contrastive learning, and multiscale semantic adaptive optimization, labeled for simplicity as SimNet, CroAtt, ConL, SemAdo, and CLS, respectively. To conduct the ablation study, we constructed several MiCLSAO variants by removing or replacing key elements from these components. Specifically, SimNet-EXP, SimNet-LPI, and SimNet-LMI represent variants where the views of expression, lncRNA–protein interaction, and lncRNA–miRNA interaction are removed, respectively. Similarly, CroAtt-EXP, CroAtt-LPI, and CroAtt-LMI represent MiCLSAO variants in which the cross-attention mechanism associated with expression, lncRNA–protein interaction, and lncRNA–miRNA interaction is removed, respectively, while CroAtt-ALL eliminates the entire cross-attention mechanism. In addition, ConL-EXP, ConL-LPI, and ConL-LMI represent the MiCLSAO variants in which the contrastive learning associated with expression, lncRNA–protein interactions, and lncRNA–miRNA interactions is removed, respectively, whereas ConL-ALL eliminates the entire contrastive learning mechanism. Meanwhile, SemAdo-ANC, SemAdo-PAR, SemAdo-CLD, and SemAdo-DES denote variants that exclude semantic adaptive optimization at the ancestor, parent, child, and descendant scales, respectively, whereas SemAdo-WO means removing semantic adaptive optimization entirely. Moreover, CLS-SVM, CLS-DT, CLS-KNN, and CLS-MLP denote the variants that replace the KAN module in MiCLSAO with SVM, Decision Tree (DT), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), respectively. 5-CV performance of MiCLSAO and its variants on NCAGO-25

As shown in Table 1, MiCLSAO achieved the highest average performance with an Fmax of 0.7976, AUPR of 0.8709, and Smin of 0.2481 outperforming all variants.

For SimNet, SimNet-LMI delivered the strongest overall results, reporting an Fmax of 0.7862, an AUPR of 0.8614, and an Smin of 0.2641. These values were 1.14% and 0.95% lower than the Fmax and AUPR of MiCLSAO, whereas its Smin was 1.6% higher. SimNet-LPI recorded an Fmax of 0.7757 and an AUPR of 0.8503, trailing MiCLSAO by 2.19% and 2.06%, and its Smin of 0.2693 exceeded MiCLSAO by 2.12%. SimNet-Exp produced an Fmax of 0.7777, an AUPR of 0.8498, and an Smin of 0.2745, falling short of MiCLSAO by 1.99%, 2.11%, and 2.64%, respectively. This pattern suggests that prior knowledge from multiple perspectives contributes distinct features of lncRNAs, each of which plays a crucial role in improving the performance of MiCLSAO.

In the case of CroAtt, CroAtt-EXP achieved the strongest performance, posting an Fmax of 0.7851, an AUPR of 0.8584, and an Smin of 0.2660. These values fell short of those of MiCLSAO by 1.25% in Fmax and 1.25% in AUPR, while Smin was higher by 1.79%. In contrast, CroAtt-ALL showed the largest decline, recording an Fmax of 0.7782, an AUPR of 0.8537, and an Smin of 0.2754, which widened the gaps to 1.94% in Fmax, 1.72% in AUPR, and 2.73% in Smin relative to MiCLSAO. These findings indicate that CroAtt effectively captures key features of lncRNAs from multiview prior knowledge and thereby enhances the performance of MiCLSAO.

Among ConL, ConL-EXP achieved the best performance, with Fmax of 0.7796, AUPR of 0.8512, and Smin of 0.2677. Compared with MiCLSAO, its Fmax decreased by 1.80%, AUPR decreased by 1.97%, and Smin increased by 1.96%. ConL-ALL exhibited the largest performance drop, with Fmax of 0.7688, AUPR of 0.8379, and Smin of 0.2757, corresponding to relative differences of 2.88%, 3.30%, and 2.76% compared with MiCLSAO. These results indicate that the ConL mechanism provides certain gains in instance-level alignment of multiview representations; however, its effectiveness remains inferior to that of the MiCLSAO model with semantic adaptive optimization, suggesting that contrastive constraints alone are insufficient to fully eliminate noise and inconsistency among heterogeneous views.

Regarding SemAdo, SemAdo-ANC reported the highest Fmax of 0.7922, yet this score was 0.54% lower than that of MiCLSAO. SemAdo-PAR delivered the top AUPR of 0.8662 and the lowest Smin of 0.2529, trailing MiCLSAO by 0.47% in AUPR and exceeding it by 0.48% in Smin. At the opposite extreme, SemAdo-WO posted the weakest results, with its Fmax reduced by 3.6% and its AUPR reduced by 3.14%, while its Smin rose by 3.95% relative to MiCLSAO, highlighting the indispensable role of SemAdo in extracting high-quality term features. This is because SemAdo-WO relies solely on GOGCN initial features oriented toward GO semantic similarity, which leads to oversmoothing and confusion among similar GO terms, whereas our multiscale semantic adaptive optimization preserves shared signals and strengthens discriminative cues to improve GO label separability at prediction. The performance patterns of SemAdo-PAR, SemAdo-CLD, SemAdo-ANC, and SemAdo-DES indicate that optimization at the child and descendant scales exerts the greatest influence on term representations. Child terms inherit the core semantics of their parents and further add contextual specificity, whereas descendant terms enrich these semantics by introducing diverse subconcepts. Thus, removing either scale reduces both fine-grained discrimination and semantic coverage. In contrast, ancestor information is largely redundant because its coarse semantics are already embedded in the term, and parent information provides only moderate additional context. Consequently, discarding these two scales has a comparatively smaller effect. Collectively, these results further reinforce the effectiveness and underlying rationale of SemAdo within the MiCLSAO framework.

When it comes to CLS, CLS-MLP achieved the best Fmax of 0.7032, CLS-DT obtained the highest AUPR of 0.6567, and CLS-KNN yielded the lowest Smin of 0.2546. Compared with MiCLSAO, the Fmax of CLS-MLP decreased by 9.44%, the AUPR of CLS-DT decreased by 21.42%, and the Smin of CLS-KNN increased by 0.56%. The success of MiCLSAO can be attributed to its ability to effectively characterize the complex relationships between lncRNAs and terms by dynamically adapting their features using KAN.

In addition, the performance comparison of these methods under traditional unweighted metrics was shown in Supplementary Table S3. In nearly all metrics, the aforementioned variants performed significantly lower than that of MiCLSAO, which provides further validation for the effectiveness of the key strategies described above.

Overall, the multiview lncRNA similarity networks, cross-contrastive learning with attention mechanism, and multiscale semantic adaptive optimization are essential for the success of MiCLSAO in predicting lncRNA functions. It is the seamless integration of these components that drives the outstanding performance of MiCLSAO.

Parameter analysis

In this section, we investigated the hyperparameters of MiCLSAO, including node embedding dimension, number of GCN layers, and learning rate. This analysis helps to determine the optimal parameters for MiCLSAO, and the results are presented in Fig. 4. For the embedding dimensions, we varied the size among 16, 32, 64, 128, and 256, and the corresponding performance of MiCLSAO was shown in Fig. 4a. As the embedding dimension increases, Fmax and AUPR initially improved but then began to decline. The optimal performance was achieved when the embedding dimension was set to 128. Meanwhile, as presented in Fig. 4b, we evaluated the impact of the number of GCN layers, ranging from 1 to 5, on Fmax and AUPR. We observed that the performance of MiCLSAO improved as the number of GCN layers increased, but began to deteriorate beyond two layers, with the best results attained at two layers. Concerning the learning rate, we assessed the performance of MiCLSAO at 0.0005, 0.001, 0.005, 0.01, and 0.05 in Fmax and AUPR, respectively. The results in Fig. 4c showed that the performance of MiCLSAO improved with the learning rate up to 0.005, beyond which it declined. Based on the observations above, the recommended embedding dimension is 128, with two GCN layers and a learning rate of 0.005 for MiCLSAO.

Parameter sensitivity analysis of MiCLSAO.

Moreover, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S4, consistent results were obtained under traditional unweighted metrics, indicating that MiCLSAO performs robustly under varying evaluations. This stability may be beneficial for providing consistent and reliable predictions across different application settings in biological contexts.

To further demonstrate the performance of our prediction method, we selected HOTAIRM1, NEAT1, and H19 as typical examples. RT-qPCR assays showed that knockdown of HOTAIRM1 suppressed thyroid cancer cell proliferation, indicating positive regulation of biological process (GO:0048518) [27]. RT-qPCR, Western blot, and confocal microscopy analyses demonstrated that NEAT1 promotes phosphorylation of DNA-PKcs and ATM, as well as their downstream targets pRPA32 and pCHK2, thereby activating the DNA damage response (GO:0006974) [28]. Experiments using antisense oligonucleotides to knock down or overexpress H19 in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) showed that H19 knockdown significantly reduced the number of HUVECs, whereas H19 overexpression enhanced their proliferation, demonstrating that H19 positively regulates endothelial cell proliferation (GO:0001938) [29].

To enhance the biological interpretability of lncRNA function prediction, we used HOTAIRM1 as an example and selected the deepest predicted GO terms, which were then mapped back to the regulatory network centered on HOTAIRM1. Specifically, we constructed a regulatory network consisting of proteins, predicted GO terms, and diseases. The lncRNA–protein, lncRNA–disease, protein–disease, and GO annotation of protein associations were obtained from NPInter [23], NONCODE [21], DisGeNET [30], and GOA [31], respectively. As shown in Fig. 5, at the GO level, the predicted terms for HOTAIRM1 exhibit consistent overlap with the GOA annotations of its associated proteins, indicating that the functions predicted by MiCLSAO are consistent with the known functional mechanisms of protein regulation mediated by the corresponding lncRNAs. At the disease level, NONCODE indicates that HOTAIRM1 is associated with acute promyelocytic leukemia, colorectal cancer, glioblastoma, and astrocytoma. We mapped the protein–disease associations obtained from DisGeNET onto the above regulatory network and found that our predictions are consistent with the prior knowledge of HOTAIRM1–disease associations recorded in NONCODE. These findings indicate that our predicted results can effectively support molecular mechanism studies along the lncRNA–protein–disease axis.

In summary, MiCLSAO demonstrates strong capability in accurately identifying known functions while also showing great potential in discovering novel, biologically relevant annotations, highlighting its practical value and promise in lncRNA functional characterization.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed an innovative lncRNA function prediction method, namely MiCLSAO, which integrated multiview cross-contrastive learning and multiscale semantic-adaptive optimization. On the one hand, multiview cross-contrastive learning was combined with attention mechanisms to extract highly discriminative features for lncRNAs from multimodal omics data. This strategy effectively enhanced the discriminability and expressiveness of features by fusing complementary information from multi-omics sources, enabling more accurate lncRNA function modeling. On the other hand, a multiscale semantic-adaptive mechanism was introduced to refine term representations by incorporating their semantic and topological relationships within the GO hierarchy. This strategy improved the semantic consistency and relational coherence of term representations and better reflects the TPR compared with traditional methods. Moreover, the KAN was employed to capture complex relationships between lncRNAs and GO terms for efficient function predictions.

Extensive experiments showed that MiCLSAO outperformed the SOTA methods across multiple metrics, significantly improving the semantic specificity and biological relevance of the predicted functional annotations. These improvements highlight MiCLSAO as a promising framework for lncRNA function prediction. To gain deeper insights into lncRNA functions, our future work will focus on integrating additional biologically relevant data, including regulatory pathways, methylation profiles, and subcellular localization information.
