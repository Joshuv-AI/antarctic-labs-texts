Promoting unstructured recess activity in urban high school: a case study of combined spatial planning and behavioural guidance strategies
Authors: Yiping Yan, Yucheng Liu, Youzhi Ke, Yang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRecess duration and available space are limited. To promote unstructured recess activity in high school from the perspective of time and space utilization, this case study aimed to examine the effects of an unstructured recess intervention involving a combination of spatial planning and a behavioural guidance strategy based on geographic information systems (GIS) on adolescent moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA) in an urban high school.MethodsAn urban high school outdoor environment was used as a case study with 485 students (mean age: 16.96 ± 0.71 years; boys: 47.2%). The effect of the intervention was assessed by a self-controlled experiment. The intervention combined the relocation of sports equipment and student activity maps, both designed using GIS. The number of participants engaging in physical activity behaviours was obtained through direct observation. Physical activity time was measured with accelerometers. Focus group interviews with students and school staff were used to explain the effects of the intervention in a semistructured manner.ResultsThe number of students who participated in MVPA in school outdoor areas increased from 216 at baseline to 294 at follow-up; significant increases (p < 0.05) in the number of participants engaging in MVPA were observed in informal and formal sports areas. In terms of least-active students, posttest and follow-up assessments revealed significantly increased MVPA time (β = 0.90, p < 0.05); age and sex were not significant moderators in the intervention (Sex: β = -0.65, p > 0.05; Age: β = 0.16, p > 0.05); and recess duration was a significant moderator in the intervention (β = 1.84, p < 0.01).ConclusionsThe combined spatial planning and behavioural guidance intervention for unstructured recess demonstrated the potential to increase the number of students engaging in MVPA and to enhance MVPA time among the least-active students. This cost-effective intervention holds promise for integration into high school recess activity programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24908-1.Keywords: Physical activity, Recess intervention, Spatial analysis, Spatiotemporal planning, Environment strategies

Background

For adolescents, engaging in at least 60 min of moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA) is fundamental to their physical health, such as preventing metabolic, cardiovascular, and psychological problems [1]. Despite these known benefits, insufficient MVPA among adolescents has become an increasingly prevalent issue [2]. Data from 1.6 million adolescents worldwide in 2016 revealed that less than one-fifth of students met the recommended physical activity (PA) levels from the World Health Organization (WHO) [3]. Owing to social distancing policies, the COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated the decline in PA levels [4]. In 2022, the WHO reported that 80% of adolescents fail to meet the recommendation for PA worldwide [2]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to promote PA among adolescents in their daily lives.

The WHO suggests that the school environment is an opportunity to promote adolescent PA [3]. Recess in school is considered to be an important opportunity to promote PA levels for students, which can contribute 17%−44% of the recommended daily PA amount for students [5]. Unstructured recess refers to scheduled periods during the school day for free physical activity and play [6]. Until 2022, recess in many countries has been primarily unstructured. For instance, Finland provides 15 min of free play every hour during recess, and the United Kingdom has 1 h of free play during lunch recess [7]. Research has shown that unstructured recess has unique advantages in encouraging students to engage in PA more autonomously and socially [6]. Thus, it is necessary for adolescents to increase MVPA during unstructured recess. Temporal and spatial constraints are key factors limiting PA participation [8, 9]. With respect to temporal constraints, a nationwide 27-year longitudinal study indicated that, compared with 1995, student weekly recess duration has decreased and tends to shorten as grade levels increase [10]. With respect to spatial constraints, the global urban population will increase by 2.2 billion people in 2050 due to urbanization [11]. This outcome, as seen in rapidly urbanizing China, will further reduce the per capita activity space for schools [12]. In addition, qualitative studies have suggested that the number of people in a given space can become a barrier to participation in recess activities and that urbanization may reduce student PA during recess [13]. On the other hand, with the rise of vertical schools (multistory buildings), students often need to use stairs, which significantly increases transit time during recess [14]. Individuals often tend to allocate their time to activities that are beneficial to them and avoid crossing areas that require higher transit costs [15]. Therefore, the vertical school structure may further reduce opportunities to participate in PA during recess. It is evident that recess interventions should be designed from the perspective of how to utilize existing space and time.

Spatiotemporal planning is based on the relationship between the supply and demand of existing space and time resources and aims to improve the organization of these resources to meet the demands of behavioural activities [16]. It includes time planning (adjusting the time spent using spaces), spatial planning (enhancing space accessibility), and behavioural guidance (providing information or decision-making support for spatial behaviour) [17]. Spatiotemporal planning as a behavioural intervention strategy has been utilized in the promotion of free healthy lifestyles in existing space and time [18]. Active play is a key activity for increasing PA during recess, and participation in play is influenced by the availability of activity equipment [19]. In interventions involving existing spaces, the addition of activity equipment is considered a simple intervention [20]. The intervention shapes the functionality of the surrounding activity areas, allowing students to perceive certain areas as activity areas with the provision of equipment while also enhancing the accessibility of these spaces. Therefore, it can be categorized as an intervention concept within spatial planning. In recent years, most research has focused on the design of equipment interventions in formal sports areas. During daily recess, a study provided loose equipment on a school playground [21]. During lunch recess, a study made recyclable play materials on a school playground aimed at enhancing student PA [22]. Within the limited recess duration, although active play is characterized by autonomous choice [23], students may not fully recognize the opportunities to play during recess. Behavioural guidance can provide recommendation information about suitable activities for users within the constraints of activity duration and available areas, thereby reducing actual and perceived barriers to PA and ultimately promoting greater participation in activities [24]. In intervention practices, since the equipment serves as the material basis for designing activity content, behavioural guidance is often combined with a spatial planning strategy. One study provided each class with a set of play equipment and “activity cards”, which provided examples of various games and activities that could be played outdoors using the equipment [25]. Spatial planning and behavioural guidance can clearly be considered strategies for recess interventions. Recently, some promising findings have been reported for multicomponent school recess interventions [21], and a combination of spatial planning and behavioural guidance strategies may be seen as a hopeful intervention. However, in terms of space utilization, no studies have reported the specific site selection process for equipment provided through spatial calculations. Without calculated site selection, students may face challenges such as the inaccessibility of equipment within a limited recess duration [26], which could weaken the effectiveness of the intervention. With respect to time utilization, previous studies have lacked a clear arrangement for the time required for the allocation of the necessary time (such as the time to reach the activity area, activity time, etc.) and space resources (such as venue location, facility points, routes to reach the areas, etc.), which could affect the efficiency of time use.

In terms of spatial planning, to improve the accessibility of facilities, a previous study created a topological network of the road network of sports venues on the basis of geographic information systems (GIS) and calculated the area covered within a 15-min walk to the venues. According to the results of the analysis, if the locations of future sports venues were distributed in the peripheral areas of the city, the accessibility to the venues in those areas would increase [27]. However, most sports facility site selection and design are aimed at urban residents. In terms of behavioural guidance, a study created a walking map that provided behavioural guidance information, and the results showed that a walking map can increase the PA frequency of community residents on the basis of GIS [24]. As schools are part of public spaces, spatial analysis technology is expected to be used in the design of recess activity interventions.

To promote unstructured recess activity in high school from the perspective of time and space utilization, this study aimed to examine the effects of an unstructured recess intervention combined with spatial planning and a behavioral guidance strategy based on a GIS for adolescent MVPA in an urban high school. The questions of this study were as follows: (1) How can unstructured recess intervention combined with spatial planning and behavioural guidance strategies based on GIS be designed? (2) Will adolescents have more MVPA in high school after the intervention?

Methods

Study design

First, this study created a multicomponent intervention plan involving behavioural guidance and spatial planning based on a GIS in the case of one high school. Second, a within-subject design was used to test the effectiveness of the intervention in this case study, with three time points: baseline, post, and follow-up. Because the development of healthy behaviours requires 66 days to be automated [28], the intervention duration in this study was designed to last 6 months. The intervention took place from November 2023 to April 2024. Because the intervention in this study was based on the design of spatiotemporal utilization, it emphasized the allocation and use of limited space in a fixed recess duration by individuals participating in recess activities. This study employed the direct observation method to capture the number of individuals who participated in physical activity in specific areas. In addition, objective measurement methods were used in this study to determine the physical activity duration during recess to assess changes in physical activity levels. Finally, by incorporating the self-reported experiences of participants, this study verified and explained the effectiveness of the intervention. The research methods, tools, and general implementation steps used to address the research questions are presented in Supplementary File 1. This study was approved by the Institution of Review Board at Shanghai University of Sport (Number: 102772023RT172).

Case school selection

In this study, a senior high school located in Jinshan District, Shanghai, China, was used as a case study. The school, with an 80-year history, has a total of 790 students across all grades (grades 10, 11, and 12). The total area of the school’s outdoor and developed area is 48,655.36 m2, and the playground is 14,748.02 m2 (18 m2 per student), which is below most areas of standards such as England (< 33 m2 per child) and America (< 80 m2 per child) [9, 29]. Each day, the case school provides six 10-min unstructured recesses between class periods (short recess) and one 15-min unstructured recess at noon (lunch recess). All recesses follow a 5-day school week (Monday–Friday). Grade 10 and 11 students recess at the same time.

Participants

Prior to recruitment, a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 to determine the required sample size for detecting meaningful changes in PA behaviours from three time points. Using an alpha of 0.05 and a power of 95%, the calculation of the power to detect a pre–post change in these measures was based on the effect sizes reported in a recent systematic review. It has been reported that the effect size is 0.52 [21], but owing to high heterogeneity, a prudent estimation of the effect size (0.25) was used for the calculation. A 20% drop-out rate was considered. The number of repetitions was three. On the basis of these parameters, the required sample was calculated to be 51.

This study excluded high school seniors who had heavy academic burdens and were preparing for college entrance examinations. Grade 10 and 11 high school students were asked to participate in this study. In each class, all the students were invited to participate in recess interventions in this study. The exclusion criteria for participants included (1) having a cognitive impairment, (2) being ill or physically unfit to participate in the PA, (3) having an injury during the study period, and (4) refusal to provide written informed consent.

Intervention planning

GIS as a spatial analysis technology has been employed to design sports facility locations [27] and behavioural guidance interventions [24]. In this study, spatial planning involved the relocation and installation of sports equipment at the school, as shown in Supplementary File 1. Behavioural guidance uses maps as information carriers to visually provide users with the locations, dimensions, routes, time, and recommended physical activities of school areas. The per capita cost of the intervention was 15.97¥/person/week (up to the follow-up).

Spatial planning

First, objective mapping of school space morphology and road network construction were conducted using GIS. Second, by employing the maximum coverage location model within GIS, the locations of the existing equipment points outdoors were reorganized. In detail, considering the inverse relationship between travel time and activity time, the shortest recess duration, constituting 1/4 (2 min) of the total recess duration, was set as the maximum threshold for travel time. Specifically, the spatial coverage range for the equipment points was designed within a 2-min walking distance (with the walking speed set at 1.2 m/s). Location selection attempts were made at Locations 1–10, and the equipment points with the minimum number of selections were chosen to ensure maximum coverage of activity needs while minimizing equipment costs; the position of the relocation equipment is shown in Fig. 1. Third, the equipment points were moved according to the relocation plan (moving and reinstallation fees: 5000¥). During the intervention, teachers distributed several existing types of sports equipment into equipment points, which both boys and girls enjoyed (cost of sports equipment loss or damage: 2400¥).

Behavioural guidance

First, the reachable range during short and lunch recesses was calculated with network analysis through GIS. On the basis of the school spatial transportation network, the maximum travel time threshold was set below the recess duration(lunch recess: total round trip 12 min; short recess: total round trip 4 min). The reachable range for short recesses, 1 and 2 min, was computed with the teaching building as the centre. During the lunch recess, the reachable range from the canteen to the activity area and then to the teaching building within 6, 9, and 12 min was calculated. Second, the shortest paths and duration of spatial accessibility were calculated. The formal and informal sports areas derived from the earlier computations were considered calculation targets. Third, recommendations for physical activities based on activity functions within formal and informal sports areas within a reachable range, including programs, modes, and methods for students, were provided. Finally, student activity booklets and maps were created. In past research, common information in behavioural guidance included activity routes, destinations, departure points, and travel lengths [24]. The spatial overview, reachable activity areas, equipment point locations, reachable spatial ranges, routes to reach these areas during recess, activity duration, and travel time for the routes were depicted in the activity booklet through Autodesk Computer Aided Design (CAD). The content of the recommended physical activities was described in textual format. In the activity map, the details included a spatial overview, reachable activity areas, equipment point locations, and reachable spatial ranges, as shown in Supplementary File 1. In the online survey, 91% of the students indicated that they could understand the information on all the maps. Before the intervention, an activity map for short and lunch recesses was separately installed in each classroom and cafeteria. Every student received the student activity booklet (total: 5000¥). To ensure the benefit of intervention for every student, the researchers held two thematic lectures at the school to explain the meaning of these maps under the name “Recess Activity Planning Maps”.

Validation of intervention feasibility

This study verified the safety and feasibility of the interventions by combining equipment relocation and guided maps through the use of pedestrian simulation software (AnyLogic 8.50). In this study, the walking speed of the students was set to 1.2 m/s. According to the behavioural guidance of the maps, the probability of participation in activities decreased with increasing distance from the teaching building, with a 10% reduction for each additional minute of travel time. A pedestrian density less than 4 person/m2 was used as the safety standard for activities. In the flow simulation for the lunch recess, the maximum travel time range from the canteen to the activity area and then to the teaching building was set to 12 min on the basis of the previous GIS calculations. Therefore, the simulation duration for a student’s one-way trip from the canteen to the activity area was set to 6 min to observe whether the student flow density and accessible range fell within the planned scope. In the flow simulation for a short recess, the maximum travel time range centred on the teaching building was 4 min. Therefore, the simulation duration for a student’s one-way trip from the teaching building to the activity area was set to 2 min. The results of the flow simulation are shown in Fig. 1. The left image illustrates the areas that students can reach during the lunch recess, including the playground, garden, and surrounding areas of the teaching building, as previously planned for behavioural guidance. Additionally, the flow traces are not shown in red, indicating that the flow density is less than 4 person/m2. The right image shows the areas students can reach during the short recess, which are limited to the area around the teaching building and a small part of the playground. Similarly, the flow traces are not shown in red (flow density < 4 person/m2). Therefore, the results suggest that the intervention design is suitable for student accessibility and safety during recess and that the implementation of the intervention is feasible.

Data collection

A mixed-method approach was utilized to assess the intervention process. This method provides a greater contextual understanding of interventions to promote PA in an environment that needs the triangulation of both quantitative and qualitative data [30]. To reflect the intervention effects, a study used direct observation and accelerometers to measure spatial behaviour and physical activity time during recess, respectively [31]. Therefore, direct observation, accelerometer, and interview data were used to evaluate the intervention in this study. Direct observation and accelerometer data were used to obtain the number of participants engaging in physical activity in specific areas and physical activity time at three time points: baseline (before the 1-week intervention), post intervention (after the 4-week intervention), and follow-up (after the 6-month intervention). The interview data were collected during the posttest.

Direct observation

Regional-level PA intensity and the play environment were observed using the System for Observing Play and Leisure Activity in Youth (SOPLAY) [32]. Video-assisted direct observation can improve the reliability of direct observation and measurement. The video-based approach with SOPLAY has been utilized in studies that observed PA behaviours during school recess [33]. In this study, videos of each identified target area were recorded throughout each data collection process. Physical activity levels were coded following the SOPLAY procedures into 3 categories: sedentary (e.g., lying down, sitting, and standing), walking (e.g., walking slowly), and vigorous (e.g., running, kicking, throwing, and shooting) [34]. In the SOPLAY coding, the “sedentary” code corresponds to sedentary behaviour (SED), the walking code corresponds to the estimated values for light physical activity (LPA), and the “vigorous” code corresponds to moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) [32]. Therefore, in this study, code conversion was performed on the basis of the correspondence between the codes and the PA intensity levels.

SOPLAY instruments were utilized to analyse each recess video, counting the number of students engaged in SED, LPA, and MVPA [32]. Owing to the prevalence of short hair among some girls, observers were unable to determine their sex. During the observation period, the intervals for scanning outdoor recesses were set at every 2 min, which is the most precise observation frequency [32]. The observation time was from Monday morning at 8:00 to Friday noon (12:00). To assess the reliability of the observations, two assessors independently scanned and recorded each adolescent’s PA behaviours at 300 identical observation video nodes within the case high school. In the case of significant discrepancies between observers (r < 0.70), discussions and retraining were conducted. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) between the observers was r = 0.86 (95% CI: r = 0.85 ~ 0.88).

Accelerometer

The accelerometer was measured on a one-to-one basis, and owing to limited availability, this study employed a stratified random sampling method to select 90 participants to wear the accelerometer [35]. The selection process was carried out using the random number generator feature in SPSS (Version 23.0). The Actigraph GT3X + accelerometer has previously been validated for measuring PA in adolescents [36]. The minutes spent on SED or engaged in LPA or MVPA during recess were assessed by accelerometers (30 Hz, 10-s epochs). Participants (n = 90) wore the accelerometers from 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM on Monday to Friday during both the baseline and the intervention. The accelerometer was worn on the right hip and secured by an elastic waist belt. In accordance with the intensity classification criteria for adolescents in this study, the accelerometer data were scored using cutpoints of Evenson in ActiLife Version 6.0 software. The intensity of PA was divided into SB (0–100 counts/min), LPA (101–2295 counts/min), and MVPA (< 2295 counts/min) [37].

Students were considered eligible for analysis if they wore the device at least 6 h per day and had 3 days of wear during baseline, posttest, and follow-up periods, with coverage of at least one short and lunch recess each day. Utilizing the median of student average MVPA time during recess, students were categorized into two distinct groups, namely, active students and least active students, on the basis of their activity status [38].

Focus group interviews

To gain a deeper understanding of intervention participant perceptions and to better explain the effectiveness of the intervention, this study conducted focus group interviews with students following the intervention. This study followed the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) checklist to ensure comprehensive [39] and transparent reporting of qualitative methods and findings (see Supplementary File 3). Data were collected using the focused interview method through a semistructured approach, which followed the principles of convenience and information saturation [40]. A total of 21 individuals were selected as interviewees, including 14 students and 7 staff members. Group interviews were conducted separately for student groups (comprising 4–6 students per group) and staff groups (comprising 7 members), each lasting approximately 40 min. The interviews were guided by a semistructured interview script. Sample questions for student groups included “What new insights or knowledge have been gained or learned through recess activity intervention?" "How do you experience the present recess being different compared to last semester?" "What are your recommendations for recess activities?" The specific interview guide can be found in Supplementary File 4. The interviews, with a total of 22,000 words, were recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Statistical analysis

All the quantitative data were subjected to statistical analyses using SPSS Statistics (v24.0.1.0; IBM, New York, USA), with significance determined at p ≤ 0.05.

Physical activity

Rainy days were excluded from the analyses (posttest: n = 1; follow-up: n = 2). The percentage of students who participated in each PA intensity (including SED, LPA, and MVPA) was calculated by dividing the number of participants in each PA intensity by the total number of students observed. A chi-square test was conducted to compare the percentages of each PA intensity through the SOPLAY observed at baseline, posttest, and follow-up. Interpolation analysis can reflect the trend of geographic data changes with spatial variations [41]. Interpolation using the radial basis function (RBF) in GIS was employed. This interpolation method revealed the centre of MVPA participation during recess, with darker colours indicating higher concentrations of active participants.

The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test indicated that the PA time was skewed; therefore, a square root transformation was applied to obtain normally distributed PA data [42]. In this study, mixed linear regression was employed to investigate the intervention’s impact on the duration of LPA and MVPA. This analysis was based on common independent variables and influencing factors in recess activities [43]. Analyses concerning recess were controlled for sex, body mass index (BMI), age, intervention, activity status, and recess duration. Interaction effects with sex, age, activity status, and recess duration during recess were examined [38]. Participants were included in the model as random effects. To test the interaction effect, the cross-product terms (sex * intervention, activity status * intervention, age * intervention, and recess duration * intervention) were individually added to the model to determine their effects on the intervention outcome. When significant interactions were found, separate models were fitted for the interaction classification. p < 0.05 was considered significant, except for the interaction terms, where the threshold was set at p < 0.1.

Qualitative data

Qualitative NVivo 14.0 software was used for coding, involving processes such as labelling, identifying categories and dimensions, enriching categories and dimensions, establishing connections between categories and dimensions, and determining core codes. All participant data were identified and referred to by pseudonyms. The entire coding process was conducted simultaneously by one researcher and one graduate student from the research team. By establishing the contextual significance of the core codes, evaluation results of the intervention effects and their interpretations were generated.

Results

Physical characteristics and demographic measures

The participants, including 229 boys and 256 girls, were selected from grades 10 and 11 to receive the intervention. A total of 90 students were selected as accelerometer subjects. Owing to device errors, forgetfulness, participant absences, and other factors, a total of 29 student data were excluded from the baseline (6 students), posttest (11 students), and follow-up (12 students) assessments. Sixty-one students participated in valid measurements at three time points. The sample size was sufficient to detect an intervention effect. For active students (64.52%), the LPA time was 6.43 ± 1.27 min (60.89%), and the MVPA time was 3.14 ± 1.32 min (29.32%).

Subjective interview findings

Three key themes related to intervention effectiveness were identified: (1) enriched spatial perception, (2) behavioural changes in activity, and (3) comfort-zone effects for active participants. In addition, 15 (71.43%) stakeholders perceived the intervention as effective. A total of 120 coded sentences were recorded. The results are described in Fig. 4.

Results of the interview topic coding and theme sentence

Acceptance of behavioural guidance

The first theme reflects the acceptance of behavioural guidance among students. In the coding analysis, this is represented by the category of “enriched spatial recognition”. The subdimensions include the following: 6 statements on “accessibility” (e.g., “Control the distance and time of activity”; “Understand the distance between spaces”) and 14 statements on “space information” (e.g., “Finding the location of underutilized spaces”; “Function of informal sports spaces”; “The location of equipment”; and “The location of activity spaces”). The focus interviews revealed that behavioural guidance clarified the quantity, location, and accessibility of school areas and altered the definition of informal sports areas. These changes are typically attributed to an increase in spatial cognition, which can help students better utilize recess time to participate in activities [44].

Effectiveness of the intervention

The second theme is consistent with the quantitative results, reflecting the effectiveness of the intervention in promoting the number of those participating in PAs. In the coding analysis, “behavioural change” represents positive feedback on the effectiveness of the intervention in increasing the number of those participating in PAs. The subdimensions include the following: 27 statements on “increased frequency of activities” (e.g., “The number of activities has increased”; “Get moving”; and “Go play ball after class"), 15 statements on “richness of activities” (e.g., “Walking has turned into playing ball”; “Learn new sports”; and “More opportunities for physical activity”), and 15 statements on “activity in informal activity spaces” (e.g., “Walking has turned into playing ball”; “More opportunities for physical activity”; and “Learn new sports”). The focus interviews indicated an increase in the frequency of students leaving the classroom. Additionally, informal areas were utilized more frequently, resulting in more physical activities and exposure to, as well as learning of, various sports during peer interactions. These changes are typically attributed to behavioural changes [45].

Different effects among students

The last theme explains the different effects of the intervention among students with varying activity levels. During the second round of coding, we found that not all the students perceived the intervention as effective. To capture this diversity, we developed a separate theme—“The comfort-zone effect for active individuals.” This theme represents the varying degrees of response to the intervention. The subdimensions include the following: 5 statements on “fixed activity type” (e.g., “Playing basketball consistently”; “Sticking to my favourite sport") and 9 statements on “no change in activity intensity” (e.g., “Keep exercising, no significant changes”; “Have a favourite sport, not interested in other activities”). The focus interviews provided several reasons, which can be seen as the comfort-zone effect. For instance, students who have a long-standing preference for one activity may be more inclined to continue with it, leading to a homogenization of activities and limiting the acceptance of higher-intensity physical activities.

Discussion

The designed intervention was implemented in a case high school, and the observation results revealed a significant increase in the number of participants engaging in MVPA across several outdoor areas compared with the baseline. The objective measurement results indicated an increase in MVPA time, with activity status and recess duration serving as moderators influencing the intervention outcomes. These findings were further supported by the focus group interviews.

Intervention design

In this study, a recess activity intervention that combines spatial planning and behavioural guidance strategies based on spatial analysis technology was designed. In past research, a intervention for adolescents have relied solely on the idea that the environment directly influences behaviour, leading students to accept external environmental changes passively [20]. In contrast, this study is grounded in the idea that people are highly sensitive to contextual cues that prompt their intentions to exercise [46]. On the basis of the quantitative spatial computation function of the GIS, this study provided students with not only more accessible activity equipment but also behavioural guidance involving spatial knowledge and the functions of MVPA through an activity booklet and map. Students acknowledged the design of the intervention, including the increased knowledge of activities and the addition of equipment, which was validated in the focus group interviews. In addition, in contrast to previous intervention designs that focused solely on providing equipment for formal sports arenas [21], this study defined multiple activity functions within informal sports areas and provided relevant equipment. Moreover, our intervention design aligns with the Playworks (a national nonprofit) recommendation to transform any area into a play area, which aims to encourage more flexible and accessible opportunities for PAs [47].

Physical activity participation

In this study, multiple areas at varying distances from the teaching building were observed, and areas where MVPA participation was concentrated were identified. This study provides a more comprehensive analysis of how the intervention affected student activity behaviour across different types of activity areas. The results of this study indicated that the total number of participants engaging in MVPA increased. Although the intervention content differed, the results of this study are similar to those of a previous intervention that focused on providing equipment in dedicated playgrounds. However, the advantage of this study lies in the increase in the number of participants engaging in MVPA across multiple areas. In addition, the area with an increased number of participants engaging in MVPA was located near the teaching building; this finding is in line with the viewpoint that areas with higher accessibility can encourage people to engage in outdoor activities more frequently [48], and future research may explore more organized intervention strategies for areas with poorer accessibility, such as teacher organization, student leadership, and activity planning [21]. Moreover, there was a significant increase in MVPA participation in some informal sports areas. The possible reason for this can be explained by the focus group interviews, particularly the coded “Function of informal sports areas”. These findings further validate the definability of exercise functions within informal sports areas. Therefore, informal sports areas should be included as intervention targets in future recess interventions.

Physical activity time

Both objective measurements and focus group interview results indicated that a significant increase in MVPA time was observed only among inactive students. The possible reason for these results is that active students exhibit a “ceiling effect”, as the most active students prefer to engage in PA during recess, regardless of the available space. In this study, active students reported an average daily cumulative MVPA duration of 21.98 min (7 recess × 3.14 MVPA minutes), accounting for 36.63% of the recommended daily physical activity. The PA of active students aligns with previous reports of recess contributions ranging from 17 to 44% of the daily PA [5]. One study reported that active students can use the time before recess to plan their activities, thereby making the most of the recess period [49]. This inclination may stem from their intrinsic motivation for PA [50], as affirmed in the focus interview code “Keep exercising, no significant changes”.

For students of different sexes, the intervention in this study resulted in a significant increase in MVPA time for both boys and girls. This differs from the combined intervention effects observed by a previous study [20], which reported that most boys perceive recess as an opportunity for competitive games, whereas girls view it as a chance to socialize with friends [31]. This study addressed this sex disparity in activity opportunities by incorporating loose sports equipment, such as shuttlecock kicking and skipping ropes for girls, facilitating both social interaction and physical activity. For students of different ages, the results of this study revealed no significant differences in effectiveness across the age groups. This is different from previous research on elementary schools, which has suggested that older students tend to occupy available game spaces during recess, such as soccer fields [43]. This may be attributed to the fact that adolescent students have a greater degree of behavioural autonomy development [51], which may enable them to autonomously choose activity locations.

This study revealed that the intervention was influenced by recess duration. This finding is consistent with previous research that revealed a positive correlation between participation in PAs and recess duration [52]. In the 10-min short recess of this study, the intervention sites were all set outdoors. The study hypothesized that the maximum distance students would walk to reach the activity areas is 2 min, and the arrangement where students had to go downstairs to participate in activities resulted in less than 5 min of activity time for the students. The limited activity time constrained the potential increase in MVPA time, which may explain why the intervention effects were not significant during the short recess. In contrast, other intervention studies conducted in schools have reported recess durations of 15 min or more [21]. This highlights the importance of extending recess duration in school environments, a consensus shared by scholars and relevant institutions involved in global recess activity research [53, 54]. On the other hand, in situations where extending recess duration is not feasible, the intervention strategy in future studies should consider incorporating indoor areas as a supplement to address the space limitations caused by short recess duration and vertical school structure. Indoor areas as intervention sites have often been overlooked in previous research; therefore, their potential deserves further exploration.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first to design a recess intervention that combines spatial planning and behavioural guidance strategies to prompt recess activity in high school. Spatial analysis technology was used to objectively plan activity areas during recess and provide a quantified recess intervention program. In addition, this study used adequate outcome measurements, including accelerometer, observation, and focus group interviews, to triangulate the count data. Most importantly, the material used in this study was from the existing equipment of the school, which is a low-cost intervention (15.97 ¥/person/week). However, the following limitations of this study should be noted.

The first limitation is the use of a case study approach. Although the case study can provide preliminary insights into the feasibility of intervention and guide future research, the results are limited by the sample being confined to a single school. This makes it difficult to determine whether these findings are applicable to all schools. Future research should involve more schools in interventions and conduct controlled experiments to validate their effectiveness. The second limitation lies in the absence of sex data in the observation method. Because some girls tended to have short hair in the case school, observers were unable to accurately determine the sex of the subjects. Therefore, to ensure the accuracy of the observations, this study did not tally changes in the number of males or females. The third limitation is observation bias; SOPLAY relies on human observers for scanning, which may introduce some degree of error [34]. To improve accuracy, future research could consider combining GPS tracking technology to objectively monitor student activity behaviour in areas during recess. The final limitation is that interventions were attempted only 10 to 15 min into unstructured recess. This study revealed that the intervention effects varied across recess durations. The results of this study indicated that recess duration may be a limiting factor in achieving intervention effectiveness. Future research should explore interventions with varying recess durations to identify the optimal duration for maximizing MVPA promotion.

Conclusion

The intervention combining spatial planning and behavioural guidance strategies increased MVPA participation and time. Recess duration and student activity status were moderators influencing the effectiveness of the intervention. The intervention developed in this study may be cost effective, which provides valuable practical experience for similar future interventions in high schools under temporal and spatial constraints. Considering the limitations of the case study method, future research should expand the sample size and explore the potential impact of different school recess durations on intervention outcomes to verify the effectiveness of the intervention in a broader context.
