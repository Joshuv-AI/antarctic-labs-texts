Comparison of conventional formulas and machine learning models for estimating serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol
Authors: Ruqiya Al Dhuhli, Nafila Al Riyami, Fatma Al Farsi, Ali Al Khamyasi, Huda Al Saadi, Said Al Alawi, Qutaiba Alfarai, Hamza Zidoum, Faisal Alsalmani, Yousef Almarshodi, Fahad Zadjali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate estimation of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is essential for cardiovascular risk assessment and monitoring. Because β-quantification is labor-intensive and impractical for routine use, several equations have been developed to estimate LDL-C indirectly, including the widely used Friedewald formula. This study evaluated established LDL-C equations and multiple machine learning models against directly measured LDL-C across different triglyceride and LDL cholesterol levels.MethodsA total of 303 serum samples, categorized by triglyceride levels, were analyzed for LDL-C using a direct homogeneous assay on the Roche Cobas® e6000 platform and compared with values estimated by twelve established equations. Agreement was assessed using correlation analysis, mean bias, and Bland–Altman plots. In addition, supervised machine learning models were developed using an 60:40 training–testing split to evaluate predictive performance.ResultsAll equations except Ahmadi showed strong correlation with direct LDL-C (r > 0.90). The Puavilai and DeLong formulas demonstrated the highest correlation (r ≈ 0.97), with Puavilai showing the lowest mean bias (0.005 mM). Among machine learning models, Artificial Neural Network (ANN), linear regression, and ridge regression achieved the highest correlation (R = 0.97). The ANN showed minimal bias (0.04 mM), while most other models slightly underestimated LDL-C. The Friedewald formula demonstrated increasing positive bias at higher triglyceride levels.ConclusionThe Puavilai formula and ANN model showed superior agreement with direct LDL-C measurements compared with the Friedewald equation. These findings suggest that alternative equations, particularly Puavilai, may improve LDL-C estimation in routine clinical practice in Oman. Further multicenter validation is warranted before national implementation.

Introduction

Cholesterol is an essential structural component of cell membranes and acts as a precursor for the synthesis of various biologically important macromolecules. In the blood circulation, cholesterol and other lipids are transported within lipoproteins, including chylomicron (CM), very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL), high-density lipoproteins (HDL), and low-density lipoproteins (LDL). Elevated circulating cholesterol levels, i.e, hypercholesterolemia, represent a major risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), which continues to be a leading cause of mortality worldwide [1]. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is a modifiable risk factor for coronary heart disease, and guideline-based LDL-C reduction targets have proved significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality [2]. Hence, routine assessment of LDL-C levels is recommended for a general prevention strategy for the general population and also evaluation and management of patients with hypercholesterolemia and other comorbidities [3].

The reference gold-standard method for measuring LDL cholesterol is beta-quantification [4,5]. However, due to its labor-intensive and time-consuming procedure, it is not practical for routine clinical use, as it is costly and requires batch processing of numerous samples. Alternative homogeneous (direct) assays have been developed to measure LDL-C without its separation from other lipoprotein particles [5]. Nevertheless, these methods remain relatively expensive, particularly in high-volume testing settings. The concept of calculating LDL cholesterol based on the lipid profile was developed a few decades ago by James Friedewald in 1972 [6]. The Friedewald formula was based on the assumption that the ratio of triglycerides (TG) to cholesterol in very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) is a factor of 5. The formula is widely adopted in practice, yet it is prone to inaccuracy especially when serum triglycerides are very high or LDL particles are low [7]. Therefore, multiple alternative equations have been developed over time and evaluated for their accuracy for estimation of LDL-C levels in serum. Studies from different countries have reported inconsistent findings regarding the most accurate estimating formula. In a study conducted in Italy, the Sampson, Martin, and Vujovic equations demonstrated greater accuracy than the Friedewald formula [8]. While research from Iran identified the Hattori and de Cordova formulae as more accurate [9]. In the context of the effect of high serum TG on LDL-C estimation, recently developed extended Martin Hopkins equation exhibits significantly improved accuracy in hypertriglyceridemia with less tendency for underestimation of risk in low LDL-C [10]. A population-specific formula was generated in Saudi Arabia that outperformed the Friedewald, Martin–Hopkins and Sampson-NIH formula [11].

In Oman, the Friedewald formula is routinely used in health laboratories to estimate LDL-C levels. According to a recent community-based survey [12], the prevalence of hypercholesterolemia is 36%, and the Friedewald formula remains the most widely applied method for LDL-C estimation in the country. The performance of the Friedewald formula warrants re-evaluation, particularly in the era of novel LDL-C–lowering therapies such as PCSK9 inhibitors and small interfering RNA (siRNA)–based treatments, which can produce substantial reductions in LDL-C levels [13]. These marked decreases may compromise the accuracy of the Friedewald formula and lead to underperformance in certain clinical settings. Therefore, this study aims to assess the accuracy of twelve established LDL-C equations, as well as machine learning models, in estimating LDL-C concentrations.

Methods

Study design and population

A total of 303 serum samples were collected from the clinical biochemistry laboratory for lipid profile analysis, irrespective of sex, age, or clinical history. Convenience block sampling was applied, and samples were categorized as per Adult Treatment Panel III of the National Cholesterol Education Program Guideline [14] according to triglyceride (TG) levels as follows: Group 1, TG < 1.7 mM; Group 2, TG 1.7–3.2 mM; and Group 3, TG 3.2–4.5 mM. Minimum sample size was calculated based on paired analysis of the mean difference of the Friedewald formula of 0.13 with standard deviation of 0.17 (adopted from a previous study [15]). With 80% power and two-sided significance level of 0.05, the minimum sample size per group was calculated as 16 paired comparisons. Exclusion criteria in serum selection were hemolyzed samples and samples from patients with homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the hospital (SQU-EC/114/2023. MREC#3012).

Lipid profile analysis and LDL-C estimation

LDL-C level was measured using a direct assay (LDL-Cholesterol Gen. 3. Roche Diagnostics International Ltd; Mannheim, Germany) using Roche Cobas® e6000 system. Total cholesterol (TC), HDL-C, and Triglyceride concentrations were measured using colorimetric assay from Roche Diagnostics International Ltd; Mannheim, Germany: Cholesterol Gen. 2 assay, HDL-cholesterol plus 4 generation assay, and TRIGLI assay. The assays were run according to the manufacturer's protocols in Roche Cobas® e6000 analyzer. LDL-C of the same samples were estimated using 12 equations (Table 1). Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.0, GraphPad Software, Inc.). Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson correlation. Bias was calculated from the difference of LDL-C measured directly minus the estimated one. Bland-Altman plots were generated to illustrate bias across different LDL-C levels.

Supervised machine learning algorithms were implemented using the Python programming language (Python Software Foundation, 3.x) in the Google Colab environment (Google, 2026). LDL estimations were based on four features: Total cholesterol, HDL-C, VLDL-C and TG. Following algorithms were implemented: Support Vector Machines (SVM) [26], Random Forest [27], K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), XGBoost [28,29], linear regression, CatBoost [30], gradient boosting, Lasso regression [31], Decision Tree and Ridge regression [32]. The 60:40 train-test split strategy employed. Model performance was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation on the training dataset. Predictive performance was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R2) and root mean squared error (RMSE). Mean and standard deviation values across the five folds were calculated to estimate model accuracy and stability. An Artificial Neural Network (ANN) regression model was developed using a feedforward multilayer perceptron architecture implemented in Keras [33,34]. The network consisted of four fully connected hidden layers with 256, 128, 64, and 32 neurons, respectively, each using the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function to introduce non-linearity. In all models The output layer comprised a single neuron with linear activation to predict the continuous outcome variable. The model was compiled using the Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation) optimizer [35], with mean squared error (MSE) as the loss function and mean absolute error (MAE) as an additional performance metric. The model was trained on the training dataset for 1000 epochs with a batch size of 32, and 20% of the training data was reserved for internal validation to monitor performance and reduce overfitting. Learning curves were made across different training samples between R2 of training and R2 of testing.

Performance of machine learning model in estimation of LDL cholesterol levels

The analysis was repeated using different machine learning models. The learning curves reveal distinct bias-variance characteristics across models, Fig. 3. Linear Regression, Ridge, Gradient Boosting, Random Forest, XGBoost, CatBoost, and the Neural Network generally exhibited good learning curves, showing a balanced bias-variance trade-off with high and converging R2 scores for both training and testing sets. Least differences between training and testing R2 were observed in Ridge, ANN and linear regression models. In contrast, Lasso Regression demonstrated high bias (underfitting) with consistently low R2 scores and a significant gap, while Decision Tree and KNN displayed high variance (overfitting), characterized by very high training R2 but much lower and less stable testing R2 scores, Fig. 3. Discussion

Accurate estimation of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is fundamental to cardiovascular risk stratification and therapeutic monitoring. Although the Friedewald formula remains the most widely used method worldwide, its accuracy may vary under certain clinical conditions. In the present study, we compared LDL-C values estimated using twelve published formulas and multiple machine learning models against measurements obtained from a direct homogeneous LDL-C assay. Among the evaluated approaches, the Puavilai and DeLong formulas, as well as the ANN model, demonstrated superior performance. Notably, the Puavilai formula showed the tightest agreement and strongest concordance with the direct homogeneous LDL-C assay.

In our study, DeLong and Puavilai formulae demonstrated the highest overall correlation with directly measured LDL-C (r = 0.9672 and r = 0.9671, respectively), with the Puavilai formula also showing the lowest mean bias of 0.005. This suggests that both formulae are reliable alternatives to direct LDL-C measurement, particularly the Puavilai formula, which demonstrated the tightest agreement. In contrast, the Ahmadi formula performed poorly, with a weak correlation (r = 0.52) and the highest negative bias (−1.85), indicating substantial underestimation. The Ahmadi formula was intended for very low LDL-cholesterol values, and hence it underperformed in our study [20]. This result is in alignment with a study in the Iranian population [9] but a high positive bias, rather than a negative bias, was observed in our study.

Although previous studies, for example a study conducted in Italy by Ref. [8], found that the Martin, Sampson, and Vujovic formulae had the best performance when compared to direct LDL-C, with the Martin formula showing improved accuracy in patients with elevated triglycerides. In our study, the Martin and Sampson formulae showed strong correlation but exhibited inferior negative mean bias of −0.54. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in population lipid profiles or the absence of extremely high triglyceride levels (>4.5 mM) in our study population. In contrast, an Iranian study by Karkhaneh et al. [9] found that the Hattori and de Cordova formulae were more accurate in their population. However, these formulae in our study exhibited moderate positive biases (0.35 and 0.11, respectively), suggesting a tendency toward overestimation. Such inter-study variation could be explained by population-specific factors—such as diet, genetics, and prevalence of metabolic syndrome—on LDL-C estimation performance. Another large-scale analysis by Sampson et al. showed that the Sampson and Martin-Hopkins formulae outperform Friedewald at high triglyceride levels, particularly above 4.5 mM [25]. However, in our triglyceride-stratified analysis (up to 4.5 mM), both formulae underperformed compared to DeLong and Puavilai, suggesting that while Sampson and Martin may be superior in hypertriglyceridemic patients, they may introduce negative bias in individuals with moderate triglyceride levels.

Despite of their computational requirements, Machine learning models are widely used in diagnostic accuracy studies. Previous studies demonstrated that machine learning models outperformed formula-based LDL estimation [[36], [37], [38], [39]]. With exception of Lasso regression, KNN, and decision tree models, all other machine learning models and ANN model exhibited good fitting in learning curve. Among these models, ANN displayed best performance in correlation and bias, which is consistence with previous study [37]. The ANN model may have demonstrated superior performance because it is capable of modeling complex, non-linear relationships among variables, which traditional linear equations (like Friedewald) cannot adequately capture [40,41]. We agree that simpler architectures may be more appropriate for small datasets and risk of overparameterization. However, the ANN demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to most linear formulas, suggesting that the added model complexity captured meaningful nonlinear patterns rather than noise.

In routine clinical practice within Omani hospitals, the Friedewald equation remains widely used method for LDL-C estimation. A well-recognized limitation of this formula is the use of a fixed divisor of 5 to estimate VLDL-C from triglycerides (TG/5) [6]. This assumption presumes a constant TG:VLDL-C ratio; however, when triglyceride concentrations exceed 4.5 mM—particularly in the presence of elevated chylomicrons and VLDL remnants—the TG:VLDL-C ratio may surpass 5, leading to underestimation of VLDL-C and consequent misclassification of LDL-C [6,25]. In our cohort, the TG:VLDL-C ratio ranged between 2.1 and 2.5, whereas the Lipid Research Clinics Prevalence Study reported ratios extending up to 8.5 [6]. Importantly, samples with triglyceride levels >4.5 mM were limited in our analysis/Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to individuals with severe hypertriglyceridemia. Additional limitations should be acknowledged. First, the reference beta-quantification method was not available, precluding comparison with the gold-standard assay. Second, all measurements were performed using the Roche Diagnostics platform; thus, extrapolation to other analytical systems cannot be assumed. Third, external validation of the Puavilai formula across other hospitals and laboratory settings was not undertaken. Accordingly, larger, multicenter studies incorporating patients with severe hypertriglyceridemia are warranted to comprehensively validate performance across the full spectrum of LDL-C and triglyceride concentrations prior to widespread clinical implementation. Our findings challenge this default reliance on Friedewald formula by showing that other formulae—particularly Puavilai—outperform Friedewald in both accuracy and agreement with direct LDL-C. This supports a potential shift in laboratory policy, particularly in populations with varying triglyceride levels, to ensure more reliable cardiovascular risk assessment.

In conclusion, accurate estimation of LDL cholesterol is essential in screening the general population and the management of patients with dyslipidemia. Given the high prevalence of hypercholesterolemia in the region and the reliance on calculated LDL-C in routine laboratory testing, this study provides critical evidence to inform national clinical laboratory policy. While the widely used Friedewald formula demonstrated strong correlation with direct measurement, the Puavilai formula showed superior performance in LDL-estimation across different levels of serum LDL cholesterol and triglyceride levels.
