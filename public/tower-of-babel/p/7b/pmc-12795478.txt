There is currently no evidence that long-COVID-19 leads to neurodegenerative diseases such as SDAT, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, or Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Josef Finsterer
Published: 2025

We read with interest the article by Carpio–Orantes pointing out that prolonged COVID-19 disease or long-COVID-19 (persistent COVID-19 symptoms for more than 3 months) leads to neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (SDAT), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Parkinson’s disease (PD), multiple sclerosis (MS), or Huntington’s disease (HD).[1] The commentary is thought-provoking, but several points should be discussed.

The first point is that there are several arguments against the hypothesis that prolonged COVID-19 leads to neurodegenerative diseases. First, prolonged COVID-19 is rarely associated with structural or functional abnormalities in the nervous system.[23] Cerebral imaging is usually normal after SARS-CoV-2 infection unless the acute infection was complicated by encephalitis, meningitis, stroke, sinus vein thrombosis, vasoconstriction syndrome, or hemorrhage.[4] Therefore, it could be useful to distinguish between long-COVID-19 patients in whom there was cerebral involvement clinically, morphologically, or functionally and those in whom this was not the case. Patients with structural cerebral lesions as a result of the infection are more likely to have long-term complications than patients without these lesions.[5] Second, SARS-CoV-2 has rarely been detected in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with cerebral involvement during acute infection. Third, although virus particles were found in all brain regions in COVID-19 patients at autopsy, these regions show little inflammation or virus cytopathology.[6] Fourth, there are no studies that clearly demonstrate that the prevalence of SDAT, ALS, MS, HD, or PD has actually increased since the end of the pandemic.

The second point is that long-COVID is explained by mechanisms, for which there is limited evidence. Long-COVID-19 has been explained by viral persistence, immunothrombosis, fusion of neurons with glial cells, neuroinflammation, decreased neurogenesis, hypocorticism due to hypothalamic–pituitary axis dysfunction, and vagal dysfunction due to dysbiosis.[1] Currently, there is not much evidence that long-COVID-19 is due to venous sinus thrombosis (VST), pituitary dysfunction, or vagal dysfunction.[7] VST has rarely been reported in the acute phase of SARS-CoV-2 infection and is limited to this phase of the disease.[8] Hypometabolism in certain brain regions (e.g., olfactory bulbs) can hardly explain the general symptoms of long COVID-19 (impaired memory, concentration, cognition, executive functions, fatigue, and exercise intolerance). There is also little evidence that the skeletal muscles are generally affected in long-COVID-19 and are therefore responsible for fatigue as one of the main symptoms. Only in patients who have acquired myositis during the acute phase of SARS-CoV-2 infection can skeletal muscle changes be detected.[9]

Third, HD or MS are not generally considered neurodegenerative diseases.[1] HD is generally considered a genetic disorder caused by a cytosin, adenine, guanine (CAG) repeat expansion > 35 in the HTT gene.[10] MS is an immunological disorder that is most likely triggered by previous viral infections.[11] Whether SARS-CoV-2 is actually involved in the development of prion diseases is controversial. In single cases, prion disease after COVID-19 has been reported, but the causal link remained unproven.[12] There is currently no increase in the prevalence of prion diseases worldwide.

The fourth point is that the proposed treatment of long-COVID-19 with fluoxetine or sertraline is usually of limited benefit. Since the underlying pathophysiology of long-COVID-19 is multifactorial, it is unlikely that a single SSRI can influence all pathophysiological mechanisms, leading to long-COVID-19.

To summarize, some of the statements in the index study are speculative and not supported by evidence. There is currently no proven case of a patient with long COVID-19 who has developed SDAT, ALS, or prion disease. Individual studies reporting abnormalities in patients with long COVID-19 provide limited evidence to support the hypothesis put forward in the index study. Longitudinal studies and biomarker research are desirable to add depth and highlight the importance of an evidence-based approach.
