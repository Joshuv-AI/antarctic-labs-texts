Antigenic and structural insights into Langya henipavirus attachment glycoprotein
Authors: Yaohui Li, Xiaoyan Huang, Xiaodong Zai, Chenfeng Mao, Ruihua Li, Yamei Feng, Yue Zhang, Zhang Zhang, Jun Zhang, Junjie Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe invasion of host cells by the henipavirus is facilitated through the interaction between viral attachment (G) and fusion (F) glycoproteins with receptors on the cell surface. Langya henipavirus (LayV) was newly identified in China in 2022. The G proteins of LayV and Mojiang virus (MojV) exhibit high amino acid homology (86%), while they are located in a unique evolutionary clade within the Henipavirus genus. In this study, the crystal structure of the LayV G protein was resolved at a 3.37 Å resolution, revealing a head domain with six β-propeller-like domains distinct from other henipavirus G proteins, such as those of Nipah virus (NiV) and Hendra virus (HeV). Furthermore, the prominent loop in the center cavity of the LayV G protein showed unique structural features. In the ELISA and SPR assays, the LayV G protein was unable to bind to the existing henipavirus-neutralizing antibodies or the ephrin-B2 receptor. Immunogenicity studies in mice demonstrated robust antibody responses elicited by the LayV G protein. These antibodies exhibited strong reactivity against both LayV and MojV G proteins. However, only weak cross-reactivity was observed with other henipaviruses. Moreover, eight monoclonal antibodies targeting the LayV G protein were generated, two of which exhibited broad binding activity across different henipavirus G proteins. These findings underscore the need for tailored vaccines and therapeutics for LayV and related novel henipavirusesKeywords: Langya henipavirus (LayV), Attachment glycoprotein, Crystal structure, Glycosylation, Immunogenicity, Monoclonal antibodies

Introduction

The Henipavirus is a genus of single-stranded, negative-sense RNA viruses. This genus includes several highly pathogenic viruses that have caused outbreaks in humans and animals. The Hendra virus (HeV) was first identified in 1994 following an outbreak in Hendra, Australia (Williamson et al., 1998). The Nipah virus (NiV) first emerged during outbreaks in Malaysia and Singapore in 1998 and is further classified into the Malaysian and Bangladeshi strains (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 1999; Clayton et al., 2016). In recent years, the known diversity of henipaviruses has significantly expanded with the discovery of the Cedar virus (CedV) in Australian flying foxes (Marsh et al., 2012), the Ghana virus (GhV) in bats from Ghana (Lee et al., 2015), the Mojiang virus (MojV) in rats from Yunnan, China (Wu et al., 2014), the Gamak and Daeryong viruses in Korean mole shrews (Lee et al., 2021), and the Angavokely virus in fruit bats from Madagascar (Madera et al., 2022). In 2022, a novel Hendra virus variant (HeV-g2) was identified in a horse in Queensland, Australia (Taylor et al., 2022). That same year, a distinct henipavirus, named Langya henipavirus (LayV), was detected during sentinel surveillance of febrile patients in eastern China (Zhang et al., 2022). The genome of LayV consists of 18,402 nucleotides and exhibits a genomic organization similar to other known henipaviruses (Zhang et al., 2022). These findings have contributed to a broader understanding of the Henipavirus genus.

Despite the diverse range of targeted host receptors, the attachment glycoprotein (G) of henipaviruses exhibits a conserved molecular structure, including an amino-terminal cytoplasmic tail, a single transmembrane helix, a stalk region, and a carboxy-terminal six-bladed β-propeller domain (Rissanen et al., 2017). In henipaviruses, viral invasion is facilitated through the interaction between viral attachment and fusion glycoproteins with receptors on the cell surface (Bradel-Tretheway et al., 2019). The structures of the attachment glycoproteins for NiV, HeV, GhV, and MojV have been elucidated (Xu et al., 2004; Bowden et al., 2008a; Bowden et al., 2008b; Lee et al., 2015; Rissanen et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2022a). Recently, some other cryo-electron microscopy studies and crystal structure analysis have defined the structure of the LayV G protein (Guo et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). The structure of the LayV F protein has also been elucidated (Isaacs et al., 2023; May Aaron et al., 2023; Byrne et al., 2024). Although these studies provided valuable structural insights, they did not address the glycosylation profile and immunogenic properties of LayV G, which are essential for understanding its antigenicity and for vaccine development.

The attachment glycoproteins of NiV and HeV recognize the receptors ephrin-B2 and ephrin-B3 (Bonaparte et al., 2005; Negrete et al., 2006), while the attachment glycoprotein of GhV specifically binds to ephrin-B2 (Lee et al., 2015). Additionally, CedV utilizes the receptors ephrin-B1, ephrin-A2, and ephrin-A5 (Laing et al., 2019). To date, the specific host receptors for MojV and LayV remain uncharacterized (Rissanen et al., 2017; Cheliout Da Silva et al., 2021). Neutralizing antibodies can neutralize NiV and HeV infections by binding to the attachment glycoproteins (Xu et al., 2013; Dong et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022a; Wang et al., 2022b). Furthermore, comprehensive structural analyses have revealed the significance of various henipavirus fusion glycoproteins as crucial targets for neutralizing antibodies (Avanzato et al., 2019; Dang et al., 2019, 2021; Mire et al., 2020; Byrne et al., 2023).

To date, there is no licensed vaccine against henipaviruses, although over 40 vaccine candidates are in development (Gomez Roman et al., 2022). Most candidate vaccines use the attachment glycoprotein G, which recognizes the receptors ephrin-B2 and ephrin-B3 on the host cell surface as the antigen. Based on the low sequence homology between LayV and HeV/NiV (∼24%), existing candidate vaccines and antibody drugs are unlikely to protect against the newly emerged henipavirus. Therefore, more efforts are needed to develop vaccines and antibodies to combat this novel pathogen. In this study, we explored the structure and immunogenicity of the LayV G protein. From a structural viewpoint, the LayV G protein exhibits steric hindrance at the receptor-binding site caused by residues 242–247 and K351, L491, and R509, along with unique glycosylation at positions 189 and 619. Our immunogenicity experiments confirm that LayV G induces distinct antibody responses compared to NiV and HeV G proteins. These features highlight its unique antigenic profile, which is relevant for vaccine and diagnostic development.

Results

The LayV G protein exhibits distinct evolutionary branches within the genus Henipavirus

We conducted multiple sequence alignment analyses to determine the amino acid homology of henipavirus G proteins (Fig. 1A). The highest amino acid homology was observed between the LayV G and MojV G proteins, reaching 86%. The amino acid homologies between the LayV G protein and Bangladesh-type NiV (Bd NiV) G and HeV G proteins were 22% and 23%, respectively. In contrast, the homology with the GhV G protein was 20%. N-glycosylation is a prominent feature of henipavirus G proteins, such as the NiV G protein. Glycosylation analysis predicted four N-glycosylation sites on the LayV G protein. Among them, N61 and N64 are located near the membrane in the stalk region, site 189 is positioned at the transition between the head and stalk regions, and site 619 is situated in a flexible region at the C-terminus of the protein (Fig. 1B). We conducted secretory expression for the extracellular domain (G) and the head domain (Gs) of the LayV G protein using the Expi293 expression system. Under non-reducing conditions, LayV-G and other henipavirus G/Gs proteins migrated as diffuse, higher-molecular-weight bands, whereas under reducing conditions they resolved into sharp single bands, indicating that these recombinant proteins were properly folded and stabilized by disulfide bonds (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, treatment of LayV-Gs with PNGase F resulted in a clear downward shift in molecular weight, confirming the presence of N-linked glycosylation in the head domain and explaining the heterogeneity observed in the untreated samples (Fig. 1D).

Using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the glycosylation profile in the head region (residues 166–623) of the LayV G protein. Our results confirmed N-glycosylation modifications at positions 189 and 619, with modification ratios of 93.92% and 29.53%, respectively. Additionally, multiple O-glycosylation sites were identified in this region (Supplementary Table S1). Position 189 exhibited substantial glycan heterogeneity with 42 distinct glycoforms detected. The predominant glycoform at this site was HexNAc5Hex3Fuc1 (18.36% relative abundance, 1.06 × 1010 signal intensity), followed by HexNAc5Hex5Fuc1 (11.72%) and HexNAc4Hex3Fuc1 (11.02%). Neuraminidase-sensitive modifications (NeuAc) were detected in 38% of glycoforms at this position. Position 619 displayed reduced glycoform diversity with 19 identified species. The dominant structure HexNAc6Hex3Fuc1 represented 49.56% of total glycosylation (6.59 × 109 intensity), followed by HexNAc5Hex3Fuc1 (13.80%) and HexNAc4Hex5 (8.88%). The position 619 showed minimal NeuAc modification ( <1% prevalence) but contained high-mannose glycans (HexNAc2Hex9 and HexNAc2Hex10) constituting 2.06% of total glycosylation (Supplementary Table S2). Comparative analysis revealed that the LayV G protein exhibits fewer N-glycosylation sites in its head domain compared to the homologous G proteins of NiV and HeV.

The crystal structure of LayV-G

The crystal structure of LayV-G was resolved at a resolution of 3.37 Å (Fig. 2A and D, Table 1). The relevant crystal structure data have been deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) with the accession code 8K80 (deposit date: 2023-07–28). The crystallization conditions consisted of 18% PEG 2000 MME, 200 mM Mg (NO3)2, and 10 mM SrCl2. The head region is comprised of six β-propeller-like domains. Intramolecular disulfide bonds formed by cysteines at positions 285–298, 494–506, 384–502, 568–577, 396–379, and 243–219 contribute to stabilizing the structure of the head region (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. S1).

Due to the high amino acid homology, the G proteins of LayV and MojV exhibited a remarkably similar structure with a Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) of 0.17 Å. Meanwhile, the structure of the LayV G protein differed significantly from NiV G protein, as evidenced by an RMSD value of 2.27 Å for the G protein head regions between LayV and NiV [PDB ID: 2VWD (Bowden et al., 2008b)] (Fig. 2C).

The receptor binding groove is located at the center of the 6β-propeller structure of the NiV G protein. From a structural perspective, the LayV G protein has difficulty in binding to the GH-Loop of ephrin-B2 and ephrin-B3. In the LayV G protein, a significant steric hindrance is caused by the DCTTGE sequence at positions 242–247 (Fig. 2D). In this region, residues K351, L491, and R509 fill part of the cavity. Notably, the AASGE at positions 304–308 forms a more prominent loop that distances the central region (Fig. 2E and F). In the ELISA and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments, there was no binding between the LayV or MojV proteins and ephrin-B2 nor ephrin-B3; however, strong binding signals were observed for the NiV, HeV, and GhV G proteins (Fig. 3A and D, and Supplementary Fig. S2).

We conducted binding experiments using previously reported neutralizing antibodies. The ELISA and SPR assays demonstrated that the neutralizing antibodies m102.4 (anti-HeV/NiV G, Fig. 3B and E), 14F8 (anti-NiV G, Fig. 3C and F), HENV32 and HENV26 (anti- HeV/NiV G, Supplementary Fig. S3), and nAH1.3 (anti-NiV G, Supplementary Fig. S3) exhibited no binding affinity towards LayV and MojV G proteins. The absence of cross-binding could be attributed to the low homology observed in the antibody binding region.

Distinct immunogenicity of LayV-G

To assess the immunogenicity of henipavirus G proteins, a panel comprised of LayV, MojV, Bd NiV, HeV, and Ghana virus (GhV) proteins was prepared. Mice were immunized with 10 μg of each protein along with aluminum and CpG as adjuvants. The henipavirus G-specific antibody responses elicited by these vaccines were detected using ELISA (Fig. 4A–C).

First, the ELISA results revealed that the LayV and MojV G proteins exhibited significant cross-specific antibody responses on days 14, 21, and 42 after immunization (Fig. 4A). Moreover, the MojV G-specific antibody responses were not significantly different from those induced by LayV-G and MojV-G 21 and 42 days after vaccination (Fig. 4A left). However, the Bd NiV, HeV, and GhV G proteins induced weak cross-antibody responses against the LayV and MojV G proteins 42 days after immunization (Fig. 4B). In addition, LayV and MojV G proteins elicited minimal cross-antibody responses against Bd NiV, HeV, and GhV G proteins (Fig. 4C).

A Luminex receptor competition inhibition assay was performed to evaluate the antibody responses elicited by G proteins of henipavirus (Fig. 4D). Bd NiV and HeV G proteins induced strong neutralizing antibody responses in the ephrin-B2 inhibition assay against Bd NiV, My NiV, HeV, and HeV-g2 G. Unsurprisingly, the LayV and MojV proteins failed to elicit functional antibody responses against the two strains of NiV and HeV (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that LayV and MojV are antigenically related but remain largely distinct from NiV and HeV.

Monoclonal antibodies demonstrate robust affinity for G proteins across diverse henipaviruses clades

A total of eight monoclonal antibodies were isolated from mice immunized with the LayV G protein using the hybridoma technique. All antibodies underwent negative screening to ensure that they do not bind to Strep-tag (Supplementary Fig. S4). These antibodies exhibited binding affinity for the LayV and MojV G proteins (Fig. 5A and B), further supporting the similar antigenicity between the LayV and MojV G proteins. Given the low amino acid homology between the LayV G protein and the NiV/HeV G proteins, most of these monoclonal antibodies showed no binding affinity for the NiV and HeV G proteins. The results indicate that LayV G and MojV G exhibit distinct antigenic sites compared with the canonical G protein of henipaviruses. LayV G and MojV G exhibit similar antigenicity and are closely related, with a relatively distant relationship to the other henipavirus genus members.

We further identified two broad-spectrum monoclonal antibodies, 9A22 and A411, through ELISA and SPR assays (Fig. 5C–F), demonstrating binding affinity for NiV-G, HeV-G, and GhV-G. Notably, monoclonal antibody A411 exhibited high affinity for LayV-G (KD =0.042 nM), MojV- G (KD = 0.038 nM), Bd NiV-G (KD = 1.89 nM), HeV-G (KD = 6.08 nM) (Fig. 5F), and GhV-G (KD = 0.43 nM) (Supplementary Fig. S5). These findings further indicate the existence of conserved antigenic sites in the G protein of henipaviruses belonging to different evolutionary clades.

Discussion

A novel henipavirus named Langya henipavirus has been identified in eastern China as a cause of febrile illness in humans. LayV exhibits significant genetic similarity to MojV, another member of the Henipavirus genus. NiV and HeV are the most pathogenic viruses in the Henipavirus genus and cause severe diseases in humans and animals. Nevertheless, the potential risks associated with LayV remain uncertain and warrant the attention of researchers. The G protein plays a crucial role in the immune response against henipaviruses. In this study, the structure of LayV G was resolved at a 3.37 Å resolution. The structure of LayV G is similar to that of MojV G, with an RMSD of 0.17 Å. In contrast, the head domain of LayV G exhibits unique structural features that distinguish it from those of NiV G and HeV G. In particular, the center of the LayV G 6β-propeller structure, which corresponds to the receptor binding groove of NiV G and HeV G, suggests the existence of different receptor binding patterns. The results of the ELISA and SPR experiments also demonstrated that LayV G and MojV G did not bind to the receptors ephrin-B2/B3, nor to reported neutralizing antibodies against NiV and HeV. Moreover, the LayV G protein displays distinct glycosylation patterns, which may have functional implications for viral entry into host cells and immune evasion.

The cryo-EM structures of LayV G, including 8TVI (Wang et al., 2024) and 8VWP (Guo et al., 2024), offer detailed views of the extracellular domain and its oligomeric form. Our 3.37 Å resolution crystal structure offers a more complete model of the head region, resolving all loops that were previously unmodeled in cryo-EM structures due to absent density. Although the 2.75 Å resolution structure 8WJB (Wang et al., 2024) provides high-resolution details of the head domain, our structure shows no Ramachandran outliers and minimal side-chain rotamer outliers, with a slightly better R_free (Supplementary Fig. S1). Together with other recent structural studies, our findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the molecular characteristics of LayV G, aiding the design of effective antibodies and antiviral drugs against this novel henipavirus.

LayV G elicits specific antibody responses against itself and MojV G. LayV G and MojV G exhibit significant similarities and belong to the same evolutionary Henipavirus genus clade. Although neutralizing antibody responses against LayV and MojV were not assessed in this study, the robust antibody responses suggest that LayV G could potentially serve as a protective antigen against LayV and MojV. However, only weak antibody responses against NiV G and HeV G were induced by LayV G immunization. The low degree of amino acid homology and distinct glycosylation patterns compared with NiV G and HeV G may contribute to the distinct immunogenicity of LayV G. Considering these differences in immunogenicity between LayV G and NiV/HeV G, it becomes imperative to develop new vaccines specifically tailored to MojV and LayV. In this study, eight monoclonal antibodies that targeting LayV G were identified. All of the antibodies demonstrated binding affinity for the MojV G protein. Unexpectedly, two additional monoclonal antibodies, 9A22 and A411, showed strong binding affinity for LayV, MojV, GhV, NiV, and HeV G proteins. This suggests that henipavirus Gs belonging to different evolutionary clades exhibit conserved antigenic epitopes. Consequently, identifying key epitope information holds promise for further development of broad-spectrum antibodies, vaccines, and novel inhibitors. These antibodies can play an important role in enhancing the detection of these lethal pathogens.

Conclusions

In this study, the structure of LayV G was resolved at a 3.37 Å resolution, which revealed the distinctive structural characteristics and glycosylation pattern of the head domain of LayV G. This knowledge will prove invaluable for developing efficacious antibodies and antiviral drugs against LayV. Moreover, the immunogenicity of the LayV G protein was evaluated in this study, and the results indicated that it has the potential to be a protective antigen against both LayV and MojV. This provides valuable information for the development of novel henipavirus vaccines. Furthermore, eight monoclonal antibodies against LayV G were identified in this study. Notably, two monoclonal antibodies, 9A22 and A411, were found to have strong binding affinity for LayV, MojV, NiV, HeV, and GhV G proteins. These antibodies could play an important role in improving the detection of these lethal pathogens.

Materials and methods

Antigen expression and purification

The sequences of the extracellular region and the head region of the G proteins from LayV (OM101130), MojV (NC_025352), GhV (NC_025256), HeV (APT69530), HeV-g2 (QYC64605), NiV Bangladeshi (Bd NiV) isolate (AAY43916), and NiV Malaysian (My NiV) isolate (AAK29088) were codon-optimized, and a tPA signal peptide and Strep-II tag were added at the N-terminus. These transgenes were synthesized (General Biosystems Co., Ltd., China) and inserted into pcDNA3.1 (+) plasmids. The plasmid was transfected into Expi-293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for expression. After transfection, the cells were cultured on a shaker at 120 rpm and 37 °C. After five days, the supernatant was centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 15 minutes (min) and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). G proteins were purified using affinity chromatography on a StrepTrap XT affinity column (GE Health Care, USA). Purified proteins were concentrated using an Amicon Ultra-15 (10 kDa) centrifugal filter (Merck Millipore, Germany) and exchanged into phosphate buffer saline (PBS) buffer at pH 7.4. The recombinant G proteins were deglycosylated by incubation with PNGase F at 37 °C for 12 hours (h). Finally, the proteins were characterized and quantified using SDS-PAGE and a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), respectively.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis

Protein samples were analyzed as follows: target protein bands (Coomassie Brilliant Blue-stained) were excised, destained with 50% (v/v) acetonitrile in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (vortexed three times for 10 min each), dehydrated, and subjected to reduction with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) (56 °C for 1 h) and alkylation with 20 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) (room temperature in darkness for 1 h). Sequential digestion was performed using trypsin (Enzyme Source, 1:40 [w/w] enzyme-to-substrate ratio, 37 °C, 16 h) and chymotrypsin (Shengxia Protein, 1:20 [w/w] enzyme-to-substrate ratio, 30 °C, 16 h) in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (pH 8.0). Peptides were extracted with 5% trifluoroacetic acid/50% acetonitrile (v/v), vacuum-dried, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid. LC-MS analysis was conducted using a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Chromatographic separation employed a Reprosil-Pur C18-AQ column (1.9 μm, 170 mm × 150 μm, Dr. Maisch) with a 66-min linear gradient from 4% to 95% acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid) at 0.6 μL/min. Mass spectrometry detection parameters: full scans at 60,000 resolution at 300–1800 m/z. Raw data were processed using Byonic 4.2.4 (Protein Metrics) with dual-enzyme specificity (trypsin and chymotrypsin).

Crystallization and data collection

For crystallization, a tPA signal peptide and a Strep-II tag were added to the N-terminus of the extracellular head domain of the LayV G protein. The recombinant protein was expressed in Expi-293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and purified using a StrepTrap XT affinity column (GE Health Care, USA). LayV G protein crystals were grown at 16 °C in sitting drops over reservoirs containing 18% PEG 2000 MME, 200 mM Mg (NO3)2, and 10 mM SrCl2. The drops were formed by mixing 200 nL of LayV G protein (11 mg/mL) in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 200 nL reservoir solution. The crystals were flash-cooled in liquid nitrogen with 20% glycerol as cryoprotectant.

Diffraction data were collected at a wavelength of 0.979 Å on a BL02U1 beamline at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. The data collection temperature was 100 K. The diffraction data were automatically processed by the Aquarium pipeline 24.

The interface residues were analyzed using Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) software. Molecular graphics were generated using PyMOL. Amino acid interactions were analyzed using the protein contact maps method (Ferruz et al., 2021).

Animal immunization

This study utilized 6–8-week-old female specific pathogen-free (SPF) mice for the vaccine immunization experiments. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee (approval number: 20230236YZA-3R). The mice were obtained from Beijing Viton Lever Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Mice were divided into six groups (n=6 per group): five groups were immunized with the G proteins of LayV, MojV, Bd NiV, HeV, and GhV, respectively, and one group was administered PBS as a negative control. Each mouse in the immunization groups received 10 μg G protein adjuvanted with 200 μg aluminum hydroxide and 20 μg CpG 1826 on days 0 and 21. Mice in the control group received only PBS. All immunizations were performed by intramuscular injection (100 μL total volume) into the hind limb muscles. Blood was collected from the tail vein on days 14, 21, and 42 after primary immunization, and serum was prepared to quantify antibody titers. All collected samples on day 42 (n = 6) were included in ELISA and neutralization analyses. For groups immunized with LayV G protein, MojV G protein, or PBS, samples collected on days 14 and 21 (n = 6 per time point) were also subjected to ELISA.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Proteins were coated onto 96-well ELISA plates at 1 μg/mL in 50 mM carbonate buffer (pH 9.6). The plates were then incubated overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, the plates were washed three times with PBST (0.1% Tween 20 in PBS) and blocked with 100 μL of 2% BSA in PBS for 1 h at 37 °C. Following blocking, the plates were washed with PBST three times. Serum was serially diluted in PBS with 0.2% BSA, with two replicates per dilution, and added to the plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Then, 100 μL of HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:20,000 dilution; Abcam, UK) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 45 min. After removing the secondary antibody, the plates were washed three times with PBST. Next, 100 μL/well of TMB substrate solution (Solarbio Life Sciences, China) was added and incubated for 5 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 μL/well of stop solution (Solarbio Life Sciences, China), and absorbance was measured at 450 nm with 630 nm as the reference wavelength.

Luminex receptor competition inhibition assay

Magnetic beads coated with the henipaviruses G proteins (Bd NiV-G, My NiV-G, HeV-G, and HeV-g2-G) were prepared as previously described (Li et al., 2020). The isolated mouse serum was diluted in PBS containing 1% BSA and added to a 96-well plate, with two replicates per dilution. Then, 20 μL of the diluted magnetic beads was added to each well; in total, 1500 magnetic beads of each type were added to the plate. Next, 10 μL of biotinylated mouse ephrin-B2 Fc chimera (R&D Systems, USA) was added to each well at a concentration of 16.5 ng/mL. The plate was incubated at 800 rpm for 1 h. Then, 10 μL of streptavidin-R-phycoerythrin (SAPE) at a concentration of 12 μg/mL was added to each well, followed by incubation at 800 rpm for 30 min. After incubation, the supernatant was aspirated using a magnetic plate separator (Luminex, USA) and washed three times with PBS containing 1% BSA. Finally, the coated magnetic beads were analyzed using a Luminex MAGPIX instrument (Luminex, USA).

Surface Plasmon Resonance

A Biacore T200 (GE Health Care, USA) was employed to measure the antibody-antigen binding kinetics by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) technique. The antibody was pre-diluted in HBS-EP + buffer (GE Health Care, USA) to a concentration of 5 μg/mL. A Protein A chip operated at a flow rate of 10 μL/min for 60 seconds (s) during the experiment, while G proteins were serially diluted starting at 100 nM and were run at a 30 μL/min flow rate. The association and dissociation times were set to 120 and 900 s, respectively.

Monoclonal antibody generation

The LayV G recombinant protein was purified and subsequently mixed with Freund's complete adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio for immunization. Blood was collected after the final vaccination to determine LayV G protein-specific antibody titers using ELISA. Splenocytes from the mice were mixed with Sp2/0 myeloma cells (ATCC, USA) at a 2:1 ratio and fused by electrofusion. Hybridoma selection and cloning were performed using HAT medium (hypoxanthine, aminopterin, and thymidine) (AtaGenix Laboratories Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). Antibody levels in hybridoma cell culture supernatant were assessed by ELISA, and negative screening was performed using an irrelevant Strep-tagged ACKR1 protein. Antibodies from hybridoma supernatants were purified using a Protein A HP column (Cytiva, USA). The purified antibodies were concentrated and buffer-exchanged into PBS (pH 7.4) using an Amicon Ultra-15 (30 kDa) centrifugal filter (Merck Millipore, Germany). Variable regions of the antibodies were sequenced by 5′ RACE and analyzed using the IMGT/V-QUEST database.

The variable regions of m102.4 (Zhu et al., 2006), 14F8 (a NiV neutralizing antibodies obtained previously), HENV32, HENV26 (Dong et al., 2020), nAH1.3 (Wang et al., 2022a), 9A22, and A411 were cloned into a human IgG1 antibody backbone in the pcDNA3.1 (+) plasmid. The chimeric antibodies were expressed in the Expi293F expression system and purified using a Protein A affinity column (GE Health Care, USA).
