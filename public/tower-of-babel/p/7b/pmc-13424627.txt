Symptomatic Inlet Patch as a Cause of Chronic Cough and Dysphagia: A Case Report
Authors: Elena Aznar Ruiz, Ana Zataraín Vallés, Marta Aparicio Cabezudo, Laura García Pravia, Marina Arroyo Cadenas, Constanza Ciriza de los Ríos
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

The inlet patch or heterotopic gastric mucosa of the proximal esophagus is a benign lesion characterized by the presence of ectopic gastric tissue, likely of embryological origin.1 Its prevalence ranges between 1.1% and 10%,2 and it is usually asymptomatic, being incidentally detected during gastroscopy. In symptomatic cases, it may cause globus sensation, dysphagia, chronic cough, or laryngopharyngeal reflux, especially when acid secretion is present. 1 Rarely, it is associated with serious complications such as ulcers, fistulas, or adenocarcinoma. When a typical endoscopic appearance is observed, histological confirmation and further surveillance are not required.3

Published studies have emphasized that acid-related mechanisms can play a significant role in chronic cough and other extraesophageal symptoms, even in the absence of pathological distal reflux. This supports the hypothesis that localized acid exposure, such as that produced by a heterotopic gastric inlet patch, may elicit chronic laryngopharyngeal symptoms through direct mucosal irritation or by triggering a cough reflex hypersensitivity.4

CASE REPORT

A 53-year-old male presented with chronic cough and intermittent high dysphagia for solids, with 2 episodes of food impaction that resolved spontaneously. He denied heartburn, chest pain, regurgitation, or associated constitutional symptoms. An upper endoscopy revealed a gastric mucosal island (inlet patch) in the upper third of the esophagus (Fig. 1), with no other abnormalities. The work-up was completed with high-resolution esophageal manometry, which showed hypotonia of the lower esophageal sphincter with transient relaxations and signs of sliding, findings that may favor gastroesophageal reflux. Esophageal body motility was normal. Baseline dual-channel esophageal pH-metry without proton pump inhibitors (Fig. 2) showed a markedly elevated acid exposure time (AET) at the proximal level (total AET, 38.1%), without a corresponding increase in distal acid exposure (total AET, 0.4%). Therefore, gastroesophageal reflux is within physiological limits. The localized acid exposure observed in the proximal esophagus may be directly attributable to the heterotopic gastric mucosa identified on endoscopy. Correct probe placement was confirmed by chest X-ray, with one sensor located in the distal esophagus and the other in the proximal esophagus.

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy showing a well-defined, oval, salmon-colored lesion (black arrows) approximately 3 cm in its longest axis is identified in the proximal esophagus; compatible with heterotopic gastric mucosa or inlet patch.

Baseline dual-channel esophageal pH-metry performed without proton pump inhibitors shows absence of pathological acid gastroesophageal reflux in the distal sensor, with a total acid exposure time (pH < 4.0) of 0.4% (normal < 4.5%). The proximal channel shows a markedly elevated acid exposure time of 38.1% (normal < 0.9%).

DISCUSSION

The findings of this clinical case support the hypothesis that heterotopic gastric mucosa may be clinically relevant in symptomatic patients. The 24-hour esophageal pH-metry showed episodes of acid exposure in the proximal esophagus, corresponding to the location of the inlet patch. In this case, the presence of symptoms such as chronic cough and dysphagia, in the absence of other abnormal test results, suggests that the inlet patch may play an active symptomatic role, likely through acid secretion by parietal cells in the heterotopic mucosa. In clinical practice, these findings may influence treatment decisions. In selected patients, treatment of symptomatic inlet patch with proton pump inhibitors, mucosal protectants such as sucralfate, and/or alginate-based preparations has been proposed, or even endoscopic ablation with argon plasma.2

Although the overall efficacy of acid suppression in chronic cough remains controversial, its selective success in cases like this reinforces the pathogenic role of focal acid secretion from heterotopic gastric mucosa.5

In our case, treatment with lansoprazole 30 mg once daily led to a partial but clinically significant improvement in the patient’s dysphagia and chronic cough. At one-year follow-up, this partial improvement persisted, indicating sustained symptomatic benefit despite the absence of complete resolution.

This clinical improvement reinforces the need to consider the inlet patch in the differential diagnosis of patients with atypical gastroesophageal reflux symptoms or persistent otolaryngological manifestations, especially when conventional studies are negative.
