Letter to the Editor regarding “Association Between Diabetes Management Behaviors and State-Level Social Vulnerability: A Cross-sectional Study”
Authors: Schawanya Kaewpitoon Rattanapitoon, Natnapa Heebkaew Padchasuwan, Nav La, Nathkapach Kaewpitoon Rattanapitoon
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the recent study by Winberg et al. [1], which investigated the association between state-level social vulnerability index (SVI) and diabetes management behaviours. The authors elegantly combined large-scale Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) SVI datasets, highlighting complex gradients of vulnerability affecting diabetes education, self-monitoring and physician visits. This work contributes meaningfully to understanding the structural determinants of type 2 diabetes management in the USA.

However, several methodological nuances and conceptual implications merit further discussion. The authors observed that individuals in states with moderate vulnerability had higher odds of diabetes education (odds ratio [OR] = 1.87, p < 0.01), while those in highest vulnerability states were more likely to see a doctor (OR = 1.48, p < 0.01) but not necessarily engage in self-monitoring. This diverges from prior studies, such as Kurani et al. [2], who demonstrated reduced quality of diabetes care in socio-economically deprived areas, and Laffey et al. [3], who found lower adherence to glucose testing in high deprivation contexts. Winberg et al. suggest possible Medicaid-mediated programmatic targeting in high-SVI states. Future analyses incorporating Medicaid expansion data (e.g. Lee et al. [4]) could clarify whether insurance coverage modulates these unexpected associations.

Moreover, Zhou et al. [5] reported that state-level vulnerability scores were associated with differences in lifestyle modification behaviours, suggesting that structural factors influence not only healthcare utilisation but also patient self-management. Similarly, Berkowitz et al. [6] identified an elevated risk of hypoglycaemia among socially vulnerable adults with diabetes, highlighting the clinical consequences of these disparities. These results contrast with Winberg et al.’s findings, where higher vulnerability was not consistently associated with poorer self-management behaviours. One possible explanation is variation in the availability of medical services, insurance coverage (including Medicaid expansion status) and differences in race, gender and demographic profiles across states, which may shape both access to care and engagement in self-management. Together, these findings support the need to interpret Winberg et al.’s behavioural outcomes within a broader biopsychosocial framework.

The authors appropriately highlight critical differences between SVI and the area deprivation index (ADI). While SVI includes race and minority status, ADI does not, raising debates over whether inclusion of race as a vulnerability metric conflates structural determinants with demographic categorisation. Rollings et al. [7, 8] recently argued that index selection significantly alters vulnerability mapping and downstream policy recommendations. Therefore, direct model comparisons using both indices could reveal whether observed behavioural associations remain robust across conceptual frameworks of deprivation.

The reported differences, while statistically significant, were relatively small in absolute terms for some behaviours (e.g. self-monitoring rates). The authors rightly caution against over-interpretation. To complement odds ratios, future studies could report number needed to treat (NNT) or population attributable fractions (PAF) to contextualise findings within intervention prioritisation.

As SVI was aggregated to the state level owing to BRFSS limitations, substantial within-state heterogeneity may remain unobserved. Previous fine-grained analyses (e.g. Spielman et al. [9]) demonstrate that census tract-level vulnerability often differs from state-level averages, potentially masking local disparities critical for targeted interventions.

Building on these findings, I propose three priority avenues:Causal inference models such as instrumental variable analysis using Medicaid expansion or natural experiments to ascertain directionality between social vulnerability and management behavioursIntegration with biological outcomes, e.g. glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) trajectories or hospitalisation rates, to assess whether behavioural differences translate into glycaemic or complication benefitsEvaluation of culturally tailored education interventions in high-SVI communities, given that education disparities persisted despite higher physician visit rates

In conclusion, Winberg et al. provide a valuable addition to the literature on social determinants and diabetes care. By addressing the above conceptual and methodological considerations, future research can advance from association to actionable causality, refining structural intervention strategies to equitably improve diabetes outcomes.
