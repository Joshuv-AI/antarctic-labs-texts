Adequacy of Single Random Skin Biopsy With Subcutaneous Sampling for the Diagnosis of Intravascular Lymphoma
Authors: Phitsinee Purngpiputtrakul, Panitta Sitthinamsuwan, Sanya Sukpanichnant, Penvadee Pattanaprichakul, Manasmon Chairatchaneeboon, Yingyong Chinthammitr, Pochamana Phisalprapa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRandom skin biopsy (RSB) is a valuable method for diagnosis of intravascular lymphoma (IVL). Previous studies have performed RSB at multiple locations. This study aimed to evaluate the adequacy of a single RSB site and the appropriate depth of the biopsies for diagnosing IVL.MethodsThe study included 209 patients who underwent RSB for IVL diagnosis. Sixty‐six hematoxylin and eosin slides from 33 patients with a definitive diagnosis of IVL from RSB were analyzed for RSB site, blood vessel types, specimen depth, and tissue area containing detectable lymphoma cells.ResultsLymphoma cells were primarily detected in the capillaries of subcutaneous tissue (89.4%). The mean depth, tissue area of the specimens, and depth of the subcutaneous tissue were 12.7 mm, 151.2 mm2, and 8 mm, respectively. There was no statistically significant difference in the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, or area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for lymphoma cell detection between RSBs obtained from the abdomen, thigh, or both locations.ConclusionsA single RSB location, either at the abdomen or thigh, is adequate for diagnosing IVL in most patients under appropriate conditions. Biopsied specimens should have adequate subcutaneous fat to increase the likelihood of detecting lymphoma cells.Keywords: cost, intravascular lymphoma, location, random skin biopsy, site, working time

Introduction

Intravascular lymphoma (IVL) is a rare extranodal lymphoma characterized by lymphoma cells primarily occupying the lumina of capillaries and small blood vessels [1, 2, 3, 4]. Based on clinical manifestations, the disease is classified into classic, hemophagocytic, and cutaneous subtypes [5]. The classic subtype presents with fever of unknown origin or organ‐specific symptoms, while the hemophagocytic subtype is associated with hemophagocytic syndrome [2, 5]. The cutaneous subtype exhibits exclusive skin involvement without affecting other organs [6]. It has a better prognosis and treatment outcome than those resulting from the systemic involvement of the classic and hemophagocytic subtypes [6, 7]. In addition to systemic symptoms of the classic subtype, its cutaneous presentation was found in 20% [8]. According to the WHO classification of skin tumors, 2023, the majority of IVL is intravascular B‐cell lymphoma. In a rare circumstance, neoplastic cells in IVL could exhibit T‐cell or NK/T‐cell phenotypes [9, 10].

A definitive diagnosis of IVL necessitates a tissue biopsy to confirm that lymphoma cells are localized exclusively within the vascular lumen [9]. Diagnostic delays often result in fatal outcomes [1, 11]. Biopsies of vital organs such as the lung, spleen, or brain are invasive and may be associated with significant morbidity [12]. Additionally, lymphoma cells are unlikely to appear in peripheral blood smears [2, 7, 13]. The lymphoma cells in IVL are rarely detected in the peripheral bloodstream because they lack receptors for extravasation of lymphocytes and migration of lymphocytes across blood vessels [2, 7]. Consequently, random skin biopsy (RSB) has emerged as a simple, minimally invasive, and appropriate procedure for diagnosis of IVL [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. In rare cases, muscle biopsy is recommended to reveal lymphoma cells within the endomysial and perimysial capillaries [20].

The benefit of RSB on normal‐appearing skin for early IVL diagnosis has been recognized in several reports [17, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Currently, RSB is considered a standard diagnostic procedure for patients with suspected IVL [15, 26, 27]. Specimens are commonly taken from the upper arm, abdomen, and thigh [16, 25, 28, 29]. Some authors recommend biopsying normal‐appearing skin from at least three locations to increase the likelihood of detecting lymphoma cells [17, 30, 31]. Nevertheless, our previous report demonstrated that two‐site skin biopsies, specifically from the abdomen and thigh, were effective for diagnosis of IVL. Our prior study also revealed that lymphoma cells were detectable in specimens from both locations in patients with a definitive IVL diagnosis [32].

Regarding the finding in our previous study, we hypothesized that a single site of RSB might be sufficient for diagnosis of IVL when an appropriate amount of subcutaneous adipose tissue is obtained in the biopsy specimen. Additionally, a few studies have focused on the number of biopsy locations required for diagnosis of IVL. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the sufficiency of a single site of RSB for diagnosis of IVL by comparing the possibility of tumor cell detection between single‐site and two‐site skin biopsy specimens. Furthermore, the medical expenses used for pathological diagnosis of IVL were compared between both numbers of biopsy specimens.

Materials and Methods

This research received ethical approval from the Siriraj Institutional Review Board, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand (certificate of approval number Si 307/2022). Patient data were kept confidential in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All specimens from patients who underwent RSB for IVL diagnosis between January 2008 and January 2022 were included in the study. RSBs were taken from the non‐lesional areas of the abdomen, thigh, upper arm, and chest, with one to six locations used per patient. All biopsy specimens were deemed appropriate because they optimally contained the epidermis, dermis, and subcutaneous fat.

All RSB specimens, comprising slides immunostained for CD3 and CD20 and slides stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), were meticulously examined. The review was conducted by an experienced dermatopathologist (P.S.) and a pathology trainee (P.P.). Patients were categorized into two groups: the positive IVL group, with detectable lymphoma cells in the vascular lumen, and the negative IVL group, without detectable lymphoma cells.

The types of blood vessels occupied by lymphoma cells were capillaries in the papillary dermis, reticular dermis, peri‐eccrine area, and subcutaneous fat; arterioles; and medium‐sized blood vessels in the skin. For the positive IVL group, the tissue area and depth of the biopsy specimen in individual sections were recorded. We measured the depth of the skin biopsy from keratin to the deepest part of the subcutaneous fat and the depth of subcutaneous fat thickness. The 2‐dimensional areas of the skin biopsy were also calculated. Some IVL patients had no detectable lymphoma cells in the RSB but had lymphoma cells in biopsy specimens from other organs.

From an economic perspective, the hospital expenses for IVL diagnosis using RSB were calculated in 2025 in the United States dollar (USD). The cost per case included the RSB procedure, standard pathology laboratory processing, and IHC staining fees for CD3 and CD20. Regarding workload, the working hours spent by dermatologists, pathology laboratory staff, and dermatopathologists on the diagnosis were calculated.

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed using PASW Statistics, version 18 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages, while numerical data are reported as the means and standard deviations. The chi‐square test was used to compare the number of histological sections with detectable lymphoma cells between different RSB locations. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. The chi‐square test was utilized to compare these parameters between RSB sites. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for each RSB site was also generated using Stata statistical software, release 17 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). A p value less than 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance.

Results

A total of 209 patients were enrolled in the study, and all RSBs were performed by dermatologists. In all, 426 RSB specimens were obtained from these patients. The distribution of RSB specimens per patient included one specimen from 11 patients, two from 189 patients, three from five patients, four from one patient, and six from three patients. The most common RSB sites were the abdomen and thigh. Only three patients underwent RSB at the upper arm, and another three patients had RSB at the chest.

IVL was definitively diagnosed in 35 out of the 209 patients (16.7%). Of these 35 patients, 33 patients had detectable lymphoma cells in the 66 RSB specimens. In contrast, the remaining two patients had detectable tumor cells in specimens from other organs (one from a bone marrow biopsy and the other from a brain biopsy). Both patients underwent biopsies at two sites, including the abdomen and thigh. Consequently, among the 176 patients without detectable lymphoma cells in the RSB specimens, only 2 were later proven to have IVL through biopsies of other sites, resulting in a false‐negative rate of 1.1% (2 out of 176 patients). In addition, all suspected IVL cases, who underwent an extra number of biopsies up to six pieces, had no IVL.

Of the 35 patients diagnosed with IVL, 12 were male (34.3%) and 23 were female (65.7%), with a mean age of 68 years (range 45–79). Fever of unknown origin was the presenting symptom in 28 patients (80%). Neurological deficits (paraplegia, status epilepticus, transverse myelitis, conus medullaris syndrome, cauda equina syndrome, encephalitis, and behavioral changes) were noted in 8 of the 35 patients (23%), while 1 patient (3%) presented with eyelid cellulitis. There was no cutaneous variant or hemophagocytic variant in our study.

Immunophenotypic analysis revealed a mature B‐cell phenotype in 34 patients (97%) and an NK/T‐cell phenotype in 1 patient (3%). Of the 31 patients who underwent bone marrow biopsy, 19 (61%) exhibited detectable lymphoma cells within vascular lumens. The overall process from incisional biopsy to diagnostic sign‐out took approximately 70 min and 40 s for a single RSB site and 133 min for two RSB sites. Retrospective data over 14 years revealed that 426 specimens were collected from patients suspected of having IVL, averaging 30 biopsy specimens annually. Calculating the full‐time equivalent (FTE) based on this workload with 1 FTE equal to 35 h per week showed that diagnosing IVL required approximately 0.0015 FTE. In other words, by calculating the different times consumed between a single and two RSB specimens, 15 h and 7 min could be saved in a year.

Discussion

RSB should be considered for patients with clinical suspicion of IVL or those with a high IVL probability score [33]. There is no consensus in the literature regarding the optimal number and location of RSB for diagnosis of IVL [27, 34, 35]. Recommended biopsy sites for RSB specimens include the upper arm, abdomen, and thigh [21, 22, 32, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. Some authors suggest that normal skin should be biopsied from at least three sites with adequate subcutaneous tissue inclusion or a minimum depth of 4 mm [30, 31]. Our previous study revealed lymphoma cells in RSB specimens obtained from both the abdomen and thigh in all IVL patients, indicating that one or two biopsy sites may be sufficient if the specimens include an optimal amount of subcutaneous tissue [32]. Furthermore, our current study demonstrated that the sensitivity, specificity, and AuROC for lymphoma cell detection did not significantly differ among RSB specimens from the abdomen, thigh, or both locations. These findings suggest that RSB at a single site is likely adequate for detecting lymphoma cells in most IVL patients under appropriate conditions.

To determine the optimal site for RSB, our study results (Table 2) demonstrated a greater prevalence of detectable lymphoma cells within the capillaries of subcutaneous fat than within other cutaneous blood vessels. Matsue et al. also demonstrated that lymphoma cells were visible in the subcutaneous tissue capillaries of all IVL patients with lymphoma cells in the dermal capillaries, but not vice versa [17]. Therefore, subcutaneous capillaries were identified as the most common site for detectable lymphoma cells, which is consistent with our previous findings [32]. These results suggested that obtaining a larger amount of subcutaneous tissue in RSB specimens increases the likelihood of detecting lymphoma cells in vascular lumens. Previous studies reported that skin at the abdomen was thicker (2.35 vs. 1.97 mm) [40] and had a greater subcutaneous tissue depth (15.73 vs. 7.92 mm) [41] than the skin at the thigh. Therefore, RSB at the abdomen yields more subcutaneous fatty tissue. Additionally, dermatologists are accustomed to performing RSB at the abdomen for other conditions, such as identifying amyloid deposition in cutaneous amyloidosis [42]. Even though the abdomen is more preferable, based on the present study, there was no statistically significant difference in lymphoma cell detection between RSB for the diagnosis of IVL from the abdomen and thigh.

Previous studies reported RSB sensitivity (50%–77.8%), specificity (98.7%–100%), PPV (96.6%–100%), and NPV (90.6%) [31, 38, 43, 44]. Our study showed higher sensitivity (90.3%–94.3%) but consistent specificity, PPV, and NPV. The increased sensitivity in our study can be attributed to the use of incisional biopsies with greater depth and a larger tissue area in the specimens. We hypothesize that the larger size of the biopsied tissue in two dimensions improves the sensitivity of lymphoma cell detection. Rozenbaum et al. reported a sensitivity of 50% for RSB with an overall median biopsy depth of 5 mm [31]. In contrast, our study reported a depth of 12.7 mm and a sensitivity of 90.3%–94.3%. Furthermore, Rozenbaum and coauthors found that individuals with histopathological evidence of disease had deeper biopsy samples than those with negative skin biopsies (6 mm vs. 3 mm, respectively).

One indication for RSB is excisional biopsy of the abdominal fat pad for patients with suspected systemic amyloidosis, where amyloid deposits in the deeper dermis and subcutaneous fat mirror the detectable lymphoma cells in subcutaneous capillaries in IVL. Wu and colleagues investigated the use of excisional biopsies of the abdominal fat pad for diagnosing systemic amyloidosis and found that diagnostic sensitivity correlated with biopsy depth. The sensitivity was highest (100%) for specimens deeper than 10 mm and lowest (24%) for specimens shallower than 10 mm [45]. Several studies have demonstrated that both the size and depth of biopsy specimens impact disease detection rates. Gracia et al. reported that the sensitivity for abdominal fat pad biopsies in diagnosing systemic amyloidosis was 100% for specimens larger than 700 mm3; however, the value fell to 50% for smaller specimens [42]. Enzan et al. reported that the median depth of IVL lesions was 3.64 mm from the skin surface [46]. Standard 4 mm punch biopsies usually reach 4–5 mm in depth [47], which may be insufficient for visualizing lymphoma cells in vascular lumens [48, 49]. In the present study, we used incisional biopsy with a large amount of fat tissue for RSB; however, if punch biopsy is generally applied in other centers, the largest size of punch equipment should be employed. Although incisional biopsies can obtain more tissue, they carry a risk of excessive bleeding, especially in IVL patients with thrombocytopenia or coagulation defects. To minimize hemorrhagic complications, punch biopsies should be the primary procedure for IVL patients with thrombocytopenia, with repeat RSB at different sites considered if the initial results are negative [39, 50]. The tissue area obtained by RSB is crucial for facilitating the diagnosis of IVL. Biopsies should be sufficiently deep to encompass more vascular structures within the subcutaneous tissue [21, 51, 52]. In our study, our mean total tissue depth for positive IVL patients was 12.7 mm; however, in practical surgical technique, we recommended incisional RSB to ensure that an adequate amount of subcutaneous tissue was obtained.

The diagnosis of IVL can be enhanced by utilizing an expanded IHC panel. According to the RSB protocol used in our hospital, CD3 and CD20 IHC staining is routinely performed. Most IVL patients exhibit a mature B‐cell immunophenotype, often characterized by strong and persistent CD20 expression [2]. In our study, 4 out of 33 IVL patients (12%) had detectable lymphoma cells solely on CD20‐stained slides, and no lymphoma cells were detected on H&E‐stained slides. Additionally, another 4 out of 33 IVL patients (12%) required supplementary IHC staining for CD79a and PAX5 due to faint CD20 staining of lymphoma cells, as previously reported [12]. We also identified a patient with NK/T‐cell IVL that exhibited cytoplasmic CD3 staining. Thus, we recommend including CD3 and CD20 in the IHC panel for all RSB specimens to enhance the detection rate of lymphoma cells. Additional IHC markers should be utilized to confirm the precise immunophenotype of lymphoma cells, as demonstrated in our study.

From an economic perspective, reducing the number of RSB specimens can lower medical expenses and decrease the workload for personnel involved in diagnosing IVL. Based on our cost and working time analysis, eliminating one RSB specimen can save 81 USD and 62.6 min. In our hospital, which treats approximately 15 suspected IVL patients annually, this translates to annual savings of 1215 USD and 15.7 h. The expense budget for each biopsy site is not much; however, time‐consuming tasks might be added to the numbers of sample sites. Furthermore, in IVL patients with thrombocytopenia, platelet dysfunction, or coagulopathy potentially caused by bone marrow invasion or hemophagocytic syndrome, the number of RSB sites may need to be limited [12, 27]. Maekawa and associates reported a fatal case of profound bleeding and hemorrhagic shock following incisional RSB in an IVL patient [50]. Therefore, platelet replacement and coagulopathy correction are mandatory before incisional RSB. In patients with bleeding disorders, biopsy depth should be limited to shallow subcutaneous tissue, or a punch biopsy with sufficient subcutaneous fat should be considered to minimize hemorrhagic complications [46]. In patients with low hemorrhagic risk, deep incisional biopsy is recommended in the abdomen or thigh. If the RBS reveals a negative result, but IVL is still highly concerned, a biopsy of other involved organs might be helpful, as shown in our two patients. They had negative IVL in skin biopsies but had visible lymphoma cells in the bone marrow and brain biopsies, respectively. In addition to reducing the incidence of procedure‐related complications, considering a single site for RSB can lower medical expenses and minimize unnecessary working hours, making it a pragmatic approach for managing IVL patients. However, if there is no limitation regarding bleeding, cost, or scar concern, two‐site biopsies can be performed.

The limitations of the study should be elucidated. First, our results, which were exclusively based on the incisional biopsy procedure, might lack generalizability or might not be applicable in the medical centers utilizing punch biopsy as a routine procedure for obtaining the RSB specimens. Second, the data were acquired from the analysis of biopsy specimens from the abdomen and thigh, which might not be practical to use in a skin biopsy of the arm or chest. A prospective study in a variety of practices would be valuable to confirm the adequacy of single RSB in the diagnosis of IVL.

Conclusions

Even though a single RSB for diagnosis of IVL renders high sensitivity and high specificity, false negativity could be encountered. In particular cases with highly suspected IVL while showing negative RSB, an additional biopsy of the involved organ is subsequently recommended. By using incisional biopsy, a single site of RSB is adequate for diagnosing IVL in most patients under proper conditions when the depth and area of the biopsied tissue are appropriately obtained. Importantly, subcutaneous tissue must be included, as lymphoma cells are commonly found in the lumens of subcutaneous capillaries. When selecting a single RSB location, abdomen or thigh, an appropriate amount of subcutaneous tissue in the biopsy specimen should be obtained. The IHC panel should routinely include CD3 and CD20 for all RSB specimens. If the IHC staining results are inconclusive, additional markers should be used to confirm the immunophenotype of the lymphoma cells. Reducing the number of RSBs can lower medical costs and working hours, potentially decreasing biopsy‐associated complications.
