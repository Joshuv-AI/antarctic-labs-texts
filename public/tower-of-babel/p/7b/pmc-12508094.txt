Basalt fiber and polycarboxylate superplasticizer effects on freeze-thaw durability of cement-fly ash stabilized aeolian sand gravel
Authors: Jie Liu, Yang Zhao, Chao Pu, Shiyu Zhu, Junjie Zhou, Bin Wang, Chunsheng Zhu, Yan Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe durability of cement–fly ash stabilized aeolian sand gravel base (CFSAGB) materials was enhanced using basalt fiber (B) and polycarboxylate superplasticizer (P). Four BP-CFSAGB mixtures with varying B–P dosages were prepared. Mechanical properties were evaluated after freeze–thaw cycling in multiple salt environments. Strength was significantly improved. The optimal mix showed 32.2% higher compressive strength and 42.9% higher tensile strength than the control after 10 freeze–thaw cycles in a 0.5% NaCl–5% Na2SO4 solution. Deterioration was found to be a physicochemical process dominated by physical damage. Basalt fibers alleviated internal stresses through crack bridging. The superplasticizer improved particle dispersion and stabilized hydration products. Although acting independently, their effects proved complementary. Microstructural analysis via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the formation of denser matrices and more stable hydration phases in the modified mixtures. These findings demonstrate that the combined use of B and P can significantly improve the freeze–thaw resistance of cement-stabilized granular base layers. The results provide mechanistic insight and technical support for the design of durable pavement base courses in salt-affected regions.Keywords: Aeolian sand, Fly ash, Basalt fiber, Polycarboxylate superplasticizer, OptimizationSubject terms: Materials science, Civil engineering

Introduction

Desertification is a prominent manifestation of land degradation, presenting a significant challenge to global ecological development in the modern era. The construction of highways through desert terrain is fraught with unique geological challenges, notably, the scarcity of construction materials, significantly increasing the cost of such infrastructure1–3. To mitigate the energy expenditure associated with highway construction in desert regions, there is a burgeoning academic interest in leveraging the abundant local aeolian sand-a resource typically overlooked-to create semirigid base materials as alternatives to conventional engineering sand. Aeolian sand, formed by the action of wind and precipitation, is prevalent in northwestern China and is characterized by its loose structure, lack of fine and cohesive particles, negligible viscosity, and poor gradation4–6. Given that aeolian sand does not conform to the standards required for highway construction, numerous researchers have conducted extensive studies on its mechanical properties and long-term stability7–11. These studies have yielded a wealth of information that not only offers a valuable resource for selecting construction materials in desert environments but also lays a solid foundation for future research endeavors.

To ensure widespread usage of aeolian sand, considerable research has been carried out on improved road performance using aeolian sand by scholars at home and abroad. For example, Khan12first studied the engineering characteristics of aeolian sand in Libya’s Shahara Desert and determined the feasibility of its use as a road construction material in arid areas. Netterberg et al.13 observed a test section of the aeolian sand grass-roots level in South Africa and reported that the road structure was able to maintain stability. Via unconfined compressive strength tests and water stability tests, Guo et al.14 revealed that cement-stabilized gravel sand has good water stability and that the 7 days unconfined compressive strength of cement-stabilized gravel sand with 11% cement doping met the conditions of high-grade highway construction. Based on an indoor test, Song et al.15 verified the mechanical strength, deformation, and durability of a cement-stabilized crushed stone base layer with different dosages of sand. The results showed that the larger the dosage of sand was, the greater the decrease in performance. Moreover, the maximum dosage of sand was recommended to be 6% by accounting for the temperature shrinkage performance, dry shrinkage performance, scouring resistance, and fatigue durability of cement-stabilized crushed stone mixes.

The above research results show that cement-modified aeolian sand exhibits good road performance, but a cement dosage that is too high can lead to early cracking and other diseases in the pavement structural layer. Therefore, many scholars worldwide have conducted in-depth research on the modification of sand. For instance, Yan et al.16 pointed out that, with increasing basalt fiber dosage, the freezing resistance of cement-cured sand first increases and then decreases, and its freezing resistance is optimal when the fiber dosage is 0.5%. Jiang et al.17 optimized the water-cement ratio of the steel-fiber all-ductile sand high-performance concrete by improving model, water reducing agent and steel fiber admixture, and carried out the proportion design. The results reveal that when the water glue ratio is 19%, the water reducing agent is 1%, and the length of steel fiber is 12 mm, its mechanical properties, anti-chlorine ion erosion performance, shrinkage resistance and other comprehensive performance are satisfactory. Zhang et al.18, sodium hydroxide and water glass were chosen as the alkali exciters, and slag was selected as the cementitious material. These researchers showed that the hydration products, produced by the alkali excitation of slag, fill the internal voids of the aeolian sand material, improve the densification of aeolian sand material, and inhibit the expansion of microcracks within the aeolian sand material. In addition, rubber particles increased their lateral deformation and toughness, and the roadbed splitting tensile and compressive properties of the aeolian sand material slightly improved. Li et al.19 investigated the modification of waste fibers to prepare the curing agent used to solidify aeolian sand. When the dosage is 0.6–1.0%, the compressive strength, frost resistance, anti-aging properties of the sand specimens are greater than those of the national standard, and there is no pollution. Xie et al.20 confirmed that with increasing basalt fiber dosage, the impact resistance of sandy concrete first increases and then decreases. Although fiber-reinforced cement-stabilized sand has been widely studied, limited research has explored the combined use of fibers with water reducers or composite water-reducing agents. To address reflective cracking and water shortages in arid desert regions, this study proposes two key innovations: (1) using 100% aeolian sand to replace fine aggregates and adding basalt fibers to enhance tensile bonding, thereby reducing shrinkage-induced cracking and maintenance costs; (2) incorporating water-reducing agents to lower optimal moisture content and porosity, improving base durability under desert conditions.

To address the above research problems, indoor simulation tests of the salt freeze-thaw cycle were carried out on different mixed ratios of BP-CFSAGB materials. In addition, the synergistic mechanism of the basalt fiber-polycarboxylate superplasticizer on the performance of the CFSAGB material was investigated via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) measurements to elucidate the effect of the superplasticizer on the durability of the CFSAGB material and to reveal the mechanism of deterioration of the material under the action of salt freezing and thawing cycles.

Test methods

Overall process

To study the durability performance of cement fly ash-stabilized loess sand and crushed stone base mixtures with basalt fibers and polycarboxylate superplasticizer, a salt freeze-thaw cycle test was used. SEM and EDS technologies were used to describe the increase in strength and the deterioration mechanism at the microscopic level. Fig. 4 shows the technology roadmap of this work.

Overall process of test.

Test scheme

Mechanical properties test

In accordance with the “Highway Engineering Inorganic Binder Stabilized Material Testing Code” (JTG 3432-2024), cylindrical specimens with dimensions of φ150 mm × h150 mm are utilized in the tests. These specimens are prepared based on the optimal moisture content and maximum dry density per the mix ratio and subsequently are cured for a specified curing age. On the day before the standard curing ends, the specimens are soaked in water for 24 h. The unconfined compressive strength and splitting tensile strength tests are conducted with a HUT305A computer-controlled electrohydraulic servo universal testing machine at a loading rate of 1 mm/min. The formulas for the unconfined compressive strength and splitting tensile strength tests are provided in Eqs. (1), (2), and (3).

RC represents the unconfined compressive strength of the specimen, MPa; P represents the maximum pressure when the specimen was destroyed, N; A represents the cross-sectional area of the specimen, mm2; and D represents the diameter of the specimen, mm.

RS represents the splitting strength of the specimen, MPa; d represents the diameter of the specimen, mm; a represents the width of the strip, mm; α represents the center of the circle corresponding to the half bar width, °; P represents the maximum pressure at the failure of the specimen, N; and h represents the height of the specimen after immersion, mm.

Salt freeze–thaw cycles test

To ensure the precision of the experimental outcomes, the temperature of the salt freeze-thaw cycles is determined based on the subgrade temperatures collected by the monitoring stations along the experimental section of the road. The freeze–thaw cycle test is ultimately set with a cycle duration of 24 h, including a low-temperature freezing phase of 16 h and a thawing phase of 8 h. The freezing temperature for the specimens during the freeze-thaw resistance test is − 18 °C, and the thawing temperature is 20 °C. Soil samples are collected from both sides of the experimental section, and these samples are analyzed via Varian Corporation’s VISTA-PRO model, a full-spectrum direct-reading inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer. The soil samples are tested for eight major ions, pH, and soluble salt content. The results of these analyses are presented in Table 7.

The test results of the eight major ions, pH, and soluble salt content in the soil, as shown in Table 7, revealed that the content of soluble salts in the soil on both sides of the experimental section of the road ranges from approximately 25.826–27.240%. These soluble salts exhibit alkalinity when dissolved in water. The content of sulfate ions (SO42−) in the soil is estimated to be between 16.56% and 18.07%, the content of chloride ions (Cl−) is approximately 0.37–0.51%, and the content of sodium ions (Na+) is approximately 5.64–6.24%. Consequently, it can be deduced that the soluble salts contain sodium chloride (NaCl) and sodium sulfate (Na2SO4). Assuming that all chloride ions are in the form of sodium chloride and that the remaining sodium ions are in the form of sodium sulfate, the content of NaCl is calculated to be between 0.37% and 0.51%, and the content of Na2SO4 is estimated to be between 2.565% and 2.935%. Accordingly, the salt solution immersion environment in a salt freeze–thaw context includes clear water, a 0.3% NaCl solution, a 0.5% NaCl solution, a 3% Na2SO4 solution, a 5% Na2SO4 solution, a 0.3% NaCl − 3% Na2SO4 solution, and a 0.5% NaCl − 5% Na2SO4 solution.

Following the “Highway Engineering Inorganic Binder Stabilized Material Testing Code” (JTG 3432 -2024), specifically the T0858-2009 standard, the durability of the mixture versus salt freeze-thaw cycles is investigated. Cylindrical specimens with dimensions of φ150 mm × h150 mm are obtained via the static compaction method, with a standard curing period of 28 days. Various mix proportions are selected for the salt freeze–thaw cycle test, with six specimens prepared for each mix proportion and subjected to 5 and 10 cycles of freezing and thawing. The durability of the mixture against salt freeze–thaw cycles is assessed via the bond deterioration ratio (BDR) and total strength ratio (TSR) values, which are calculated via Eqs. (4) and (5).

The BDR is the residual strength ratio of the specimen after N freeze-thaw cycles, %; RDC is the compressive strength of the specimen after immersion curing to the corresponding age, MPa; and RC is the standard compressive strength of the specimen, MPa.

The TSR is the strength ratio of the freeze-thaw splitting test, %; RT2 is the splitting tensile strength of the second group of specimens after freeze-thaw cycles, MPa; and RT1 is the splitting tensile strength of the first group of specimens without freeze-thaw cycles, MPa.

Microscopic tests

A series of experiments are conducted to investigate the microstructural properties of basalt fiber and polycarboxylate superplasticizer within cement fly ash stabilized aeolian sand gravel base materials under various freeze-thaw conditions. The specimens are subjected to 10 cycles of freeze-thaw treatment with clear water or solutions of 0.3%NaCl, 0.5%NaCl, 3%Na2SO4, and 5%Na2SO4. The resulting damaged specimens are then analyzed via SEM to examine the microstructure. Additionally, EDS is employed to study the compositional structure of the mixture products.

Test ratio

A cement-to-fly ash ratio of 1:3 was adopted in accordance with relevant literature, as it has been shown to promote pozzolanic activity while maintaining mechanical performance23. The fine aggregate is entirely replaced by aeolian sand in the suspension-dense cement-fly ash stabilized aeolian sand gravel mixture, with a cement dosage of 5%24. The fixed mix ratio for the cement fly ash stabilized aeolian sand gravel is cement: fly ash: gravel: aeolian sand = 5: 15: 34: 4622. The dosages of 0.1% for basalt fiber and 1.00% for polycarboxylate superplasticizer (both by mass of cementitious materials) were selected based on their identification as optimal levels in our group’s previous research, providing an ideal balance between enhanced mechanical properties and improved workability25. Results

Salt freeze–thaw cycle compressive strength test results and analysis

After standard curing for 28 days, the standard curing specimens of the BP-CFSAGB mixtures with ratios of B0P0, B0P1, B1P0, and B1P1 are subjected to 5 and 10 cycles of salt freeze-thaw tests. The results are shown in Figs. 5a–g.

Change in the BDR with the number of salt freeze–thaw cycles: (a) Clear water, (b) 0.3%NaCl, (c) 0.5%NaCl, (d) 3%Na2SO4, (e) 5%Na2SO4, (f) 0.3%NaCl + 3%Na2SO4, (g) 0.5%NaCl + 5%Na2SO4.

As shown in Figs. 5a–g, the salt freeze–thaw cycle tests were conducted under identical immersion conditions. In all mix groups, the residual compressive strength ratio (BDR) decreased with an increasing number of freeze–thaw cycles. For example, the BDR after 10 cycles was consistently lower than that after 5 cycles, indicating progressive internal damage to the specimen structure. This degradation was attributed to both the freeze–thaw action and the coupled effect of salt ion infiltration, which accelerated microcrack formation and weakened the bonding within the matrix26.

This trend corresponds to the observed reduction in unconfined compressive strength, demonstrating that repeated freeze–thaw cycles in a salt-rich environment severely compromise the durability of CFSAGB materials. Under the same environmental conditions and freeze–thaw cycles, the BDRs followed the order: B1P1 > B1P0 > B0P1 > B0P0. This suggests that the inclusion of basalt fiber, polycarboxylate superplasticizer, and especially their combination, contributes to improved freeze–thaw resistance.

Compared to the unmodified control group (B0P0), the compressive strength of the B1P1 mix (with both basalt fiber and superplasticizer) increased by 4.0 –4.5 MPa after 5 cycles, corresponding to a 27.9–38.1% improvement. After 10 cycles, strength improvements of 3.8–4.5 MPa (32.2–40.2%) were recorded. These enhancements reflect the ability of basalt fibers to bridge microcracks and absorb freeze-induced stress, while the superplasticizer improves particle dispersion and reduces porosity, limiting salt ingress. When used together, the combined effect was greater than either additive used alone, indicating a synergistic interaction that enhances structural integrity under repeated salt freeze–thaw conditions.

Furthermore, while mixes incorporating only basalt fiber or only superplasticizer exhibited improved antifreeze performance relative to the control, their enhancement levels were consistently lower than that of the B1P1 mix. This highlights the advantage of the combined modification strategy.

Finally, the degree of compressive strength loss after 10 freeze–thaw cycles varied across different solution environments. The most severe deterioration occurred in the NaCl–Na2SO4 mixed solution, followed by Na2SO4 and NaCl solutions, with the least damage observed in the clear water condition. This pattern underscores the aggressive nature of combined salt environments and further validates the importance of additive-based reinforcement in resisting compound deterioration mechanisms.

Salt freeze–thaw cycle splitting strength test results and analysis

Freeze-thaw splitting tests under 5 and 10 cycles of salt-freeze-thaw cycles were conducted on the standardized recuperation specimens of the BP-CFSAGB mixes with fit ratios of B0P0 ratio, B0P1 ratio, B1P0 ratio, and B1P1 ratio, respectively, after they were cured for up to 28 days. The results of the tests are shown in Figs. 6a–g.

Changing trend of the TSR with the number of salt-freeze thaw cycles: (a) Clear water, (b) 0.3%NaCl, (c) 0.5%NaCl, (d) 3%Na2SO4, (e) 5%Na2SO4, (f) 0.3%NaCl + 3%Na2SO4, (g) 0.5%NaCl + 5%Na2SO4.

As shown in Figs. 6a–g, the tensile strength ratio (TSR) of all mixtures decreased with an increasing number of salt freeze–thaw cycles under the same immersion environment. Specifically, the TSR values after 10 cycles were consistently lower than those after 5 cycles, indicating progressive deterioration of internal structure due to the combined effects of freeze–thaw action and salt ion intrusion. This structural degradation led to a reduction in splitting tensile strength, reflecting the material’s weakening resistance under repeated environmental stress.

Under identical environmental and freeze–thaw conditions, the TSR values followed the order: B1P1 > B1P0 > B0P1 > B0P0. This trend demonstrates that the incorporation of basalt fiber, polycarboxylate superplasticizer, and particularly their combination, enhanced the frost resistance of CFSAGB materials.

Quantitatively, after 5 cycles, the B1P1 group exhibited tensile strength increases of 0.37–0.40 MPa compared to the control group (B0P0), corresponding to relative improvements of 38.1–40.8%. After 10 cycles, the improvements ranged from 0.35–0.43 MPa, with relative increases between 39.8% and 54.4%. These enhancements can be attributed to the fiber’s ability to inhibit crack propagation and absorb stress, while the superplasticizer contributes to reduced porosity and more uniform hydration. Their combined use results in a synergistic effect, whereby the material benefits from both mechanical reinforcement and microstructural refinement.

While the addition of only basalt fiber or only polycarboxylate superplasticizer also improved freezing resistance, the degree of enhancement was consistently lower than that observed in the composite-modified mixes. This further confirms the existence of a synergistic interaction between the two additives in improving the tensile durability under freeze–thaw conditions.

Finally, after 10 freeze–thaw cycles, the TSR deterioration varied with the type of solution. The most severe decline was observed in the NaCl–Na2SO4 mixed solution, followed by Na2SO4 and NaCl solutions, while the smallest reduction occurred in the clean water environment. This trend reflects the compounding effects of chloride and sulfate ions, which intensify structural damage under cyclic freezing conditions.

Microscopic mechanism analysis

To investigate the durability properties of BP-CFSAGB mixtures under salt freeze-thaw cycles, SEM and EDS were employed to conduct microscopic tests at various curing ages. After 10 freeze-thaw cycles at a curing age of 28 days in clear water, 0.3% NaCl solution, 0.5% NaCl solution, 3% Na2SO4 solution, and 5% Na2SO4 solution environments, the BP-CFSAGB mixture samples were characterized using SEM (5000× magnification) and EDS to analyze the elemental mass fractions between the aggregates and the bonding surfaces of the aeolian sand. The results were then analyzed to assess the effects of basalt fibers, polycarboxylate superplasticizer, and external conditions on the CFSAGB mixtures.

Fig. 7 presents the SEM images and EDS analysis of specimens damaged after 10 freeze–thaw cycles at a curing age of 28 days in a clear water environment. From Figs. 7a–d, the interior of the mixture appears relatively loose, with a significant presence of reticular and flocculent hydration products, as well as massive and amorphous gels. EDS analysis of the bonding surface revealed that the chemical elements were predominantly O, Si, Ca, Al, and Mg, indicating that the hydration products were primarily C–S–H and Ca(OH)2, with only a small amount of acicular ettringite. Notably, the mixture containing polycarboxylate superplasticizer showed a reduced amount of ettringite compared to the mixture without the superplasticizer. This is because polycarboxylate superplasticizer significantly accelerates the hydration reaction, facilitating the early conversion of ettringite27. Additionally, from a microscopic perspective, although large pores were present in the mixture containing only basalt fibers, the fibers, distributed within the matrix, effectively dispersed the expansion stress induced by freeze-thaw cycles, which improved the macroscopic mechanical performance of the mixture.

SEM-EDS images of clear water: (a) B0P0 ratio, (b) B0P1 ratio, (c) B1P0 ratio, (d) B1P1 ratio.

Figs. 8 and 9 show the SEM images and EDS analysis of specimens damaged after 10 freeze-thaw cycles in 0.3% and 0.5% NaCl solution environments at 28 days of curing. As observed in Figs. 8a–d and 9a–d, the mix is characterized by significant internal looseness, with numerous reticulated and flocculent hydration products, alongside a small amount of needle-like hydration products. Due to the salt-frost erosion, cracks exhibit a connecting phenomenon, and fly ash particles show signs of spalling. EDS analysis of the bonding surface revealed that the chemical elements present were mainly O, Si, Ca, and Al, indicating that the hydration products were mainly C–S–H, Ca(OH)2, and a small amount of calcium alumina. As the NaCl solution concentration increases, the internal erosion of the mixture becomes more severe. Comparison between Figs. 8a and 9a show that the fly ash particles exhibit more significant spalling. Freeze–thaw cycles cause microcracks and tiny pores to connect, creating a larger damaged interface within the material, where chloride ions and hydration products generate Friedel’s salt, resulting in reduced macroscopic performance28.

SEM-EDS images of 0.5%NaCl: (a) B0P0 ratio, (b) B0P1 ratio, (c) B1P0 ratio, (d) B1P1 ratio.

Figs. 10 and 11 show the SEM images and EDS analysis of specimens damaged after 10 freeze–thaw cycles in 3% and 5% Na2SO4 solution environments at a curing age of 28 days. The internal damage to these specimens is a result of both freeze-thaw cycles and sulfate erosion. As seen in Figs. 10a–d and 11a–d, the internal structure of the mix shows significant looseness, where part of the slurry surrounding the fly ash particles has loosened, and the fly ash particles exhibit signs of exfoliation. There is also a pore interconnection phenomenon, with numerous reticulated, flocculent, and needle-like hydration products. EDS analysis revealed that the chemical elements in the specimens were predominantly O, Si, Ca, Al, and S, indicating that the hydration products were primarily C–S–H, Ca(OH)2, and acicular ettringite. As the concentration of Na2SO4 increases, the occurrence of sulfur (S) within the specimens also rises due to immersion in Na2SO4 solution.

SEM-EDS images of 5%Na2SO4: (a) B0P0 ratio, (b) B0P1 ratio, (c) B1P0 ratio, (d) B1P1 ratio.

Both polycarboxylate superplasticizer and basalt fibers mitigated the progression of sulfate attack. Compared to the mixture without polycarboxylate superplasticizer (Figs. 10a,b, and 11a,b), specimens incorporating polycarboxylate superplasticizer (Figs. 10c,d, and 11c,d) exhibited reduced sulfate penetration at cracks and pores. This could be attributed to the formation of a more refined pore structure due to reduced water content. In comparison to specimens without fibers, the microstructures of the B1P0 and B1P1 groups were more compact, owing to the bridging effect of the fibers. The tight bonding between the fibers and the binder restricted crack propagation and material spalling. Although ettringite formation increased with rising sulfate concentration, the synergistic effects of polycarboxylate superplasticizer and basalt fibers in the B1P1 specimens better adapted to the stress expansion and salt erosion. Compared to specimens with only individual admixtures or without additives, the B1P1 ratio maintained the integrity of the microstructure, which manifested in greater macroscopic mechanical strength.

The internal damage mechanism of the mixtures under the action of saltwater freezing is schematically shown in Fig. 12. The combination of SEM and EDS results show that the internal damage to the mix is severe under the influence of salt freezing, the internal structure of the ITZ and mix has deteriorated, and the evolution of the pore size structure is complicated. As shown in Fig. 12a, aeolian sand was suspended between the coarse aggregates; fly ash was free in the sample, which filled the micropores of the mix; basalt fibers were uniformly distributed in the mix to form a reticulated structure; a small amount of micropores before the initial cycle; and a small amount of hydration products filled the micropores of the mixture. First, as shown in Fig. 12b, at the beginning of the salt-freeze-thaw cycle, the salt enters the specimen with water and fills the internal pores of the mix. As shown in Fig. 12c, after the salt solution enters the internal pores of the mix, the internal saturation increases rapidly, and when the temperature decreases, freezing and expansion stresses are generated, causing internal damage to the mix. As shown in Fig. 12b–e, the initial pores and cracks are further expanded by the continuous salt-freeze-thaw cycle, generating more pores and cracks, and some of the hydration product fills the pores and cracks. In addition, some of the salt invading the interior of the mixture chemically reacts with hydration products such as CaOH2 to generate new salts, and some of the salt is in the saturated or supersaturated state. Some of the salt is in the saturated or oversaturated state, leading to salt crystal precipitation, so that the internal salt expansion stress of the mixture, which causes pore expansion, from the macro-representation of the mechanical properties of the mixture, decreases. As shown in Fig. 12f, under the action of the salt-freeze-thaw cycle, many pores and cracks are generated, some of the pores and cracks are connected, and some hydration products fill the pores and cracks.

In this study, the synergistic effect of basalt fibers and polycarboxylate superplasticizer improved the microstructural stability and durability of the mixture. The fibers inhibited the propagation of microcracks and improved stress distribution, reducing the physical effects of freeze-thaw damage. The polycarboxylate superplasticizer promoted the early formation of hydration products (mainly C–S–H gel) around the fibers, effectively bridging the structure and enhancing both its structural and chemical stability. The combined effect of both components densified the ITZ, enhanced the continuity of hydration products, and thereby improved the overall durability of the material (Figs. 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11).

Conclusions

To study the synergistic effect of basalt fiber and polycarboxylate superplasticizer on the durability of cement fly ash-stabilized aeolian sand base. The effects of the 28 days unconfined compressive strength and splitting strength of the mixture under salt freeze-thaw cycles were studied. The mechanism of strength development was explained via a combination of SEM and EDS techniques. Through analysis and discussion, the following conclusions can be drawn:

With increasing freeze–thaw cycles, the residual compressive strength ratio (BDR) and tensile strength ratio (TSR) of all samples decreased. Among the tested mixtures, B1P1 (containing both basalt fiber and polycarboxylate superplasticizer) showed the highest frost resistance. Compared to the blank group (B0P0), the compressive and tensile strengths of B1P1 increased by 31.7–40.2% and 39.8–54.4%, respectively, after 5 and 10 salt freeze–thaw cycles, confirming the effectiveness of the composite modification in improving durability.

The synergistic use of basalt fiber and polycarboxylate superplasticizer enhanced the microstructure by increasing density and hydration product stability, thereby improving mechanical properties and durability.
