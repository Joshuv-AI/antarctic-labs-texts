Application of virtual reality in optimizing surgical outcomes and resident training in percutaneous nephrolithotomy: A randomized control trial
Authors: Ankur Mittal, Gurpremjit Singh, Vikas K. Panwar, Deepak Kumar, Omang Agarwal, Arup K. Mandal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposePercutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) necessitates that the urologist comprehensively understands the stone’s location and its relation to the intrarenal anatomy. We evaluated the use of virtual reality (VR) as a preoperative tool for planning for PCNL surgery and its assessment for training among residents in PCNL.Materials and MethodsThis prospective, randomized control trial took place between May 2021 to June 2022. Resident and consultant surgeons rated their understanding of the anatomy with computed tomography (CT) alone and after CT+VR. The CT scans from patients randomized to the intervention group created a patient-specific three-dimensional VR model (Anatomage software version 6.02).ResultsThe trial enrolled 90 participants and was randomized to the control group (CT alone) and the VR group. The mean age of the participants was 42.09±13.48 years. There were 63 males (70.0%) and 27 females (30.0%). Residents had an improved understanding of the location of the stone, size of the stone, shape, orientation of the calyx, optimal entry of the calyx, and navigation. Residents had a better ability to formulate a surgical plan with VR models. The consultant surgeon’s assessment showed CT and VR had non-significant results for understanding based on the above parameters. Preoperative evaluation in patients in the VR group with S.T.O.N.E score >8 showed a statistically significant better understanding of consultants (p<0.05). They recommend VR to colleagues if patients have a S.T.O.N.E score >8 (p<0.05).ConclusionsVR improved the understanding of PCNL for residents in all cases and consultants in complex anatomy cases.

INTRODUCTION

Kidney stones have a prevalence of 6.4%–13% in Asia [1]. Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is the preferred method for treating kidney stones larger than 2 cm [2]. However, a significant learning curve is needed for PCNL. The primary and most challenging stage of PCNL appears to be gaining safe access to the renal calyces [3]. According to a recent study, only 32% of American urologists who perform PCNL routinely gain percutaneous access independently [4]. Possible factors contributing to this phenomenon could be attributed to inadequate training, personal preferences, various external factors, the presence of more advanced equipment in the radiology suite, or the financial implications linked with the extended time required to gain access [3]. Teaching PCNL is more complicated than performing it. Incorporating teaching and training skills into most urological training programs is difficult. PCNL proficiency is required for the education of a trainee [5].

In order to devise a proficient entry pathway into the renal pelvis, it is imperative to possess a comprehensive understanding of its three-dimensional (3D) configuration. A better preoperative planning can be done by using a computed tomography (CT) to create 3D models. However, the method is seldom utilised as a standard [6]. CT provides detailed two-dimensional images that illustrate the structure of the kidneys. Brehmer et al. [6] showed that 3D CT scans on a two-dimensional (2D) screen improved outcomes and reduced punctures. The incorporation of 3D CT reconstructions into a tablet-based augmented reality system resulted in a reduction in the duration of puncturing procedures for trainees, as well as a drop in fluoroscopy time for both trainees and specialists [6].

Although the expensive cost of computer-based simulators restricts their adoption, virtual reality (VR)-based training can become essential to clinical training [7]. 3D CT reconstruction is a surface-rendered image with limited interaction, mainly rotation and zoom. However, 3D VR provides an interactive model which can even simulate the tract of entry, show detailed vascular anatomy, improve understanding of complex anatomy and help in better preoperative planning [8].

To increase the resident and consultant surgeons’ preoperative comprehension, we used CT to create a VR model. The utilization of interactive 3D simulation technology aids in the visualization and analysis of the patient’s renal architecture concerning the presence of an intrarenal calculus. This randomized controlled trial evaluated the efficacy of VR as a preoperative tool for planning PCNL surgery. The second objective was to assess its use for training among residents in PCNL.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective, randomized control trial occurred at All India Institute of Medical Sciences between May 2021 and June 2022. The Institutional Review Board of All India Institute of Medical Sciences approved the study (approval number: AIIMS-RCT-341), and the study was registered in Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI) (CTRI/2021/05/033652). The trial followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and Good Clinical Practice Recommendations. To increase the transparency of any systemic errors, CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) was implemented. Inclusion criteria were patients aged 18–70 years undergoing PCNL, single or multiple stones, normal renal function and willing to give informed consent. For residents involved, only those in the final year and having completed more than ten cases of PCNL were included. The exclusion criteria were pregnant women, uncorrected coagulopathy, patients with renal impairment, resident surgeons who had performed less than ten cases of PCNL.

1. Sample size

In a study by Rassweiler-Seyfried et al. [9], the mean radiation exposure time in augmented reality using an iPad was 380.71 seconds, with a standard deviation of 287.09 seconds. The mean radiation exposure time in the control group was 66.93 seconds, with a standard deviation 50.80 seconds. To determine the sample size for our study, we conducted a two-sample comparison of means test. Using an alpha of 0.05 (two-sided) and a power of 0.90, with m1=263, m2=132, sd1=250, sd2=100, and n2/n1=1.0, the required sample size for each group was calculated to be 45.

2. Preoperative evaluation

Patients undergoing PCNL surgery in the Department of Urology were enrolled based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. The patients were subjected to randomization using an online random number generator program. Sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes were utilized to conceal the treatment assignment for each individual case. These cases were then allocated to either the control group, in which the operation will be planned with surgeons and residents review of the CT only, or an intervention group, in which the surgeons and resident will review both the CT along with a 3D VR model of the patient’s anatomy that will be created from the source CT. All the CT scans in the study were conducted in the supine position to ensure standardization in data collection. It also maintains consistency in anatomical assessment across all patients and uniform reporting of scores. Enhanced CT scans were used to ensure uniformity in all patients, and they standardised imaging quality, leading to accurate stone burden assessment and consistency in anatomical evaluation.

In the patients randomized to the intervention group, a 3D VR model was created (Anatomage software version 6.02) (Supplementary Fig. 1). This model included the stone, the kidney mass, the pelvicalyceal system, the renal artery, the renal vein, and additional anatomic structures, such as the ribs, colon, spleen, splenic artery, splenic vein, ureter, and collecting system, renal cysts (if present) and spine. This was an interactive 3D model. The VR models were created using a semi-automated approach. The user identified structures of interest on the CT scans, and the software utilized this information to generate the 3D VR models. The creation of VR models was done using Anatomage Table and software version 6.0. The Digital Imaging and Communications (DICOM) standard format can be uploaded to the table software using a USB drive. The software itself generates the 3D model, which can be trimmed to the area of anatomy [10]. The process of creating each VR model took less than 2 minutes from start to finish. The uploaded CT scan image quality greatly affects the model created. We used less than or equal to 1 mm CT slice thickness for the model creation. Any images with greater than 1 mm slice thickness are not suitable for model creation.

The Anatomage software enables the VR model to be rotated 360° to simulate a prone position, zoomed in, and measure length and angle from the calyceal fornix to the skin on the virtual dissection table. This is essential in deciding safe percutaneous access to the desired calyx.

For each control group, patient surgeons and residents reviewed CT scans preoperatively and intraoperatively. Preoperative and postoperative assessment forms were filled by them accordingly.

In the intervention group, surgeons and residents reviewed the 3D VR models and a CT scan and analyzed the intra-renal pelvicalyceal anatomy and the planned percutaneous puncture site. Preoperative and postoperative assessment forms were filled by them accordingly.

Excellence in PCNL is usually obtained after performing more than 100 PCNLs [1112]. The consultants performing the surgery in this study had already performed more than 500 PCNLs.

A 3D VR model is used mainly as a preoperative assessment tool to better understand the anatomy and complexity of the procedure. However, the actual puncture was taken using fluoroscopic access in all the cases, which is standard in our center.

Demographic data included age and sex. Clinical data included baseline blood investigations, stone size, stone number, S.T.O.N.E score, and side of operation. S.T.O.N.E nephrometric score uses five parameters that are easy and quick to calculate from a CT scan. Abdrabuh et al. [13] showed that the higher the S.T.O.N.E score, the greater the complexity of the stone for PCNL and lesser the clearance rate in PCNL. Based on this the scores less then or equal to eight were taken as low complexity. Operative data collection had X-ray exposure time, total time spent introducing needle to collecting system, number of attempts to puncture desired calyx, total operative time, any pelvic perforations, complete retrograde contrast injected, infundibular tear, and vessel injury. Postoperative data collection included changes in hemoglobin level, need for blood transfusion, and surgery-related complications, which were graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification.

3. Statistical analysis

The continuous variables were reported using either the mean±standard deviation or the median and interquartile range, as specified. We compared proportions and odds ratios for categorical data using the chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests as and when required. The means were compared using the Student’s t-test and Mann–Whitney U test as applicable. Paired t-test was used to compare readings of continuous variables at the endpoint of time. All the statistical tests were performed with a significance level of α=0.05 (95% confidence interval), and analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

The trial enrolled 90 randomized participants in the control group (CT alone) and VR group (VR). The consort diagram is shown in Fig. 1. The mean age of the participants was 42.09±13.48 years. There were 63 males (70.0%) and 27 females (30.0%).

1. Intraoperative and postoperative characteristics

The total exposure time, X-ray exposure time, number of punctures, and postoperative data were not different between the two groups (Table 1).

2. Preoperative resident evaluation (CT group alone vs. CT+VR group)

The resident surgeon’s assessment showed that post-VR review, their understanding significantly improved in all parameters (p<0.05) (Table 2). Residents had an improved understanding of the location of the stone, size of the stone, shape, and orientation of the calyx, optimal entry of the calyx, and navigation with a rigid nephroscope (Fig. 2).

3. Postoperative resident evaluation (CT group alone vs. CT+VR group)

Post-VR assessment residents thought of better navigation (p<0.05) for intra-renal anatomy, correlation with the calyx of entry, location of the stone, size of the stone, and degree of hydronephrosis (Table 3).

4. Postoperative VR usefulness

Residents had a poor ability to formulate a surgical plan with CT alone and decreased confidence in performing the surgery only after CT review (Table 4). They felt confident post-VR review and suggested it to be better for surgical planning, future surgeries, the recommendation to colleagues, and teaching of residents.

5. Preoperative consultant evaluation

The consultant surgeon’s assessment showed CT and VR had non-significant results for understanding based on the location of the stone, size of stone, shape, and orientation of the calyx, optimal entry of calyx, and navigation with rigid nephroscope (Table 5). Although preoperative evaluation in patients in the VR group having S.T.O.N.E score >8 showed that there was a statistically significant better understanding of consultants concerning the location of stone, shape, and orientation of calyx, optimal entry of calyx, and navigation with rigid nephroscope (p<0.05) (Table 6). In this study, a cutoff of eight was selected to indicate the critical point at which PCNL procedure complexity increases. Scores of eight and above in the S.T.O.N.E nephrolithometry scoring system, which goes from 5 to 13, typically indicate a combination of factors that contribute to greater surgical difficulty, such as larger stone size, multiple involved calyces, and increased stone density. We sought to investigate the effects of VR training and optimisation in situations where the procedure’s complexity is high enough to require advanced training methods, by concentrating on cases with a score of eight or higher.

6. Postoperative consultant evaluation

Post-VR assessment consultants were of the opinion of statistically similar results concerning the technical difficulty of the surgery, navigation of intra-renal anatomy, location of the stone, size of the stone, and degree of hydronephrosis (p>0.05) (Table 7).

7. Postoperative VR questionnaire

Consultants could formulate a surgical plan with CT alone and were confident about performing the surgery only after CT review in cases with S.T.O.N.E score ≤8 (Tables 5, 6, 7). In patients having S.T.O.N.E score >8, there was reduced ability to formulate a surgical plan based on CT alone. In all these cases, VR was a valuable surgical planning tool and better for use in future surgery with S.T.O.N.E score >8. They recommend VR to colleagues if patients have a S.T.O.N.E score >8 (p<0.05). VR is a valuable tool for teaching residents in case of any S.T.O.N.E score and S.T.O.N.E score >8 (Table 8).

DISCUSSION

Training with live patients is best for beginners. However, the existing conventional training method creates several ethical challenges, such as significant complications, a high expense, and a shortage of patients for practical experience. Training on animals under anesthesia is the next best option. Percutaneous access training offers realistic anatomy as well as a tissue feel. One will need legal approval from the animal ethics committee, a knowledgeable veterinarian assistant, a laboratory anesthesia setup, and the standard animal costs for animal training. To overcome these obstacles, the newbie needs bench model training before intraoperative instruction on live subjects [7].

VR models help the surgeon to alter the simulation of the kidney and intrarenal anatomy; one can even gaze along the intended nephrostomy route and practically go to the planned calyx of entry. This engagement is both visual and tactile. The body and the stone are manipulated manually. Indeed, in this investigation, every anatomical component can be separated to assist in elucidating anatomy and individual parts [14]. Currently, existing imaging software possesses the capability to generate volumetric 3D depictions of CT scans. Nevertheless, these visualizations are limited to a 2D display medium [615]. Recent technology breakthroughs have facilitated the production of authentic copies of the renal collecting system and stones using 3D printing. However, this method is associated with high costs and limited availability. The 3D-printed models offer a superficial representation of the kidney and the underlying stone [815].

The VR mode used in this study increased the resident surgeon’s understanding of the location of the stone, size of the stone, shape, and orientation of the calyx, optimal entry of the calyx, and navigation with rigid nephroscope in both preoperatively and postoperatively assessment. They had better confidence post-VR review than CT alone and suggested its use for surgical planning for future surgeries and recommendations to colleagues, and teaching purposes. However, the consultant surgeon’s assessment should be that only in cases of complex anatomy was there a statistically significant difference concerning the location of the stone, shape, and orientation of the calyx, optimal entry of the calyx, and navigation with a rigid nephroscope. Post-VR assessment consultants were of the opinion that statistically similar results concerning the technical difficulty of the surgery, navigation of intra-renal anatomy, location of the stone, size of the stone, and degree of hydronephrosis. They recommended VR to colleagues for patients with S.T.O.N.E scores >8 and recommended VR for teaching.

Parkhomenko et al. [14] did a pilot study in which they compared 25 VR study patients to 25 retrospectively matched-paired of non-VR patients. They showed that VR improved urologists’ understanding of the renal anatomy and patient comprehension of their surgery [14]. They showed that VR had benefits regarding decreased fluoroscopy time and less blood loss, along with a trend toward fewer access tracts and higher stone-free rates [14]. Tan et al. [16] did a retrospective study of 139 patients with complex renal stones and divided them into two groups. Group A patients (n=72) had the 3D reconstruction technique before PCNL. Two weeks after PCNL, Group A had a stone clearance rate of 81.9% while Group B had a rate of 64.2% (p<0.05). The success rates for the first-time puncture were 87.5% for Group A and 47.8% for Group B, with a significant difference between them (p<0.05). The hemoglobin decline and hospital stay had no significant difference between the two groups [16]. Our study is the first randomized control trial to assess CT and VR imaging techniques. There was no statistically significant difference in fluoroscopy time, blood loss, and access tract in our study.

A 3D VR model helps in better preoperative planning by determining the optimal trajectory and desired calyx of entry, but the actual puncture needs to be adapted to real-time imaging, respiratory motion and the patients’ anatomy in prone position.

VR can also help plan surgeries like partial nephrectomy, donor nephrectomy, and complex anatomy [171819]. VR models may be built from non-contrast or contrast CT scans, and the software allows for model construction without a software engineer. The Anatomage Table is a one-time investment and can be used to reconstruct images of any body part. Therefore, it can be used by other departments of the hospital too. It has an interactive user interface and can be used without the need for any additional personnel [20]. Implementing VR models in training, such as Anatomage, requires significant initial costs, maintenance and software upgradation costs. However, there are many long-term benefits, like repeated practice in risk-free environments, which can lead to reduced surgical errors and enhanced skills. In resource-constrained settings, institutional use and collaboration with existing training centers which have the technology can help in skills training.

The other technique for training PCNL is 3D printing of the CT scan. The problem with 3D printing is the initial cost of the equipment, the need for a skilled technician for the 3D reconstruction of the images, and the cost of the consumables. Recently, cost-effective multiheaded bioprinters have been developed [162122].

There are several limitations to this study. CT scan quality affects the creation of VR models. VR will be inaccurate if the CT scan is a low-slice image or the stone is not visualized in all the cuts. The VR image formation is also dependent upon the understanding of CT anatomy. Postoperative CT scan follow-up data were not part of our study design. Therefore we do not have specific information regarding the completeness of stone removal in the VR versus the non-VR group. Lack of follow-up data is a significant limitation to this study. We acknowledge that the lack of postoperative CT scan follow-up data is a limitation of our study, and future research should consider incorporating such assessments to evaluate the impact of the VR model on stone clearance outcomes. Another limitation is that we did not collect specific information from the consultants regarding their initial surgical plan, and only selected the calyx of entry after viewing the CT scan. It would have provided valuable insights into the impact of the VR model on decision-making. It is an important point for future studies to investigate the potential changes in surgical planning and their clinical implications. All the CT scans were recorded in the supine position, which can be another limitation, as anatomy changes a little in the prone position. Another limitation of this study is that the results are drawn from a single location. The findings of this study may not represent a broader population and a multi-centre study is required for validation of the results. A practical limitation of the VR model is the difficulty of obtaining reimbursement. Existing models depend on institutional licensing and collaboration across various departments to distribute expenses across training, planning the surgeries and operative use. VR models require substantial inputs for procedure upgrades and equipment advancement; this can be alleviated by modular, open standard platforms and collaborations between manufacturers and healthcare providers. Using conventional DICOM to create and alter the VR models helps in faster development and integration of novel surgical innovations.

Based on the limitations of this study, future studies that may be done are to assess the stone-free rate using the VR model compared to the CT model, and studying the cost-effectiveness of the VR model can also be another study. We can also do a multi-centre study to understand the applicability of the results to the broader population. A study based on an unenhanced CT scan can also be used to assess the effectiveness of VR technology in the assessment of stones and stone clearance rates.

CONCLUSIONS

VR improved the understanding of PCNL for residents in all cases and consultants in complex anatomy cases. Residents were more confident in surgery post-VR review, whereas consultants had a similar understanding of CT or VR in patients with S.T.O.N.E scores ≤8. However, consultants had a better understanding and ease in surgery post-VR in patients with S.T.O.N.E scores >8. Residents and consultants both recommend VR for teaching PCNL surgery.
