Effects of replacing chemical fertilizer with organic fertilizer on organic carbon mineralization and carbon cycle functional genes in yellow soil
Authors: Shengmei Yang, Xiaoli Wang, Jianjun Duan, Sanwei Yang, Jin He, Chen Fang, Na Zhao, Yizhu Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFertilization-mediated soil organic carbon (SOC) mineralization is a key process in agroecosystem carbon cycling, yet the microbial mechanisms involved under different fertilization regimes remain unclear. This study, based on a three-year field experiment in acidic yellow soil (Ultisol) in Guizhou, integrated SOC mineralization incubation and metagenomic sequencing to compare SOC mineralization and functional gene profiles under no fertilization (CK), chemical fertilizer alone (NP), and replacing chemical fertilizer with 50% or 100% organic fertilizer (1/2NPM and M). Fertilization significantly increased cumulative mineralized SOC (Ct) (p < 0.05); NP showed high mineralization, whereas organic-fertilizer replacement reduced the cumulative mineralization ratio (Ct/SOC). Metagenomic analysis indicated NP did not substantially alter carbon-cycling genes but lowered the C/N ratio, increasing microbial diversity and driving “carbon-compensation” mineralization. Conversely, 1/2NPM and M improved soil pH, available phosphorus (AP), and nitrate nitrogen (NO₃−-N), reshaped microbial community structure, up-regulated carbon-fixation genes (korA, facA, coxS), and suppressed carbon-degradation genes (pel, chi), enhancing carbon sequestration capacity. Introduction

Excessive and prolonged application of chemical fertilizers has been shown to cause soil acidification, compaction, and elevated greenhouse gas emissions1,2. In contrast, organic fertilizers can provide balanced nutrients, improve soil structure, alleviate acidification, and promote microbial activity3. Consequently, the replacement of organic for chemical fertilizers is regarded as an effective management strategy for maintaining agricultural productivity while reducing adverse environmental impacts. Soil organic carbon (SOC) mineralization plays a critical role in terrestrial ecosystem carbon cycles and has significant effects on regulating atmospheric CO₂ concentrations and mitigating climate change4,5. Soil microorganisms are key drivers of SOC mineralization, and microbial abundance, community structure, and functional composition determine the direction and rates of microbial catabolism and anabolism, which in turn affect soil carbon emissions and sequestration6. Therefore, within the context of climate change, conducting in-depth research on the effects of replacing chemical fertilizers with organic fertilizers on organic carbon mineralization and carbon cycling functional genes in yellow soils is of great significance for soil nutrient management and the sustainable development of agriculture.

Numerous studies have documented the effects of different fertilization practices on SOC mineralization7. Fertilization can modulate the SOC mineralization process by altering the input of exogenous organic carbon, changing soil physicochemical properties, and reshaping microbial community structure8. However, there is currently no consensus regarding the effects of chemical fertilizer application on SOC mineralization, as multiple studies have reported stimulatory, inhibitory9, or negligible influences on soil carbon mineralization10. Jing et al.11 suggested that such discrepancies may result from variations in soil stoichiometry following fertilization: low nitrogen inputs were found to enhance SOC-degrading enzyme activity and CO₂ emissions, whereas high nitrogen inputs produced inhibitory effects. In comparison with chemical fertilizers, the notion that organic fertilizers accelerate SOC mineralization by improving microbial habitat conditions has been widely accepted12. Nevertheless, Lin et al.13 reported that the proportion of organic fertilizers replacing chemical fertilizers can significantly influence SOC mineralization, with changes in bacterial community composition following organic fertilizer application being a primary driver of accelerated carbon mineralization14.

The effects of fertilization on soil carbon mineralization and carbon cycling can be reflected through changes in the microbial ecological functions involved in carbon cycling15–17. Chen et al.18 reported that the application of chemical fertilizers stimulates soil microorganisms to upregulate enzymes and functional genes associated with carbon degradation, thereby alleviating carbon limitation. Chemical fertilization also helps maintain relatively stable abundances of functional genes related to carbon fixation and degradation, thus accelerating carbon turnover in soils19,20. Furthermore, Li et al.21 found that applying chemical fertilizers alone or in combination with maize straw was more effective than maize straw return alone for increasing the abundances of functional genes such as korA, korB, and IHD1 in the reductive tricarboxylic acid (rTCA) cycle, thereby enhancing soil carbon sequestration. In addition, studies on fertilization have yielded divergent findings regarding soil methane metabolism. For example, Hu et al.22 in a 30-year fertilization experiment, observed that the sole application of chemical fertilizers promoted the expression of functional genes linked to methane oxidation, whereas Zheng et al.23 found that chemical fertilization inhibited methane oxidation. Such inconsistencies may be closely related to variations in soil type, cropping systems, and fertilization regimes.

SOC mineralization is governed by the structure of soil microbial communities, particularly those participating in carbon cycling24. However, current research offers incomplete quantitative evidence for the causal chain linking microbial community structure, functional genes, and carbon mineralization under scenarios in which organic fertilizers replace chemical fertilizers. This gap is especially pronounced in scale-resolved analyses within agroecosystems subjected to long-term fertilization.

This study was carried out as part of a three-year fixed-site experiment conducted in the acidic yellow soil region of Guizhou, combined with laboratory incubation tests of carbon mineralization and metagenomic sequencing analyses. The study systematically examined (I) the effects of replacing chemical fertilizers with organic fertilizers on SOC mineralization, carbon cycling-related functional genes, and associated microbial communities; and (II) the combined influence of soil physicochemical properties, microbial community structure, and carbon cycling-related functional genes on soil organic carbon mineralization. Quantitative, scale-resolved evidence for this causal chain under long-term fertilization is presented, thereby furnishing a theoretical basis for optimization of nutrient management and enhancement of carbon sequestration in acidic Ultisol agroecosystems.

Materials and methods

Site description

The experiment was conducted in Jichang Township, Xixiu District, Anshun City, Guizhou Province (106°05’5’’ E, 26°06’29’’ N), at an elevation of 1271 m. It is a humid subtropical monsoon climate area, with a mean annual temperature of 13.2–15 °C and annual precipitation of 1250–1400 mm. The soil of the experimental site is classified as yellow soil. The experiment began in 2020. Undisturbed topsoil (0–20 cm; plow layer) was collected using a five-point composite sampling method. After air-drying and passing through a 2 mm sieve, samples were immediately analyzed: soil was determined at a soil-to-water ratio of 1:2.5 (w/v) with a pH meter (ISO 10390:2018); SOC was measured using the H2SO4–K2Cr2O7 method (Walkley and Black (1934) and NY/T 1121.6–2006); alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen (AN) was determined by the alkali diffusion method (NY/T 1081–2006); available phosphorus (AP) was extracted with 0.5 M NaHCO3 solution at pH 8.5 and determined by molybdenum–antimony colorimetry (Olsen et al. (1954) and NY/T 1121.7–2006); and available potassium (AK) was determined by NH4OAc extraction followed by flame photometry (NY/T 889–2004). Experimental design and soil collection

The experiment was a one-way randomized block design with four fertilization treatments: no fertilizer (CK), chemical fertilizer alone (NP), replacing chemical fertilizer with 50% organic fertilizer (1/2NPM), and replacing chemical fertilizer with 100% organic fertilizer (M). Each treatment was replicated three times, resulting in a total of 12 plots, each with an area of 22 m² (3.3 m × 6.6 m). The specific fertilization rates are provided in Table S1.

The cropping pattern was a 2:2 maize–soybean intercropping system, featuring maize cultivar “Jinyu 908” and soybean cultivar “Fendou 97”. Row spacing was 60 cm for maize and 40 cm for soybean (Fig. S1). Maize plants were positioned 30 cm apart within rows (one plant per hill), and soybean plants were positioned 20 cm apart within rows (two plants per hill). Nitrogen fertilizer (urea, containing 46.2% N) was administered in two equal applications at a basal-to-topdressing ratio of 1:1, whereas phosphorus fertilizer (superphosphate, containing 12.0% P2O5) and organic fertilizer (organic matter ≥ 45%, N + P2O5 + K2O ≥ 5%) were incorporated into the soil as a single basal application. Land preparation and sowing were conducted on April 22, 2022. Agronomic management during the growing period was performed in accordance with local agricultural practices, as described in Supplementary Text 1.

Soil samples were collected from the 0–20 cm soil layer using a five-point sampling method after the maize and soybean harvests; three mixed samples were collected per treatment. Fresh soil samples were sieved through a 2 mm sieve. One portion was stored in a − 80 °C refrigerator for metagenomic sequencing, while another was retained in a − 4 °C refrigerator for SOC mineralization incubation experiments. The remainder of the soil was naturally air-dried and ground to allow filtering through 2 mm, 0.25 mm, and 0.149 mm sieves to determine the basic soil properties.

Basic soil properties were determined according to standard methods. Analytical procedures for pH, SOC, AN, and AP were described previously. Soil water content (SWC) was measured gravimetrically as the mass loss of a 10 g oven-dried soil subsample at 105 °C until constant weight (ISO 11465:1993); total nitrogen (TN) was determined using the Kjeldahl method (NY/T 53–1987), and total phosphorus (TP) was measured by sodium hydroxide fusion followed by molybdenum–antimony colorimetry (NY/T 88–1988). Ammonium nitrogen (NH₄⁺-N) and nitrate nitrogen (NO₃−-N) were quantified by indophenol blue colorimetry (NY/T 970–2006) and ultraviolet spectrophotometry (NY/T 1978–2010), respectively. Dissolved organic carbon (DOC) and microbial biomass carbon (MBC) were measured using the chloroform fumigation–extraction method with 0.5 mol L−¹ K₂SO₄25, whereas readily oxidizable organic carbon (ROC) was determined by oxidation with 0.333 mol L−¹ KMnO₄26.

Laboratory incubation

Laboratory incubation for SOC mineralization was conducted using the alkali absorption method21. A 30 g subsample of fresh soil (sieved through a 2 mm mesh) was weighed and placed in a 50 mL beaker. Each treatment was conducted in triplicate, and six blanks were included. After adjusting soil moisture to 30%, the beakers were placed at the bottom of 1 L incubation flasks and preincubated in the dark at 25 °C for 7 days. After preincubation, an alkali absorption cup containing 10 mL of 0.1 mol L−¹ NaOH was placed at the bottom of each flask; the flasks were then sealed and incubated in the dark at 25 °C. On days 1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, and 30 of incubation, the alkali absorption cups were replaced and soil moisture was adjusted. Then 2 mL of 1 mol L−¹ BaCl₂ was added to the alkali absorption cups, followed by two drops of phenolphthalein indicator. The solution was titrated with 0.1 mol L−¹ HCl to the phenolphthalein endpoint (colorless). SOC mineralization was calculated from the amount of CO₂ evolved. The SOC mineralization rate and cumulative mineralization were determined following the procedure described by Guo et al.27

Soil metagenomic sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Sequencing was performed by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., using the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Following acquisition of the raw read dataset, adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed with Fastp (v0.20.0; https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp): adapters at the 3′ and 5′ termini were removed, and reads with an average Phred quality score below 20 or a post-trimming length < 50 bp were excluded. High-quality reads were subsequently assembled de novo using MEGAHIT (v1.1.2; https://github.com/voutcn/megahit), and assembled contigs with lengths ≥ 300 bp were retained as the final assembly. Open reading frames (ORFs) within the contigs were predicted with Prodigal (v2.6.3; https://github.com/hyattpd/Prodigal); genes with nucleotide lengths ≥ 100 bp were retained and translated into amino acid sequences. CD-HIT (v4.6.1; http://www.bioinformatics.org/cd-hit/) was employed to cluster genes using a sequence identity threshold of 95% and a coverage threshold of 90%, designating the longest gene in each cluster as the representative sequence and thereby constructing a non-redundant gene catalog. SOAPaligner (v2.21; http://soap.genomics.org.cn/) was then applied to map high-quality reads from each sample against the non-redundant gene catalog (sequence identity ≥ 95%) to estimate gene abundances.

The non-redundant gene catalog was subsequently searched against the NCBI NR database (September 1, 2022 release; https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast/db/FASTA/) using DIAMOND (v2.0.13; http://ab.inf.uni-tuebingen.de/software/diamond) in BLASTp mode (E-value ≤ 1e-5). Taxonomic annotation was derived from the taxonomy information associated with NR entries, and species relative abundances were calculated by aggregating the abundances of genes assigned to each species. Functional annotation was performed by aligning the non-redundant gene catalog to the KEGG database (release 103.1, September 2022; https://www.genome.jp/kegg) using DIAMOND in BLASTp mode (E-value ≤ 1e-5), followed by KEGG pathway assignment with KOBAS (v2.0).

Statistical analysis

Data were compiled using Excel 2010. Soil physicochemical properties and carbon mineralization indices were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v27). After confirming homogeneity of variances with Levene’s test (p ≥ 0.05), differences among treatments were assessed through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with multiple comparisons performed using Duncan’s test and results adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method (α = 0.05). Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine relationships between gene abundances and soil variables, and correlation heatmaps and histograms were constructed in OriginPro. Metagenomic sequencing data were processed and statistically analyzed via the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://www.majorbio.com/). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was conducted based on Bray–Curtis distances, and differences in carbon-cycle functional genes and microbial communities among fertilization treatments were assessed through analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) and Kruskal–Wallis tests. Mantel tests were employed to examine coupling relationships between soil factors and both gene abundances and microbial communities. Finally, partial least squares path modeling (PLS-PM) was performed in R (v4.5.2) using the “plspm” package to quantify integrated influence pathways among variables.

AP, NH4+-N, NO3−-N, organic carbon fractions (DOC, MBC, ROC), and active carbon pool ratios (DOC/SOC, MBC/SOC) showed an initial increase, followed by a subsequent decrease, with the highest values occurring under the 1/2NPM treatment. The C/N ratio was lowest in the NP treatment, whereas AN and ROC/SOC did not differ significantly among treatments (p > 0.05).

Effects of replacing chemical fertilizer with organic fertilizer on soil organic carbon mineralization

The SOC mineralization rate was characterized by a three-phase dynamic pattern, consisting of an initial rapid decline, followed by a slower decline, and eventual stabilization (Fig. 1a). Fertilization significantly increased Ct (p < 0.05). On the 46th day of incubation, Ct in the NP, 1/2NPM, and M treatments was 12.41%, 9.15%, and 11.19% greater, respectively, than that in the CK treatment (Fig. 1b).

Patterns of SOC mineralization rate (a), cumulative mineralization (b), and Ct/SOC ratio (c) during 46 days of incubation under different fertilization treatments. Different letters indicate significant differences between treatments (p < 0.05); error bars represent standard deviation.

As the proportion of organic fertilizer replacing chemical fertilizer increased, the Ct/SOC ratio showed a decreasing trend. In the M treatment, the Ct/SOC was 6.65%, which was 11.48% and 15.80% lower than those in the CK and NP treatments, respectively, whereas the 1/2NPM treatment was 12.64% lower than NP (Fig. 1c). These results suggest that, compared with the sole application of chemical fertilizer, the partial or full replacement of chemical fertilizer with organic fertilizer can enhance soil carbon sequestration capacity.

Effects of replacing chemical fertilizer with organic fertilizer on soil carbon cycle function genes

Fifty carbon cycle functional genes were identified across all soil samples, primarily comprising those involved in carbon fixation (18 genes), carbon degradation (27 genes), and methane metabolism (5 genes) (Table S2). Within soil carbon cycle functional groups, genes associated with carbon fixation exhibited the highest abundance, followed by those related to carbon degradation, while genes linked to methane metabolism displayed the lowest abundance (Fig. 2a). PCoA revealed that fertilization explained most of the variation in the soil carbon cycle functional profiles, with PC1 and PC2 accounting for 58.18% and 9.67% of the total variance, respectively. ANOSIM indicated statistically significant differences in the abundance of carbon cycle-related functional genes among fertilization treatments (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2b).

Kruskal–Wallis tests indicated that fertilization significantly altered the abundance of functional genes related to carbon fixation and carbon degradation (p < 0.05), but had no significant effect on the abundance of genes associated with methane metabolism. Compared with the control (CK), both the NP and 1/2NPM treatments did not significantly affect the abundance of carbon cycle-related functional genes, whereas the M treatment significantly increased the abundance of korA in the reductive tricarboxylic acid (rTCA) cycle, which is involved in CO₂ fixation, and decreased the abundance of pel in the pectin degradation pathway, a key gene in pectin degradation (Figs. 2c and 3). Furthermore, compared with NP, both 1/2NPM and M significantly increased the abundance of coxS (encoding CO oxidase) and decreased the abundance of chi (involved in chitin degradation) (Figs. 2c and 3). Notably, compared with NP, the M treatment significantly increased the abundance of facA (associated with multiple metabolic cycles) while decreasing that of pel (Figs. 2c and 3).

Effects of replacing chemical fertilizer with organic fertilizer on soil carbon cycle microbial communities

The results of microbial community Alpha diversity analysis showed that the carbon cycle microbial community Shannon and Simpson indices differed statistically among the fertilization treatments. The NP treatment significantly reduced the soil carbon cycle microbial community Simpson index compared with the CK treatment. The soil carbon cycle microbial community Shannon index was significantly lower in the M treatment compared with the NP treatment (Fig. 4a, b). The PCoA showed that fertilization treatments significantly affected soil carbon cycle microbial community structure (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4c).

Soil carbon-cycling microbial communities at the genus level under different fertilization treatments: Simpson diversity index (a); Shannon diversity index (b); principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) (c); relative abundance of carbon-cycling genera (d); and genera significantly affected by fertilization (e).

The microbial community abundance calculations showed that the horizontal dominant microbial communities at the genus level of the soil carbon cycle were unclassified_c_Actinomycetia, unclassified_p_Chloroflexi, unclassified_p_Acidobacteria, unclassified_c_Alphaproteobacteria, unclassified_c_Acidobacteriia, and unclassified_f_Streptosporangiaceae (Fig. 4d). Fertilization significantly affected the abundance of soil carbon cycling microbial communities. Compared with the CK treatment, the NP and 1/2NPM treatments significantly decreased the abundance of unclassified_p_Chloroflexi, the 1/2NPM and M treatments significantly increased the abundance of unclassified_c_Alphaproteobacteria, and the M treatment enhanced the abundance of Bradyrhizobium. Compared with the NP treatment, the 1/2NPM and M treatments significantly increased the abundance of unclassified_p_Chloroflexi, while the 1/2NPM and M treatments increased the abundance of unclassified_c_Alphaproteobacteria and Bradyrhizobium, respectively.

A high degree of similarity in both composition and abundance was observed among microbial communities harboring functional genes associated with carbon fixation (korA, coxS, facA) and carbon degradation (pel, chi). This finding suggests that members of the same genus may perform multiple ecological functions within a given ecosystem, and that functional redundancy is likely to occur among different microbial communities (Fig. 5).

Correlation analysis of basic soil properties with carbon cycle functional genes and microbial communities

The Mantel test results showed that soil physicochemical properties exerted differential regulatory effects on carbon-cycling functional genes and microbial communities (Fig. 6). Soil pH and SOC were strongly correlated with the abundances of carbon-fixation and carbon-degradation functional genes, as well as with the composition of carbon-cycling microbial communities (Mantel’s 0.3 < r < 0.5, p < 0.05). A moderate correlation between ROC/SOC and carbon-fixation and carbon-degradation functional genes was observed (Mantel’s 0.3 < r < 0.4, p < 0.05). AP and NO₃−-N were moderately correlated with the composition of soil microbial communities (Mantel’s 0.2 < r < 0.4, p < 0.05). The C/N ratio was strongly correlated with methane-metabolism functional genes (Mantel’s 0.4 < r < 0.5, p < 0.05). Overall, the regulatory patterns of carbon-cycling functional genes and microbial communities were found to differ depending on environmental factors; however, pH and SOC consistently appeared as key drivers of biological processes involved in soil carbon cycling.

Environmental drivers of soil microbial functional genes and taxa. The thickness of the curves corresponds to Mantel’s r values. The color indicates statistical significance. Blue and orange lines represent strong correlations at p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 levels, respectively.

To further elucidate the causal relationships among environmental factors, microbial communities, carbon-cycling functional genes, and SOC mineralization, PLS-PM was conducted (Fig. 7). The model achieved a goodness-of-fit (GoF) value of 0.60. Soil properties (pH, AP, NO₃−–N) were found to positively influence microbial community groups involved in carbon-cycling processes (path coefficient = 0.76, p < 0.01). This community structure was observed to positively regulate carbon-fixation genes (path coefficient = 0.78, p < 0.01) while suppressing carbon-degradation genes (path coefficient = − 0.77, p < 0.001), thereby indirectly promoting SOC sequestration. In addition, the C/N ratio was found to indirectly reduce Ct by positively influencing the Simpson diversity index (path coefficient = − 0.81, p < 0.05).

Discussion

Effect of replacing chemical fertilizer with organic fertilizer on carbon-cycling functional genes

Functional genes, the “molecular tools” used by microorganisms in metabolic processes, are considered indicators of the potential of soil microbial communities in the carbon cycle28. Functional genes associated with the carbon cycle exhibit high sensitivity to environmental gradients, changes in fertilizer type, and agricultural management practices. For example, Du et al.29 reported that chemical fertilizers significantly increased the abundance of genes involved in the degradation of readily decomposable carbon, such as starch and hemicellulose, in peach orchard soils. In contrast, Hu et al.30 demonstrated that organic fertilizers specifically enhanced the abundance of genes responsible for recalcitrant carbon degradation in black soils (Mollisols). However, the abundance of carbon cycle functional genes remained unchanged under the NP treatment, consistent with previous findings20. The soil nitrogen mining hypothesis suggests that the expression of functional genes is governed primarily by the nutrient requirements of soil microorganisms31,32. The NP treatment resulted in markedly higher soil NO3−-N and NH4+-N concentrations, and the ample inorganic nitrogen supply may have reduced microbial regulation of these functional genes. In contrast, the application of organic fertilizer (M) decreased the abundance of the pel gene (encoding pectate lyase) (Figs. 2c and 3), a trend also reported in several previous studies29. Microbial activity responds to varying external conditions by selectively degrading substrates to balance elemental requirements33; hence, the reduction in pel gene abundance is likely attributable to microbial selectivity driven by the composition of the added carbon sources.

Furthermore, the application of organic fertilizer was found to significantly increase SWC, which not only improved the microbial habitat but also likely enhanced the transcriptional activity of multiple carbon-fixation-related genes—including those in the rTCA cycle (korA), the multisystem cycle (facA), and the CO oxidation pathway (coxS)—by alleviating water stress (Fig. S2). This water-mediated regulatory effect is consistent with findings reported by Guo et al.34, who identified soil moisture as a critical environmental factor regulating microbial carbon-fixation functionality. Such increases in water availability can indirectly enhance the potential of soils to act as carbon sinks.

Effects of key environmental factors, microbial community structure, and carbon-cycling functional genes on SOC mineralization

In this study, the SOC mineralization rate during incubation followed a typical three-phase trajectory: an initial rapid decline, followed by a gradual decline, and eventual stabilization. This trajectory is consistent with findings across ecosystems35. This temporal pattern is generally interpreted as indicating a shift in carbon pool availability from labile-carbon dominance to recalcitrant-carbon dominance27.

Fertilization increases the content of organic carbon components, directly influencing the assembly of microbial communities and enhancing their activity, thereby regulating the process of SOC mineralization36. In this study, MBC in fertilization treatments was significantly increased by 23.70%–43.18% compared with the CK treatment. Moreover, MBC exhibited a significant positive correlation with Ct (Table S3), supporting the proposed “nutrient–microbial activation–enhanced mineralization” cascade effect37. Notably, although Ct was generally increased by fertilization, higher Ct and Ct/SOC values were observed in the NP treatment relative to the 1/2NPM and M treatments. Although the 1/2NPM and M treatments contained higher levels of active fractions, the NP treatment resulted in a greater mineralization effect, indicating that a low C/N ratio triggered a microbial “carbon compensation” mechanism38. In this process, r-strategist microbial taxa simultaneously decompose both labile and recalcitrant SOC to meet their carbon requirements. This distinct mineralization pathway is likely associated with the higher microbial community diversity observed under NP treatment39.

PLS-PM analysis showed that a low C/N ratio significantly increased microbial community diversity, thereby driving higher mineralization (p < 0.05) and establishing “stoichiometric stress–community diversification–carbon loss” as the core transmission chain underlying high mineralization. Although the Simpson index did not significantly predict the abundance of carbon fixation and carbon degradation functional genes, taxonomic annotation revealed that many microbial genera harbor multiple carbon-cycling functions, and phylogenetically distinct coexisting microbial groups display a high degree of functional redundancy (Fig. 5). Maron et al.40 suggested that higher microbial community diversity retains a more diverse assemblage of low-redundancy microbial taxa capable of performing specialized carbon decomposition processes41, which are of considerable importance for SOC mineralization. Furthermore, the chemical properties of inorganic fertilizers can destabilize recalcitrant organic carbon, thereby promoting the simultaneous degradation of unstable and recalcitrant organic carbon fractions42.

Soil carbon-cycling microorganisms are regarded as central drivers of biogeochemical fluxes43. Their community structure and activity are key in determining the fate of exogenous carbon within the “microbial carbon pump44. Replacing chemical fertilizers with organic fertilizers simultaneously supplied carbon sources and altered soil pH, AP, and NO₃−-N, thereby reshaping the composition of soil carbon-cycling microbial communities (Fig. 7). This replacement upregulated carbon fixation genes and suppressed carbon degradation genes, which helps maintain the balance between carbon fixation and carbon degradation45, ultimately contributing to potential carbon sequestration, as reflected by a low Ct/SOC ratio. In this process, soil pH played a critical role in regulating the microbial potential for carbon fixation and degradation (Fig. 6). The optimal pH ranges for functional genes related to carbon fixation and those related to carbon degradation differ substantially (Fig. S2). This difference can be attributed to the specific pH preferences of microbial communities that harbor different carbon-cycling functional genes46,47. Previous studies have suggested that the pH range suitable for microbial life is relatively narrow48,49. When pH values range from 4.5 to 8.550, pH has been identified as a principal factor influencing soil microbial community composition and activity51,52, which is consistent with the results of the present study.

AP has been recognized as a common limiting factor in soil carbon cycling53. Mechanistically, P serves as a structural element in energy molecules and metabolic intermediates, such as ATP and phosphoenolpyruvate, and directly determines the flux of the rTCA cycle in autotrophic microorganisms54,55. In this study, AP levels increased by 77.05% and 58.34% in the 1/2NPM and M treatments, respectively, accompanied by concurrent increases in the abundances of the facA and korA carbon-fixation genes, which indicates that alleviation of P limitation enhances carbon-fixation capacity (Fig. 7). Furthermore, NO3−-N, the primary form of mineral nitrogen absorbed and utilized by microorganisms56, has been shown to function as an electron acceptor for autotrophic carbon-fixing microbial populations. Under micro-oxic conditions, NO3− can facilitate denitrification coupled with CO₂ fixation, thereby indirectly increasing carbon input into the “microbial carbon pump”.

Our study offers valuable insights into SOC mineralization and microbial carbon cycling under conditions where organic fertilizers replace chemical fertilizers. Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The SOC mineralization assay employed was based on laboratory incubations, which may not fully reflect mineralization processes under field conditions. Moreover, the metagenomic approach used assesses the genetic potential of microbial communities rather than direct measurements of actual carbon-cycling processes. Therefore, future studies should aim to link in situ field measurements of SOC mineralization and enzyme activity to microbial carbon-cycling functional genes.

Conclusion

This study elucidates changes in SOC mineralization, microbial community structure, and carbon-cycling functional genes under conditions in which organic fertilizers replace chemical fertilizers. By integrating analyses of basic soil properties, functional genes, microbial community composition, and SOC mineralization, fertilization was found to enhance the cumulative mineralization of SOC. Notably, the treatment with chemical fertilizers alone exhibited high mineralization characteristics, whereas the replacement of the chemical fertilizers with organic fertilizers resulted in a decreased cumulative mineralization ratio. The microbial mechanisms underlying these phenomena were investigated from a carbon-cycling perspective. Specifically, no significant effect on soil carbon-cycling functional genes was observed in the NP treatment; however, the low C/N ratio in this treatment environment stimulated greater microbial diversity, which in turn drove “carbon-compensation” mineralization, leading to carbon loss. In contrast, the partial replacement of chemical fertilizers with organic fertilizers increased soil pH, AP, NO₃--N, selectively upregulated carbon fixation genes, and suppressed carbon degradation genes, thereby directing the “microbial carbon pump” toward accumulation and enhancing soil carbon sequestration.
