Optimal lidocaine propofol mixture for painless induction of anesthesia
Authors: Hitham M. A. Elsayed, Roshdi R. Al-Metwalli, Mohammed Abdelraheem, Fawzy Badawy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:To determine the median effective dose (ED50) of 2% lidocaine mixed with propofol for preventing pain during the induction of anesthesia using a modified Dixon’s up-and-down method.Materials and Methods:Thirty ASA (American Society of Anesthesiologists) grade I and II patients, aged 18–60 years, undergoing elective surgery were enrolled. Using the modified Dixon’s up-and-down method, patients received varying doses of lidocaine mixed with 20 mL of propofol MCT/LCT (Medium Chain Triglyceride/Long Chain Triglyceride) emulsion. The primary outcome was the calculation of the ED50 of 2% lidocaine, defined as the midpoint dose at which 50% of patients experienced painless injection. Secondary outcomes included pain scores during injection, heart rate changes, and withdrawal movements.Results:Thirty patients completed the study. The ED50 of lidocaine premixed with 20 mL of propofol MCT/LCT emulsion to prevent pain during the induction of anesthesia was 32.14 mg (95% CI = 32.09 mg–32.18 mg), equivalent to 1.46 mg/mL (95% CI: 1.02 mg/mL–1.91 mg/mL). Demographic and clinical characteristics of patients who experienced pain and those who did not were not statistically significant.Conclusion:Our study concluded that the ED50 of lidocaine premixed with 20 mL of propofol to prevent pain during the induction of anesthesia was 32.14 mg (95% CI = 32.09 mg–32.18 mg), equivalent to 1.46 mg/mL (95% CI: 1.02–1.91 mg/mL).

Introduction

Propofol is considered the intravenous agent of choice for the induction of general anesthesia due to its favorable characteristics, including rapid onset of action, smooth induction, and quick recovery. However, many patients still regard the pain associated with propofol injection as one of the worst perioperative experiences.[1] To address this issue, various strategies have been explored to alleviate this discomfort, including the use of local anesthetics, opioids, ketamine,[2345] tramadol, ondansetron, and even ice gel packs. Additionally, new formulations of propofol have been developed to reduce injection pain.[6789]

Lidocaine has emerged as the most effective adjunct to prevent pain associated with propofol injection and has been extensively investigated, both as a pretreatment with a tourniquet[10] and when mixed directly with propofol.[11] Studies have shown that both lidocaine pretreatment and lidocaine-propofol admixture significantly reduce the incidence of pain by more than 50%.[12] We hypothesize that the median effective dose of lidocaine in the admixture requires further investigation using a simple and reliable modified Dixon’s up-and-down method.

Materials and Methods

This prospective, randomized, double-blind study was conducted at Sohag University Hospital following approval by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Sohag University Hospital (Soh-Med-22-11-15, dated 07-11-2022) and registration at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05898061). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. Patients aged 18 to 60 years, scheduled for elective surgery under general anesthesia, and classified as ASA I or II were included. Exclusion criteria included a history of drug allergy, hepatic or renal impairment, cardiac problems, neurologic deficits, psychiatric disorders, or preoperative anxiety requiring sedation.

Patients were not premedicated. In the operating room, a 20-gauge venous catheter was inserted into a vein on the dorsum of the non-dominant hand. Continuous monitoring of pulse oximetry, electrocardiography, noninvasive blood pressure, and end-tidal carbon dioxide was applied from induction to the end of surgery. To ensure blinding, patients were informed that pain might occur during anesthetic injection but were unaware of the administered medication. One anesthetist prepared the propofol-lidocaine mixture by drawing 20 mL of propofol MCT/LCT emulsion (Propofol Lipuro 1%, B. Braun, Germany) into a 50 mL syringe (Inspire, Alshifa Medical Products Co.). A predetermined dose of 2% lidocaine (DBK Pharma, Egypt) was added, and the total volume was adjusted to 20 mL by discarding any excess. A second anesthetist, blinded to the lidocaine dose, injected the mixture at a fixed rate of 20 mL/min using a syringe pump (Injectomat® Agilia, Fresenius Kabi, France) and assessed pain during injection. A third anesthetist, also blinded to the dose, managed pre-oxygenation and anesthetic administration. The injection was discontinued upon the loss of the eyelash reflex, indicating unconsciousness. In the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU), patients were queried about any recall of pain during induction to detect late-onset pain.

The verbal analog scale (VAS; 0 = no pain, 10 = intense pain) was explained preoperatively, and patients were asked to rate their pain intensity. The lidocaine dose in the propofol-lidocaine mixture for each patient was determined using the response of the previously tested patient, following a modified Dixon’s up-and-down method with a step size of 0.5 mL (10 mg).[13] The initial dose of 10 mg lidocaine for the first patient was based on prior studies,[1415] which demonstrated successful prevention of propofol injection pain in some cases. If a patient reported pain, the next patient received 10 mg more lidocaine; if no pain was reported, the dose was decreased by 10 mg. This process was repeated until seven crossover midpoints were obtained. The median effective dose (ED50) of lidocaine, resulting in painless propofol injection in 50% of patients, was calculated as the midpoint of all independent pairs of doses after achieving seven crossover points.

Our primary outcome was to calculate the ED50 of 2% lidocaine added to 20 mL of propofol MCT/LCT emulsion to prevent pain during injection using the Dixon up-and-down sequential method. Secondary outcomes included pain scores during injection and recall in the recovery room (VAS; 0 = no pain, 10 = intense pain), heart rate changes, withdrawal movements, demographic data, type and duration of surgery, and time to unconsciousness. Any adverse reactions, such as toxicity or local venipuncture site reactions, were monitored and reported.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to calculate the mathematic mean (SD) of demographic, surgical, and other continuous data. Unpaired t-test was used to compare continuous data and Chi-Square Test for categorical variables. Two-sided P values of less than 0.05 were considered to be significant. The Dixon’s Up-and-Down method was used to calculate ED50. We recorded the initial dose of each patient according to the previous patient’s response and reported the response whether pain or no pain. After plotting the Dixon’s Up and Down curve, we determined 7 cross-over points from “pain” to “no pain” [Figure 1]. We calculated the midpoint for each cross-over. ED50 was calculated as the arithmetic mean of all midpoints. The ED50 of lidocaine for preventing pain during the injection of 20 mL propofol MCT/LCT emulsion was 32.14 mg (95% CI: 32.09 mg–32.18 mg), equivalent to 1.46 mg/mL (95% CI: 1.02 mg/mL–1.91 mg/mL). Lidocaine was effective in 15 cases (no pain) and ineffective in 15 cases, with a mean VAS score of 4.06 ± 0.96 for the prevention of injection pain. Only five of the 15 patients who experienced pain during propofol injection recalled this pain after being transferred to the PACU (mean VAS score = 5.09 ± 1.14). No adverse reactions, such as toxic effects or local reactions at the venipuncture site, were observed. Hemodynamics remained stable for all patients during the induction of general anesthesia, and no vasoactive drugs were required

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the median effective dose (ED50) of 2% lidocaine, when premixed with 20 mL of propofol MCT/LCT emulsion to prevent pain during the induction of anesthesia, is 32.14 mg (95% CI = 32.09 mg–32.18 mg). This dose is equivalent to 1.46 mg/mL (95% CI: 1.02 mg/mL–1.91 mg/mL).

Lidocaine can be used to prevent propofol injection pain through two techniques: pretreatment or mixing with propofol.[1011] A systematic review by Euasobhon et al.,[12] which included 72 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with 8,845 participants, confirmed that both lidocaine admixture and pretreatment significantly reduce injection pain, with no major difference in efficacy between the two methods. Scott et al.[16] further observed that mixing lidocaine with propofol was more effective than pretreatment in reducing pain at lower doses. Several studies have identified lidocaine doses of 30 mg–40 mg mixed with 20 mL of propofol as the most effective range for pain prevention, which aligns with our finding of 32 mg as the ED50.[111517]

Unlike previous studies, we added lidocaine as the only diluent to avoid altering the pH and aqueous state of propofol. The addition of saline could alter the pH and aqueous state of propofol, potentially compromising the results.[18] In our study, the admixture was administered immediately after preparation to mitigate the possible risk of pulmonary embolism due to oil droplet formation, which has been shown to occur only 30 min after mixing lidocaine and propofol.[19] Additionally, our study uniquely employed a constant injection speed of 20 mL/min to control the impact of injection rate on propofol-induced immediate or delayed pain.[20] Since the loss of consciousness from propofol can interfere with delayed pain perception, the infusion was stopped as soon as unconsciousness was achieved.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to use the Dixon sequential method to determine the ED50 of lidocaine mixed with 20 mL of propofol. This method effectively concentrates experimental doses near the effective response rate, optimizing sample size and accuracy.[13]

Limitations

While the Dixon up-and-down sequential method proved to yield accurate results with a small sample size—which was our primary outcome—it does not provide sufficient evidence for the upper tail of the data distribution. This makes it challenging to determine the ED95 accurately, which will be the goal of our future study. The study was conducted at a single center, which may limit its external validity. Although covariates such as age, sex, and patient demographics—factors that may influence propofol pharmacokinetics and pain perception—were not stratified in this study, there were no significant differences in patient demographics or clinical characteristics between the effective and ineffective groups [Table 1].

Conclusion

This study concludes that the median effective dose (ED50) of 2% lidocaine, when premixed with 20 mL of propofol MCT/LCT emulsion to prevent propofol injection pain during the induction of anesthesia, is 32.14 mg (95% CI = 32.09 mg–32.18 mg), equivalent to 1.46 mg/mL (95% CI: 1.02 mg/mL–1.91 mg/mL). This finding provides a practical approach to achieving painless induction with propofol. Further research is needed to determine the ED95, explore patient-specific factors, and validate these results in larger, multicenter trials.
