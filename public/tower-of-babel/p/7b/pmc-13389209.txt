Sterically-extended asymmetric conjugated hole-selective layer for perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells
Authors: Tingxia Yan, Chenxu He, Chi Li, Xinhua Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbazole-based self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) were widely adopted as hole-selective layers (HSLs) and recombination junction material in perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells (P/S-TSCs), yet it remains challenging to simultaneously suppress excessive molecular aggregation and maintain continuous charge-transport pathways, particularly on textured silicon substrates. Herein, we report series of sterically extended asymmetric carbazole-based SAMs, 4-(11H-benzo[a]carbazol-11-yl)benzoic acid (BABCz), 4-(9H-dibenzo[a,c]carbazol-9-yl)benzoic acid (BADBCz), and 4-(3’,6’-dimethoxy-9H-[3,9’-bicarbazol]−9-yl)benzoic acid (MeO-BADCz), in which MeO-BADCz engineers to construct a continuous three-dimensional C–H···π-mediated charge-transport network while effectively restraining long-range crystallization. The MeO-BADCz monolayer combines strong anchoring to substrate, pronounced work-function modulation, and efficient defect passivation at the buried interface, enabling pinhole-free, highly crystalline wide-bandgap (1.68 eV) perovskite films with suppressed non-radiative recombination. Through these advancements, the optimized wide-bandgap perovskite solar cells (PSCs) incorporating MeO-BADCz as the HSL deliver a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 24.05% with negligible hysteresis and retain 97.1% of their initial efficiency after 1000 h of continuous operation under ISOS-L-1 protocol. When implemented as the recombination layer on textured silicon heterojunction (SHJ), MeO-BADCz further enables P/S-TSCs with a certified PCE of 33.04% and robust stability under damp-heat-light conditions. This work demonstrates a steric-engineering concept for asymmetric carbazole-based SAMs and highlights texture-tolerant multidimensional charge-transport networks as a key motif for scalable tandem photovoltaics.

Introduction

Perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells (P/S-TSCs) represent one of the most promising pathways for next-generation photovoltaics, as their complementary light absorption and reduced thermalization losses enable power conversion efficiencies (PCE) beyond the Shockley-Queisser limit1, with certified efficiencies above 34%2. Nevertheless, severe non-radiative recombination losses3, charge-transport mismatch4, and insufficient interfacial stability between the perovskite and crystalline silicon (c-Si) remain fundamental barriers that hamper further efficiency advancement and large-scale deployment5.

Self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) have emerged as a recombination junction material facilitating the tremendous strides in the tandem community6,7. As the functional junction coupling the two sub-cells, the SAMs-functionalized interconnecting layer, critical in work function (WF) modulation8,9, minimizes energy losses10,11 and provides a chemically robust interface to support high-quality perovskite growth12,13. However, commonly used symmetric carbazole-based SAMs, such as PACz series, suffer from inherent structural constraints, including a rigid π-conjugated backbone, limited anchoring functionality, weak intermolecular coupling, and inadequate steric modulation14–16. These structural deficiencies often induce unfavorable aggregation, hindering the formation of uniform, pinhole-free molecular layers17 and resulting in poor electronic contact and inefficient hole extraction, accompanied by pronounced non-radiative recombination at the perovskite/SAMs interface18–20. This problem is further amplified on rough substrates like the silicon heterojunction (SHJ) solar cell, leading to insufficient carrier extraction and energy losses in tandem device21–23. To overcome these issues, recent strategies have focused on molecular asymmetry design to tailor dipole orientation and suppress excessive aggregation24,25, as well as introducing functional substituents such as methoxy or methylthio groups to passivate interfacial traps and enhance carrier mobility26,27. Although these approaches have shown notable efficacy, the fundamental understanding of how molecular configuration governs aggregation behavior and charge-transport continuity remains elusive. Specifically, a clear and universal design principle remains absent for simultaneously suppressing excessive molecular aggregation while preserving continuous and efficient charge-transport pathways.

Herein, we rationally designed and synthesized three asymmetric carbazole-based molecules: 4-(11H-benzo[a]carbazol-11-yl)benzoic acid (BABCz), 4-(9H-dibenzo[a,c]carbazol-9-yl)benzoic acid (BADBCz), and 4-(3′,6′-dimethoxy-9H-[3,9′-bicarbazol]−9-yl)benzoic acid (MeO-BADCz) to systematically investigate the interplay between aggregation behavior and charge-transport efficiency. By introducing asymmetric steric hindrance, we precisely modulated molecular conformation and torsional angles to dictate their crystalline packing motifs. MeO-BADCz adopts a triple-segment twisted conformation via its spatially extended conjugated structure. This unique architecture not only suppresses detrimental aggregation but also promotes the interdigitation of functional groups, thereby enhancing spatial efficiency and charge delocalization. These synergistic effects, combined with strengthened molecular anchoring and interfacial passivation, collectively contribute to superior device performance. Consequently, optimized MeO-BADCz-based wide-bandgap (1.68 eV) perovskite solar cells (PSCs) delivered a remarkable PCE of 24.05% and retained 97% of their initial efficiency after 1000 h under ISOS-L-1 protocol. Additionally, MeO-BADCz-functionalized recombination layers were extended to textured SHJ solar cells for fabricating P/S-TSCs, achieving a certified PCE of 33.04%, while maintaining excellent photostability. This work provides key insights into the balancing mechanism between aggregation behavior and charge transport.

Results

Molecular properties and thin-film aggregation

Molecular structures of BABCz, BADBCz, and MeO-BADCz were illustrated in Fig. 1a–c. The synthesis route followed several key transformations, including amine-protecting group manipulation, Buchwald-Hartwig cross-coupling, Ullmann coupling, and alkaline hydrolysis, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1. Their structures were confirmed by Mass, 1H/13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (Supplementary Figs. 52–67), and element analysis. The thermogravimetric (TG) curves in Supplementary Fig. 2 show that all three molecules possess good thermal stability, with decomposition temperatures higher than that of the perovskite. To elucidate the structure-property relationship, the three molecules were optimized using density functional theory (DFT). The electron density distributions of BABCz, BADBCz, and MeO-BADCz were revealed by electrostatic potential (ESP) analysis, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. The negative ESP regions were primarily located in the carboxyl anchoring groups for all molecules. However, in MeO-BADCz, the methoxy substitution introduces an additional significant negative ESP region, yielding a substantially larger molecular dipole moment compared with BABCz and BADBCz28. A larger dipole moment facilitates a greater downshift of the ITO WF upon SAM deposition, thereby reducing charge transport barriers. Compared with BABCz, BADBCz showed a slight HOMO downshift from −5.60 to −5.62 eV due to enhanced π-delocalization29 whereas MeO-BADCz exhibited improved photoelectrical stability arising from sterically extended conjugation and more efficient charge delocalization, with a HOMO of −5.48 eV and a LUMO of −1.40 eV. According to the CV measurements, the HOMO levels of BABCz, BADBCz, and MeO-BADCz were −5.38, −5.49, and −5.13 eV, respectively. The optical band gaps, derived from the absorption onset, were 3.17 eV for BABCz, 3.05 eV for BADBCz, and 3.11 eV for MeO-BADCz. Consequently, the corresponding LUMO levels were calculated to be −2.21 eV, −2.44 eV and −2.02 eV, as detailed in Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5, showing a consistent trend with DFT. The HOMO energy levels are well delocalized across the MeO-BADCz molecular skeleton, suggesting an extended conjugated system that facilitates enhanced hole transport properties30. The electrochemical stability of the SAM molecules was evaluated through repeated CV scanning (Fig. 1d–f). With increasing CV cycles, the current density of BABCz and BADBCz decreased markedly, whereas MeO-BADCz showed only slight variations, indicating its superior electrochemical stability31,32. And the CV curves at different scan rates also reveal the same trend, in Supplementary Fig. 6. We evaluated the thermal and photostability of the SAM molecules and exposed them to 1 sun equivalent illumination for 24 h. Under photo-thermal aging, the three synthesized SAMs showed superior stability owing to their conjugated structures (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8), while the commercial 4PACz exhibited the most pronounced color change and minor impurity peaks in the 1H-NMR spectra (Supplementary Fig. 9).

To obtain the particle size distribution in the SAM precursor solution, dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were conducted. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, BABCz exhibited a broader particle size distribution (173–380 nm) compared to BADBCz (106–208 nm). In contrast, the non-planar, twisted π-conjugated structure of MeO-BADCz resulted in a significantly narrower size distribution (36–67 nm), thereby facilitating more uniform molecular dispersion on the ITO surface and enabling the formation of a homogeneous HSL upon annealing20,33. To further investigate the aggregation properties of the SAM-based assembled molecular layers, grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) was employed to evaluate the crystallinity of the three molecules (Fig. 1g–i). The GIWAXS patterns (Supplementary Fig. 11) reveal that BABCz exhibits discrete Bragg spots, indicative of highly ordered and well-oriented crystallinity34. BADBCz exhibits moderate crystallinity, whereas MeO-BADCz shows the weakest crystalline features, indicating that steric hindrance more effectively suppresses molecular aggregation35. To directly address the concern regarding molecular aggregation and distribution, we performed additional LAMMPS simulations to compare the behavior of BABCz, BADBCz, and MeO-BADCz36. A 126.5 Å × 126.5 Å molecular model of the ITO surface was constructed, with 200 BABCz, BADBCz, or MeO-BADCz molecules randomly and uniformly distributed on the surface (Supplementary Fig. 12). Figure 1j–l shows the molecular dynamics (MD) simulation snapshots from the initial configuration to 1000 ps of simulation time. Benefiting from its extended steric hindrance, MeO-BADCz exhibited pronounced conformational self-adaptation, which contributed to more uniform and stable surface coverage.

Stacking patterns

To elucidate the molecular structure changes that affect intermolecular interactions and stacking modes, the single crystal structures of BABCz (CCDC: 2506060), BADBCz (CCDC: 2506071), and MeO-BADBCz (CCDC: 2470837) were obtained via slow solvent evaporation from CH2Cl2. It is noteworthy that for BABCz, the compound crystallizes in the monoclinic space group C2/c, with one independent molecule in the asymmetric unit along with one dichloromethane solvent molecule, and the crystallographic parameters were summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The tracking arrangements of BABCz, BADBCz, and MeO-BADCz within their individual unit cells, and include views along the a-, b-, and c-axes were shown in Supplementary Fig. 13. As shown in Fig. 2a, the BABCz adopted a highly twisted π-skeleton, in which the 11H-benzo[c]carbazole (BCz) core exhibited a dihedral angle of 169.9° (Supplementary Fig. 14). Additionally, a pronounced twist of 61.849° was observed between the BCz moiety and the phenyl ring, giving rise to a T-shaped stacking motif in the solid state. In this geometry, parallel neighboring molecules displayed a longitudinal slip of 6.674 Å, indicating insufficient π–π overlap for conventional interactions37. Nevertheless, the twisted architecture enabled multiple C–H···π contacts, including 3.049 Å between BCz units (red line) and 3.339 Å between the phenyl and BCz core (orange line, Fig. 2b), 3.187 Å between BCz groups, 3.784 Å between phenyl and BCz core (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 15), and 3.361 Å in the fishbone-like arrangement (Fig. 2d). Upon substitution with 9H-dibenzo[a,c]carbazole (DBCz), the dihedral angle increases substantially to 89.816° (Fig. 2e), accompanied by an enlarged slip distance of 8.580 Å (Fig. 2f). Under this conformation, the intermolecular C–H···π interactions in BADBCz become significantly restricted, as evidenced in the parallel-neighboring dimer where the measured distances were 4.062 Å and 6.390 Å (Fig. 2f, g), both of which exceed typical π-interaction ranges38,39. A shorter T-shaped edge-to-face C–H···π interaction of 3.466 Å was still present (Fig. 2h). Compared with BABCz, BADBCz showed reduced aggregation; however, this suppression comes at the cost of weakened charge-transport pathways, ultimately compromising its functional performance. In MeO-BADCz, as shown in Fig. 2i, the dihedral angles between the carbazole and phenyl rings (68.728°), between the 3,6-dimethoxycarbazole (MeOCz) and phenyl rings (60.543°), demonstrated a three-dimensionally extended conjugation. This conformation allowed a greater diversity of dimer geometries than those observed in BABCz and BADBCz. For instance, the parallel-neighboring dimer exhibits C–H···π interactions of 3.202 Å (MeOCz, orange line) and 3.578 Å between the carbazole and phenyl rings (red line, Fig. 2j), the sterically extended conjugated framework induced a longitudinal slip distance of 8.139 Å (Fig. 2j). In the packing model (Fig. 2k), all three planar segments of MeO-BADCz participate in significant C–H···π contacts (3.511 Å, 3.571 Å, 3.177 Å). The vertical-shear-type dimer (Fig. 2l) features a 3.588 Å C–H···π interaction, while another parallel-neighboring dimer (Fig. 2m) shows a 3.534 Å interaction between carbazole cores, in addition to a neighboring MeOCz–MeOCz distance of 4.174 Å (blue line). Moreover, these dimers interlaced like the fingers of two hands along the a-axis in Fig. 2m and Supplementary Fig. 16. Compared with BABCz and BADBCz, MeO-BADCz not only exhibited the most effective suppression of aggregation but also formed more efficient and continuous pathways for charge transport (Fig. 2n, Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). Such a three-dimensional charge-percolation network suggested its strong potential for efficient charge extraction on textured silicon substrates. The single-carrier devices with the structure of ITO/SAMs/MoOx/Ag were fabricated to elucidate the hole transport capabilities of BABCz, BADBCz, and MeO-BADCz, as illustrated in the Supplementary Fig. 19. The device based on MeO-BADCz demonstrates the most pronounced ohmic behavior, which is related to its more effective C-H···π interactions.

Multifunctional modulation at HSL/perovskite interface

To investigate the anchoring adsorption of SAMs on the ITO substrate, the adsorption energies (Eads) for BABCz, BADBCz, and MeO-BADCz on the ITO substrate were simulated using DFT (Supplementary Fig. 20 and Supplementary Note 1). The values were −1.79, −1.74, and −3.36 eV, respectively, demonstrating the substantially stronger interfacial affinity of MeO-BADCz. The interactions were confirmed by high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (HR-XPS), which revealed a more pronounced shift in the In 3d binding energy at the ITO/MeO-BADCz interface compared to ITO/BABCz, further confirming stronger interfacial binding (Fig. 3a). To probe the spatial distribution of SAMs on ITO, atomic force microscopy-infrared spectroscopy (AFM-IR) was employed to map the -COOH groups on ITO. As shown in Fig. 3b–d and Supplementary Fig. 21, BADBCz showed significant signal attenuation, whereas MeO-BADCz maintained strong infrared signals before and after rinsing, indicating stronger adsorption and improved interfacial stability on the substrate. On the textured silicon (c-Si) substrates (Supplementary Fig. 22), MeO-BADCz also exhibited a more intense and uniform IR response than BABCz, indicating that it achieves good conformal coverage on both planar ITO and textured silicon. Meanwhile, charge density difference analysis provides direct visualization of the enhanced interaction at the electronic structure level. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 23, pronounced electron accumulation was observed between the carboxylate oxygen atoms and the ITO surface for MeO-BADCz, accompanied by electron depletion in the O-H bond region of the carboxylic acid. Figure 3e illustrates the differential charge density distribution of the optimized ITO/SAM systems, along with the corresponding line profile perpendicular to the differential charge density model. Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurements were performed to determine the WF distribution. The WF of the tip was calibrated using a standard gold sample with a known WF of 5.1 eV (Fig. 3f–h, Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Note 2). The average WF increased from 4.72 eV for bare ITO to 4.94 eV for BADBCz-treated ITO, 5.07 eV for BABCz-treated ITO, and 5.12 eV for MeO-BADCz-treated ITO. The highest WF achieved by MeO-BADCz can be attributed to its larger molecular dipole moment, favoring efficient hole extraction. The interfacial energy-level alignment of perovskite films deposited on different SAMs was further characterized by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy, with the corresponding results shown in Supplementary Figs. 25–27. For the MeO-BADBCz-based sample, a smaller band offset between the HOMO level of the SAM and the valence band maximum (VBM) of the perovskite was identified, which was beneficial for hole selectivity and for reducing interfacial non-radiative recombination, consistent with the results of KPFM. For textured c-Si substrates, MeO-BADCz deposited on c-Si/ITO exhibited a much lower CPD value (−516 mV) than c-Si/ITO/BADBCz (−242 mV) (Supplementary Fig. 28a–c), indicating more favorable energy-level alignment and reduced interfacial barriers for charge extraction. The variation in CPD (ΔCPD) was further analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 28d), yielding values of 132 mV for BADCz and 75 mV for MeO-BADCz, which reflect the uniformity of molecular coverage on the textured surface. The smaller ΔCPD of MeO-BADCz suggests more conformal coverage over textured c-Si, facilitating Ohmic contact formation, consistent with the behavior observed on planar ITO substrates.

The contact angles of the perovskite precursor solution (Supplementary Fig. 29) showed improved wettability on MeO-BADCz-modified ITO (~ 79.8°), compared to BABCz (~84.6°) and BADBCz (~86.8°), favoring uniform perovskite deposition and high-quality crystallization40. Perovskite films were then deposited on SAM-modified ITO to evaluate the influence on film crystallization. The average grain sizes were 286 nm (BADCz), 302 nm (BABCz), and 382 nm (MeO-BADCz), indicating a substantial improvement for MeO-BADCz-based perovskite film (Supplementary Fig. 30). Examination of the buried interfaces revealed few pinholes for BABCz and BADBCz, whereas MeO-BADCz films were nearly pinhole-free (Supplementary Fig. 31). AFM and X-ray diffraction (XRD) revealed the decreased roughness and stronger diffraction peak for MeO-BADCz-based perovskite film, indicating improved crystallization quality (Supplementary Figs. 32 and 33). The smoother buried interface was beneficial for improved contact between the perovskite film and the HSL, resulting in efficient hole extraction. To assess the passivation capability of MeO-BADCz, XPS analysis revealed a shift of the Pb 4f peaks to lower binding energies compared to BABCz and BADBCz in Supplementary Fig. 34. This shift could be attributed to the electron-donating methoxy group in MeO-BADCz41,42. Furthermore, after mixing intermediate C4 with lead iodide, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy showed a 5 cm−1 downshift in the characteristic C-O peak in Supplementary Fig. 35, confirming the interaction43. Additionally, the defect formation energies of perovskites with and without MeO-BADCz were calculated (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 36). The results showed that the defect formation energies for PbI, IPb, VI, and VPb at the perovskite/MeO-BADCz interface were 2.87, 3.64, 5.26, and 4.83 eV, respectively. The formation energy of the PbI anti-site defect in the MeO-BADCz system was significantly higher than that in the bare perovskite, indicating that MeO-BADCz effectively passivates lead-related defects in the 3D perovskite film. The CPD measured by KPFM showed that the buried surface of the perovskite in contact with MeO-BADCz exhibits a lower surface potential (−315 mV) than those on BADBCz (−224 mV) and BABCz (−276 mV) (Fig. 3j–l). This suggested a more p-type interface character and the formation of upward band bending. Such band bending facilitates the extraction of photogenerated holes to the HSL while repelling electrons from the HSL/perovskite interface, thereby reducing charge recombination loss44.

In addition, in-situ time-dependent steady-state PL was conducted to trace the photo-induced phase segregation of wide-bandgap perovskite dynamically under laser illumination (365 nm CW laser) in N2 atmosphere. The SAM/perovskite samples were measured every 5 min over a total duration of 85 min (Supplementary Fig. 37). The perovskite film deposited on MeO-BADCz exhibited the smallest degree of photoluminescence decay and a relatively low extent of phase segregation. In contrast, the BADBCz-based film shows pronounced PL decay and more severe phase segregation, with the PL peak shifting from 739 to 733 nm, corresponding to a blue shift of 6 nm (Supplementary Fig. 37a). This behavior is likely attributable to the limited passivation capability and poor electrochemical stability of BADBCz, which result in a nonuniform chemical distribution and a higher density of defects in the perovskite film, thereby accelerating PL decay. Although no obvious phase segregation is observed for BABCz, its luminescence intensity decreases markedly under ultraviolet irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 37b). These results confirm that MeO-BADCz can effectively suppress phase segregation in wide-bandgap perovskites while efficiently passivating defects, which is consistent with the DFT results for defect formation energies.

Devices performance for p-i-n WBG single junction cells

Based on our investigation into the asymmetric SAMs and their interactions with ITO and perovskite, we applied them as HSL to fabricate inverted wide-bandgap PSCs, with an architecture of ITO/HSL/Perovskite/LiF/C60/BCP/Ag in Fig. 4a. The BADBCz-based champion device achieved a PCE of 21.97%, with a Voc of 1.202 V, a Jsc of 21.89 mA cm⁻², and an FF of 83.48% in Fig. 4b. When BABCz was used as the HSL, device performance improved to 22.92% (Fig. 4c) in reverse scan. The best performance was achieved with MeO-BADCz, which combines enhanced interfacial charge transfer and superior perovskite quality. The corresponding champion device exhibited a PCE of 24.05% (Voc = 1.261 V, Jsc = 22.13 mA cm⁻², FF = 86.18%) in reverse scan, and a PCE of 23.89% (Voc = 1.255 V, Jsc = 22.14 mA cm⁻², FF = 85.97%) in forward scan, resulting in a negligible hysteresis index (HI) of only 0.06% (Fig. 4d). To our knowledge, achieving a champion PCE of 24.05% represents one of the highest values in wide-bandgap PSCs (Supplementary Fig. 38 and Supplementary Table 2). The stabilized PCE and current density achieved with the PSC based on MeO-BADCz surpassed those of BADBCz and BABCz (Fig. 4e), consistent with overall performance trends. This enhancement was further supported by the EQE results (Fig. 4f), which showed integrated current densities of 20.91, 21.17, and 21.35 mA cm⁻² for BADBCz, BABCz, and MeO-BADCz devices, respectively, consistent with the J–V derived JSC values within a 5% deviation. To verify the performance advantage of MeO-BADCz, the statistical distributions of performance parameters were illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 39 and Supplementary Table 3. The standard deviation measured for MeO-BADCz-based devices was less than that observed in the devices utilizing BABCz and BADBCz, indicating excellent reproducibility of the devices. Moreover, compared with representative commercial SAMs, MeO-BADBCz outperformed both 4PADCB and MeO-2PACz (Supplementary Fig. 40). Comprehensive stability studies were then carried out in accordance with the stability protocols for PSCs. Under the ISOS-D-2 protocol (65 °C, 50% RH, with day/night cycles), the encapsulated devices based on MeO-BADCz demonstrated superior stability, retaining 90.5% of their initial PCE (Fig. 4g), which significantly surpasses that of BABCz (80.1%) and BADBCz (65.3%). The statistical distribution of the stability results has been provided by error bars in the stability plots. MeO-BADCz exhibits the highest normalized PCE retention while maintaining the highest absolute PCE during the aging process (Supplementary Fig. 41). In addition, the relatively smaller variation observed for MeO-BADCz indicates a better reproducibility. Operational stability was assessed under a nitrogen atmosphere following the ISOS-L-1 guidelines (unencapsulated, continuous 1 sun illumination) (Fig. 4h). The devices employing MeO-BADCz retained 97.1% of their initial PCE after 1000 h, substantially outperforming BABCz (90.5%) and BADBCz (85%). This outstanding operational stability arises from the superior electrochemical robustness of MeO-BADCz and the high-quality perovskite films it enables. This result establishes that a well-designed carboxylic acid-based SAM can achieve benchmark efficiency and stability, thereby challenging the conventional view that phosphonic acid anchors are inherently superior.

Charge dynamics

To further investigate the influence of sterically-extended asymmetric SAM on carrier dynamics in perovskite films, steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements were conducted. As shown in Fig. 5a, the MeO-BADCz-based perovskite film exhibits a lower PL peak intensity and more pronounced quenching than BABCz and BADBCz, indicating more efficient extraction of photogenerated holes45. This conclusion could be further elucidated by TRPL measurements (Fig. 5b). Specifically, the average carrier lifetime (τave) decreases from 331.38 ns for BADBCz films to 268.85 ns upon BABCz, while MeO-BADCz shows a shorter lifetime of 145.77 ns, evidencing faster charge carrier extraction46. Transient photovoltage (TPV) and transient photocurrent (TPC) measurements were performed to investigate charge recombination and extraction. As shown in Fig. 5c, the MeO-BADCz-based device demonstrated the longest TPV decay lifetime (1.20 ms), followed by BABCz (1.11 ms) and BADBCz (1.09 ms). Similarly, TPC measurements revealed a faster photocurrent decay in the MeO-BADCz device (3.562 µs) compared with BABCz and BADBCz (3.457 and 3.982 µs, respectively, Supplementary Fig. 42), further confirming its more efficient charge-extraction capability. These results collectively confirmed that the construction of a MeO-BADCz hole transport channel promotes carrier extraction and reduces non-radiative recombination losses at the perovskite/SAM interface17. To quantify the non-radiative recombination losses at the SAM-perovskite interface, the photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) was measured to estimate the quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS). To verify the trap density at the buried interface of the treated perovskite films, capacitance–voltage (C–V) measurements were conducted on devices based on BADBCz, BABCz, and MeO-BADCz under varying bias voltages. Figure 5d shows the drive-level capacitance profiling (DLCP) curves derived from the C–V characteristics47. A substantial reduction in buried-interface (SAM side) trap density was observed for the optimized devices, with the MeO-BADCz-based device exhibiting the lowest value, indicating its superior passivation capability. In contrast, the trap density in the upper part of the perovskite layer showed little variation among the different substrates, suggesting that their influence is primarily confined to the buried interface region27. Built-in potentials (Vbi) were evaluated using Mott–Schottky measurements (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Note 3), revealing values of 1.226 V for MeO-BADCz, 1.183 V for BABCz, and 1.149 V for BADBCz. The highest Vbi achieved with MeO-BADCz indicates more favorable interfacial band alignment and a stronger internal electric field for charge extraction. Consistent with this, Nyquist plots (Fig. 5f) were fitted using an equivalent circuit model, yielding recombination resistance (Rrec) values of 3782, 3217, and 2290 Ω cm⁻² for MeO-BADCz, BABCz, and BADBCz, respectively. The corresponding series resistance (Rs) values were 9.4, 11.2, and 12.7 Ω cm⁻². The combination of the highest Rrec and the lowest Rs in the MeO-BADCz-based device reflects its continuous three-dimensional charge-transport network, which promotes efficient carrier transport and suppresses recombination, ultimately enhancing both FF and VOC.

Photovoltaic performance of a tandem configuration

To determine the efficacy of sterically-extended asymmetric as the recombination junction material in P/S-TSCs depicted schematically in Fig. 6a, we fabricated serially connected tandem photovoltaic devices using double-CZ c-Si bottom cells with submicron-pyramid texture structures in Supplementary Fig. 44. The champion PCE for the BADBCz-based devices was 31.04%, with Voc at 1.894 V, Jsc at 20.51 mA cm−2 and FF at 79.91% in Supplementary Fig. 45. Consistent with the trend observed in single-junction PSCs, MeO-BADCz delivered the best overall performance when employed as the HSL in the recombination junction in the tandem configuration. The MeO-BADCz-based tandem device achieved an impressive PCE of 33.53%, Voc of 1.968 V, Jsc of 20.76 mA cm−2, and FF of 82.07%. Furthermore, this tandem configuration has been submitted to a third-party institution (CPVT) for certification, with the resulting PCE certified at 33.04% efficiency (VOC = 1.960 V, JSC = 20.66 mA/cm2, FF = 81.58%) (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 46). To our knowledge, the achieved PCE of 33.04% is among the highest certified reports for state-of-the-art P/S-TSCs48, underscoring the significant effectiveness of sterically extended asymmetric strategy. Notably, this device exhibited a steady-state efficiency of 33.32% and current density of 19.15 mA cm−2 at 1.74 V, surpassing the performance of BABCz-based and BADBCz-based tandems (Supplementary Fig. 47). The EQE and reflection spectra of the tandem device show integrated photocurrents of 20.77 and 20.78 mA cm−2 for the two sub-cells, respectively, indicating well-matched currents. A comprehensive stability study was conducted to determine the potential of P/S-TSCs applications. Discussion

In summary, three carbazole-based molecules—BABCz, BADBCz, and MeO-BADCz—were designed via an asymmetric conjugation strategy to tune molecular packing. BABCz forms favorable aggregates that establish efficient charge transport channels, primarily mediated by C–H···π interactions. In contrast, BADBCz exhibits loose molecular aggregation, which limits charge transport and functionality. Among them, MeO-BADCz reconciles a triple-segment twisted backbone and methoxy-substituted bicarbazole core, generating a three-dimensional C–H···π-linked percolation network, while strong anchoring, favorable work-function tuning, and improved wettability yield smooth, defect-lean buried interfaces. These results provide a clear and universal design principle that remains absent for simultaneously suppressing excessive molecular aggregation while preserving continuous and efficient charge-transport pathways and offering a practical guideline for next-generation texture-compatible SAM materials in tandem photovoltaics.

Methods

Materials

For the synthesis of the target compound, the following materials are commercially available. 9H-Dibenzo[a,c]carbazole (97%), 11H-Benzo[c]carbazole (97%), 3,6-Dimethoxy-9H-carbazole (98%), tert-butyldimethylsilyl chloride (1 M in THF), tetrabutylammonium fluoride (97%), ethyl p-iodobenzoate (98%), and 1,2-dichlorobenzene (99.99%, anhydrous), Pd(OAc)2 (47% Pd) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. These materials were used directly without further purification. For the fabrication of perovskite, formamidinium iodide (FAI) and methylammonium bromide (MABr) were synthesized by reacting formamidine acetate (Aladdin) and hydroiodic acid (57 wt% in H2O, Aldrich), methylamine (30–33 wt% in ethanol, Aladdin), and hydrobromic acid (48 wt% in H2O, J&K), respectively. Cesium iodide (99.9%), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%, anhydrous), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9 %, anhydrous), and ethyl acetate (EA, 99.8%, anhydrous) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Ethanol (≥99.7%, anhydrous) was purchased from China National Pharmaceutical Group Corporation. Lead iodide (99.998%) was purchased from TCI, while 2,9-dimethyl-4,7-diphenyl-1,10 phenanthroline (BCP) was purchased from Lumtec. Indium-tin-oxide (ITO), lead bromide (99.999%), and Fullerene-C60 were bought from Advanced Election Technology CO., Ltd. Tetrakis(dimethylamino) Tin(IV) (TDMASn) was purchased from Aimou Yuan, Nanjing, China. Lithium fluoride (LiF) and magnesium fluoride (MgF2) were purchased from Beijing Dream Material Technology Co., Ltd. Indium zinc oxide (IZO) was purchased from Hebei Rechen Advanced Material Technology CO., Ltd. The commercial UV-curable cycloaliphatic epoxy resins were purchased from the adhesive Norland Optical Adhesive with code 86.

Single-crystal analysis

Single crystals of BABCz, BADBCz, and MeO-BADCz were obtained from dichloromethane by slow solvent evaporation and then subjected to crystallography analysis. The crystallographic data were collected by a Rigaku XtaLAB-Synergy-Rdiffractometer equipped with graphite monochromated Cu Kα (λ = 1.54184) at 298.02 K for BABCz, Mo Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å) at 306 K for BADBCz, and Mo Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å) at 100 K for MeO-BADCz, using the phi and omega scans technique. The structure was solved by direct methods using OLEX2 and SHELXL software packages.

Single-junction perovskite solar cell fabrication

Laser-etched ITO glass substrates (7–9 Ω m−2 square) were ultrasonically washed with a detergent solution, deionized water, and anhydrous ethanol for 15 min and dried with N2 flow. Next, the substrates were further cleaned with plasma treatment for 30 min and then immediately transferred into a dry nitrogen-filled glove box. The HSL (2 mg mL−1 in DMF) for BABCz, BADBCz, and MeO-BADCz were spin-coated on the substrate at 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 10 min. 1.4 M wide-bandgap (1.68 eV) perovskite Cs0.05FA0.8MA0.15Pb(I0.75Br0.25)3 precursor solution was prepared by dissolving a mixture of FAI, MABr, PbI2, PbBr2, and CsI in a mixed solvent DMF/DMSO (4:1, v/v), and then magnetically stirring for several hours. 100 μL perovskite precursor solution was spread on the whole area of HSL/ITO substrates and spun using the spin-coating process (3500 rpm for 40 s, 5 s acceleration). According to the antisolvent method, 180 μl of ethyl acetate was dropped on the film in 10 s before the end of the program. The resulting perovskite film was then annealed at 100 °C for 30 min. Finally, 1 nm LiF, 25 nm of C60, 8 nm of BCP, and 120 nm of Ag were sequentially evaporated at rates of 0.05 Å/s, 0.2 Å/s, 0.1 Å/s, and 0.8 Å/s, to finish the device. When preparing devices for stability testing, 15 nm SnO2 at 90 °C deposited by atomic layer deposition (ALD) was used to replace BCP. Other layers were kept the same. The ALD-SnO2 deposition with the following processing sequence for 120 cycles: 0.15 s TDMASn pulse, 12 s purge (50 sccm Ar), 0.33 s deionized water pulse, and 12 s purge (50 sccm Ar).

Monolithic perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells (P/S-TSCs) fabrication

The silicon bottom cell Czochralski n-type silicon wafer (1.2–1.5 Ω, 130 µm) was used for the fabrication of the heterojunction silicon bottom cell. The double-sided texturing was conducted using alkaline solution (KOH), yielding a pyramid size of 1.5–2.5 µm. These textured wafers were then cleaned by a standard Radio Corporation of America cleaning process with a dip in the hydrofluoric acid. The SHJ was formed through a plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition system (AKT-25KAXi PECVD). 6 nm of hydrogenated intrinsic a-Si:H and 14 nm of hydrogenated n-doped nc-Si:H were deposited on the front side of the textured wafer, sequentially. On the rear side of the wafer, 8 nm of hydrogenated intrinsic a-Si:H and 10 nm of hydrogenated p-doped a-Si:H layers were deposited. After that, the ITO layers were sputtered with a thickness of 10 nm and 115 nm on the front and rear sides of the wafer, respectively. A mask with an opening area of 12 mm × 12 mm was used. An additional annealing at 180 °C was conducted to recover the damage caused by sputtering. These wafers were sliced into a shape of 25 mm × 25 mm by laser.

For the perovskite top sub-cell, it is very necessary to use honeycomb suction cups. The substrates were ultrasonically washed with anhydrous ethanol for 15 min and dried with N2 flow. Next, the cleaned substrates were plasma-treated for 15 min and then immediately transferred into a dry nitrogen-filled glove box. The deposition processes of HSL, LiF, and ALD-SnO2 were the same as those of the single-junction PSCs. For the perovskite film, the solution concentration of Cs0.05FA0.8MA0.15Pb(I0.75Br0.25)3 was increased to 1.5 M, 150 μL perovskite precursor solution was spread on the silicon/HSL substrates and spun using the spin-coating process (3000 rpm for 30 s, 6 s acceleration), 250 μl of ethyl acetate was dropped on the film in 5 s before the end of the program. The resulting perovskite film was then annealed at 100 °C for 30 min. Then, 15 nm of C60 was evaporated at a rate of 0.2 Å/s to the device. After the deposition of ALD-SnO2, a 100 nm-thick IZO transparent electrode (100 nm, 30–40 Ω/sq) was deposited by magnetron sputtering through a shadow mask at room temperature with an RF power of 45 W. Lastly, 180 nm and 400 nm Ag were evaporated on the front and rear, respectively, through a shadow mask at a rate of 1.0 Å/s. Finally, an anti-reflective coating of MgF2 (1 Å/s, 150 nm) was thermally evaporated over the active area.

Characterizations and measurements

The 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR spectra were performed on the Bruker AVANCE III 400 MHz spectrometer. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectra were recorded on a Cary 5000 spectrophotometer. The water contact angle was measured by a DataPhysics contact angle tester. The top-view and cross-section morphologies of the perovskite films were investigated using field emission SEM (Apreo S LoVac, Thermo). The surface roughness and surface potential of perovskite films by AFM and KPFM were carried out using Oxford Jupiter XR. The AFM-IR was obtained in a Contact Mode AFM Probe for nanoIR3 in the ambient atmosphere. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was obtained using ESCALAB Xi+ XPS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with Al Kα X-ray radiation (1486.6 eV). Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) (excitation wavelength 460 nm), TRPL was conducted with Edinburgh Instruments LTD (FLS 980). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) experiments were conducted by a dynamic light scattering particle size Analyzer (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90). The DLCP method uses a variable δV (e.g., 20–200 mV) to measure the junction capacitance and acquire the capacitance contribution from the trap states by taking advantage of the information embedded in the higher-order terms. Current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics were measured using a Keithley 2401 SourceMeter under 100 mW/cm2 simulated AM 1.5 G irradiation with a solar simulator (Enli Tech, Taiwan) with 100 mV/s scan speed and 10 ms dwell time. The light source was warmed up for 20 min and equipped with a calibrated Si reference cell (SRC-2020, Enlitech) to correct the light intensity before the J–V test. The active area of single-junction PSCs was defined by a metal shadow mask of 0.1 cm2 and monolithic P/S-TSCs were defined by an insulating shadow mask of 1 cm2, respectively.

The EQE spectra were recorded by a QE measurement system (QE-R, EnliTech), and the light intensity at each wavelength was calibrated with standard single-crystal Si and Ge reference solar cells. The perovskite top cell was measured while saturating the Si bottom cell with continuous biased light from a white light equipped with a long pass (>800 nm) filter. To maintain short-circuit conditions, a bias voltage of ~0.5 V was applied during the measurement. The Si bottom cell was measured while saturating the perovskite top cell with continuous biased light from a white light equipped with a low-pass (<800 nm) filter, and the bias voltage was ~1.0 V. Electrochemical impedance, TPV, and Mott–Schottky measurements were tested in the dark using an electrochemical workstation (Zennium Zahner, Germany), at room temperature with 40% humidity.

Stability measurement

The long-term stability assessment of the PSCs was carried out by repeating the J–V characterizations over various times. For the encapsulation of PSCs, the UV-cured encapsulation method used a UV-curing adhesive (commercialization of NOA86) that cured for no more than 10 s at 365 nm (under an irradiance of 45 mW/cm2). For the damp-heat stability test (ISOS-D-2), encapsulated devices were kept in ambient air with ~50% RH and heated at 65 °C with natural day/night. The long-term MPP tracking (ISOS-L-1) was carried out on the Multi-Channels Solar Cells Stability Test System, Wuhan 91PVKSolar Technology Co., Ltd, China. The unencapsulated solar cells were fixed at the Vmpp and under constant 1sun illumination, and the current density variation was recorded every 60 s at room temperature in a nitrogen flowing atmosphere.

Computational method

The ground-state geometry optimization was calculated using the DFT method at the B3LYP/def2-TZVP level of theory in ethanol solvent based on density (SMD) implicit solvation model with the Gaussian 09 program package49. Stationary points were verified by frequency analysis. The optimized structures were found to be stable. The calculated molecular electronic static potential (ESP) results were obtained with the Multiwfn 3.7 program50. Theoretical adsorption mode between SAMs and ITO or perovskite, based on the DFT and the Projector Augmented Wave (PAW) potential, was implemented with the Device Studio51, which provides several functions for performing visualization, modeling, and simulation. Molecular geometry simulation using BDF52,53. software integrated into the Device Studio program. We used the B3LYP functional to calculate the molecules and optimized the molecular structure using the Def2-SVP basis set. For the calculation of the doping mechanism, DFT calculations were performed by using the DS-PAW54 package in the Device Studio program. The generalized gradient approximation in the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) format was used to compute exchange and correlation energies, and a plane wave basis set cutoff energy of 600 eV was adopted. Grimme’s DFT-D3 was used for dispersion correction. A mesh of 1 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-points was used for the Brillouin-zone integration. The criterion of electron self-consistent iteration was set as 1.0 × 10−5 eV, and the maximum force was relaxed down to 0.05 eV Å−1. Multiwfn and VESTA55 are used to analyze and visualize electronic structure data. Organic molecular interface models were constructed by placing BABCz, BADBCz, and MeO-BADCz molecules onto an indium tin oxide (ITO) surface using the PACKMOL package56. For each system, 200 molecules were deposited on the substrate to ensure statistically meaningful surface coverage and enable direct comparison among different molecular species. All intermolecular and molecule–substrate interactions were described using the OPLS-AA all-atom force field57. Prior to MD simulations, the system was energy-minimized using the conjugate gradient algorithm to eliminate unfavorable atomic contacts and reduce the potential energy of the initial configuration. The system was then pre-equilibrated at 298.15 K for 250 ps, followed by gradual heating to 373.15 K over the subsequent 750 ps to obtain a stable structure at the target temperature. A 1 ns NVT simulation was subsequently performed with temperature controlled by a Nosé–Hoover thermostat. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the particle–particle particle–mesh method with a real-space cutoff of 10 Å. A time step of 1 fs was employed throughout. All MD simulations were carried out using LAMMPS58.
