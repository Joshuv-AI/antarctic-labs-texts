Radiofrequency rhizotomy for multiple sclerosis–related trigeminal neuralgia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bardia Hajikarimloo, Salem M Tos, Ibrahim Mohammadzadeh, Dorsa Najari, Azin Ebrahimi, Arman Hasanzade, Fatemeh Ghorbanpouryami, Mohammad Amin Habibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiofrequency rhizotomy (RFR) is one of the primary therapeutic options for managing patients suffering from multiple sclerosis-related trigeminal neuralgia (MS-TN). Given the increasing use of RFR for MS-TN and the absence of consensus on its efficacy, we aimed to assess the clinical application of RFR in this patient population.MethodsOn April 7, 2025, we conducted a systematic literature search. Studies that evaluated the pain-related outcomes of RFR in MS-TN patients were included.ResultsFifteen studies involving 278 MS-TN patients who received RFR were included. The meta-analysis revealed a pooled pain-free rate and adequate pain-relief rate of 78% (95% CI: 58%-93%) and 96% (95% CI: 89%-100%) at the initial follow-up. In addition, the meta-analysis demonstrated a pooled pain-free rate and adequate pain-relief rate of 64% (95% CI: 25%-95%) and 86% (95% CI: 63%-99%), respectively, at the last follow-up. The pooled complication rate in our study was 9% (95% CI: 1%-22%).ConclusionsRFR is associated with substantial immediate pain relief rates, reasonable complication rates, and favorable long-term pain-related outcomes. However, the long-term outcomes show considerable variability and should be interpreted cautiously. RFR can be considered one of the main therapeutic options for patients suffering from MS-TN, where the pain has limited their daily performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03076-4.Keywords: Radiofrequency, Multiple Sclerosis, Trigeminal Neuralgia, Pain, Meta-Analysis

Introduction

Management of trigeminal neuralgia (TN) in the setting of multiple sclerosis (MS) is challenging [1–3]. MS-related TN (MS-TN) develops due to demyelinating plaques in the trigeminal pathways [1–3]. Compared to classical TN, MS-TN exhibits a more aggressive clinical behavior, as it tends to develop at younger ages, displays higher rates of bilateral cases, and has more occurrences of refractory cases [1–3]. MS-TN patients usually require more aggressive treatment options in the earlier stages of the clinical course due to poor pain-related response rates to conventional pharmacological agents [4–6].

Several therapeutic options are available for the management of the MS-TN, encompassing microvascular decompression (MVD), stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), and various percutaneous rhizotomy techniques such as glycerol rhizotomy (GR), balloon compression (PBC), and radiofrequency rhizotomy (RFR). RFR is one of several therapeutic options currently available for managing MS-TN patients. RFR is a minimally invasive procedure that selectively damages the trigeminal nerve fibers responsible for TN symptoms [7]. The application and indication of RFR are not clearly defined, as MS-TN arises from central demyelination rather than vascular compression, raising questions about the durability and efficacy of this procedure. Additionally, MS-related sensory symptoms can complicate the conduction of the procedure or the evaluation of the intervention results [1–3].

Several studies have evaluated the role of RFR in managing MS-TN patients [8–22]. They demonstrate that RFR yields encouraging pain-related outcomes and complication rates [8–22]. Regarding the increasing use of RFR in MS-TN patients, despite the lack of consensus on its effectiveness, we aimed to evaluate the clinical application of RFR in this patient group. Our goal was to synthesize the available data to assess pain relief rates, procedure-related complications, and identify predictive factors for RFR in MS-TN patients.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

On April 7, 2025, the following keywords and their equivalents were used to conduct a search in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science: “Radiofrequency Ablation,"“Multiple Sclerosis,"and “Trigeminal Neuralgia". Supplementary Table S1 presents the search queries for each database. This study followed the “Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)” guidelines [23].

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

- Administration of RFR to treat MS-TN.

- Administration of RFR to treat MS-TN.

- Reporting on Barrow Neurological Institute (BNI) or other pain scores for MS-TN following RFR.

- Studies involving a minimum of five MS-TNs treated with RFR.

- Studies involving a minimum of five MS-TNs treated with RFR.

Exclusion criteria

- Reviews, book chapters, conference abstracts, preprints, and editorials.

- Reviews, book chapters, conference abstracts, preprints, and editorials.

- Inability to separate the data of MS-TN from other TNs.

- Inability to separate the data of MS-TN from other TNs.

- Inability to extract the outcomes of RFR from alternative treatments.

- Inability to extract the outcomes of RFR from alternative treatments.

- Studies published in a language other than English.

- Studies published in a language other than English.

Study selection process

The screening was carried out by two authors (S.T. and M.H.) using Covidence Software, while a third author (B.H.) resolved any conflicts. Duplicates were managed by Covidence. The screening occurred from April 7, 2025, to April 10, 2025.

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

A.H. and D.N. conducted the data extraction, while B.H. addressed the conflicts. The data spreadsheet included three sections: baseline characteristics and RFR-related outcomes. The risk of bias (RoB) evaluation was performed by B.H. and I.M. using the Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies—of Interventions tool (ROBINS-1) [24]. M.H. resolved the conflicts. The initial pain-free rate was established as BNI-I or similar criteria at the first follow-up, while the initial adequate pain relief rate was defined as BNI-I-IIIb or similar criteria at the first follow-up. The last pain-free rate was defined as BNI-I or similar criteria at the final follow-up, and the last adequate pain relief rate was described as BNI-I-IIIb or similar criteria at the last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using the R language (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Version R-4.4.2). To unify the parameters, if a variable was reported as a median, it was converted to a mean using the method of Luo et al. [25]. The random-effects model was applied when I2 exceeded 40% or when Cochran's Q was substantial (P < 0.1). Following the meta-analysis, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, publication bias evaluation using a funnel plot with Egger’s test, and meta-regression were performed. A value lower than 0.05 was considered significant. For each outcome, only studies wth reporting data were included in the corresponding meta-analysis, and those with missing outcomes were excluded from the pooled analysis.

Results

Study selection process

The search yielded 311 studies, of which 147 were duplicates (Fig. 1). After excluding duplicates, 164 studies were evaluated in the title and abstract screening process. During this step, 100 studies were deemed irrelevant and excluded. Consequently, 64 studies proceeded to full-text screening. In this phase, 49 studies did not meet the inclusion criteria and were removed. Thus, 15 studies were included.

PRISMA flowchart of the current study

Risk of bias assessment

The RoB assessment of the included studies revealed that most studies were associated with a moderate RoB (Supplementary Fig. S1). The confounding and outcome measurement domains were the primary methodological concerns of the included studies, mainly given the lack of data on critical clinical parameters such as MS subtype, medication use, comorbidities, and pain duration before RFR. This highlights the inherent limitations of the retrospective design employed in the included studies. In general, the methodology of the included studies appears to be acceptable; however, the interpretation of the findings in our study should be conducted with meticulous care.

Baseline characteristics

Fifteen studies involving 278 MS-TN patients who received RFR were included (Table 1). All studies were conducted retrospectively. The mean age ranged from 45 to 63.5 years. The male and female genders comprised 42.1% (101/240) and 57.9% (139/240) of the included patients, respectively. Regarding laterality, 87.4% (173/198) were unilateral, while 12.6% (25/198) were bilateral. The affected side was right in 47.6% (98/206) of cases and left in 52.4% (108/206) of cases. None of the included studies reported the type of MS. The V3 division (38.5%, 80/208) was the most frequently involved division, followed by the V2-V3 division (35.1%, 73/208) and the V2 division (14.9%, 31/208). The mean onset time of TN-related symptoms to RFR ranged from 14 to 103.2 months. The rates of prior MVD ranged from 0% to 7.84%, prior GR rates ranged from 0% to 32.6%, and prior SRS rates ranged from 0% to 26.1%.

Meta-analysis of the outcomes

The meta-analysis of pain-free rates at the initial follow-up yielded a pooled estimate of 78% (95% CI: 58%−93%) (Fig. 2A). The meta-regression analysis did not identify any potential sources of heterogeneity for pain-free rates at the initial follow-up (Supplementary Table S2). The meta-regression for the adequate pain relief rate in the initial follow-up revealed a pooled estimate of 96% (95% CI: 89%−100%) (Fig. 2B). The meta-regression demonstrated that male gender (Estimate: 0.0061, P = 0.003) was associated with a higher adequate pain relief rate at the initial follow-up (Supplementary Table S2).

Proportion meta-analysis of (A) initial pain-free rates, B initial adequate pain-relief rates, C pain-free rates at the last follow-up, D adequate pain-relief rates at the last follow-up, and (E) Complication rates

The meta-analysis of the pain-free rate at the last follow-up exhibited a pooled estimate of 64% (95% CI: 25%−95%) (Fig. 2C). The meta-regression showed that younger ages (Estimate: −0.320, P < 0.001) were associated with higher pain-free rates at last follow-up (Supplementary Table S2). In contrast, prior SRS (Estimate: −0.305, P = 0.015) was associated with lower pain-free rates (Supplementary Table S2). The meta-analysis of the adequate pain relief rate at the last follow-up showed a pooled estimate of 86% (95% CI: 63%−99%) (Fig. 2D). The meta-regression indicated that longer follow-up durations (Estimate: −0.071, P = 0.008) and prior SRS (Estimate: −0.344, P < 0.001) were associated with higher adequate pain relief rates at the last follow-up (Supplementary Table S2).

The meta-analysis of the complication rate showed a pooled estimate of 9% (95% CI: 1%−22%) (Fig. 2E). The meta-regression analysis found no potential sources of heterogeneity for pain-free rates at the initial follow-up (Supplementary Table S2).

Sensitivity analysis

The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis of the pain-free and adequate pain-relief rates meta-analyses at initial follow-ups showed that the pooled estimate remained stable, and none of the included studies had a significant impact on the results (Supplementary Fig. S2A-B). Similarly, the sensitivity analysis for pain-free and adequate pain-relief rates meta-analyses at the last follow-ups indicated that results were stable during the leave-one-out process (Supplementary Fig. S2C-D). The sensitivity analysis of the complication rates showed that omission of any of the studies significantly impacted the pooled estimate (Supplementary Fig. S2E).

Publication bias

The funnel plot and Egger’s test for the initial pain-free rate (P = 0.645) and adequate pain relief rate (P = 0.883) indicated no publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S3A-B). The funnel plots for pain-free and adequate pain relief rates at the last follow-up exhibited some degree of asymmetry; however, the Egger’s test (P = 0.513 and P = 0.847) indicated no publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S3C-D). The funnel plot for the complication rate displayed a symmetrical pattern, and the Egger’s test (P = 0.866) indicated no publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S3E).

Discussion

Management of MS-TN patients is challenging due to the concurrent presence of an underlying demyelinating disorder and facial pain. Pain management in MS-TN patients typically necessitates more invasive approaches than pharmacotherapy. MVD, SRS, RFR, GR, and percutaneous balloon compression represent the primary non-pharmacological therapeutic options in the current neurosurgery literature for managing MS-TN. Our findings suggest that RFR is associated with promising initial pain-free (78%) and adequate pain-relief (96%) rates, along with a lower yet acceptable pain-free rate of 64% and an adequate pain-relief rate of 86% at the last follow-up (Table 3). The findings show a pooled complication rate of 9%, which highlights the necessity of meticulous patient selection.

SRS is a minimally invasive procedure that delivers high doses of focused radiation to a targeted area [26, 27]. Previous studies demonstrated that SRS is associated with an adequate pain relief rate of 50% to 80% at initial follow-up and 40% to 80% at last follow-up [12, 16, 28–30]. Lee et al. compared SRS and RFR in individuals with MS-TN [12]. They demonstrated that the initial pain-free rate was insignificantly higher in the RFR group (RFR: 16% [3/19] vs. SRS: 9% [2/23], P = 0.701) [12]. They showed that adequate pain relief rate was not significantly different (RFR: 58% [11/19] vs. SRS: 57% [13/23], P = 0.929) [12]. They demonstrated that pain-free onset was significantly shorter in the RFR group than in SRS (RFR: 0 weeks vs. SRS: 15 weeks, P < 0.001) [12]. On the last follow-up in their study, the pain-free rate was significantly higher in the RFR group (RFR: 44% [7/16] vs. SRS: 11% [2/18], P = 0.031); however, there was no significant difference regarding adequate pain relief rates (RFR: 100% [16/16] vs. SRS: 83% [15/18], P = 0.087) [12]. On the other hand, they showed that RFR was associated with significantly higher paresthesia rates (RFR: 81% [13/16] vs. SRS: 19% [7/18], P = 0.012) [12]. Holland et al. showed that the mean number of additional interventions to reach satisfactory pain-related outcomes was significantly higher in the RFR group (RFR: 2.7 vs. SRS: 2, P = 0.04) [15]. They also showed that there was no significant difference between SRS and RFR regarding complication rates (RFR: 60% [3/5] vs. SRS: 66% [4/6], P = 0.701) [15]. SRS is associated with a slower onset of pain relief and inferior long-term pain-related outcomes, while it is correlated with longer pain recurrence time and lower complication rates. RFR can be considered for MS-TN patients demanding immediate pain relief, while SRS may be more suitable for those who prefer a low-risk intervention.

MVD is a promising therapeutic option for managing classical TN [31–33]. However, in MS-TN, the underlying etiology that results in the development of pain symptoms is central demyelination rather than vascular compression. MVD can be considered in cases with obvious vascular compression on imaging studies. Hatipoglu Majernik et al. demonstrated that MVD achieved a 100% (15/15) adequate pain-relief rate and a 20% (3/15) pain-free rate at the last follow-up [34]. Broggi et al. demonstrated that MVD resulted in a pain-free rate of 38.5% (15/39) and an adequate pain-relief rate of 61.5% (24/39) at the last follow-up [35]. These results indicate the inherent limitation of MVD in MS-TN patients, where the primary pathology is central demyelination rather than neurovascular compression. Therefore, while MVD may offer benefits in selected individuals with obvious vascular contact on radiological studies, its efficacy is expected to be lower than in classical TN, and this should be considered in clinical decision-making. Thus, RFR is preferred over MVD in MS-TN patients without apparent vascular compression.

Percutaneous rhizotomies, including RFR, GR, and PBC, are minimally invasive options for MS-TN patients [2]. Texakalidis et al. demonstrated that none of these techniques demonstrated better outcomes compared to one another; nevertheless, PBC showed higher rates of postoperative mastication weakness than GR (Odds ratio [OR]: 8.58) [2]. Lozouet et al. found that at the 3-month follow-up, 17 out of 18 patients who received RFR experienced adequate pain relief, as did three out of four patients with PBC [8]. In their final follow-up, 16 out of 19 RFR patients had adequate pain relief, while one out of three patients with PBC experienced adequate pain relief [8]. Noorani et al. demonstrated that RFR and PBC are associated with longer pain relief than GR (P = 0.013) [14]. Due to the insufficient direct comparison of percutaneous rhizotomy approaches, identifying the optimal modality is challenging in MS-TN. The following studies should focus on conducting trials with larger datasets and similar baseline features and follow-up periods to evaluate different percutaneous rhizotomy approaches.

We demonstrated that the induction of RFR in MS-TN patients is associated with promising pain-related outcomes and an encouraging safety profile. We highlighted that RFR achieves substantial immediate pain relief rates, making it a potential first-line option following the failure of medical treatments. Therefore, RFR should be considered for patients experiencing acute pain or functional impairments due to TN who require immediate relief. While comparisons with other options, including SRS and MVD, provide practical context, they are based on limited data and lack statistical support. These comparisons should be interpreted with caution.

Future studies should focus on enhancing design, such as incorporating a prospective approach, multi-center involvement, and randomization in patient selection to achieve more reliable and generalizable outcomes. Additionally, research should compare various approaches to establish their indications and superiority. A major focus of upcoming studies should be on how different types of MS influence clinical outcomes.

One limitation of our study is the retrospective and single-center nature of most included studies and the lack of prospective studies. This limitation may lead to selection bias, restricting our meta-analysis findings' generalizability, reliability, and external validation. Variations in the definition of initial and last follow-up present another limitation, which may have introduced heterogeneity in result reporting. Although we tried to standardize definitions given the reported time points, inconsistencies may still affect pooled estimates. Significant heterogeneity complicates our analysis, and to address this issue, we conducted a meta-regression to identify sources of heterogeneity. Furthermore, we cannot stratify pain-related results based on MS subtype, as most studies included in the review did not report this data, restricting the generalizability of our findings across different MS subtypes. Variations in defining pain-related outcomes add to our challenges, even though we attempted to standardize the pain scale to lessen this limitation’s impact on our findings. Moreover, the absence of patient-level data and the prevalence of retrospective, single-center studies, particularly from the US, raise concerns about data overlap, which could inflate the estimates. Although we aimed to reduce this likelihood by carefully assessing study periods and participating hospitals, the possibility of duplication cannot be entirely ruled out. Another limitation is the lack of detailed data regarding the lesioning parameters or interventional technique, which may considerably impact the outcomes and adverse event rates. Future studies should systematically report this to enable better comparisons and inform clinical practice.

Conclusion

We demonstrated that RFR is a promising option for MS-TN patients. RFR resulted in considerable immediate pain relief with reasonable complication rates. While long-term pain-related outcomes are favorable, the wide confidence interval indicates notable variability; therefore, these results should be interpreted cautiously. RFR can be considered one of the primary therapeutic options for patients suffering from MS-TN, particularly when pain has limited their daily activities. Although it may be viewed as a promising therapeutic approach, comparisons with other modalities remain narrative and should be interpreted carefully due to the lack of pooled analyses. Another limitation is the incomplete reporting of significant clinical variables across the included studies. Important parameters such as MS subtype, pain duration prior to the procedure, medication regimens, and comorbidities were often missing. Given the substantial amount of missing data, we did not report or analyze these factors, which restricts the depth of subgroup interpretation and clinical contextualization.
