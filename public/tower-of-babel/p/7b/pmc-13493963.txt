Raphia hookeri seed-inspired weakly anisotropic nanocomposites
Authors: Jiajun Mao, Jia Yan, Ziyu Wang, Qunfeng Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe composites inspired by nacre, bone and teeth etc, often display anisotropic mechanical properties due to the high aspect ratio of their structural units, resulting in sudden failures under specific loading conditions. It still remains great challenge for fabricating the nanocomposites with weakly anisotropic mechanical properties. Herein, we discover the unique structure of the Raphia hookeri seed with a weakly anisotropic crack resistance in multiple directions. The remarkable mechanical properties stem from its tightly bonded, isodiametric cells and interlocking pits. Drawing inspiration from Raphia hookeri seed, we demonstrate an artificial Raphia hookeri seed nanocomposites by assembling layered polymethyl methacrylate-graphene microspheres with pits, which were then interwoven with polymethyl methacrylate to mimic the essential hierarchical structure. The artificial Raphia hookeri seed exhibits effective toughening performance across all crack orientations, and achieves a fracture toughness of up to 4.84 MPa m1/2 and specific toughness of 4.28 MPa m1/2/(Mg m−3), which are higher than both natural Raphia hookeri seed and previously fabricated weakly anisotropic polymer nanocomposites. The optimal distribution of pits and enhanced interfacial interactions between microspheres effectively inhibit crack propagation, contributing to the artificial Raphia hookeri seed’s superior toughness in all directions. This pit-enabled granular microsphere architecture provides an avenue for fabrication high-performance weakly anisotropic nanocomposites.Subject terms: Composites, Structural properties

Introduction

Significant efforts have been devoted to developing optimal nanocomposites with high strength and toughness, inspired by natural structural materials, such as nacre, bone, and teeth1–3. These materials often achieve a balance between strength and toughness through efficient toughening mechanisms, such as crack bridging and deflection. Many methods, such as layer-by-layer assembly, flow-induced alignment, freeze-casting, and additive manufacturing, have been developed for fabricating the nacre-inspired nanocomposites through enhancing the toughness via brick arrangement, waviness, mineral bridges, gradients, and 3D interlocks4–9. Enamel is another typical stiff and tough nanocomposite, which inspires a series of enamel-like nanocomposites with high hardness, stiffness, damage resistance, and fracture toughness, for example, ZnO nanowire@polymer matrix10, hydroxyapatite@Mg3(PO4)2/FePO411, and hydroxyapatite@polyvinyl alcohol12.

However, their exceptional mechanical performance is typically observed in specific directions. For instance, nacre, a layered material composed of high-aspect-ratio aragonite platelets, exhibits weak mechanical resistance in the direction parallel to the platelets4. Similarly, bone and teeth, which consist of hydroxyapatite nanowires and mineralized collagen fibrils embedded in an organic matrix, respectively13,14, display anisotropic mechanical properties that can lead to catastrophic splitting fractures along the longitudinal direction. Consequently, bioinspired nanocomposites replicating these natural microstructures also exhibit high mechanical anisotropy15–17. Meanwhile, the demand for weakly anisotropic composites with serious damage tolerance is rapidly increasing18, particularly in complex additively manufactured aerospace components19, omnidirectional pressure vessels for deep-sea systems20, and mechanically biocompatible orthopedic implants21. Currently, these scenarios typically employ engineering plastics or engineering alloys. However, these materials often struggle to achieve both isotropy and high mechanical performance simultaneously, necessitating bioinspired design strategies to overcome current limitations.

Seed encapsulations in hard shells provide mechanical reinforcement, serving both protective and dispersal functions22. Typically, these shells are weakly anisotropic structural materials capable of sustaining loads from multiple directions with similar mechanical responses. For example, walnut and pistachio seed shells consist of interlocking 3D puzzle-like cells23,24, whose arrangement imparts weakly anisotropic mechanical properties. Ginkgo seed shells exhibit a weakly anisotropic structure, consisting of bonded sclereids and “interlocked” pit pairs, which help resist crack propagation in all directions25. Researchers have repeated kneading of graphene oxide in a polymer matrix to mimic pistachio shells, the mortise-tenon joints design in the structure provides the composite with high tensile strength26. Despite the existence of such naturally weakly anisotropic structural materials or bioinspired nutshell, relatively little attention has been given to the biomimetic design of mechanically weakly anisotropic composites. Most existing studies on the fabrication of weakly anisotropic materials with mechanical reinforcement have primarily employed nanoscale reinforcement or 3D-printing strategies27–30. The first approach involves dispersing nanoparticles within a polymer matrix system; however, these systems suffer from inhomogeneous distribution of nanoparticles and weak interfacial interactions between nanoparticles and polymer matrix. The second approach focuses on constructing materials with microscale building blocks that form isotropic networks, but this method is limited by dimensional accuracy and the low strength of interlayer joints.

Here, we discover that the Raphia hookeri seed (RHS) has remarkable crack resistance due to the weakly anisotropic structure. The RHS is composed of numerous bonded isodiametric sclereids, with adjacent sclereids containing interconnecting pits. The multi-level weakly anisotropic structure and the presence of pits that hinder crack propagation enable the RHS to resist crack propagation in all directions. Inspired by the weakly anisotropic structure from RHS, we demonstrate an artificial RHS (ARHS) nanocomposite by assembling layered polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)-graphene microspheres. The ARHS features a structure of tightly packed cells, with each cell exhibiting a periodic layering pattern and penetrating canals. The prepared ARHS exhibits excellent specific toughness in all directions (fracture toughness of up to 4.84 MPa m1/2 and specific toughness of 4.28 MPa m1/2/(Mg m−3)), which is higher than natural nut shells (RHS and ginkgo seed shell), engineering plastics and some engineering alloys (such as Fe alloy). This is attributed to the optimal distribution of pits, isodiametric units and enhanced interfacial interactions of ARHS nanocomposite.

Results

Structure and composition of RHS

The RHS is encased in a fruit wall derived from the Raphia hookeri palm, which serves both protective and dispersal functions31. RHS is a heterogeneous material composed of three main parts (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1): the outer shell with various ridges and furrows, the white hardened endosperm, and the porous region. The outer shell primarily consists of numerous tightly packed polygonal sclereids of equal diameter (Fig. 1b). The sclereids have an average diameter of 61.2 μm (Supplementary Fig. 2), is similar to that of pistachio shells24 and ginkgo seed shell25. However, the outer shell and seed system of RHS are integrated, unlike the separation of the outer shell and seed in pistachio and ginkgo seed shells, likely due to genetic and environmental factors. High-resolution scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of the fractured and polished surface of RHS show a periodically alternating layering in sclereids (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3), with an average layer thickness of 183.6 nm. Sclereids are typical sclerenchyma cells, with their cell walls composed of cellulose microfibrils arranged in a helical pattern and embedded in a matrix of lignin and hemicellulose32. The composition of cellulose, lignin, hemicellulose, water, and other components (such as pectin) was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography33, with their respective weight percentages being 47, 19, 18, and 16% (Supplementary Fig. 4). Additionally, fracture surfaces of the sclereids reveal that adjacent sclereids contain numerous interconnecting pits (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 5), which function as canals for transporting water and nutrients34. Similar pits have also been observed in ginkgo seed shells25 and pistachio nutshells23, although the pit density in RHS is slightly lower than that in ginkgo seed shells and pistachio nutshells.

In seeds, tissue hardening is typically attributed to lignification, as seen in the sclereids mentioned above. However, rigid secondary cell walls can also be primarily composed of polysaccharides, as observed in the endosperm cells of palm species or the tagua nut35. In RHS, the hardened endosperm constitutes the majority of the seed system, formed through the accumulation and fusion of endosperm cells (Supplementary Fig. 6). The endosperm cells demonstrate an average diameter of 107.7 μm, which is larger than that of sclereids in the outer shell. Furthermore, the endosperm cells contain a huge lumen and no layered structure in their cell walls is observed. These lumens form as nutrients accumulate within the endosperm cells, generating mechanical pressure that dissolves the mesocarp cells, leaving behind only the cell walls36. The polysaccharide composition of the endosperm cell wall differs significantly from that of somatic cell walls. Endosperm walls are typically rich in storage hemicelluloses, such as mannans, galactomannans, glucomannans, and xyloglucans, but contain very low levels of cellulose37,38. Hemicelluloses serve as a carbohydrate reservoir for the germinating embryo and, depending on their specific physicochemical properties, may also play additional roles in mechanical protection of the seed system39. Similar to sclereids, there are numerous pits in hardened endosperm cells (Supplementary Fig. 6), and the diameter of these pits is larger than that of sclereids. In addition, there are some porous brown regions in the endosperm part (Supplementary Fig. 1f–h), which are formed by the loss of water and nutrients by endosperm cells40. However, these brown regions account for only a small proportion of the seed system and contribute less the mechanical properties41.

Generally, the adjacent sclereids are bonded by some distinct compound middle lamella (CML, Supplementary Fig. 3), which is softer and weaker than the inner cell walls15,42. This is because the primary component of these CML is lignin, while the main component of the cell wall is cellulose. CML also occurs in neighboring endosperm cells but is less pronounced than in sclereids. The morphology after delignification treatment of the endosperm part was observed to analyze the distribution of lignin (Supplementary Fig. 7). After delignification, gaps are exposed between adjacent endosperm cells, which have a thickness of approximately 1.1 μm is just the CML. The outer shell and endosperm part, consisting of both stiff (cell) and soft (CML) constituents arranged in a specific manner, plays a crucial role in enhancing toughness15.

Mechanical properties of the RHS

Given that the outer shell and endosperm constitute the majority of the seed, it is reasonable to assume that these two components are primarily responsible for resisting loading stress. Nanoindentation measurements were conducted on the outer shell of Raphia hookeri in its dry state with a water content of 5.05%, revealing that the Young’s modulus of the endosperm part is 5.98 ± 0.29 GPa (Supplementary Fig. 8). The Young’s modulus of the outer shell is 1.2 times higher than that of the endosperm part. This is because the endosperm cells contain more hemicellulose, while the sclereids contain more cellulose, and the modulus of cellulose is significantly higher than that of hemicellulose43. The modulus of the outer shell of RHS is higher than that of wood, epoxies, engineering plastics, and some artificial materials2,44–47, a property attributed to their multi-level assembly based on high-modulus cellulose.

In addition to having a modulus superior to other natural materials, the unique structure of RHS also gives it the ability to resist crack propagation in different directions. We conducted fracture toughness studies of RHS using three-point bending tests. The three orthogonal planes of the RHS are defined as radial (R), tangential (T), and longitudinal (L). The four crack directions are TR, LR, LT, and TL, where the first letter represents the normal direction of the crack plane, and the second letter represents the crack propagation direction (Supplementary Fig. 9). Significant differences in the initial fracture toughness (KIC) were observed across these directions: the TR direction exhibited a KIC of 1.23 ± 0.13 MPa m1/2, the LR direction showed 1.04 ± 0.18 MPa m1/2, the LT direction had the highest KIC at 1.39 ± 0.19 MPa m1/2, and the TL direction exhibited the lowest at 0.67 ± 0.14 MPa m1/2. These results are close to the initial fracture toughness observed in ginkgo seed shells25 and bone14, suggesting effective crack resistance in all four directions.

In addition, the crack resistance curves (R-curves) for all four crack directions (Supplementary Fig. 9) indicate that the external toughening mechanisms in each direction require more energy to drive crack propagation48. There is no significant difference in fracture toughness (KJC) of the four directions, with an average value of approximately 2.97 ± 0.22 MPa m1/2. The close packing of nearly equidimensional cells significantly contributes to weakly anisotropic fracture toughness. The rounded boundaries of equidimensional cells help reduce stress concentration at the tips, and the uneven stress distribution across the cells promotes the activation of external toughening mechanisms19. SEM images (Supplementary Fig. 10) taken after three-point bending tests revealed that the interior of the RHS underwent significant deflection paths and formed long zigzag-shaped crack tails, thus achieving an effective crack-shielding effect.

Compared to natural structural materials with high anisotropy, such as abalone shells4 and bone5 (where fracture toughness can differ by more than ten times between lamellar orientations), RHS exhibit weakly anisotropic fracture toughness. In nature, protective biological structures (such as mollusk shells or exoskeletons made of insect cuticle) primarily reduce crack tip stress through extrinsic toughening mechanisms (such as crack deflection or bridging) after crack initiation, which helps suppress crack propagation2. Similar to what we observed previously in ginkgo seed shells25, the extrinsic toughening mechanism in RHS is attributed to the preference of its pit network for crack bifurcation, a phenomenon we call “pit-guided crack propagation”. A detailed observation of the fracture morphology (Supplementary Fig. 11) showed that cracks entered the rigid sclereids from the cell matrix layer through pits, and cracks along the cell edges resulted from the breakage of lignin bonds between the cell walls. Since pits are a typical hollow structure, they act as defects that guide cracks from the middle lamella into the interior of the thick-walled sclereids. Samples in different directions were subjected to three-point bending tests, and the crack propagation mode was similar in different directions induced by pits (Supplementary Fig. 12). Here, we define the region inside the RHS damaged by pits as A, and the total area of the specimen as S. Based on the analysis of ten fractured samples, the fracture percentage caused by pits (P = A/S × 100%) was found to be 67.4 ± 8.1%. Although pits are also present in pistachio and walnut shells23–25, the pit density is higher in RHS. Moreover, unlike ginkgo seed shells, pits in RHS occur not only in sclereids but also in endosperm cells, and these pits are not formed via fiber assembly. Due to the hollow structure of these pits, they act as defects that guide the cracks from the compound middle lamella into the interior of the sclereid.

Water generally acts as a plasticizer in cellulose-rich structural materials, such as wood, bamboo, and nut shells, primarily affecting their mechanical properties under different moisture contents49. We compared the fracture toughness of dried, initial, and supersaturated water RHS (Supplementary Fig. 13). In the initial state, the thick cell walls of RHS contain naturally saturated moisture, with free water inside the cells. This appropriate water level is beneficial for seed plasticity. In the dried state, compared with the initial state, both strength and modulus increased, while the reduced ductility of the seed coat led to a slight decrease in toughness. After repeated hydration, the cells were also fully hydrated, and their KIC and KJC values were significantly lower than those of both the dried and moist samples. The fracture morphology of dried and repeatedly hydrated RHS indicated that changes in moisture content did not affect the crack propagation path; the vibrations caused by pits were nearly identical to those in the initial state samples.

Fabrication of the ARHS

Guided by detailed mechanistic insights into pit-guided crack propagation, we designed and fabricated ARHS that mimic the structural features of RHS. PMMA/GO (with a thickness of approximately 1.2 nm and a lateral size of about 2.5 µm, Supplementary Fig. 14) layered microspheres with pore channels having a diameter of 500 micrometers were initially synthesized using a hard template method (Supplementary Fig. 15). PMMA/GO and GO layers were sequentially deposited at the intersections of multiple nylon threads, with each subsequent layer being deposited after the previous one naturally dried (Supplementary Fig. 15a). The incorporation of deposited GO layers between PMMA/GO layers created distinct boundaries between the layers. The cured PMMA/GO maintained its layered and “pit” structure after the removal of the template (Supplementary Fig. 15b–e). The morphology of PMMA/GO microspheres is significantly influenced by the PMMA concentration. We prepared three types of PMMA/GO nanocomposites with PMMA concentrations of 15, 20, and 25 wt% (Supplementary Fig. 16). The average layer thicknesses formed by PMMA/GO solutions at concentrations of 15 wt%, 20 wt%, and 25 wt% were approximately 65 µm, 75 µm, and 72 µm, respectively. As the PMMA concentration increased, the morphology of the PMMA/GO nanocomposites became more spherical. However, when the concentration was too high, such as 30 wt%, the PMMA solution became too viscous and difficult to shape50. Therefore, a 25 wt% concentration of PMMA is used in the biomimetic seed design to ensure that the artificial cells are sufficiently spherical (Fig. 1e, f). This ensures better isotropy and enhances the overall mechanical properties. Furthermore, the PMMA/GO microspheres were reduced with ascorbic acid (designated as PMMA/rGO) prior to assembly into ARHS to improve mechanical properties.

The microspheres were assembled into a block material using a bottom-up method (Fig. 1g). Uniformly sized microspheres were arranged in an orderly manner within a rectangular silicone container with an open front. These prepared spheres were then placed into a mold for pre-pressing to ensure that the distance between the spheres did not increase during resin infusion. Epoxy resin was slowly poured through the container opening, and vacuum pumping was applied to minimize gaps between the biomimetic microspheres and facilitate resin infiltration into them. This process resulted in a structure of interconnected internal spheres, resembling the tightly packed sclereids and the densely arranged endosperm cells in the RHS. The infiltration of epoxy resin created a polymer-like interface between the PMMA/rGO biomimetic spheres, analogous to the intercellular layers of sclereids in the pericarp and endosperm cells of the RHS. Additionally, pits originally formed by nylon threads from the cavity to the outer wall of the PMMA/rGO biomimetic spheres either disappeared or diminished due to resin infiltration, indicating strong connections between adjacent spheres. Finally, the ARHS is obtained through hot-pressing (Fig. 1h). The as-prepared ARHS exhibits a closely packed microsphere structure, with the diameter of the microspheres larger than that of the RHS (Fig. 1i). The microsphere diameter in the ARHS is smaller than that of individually synthesized microspheres, as they undergo two compressions during the assembly process. Close observation of the microspheres reveals a clear periodically layered pattern, with the number of periods in the helicoidal cell wall being 6 (Fig. 1j). Magnified images of the microspheres show that they have internal channels (Fig. 1k). However, the difference in internal channels between the ARHS and those in RHS is that the channels in ARHS are not always interconnected.

In addition, silane coupling agents (3-(methacryloyloxy) propyltrimethoxysilane, γ-MPS) were used to enhance the interlayer interactions within the microspheres as well as the interfacial interactions between the microspheres51. A new peak corresponding to C-O-Si from the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) appears in the ARHS (Supplementary Fig. 17a), which indicates the reaction between PMMA and γ-MPS happened during the surface-modifying process. Meanwhile, in the high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) of Si (Supplementary Fig. 17b), the C-O-Si covalent bond also appeared, which is consistent with the results from the FT-IR. These covalent bonds can strengthen the interfacial interaction between the PMMA/rGO lamellar and microsphere. In the biomimetic RHS without interfacial crosslinking, cracks were observed to propagate along the interfaces of the biomimetic cells (Supplementary Fig. 18). The C-O-Si bonds formed between the PMMA and rGO mimic the interactions of cellulose-lignin in the plant cell wall52. We prepared ARHS with 1, 2, and 3 wt% γ-MPS, and compared their fracture toughness (Supplementary Fig. 19). The ARHS with 2 wt% γ-MPS exhibits the highest fracture toughness.

Mechanical properties of the ARHS

Figure 2 shows a comparison of the fracture toughness and tensile strength of ARHS, RHS, and the blend bulk. The blend bulk was prepared by mixing PMMA and rGO in a specific proportion based on the composition of ARHS. The fracture toughness KJC during crack propagation was calculated using the R-curve method. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, the ARHS demonstrates a higher fracture toughness of 4.84 MPa m1/2, which is 4.1 and 1.5 times higher than that of the blend bulk (1.17 MPa m1/2) and RHS (2.97 MPa m1/2), respectively. The improved fracture toughness indicates the functional role of the hierarchical structure and interfacial interactions between the microspheres. In material selection, especially for structural applications, density represents a critical factor. While metallic glasses are among the toughest man-made materials developed to date, their high density restricts their practical applications2,6. To compare the ARHS and RHS with other shell materials in more detail (Fig. 1l and Supplementary Table 1), we considered the density and used specific fracture toughness (KC/ρ) and specific modulus (E/ρ). The density of ARHS is 1.13 g/cm3, where the specific toughness is 4.28 MPa m1/2/(Mg m−3) and specific modulus is 5 GPa/(Mg m−3). Although our ARHS does not exhibit highest toughness and modulus comparable to that of certain anisotropic bioinspired nacre (such as Al2O3/PMMA, Al2O3/SiO2), enamel materials (HA/ZrO2/PVA), Al-alloys, and GFRP. It still demonstrates favorable toughness and modulus, which are higher than some bioinspired nacre (such as montmorillonite/polyvinyl alcohol, rGO/EP), nut shells (ginkgo seed shell and raphia seed shell), engineering plastics (PVC, nylon, epoxy), and Fe-alloys2,5,6,44–47,53,54. This improvement is attributed to the dispersion of stress by the microsphere units, the interfacial interactions, and the pit-induced toughening mechanism. These factors work together to enhance the material’s ability to resist crack propagation and improve overall toughness. We measured the anisotropy of the mechanical properties of ARHS, including strength and toughness, by evaluating the relative differences between the maximum and minimum R-curve values measured in different directions. The compression direction of ARHS is defined as the L direction, and the other two orthogonal planes are the T and R directions. Similarly, the first letter represents the normal direction of the crack plane, and the second letter represents the crack propagation direction. The four crack propagation directions are TR, LR, LT, and TL (Fig. 2b). As shown in Fig. 2c, there was no significant difference in fracture toughness (KIC) for crack initiation in the four directions, with an average value of 0.86 MPa m1/2. However, the KJC values showed slight variations: the KJC in the TR direction was 4.23 ± 0.73 MPa m1/2, the lowest in the LR direction at 4.04 ± 0.48 MPa m1/2, the highest in the LT direction at 4.84 ± 0.57 MPa m1/2, and 4.36 ± 0.41 MPa m1/2 in the TL direction. The average number of tested samples is at least 5 of each sample. The 95% confidence interval is calculated as 4.84 ± 0.0617 (4.766, 4.890), 4.23 ± 0.083 (4.137, 4.303), 4.04 ± 0.1244 (3.912, 4.160), and 4.36 ± 0.122 (4.232, 4.481) of LT, TE, LR, and TL, respectively. This result indicates that ARHS has weakly anisotropic fracture properties. Moreover, the tensile strength of ARHS also demonstrates nearly weakly anisotropic characteristics, with longitudinal and transverse tensile strengths of 141.3 MPa and 132.5 MPa, respectively, which are higher than those of RHS and the blended bulk material (Fig. 2d). This weakly anisotropic mechanical performance is attributed to the weakly anisotropic nature of the interlocked sclereid structure. Unlike the platelets in bioinspired nacre and bioinspired nanowires in enamel2, the building blocks employed in the ARHS are near-spherical. Such building blocks with a low aspect ratio lead to smaller differences in interfacial properties and crystallographic orientations along different directions, thereby yielding a lower anisotropy index.

To evaluate the role of each structural design element, we also fabricated three benchmark PMMA-GO composite materials: ARHS-NI, which lacks interface design between microspheres; ARHS-NF, which omits a pre-set framework; and ARHS-ER, which has a similar structure to ARHS but uses epoxy resin instead of PMMA at the microsphere interface (Fig. 2e, f). The toughness of ARHS was found to be 1.7 times higher than that of ARHS-NI, highlighting the importance of combining spherical interfaces and pit connections to enhance crack resistance. The toughness of ARHS was also 1.5 times higher than that of ARHS-NF, suggesting that preheating and pressing before injecting PMMA into the spheres are necessary to ensure a tight bond between the spheres. Additionally, the modulus of the biomimetic seed shells was measured using quasi-static nanoindentation, yielding a value of 5.3 ± 0.6 GPa. The primary component of the ARHS is low-modulus PMMA, which accounts for its lower modulus compared to natural seed shells. Notably, the modulus of ARHS-NI is higher than that of ARHS, but its fracture toughness is lower, as the encapsulating filler is the relatively high-modulus epoxy resin. Therefore, the combination of hard spheres, soft interfaces, interfacial interactions, and a tightly packed structure is a key factor in achieving high mechanical performance in materials.

In addition to pit connections and interfacial interactions, the concentrations of the polymer and GO also significantly impact the performance of ARHS. At higher PMMA solution concentrations, the resulting product retained relatively intact spherical shapes (Supplementary Fig. 21). In contrast, lower concentrations resulted in reduced precipitation of droplets during natural drying, leading to an imbalanced accumulation of polymer chains near the intersections, ultimately forming irregular microsphere particles. The fracture toughness of ARHS also increases with PMMA solution concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 22). The round shape helps reduce stress concentration at the boundaries, and this uneven stress distribution enhances the external toughening mechanisms of crack propagation19. We chose to keep the PMMA concentration fixed at 25% and observed concentric ring structures at GO ratios of 2, 4, and 6 wt%. Furthermore, the micropore cross-sections revealed pore structures that sequentially pass through the concentric layers from the cavity (Supplementary Fig. 23). The best performance of ARHS was observed at a GO ratio of 4%. Increasing the GO content can enhance the modulus and strength of the biomimetic material, but excessive GO content can lead to the collapse of the spherical structure and a decrease in toughness (Supplementary Fig. 24). Ultimately, we achieved the optimal design for ARHS, with PMMA and graphene concentrations of 25% and 4%, respectively.

Additionally, microsphere size significantly affects ARHS performance. We fabricated biomimetic materials with two different microsphere sizes (500 μm and 1 cm in diameter, Supplementary Fig. 25). The microspheres with a diameter of 500 μm exhibited better fracture toughness, indicating that smaller sizes provide more deflection paths to dissipate energy. The hot-pressing process can alter microsphere size and reduce gaps between units, thereby enhancing mechanical properties. Researchers often use the hot-pressing process in biomimetic design to create dense films or bulk materials, significantly improving their mechanical performance57. We applied the hot-pressing method to enhance the fracture toughness of ARHS. Electron microscope observations showed that after 20%, 30%, and 40% compression rates during hot-pressing, the microspheres in ARHS were compressed axially, and the longitudinal direction was also constrained (Supplementary Fig. 26). Despite deformation, the biomimetic microspheres remained tightly bonded together. The best mechanical performance was observed at a 20% compression rate (Supplementary Fig. 27), where the microsphere diameter was approximately 100 micrometers. At compression rates of 20% and 30%, the concentric layer structure of the microspheres was preserved during hot pressing. However, when compressed to 60% of the original height, the concentric layer structure was disrupted, resulting in reduced mechanical performance.

Fracture mechanism of ARHS

One important reason RHS and ARHS exhibit high isotropy is that their constituent units are nearly equidimensional cells that possess strong rotational symmetry. In contrast, other natural structural materials2 and their biomimetic counterparts7–10, whose constituent units have high aspect ratios, exhibit anisotropic mechanical properties. For example, the nacre layer and artificial nacre are composed of high-aspect-ratio hexagonal or rectangular units arranged in an alternating layered structure within an organic matrix. These materials display excellent strength and toughness in the longitudinal direction (parallel to the layers) due to the interlayer slip mechanism enabled by the stacking of layered soft organic matter57,58. However, this mechanism does not operate under transverse stretching, resulting in significantly reduced mechanical resistance in that direction. Similarly, the anisotropic mechanical properties of teeth3 and artificial teeth10 arise from the alignment of nanofibers in the organic matrix, which creates highly anisotropic behavior. In contrast to these high-aspect-ratio structural units, nearly spherical units have more rounded boundary connections, resulting in less stress concentration and providing better resistance to crack propagation.

The perfect combination of structure and mechanical properties can be attributed to the hierarchical structure of ARHS and the rational distribution of its pores. To understand the interaction mechanism between the two, we synthesized five types of ARHS materials with different numbers of pits. The number of pits in a single microsphere can be adjusted by the number of crossed nylon threads. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 28, microspheres with 4, 6, 8, and 10 pits were prepared, and the corresponding biomimetic seed materials were named ARHS-4, ARHS-6, ARHS-8, and ARHS-10. The R-curve of ARHS-6 showed the highest fracture toughness, being 1.1, 1.4, and 1.8 times higher than that of ARHS-4, ARHS-8, and ARHS-10, respectively (Fig. 3a). The pits represent relatively weak and easily breakable areas in the cells, capable of guiding crack paths and promoting crack propagation (Supplementary Figs. 29–31), thus enhancing the fracture resistance of the biomimetic seed shell. From the two fracture cross-sections of a sample after fracture (Fig. 3b–e), when the crack propagates to the pits, the intact microsphere is divided into two halves by the pit. In addition, the pore volume and specific surface area of ARHS-6 were close to those of natural seed shells (Fig. 3f). When the number of pits in the seed shell was 4, the pore content was relatively low, offering fewer crack deflection paths. However, when the number of pits was too high, the sample’s pore content increased, making the material more prone to damage. Nanoindentation analysis revealed that the modulus of the ARHS inside the microsphere was stronger (Fig. 3g, h and Supplementary Fig. 32), with a Young’s modulus of 5 GPa, while the Young’s modulus of PMMA filling the intercellular layers and pits was 1 GPa. When cracks encountered pits, those that had originally propagated along the microsphere interface layer were forced to propagate along the weaker pit direction. The weak interfaces between the microsphere structure and pits, along with the interlocking effect between microspheres, guided the crack path and promoted stable crack propagation, thus improving the fracture resistance of ARHS.

The fracture mechanisms of ARHS and RHS were compared (Fig. 4). In RHS, cracks typically propagate along the intercellular layers of sclereids, and when they encounter pits, the majority of the fracture paths are guided through these pits (Fig. 4a). The pit-induced cracks damage the sclereids through shear and tension, resulting in two distinct failure modes: the breakage of sclereid under shear and the cleavage of cell wall under tension (Fig. 4b–f). In ARHS, we observed similar fracture mechanisms (Fig. 4g). The separation between the biomimetic microspheres mainly follows two modes: tension and shear. If the stress state between two microspheres is predominantly tensile, the crack deflection at the pits often induces the microspheres to split. On the other hand, if the stress state is dominated by shear, the crack tends to penetrate the entire cell, either terminating at the cell cavity, changing direction, or continuing through the microsphere. As shown in Fig. 4h, the crack propagation path forces two sclereids (marked by white dashed lines) to split, tearing the cell wall. In Fig. 4i, the propagating crack forces the cell to separate from the adjacent cell, causing sliding between the two cells and eventually leading to microsphere rupture. In this process, if the adjacent microspheres separate under tension, delamination and tearing of the cell wall occur.

The fracture toughness of ARHS remains relatively stable in saline and ethanol solvents. After soaking in ethanol and salt water for 24 h, the maximum fracture toughness retention rates reached 99.3% and 93.4%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 33). Furthermore, the samples exhibit favorable fatigue resistance (Supplementary Fig. 34). These properties enable potential applications in deep-sea equipment59 and biomedical implants60. In addition, large-scale preparation of the ARHS can be achieved through continuous extrusion of microspheres61 followed by laser drilling or 3D printing techniques30.

Discussion

In summary, we discovered that the Raphia hookeri seed (RHS) exhibits a structure composed of isodiametric sclereids stacked together and interconnected by pits. This unique microstructure endows RHS with weakly anisotropic properties and a high degree of weakly anisotropic crack resistance. The pit-guided crack propagation demonstrates an effective toughening mechanism for RHS. We successfully synthesized ARHS nanocomposites by assembling layered polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)-graphene microspheres. Due to the optimal distribution of pits, isodiametric units, and enhanced interfacial interactions, the ARHS demonstrates weakly anisotropic properties with a toughness of up to 4.84 MPa m1/2 and specific toughness of 4.28 MPa m1/2/(Mg m-3), which are higher than natural nut shells, engineering plastics and some engineering alloys. This pit-enabled granular microsphere architecture opens an avenue for designing high-performance, weakly anisotropic materials and holds great potential for engineering applications.

Methods

Materials

Polymethyl Methacrylate (PMMA, molecular weight ~15,000, the viscosity ranges from 93 to 373 mPa·s) was purchased from Adamas-Beta, GaoxiTech Co. supplied graphene and Sigma-Aldrich Co. provided 3-(Trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate (98%), N,N-dimethylformamide (≥95%) were sourced from Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. Deionized water (resistivity > 18 MΩ cm-1) was obtained from a Millipore Milli-Q Biocel water purification system.

Sample preparation

The sample used to characterize the morphological characteristics and mechanical properties of the outer shell and hardened endosperm was obtained from purchased Raphia hookeri seed (RHS, commonly known as Thousand Eyes Bodhi). Before testing, the sample was cleaned five times with deionized water, sonicated for 1 min each time, and dried at room temperature and normal pressure. All spare Raphia hookeri seed samples should be stored in a cool and dry environment. Based on the ellipsoidal morphology of the Raphia hookeri seed, the orientation of the sample can be divided into three directions: tangential (T), radial (R), and longitudinal (L).

Characterization of Raphia hookeri seed

The seed sample was cut into block-shaped samples measuring approximately 6 × 1 × 1 millimeters in length, width, and height. The surface morphology of the sample was obtained by observing the fractured sections of the prepared seed from different directions in a top view. To observe the pore structure within the seed, the sample was polished to eliminate stress-induced defects introduced during fracturing. The samples were cut into blocks matching the observed cross-sectional morphology, sequentially polished with P800, P1200, P3000, and P5000-grade silicon carbide sandpaper, and then polished on silk with 8000- and 10000-mesh diamond paste. Next, the sample was sonicated five times in a closed centrifuge tube water bath for 1 min each time to remove small particles that adhered during each polishing stage, and finally dried at room temperature. The sample was attached to the electron microscope stage using conductive adhesive and sputter-coated with gold particles for 60 s at a current of 20 mA. SEM characterization was performed using a Quanta 250FEG instrument produced by FEI, with a working voltage of 5 kV and a working current of 10 μA.

Biomimetic preparation of microsphere

A cubic hollow template is created using PDMS. Nylon threads are inserted into the PDMS template and intersect at a single point in different directions. A specific amount of PMMA is dissolved in 20 mL of DMF solution and stirred for 48 h. The GO aqueous solution is replaced with a GO DMF solution through centrifugation. A predetermined amount of GO solution is mixed with the PMMA solution. The PMMA/GO mixed solution is deposited as spherical droplets at the intersection of the nylon threads and dried for approximately 30 min, followed by the deposition of GO spherical droplets. The deposition of the PMMA/GO solution and the GO solution is repeated six times. The resulting droplets are allowed to dry naturally before the nylon threads are removed for further use.

Biomimetic preparation of microsphere

The dried PMMA/GO microspheres were arranged in an orderly manner within a PDMS template. These prepared spheres were then placed into a mold for pre-pressing to ensure that the distance between the spheres did not increase during resin infusion. The temperature, pressure, and time for pre-set hot-pressing are 80 °C, 1 MPa, and 4 h. Permeate the GO DMF solution through the template using a vacuum process, repeating this step five times in a vacuum dryer. The resulting sample is hot-pressed at 80 °C in the mold for 2 h and then immersed in a silane coupling agent, 3-(methacryloyloxy) propyltrimethoxysilane (γ-MPS), for 12 hours to facilitate crosslinking and obtain the final biomimetic seed sample.

Mechanical characterization

All toughness tests satisfied the plane-strain conditions, the thickness of specimen is ~2 mm, and the specimens were notched ( ~ 1 mm) using a diamond saw (300 µm thickness), and then the notch was sharpened by a razor blade. An equivalent relationship between compliance and crack length was applied during single-edge notched beam (SENB) testing. The notches were first introduced with a low-speed diamond saw, then sharpened with a razor micro-notching technique. Micro-notches, with root radius ~3–5 µm, were obtained by repeatedly sliding a razor blade over the saw-cut notch using a custom-made rig, while continually irrigating with a 1 µm diamond slurry. All fracture toughness tests met the plane-strain and small-scale yielding criteria for valid K values determination in accordance with ASTM Standard E1820-0662.

The specimens are rectangular with ~2 mm height, ~2 mm width, and ~15 mm length and the mass was measured using an electronic balance in an ambient (a relative humidity of 35% and a temperature of 25 °C). The density of the RHS and ARHS was calculated as their total mass divided by their total volume. For the three-point bending test, the specimens were loaded with a support span of 7.75 mm. The maximum crack extension is given by Δamax = 0.25b (b is the uncracked ligament width), which corresponds to Δa ≈ 0.25 mm6. The average number of tested specimens is at least five for each sample. Multiple replicates were used for all tests to eliminate individual differences in biological samples and ensure the robustness of the experimental results.
