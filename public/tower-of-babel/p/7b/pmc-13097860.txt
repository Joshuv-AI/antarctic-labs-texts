Parental polystyrene nanoplastics exposure increases susceptibility to pathogen infection in offspring via H3K36me3-UPRER-collagen pathway
Authors: Fang Liu, Yan Zhang, Wenqiao Fu, Yingwen Cui, Yi Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlastic pollution, particularly nanoplastics, poses a serious threat to the health of humans and wildlife. Using Caenorhabditis elegans as a model, we found that exposure to polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-NPs) at 1000 μg/L for 48 h significantly reduced survival upon Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA14 infection in the parental generation (P0), with a 42.74% decline. Notably, this impaired immunity was also observed in the first filial generation (F1) and second filial generation (F2), where survival decreased by 31.27% and 19.55%, respectively. This transgenerational decline in innate immunity was not attributable to residual nanoplastics in the progeny but was mediated through H3K36me3-dependent epigenetic regulation. Mechanistically, PS-NPs suppressed the endoplasmic reticulum unfolded protein response (UPRER), particularly the IRE-1/XBP-1 pathway, and downregulated collagen-related genes, thereby compromising epithelial defenses. ChIP-qPCR and Western blotting further confirmed reduced H3K36me3 enrichment on UPRER and collagen gene promoters. Importantly, this epigenetic inheritance occurred independently of small RNAs or N6-mA DNA methylation. Collectively, these findings reveal an unrecognized mechanism by which nanoplastic exposure weakens immunity across generations through histone modification, underscoring the long-term biological risks posed by environmental nanoplastics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02779-2.Keywords: Polystyrene nanoparticles, Innate immunity, Endoplasmic reticulum unfolded protein response, Epigenetics, Caenorhabditis elegans

Introduction

Nanoplastics have emerged as a new class of environmental toxicants capable of crossing biological barriers, entering systemic circulation, and accumulating in various tissues [21, 52]. Increasing evidence shows that nanoplastics can interfere with fundamental physiological processes, including redox balance [27], neuronal signaling [32], reproductive function [51], and innate immune activity [50]. Among these effects, immune dysregulation is of particular concern because innate immunity serves as the first line of defense against pathogens and is closely linked to overall physiological homeostasis [12, 23]. Nevertheless, despite growing recognition of nanoplastic toxicity, the molecular mechanisms through which nanoplastics disrupt innate immune responses, and whether these effects can be transmitted across generations, remain largely unclear.

Epigenetic inheritance provides a compelling framework for understanding how environmental exposures generate long-lasting biological consequences. Epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs, can store regulatory information in germ cells and transmit phenotypic changes to offspring without altering DNA sequence [3, 10]. In C. elegans, a well-established model for investigating transgenerational epigenetic regulation, nanoplastic exposure perturbs several fundamental regulatory pathways. MET-2-dependent deposition of H3K9me3 has been identified as a pivotal chromatin modification mediating PS-NPs-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) toxicity, highlighting the importance of repressive histone marks in shaping organismal stress responses [33]. Small non-coding RNAs constitute an additional regulatory tier,notably, the intestine-enriched miRNA mir-794 alleviates PS-NPs-evoked ROS damage through a localized epigenetic mechanism [24]. Long non-coding RNAs further influence nanoplastic responses by recruiting chromatin-modifying complexes, a process implicated in the locomotor dysfunction observed following chronic low-dose PS-NPs exposure [59]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that nanoplastics disrupt both chromatin-based and RNA-mediated regulatory networks in C. elegans, providing mechanistic insight into how environmental particles generate heritable physiological alterations. However, it is still unknown whether nanoplastics can influence innate immune capacity in a transgenerational manner and which epigenetic mechanisms mediate such inheritance.

Innate immunity in C. elegans is governed by a network of conserved pathways that interface with cellular stress responses [29]. Among them, the endoplasmic reticulum unfolded protein response UPRER plays a central role by coordinating protein folding, secretory homeostasis, and immune gene expression [61]. The three canonical UPRER branches-PERK, ATF6, and the IRE-1/XBP-1 axis-enable cells to restore proteostasis when challenged by infection, metabolic stress, or toxic insults [13]. Recent work has shown that activation of the IRE-1/XBP-1 pathway strengthens resistance to bacterial pathogens, whereas disruption of ER proteostasis markedly impairs host defense [60]. In parallel, Ginkgolide A has been reported to enhance antimicrobial immunity by activating the mitochondrial unfolded protein response through the DVE-1/SATB2 axis across species [9]. Collectively, these findings highlight the unfolded protein response as a central regulatory hub that integrates cellular stress signals with innate immune function. However, whether nanoplastics perturb UPRER signaling, and how such alterations may influence immune capacity across generations, remains largely unknown.

In this study, we show that parental exposure to PS-NPs markedly suppresses innate immunity in C. elegans and that this defect persists into the F1 and F2 generations. Through transcriptional profiling, confocal imaging, genetic analysis, and chromatin immunoprecipitation, we demonstrate that PS-NPs inhibit the IRE-1/XBP-1 branch of the UPRER and downregulate collagen gene expression, thereby weakening both proteostatic and structural components of innate immunity. Importantly, we identify H3K36me3, catalyzed by the germline methyltransferase MES-4, as a key epigenetic mark required for transmitting these immune defects to progeny. Loss of H3K36me3 enrichment at UPRER and collagen-related loci establishes a heritable transcriptional repression program that persists across generations. Together, our findings uncover a previously unrecognized H3K36me3-UPRER-collagen axis that links nanoplastic exposure to transgenerational innate immune dysfunction. This work expands the current understanding of nanoplastic toxicity and provides new mechanistic insight into how environmental stressors reshape host defense capacity across generations.

Results

Characterization of PS-NPs

Prior to evaluating their biological effects, we characterized the physicochemical properties of the PS-NPs used in this study. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that both unlabeled PS-NPs and red fluorescent carboxyl-functionalized PS-COOH nanoparticles exhibited uniform spherical morphology, smooth surfaces, and good dispersibility without obvious aggregation (Fig. 1A and C). The average particle diameters of PS-NPs and PS-COOH were 82.5 ± 2.26 nm and 86.5 ± 3.05 nm, respectively (Fig. 1B and D), confirming that the particles were well within the 50–100 nm range suitable for C. elegans exposure experiments. These findings verified the homogeneity and stability of the nanoplastic preparations, ensuring their reliability for subsequent biological assays.

Transgenerational inheritance of the decrease in innate immunity of C. elegans induced by PS-NPs

To determine whether PS-NPs exposure impairs innate immunity in C. elegans, synchronized L4-stage wild-type worms were exposed to different concentrations of PS-NPs (0, 50, 250, 500, and 1000 μg/L) for 48 h and then transferred to NGM plates seeded with P. aeruginosa PA14. Survival analysis showed that worms exposed to 50–500 μg/L PS-NPs exhibited no significant reduction in survival compared with controls (P > 0.05). In contrast, exposure to 1000 μg/L PS-NPs caused a 42.74% reduction in survival following PA14 infection (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1E). Accordingly, 1000 μg/L was selected as the working concentration for subsequent experiments.

To examine whether the immunosuppressive effect of PS-NPs can be inherited across generations, only the P0 generation was directly exposed to 1000 μg/L PS-NPs for 48 h. The P0 worms were then synchronized to generate the F1 generation, and the F1 worms were grown and infected with PA14 without any PS-NPs exposure at any stage. The same exposure-free procedure was used to obtain and infect the F2 and F3 generations (Fig. 1F). Despite the absence of direct exposure, the unexposed F1 and F2 offspring still displayed reductions in survival of 31.27% and 19.55%, respectively (P < 0.05), whereas the F3 generation exhibited survival comparable to controls (P > 0.05) (Fig. 1G-J). These results underscore that the observed immune defects in F1 and F2 worms arose from transgenerational inheritance rather than direct nanoparticle exposure, as only the P0 generation encountered PS-NPs. During exposure, worms ingested nanoparticles from NGM plates containing a uniform suspension of PS-NPs mixed with E. coli OP50. Uptake was confirmed using red fluorescent PS-COOH nanoparticles and visualized by laser confocal microscopy. Fluorescent signals were detected in the pharyngeal bulb and intestinal lumen, indicating ingestion and intestinal accumulation (Fig. 1K). Previous studies have reported the presence of microplastics in reproductive tissues [58]. To determine whether PS-NPs could be transmitted to the offspring after parental exposure, we performed laser confocal imaging on F1 worms. No red fluorescence-labeled PS-NPs were detected in the offspring, indicating that nanoparticles were not physically transmitted to the next generation (Fig. 1L).

IRE-1/XBP-1 is essential for the transgenerational inheritance of the innate immunity decline induced by PS-NPs in C. elegans

As a classic model organism, C. elegans possesses an innate immune system that is pivotal in defending against pathogen infections. Previous research has identified several conserved signaling pathways underlying C. elegans innate immunity, including the PMK-1/P38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway [20, 34, 48], the insulin/IGF-1 signaling transduction pathway (IIS) [46], the endoplasmic reticulum UPR [40, 60] and the mitochondria UPR [36, 37, 56]. The immune-related genes examined in this study encode distinct classes of regulatory factors, including the FOXO transcription factor DAF-16, the Nrf2-like oxidative stress regulator SKN-1, the p38 MAPK kinase PMK-1, mitochondrial UPR regulator ATFS-1, UPRER transducers ATF-6 and PEK-1, the ER chaperone HSP-4, the ER stress sensor IRE-1, and the UPRER transcription factor XBP-1. To identify which pathways are involved in PS-NPs-induced immunosuppression, we performed P. aeruginosa PA14 infection assays using mutants defective in these key immune pathways.

In the P0 generation, PS-NPs exposure caused a 38.56% reduction in survival in wild-type worms after PA14 infection (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Similarly, daf-16(mu86) (IIS pathway), skn-1(zu67) (Nrf2 ortholog), and pmk-1(km25) (p38 MAPK) mutants exhibited survival reductions of 31.83%, 38.04%, and 40.24%, respectively (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 2B-D). Three UPR-related mutants-atfs-1(gk3094) (mitochondrial UPR regulator), atf-6(ok551), and pek-1(ok275) (UPRER transcription factors)-also showed 33.50%, 31.59%, and 38.57% decreases in survival following PS-NP treatment (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2E-G).

Notably, the same phenotypes were observed in the F1 (Fig. 2K-P) and F2 (Fig. 2Q-T) generations, supporting a transgenerational effect. These findings suggest that PS-NPs-induced suppression of innate immunity is not dependent on the PMK-1 or IIS pathways but specifically requires an intact IRE-1/XBP-1 axis within the ER UPR pathway. These results indicated that the transgenerational inheritance of the innate immunity decline induced by PS-NPs is mediated by the IRE-1/XBP-1 signaling pathway of the UPRER.

To further validate this conclusion, we randomly selected downstream target genes of the transcription factor XBP-1-specifically, the ABU gene family, which is involved in immunity and stress responses-and performed RT-qPCR to measure their mRNA transcript levels. Our results revealed that exposure to PS-NPs significantly downregulated the expression of UPRER-related genes (abu-1, abu-6, abu-7, abu-8, abu-10, abu-11, and abu-15) in the P0, F1 and F2 generations (Fig. 2U). We also verified the changes in the content of core proteins of UPRER via Western blot experiments. PS-NPs exposure in the parental generation significantly decreased the level of phosphorylation IRE1 and XBP1s in both the P0, F1, and F2 generations. However, the levels of PERK and ATF6 did not change (Fig. 2V). These results indicate that IRE-1/XBP-1 is essential for the transgenerational inheritance of the innate immunity decline induced by PS-NPs in C. elegans.

PS-NPs may reduce the innate immunity of C. elegans against the P. aeruginosa PA14 by suppressing the expression of collagen genes

C. elegans lacks an acquired immune system, and its defense against pathogenic bacteria relies on behavioral avoidance, physical barriers, and physiological immune responses. The physical barrier primarily consists of the body surface and intestinal lumen, which are protected by multiple layers of cuticle. The cuticle is composed mainly of collagen, along with a small amount of chitin, and serves as the nematode’s first line of defense against external pathogens. The integrity of this structure is essential for resistance to bacterial and fungal infections [28].

To determine whether PS-NPs-induced innate immunosuppression is associated with alterations in collagen integrity, we examined epidermal morphology using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Worms were exposed or unexposed to PS-NPs for 48 h and subsequently infected with P. aeruginosa PA14 for 24 h. SEM imaging revealed that worms exposed to PS-NPs displayed a rough, wrinkled surface with disrupted cuticular patterns, whereas control worms maintained a smooth and regular epidermal structure (Fig. 3A).

To further validate these observations, we measured the transcript levels of several collagen genes (col-141, col-145, col-146, col-156, col-157, col-161, and col-169) by RT-qPCR. PS-NPs exposure significantly reduced the expression of these collagen genes in the P0 generation (Fig. 3B). Notably, the downregulation of collagen gene expression persisted in the unexposed F1 and F2 generations (Fig. 3C and D), indicating a transgenerational effect. Taken together, these results suggest that reduced collagen gene expression contributes to impaired innate immunity in PS-NPs-exposed worms. The persistence of collagen downregulation in the offspring further implies that collagen genes are involved not only in mediating the innate immune response but also in transmitting the epigenetic memory of immunosuppression across generations.

H3K36me3 histone modification is required for the epigenetic changes induced by PS-NPs

The disappearance of the PS-NPs-induced innate immunity decline in the F3 generation suggests that this phenotype arises from epigenetic rather than genetic alterations. Major epigenetic mechanisms in C. elegans include histone modifications, DNA methylation, and non-coding RNAs. To determine whether histone methylation contributes to the observed immunosuppression, we examined mutants corresponding to four major histone methylation marks-H3K4me3, H3K9me3, H3K27me3, and H3K36me3-using set-2(ok952), set-32(ok1457), utx-1(ok3553), and mes-4(ok2326), respectively. After PS-NPs exposure, wild-type worms showed a 32.29% reduction in survival following P. aeruginosa PA14 infection (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). The set-32(ok1457), set-2(ok952), and utx-1(ok3553) mutants also exhibited 34.26%, 42.14%, and 38.45% reductions in survival, respectively (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 4B-D). In contrast, the mes-4(ok2326) mutant displayed no significant decrease in survival (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4E).

This pattern persisted across generations. In the F1 generation, PS-NPs exposure caused significant reductions in survival in wild-type, set-32(ok1457), set-2(ok952), and utx-1(ok3553) mutants (P < 0.05), whereas mes-4(ok2326) again showed no significant change in survival (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4F-J). A similar trend was observed in the F2 generation: wild-type exhibited significant survival declines following PS-NPs treatment (P < 0.05), while mes-4(ok2326) continued to show no detectable reduction (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4K-L). These findings indicate that MES-4-dependent H3K36me3 methylation is essential for the transgenerational innate immunity decline induced by PS-NPs exposure.

To further confirm this conclusion, we assessed histone methylation levels by Western blot. Consistent with the survival phenotypes, H3K4me3, H3K9me3, and H3K27me3 levels remained unchanged after PS-NPs exposure, whereas H3K36me3 levels were markedly reduced in the P0, F1, and F2 generations (Fig. 4M). We next performed ChIP-qPCR to determine whether the reduced expression of UPRER-related and collagen-related genes was associated with altered H3K36me3 deposition. PS-NPs exposure led to a significant decrease in H3K36me3 enrichment at the promoters of both gene sets across all three generations examined (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4N-O), consistent with their transcriptional repression. Together, these findings demonstrate that PS-NPs induce heritable epigenetic alterations by reducing H3K36me3 deposition on UPRER and collagen gene loci. Loss of this activating histone mark suppresses gene expression, disrupts ER proteostasis and cuticular integrity, and thereby promotes the transgenerational inheritance of weakened innate immunity. Chromatin modifications established in PS-NPs-exposed P0 worms persist across generations to transmit heritable immune memory.

XBP1 functions in the intestine to mediate the decrease in innate immunity of C. elegans induced by PS-NPs

The execution of complex biological functions in an organism requires coordinated communication between tissues, and C. elegans exhibits extensive inter-tissue signaling networks. Tissue-specific gene perturbation enables precise spatiotemporal dissection of gene function and reveals how signals propagate across tissues. XBP-1, a transcription factor activated by the ER stress sensor IRE-1, binds sequence-specific promoter elements and mediates the IRE-1-dependent unfolded protein response [53]. In the P0 generation, PS-NPs exposure reduced survival by 34.77% in the germline-specific RNAi strain DCL569 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5B). Similarly, hypodermal (NR222), neuronal (MAH677), and muscle-specific (NR350) RNAi strains showed survival reductions of 41.21%, 26.55%, and 35.12%, respectively, after PS-NPs treatment (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 5C-E). We next examined whether this tissue-specific requirement persists across generations. In the F1 generation, PS-NPs exposure continued to significantly reduce survival in the germline-specific (DCL569), hypodermal-specific (NR222), neuronal-specific (MAH677), and muscle-specific (NR350) RNAi strains (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 5G–J), whereas intestinal XBP-1 knockdown again showed no significant change (P > 0.05) (Fig. 5K). The loss of the PS-NPs-induced immunosuppression phenotype in VP303 confirms that intestinal XBP-1 is the critical site of action and that this requirement is stably inherited across generations. Together, these findings demonstrate that XBP-1 acts predominantly within the intestine to mediate PS-NPs-induced innate immune decline. This indicates that PS-NPs reduce immunity by autonomously suppressing the intestinal UPRER, and that this tissue-specific regulatory mechanism exhibits a clear transgenerational inheritance pattern.

MES-4 functions in the germline to mediate the transgenerational inheritance of the decrease in innate immunity of C. elegans induced by PS-NPs

The histone lysine N-methyltransferase MES-4 could activate the methyltransferase activity of histone H3K36me3 and was involved in germline sex determination and heterochromatin formation [5]. To determine in which tissue MES-4 acts to mediate PS-NPs-induced transgenerational immunosuppression, we used tissue-specific RNAi strains of C. elegans and knocked down MES-4 in the hypodermis, neurons, germline, muscle, and intestine (Fig. 6A). In the P0 generation, PS-NPs exposure significantly reduced survival after P. aeruginosa PA14 infection in the hypodermal RNAi strain NR222 by 42.79% (P < 0.05), in the muscle RNAi strain NR350 by 32.15% (P < 0.05), in the neuronal RNAi strain MAH677 by 31.57% (P < 0.05), and in the intestinal RNAi strain VP303 by 38.47% (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6C-F). In contrast, germline-specific knockdown of mes-4 in DCL569 abolished the PS-NPs-induced effect, showing no significant change in survival compared with controls (P > 0.05) (Fig. 6B).

The decrease in the innate immunity of C. elegans induced by PS-NPs does not occur through the effects of N6-mA-DNA methylation and non-coding RNAs

Epigenetic modifications predominantly encompass DNA methylation, histone methylation, and the functions of non-coding RNAs. Owing to their pivotal and substantial contributions within the realm of epigenetics, these modifications have garnered extensive attention and been the subject of numerous reports in the scientific literature [26, 38, 49]. Our previous findings demonstrated that PS-NPs-induced innate immune decline in C. elegans depends on histone H3K36me3 methylation. To determine whether other epigenetic mechanisms contribute to this phenotype, we examined mutants defective in N6-mA DNA methylation and several classes of non-coding RNAs, including miRNAs, piRNAs, lncRNAs, and tRNAs. In the P0 generation, PS-NPs exposure significantly reduced survival in wild-type worms by 34.21% (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7A). Comparable reductions were observed in nrde-4(gg129), csr-1(tm892), prg-1(n4357), hrde-1(tm1200), and F09F7.7(ok3133) mutants, with survival decreases of 36.47%, 33.54%, 30.97%, 31.28%, and 31.34%, respectively (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 7B-F).

The F1 generation displayed the same trend. PS-NPs exposure caused significant reductions in survival in wild-type, nrde-4(gg129), csr-1(tm892), prg-1(n4357), hrde-1(tm1200), and F09F7.7(ok3133) mutants (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 7G–L). None of the mutants showed resistance to PS-NPs-induced immunosuppression, indicating that the innate immune suppression caused by PS-NPs does not depend on N6-mA DNA methylation or on miRNA, piRNA, lncRNA, or tRNA regulatory pathways. These findings further support the conclusion that histone H3K36me3 methylation is the critical epigenetic mechanism underlying PS-NPs-induced transgenerational immunosuppression in C. elegans.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that parental exposure to PS-NPs weakens innate immunity in C. elegans and that this impairment can be transmitted to unexposed offspring through an epigenetic mechanism involving histone H3K36me3 modification. By integrating physiological, molecular, and genetic analyses, we reveal that PS-NPs disrupt the UPRER and collagen-related pathways, establishing a novel H3K36me3-UPRER-collagen axis that mediates transgenerational immunotoxicity. Nanoplastics have been recognized as emerging environmental toxicants with the capacity to penetrate biological barriers and accumulate within organisms [4]. While previous research has mainly focused on oxidative stress [14], neurotoxicity [2], and reproductive damage [51], the heritable immune consequences of nanoplastic exposure have remained unclear. Our findings provide direct evidence that PS-NPs not only compromise innate immunity in the exposed parental generation but also transmit this immunosuppression to subsequent generations (up to F2) in the absence of continued exposure. This transgenerational phenotype is consistent with the concept of toxicological memory, whereby environmental stressors induce persistent physiological changes through epigenetic mechanisms [55]. The disappearance of the phenotype in F3 further suggests that these effects are epigenetic rather than genetic mutations.

Our results identify the IRE-1/XBP-1 pathway as essential for PS-NPs-induced immunosuppression. The UPRER is a conserved stress-response mechanism that maintains proteostasis by regulating ER chaperones and degradation systems [6, 11]. In C. elegans, the activation of UPRER enhances pathogen resistance via the IRE-1/XBP-1 signaling cascade [40, 60]. In contrast to these protective roles, our study shows that PS-NPs suppress IRE-1 phosphorylation and downregulate UPRER-related genes, including members of the ABU family. Such inhibitions likely compromise protein folding capacity and immune signaling, thereby increasing susceptibility to infection.

Emerging evidence further indicates that UPRER activity can interface with epigenetic processes to influence immune responses across generations. For example, supplementation with S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) enhances innate immunity in C. elegans through H3K4me3-mediated activation of immune and UPRER-related genes, and this enhanced immune state is inherited by offspring [19]. These observations support the concept that UPRER-derived stress signals can be converted into germline epigenetic memory through histone methylation-dependent mechanisms. Comparable findings have been reported in higher organisms: Chen et al. observed that PS-NPs disrupt ER proteostasis and trigger maladaptive stress responses in mouse lungs [7]. Therefore, PS-NPs exposure may act as a chronic stressor that overwhelms adaptive UPRER capacity, ultimately shifting the response from protection to dysfunction. The persistence of this inhibition across generations further suggests that the ER stress response can be epigenetically programmed by environmental pollutants.

Besides molecular signaling, PS-NPs also damage the nematode cuticle, the primary physical barrier against pathogens. SEM analysis and transcriptional profiling revealed that PS-NPs downregulated multiple collagen genes (col-141, col-156, col-161, etc.), resulting in surface roughness and deformation. Collagen plays a dual role in maintaining epidermal integrity and modulating immune signaling [8, 63]. Similar phenomena have been observed in other aquatic organisms: PS-NPs exposure disrupts the intestinal epithelium and immune homeostasis in crustaceans and fish [16, 17]. These findings collectively suggest that collagen-mediated cuticular integrity is an essential component of innate immunity, and its disruption may amplify susceptibility to infection. Interestingly, the downregulation of collagen genes persisted into F2 progeny, consistent with heritable transcriptional silencing. This observation bridges the molecular and structural dimensions of immune regulation, highlighting that nanoplastics compromise both the immune signaling network and the physical barrier system in a heritable manner.

One of the most striking findings of this study is that the transgenerational suppression of immunity depends on histone H3K36me3 modification mediated by the methyltransferase MES-4. We observed that PS-NPs exposure reduced global H3K36me3 levels and diminished its enrichment on promoters of UPRER and collagen genes. Given that H3K36me3 is an active transcriptional mark involved in maintaining germline gene expression [15], its reduction likely results in sustained gene repression in both parental and offspring generations. This mechanism is consistent with previous findings that environmental stressors can modify histone methylation to establish heritable transcriptional programs. Recent studies have demonstrated that MET-2-mediated histone H3K9me3 methylation has been confirmed as a key regulatory site for the reactive oxygen species (ROS) toxic effects induced by PS-NPs in C. elegans [33], whereas altered histone methylation contributes to PS-NPs-induced endocrine disruption in mice [1]. Our data extend these concepts by showing that H3K36me3 acts as an epigenetic bridge connecting ER stress, collagen expression, and immune memory. Moreover, our genetic analysis indicated that DNA methylation (N6mA) [31] and small RNA pathways [24, 25] were not required for the observed inheritance, distinguishing this mechanism from small RNA-dependent transgenerational responses. Thus, PS-NPs exposure primarily triggers a histone-based inheritance system, which may represent a conserved mode of transmitting environmental memory.

The discovery that nanoplastic exposure can induce heritable immune dysfunction has significant implications for both environmental toxicology and human health. The persistence of PS-NPs-induced epigenetic alterations across generations suggests that even low-level chronic exposure may have long-term biological consequences. Given that micro- and nanoplastics have been detected in human blood, placenta, and organs [22, 30], the potential for similar epigenetic effects in mammals cannot be excluded. Our findings highlight the importance of considering epigenetic inheritance as a key dimension of pollutant toxicity. Future studies should explore whether interventions targeting chromatin modifiers or UPRER activators can mitigate nanoplastic-induced immunosuppression. Integrating multi-omics approaches, including ChIP-seq and single-cell RNA-seq, may further elucidate how specific cell types encode and transmit environmental stress memories.

In summary, our study reveals that PS-NPs impair innate immunity in C. elegans through inhibition of the IRE-1/XBP-1 branch of the UPRER and downregulation of collagen genes (Fig. 8). These effects are transmitted across generations via reduced H3K36me3 methylation mediated by MES-4, establishing a heritable immune memory independent of small RNAs or DNA methylation. The elucidation of this H3K36me3-UPRER-collagen regulatory axis provides a mechanistic framework for understanding how environmental nanoplastics can exert transgenerational impacts on immune function and underscores the broader concept that epigenetic inheritance links environmental exposure to long-term organismal health.

Parental exposure to PS-NPs inhibits the IRE-1/XBP-1 branch of the UPRER and downregulates collagen gene expression, compromising both molecular and structural immunity. PS-NPs also reduce germline histone H3K36me3 levels via MES-4, establishing an epigenetic mark that transmits transcriptional repression of UPRER and collagen-related genes to F1-F2 progeny. Methods

Worm strains and cultivation

Worms were maintained and propagated under standard conditions [42, 44]. The following C. elegans strains were provided from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (CGC at the University of Minnesota, USA). The strains used in this study included: N2 Bristol wild-type, DCL569 (mkcSi13 II, rde-1(mkc36), NR222 (rde-1(ne219);kzIs9), NR350 (rde-1(ne219);kzIs20), VP303(rde-1(ne219);kbls7), MAH677 sid-1(qt9), AY102 pmk-1(km25), KU25 pmk-1(km25), CF1038 daf-16(mu86), EU1 skn-1(zu67), VC3201 atfs-1(gk3094), RB772 atf-6(ok551), RB545 pek-1(ok275), RB925 ire-1(ok799), SJ17xbp-1(zc12), VC1099 hsp-4(gk514), RB1025 set-2(ok952), VC967 set-32(ok1457), VC2862 utx-1(ok3553), VC2552 F09F7.7(ok3133), SX922 prg-1(n4357), WM194 csr-1(tm892), YY453 nrde-4(gg129), YY538 hrde-1(tm1200), VC1874 mes-4(ok2326).

Bacterial strains

The bacterial strains used included Escherichia coli OP50 and P. aeruginosa PA14, cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C [47]. RNAi bacterial strains from the Ahringer library were grown in LB supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin at 37 ℃.

Survival assay of C. elegans resistance to P. aeruginosa PA14

All the resistance assays against P. aeruginosa PA14 were conducted at 25 ℃ according to the standard protocol [44]. Synchronized L4-stage N2 (wild-type) or mutant worms were transferred onto NGM (Nematode Growth Medium) plates with or without PS-NPs and exposed for 48 h. PS-NPs were premixed with E. coli OP50 and evenly spread onto NGM plates before use.

After the 48-h exposure, a portion of the worms were directly subjected to the parental innate immunity assay, while the remaining worms were used for synchronization to generate unexposed F1 offspring. For both parental and offspring assays, adult worms (P0) or L4 larvae (F1/F2) were transferred onto NGM plates seeded with P. aeruginosa PA14 and supplemented with 50 μM FUDR (Sigma-Aldrich). To prepare P. aeruginosa PA14 plates, 100 μL of PA14 culture was spread onto each NGM plate, incubated at 37 °C for 16 h, and then equilibrated at 25 °C for at least 2 h before use [18, 39].

RNAi-based resistance assays were conducted using the same procedures except that worms were fed RNAi bacteria grown on NGM plates containing 5 mM IPTG and 100 μg/mL ampicillin. All survival assays were performed at 25 °C, and worm viability was monitored every 12 h until all animals had died. Each experiment included more than 40 worms per condition and was independently repeated three times. Survival data are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA interference

The bacterial strain HT115 (DE3) carrying the empty vector L4440 as a control and the RNAi bacteria required for the experiment were used. All RNAi clones were verified before use. These RNAi bacteria were cultured overnight at 37 ℃ with a rotation speed of 180 rpm in LB liquid medium containing ampicillin at a concentration of 100 μg/mL. Then, the RNAi bacteria were freshly inoculated into NGM medium containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 5 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) and incubated overnight at 25 ℃ [43, 45, 62]. Gene-specific RNAi was achieved by transferring L1 larvae onto the above-mentioned fresh plates.

Airyscan laser confocal microscope

The N2 Bristol wild-type strain was synchronized, and the eggs were transferred onto NGM plates. When worms reached the L4 stage, they were exposed to NGM plates with or without 1000 μg/L PS-NPs for 48 h. Images were acquired using an Airyscan laser confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) with a 40 × objective lens and 10 × eyepiece magnification. Three plates were used per experiment, each containing approximately 40 worms. All experiments were independently repeated three times. For statistical analysis, 30 worms per plate were randomly selected (30 × 3 in total).

Quantitative real-time PCR

For the parental (P0) generation, synchronized L4-stage worms were transferred onto NGM plates either without PS-NPs (control) or containing 1000 μg/L PS-NPs and exposed for 48 h. Worms were then transferred to P. aeruginosa PA14 plates for infection for an additional 48 h.

For the first filial generation (F1), P0 worms that had been exposed or unexposed to PS-NPs were synchronized, and the resulting larvae were grown to the L4 stage before being transferred to PA14 plates for 48 h of infection. The same procedure was applied to the second filial generation (F2), in which synchronized F1 worms were grown to the L4 stage and subsequently infected with PA14 for 48 h.

For RNA extraction, worms were washed from plates into 15 mL centrifuge tubes using M9 buffer, washed several times to remove bacteria, and allowed to settle by gravity. Worm pellets were transferred to a pre-chilled mortar, flash-frozen with liquid nitrogen, and ground thoroughly. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and cDNA was synthesized according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following optimization of primer annealing temperatures and amplification efficiencies, quantitative real-time PCR was performed using standard conditions. The gene pmp-3 was used as an internal reference [41, 54, 57], and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. All experiments were independently repeated three times. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

H3K36me3 ChIP-qPCR

The ChIP assay was performed using the ChIP Assay Kit (Beyotime, P2078, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nematode samples were collected from PS-NPs-exposed and control groups across parental and offspring generations. Worm pellets were washed three times with ice-cold M9 buffer and homogenized in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle. The homogenate was incubated in cross-linking buffer (M9 containing 2% formaldehyde) at 37 °C for 10 min, and the reaction was quenched with glycine to a final concentration of 0.125 M, followed by rotation at 25 °C for 5 min.

Samples were then washed with SDS lysis buffer, and complete lysis was achieved by incubation on ice for 10 min. Chromatin was fragmented by sonication using a 2-mm probe at 40 W (5-s pulse on, 10-s pulse off) for 12.5 min. The lysate was diluted with ChIP dilution buffer, and 10% of each sample was saved as an input control. The remaining lysate was pre-cleared with Protein A/G agarose beads pre-blocked with salmon sperm DNA for 30 min at 4 °C with rotation.

After centrifugation (1000 g, 1 min, 4 °C), the supernatant was transferred to new tubes and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-H3K36me3 antibody (CST #4909, 1:50). For negative controls, PBS was used instead of primary antibody. Immune complexes were captured with Protein A/G agarose/salmon sperm DNA for 1 h at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation (1000 g, 1 min, 4 °C). Beads were washed sequentially with low-salt wash buffer (1 ×, 5 min), high-salt wash buffer (1 ×, 5 min), LiCl wash buffer (1 ×, 5 min), and TE buffer (2 ×, 5 min).

Chromatin was eluted in elution buffer (1% SDS, 0.1 M NaHCO₃) at 25 °C for 15 min. Cross-links were reversed by adding NaCl to a final concentration of 0.3 M and heating at 65 °C for 4 h. DNA was purified using the Beyotime PCR/DNA Purification Kit (D0033) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Purified DNA was subjected to quantitative PCR (qPCR) [35]. For each target gene, three biological replicates were analyzed, and each biological replicate was measured in triplicate. Primer sequences used for ChIP-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Western blot analysis

After washing the parental C. elegans exposed or unexposed to PS-NPs and their offspring C. elegans with M9 buffer, grind the C. elegans thoroughly with liquid nitrogen. Mix the obtained homogenate with the protein lysate, incubate it on ice for 30 min, and then centrifuge at 12,000 rpm for 20 min to separate the supernatant. Quantify the protein concentration using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay method to obtain a protein sample with a total protein amount of 80 μg/μL. Separate the sample using a 12% SDS–polyacrylamide gel, transfer the proteins to a membrane, and perform exposure and development after incubating with the primary antibody and the corresponding secondary antibody.

Phosphorylated IRE1 protein was detected using an anti-IRE1 (phospho S724) antibody from rabbit (1:1000 dilution, Abcam, ab48187), an anti-XBP1 antibody from rabbit (1:1000 dilution, Abcam, ab220783), an ATF6 antibody from rabbit (1:1000 dilution; HUABIO, EM1701-94), a Tri-Methyl-Histone H3 (Lys36) antibody from rabbit (1:1000 dilution, CST, #4909), a Tri-Methyl-Histone H3 from rabbit (Lys27) (1:1000 dilution, CST, #9733), an Anti-Histone H3 antibody from rabbit (1:1000 dilution, Abcam, ab1791), an Anti-Histone H3 (trimethyl K4) antibody from rabbit (1:1000 dilution, Abcam, ab8580), a Histone H3 (tri methyl K9) antibody from mouse (1:1000 dilution, HUABIO, M1112-3), an Anti-PERK antibody from rabbit (1:1000 dilution, Abcam, ab229912), and a GAPDH Antibody (1:1000 dilution, HUABIO, ET1601-4).

Statistical analysis

All experiments in this study were independently repeated three times, and the data were expressed as the mean ± SEM. All statistical data were calculated using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) software. After testing the equal distribution and equal variance of the data within the dataset, the student's t-test was used to analyze the statistical significance of the data between groups. For the survival rate analysis, the Kaplan–Meier test was used to analyze the survival rate, and the log-rank test was used to calculate the P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant statistical difference, and NS was considered to indicate no statistical difference.
