Descriptive analysis of frequency and antimicrobial susceptibility of pathogens isolated from acute leukemia patients with febrile neutropenia over a four-year period at a tertiary hospital
Authors: Adan Iftekhar, Afia Zafar, Mohammad Zeeshan, Usman Sheikh, Zehra Fadoo, Syed Waqas, Ammarah Baig, Ayesha Sadiqa, Muhammad Shayan Ashfaq, Seema Irfan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFebrile neutropenia (FN) is a medical emergency in patients with acute leukemia (AL), which increases the risk of life-threatening infections. Emerging antimicrobial resistance (AMR) further complicates management. The study aims to identify pathogen frequency and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns.MethodsA retrospective descriptive study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital from January 2020 to December 2023. Data on AL patients with FN were retrieved from the hospital information system using ICD codes.ResultsOf 478 FN episodes identified, 217 (45%) were microbiologically documented. The population consisted of 61.7% males, equally divided between adults (mean age, 38 years) and children (mean age, 8.4 years). AML was the predominant leukemia subtype (52.4%). Respiratory infections (40.5%) and gastrointestinal infections (33.6%) were the leading sources, with 21% having an unknown focus. Chest X-ray detected infection signs in 37.3% of episodes. Hospital stays exceeded 10 days in 67% of admissions, with mortality at 16.5%. A total of 335 pathogens were identified from 217 episodes, with 20.7% involving multiple infections and 4.6% polymicrobial. Gram-negative rods (GNRs) predominated (45.6%), followed by gram-positive cocci (GPCs) (30.7%), fungi (12.2%), viruses (7%), parasites (2.3%), and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (1.7%). Blood cultures yielded 71% of isolates. Leading GNRs were Escherichia coli (37.7%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (14.5%), and Klebsiella species (13.2%). Among GPCs, Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) (41.1%), Enterococcus spp. (22.5%), and Staphylococcus aureus (18.6%) predominated. Common fungal pathogens included Aspergillus flavus complex (29.2%) and Candida parapsilosis (14.6%). Antimicrobial resistance among Enterobacterales was 77% to ceftriaxone, 63% to piperacillin–tazobactam, and 47% to carbapenems. Among GPCs, 95% of CoNS and 72% of S. aureus were methicillin-resistant; 76% of Enterococcus were vancomycin-resistant. Overall, 64% of isolates were MDR (62% GPCs, 65.6% GNRs). MDR infections had significantly higher mortality than non-MDR infections (14.0% vs. 3.5%, p = 0.012). Meropenem and vancomycin were the most commonly used empiric agents in FN patients, each administered in 70% of the episodes, however 41% of the FN episodes were not adaequately covered by the empirical regimen.ConclusionFN in AL patients is frequently associated with infections with GNRs, with a high prevalence of MDR organisms contributing to increased mortality. Surveillance of pathogen distribution and resistance patterns is essential to guide empiric therapy and improve outcomes in this high-risk population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Febrile neutropenia, Acute leukemia, Antimicrobial susceptibility, Multidrug resistance, Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)

Introduction

Febrile neutropenia (FN) is defined as fever in the setting of neutropenia, it represents a major oncologic emergency and remains one of the most serious complications in patients undergoing treatment for hematologic malignancies [1, 2]. Among hematologic malignancies, patients with acute leukemia (AL) experience the most profound and prolonged neutropenia, making them particularly vulnerable to severe infections [3]. The underlying immune dysfunction, driven by impaired granulocyte function, creates an environment conducive to a wide range of pathogens [4]. Moreover, repeated hospitalizations and prolonged treatments further increase the likelihood of hospital-acquired infections with multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens. Early and appropriate empiric antibiotic administration within the first hour of suspicion remains the cornerstone of management [5], whereas delays in appropriate therapy, whether due to antimicrobial resistance or misdiagnosis, are established risk factors for poor prognosis [6]. Severe complications due to delayed diagnosis and limited access to intensive care facilities increase mortality tenfold in low-resource settings [7]. Pathogen profile and resistance pattern could guide antibiotic selection.

In higher income countries (HICs), around the era of 1970–80s, Gram-negative rods (GNRs) predominated, but following prophylactic use of fluoroquinolone and central line insertions, Gram-positive cocci (GPCs) became more common [8], particularly Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS), viridans streptococci, and enterococci [9]. Methicillin-resistant CoNS (MR-CoNS) are reported at very high rates among cancer patients [10]. Resistant Enterococcus species, particularly those with ampicillin and vancomycin resistance, are especially relevant in transplant populations, where they rank among the leading causes of bloodstream infection [11].

GNRs are now being reported more frequently than GPCs in many centers [12, 13]. GNRs such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter species, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa remain clinically significant [14, 15], complicated by the increasing prevalence of extended-spectrum β-lactamase and carbapenemase-producing MDR strains, underscoring the evolving resistance landscape in high and low-income countries [16–19].

Studies from Pakistan have shown similar trends. A 1991 study conducted at our tertiary care hospital found P. aeruginosa as the most frequent isolate, while a later study conducted at the same center (1999–2002) showed an almost equal distribution of GPCs and GNRs [20]. By 2016–2017, S. aureus and CoNS had become predominant pathogens [21]. Another study from the same period reported that GNRs were the most prevalent organisms [22].

In light of the observed shift in the frequency and distribution of isolated organisms over time, and the increasing antimicrobial resistance (AMR) we aimed to analyze the frequency and antimicrobial susceptibility of pathogens isolated from AL patients with FN over a four-year period.

Methods

This descriptive, retrospective, single-center study was conducted at the Microbiology Laboratory, Department of Pathology and Lab medicine, Aga Khan University, Karachi, Pakistan from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2023.

Study population

Patients with AL who developed FN at Aga Khan University Hospital, Karachi, Pakistan, were included in the study.

Inclusion criteria

Male and female, adult and pediatric patients with AL, who developed FN and had microbiologically documented infection (MDI) been included in the study.

The pediatric population was defined as patients aged 1–17 years.

Exclusion criteria

AL patients who developed FN and did not have a MDI were excluded from the study.

Specimens included

Clinical specimens submitted for culture—including blood, tracheal aspirates, bronchoalveolar lavage, ear, nose, wound, nasopharyngeal and throat swabs, urine, stool, cerebrospinal fluid, and central venous catheter tip samples were included in the analysis. In addition to pathogenic isolates, organisms classified as colonizers or potential contaminants were also analyzed, given the high-risk nature of this patient population. These organisms were reported by the laboratory, as per lab protocol, due to the possibility of their isolation in subsequent cultures, their association with central lines, or their potential to cause genuine infection. However, contaminants were excluded from pathogen frequency analysis.

Specimens submitted for non–culture-based diagnostic testing were also included for the detection of viral, parasitic, bacterial and fungal infections. These diagnoses were established through serological assays, antigen detection tests, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and microscopy as reported in the laboratory records.

Data collection

The FN episodes were identified from the medical records of AL patients through ICD Code. Each FN event of a patient was taken as a single episode. Electronic medical records of patients were retrospectively reviewed. Relevant data were extracted from the Hospital Information Management System (HIMS) using patients’ medical record (MR) numbers, with admission and discharge dates cross-verified to ensure accurate identification of each hospitalization episode. Data was collected and recorded by two reviewers in a secure Excel sheet, with cross-verification of all entries. Any duplicate entries were excluded, defining duplicate as the same organism with an identical antimicrobial susceptibility pattern recovered from the same patient during the same hospitalization; only the first isolate was included in the analysis.

Operational definitions

FN was defined as a single oral temperature ≥ 38.3 °C or sustained ≥ 38.0 °C for over 1 h in a patient with absolute neutrophil count (ANC) < 500 cells/µL or expected to fall below 500 cells/µL within 48 h [23].

Potential contaminant was defined as organism meeting most of the following criteria: isolated from a single culture or from a single blood culture bottle, presence of signs and symptoms not attributable to the site of organism isolation, absence of radiological and laboratory evidence of infection, a non-complicated hospital course with discharge within 3–4 days of admission without escalation of antibiotic therapy, or concurrent isolation of another true pathogen. Low virulent organisms meeting the above criteria such as CoNS, Leuconostoc species, Corynaebacterium species, Pseudomonas stutzeri were included.

Colonizer was defined as an organism isolated from a blood culture of central line but absent in peripheral culture, or as organism present in low count from a non-sterile site without signs or symptoms attributable to that organism at that site, and without radiological and laboratory evidence of infection. Organisms meeting the above criteria included CoNs isolated from central line blood cultures or catheter tips, Aspergillus and Klebsiella spp. from respiratory samples, and Group B β-hemolytic streptococci and Enterococcus species from urine. However, these were included in pathogen frequency analysis.

Fungal infections were classified as invasive—proven, probable, or possible— as per Consensus definitions of the Infectious Diseases Group of the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer and the Mycoses Study Group (EORTC-MSG) [24].

Polymicrobial infection was defined as the isolation of more than one pathogen from the same specimen of same date.

Multiple infection was defined as the recovery of different organisms either from the same site at different times during a prolonged admission, or from different specimen sources around the same time, reflecting more than one active site of infection.

MDR among GNR and GPC was determined based on previously published consolidated criteria as resistance to at least one antimicrobial agent in three or more antimicrobial classes [25].

Identification of microorganisms and antibiotic susceptibility

All the culturable microorganisms were identified using standard microbiological protocols; microscopy (Gram stain, lactophenol cotton blue stain, corn meal tween agar findings, Kinyoun stain), colony morphology, and conventional biochemical characteristics throughout the study period. For selected bacterial pathogens, particularly those demonstrating resistant phenotypes, identification was further confirmed using commercially available API bioMérieux strips. Additionally, all yeast isolates apart from Candida albicans and Candida tropicalis were identified using the Yeast Vitek®ID card (Automated instrument for identification).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of bacterial isolates from sterile sites (e.g. blood, csf) was performed using both the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method (Oxoid™ disks) and automated minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination using the Vitek® system (bioMérieux) throughout the study period. For isolates recovered from non-sterile specimens, susceptibility testing was conducted using disk diffusion where applicable, and MIC testing via the Vitek® system was performed selectively for organisms, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas species, and urinary Enterobacterales, or when clinically indicated. For colistin MIC, Broth microdilution (BMD) method was used.

Antifungal susceptibility testing (AFST) was performed using the disk diffusion method on methylene blue–supplemented Mueller–Hinton agar for C. albicans, C. tropicalis, and Candida parapsilosis. For all other Candida species, and one resistant C. parapsilosis, MICs were determined using the BMD method with YeastOne® Sensititre plates.

For most of the bacterial isolates, antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed and interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [26] applicable during the study period and European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) [27] were applied only in a small number of isolated cases. For most of the fungal isolates, AFST was performed and interpreted according to CLSI [28, 29] and EUCAST criteria was used in limited number of cases [30].

Susceptibility data for GNRs other than Enterobacterales were grouped as ‘other GNRs’ because individual species were present in limited numbers. Susceptibility data for GPCs, including CoNS and S. aureus, were grouped as Staphylococcus species, while Enterococcus and Streptococcus species were grouped as ‘other GPCs’ due to the same reason.

Ethical approval

Exemption (Case No. 2023-8609-24894) was granted by the Ethical Review Committee of Aga Khan University Hospital.

Statistical analysis

The data were coded, organized, and entered into Stata 17.0. Descriptive statistics such as frequencies, percentages, mean, mode, median, ranges were used to present the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the participants, as well as the frequency of isolated organisms and their antimicrobial susceptibility patterns.

Results

Over a four-year period, January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2023, a total of 478 FN episodes were recorded. Among these, no MDI was identified in 261 episodes (54.6%), while 217 (45%) FN events were associated with MDI, corresponding to 162 distinct patients, some of whom were admitted multiple times and experienced multiple or polymicrobial infections during different episodes.

Sociodemographic, clinical characteristics and outcome of the study population

The patient population was evenly divided between adults (n = 81) and children (n = 81) (50% each). The age of the study population ranged from 1 to 83 years. The pediatric group had a mean age of 8.4 ± 5.15 years, while the adult group had a mean age of 38 ± 13.95 years. Of the total patients, 100 (61.7%) were males, while 62 (38.2%) were females. The AL subtypes were nearly equally represented (ALL n=77; 47.5%, AML n=85; 52.4%). In adults, diabetes mellitus was the most common comorbidity (n=8; 9.8%), followed by hypertension and atrial fibrillation (n=6 each; 7.4%), whereas Down syndrome was seen in 2 children (2.4%). The focus of fever was most commonly attributable to respiratory tract infections in 88 cases (40.5%), followed by gastrointestinal tract infections in 73 cases (33.6%), while the focus remained unknown in 46 cases (21.0%). Most patients were on active or recent chemotherapy (n = 202, 93%), and had central venous lines (n = 151, 69.5%). At presentation, 189 patients (87%) had severe neutropenia (ANC < 500 cells/µL), while 28 patients (13%) presented with an ANC of approximately 1000 cells/µL that subsequently declined to below 500 cells/µL during hospitalization. The median ANC was 68 (IQR: 0–358), while the mean ANC was 212.5 ± 297.1. A subset had undergone bone marrow transplantation (n = 17; 8%) or had multiple prior hospital admissions (n = 85; 39.2%). A total of 145 FN episodes (67%) required hospitalization for more than 10 days. Septic shock requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission occurred in 64 episodes (29.4%). Among all FN episodes, 36 (16.5%) were fatal, whereas in 181 (83.4%) episodes, patients were successfully discharged as shown in Table 1.

Imaging evidence of infection in FN episodes

Among 217 FN episodes, signs of infection were detected in 81 episodes (37.3%) on chest X-ray, 54 episodes (24.9%) on Computed Tomography (CT), 18 episodes (8.3%) on ultrasound, and 11 episodes (5.1%) on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Some episodes had positive findings on more than one imaging modality.

Multiple and polymicrobial infections

Out of 217 FN episodes, 45 (20.7%) involved multiple infections, 10 (4.6%) were polymicrobial, most commonly from blood (6/10, 60%), and 11 (5.1%) had both multiple and polymicrobial infections.

Microbial profiles

A total of 381 organisms were identified from 217 FN episodes in 162 patients, comprising 342 culturable and 39 non-culturable isolates. GNRs accounted for 154 (40.4%), GPCs for 148 (39%) and fungi 41 (11%) of the isolates. Potential contaminants included 46 isolates (45 gram-positive and 1 gram-negative), while colonizers comprised 28 isolates (26 gram-positive, 1 gram-negative, and 1 fungal). After excluding contaminants (n = 46), a total of 335 pathogens were identified, with GNRs significantly more prevalent (n = 153; 45.6%) compared to GPCs (n = 103; 30.7%) (p-value < 0.001), followed by fungal (n = 41; 12.2%) viruses (n = 24; 7%), parasites (n = 8; 2.3%), and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) (n = 6; 1.7%) as shown in Fig. 1. Among the fungal infections, 27 (69%) cases were classified as invasive fungal disease, including 19 (48.7%) cases of candidemia and 8 (20.5%) proven invasive fungal infections from other sites. The remaining cases included 4 (10.2%) classified as probable and 5 (13%) as possible invasive fungal infection.

Antimicrobial agents administered before culture and appropriateness of empiric antimicrobial therapy

During the study period, ciprofloxacin, acyclovir and fluconazole were used as prophylactic agents in our institution. For empiric therapy in FN patients, meropenem and vancomycin were most frequently used (n = 151; 70% each), followed by pip-taz (n = 117; 54%) and amikacin (n = 89; 41%). The empiric antimicrobial regimen was appropriate in 128 (59.0%) of 217 episodes, while in 89 (41.0%) episodes, the empiric regimen was not adequate to cover the isolated organisms.

Antimicrobial susceptibility pattern of GNRs

Among Enterobacterales, 30/81 (37%) were susceptible to pip-taz and 20/87 (23%) to ceftriaxone, while 38/72 (53%) were susceptible to meropenem. Non-Enterobacterales GNR were susceptible to pip-taz (28/41, 68%), ceftazidime (27/44, 61%), and meropenem (32/52, 61.5%), as shown in Fig. 4.

Antimicrobial susceptibility pattern of Candida species

None of the three C. auris isolates were susceptible to fluconazole. Fluconazole susceptibility was observed in five out of six isolates of C. parapsilosis, while all tested isolates of C. albicans (n = 3) and C. tropicalis (n = 4) were susceptible to azoles. All tested Candida isolates were susceptible to caspofungin (n = 17; 100%) and amphotericin B (n = 6; 100%).

Prevalence of multidrug-resistant organisms and associated mortality

Overall multidrug resistance could be assessed in 234 clinically significant isolates, of which 150 were classified as MDR, corresponding to an overall prevalence of 64%. Among GPCs, 62 of 100 (62.0%) met the criteria for MDR, while 88 of 134 GNRs (65.6%) were classified as MDR. Mortality was higher in patients infected with MDR isolates, occurring in 21 of 149 cases (14.0%), compared with 3 of 85 cases (3.5%) in the non-MDR group (p-value = 0.012, Fisher’s exact test).

Microbiological profile of infections in admissions exceeding 10 days

Among 158 admissions with hospitalization exceeding 10 days and clinically significant isolated pathogens, 113 episodes (71.5%) were associated with MDR organisms. Of the remaining 45 episodes, 25 (16%) episodes involved susceptible bacterial isolates, MRSA was identified in one episode, two involved Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, and one involved Burkholderia cepacia. Fungal pathogens were identified in 12 (7.5%) episodes, while parasitic and viral infections were identified in two episodes each.

Discussion

The antimicrobial susceptibility pattern observed in our study highlights a substantial burden of MDR organisms, with resistance spanning multiple antimicrobial classes. Enterobacterales were the most frequently isolated GNRs and demonstrated high levels of resistance to commonly used beta-lactam antibiotics, including third-generation cephalosporins, amoxicillin–clavulanate, pip–taz, and carbapenems, underscoring the limited efficacy of standard empiric regimens in our setting. These findings are broadly comparable to a large multicenter study conducted across 16 countries, which reported reduced susceptibility rates of 66.1% for meropenem and 53.6% for pip-taz, values that closely mirror those observed in our cohort [31]. In contrast, studies from Western world reported substantially lower resistance among Enterobacterales [32, 33]. Fluoroquinolone susceptibility was markedly reduced in our isolates, with 87% of Enterobacterales demonstrating resistance to ciprofloxacin. This high resistance rate likely reflects widespread fluoroquinolone exposure, including prophylactic use in high-risk patients. Despite concerns that colonization with fluoroquinolone-resistant Enterobacterales (FQRE) may compromise the effectiveness of prophylaxis [34], prior data in hematopoietic cell transplant recipients suggest that fluoroquinolone prophylaxis may still reduce the frequency of FN and bacteremia, even among patients colonized with FQRE [35].

The antimicrobial susceptibility of other GNRs shows that β-lactam agents have only moderate effectiveness, with meropenem, pip-taz, and ceftazidime demonstrating 61–68% susceptibility. A notable increase in resistance among P. aeruginosa isolates was observed, with resistance to key agents such as pip–taz, ceftazidime, ciprofloxacin, and meropenem reaching 16–29%, when compared with historical data from the same institution, where imipenem and ceftazidime resistance among P. aeruginosa isolates from FN patients during 2001–2006 was only 6% [36]. A similar trend has been observed globally, with an increasing rate of MDR P. aeruginosa in this population [37]. Likewise, the antibiotic resistance pattern of Acinetobacter species in this study underscores its MDR nature, with nearly half of the isolates resistant to β-lactams, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, and carbapenems. This high level of resistance limits treatment options and aligns with trends reported in other studies [38, 39].

In contrast to β-lactam and fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides retained relatively preserved activity in our cohort, with above 80% of Enterobacterales and other GNRs remaining susceptible to amikacin, supporting its continued role as part of combination empiric therapy. Colistin exhibited the highest intermediate susceptibility against MDR Enterobacterales, P. aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter species (86.6% of GNRs). Tigecycline susceptibility, though checked in limited number of isolates, was also over 90% among Enterobacterales and 100% in Acinetobacter species. However, the use of these agents should be judicious due to potential toxicity, limited tissue penetration, and the risk of emerging resistance.

The high prevalence of methicillin resistance among GPCs in our cohort reflects a trend seen in immunocompromised and FN populations, where resistant staphylococcal strains are commonly reported [40]. Despite this, staphylococci and streptococcus species retained full vancomycin susceptibility. In contrast, the high proportion of vancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VRE) in our population is particularly concerning. This emerging threat has been previously highlighted in a study that identified VRE in three tertiary care hospitals within the country [41]. In 2017, the prevalence of VRE reached approximately 50% in Australia and surpassed 20% in multiple European countries [42, 43].

All Candida species were susceptible to echinocandins and amphotericin B. However, a few azole-resistant isolates were observed. C. auris (n = 3) and one isolate each of C. parapsilosis and C. rugosa were resistant to fluconazole. Isolation of fluconazole-resistant C. auris is consistent with global alerts regarding this pathogen’s rapid spread in healthcare settings [44]. Although C. parapsilosis was historically considered fully susceptible to fluconazole, resistance in C. parapsilosis is of particular concern, as this species has been increasingly reported to develop reduced susceptibility to azoles due to biofilm formation and genetic mutations [45]. One of the 20 Candida isolates in our study was C. rugosa, a species that studies indicate is not only emerging as a cause of invasive fungal infections but is also exhibiting a trend toward increased resistance to azole antifungal agents [46].

In this study, GNR was more prevalent than GPC among the isolated pathogens. This predominance of GNR aligns with trends observed in immunocompromised patients, particularly those with chemotherapy-induced neutropenia, where factors such as gut translocation, mucosal barrier injury, and the use of invasive devices contribute to an increased risk of GNR infections [47, 48]. Similar to our findings, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and Klebsiella spp. were the most common organisms frequently implicated in serious infections among neutropenic hosts [49, 50]. In contrast, GPCs, though less frequent than GNRs, remain clinically important, often associated with central line–related infections and skin or mucosal colonization. BSIs were the most frequent source of culture-positive episodes (71.3%), followed by the respiratory tract, consistent with the findings of other studies [51].

In a similar study from our institution, fungal isolates in blood cultures of FN patients were reported at approximately 4% [36], whereas in the current study, 7.7% were fungal. A more recent study reported a 5% prevalence of candidemia among febrile pediatric patients with lymphoreticular malignancies, with the majority of cases attributed to non-albicans Candida species [52]. In our study, Aspergillus flavus complex and C. parapsilosis were the most frequent fungal isolates. A. flavus has been reported as the predominant Aspergillus species in both clinical and environmental isolates in Pakistan [53]. While C. albicans was traditionally the most frequent species, recent years have seen a significant rise in non-albicans Candida infections, with C. parapsilosis now emerging as a cause of invasive candidiasis [54, 55].

Of 478 recorded FN episodes, MDI were identified in 45.3% of cases, aligning closely with global data showing that 30–50% of FN episodes are associated with a defined infectious etiology [56]. In our study, the focus of fever was most commonly respiratory (88 episodes, 40.5%) and gastrointestinal (73 episodes, 33.6%), while in 46 episodes (21.0%) the focus remained unknown. Standard imaging modalities, including chest X-ray, CT, MRI, and ultrasound, detected signs of infection in many FN episodes, but in cases where the focus remained undetected, emerging non-invasive approaches such as pathogen-targeted PET tracers, CT radiomics and advanced imaging modalities may offer enhanced detection [57–60]. In our cohort, 67% of FN patients had a hospital stay exceeding 10 days, which aligns with evidence that hematologic malignancies, high-dose chemotherapy regimens, prolonged neutropenia, and BSI caused by MDR GNR have been identified as key predictors of extended hospital length of stay among cancer patients with FN [61]. The predominance of MDR organisms among admissions with prolonged hospitalization reflects the substantial burden of AMR in this patient population. The identification of non-fermenting GNRs and invasive fungal pathogens further reflects the complexity of infections encountered during extended hospital stays. Nearly one-third of FN episodes progressed to septic shock requiring ICU admission, with a mortality rate of 16.5% in this cohort, which is comparable to rates reported in other LMIC settings [62], but remains higher than those observed in HICs [63]. Mortality was higher in patients infected with MDR isolates, when compared with the non-MDR group. Two patients infected with MDR-VRE developed infective endocarditis, both of whom had relapsed AML, prolonged hospitalization (> 10 days), and one had undergone bone marrow transplantation; unfortunately, both cases resulted in mortality. These findings emphasize the need for early recognition of infection and aggressive management in high-risk patients.

In our cohort, modification of empiric therapy was required in many cases to adequately cover the isolated organisms. In contrast, studies from HICs report that standard empiric regimens, are generally adequate and yield good outcomes [32, 33]. The discrepancy likely reflects differences in local resistance patterns, with higher prevalence of MDR Enterobacterales in our population contributing to the reduced adequacy of empiric therapy and the frequent need for modification.

The limitation of the current study is that it is a single-center, retrospective design, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings. Additionally, molecular characterization of resistant organisms, including the detection of resistance enzymes and genes, was not performed. This limits our ability to fully understand the underlying mechanisms of resistance and their potential for nosocomial transmission. Although the use of antimicrobials in FN episodes was taken into account, detailed information on the duration, exact dosing, and precise timing of initiation and discontinuation was not evaluated, therefore, the study is limited by the lack of detailed and comprehensive data on antimicrobial use.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that GNRs were the predominant pathogens identified in FN among patients with AL, with a substantial burden of MDR organisms and a high mortality rate. Continuous local AMR surveillance, timely consideration of modification in empiric choice, and integration of rapid diagnostic tests are essential to support informed clinical decision-making and improve outcomes in this high-risk group.
