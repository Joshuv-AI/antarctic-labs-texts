TGF-β–induced Tregs release extracellular vesicles that modulate CD4+ T-cell proliferation and phenotype in a Nrp1-dependent manner
Authors: María José Moya-Guzmán, Eduardo González-Mienert, Camila Pinto, Karina Pino-Lagos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuropilin-1 (Nrp1) is one of the receptors for TGF-β and is associated with the suppressive function of T regulatory cells (Tregs). Based on previous findings on natural Tregs, this work investigated the role of Nrp1 on induced Tregs (iTregs) and their small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) by using a conditional knockout for Nrp1 on FoxP3+ cells. sEVs derived from iTregs were obtained by ultracentrifugation, purified using size exclusion columns, and characterized by nanoparticle tracking analysis. sEVs’ impact on CD4+ T-cell cultures was evaluated using functional modulation assays, including CD4+ T-cell phenotypic analysis by flow cytometry and ELISA. We observed a decrease in iTreg induction when CD4+ T cells came from the Nrp1 knockout (KO) mice, indicating that Nrp1 is required for de novo FoxP3 induction/expression. Second, CD4+ T cells cultured with sEV-derived from wild-type (WT) iTregs reduced CD4+ T-cell proliferation, decreased the expression of CD25, and induced FoxP3 and CD73. In contrast, sEVs derived from Nrp1KO iTregs were unable to suppress CD4+ T-cell proliferation and to favor CD25 and CD73 expression. With respect to cytokine production, both sEVs derived from WT and Nrp1KO iTregs inhibited IL-10 and IFN-γ secretion, indicating that Nrp1 is not required for the regulation of these cytokines by sEV. Overall, this study provides new information into the role of Nrp1 in immunomodulation, revealing that Nrp1 signaling plays a role in iTreg differentiation by permitting FoxP3 expression and that iTreg-derived sEVs depend on Nrp1 to control CD4+ T-cell proliferation and phenotype.

Introduction

T regulatory cells (Tregs) correspond to a specialized subpopulation of essential cells for peripheral tolerance, with the unique capability to regulate the homeostasis of the immune system through the suppression of excessive immune responses that may trigger autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammation, and tissue damage.1,2 In mouse, Tregs are characterized by the expression of the transcription factor FoxP3, which can interact with 700 to 1400 genes and is pivotal for Treg differentiation and function. Tregs can generate in the thymus through the recognition of self-antigens (also known as natural Tregs [nTregs]) or can differentiate in the periphery from CD4+FoxP3− T cells to acquire Treg function in the presence of cytokines such as IL-2, TGF-β, IL-10, and IL-35 (known as induced Tregs [iTregs]).3–6 Tregs suppress the activation and function of other immune cells, including CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, B cells, NK cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells (DCs).5 A recent cell-to-cell contact independent suppression mechanism attributed to Tregs is the release of small extracellular vesicles (sEVs), tiny particles up to 150 nm in size that may contain miRNA such as miR-155, miR-503, miR-330, miR-9, Let-7b, and Let-7d, among other molecules, which regulate multiple genes involved on activation and proliferation of effector immune cells.2 These sEVs also harbor key molecules as CD25, CD73, CTLA-4, and Neuropilin-1 (Nrp1) that participate on immune regulation.2,7

Nrp1 is a glycoprotein that belongs to the neuropilin family, a group of proteins characterized for having a unique transmembrane segment and for being highly conserved among the vertebrate lineage.8 This protein has been identified on DCs, macrophages (alveolar, bronchial, intravascular, and associated to tumors), T-cell populations (CD8+, Tregs, T follicular helper [Tfh], and NKT), mast cells, osteoblasts, gastrointestinal neuroendocrine cells, adipocytes, kidney podocytes, and olfactory epithelium.9 Due to its potential role as a Treg marker and its contribution to immune tolerance, our group has been interested in the involvement of Nrp1 in the context of transplantation. Using a murine skin transplantation model, we have reported that Nrp1 expression is downregulated on nTregs during transplant rejection, which associates with a decrease on Eos expression, a transcription factor linked to Treg stability. In the same study, adoptive transfer of nTreg Nrp1+ prevented skin transplant rejection whereas conventional CD4+ T cells (Tconv) up-regulated Nrp1 and Eos only when co-transferred with nTreg Nrp1+.10 Targeted Nrp1 depletion on Tregs revealed that this protein is necessary to exert immune suppression in vivo as transplant recipients harboring Nrp1 knockout (KO) Tregs are unable to prevent skin graft rejection due to, at least, a dysregulation on cytokine production given by limited IL-10 production and overexpression of IFN-γ.11 Furthermore, we demonstrated that nTregs secrete sEVs harboring Nrp1, which is required to suppress in vitro in a cell-to-cell independent manner and in vivo in a skin transplant model. Interestingly, sEV cargo obtained from Nrp1KO nTregs contains a modified content of proteins reflecting a reduced abundance on immune regulatory molecules such as CD25 and CD73, among others.7 Because most of the research on Tregs is based on nTregs instead of iTregs, it becomes interesting to understand the biology of iTregs since they can be considered as a more abundant source for cell therapy or, as an alternative, their sEVs. In this report we demonstrate that Nrp1 is required by CD4+ T cells to become FoxP3+ Tregs. In addition, our data indicate that Nrp1 is present on sEVs produced by iTregs and is involved on sEV-mediated suppression function and phenotype modulation.

Materials and methods

Mice

Female and male mice aged 6–8 weeks were used. FoxP3GFP, FoxP3CreYFP, and Foxp3CreYFPNrp1flox/flox mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). All mice were maintained under pathogen-free conditions at the animal facility located at Facultad de Medicina, Universidad de los Andes, Santiago, Chile. Euthanasia was conducted by CO2 asphyxiation. In none of the experiments did the animals show signs of pain or distress. This study was carried out after the revision and approval of its experimental protocol (#CEC2021017) in accordance with the recommendations of bioethical committee guidelines from the Faculty of Medicine, Universidad de los Andes, and the Agencia Nacional de Investigación y Desarrollo.

Induction of Tregs

Splenic CD4+CD25− T cells were magnetically isolated (#19852A, Stem Cell Technologies) and cultured in 24-well plates previously coated with 10 μg/mL anti-CD3 (clone 2C11, BioLegend) and 1 μg/mL anti-CD28 (clone 37.1, BioLegend) and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 5 days. For iTreg induction, 2 × 105 cells were seeded containing 1 mL of complete RPMI media (cRPMI, RPMI supplemented with penicillin-streptomycin 1% [Corning, NY, USA], HEPES 1% [Gibco, MD, USA], β-mercaptoethanol 0.1% [Sigma, MO, USA], and 10% of sEV-free FBS [Gibco]) containing 100 IU/mL of human IL-2 (#200-02, Peprotech, NJ, USA) and 10 ng/mL of hTGF-β1 (#100-21, Peprotech). After 5 days of iTregs in culture, cells were harvested for phenotype analysis by flow cytometry, and supernatants for sEV isolation.

sEV isolation and characterization

sEVs were isolated using differential centrifugation. In brief, a first step using 300 g for 5 min, followed by 2,000 × g for 20 min, was applied for cell removal and elimination of dead cells, respectively. Then, supernatants were centrifuged twice at 10,000 × g for 30 min, followed by a last step applying 100,000 × g for 90 min. The pellets were resuspended in ∼100 μL of exo-free cRPMI (prepared with ultracentrifuged FBS to remove sEVs), which were subjected to sEV enrichment using IZON Columns (qEV original/35 nm Gen 2, Christchurch, New Zealand) following the provider’s instructions. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was performed using a NanoSight NS300 (Malvern Instruments, UK), equipped with a 532-nm laser and a 565-nm LP filter. PBS 1× was used to dilute the starting samples. Three videos of 30 sec were recorded of each sample, with temperature monitored along the readings (25 °C). Videos recorded for each sample were analyzed with the NTA software to determine size and concentration of particles from samples of resuspended sEVs. The NanoSight instrument was calibrated using 100-nm diameter polystyrene latex beads and 100-nm diameter 532-nm green fluorescence standards (all from Malvern Panalytical, UK).

Western blot

Western blotting was conducted as previously outlined.12,13 In brief, sEV and cell samples were lysed in RIPA buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) on ice for 20 min. The lysates were vortexed and centrifuged at 17,200 × g for 20 min, and the supernatants were stored at −80 °C until further use. The protein concentration was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Life Technologies, USA). A 5× reducing sample buffer (60 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 25% glycerol, 2% SDS, 5% β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.04% bromophenol blue) was added to the lysates in the appropriate ratio; samples were heated at 95 °C for 10 min and then cooled to room temperature before separation using 12% SDS-PAGE. Proteins were resolved under completely denaturing and reducing conditions, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, blocked with 4% nonfat powdered milk in PBS-T (0.1% Tween-20), and probed with antibodies. The primary antibody used for immunoblotting was a mouse polyclonal anti-Nrp1 (dilution 1:200, #AF566) from R&D Systems, and the secondary antibody was Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (dilution 1:10,000, #21057) from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Protein bands were detected using Odyssey Imager equipment model 9140 from Li-COR (USA).

In vitro modulation assay

Splenic CD4+CD25− T cells were magnetically isolated (19852A, Stem Cell Technologies) and labeled with 5 µM CellTrace Violet (CTV, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 5 × 105 cells were cultured for 3 days in 500 μL of complete RPMI media in 48-well plates, previously coated with 5 μg/mL αCD3 (clone 2C11, BioLegend) and 2 μg/mL αCD28 (clone 37.51, BioLegend). Cells were treated with 2.5 × 108 sEV particles derived from iTreg cells of either WT or Nrp1KO condition. T-cell proliferation was analyzed tracking CTV dilution by flow cytometry. Suppression was calculated as previously described.14

Flow cytometry

Cell samples were stained with antibodies against CD4 (clone RM4-5 and GK15), CD25 (clone PC61), Nrp1 (clone 3E12), CD44 (clone IM7), and CD73 (clone TY/11.8). All antibodies were conjugated with PE, PerCP-Cy7, APC, or APC-Cy7 and the staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Data acquisition was performed using a BD FACSCanto II cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (TreeStar).

ELISA

Supernatants from modulation assays were collected and stored at −80 °C for cytokine quantification by ELISA (sandwich) test. Pure and biotin-conjugated antibodies for the following cytokines were purchased from BioLegend: IFN-γ (clone R4-6A2), IL-17 (clone TC11-8H4), and IL-10 (clone JES5-16E3) in addition to the corresponding recombinant murine cytokines for the standard curves (all from Peprotech).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad software (10.2.3). Mann–Whitney and one-way ANOVA (multiple comparison) tests were used. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant, and the significance is indicated in each figure legend.

Results

Nrp1 is required for the differentiation of iTregs

Previous work by us and others have shown that sEVs obtained from nTregs suppress T-cell proliferation, skew T-cell differentiation (restraining the development of Th1 cells), act on macrophage polarization, and control in vivo inflammatory responses.7,15,16 In this study we aimed at characterizing the direct effect of sEVs isolated from iTregs (instead of nTregs) on conventional CD4+ T cells and to evaluate the contribution of Nrp1 on the potential immune modulatory effect of sEV. iTregs were generated from splenic CD4+ T cells isolated from WT and FoxP3Cre/YFPNrp1flox/flox (Nrp1KO) mice, following the traditional protocol that includes polyclonal activation given by plate-bound αCD3 and αCD28, in addition to IL-2 and TGF-β.17,18 After 5 days in culture, the phenotype of iTregs was determined by flow cytometry, and culture media was processed to enrich for sEVs. Fig. 1A shows representative dot plots displaying CD4+ T-cell phenotype after isolation (day 0 of culture), with the corresponding gating strategy in Fig. S1. The bar graph on the right indicates that freshly isolated CD4+ T cells express low levels of CD25 (∼10% for both WT CD4+ T cells and Nrp1KO CD4+ T cells). Furthermore, ∼10% of WT CD4+ T cells and ∼9% of Nrp1KO CD4+ T cells express Nrp1, whereas ∼40% of WT CD4+ T cells and Nrp1KO CD4+ T cells express CD73. Moreover, after 5 days in culture, ∼95% of WT CD4+ T cells become FoxP3+, whereas only ∼55% of Nrp1KO CD4+ T cells become FoxP3+. In addition, ∼100% of WT and Nrp1KO CD4+ T cells express CD25, ∼40% of WT CD4+ T cells express Nrp1, but only ∼6% of Nrp1KO CD4+ T cells are Nrp1+ (Fig. 1B). In fact, the small frequencies of Nrp1+ cells in the Nrp1KO condition may correspond to scarce contamination of non-Tregs (FoxP3−). Last, high expression of CD73 (∼100%) was found on CD4+ T cells regardless of their genotype. Gating strategy for day 5 analysis can be found in Fig. S2. In sum, we can conclude that Nrp1 expression is required for FoxP3 expression during the differentiation of iTregs.

The lack of Nrp1 does not prevent sEV release by iTregs

sEV enrichment was performed as described in the Materials and methods. On day 5, culture media from iTregs produced from WT and Nrp1KO CD4+ T cells was collected and processed for ultracentrifugation followed by size-exclusion separation. NTA was used to determine size and concentration of sEV, observing an average size of <200 nm for sEVs obtained from WT and Nrp1KO iTregs (Fig. 2A). A detailed analysis by particle size indicates that ∼90% of sEVs fall in the 51–250 nm range regardless of their origin (Fig. 2B). However, we found a 2-fold increment on the concentration of sEVs produced by Nrp1KO iTregs compared to WT iTregs (∼2 × 1010 vs ∼1 × 1010, respectively) (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the presence of Nrp1 was detected on WT iTregs and sEVs isolated from WT iTreg cultures, whereas Nrp1KO iTregs, and sEVs, show minimal presence of this protein (Fig. 2D). Overall, we can state that Nrp1 is present on sEVs obtained from iTregs, and that the lack of Nrp1 on iTregs alters particle quantity, suggesting a role for Nrp1 on sEV biogenesis.

Nrp1 is required for iTreg-derived sEV suppressive function

Since sEVs from nTregs have suppressive activity that depends on Nrp1,7 we sought to characterize this function on sEVs obtained from iTregs. For this purpose, naive CD4+CD25− T cells were isolated from spleens of FoxP3GFP reporter animals, labeled with CTV, and activated polyclonally in vitro in the presence of 2.5 × 108 sEVs from WT or Nrp1KO iTregs. After 72 h, cells were recovered to track CTV dilution/proliferation by flow cytometry and the supernatants were collected for cytokine quantification. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, sEVs obtained from WT iTregs reduced CD4+ T-cell proliferation from ∼100% (control condition without sEV) to ∼60%. Interestingly, sEVs obtained from Nrp1KO iTregs were unable to inhibit CD4+ T-cell proliferation. The same effect was observed when calculating the percentage of suppression (Fig. 3C). In sum, our data indicate that Nrp1 is required for the modulation of CD4+ T-cell proliferation mediated by iTreg-derived sEVs.

Nrp1+ sEVs modulate CD4+ T-cell phenotype and function

During the recovery and analysis of the “modulated” CD4+ T cells, we noted that a small fraction of cells incubated with sEVs obtained from WT iTregs became FoxP3+ in comparison to activated CD4+ T cells alone (∼3% vs ∼1%, respectively). On the contrary, sEVs obtained from Nrp1KO iTregs were ineffective in triggering FoxP3 expression (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, we found a positive correlation between the ability to induce FoxP3 and the suppressive effect of the sEVs, suggesting that a short test (72 h) such as this modulatory assay could inform us as to how good of immunosuppressors sEVs may be (Fig. 4B). In addition, we studied the expression of other Tregs-related markers such as CD25, CD44, CD73, and Nrp1. As shown in Fig. 4C–F, activation upregulated the expression of CD25 (∼98% vs <0.5% in unactivated CD4+ T cells), CD44 (∼45% vs ∼20% in unactivated CD4+ T cells), and Nrp1 (∼10% vs ∼5% in unactivated CD4+ T cells). With respect to CD73, a downregulation on this molecule was found upon activation (∼55% vs ∼15% with stimulus). Furthermore, CD4+ T cells cultured with sEVs obtained from WT iTregs decreased CD25 expression (from ∼98% to ∼88% with sEVs) but upregulated CD44 (from ∼45% to ∼70% with sEVs) and CD73 (from ∼15% to∼50% with sEVs). The same trend was found for Nrp1 expression, but no statistical difference was obtained (from ∼10% to ∼18% with sEVs). This modulatory effect seems Nrp1-dependent because CD4+ T cells cultured with sEVs obtained from Nrp1KO iTregs display similar levels of CD25, CD73, and Nrp1 expression than CD4+ T cells activated alone (Fig. 4C–F). However, the upregulation of CD44 expression observed on CD4+ T cells cultured with sEVs obtained from WT iTregs was also seen when using sEVs from Nrp1KO iTregs (Fig. 4D), suggesting that Nrp1 does not control the dynamics of CD44 expression.

Next, we sought to investigate the secretion of key cytokines by sEV-modulated CD4+ T cells. Our results indicate that activated CD4+ T cells secrete IL-10, which is inhibited in the presence of sEVs obtained from WT iTregs (∼4,000 pg/mL vs ∼1,000 pg/mL, respectively). The same effect was observed on CD4+ T cells modulated by sEVs obtained from Nrp1KO iTregs (∼4,000 pg/mL vs ∼1,500 pg/mL, respectively) indicating that sEV inhibitory function on IL-10 is Nrp1-independent (Fig. 5A). Then, we also evaluated the production of IFN-γ and IL-17A (Fig. 5B, C). Our results show that CD4+ T cells produce IFN-γ upon activation (∼4,000 pg/mL vs ∼300 pg/mL on unactivated CD4+ T cells), and the addition of sEVs released by either WT or Nrp1KO iTregs decrease IFN-γ production, but no statistical significance was obtained (Fig. 5B). With respect to IL-17A, CD4+ T cells release IL-17A upon activation (∼2,500 pg/mL vs ∼0 pg/mL on unactivated CD4+ T cells), but the inclusion of sEVs obtained from WT iTregs does not change IL-17A levels. Although sEVs obtained from Nrp1KO iTregs seem to slightly increase IL-17A production, no statistical significance was obtained to conclude a modulatory role by sEVs obtained from WT or Nrp1KO iTregs on IL-17A secretion. Therefore, the results indicate that sEVs produced by iTregs regulate IL-10 and IFN-γ secretion on CD4+ T cells, which is independent of Nrp1 expression by iTregs as a source of sEVs.

Discussion

This study investigated the role of Nrp1 on sEVs obtained from Tregs produced in vitro in the presence of IL-2 and TGF-β. First, we observed that Nrp1 is required for iTreg generation, revealing the importance of this protein on CD4+ T-cell differentiation, since CD4+ T cells isolated from Nrp1KO did not upregulate FoxP3 expression as WT CD4+ T cells (Fig. 1). It has been described that TGF-β induces the expression of FoxP3 by activating SMAD3 and STAT5, 2 proteins that bind to the regulatory regions CNS1 and CNS2, respectively, of the FoxP3 gene.19 This generates key demethylation reactions that lead to FoxP3 expression. Based on this, it is tempting to suggest that Nrp1 may favor the activation of TGF-β signaling pathway upon binding to this cytokine receptors.20 In the same line, Nrp1 silencing in the presence of TGF-β results in a reduction of SMAD3 phosphorylation, whereas Nrp1 overexpression generates an increment, attributed to the promotion of TGFβR1 and TGFβR2 internalization.21 These antecedents support the notion that CD4+ T cells of Nrp1KO mice are less efficient on iTregs differentiation because the absence of Nrp1 could limit TGF-β signaling affecting FoxP3 expression. Additional studies have reported that Nrp1 controls Tregs stability, compromising Tregs’ suppressive function in the absence of Nrp1,22 and that among iTregs, 2 subsets of cells can be found based on Nrp1 expression, indicating that iTregs’ Nrp1+ is a better suppressor than iTregs’ Nrp1− counterpart.23 However, our results show that Nrp1 is required for CD4+FoxP3− T cells to become FoxP3+ (iTregs) under the differentiating conditions. With respect to sEV obtention and characterization, we did not observe differences on sEV average size or size distribution (Fig. 2), but the number of particles was highly increased in the condition using Nrp1KO iTregs as cellular source. Thus, we believe Nrp1 may be involved in vesicle biogenesis (formation, export, recycling, or degradation) because it has been reported that Nrp1 interaction with VEGFR2 mediates VEGFR2 signaling and availability through Rab proteins.24 Then, it is plausible to propose that in the absence of Nrp1, sEV dynamics are altered, dysregulating vesicle production/release.

The release of sEVs by nTregs has been described as a modulatory mechanism that allows for the transfer of miRNA, RNAs, and cytokines, affecting the local microenvironment and its immunological properties25; however, these studies have focused on freshly isolated FoxP3+ Tregs (or nTregs). Because activated nTregs may differ from iTregs,26 this work gains relevance for investigating the properties of sEVs isolated from iTreg cultures in addition to a potential role of Nrp1 on sEV-mediated suppression. In average, sEVs from WT iTregs showed 35% of suppression compared to the activated control without sEVs (Fig. 3). Even though high variability was found between sEV batches, all displayed suppressive function. This is particularly interesting because even when CD4+ T cells are subjected to strong/artificial polyclonal activation (with monoclonal antibodies against CD3 and CD28), in contrast to antigens that drive restrictive, oligoclonal activation,27 sEVs modulated/reduced CD4+ T-cell activation and proliferation. Furthermore, when we studied the sEV-modulated CD4+ T-cell phenotype, we found a direct relation between the percentage of suppression and the frequencies of CD4+ T cells that became FoxP3+, indicating the ability of sEVs obtained from iTregs to induce FoxP3 on conventional CD4+ T cells (Fig. 4). In fact, sEV could contain regulatory molecules such as miR-10a, miR-155, and miR-31, which function as positive regulators of FoxP3.28 Alternatively, proteins that participate in signaling pathways driving FoxP3 expression could also be involved, such as STAT5, SMAD3, NFAT, AP1, and TIEG1.29 Beside the changes on FoxP3 expression, sEVs from iTregs also modulated CD25, CD44, CD73, and Nrp1 expression (Fig. 4). CD25 is the α chain of IL-2 receptor, and it is expressed at high levels on activated T cells and Tregs.30 On Tregs, CD25 is indispensable for IL-2 uptake—a cytokine that is not produced by this cell type—permitting their activation, proliferation, and survival. Conversely, IL-2 consumption by Tregs functions as an immunoregulatory mechanism by depriving effector T cells of this growth factor.31 Thus, it is important to highlight that the reduction of CD25 expression does not occur on CD4+FoxP3+ T cells present in the culture, but on their FoxP3− counterpart. A decrease on CD25 expression on these cells indicates that, upon initial activation, IL-2 is captured in a limited fashion, blocking optimal proliferation due to the presence of sEVs from iTregs. CD44 is a T-cell marker of activation and memory and has functions of cell adhesion and migration.32 Our results show that CD4+ T cells up-regulate CD44 expression upon polyclonal activation, which is enhanced in the presence of sEVs obtained from either WT or Nrp1KO iTregs, indicating that Nrp1 is not involved on the dynamics of CD44 expression and that iTregs secrete sEVs containing mediators able to trigger CD44 expression, which could be favoring various processes from cell activation, survival, adhesion, and migration, among others.33

CD73 is an ectonucleotidase with immunosuppressive effects. Most of the studies involving CD73 on T cells are on Tregs and CD8+ T cells, but there are effector CD4+ T-cell subsets (T helper precursors) in which this protein exerts modulatory activity through the release of adenosine as well.34 Thus, the treatment of CD4+ T cells with sEVs from iTregs modulates the CD4+ T-cell phenotype to favor an immunosuppressive milieu. As observed previously in vivo,10 the expression of Nrp1 increased on activated CD4+ T cells, and augmented even more in the presence of sEVs from WT iTregs. In this case, Nrp1 enrichment on the CD4+ T-cell surface could result on susceptibility to TGF-β effects, which overall could impact FoxP3 expression, cell activation, and proliferation. With respect to cytokine production, the main findings indicate that sEVs from iTregs regulate IL-10 and IFN-γ secretion in a Nrp1-independent manner (Fig. 5). In general, IL-10 is commonly described as an anti-inflammatory cytokine that contributes to the maintenance of immune homeostasis.35 Also, IFN-γ acts as a proinflammatory cytokine orchestrating leukocyte cell growth, maturation, and proliferation, and promoting CD4+ T-cell activation.36 Our data show that sEVs obtained from iTregs block IL-10 and IFN-γ production regardless of Nrp1 presence (Fig. 5). Recognizing the limitation of these experiments as the lack of CD4+ T-cell subset identity present in these cultures (cytokine intracellular staining was not performed), we could suggest that CD4+IFN-γ+IL-10+ T cells are the targets of sEV because activating signals not only trigger the production of inflammatory molecules but also anti-inflammatory ones such as IL-10.37 With respect to IL-17, also considered a proinflammatory cytokine with a role in T-cell differentiation,38 we found that sEVs from WT iTregs do not modulate IL-17 production (in comparison with CD4+ T-cell activated alone), but when treated with sEVs obtained from Nrp1KO iTregs, we observed elevated IL-17 levels (non–statistically significant results), which may suggest an involvement of Nrp1 in controlling Th17 differentiation,39 but further experiments are required to conclude on this effect.

Overall, this study demonstrates that sEVs purified from WT iTregs modulate CD4+ T cells. This effect is lost when sEVs come from Nrp1KO iTregs.7 The open question that remains unsolved is why the lack of Nrp1, in the cell or sEV, causes an almost complete loss of the suppressive activity. One hypothesis suggests that sEV cargo becomes altered if obtained from Nrp1KO cells, because sEV content depends on the cellular source.40 In the case of Nrp1KO nTregs isolated directly from mouse spleen, in which no evident phenotypic changes were found with respect to WT nTregs (Eos, CD49b, CD25, Helios, CD103, CD73, T-bet, and GATA-3 expression), we did identify differences on sEV protein content.7,11 In the mentioned studies, no differences on CD25 and CD73 expression were observed, but there are still possibilities of undetected phenotypic changes due to technical limitations. An alternative hypothesis points to the lack of Nrp1 on sEVs. Many sEV surface ligands bind to specific receptors on the target cell, triggering cell signaling. This process can even induce sEV endocytosis, facilitating the transfer of the bioactive content.41 Thus, the lack of Nrp1 on sEVs may be affecting sEV recruitment, fusion, or endocytosis, which is supported by the fact that Nrp1 is present on the surface of different cells to stabilize the immune synapse.42 Even more, neuropilins interact with polysaccharides like heparan sulfate, which supports cell adhesion,43 or with GIPC proteins involved on sEV internalization.44 Since sEV content must reach the target cell, it suggests that Nrp1 could be exerting a key role on this process, not only facilitating the proximity of the cells but contributing to the fusion for efficient delivery of sEV content. This role would be lost in the Nrp1KO condition.

Additional experiments to investigate the role of Nrp1 on sEVs from Tregs consider the pretreatment of sEVs with anti-Nrp1 blocking antibody to evaluate whether the suppressive function is lost in the absence of Nrp1 interactions. This strategy would offer direct functional information about the role of iTreg-derived sEVs. Other experiments could test sEV-modulated CD4+ T-cell functions on other cells. From an application perspective, our results reveal high variability on the suppressive function between sEV batches, highlighting the necessity to establish a standardized protocol for sEV obtention and use. A potential strategy for determining sEV functionality/batch includes the ability to induce FoxP3 expression on CD4+ T cells, which positively correlates with sEV suppressive activity.
