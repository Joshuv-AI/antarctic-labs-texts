Interactive effects of early life adversity and adolescent basolateral amygdala activity on corticolimbic connectivity and behavior
Authors: Caitlyn R Cody, Emilce Artur de la Villarmois, Anabel MM Miguelez Fernández, Janelle P Lardizabal, Kuei Y Tseng, Heather C Brenhouse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCorticolimbic development is shaped by the environment and relies on coordinated neuronal activity. Prior work revealed that early life adversity (ELA) leads to hyperinnervation of basolateral amygdala (BLA) projections to the prefrontal cortex (PFC) beginning in early adolescence. Both ELA and corticolimbic hyperconnectivity are associated with anxiety-like behavior, however the underlying developmental processes driving these effects are largely unknown. Here we investigated interactive impacts of rearing environment and neuronal activity on behavior and corticolimbic connectivity in rats. We first found that BLA-PFC hyperinnervation was associated with enhanced BLA-evoked PFC local field potentials in adolescents exposed to maternal separation (MS) ELA. Since ELA reportedly increases activity in the early-developing BLA, we further examined whether reducing BLA activity during adolescence influences behavior or enduring PFC innervation. During early adolescence, MS animals displayed heightened exploratory behaviors in an open field. Differences between rearing groups were not present during acute inhibition of glutamatergic BLA neurons, as BLA inhibition resembled the effects of MS on adolescent exploratory behaviors. To examine longer-lasting impacts of adolescent BLA activity on PFC innervation, BLA-originating axonal boutons were quantified in the PFC during emerging adulthood after adolescent BLA inhibition. We expanded previous findings to show that MS causes enduring BLA-PFC hyperinnervation. Surprisingly, adolescent BLA inhibition itself increased BLA-PFC innervation in control animals, suggesting that hyperpolarization of output neurons during early adolescence may contribute to aberrant development of efferent projections. Taken together, our results indicate that ELA yields increased BLA-PFC innervation in adulthood that may involve enhanced inhibitory signaling within developing BLA circuitry.

Introduction

Early postnatal life is a dynamic period of brain development that involves a coordinated symphony of circuit maturation. External stimuli impact this development, thereby regulating the degree of connectivity to other brain areas. Subcortical limbic regions undergo substantial synapse formation and pruning during early development, while higher-order areas such as the prefrontal cortex (PFC) mature throughout adolescence (Drzewiecki et al., 2016; Huttenlocher, 1984, 1990; Huttenlocher and Dabholkar, 1997; Konrad et al., 2013). Therefore, early postnatal life and adolescence are two sensitive periods when changes in neural activity due to experience can influence long-lasting functional connections.

The basolateral amygdala (BLA) integrates aversion- and reward-related circuitry and provides threat-related information to cortical regions for contexualization (Janak and Tye, 2015). Monosynaptic BLA connectivity with the PFC increases throughout adolescence in healthy rats (Bacon et al., 1996; Krettek and Price, 1977; Mcdonald, 1996; Verwer et al., 1996), facilitating the integration of emotional information with cognitive appraisals of the environment (Cunningham et al., 2002). Adolescence is a dynamic period when synapses undergo significant refinement in the frontal cortex (Andersen et al., 2000; Andersen and Teicher, 2008). These reported changes are thought to involve glutamatergic afferents with local remodeling of inhibitory-excitatory synaptic balance (Caballero et al., 2016, 2021), and coincide with heightened emotional regulation (Gee et al., 2013; Swartz et al., 2014; Honeycutt et al., 2020). Importantly, neuronal activity during sensitive periods directly impacts pre- and post-synaptic plasticity within the PFC (Guadagno et al., 2021). Challenges to the postnatal amygdala that alter local activity may therefore impact the long-term development of efferent projections.

Theoretical frameworks regarding environmental influences on development posit that postnatal brain circuitry has evolved to “expect” an appropriate quantity and quality of caregiving stimuli in order to develop typically (Zeanah et al., 2011). Disruptions to the expected environment can be detrimental to the postnatal brain and can exert cascading effects on subsequent maturation (Knudsen, 2004; Hensch, 2004; Tseng et al., 2009; Tottenham and Gabard-Durnam, 2017). One such form of disruption includes early life adversity (ELA), where the species-expectant environment is violated (Nelson and Gabard-Durnam, 2020; Lupien et al., 2009). ELA can lead to an increased risk for developing affective disorders such as anxiety, major depressive disorder, and substance use disorder, which often emerge in adolescence and continue through adulthood (Andersen and Teicher, 2008; Kessler et al., 2001; Davey et al., 2008; Smyke et al., 2007; McEwen, 2008; Maccari et al., 2014; Callaghan and Tottenham, 2016; Hane and Fox, 2016; Krugers et al., 2016; McLaughlin et al., 2012). Our group and others have shown that the maternal separation (MS) model of ELA in rats induces corticolimbic and anxiety-related changes that resemble those reported in ELA-exposed humans (Gee et al., 2013). Particularly, MS induces bidirectional glutamatergic hyperinnervation between the PFC and BLA (Honeycutt et al., 2020; Manzano Nieves et al.; Haikonen et al., 2022), as well as enhanced anxiety-like behavior (Honeycutt et al., 2020). Heightened descending innervation from the PFC to the BLA was transiently observed in infancy (Haikonen et al., 2022), while ascending innervation from the BLA to the PFC was observed in adolescent rodents starting on postnatal day (p)40 in male rats and p30 in females (Honeycutt et al., 2020). However, enduring effects of MS on BLA innervation to the PFC was not previously measured. Here we assessed males and females in emerging adulthood to test the hypothesis that MS causes enduring hyperinnervation. Since MS has been proposed to enhance glutamatergic activity within the BLA in preweanlings (Guadagno et al., 2018), we further hypothesized that preventing heightened activity of the BLA during the early adolescent period would interrupt hyperinnervation to the PFC.

The present study focused on manipulating early adolescent glutamatergic BLA activity to investigate the interactive impacts of early life environment and BLA activity on behavior and synaptic connectivity with the PFC. We first determined whether MS exposure led to facilitation of BLA-evoked local-field potentials (LFP) within the PFC in adolescence. Next, to test the role that BLA activity plays in the developing innervation to the PFC, BLA activity was manipulated from p33-39 in both control and MS-reared animals via an inhibitory DREADD to reduce excitatory activity during early adolescence. In an exploratory analysis, an open field test (OFT) was performed during chemogenetic BLA inhibition to test whether early adolescent BLA activity regulated anxiety-like behaviors differentially after MS exposure. Lastly, histology was performed at p60 to determine if earlier BLA inhibition prevented the establishment of enduring long-range glutamatergic PFC hyperinnervation following MS.

Materials and methods

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the 1996 Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH) with approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees at Northeastern University and the University of Illinois Chicago.

Subjects and maternal separation (MS)

Sprague Dawley rats were housed under standard laboratory conditions in a 12h light/dark cycle (lights on at 0700h), in a temperature and humidity-controlled room with access to food and water ad libitum. All experimental subjects were bred in house. Male and female rats were co-housed, and pregnancy was assessed daily. Males were removed once pregnancy was confirmed. The day of birth was designated as p0. Litters were culled on p1 to 12 pups (6 male, 6 female) when possible, toe clipped on p5 for animal identification, and weaned on p21. Whole litters were randomly assigned to control (Con) or maternal separation (MS) rearing conditions. Pups in control litters were left undisturbed except for the husbandry procedures described above and regular weight recording and handling twice per week. Pups in the MS condition were separated from their dams and littermates in individual cups with home cage pine shavings within a circulating water bath thermo-regulated at 37 °C from p2-P10 for 3.5 h each day. From p11-20, when body temperature can be self-regulated, pups were separated into individual mouse cages for 4 h each day. All separations began at approximately 0900h; pups were kept in a separate room from the dam during separations. Upon completion, pups were reunited with their dams and littermates and left undisturbed until the next day. On p21, pups were weaned from the dam into standard rat cages with an age, sex, and condition matched partner.

Experimental design

In order to determine if our previous findings of MS-induced BLA-PFC hyperinnervation were functionally relevant (Honeycutt et al., 2020), we first recorded local field potentials in the PFC following BLA stimulation (See Section 2.3). In order to determine if our previous findings of MS-induced BLA-PFC hyperinnervation was dependent on adolescent BLA activity, we utilized an inhibitory chemogenetic approach in glutamatergic BLA cells to reduce adolescent BLA activity for a weeklong period. Local Field potential (LFP) recordings of prefrontal response to basolateral amygdala (BLA) stimulation

Early adolescent (p30-45) control and MS rats (n = 6 per group) were anesthetized with 8 % chloral hydrate (400 mg/kg, i.p.), placed in a stereotaxic apparatus (ASI Instruments, MI), and maintained at 37–38 °C using a Physitemp TCAT-2LV Controller (Physitemp Instruments, NJ. All recordings were conducted in the prelimbic region using a concentric bipolar electrode (SNE-100 × 50 mm; Rhodes Medical Instruments, CA) while stimulating the BLA (concentric bipolar electrode NE-100 × 50 mm; Rhodes Medical Instruments, CA) through a computer-controlled pulse generator (Master-8 AMPI, Jerusalem, Israel) as previously described (Thomases et al., 2013; Cass et al., 2013). Specifically, single square pulse stimulation (300 μS duration) was delivered into the BLA every 15 s and changes in the input-output curve of PFC responses were estimated by comparing the amplitude of BLA-evoked LFP at different stimulating intensities (0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0 mA) using single (300 μS duration) square pulses delivered every 15s in both control and MS rats.

Viral injections and chemogenetic inhibition

A separate cohort of male and female rats (n = 9–13 per group) received a bilateral stereotaxic surgical injection of the inhibitory DREADD AAV9-CAMKIIa-HM4DGi-mCherry (Addgene, Watertown, MA) to the BLA on p26 in order to target glutamatergic cells within the BLA. Rats were anesthetized with 5 % isoflurane mixed with oxygen, placed in a stereotaxic apparatus (Kopf, Tujunga, CA) and maintained at 37–38 °C using a heating pad. A constant level of isoflurane (2–3 %) was delivered during the surgical procedure and the level of anesthesia was adjusted accordingly depending on the animals’ breathing rate and the pedal reflex. After exposing the skull, two small (1 mm) holes were drilled above the M/L and A/P coordinates of the BLA (A/P: 1.9 mm, L/M: ±4.5 mm, D/V: 6.75 mm). A Hamilton syringe (Hamilton, Reno, NV) was slowly lowered to the D/V coordinates and 200 nL of virus was injected at a rate of 50 nL/min via a Harvard Apparatus Pump 11 Elite Microinfusion pump (Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA) for a total of 4 min. The Hamilton was left at the surgical site for 1 additional minute, for a total of 5 min/hemisphere, before slowly being withdrawn. The surgical incision was closed with sterile sutures and treated with triple antibiotic ointment. Rats were placed back into their home cage following the return of movement and reflexes. Of note, all surgeries were performed by the same right-handed experimenter (CRC).

Functional validation of DREADD in the BLA

One cohort of male and female rats (n = 8 per group) were used in an electrophysiology experiment to validate the functional expression of the hM4D (Gi) receptor on BLA pyramidal neurons. All brain slicing procedures were conducted as previously described (Flores-Barrera et al., 2017, 2020) (Flores-Barrera et al., 2017, 2020). Specifically, coronal brain slices (350 μm-thick) containing the BLA were obtained within 7–8 days post-DREADD delivery (p25). All recordings were conducted at 33–35 °C in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing (in mM): 122.5 NaCl, 3.5 KCl, 25 NaHCO3, 1.0 NaH2PO4, 2.5 CaCl2, 1.0 MgCl2, 20 glucose, 1.0 ascorbic acid (pH 7.40–7.43, 295–305 mOsm) under constant oxygenation with 95 % O2–5 % CO2. The patch electrode was pulled from borosilicate glass (8–10 MΩ) and filled with a potassium-based internal solution containing 0.125 % Neurobiotin and (in mM): 115 K- gluconate, 10 HEPES, 20 KCl, 2.0 MgCl2, 2.0 Mg-ATP, 2.0 Na2-ATP, 0.3 Na2-GTP (pH 7.23–7.28, 280–282 mOsm). After 5 min of obtaining the whole-cell configuration, a 5–10 min period of baseline activity was obtained using a set of somatic current pulses applied in current-clamp mode to measure changes in neuronal excitability (input resistance, rheobase, latency to the first spike) prior to bath application of the DREADD ligand CNO (10 μM, 10 min). The same protocol of somatic current pulses was applied in the presence of CNO to determine changes in BLA neuronal excitability. All data included in this study was collected under 30 min from obtaining the whole-cell configuration, and one cell per slice was measured.

Chemogenetic inhibition

Rats were given 1 week to recover from surgical procedures before being assigned to a saline or CNO treatment group. CNO was given daily to ensure a repeated interruption to BLA activity during a weeklong period of adolescence. Daily intraperitoneal saline or CNO injections (2 mg/kg; Tocris Bioscience, Minneapolis, MN) began on p33 and continued until p39.

Open Field Test (OFT)

On p33, rats were brought into the behavioral suite and allowed to habituate to the room for 15 min (n = 9–13 per group). 30 min after the first CNO or saline injection, rats were placed into the open field (100 cm × 100 cm black acrylic arena) and allowed to explore for 10 min. Behavior was recorded via an overhead CCTV camera (Panasonic WV-CP500, Secaucus, NJ), and behavioral analysis was conducted via Noldus Ethovision 9.0 Behavioral Tracking software. Velocity, distance moved, latency, frequency, and time in the central zone was analyzed. The central zone of the arena was defined as the inner 30 × 30 cm portion of the arena (Fig. 4A).

Histology

On p60, rats were sacrificed to quantify BLA innervation to the PFC via axonal bouton count (n = 5–11 per group). Rats were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with ice cold saline and 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) solutions. Brains were extracted and preserved in 4 % PFA for 24 h in individual scintillation vials, followed by cryoprotection in 30 % sucrose solution until brains reached optimal density. Brains were then cryosectioned on a Leica 6800 cryostat, collecting 100 μm sections across the PFC and BLA regions. Slices were placed in well plates with phosphate buffered saline and stored at 4 °C until further analysis. Sections were serially collected in 6 well increments, such that each well contained slices 600 μm apart. Slices that included the injection site were mounted on Fisherbrand ColorFrost Plus slides and imaged on a Zeiss Imager M.2 microscope to verify viral expression as detailed in Honeycutt et al., 2020) (Honeycutt et al., 2020). Briefly, slices with dual hemispheric expression in the BLA underwent volumetric analysis via the Cavalieri probe within StereoInvestigator. Sections containing DREADD expression were analyzed at 2.5X magnification to determine the extent of bolus infection within the BLA. Average BLA volume was measured, and the volume of DREADD expression was quantified as a percent of the total BLA volume. Animals with a low total bolus volume were excluded from analysis (<∼50 %), with a maximum volume of 100 % and an average bolus volume of >65 % of the BLA across groups. This was done to ensure that BLA viral bolus size did not correlate with the recorded PFC bouton count, and that bolus size did not differ between saline and CNO treated animals (Supplemental Fig. 1). 2–4 animals per group were removed from histological analysis due to poor histological quality.

Axonal innervation

The corresponding PFC tissue of all included animals (n = 5–12 per group) was processed using immunohistochemistry to amplify mCherry expression in BLA-originating PFC axonal boutons. Sections containing the prelimbic (PL) and infralimbic (IL) regions were prepared as detailed in Falcy et al. (2020). Briefly, sections were stained with a rabbit anti-mCherry primary antibody (1:2500, Abcam ab167453) and an Alexafluor 488 Goat anti-Rabbit secondary antibody (1:1000, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories). Slice thickness and imaging protocols were modified from Thomas et al. (2020). Three 100 μm representative slices across the A/P axis of the PFC were imaged on a LSM800 Zeiss confocal microscope. One Z-stack image was taken in both layer 2 and 5 of both the PL and IL regions in both hemispheres, resulting in eight Z-stacks per slice, across three slices, totaling 24 Z-stacks per subject. All Z-stacks imaged the entire 100 μm Z depth of the imaging site. Image stacks were taken in 0.5 μm Z-steps with a 63X/1.4 oil objective with a pinhole size of 1 Airy unit. Images were acquired with a voxel size of 0.09 μm × 0.09 μm x 0.5 μm. Image stacks were 3-D reconstructed using Arivis Pro Vision4D software (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Oberkochen, Baden-Württemberg, Germany) and analyzed for bouton detection via the BlobFinder operator, which is designed to label small round structures, making it highly useful for bouton detection. Settings were determined using representative stacks from all groups and optimized for bouton detection with minimal noise and false detection of axon fibers. Detection diameter was set to 1 μm, with a probability threshold of 10 % and a split sensitivity of 75 %.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 10.0 Software (GraphPad Software Inc, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used for all data analyses and graphical representation. Sample sizes were determined by power analyses using effect sizes from prior experiments. For relevant interactions or when post-hoc analyses were pre-planned, Holm-Sidak's multiple comparisons tests were performed. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta-squared (ηp2) for significant main effects and interactions. For simplicity, only highlighted significant comparisons are noted in the Results section. For full statistical details, see the Supplemental Materials.

Electrophysiological analysis

Sex x rearing x frequency three-way ANOVAs were conducted with frequency as repeated measures in BLA-evoked PFC LFP analyses.

Histological analysis

A hemisphere x sex x rearing three-way ANOVA with hemisphere as a repeated measure was first conducted in saline-treated animals to determine whether samples should be analyzed separately or pooled. A main effect of hemisphere was found, prompting further analysis to be separated with respect to hemisphere. No main effects of sex or interactions with sex were found, prompting subjects to be pooled with respect to sex in further analyses. Therefore, layer x rearing x treatment three-way ANOVAs, with layer as a repeated measure, were conducted in each hemisphere for all analyses of PFC innervation.

Behavioral analysis

Sex x rearing x treatment three-way ANOVAs were conducted for behavior in the open field. When rearing x treatment interactions were observed, two-way ANOVAs were run to assess treatment-specific effects of sex and rearing (MS or CON).

Results

MS leads to facilitation of BLA-evoked LFP in the late adolescent PFC

Fig. 1 summarizes the LFC data obtained in the PFC while stimulating in the BLA using single square pulses delivered every 15 s. A three-way ANOVA revealed a main effect of rearing (F1,20 = 102.0; p < 0.0001; ηp2 = 0.711) and a frequency x rearing interaction (F4,80 = 38.23; p < 0.0001; ηp2 = 0.656) on PFC local field potential (Fig. 1A–C; n = 6/group), with MS exposure facilitating BLA-evoked activity in both males (Fig. 1B) and females (Fig. 1C). These findings indicate that exposure to MS results in increased BLA-evoked activity within the PFC in early adolescence.

Chemogenetic BLA inhibition during early adolescence does not prevent MS-induced PFC hyperinnervation in emerging adulthood, but increases PFC innervation in control animals

Fig. 2 summarizes the DREADD strategy used to inhibit the BLA. Ex-vivo electrophysiological recordings of BLA pyramidal neurons show the effect of the inhibitory DREADD at 7–8 days post viral injection. Relative to baseline (aCSF), bath application of the DREADD ligand CNO (aCSF + CNO 10uM, 10 min) induced a significant decrease in RMP (t14 = 3.114, p = 0.0076; Fig. 2D) and increase in rheobase (t14 = 4.124, p = 0.001; Fig. 2E) in slices containing the inhibitory DREADD AAV9-CAMKIIa-HM4DGi-mCherry.

Considering previous reports that ELA impacts the BLA and PFC in a lateralized fashion (Guadagno et al., 2020; Ishikawa et al., 2015), we first used a mixed model design to compare effects of sex, rearing, and hemisphere on total bouton count within saline-treated subjects that did not receive BLA inactivation (Fig. 3D–G). A main effect of rearing was observed in both the PL (F1,26 = 6.442; p = 0.0175; ηp2 = 0.394) and IL (F1,24 = 5.192; p = 0.0319; ηp2 = 0.517), such that MS-reared animals showed higher amounts of innervation compared to control-reared animals, with no effects of sex in either subregion (PL: F1,26 = 0.0857; p = 0.772; ηp2 = 0.008; IL: F1,24 = 0.034; p = 0.854; ηp2 = 0.007). A further main effect of hemisphere in the PL (F1,26 = 4.341; p = 0.0472; ηp2 = 0.143) led us to analyze hemispheres separately in further analyses. Of note, when bouton count was averaged across hemisphere in both the PL and IL, no effects of treatment (PL: F(1,66) = 0.5070; p = 0.4786; ηp2 = 0.002; IL; F(1,69) = 0.1889; p = 0.6652; ηp2 = 0.003) or rearing (PL: F(1,66) = 1.06; p = 0.3070; ηp2 = 0.016; IL; F(1,69) = 0.0296; p = 0.8639; ηp2 = 0.001) were found, highlighting the importance of conducting further analysis in separate hemispheres (Supplemental Fig. 2). To test the hypothesis that early adolescent BLA inactivation of glutamatergic neurons would prevent MS-induced hyperinnervation of the PL or IL, each hemisphere was analyzed for layer-specific interactions of rearing (MS or Con) and treatment (CNO or saline) using three-way ANOVA. Layer-specific effects were investigated because we have observed MS to differentially affect development of BLA projections to superficial versus deep layers of the PL and IL (Honeycutt et al., 2020).

Exposure to MS alters adolescent behavior in a treatment dependent manner

MS and Con rats with inhibitory DREADD expression in the BLA were given an i.p injection of either CNO (2 mg/kg) or saline 30 min before the open field test on p33. Animals (n = 10–12 per group) were assessed via three-way ANOVAs for distance moved, time spent, number of entries, and latency to enter the center zone of the arena. Due to the presence of a treatment x rearing interaction in time spent, number of entries, and latency to enter the center zone, these domains were broken up by treatment and further analyzed via two-way ANOVAs to assess effects of rearing and sex. No effects of rearing, treatment, or sex were observed on total distance moved in the open field and was therefore not analyzed further.

A three-way ANOVA revealed a main effect of rearing (F1,78 = 5.494; p = 0.0217; ηp2 = 0.067) and a rearing x treatment interaction (F1,78 = 6.317; p = 0.0141; ηp2 = 0.076) for frequency of visits to the center. Two-way ANOVAs within each treatment group revealed a main effect of rearing (F(1,38) = 13.63; p = 0.0007; ηp2 = 0.264) in saline treated animals, such that MS reared animals entered the center more often than control-reared animals (Fig. 4D). No effects were seen in CNO treated animals (Fig. 4G).

Three-way ANOVAs revealed a rearing x treatment interaction for center duration (F1,78 = 5.061; p = 0.0274; ηp2 = 0.062). Two-way ANOVAs further assessed effects of rearing condition and sex within each treatment group. In saline-treated animals, analyses revealed a main effect of rearing (F(1,39) = 5.907; p = 0.0198; ηp2 = 0.132) such that MS-reared animals spent more time in the center of the arena than control-reared animals (Fig. 4E). No effects were seen in CNO-treated animals (Fig. 4H).

Three-way ANOVAs revealed main effects of sex (F1,78 = 6.425; p = 0.0133; ηp2 = 0.078) and treatment (F1,78 = 6.547; p = 0.0125; ηp2 = 0.079) on the latency to enter the center. Analyses also revealed a rearing x treatment interaction (F1,79 = 4.611; p = 0.0349; ηp2 = 0.057). Two-way ANOVAs were conducted to assess effects of rearing condition and sex within each treatment group. In saline-treated animals, analyses revealed a main effect of rearing (F(1,39) = 4.578; p = 0.0387; ηp2 = 0.105) such that MS-reared animals had a lower latency to enter the center of the arena than control-reared animals (Fig. 4F). No differences were seen in CNO treated animals.

Discussion

In the current study, MS led to enduring hyperinnervation of the PFC from the BLA through emerging adulthood, expanding upon prior work showing that MS-induced hyperinnervation begins in adolescence (Honeycutt et al., 2020). We also observed facilitation of BLA-evoked LFP in the adolescent PFC following MS, suggesting that the increased number of synaptic boutons in the PFC yields an overall activation of PFC principal neurons. Analysis of whether MS-induced hyperinnervation was lateralized yielded no overall rearing x hemisphere interactions, however we did reveal significant hyperinnervation in the right, but not left PL; this is notable given previous reports that ELA leads to increased synaptic plasticity in the right, but not the left BLA (Guadagno et al., 2020) and BLA to PFC projections are largely ipsilateral (Cunningham et al., 2002). While we observed MS-induced hyperinnervation as expected, our hypothesis that BLA inhibition during the early adolescent period would attenuate enduring hyperinnervation following MS was not supported. Rather, BLA inhibition from p33-39 caused an increase in BLA-PFC connectivity in control-reared adult animals.

Our findings that BLA inhibition surprisingly leads to an increase in BLA-PFC connectivity may be understood with the context that the BLA and PFC share significant reciprocal projections. Notably, we observed most robust hyperinnervation after MS in layer 2, where the BLA has been shown to preferentially target PFC neurons that project back to the amygdala (Cheriyan et al., 2016), suggesting MS impacts reciprocal connectivity between the BLA and PFC. The heterogeneity of BLA inputs to the PFC may lead MS to yield increased PFC innervation to BLA GABAergic neurons, which could increase feedforward inhibition onto BLA principal neurons during early life. Approximately 20 % of the neuronal population within the BLA is GABAergic, and these interneurons have a diverse range of connections to other populations within the BLA, leading to tight control over BLA principal neurons. The remaining 80–85 % of BLA neurons are comprised of excitatory glutamatergic cells (Andrási et al., 2017; Vereczki et al., 2021). These excitatory cells project both locally within the BLA and to the PFC, as well as to other limbic areas such as the central amygdala (Kim et al., 2017), and ventral hippocampus (Sah et al., 2003; Swanson and Petrovich, 1998). These limbic regions also provide projections back to the PFC, which highlights the role of the BLA as a hub that coordinates appetitive and safety information. Therefore, it is possible that chemogenetic BLA inhibition in the current study mimicked a transient MS-attributable inhibition of BLA principal neurons, which drives compensatory synapse formation in the PFC.

Studies examining changes to amygdala interneuron activity and resulting behavioral effects suggest that GABA activation in the BLA can alter fear conditioning behaviors through their actions on distinct principal neuron subtypes (Wolff et al., 2014). We observed here that early adolescent BLA inactivation preferentially increased innervation to the PL and not IL, which may point to a sensitivity of this age for BLA activity to influence behaviors that rely on PL inputs, specifically; BLA projection neurons targeting the PL are active during states of high fear, whereas those targeting the IL are recruited during fear extinction (Senn et al., 2014). It is therefore conceivable that BLA activity during early adolescence is more important for developing connectivity related to fear expression.

MS-attributable BLA-PFC hyperinnervation may be driven by postsynaptic plasticity within the PFC that emerges during adolescence, a pivotal period for PFC development. For example, MS increases expression of GluN2A-containing NMDA receptors within the PL (Wieck et al., 2013), and reduces GluA2-positive AMPA receptors (Ganguly et al., 2019) in adolescence. The postnatal environment has repeatedly been shown to be capable of directly impacting PFC activity and receptivity (Stern et al., 2024; Ayala-Rodríguez and García-Colunga, 2023), and the nature of glutamate receptivity can influence development of inputs (Kelsch et al., 2014). Future work should therefore aim to assess the enduring postsynaptic changes in the PFC throughout adolescence and into adulthood.

In order to assess the immediate effects of BLA inhibition on behavior, we performed the open field test (OFT) as a non-invasive assessment of exploration on the first day of CNO treatment (p33). MS-reared animals at this early adolescent age exhibited faster center entry and increased center time and duration relative to controls in saline treated animals only, suggesting increased exploratory behavior. Our findings are largely consistent with other assessments of MS-reared animals in early adolescence (Wang et al., 2015, 2022; Kundakovic et al., 2013), yet by late adolescence, MS-reared animals display reduced center time (Jin et al., 2018). Importantly, the OFT has also been used as a measure of risk-seeking (Palm et al., 2014), and ELA is known to increase risk-seeking and impulsivity in adolescence (Rincón-Cortés, 2023; Kim et al., 2018; Duckworth et al., 2013; Hostinar et al., 2012; Lovallo, 2013; Oshri et al., 2018; Lovic et al., 2011; Roman et al., 2006; Marmendal et al., 2006; Lundberg et al., 2017). Risk-taking, exploratory behaviors, and anxiety-like behaviors are heavily regulated by BLA communication with output structures such as the nucleus accumbens, central amygdala PFC, and hippocampus (Janak and Tye, 2015). It is possible therefore that the exploratory behaviors seen during the OFT in early adolescence may be attributable to increases in risk-seeking behaviors, and that this behavioral phenotype shifts as animals continue to mature. Importantly, effects of MS were not seen in CNO-treated animals, suggesting that BLA inhibition is sufficient to induce increased exploration in control-reared animals to MS-induced levels. These findings are in line with our results showing that BLA inhibition in control-reared animals increases BLA-PFC innervation to MS-induced levels in the right PL. Therefore, BLA inhibition appears to increase both exploratory behavior and BLA-PFC innervation that resemble MS-reared animals. These findings should be considered with the limitation that surgery, which is a stressful experience in and of itself, was conducted during juvenility. This may be considered a second hit of stress following MS exposure, which can alter typical stress responses (Kuhlman, 2024).

Our findings that MS increased exploratory behavior and facilitated BLA-evoked PFC activation in adolescence, with enduring BLA-PFC hyperinnervation, suggests that BLA activity during adolescence drives both altered behavior and corticolimbic communication. Therefore, findings from the present study together with others displaying exacerbation of BLA-PFC connectivity (Honeycutt et al., 2020; Manzano Nieves et al.; Haikonen et al., 2022) suggest that ELA is poised to heavily influence circuits controlling affective regulation. Taken together, here we report that MS increases adolescent exploratory behavior and causes enduring BLA hyperinnervation to the PFC. Attenuation of early adolescent BLA activity also increased adolescent exploratory behavior and later innervation in control-reared animals. These findings point to a potentially significant impact of ELA on developing inhibitory signaling mechanisms within the BLA.
