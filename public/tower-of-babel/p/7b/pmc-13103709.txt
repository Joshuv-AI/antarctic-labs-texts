Increased prey choice is associated with higher kleptoplastidic reliance in Mesodinium ciliates
Authors: Amelie L'Etoile‐Goga, Holly V. Moeller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKleptoplastidic organisms display varying levels of reliance on stolen plastids, with some lineages evolving complex strategies for plastid (and other organelle) integration into metabolism. More complex methods of plastid integration typically coincide with greater prey specialization, but it remains unclear whether or how kleptoplastidic lineages preferentially obtain organelles from compatible prey types. Here, we used the Mesodinium genus of ciliates, which span a gradient of reliance on kleptoplasty, to test for prey specialization during ingestion. We used two Mesodinum species: a highly kleptoplastidic species, M. rubrum, and a more mixotrophic species, M. chamaeleon. We conducted a series of experiments offering Mesodinium two different cryptophyte prey species simultaneously. Mesodinium and cryptophyte prey populations were measured across six different prey combinations. We observed that M. rubrum showed greater prey selectivity than M. chamaeleon at the time of ingestion. Despite this greater selectivity, M. rubrum was still unable to completely select for compatible prey types during feeding. Growth of M. rubrum was not affected by prey type over the course of this study, while limited effects on the growth of M. chamaeleon were seen. Preferential feeding by M. rubrum suggests the presence of a prey identification system that is not present in M. chamaeleon, consistent with our hypothesis that prey selectivity increases with increased reliance on kleptoplasty.

INTRODUCTION

A central question in evolutionary biology is how eukaryotes acquired the chloroplasts and mitochondria that they depend upon for energy. Chloroplasts, in particular, have been horizontally acquired by eukaryotic lineages at least six times during the evolution of the modern eukaryotic phytoplankton (Archibald & Keeling, 2002; Falkowski et al., 2004; Keeling, 2010), yet little is known about the evolutionary transition from heterotrophy to phototrophy. Modern day kleptoplastidic lineages, which retain functional plastids from algal prey (Johnson, 2011b; Yamada et al., 2023) provide a potential window into hypothetical evolutionary intermediaries. A diverse range of kleptoplastidic lineages have evolved, which display various degrees of dependence on stolen organelles and many different strategies for integration of stolen plastids (Johnson, 2011a; Stoecker et al., 2009). Kleptoplastidic organisms range from transient (e.g., Strombidium ciliates; Hughes et al., 2021; McManus et al., 2018) to nearly complete (e.g., Dinophysis dinoflagellates, Mesodinium ciliates; Hansen et al., 2013) reliance on acquired photosynthesis to meet their carbon needs.

The Mesodinium genus of ciliates provides a model system for studying organelle integration because species within the genus form a gradient of reliance on kleptoplastids (Moeller & Johnson, 2018, 2023). On one end of this spectrum are species like M. rubrum (=Myrionecta rubra) and M. major, which are highly photosynthetic, deriving greater than 95% of their carbon from the photosynthetic activity of kleptoplastids (Smith & Hansen, 2007). Indeed, M. rubrum is so successful in its use of stolen organelles that it can contribute up to 70% of local primary production (Stoecker et al., 1991) and form “blooms” that function as biogeochemical hotspots (Herfort et al., 2012). Mesodinium rubrum also has an elaborate prey organelle regulatory system in which it separates stolen prey nuclei (kleptokarya) from other prey organelles and allows these transcriptionally active nuclei to direct plastid repair and division (Johnson et al., 2007). On the other end of the gradient of reliance are heterotrophic lineages like M. pulex and M. pupula, which do not retain chloroplasts (Jakobsen et al., 2006; Tarangkoon & Hansen, 2011). Further, the intermediate species M. chamaeleon and M. coatsi are kleptoplastidic but only derive about half of their carbon from photosynthetic activity (Garcia‐Cuetos et al., 2012; Moeller et al., 2021; Moeller & Johnson, 2018). Mesodinium chamaeleon maintains a more transient control of stolen organelles and must replenish its stolen plastids more frequently than M. rubrum (Moeller & Johnson, 2018; Moestrup et al., 2012).

As kleptoplastidic lineages evolve increasing reliance on stolen prey organelles, they may also become more specialized in their prey choice in order to obtain the specific organelles with which they are most compatible (Johnson, 2011a; Yamada et al., 2023). The relationship between increased reliance on kleptoplasty and the narrowing of prey range can be seen in the genus Mesodinium. Mesodinium rubrum is highly photosynthetic and highly specialized: Blooms of M. rubrum are typically supported by a single plastid type from either a Teleaulax‐ or Gemingera‐clade cryptophyte (Herfort et al., 2011; Johnson et al., 2016). In contrast, the more mixotrophic M. chamaeleon retains functional plastids from at least half a dozen different cryptophyte lineages (Moeller & Johnson, 2018; Moestrup et al., 2012), although some kleptoplastid types appear to support higher growth rates and more sustained growth (Moeller et al., 2021; Moeller & Johnson, 2018). Finally, the heterotrophic M. pulex feeds on a wider diversity of cryptophyte algae, as well as some dinoflagellates (Tarangkoon & Hansen, 2011).

We hypothesized that physiological (e.g., increased performance on some prey types) and ecological (e.g., preferential grazing) specialization may co‐evolve in kleptoplastidic lineages. Mechanisms that allow for the preferential capture, ingestion, and retention of organelles from lineages wherein plastids provide the most growth benefits would allow Mesodinium to maximize its growth rates and avoid prey that do not provide growth benefits. Presumably, if Mesodinium ciliates can select for preferred prey early in the prey encounter process (i.e., through preferential capture), they will minimize the energetic and opportunity costs of mistaken prey ingestion. Mesodinium have been identified as ambush raptorial predators (Jakobsen et al., 2006; Jiang & Johnson, 2021), meaning they sense prey from a distance and then sit and wait for prey to approach, attacking prey when they are close. This hunting strategy typically involves identifying approaching prey using mechanical or chemical signals (Kiørboe, 2011; Verity, 1991). This remote sensing capability may allow for Mesodinium to identify and preferentially attack compatible prey. Indeed, some laboratory studies have demonstrated prey selectivity in M. rubrum in which incompatible prey were not ingested (Peltomaa & Johnson, 2017). However, plastid sequences from field samples have shown that M. rubrum cells contain organelles from a variety of prey, including species outside of the Teleaulux/Geminigera clade (Myung et al., 2011; Park et al., 2007). More prey choice studies, including a greater diversity of prey and additional Mesodinium lineages, are required to determine whether preingestion identification and selection of prey is occurring and, if so, to what degree this process happens.

Here, we tested the hypothesis that preingestion prey selectivity is more pronounced with increased reliance on kleptoplastids in the Mesodinium genus. We measured clearance rates and growth rates of M. rubrum and M. chameleon when offered different combinations of preferred and nonpreferred prey. These experiments allowed us to ask what, if any, feeding preferences are shown by M. rubrum and M. chameleon when offered different prey types simultaneously. We hypothesized that M. rubrum, a more photosynthetic species, would show a greater degree of prey preference than M. chamaeleon, a more mixotrophic species. Specifically, we expected that M. rubrum would fail to ingest non‐Teleaulax or Hemiselmis prey, whereas M. chamaeleon would be broader in its prey ingestion. We also expected that M. chamaeleon's short‐term growth rate would vary with prey species offered, whereas M. rubrum's would be unaffected. That expectation was because our experiment was relatively short (72 h) compared to the typical lifetime of kleptoplastids (up to 8 weeks in low light conditions) in M. rubrum ciliates (Johnson et al., 2006), and thus we expected recently ingested plastids to contribute a relatively small portion of the ciliate's carbon. Here, we observed that the generalist M. chameleon, indeed, does not discriminate between prey types while the specialist, M. rubrum, is more selective.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Species used and culture maintenance

To test for prey preference in ingestion across kleptoplastidic Mesodinium species, we used two species of Mesodinium and five cryptophyte prey species. The Mesodinium species used were the highly photosynthetic M. rubrum (MR) strain CBJR05, which is known to be a specialist on cryptophytes from the Teleaulax/Geminigera clade, and the more mixotrophic M. chamaeleon (MC) strain NRMC1802, which is known to ingest and retain plastids from at least five cryptophyte species (Moeller et al., 2021; Moeller & Johnson, 2018). The M. chamaeleon and M. rubrum clades are the most closely related among the four generally recognized Mesodinium species, with 89.3%–96% sequence similarity in the partial 18S‐28S rDNA region sequence used by Johnson et al. (2016). The cryptophyte prey species used were the red (phycoerythrin‐containing) Rhodomonas salina (RS; strain CCMP 757), Storeatula major (SM; strain g), and Teleaulax amphioxeia (TA; strain GCEP01) and the blue‐green (phycocyanin‐containing) Hemiselmis pacifica (HP; strain CCMP 706) and Chroomonas mesostigmatica (CM; strain CCMP 1168). For brevity in the figures, we use two letter species abbreviation codes to refer to cryptophyte prey (red RS, SM, and TA; blue‐green CM and HP; see full list in Table S1).

Cultures were maintained in filtered Santa Barbara coastal seawater amended with f/2‐Si nutrients (Guillard & Ryther, 1962) at 18°C. A 12:12 h light:dark cycle was used with a light level of 20 μmol photons · m−2 · s−1 during light hours. Mesodinium. rubrum cultures were maintained on Teleaulax amphioxeia with weekly feedings at a 5:1 cryptophyte:Mesodinium ratio. Mesodinium cultures were maintained on Storeatula major with the same cryptophyte:Mesodinium ratio.

Prey preference experimental design

In order to assess preference between prey types, we conducted six experiments per Mesodinium species in which one Mesodinium species was offered two different species of prey. For each prey pair, one cryptophyte species with red chloroplasts was used and one species with blue/green chloroplasts was used. This allowed us to use flow cytometry to differentiate between prey species in order to quantify species‐specific ingestion rates (see “Identifying and quantifying cells using imaging flow cytometry” section). For all possible prey combinations, we set up three experimental treatments: (1) Mesodinum alone, (2) the combined prey species in the absence of Mesodinium, and (3) both prey and Mesodinium. We computed ingestion rates by comparing the growth rates of prey in the presence and absence of Mesodinium (Jeong & Latz, 1994), and we compared Mesodinium growth rates in the presence and absence of prey to determine the effects of prey on Mesodinium growth. Treatments were set up in triplicate for a total of nine experimental flasks per experiment. Our target cryptophyte:Mesodinium ratio was 10 total prey cells to one Mesodinium, so we inoculated the prey‐containing flasks with a target prey concentration of 5000 cryptophyte cells · mL−1 of each species and the Mesodinium‐containing flasks with an additional target ciliate concentration of 1000 cells · mL−1. Immediately after inoculation, each flask was sampled using flow cytometry; sampling was then done every 24 h for 3 days (for a total of four timepoints per flask).

Identifying and quantifying cells using imaging flow cytometry

To determine population growth rates and to distinguish prey types, we conducted sampling using a FlowCam 8400 imaging flow cytometer with a 532‐nm laser (Yokogawa Fluid Imaging Technologies, Scarborough, Maine, United States). See Moeller et al. (2025) for a full description of the methodology. In brief, subsamples of cultures were diluted to <2000 cells · mL−1, and 1 mL of diluted sample was run on the flow cytometer to obtain images of all Mesodinium and cryptophyte cells. We then classified cells into Mesodinium and cryptophyte categories using the FlowCam automated image classification system, and we exported particle counts and optical properties associated with chlorophyll fluorescence, phycoerythrin fluorescence, and cell size.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of FlowCam data was used to determine population growth rates for all prey and Mesodinium in order to determine species‐specific clearance (i.e., attack) rates. All data analysis was conducted in the programming language R (version 4.3; R Core Team, 2025). First, cryptophyte cells were categorized by species using the flowPeaks package (Ge & Sealfon, 2012), which allowed us to differentiate between red and blue‐green plastid‐containing cells based on the intensity of phycoerythrin fluorescence (Moeller et al., 2025). For each experimental flask, we fit a line through a log of population density to estimate the growth rates of both Mesodinium and prey in each replicate. Using the equations of Heinbokel (1978) modified by Frost (1972) as described in Jeong and Latz (1994), we calculated Mesodinium ingestion rates of cryptophytes by taking the difference between prey growth rates from the control flasks and from the flasks where Mesodinium were present. This allowed us to calculate prey‐specific ingestion (i.e., the number of prey cells ingested per Mesodinium cell per unit time; a measure of prey obtained) and clearance rates (i.e., the volume of media filtered by a Mesodinium cell per unit time, a measure of feeding effort) for each prey combination. We used a two‐sided t‐test to test for significant differences in ingestion and clearance rates between species within experimental pairs.

To test for the effects of prey consumption on Mesodinium growth rates, we compared Mesodinium growth rates in control (prey‐free) flasks with growth rates in the presence of prey. We averaged control growth rates for M. rubrum and M. chamaeleon across all experimental treatments to create a reference “starved” growth rate for each Mesodinium species. For both M. rubrum and M. chamaeleon, we compared growth rates across the six prey treatment experiments with the starved growth rate using an analysis of variance (ANOVA, corrected for multiple hypothesis testing with a Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference test).

Because FlowCam data also provided us with estimates of mean cell size for each population at each sample timepoint, we also computed growth rates and clearance rates as a function of cellular biovolume. To do this, we first multiplied mean cell volume (in μL) by population size to obtain a measure of abundance in units of biovolume concentration (e.g., μL Mesodinium biovolume per mL of culture volume). We then performed growth rate and clearance rate calculations as described above. Contrasting cellular and biovolume rates allowed us to determine whether differences in cryptophyte prey cell size across species or differences in Mesodinium cell size across treatments were important drivers of ingestion interactions.

Finally, we calculated gross growth efficiency (GGE) for Mesodinium in each of the experiments to determine the growth yielded per prey ingested. We used estimates of cellular carbon from previously published studies (M. chamaeleon, Chroomonas mesostigmatica, Hemiselmis pacifica, Rhodomonas salina, and Storeatula major from Paight et al., 2023; Teleaulax amphioxeia from Peltomaa & Johnson, 2017; M. rubrum from Stoecker et al., 1991) to convert ingestion rates from cryptophyte cells per Mesodinium cell per day to units of ng cryptophyte carbon to ng Mesodinium carbon per day. We defined GGE as the unitless ratio of growth rate (in units of ng C per cell per day) to cell‐specific carbon ingestion rate: GGE=μi=growth ratengMesodiniumC · Mesodiniumcell−1 · d−1ingestion ratengpreyC · Mesodiniumcell−1 · d−1.

RESULTS

Mesodinium rubrum exhibited clear prey preferences whereas M. chamaeleon preferences were minimal

When offered two cryptophyte species simultaneously, the highly photosynthetic lineage Mesodinium rubrum exhibited a clear preference for Hemiselmis pacifica and Teleaulax amphioxeia prey types (Figure 1). Clearance rates on H. pacifica and T. amphioxeia were three to five times greater than those on other prey when offered simultaneously and two to three times greater than when the other prey were the only prey offered (compare Figure 1a,b with Figure 1c–e). Comparatively, M. rubrum did not discriminate between H. pacifica and T. amphioxeia when offered these two prey types simultaneously (Figure 1c).

Asterisks indicate significantly different attack rates on the two prey species (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01; two‐sided t‐test).

In contrast, the more mixotrophic Mesodinium chamaeleon was broader in its prey consumption (Figure 2). Clearance rates were generally more variable (including on the same cryptophyte prey species across different experimental pairings; compare blue bars across rows and red bars across columns in Figure 2) and larger in magnitude than those of M. rubrum (compare y‐axes of Figures 1 and 2), and significant differences in attack rates on paired prey types were rare. However, our experiments indicated that M. chamaeleon prefers to ingest Hemiselmis pacifica over Teleaulax amphioxiea (Figure 2f) and Storeatula major over Chroomonas mesostigmatica (Figure 2b) when offered these pairs of cryptophyte prey simultaneously. Results for both ciliates were similar when clearance rates were scaled by the biovolume of the ciliates and the prey ingested (Figures S1–, S2; biovolumes for each species and treatment are shown in Figures S3–, S4).

Mesodinium ingestion rates reflected prey preferences and prey availability

Mesodinium rubrum and M. chamaeleon ingestion rates varied across prey combinations. Mesodinium rubrum ingested Teleaulax amphioxeia at significantly higher rates than it ingested blue‐green cryptophytes (Figure S5c,f) when offered both simultaneously. When offered Storeatula major and Hemiselmis pacifica concurrently, M. rubrum ingested S. major at a higher rate than H. pacifica (Figure S5b). Mesodinium chameleon showed similar ingestion patterns with higher ingestion rates of S. major when offered S. major and H. pacifica simultaneously (Figure S6b).

Differences in ingestion rate reflect both prey preference and prey abundance. Even when differences in clearance rate exist, preferred prey may not be ingested at relatively higher rates if that prey's relative abundance is low. This phenomenon can be compounded by a feedback loop: Preferential ingestion of one prey type decreases its relative abundance, causing the nonpreferred prey to be more available for ingestion. We therefore compared prey proportional abundance to proportional ingestion for all experiments (Figure 3). Consistent with our clearance rate data, Mesodinium rubrum demonstrated preferential feeding on Teleaulax amphioxeia and Hemiselmis pacifica, whereas M. chameleon generally ingested prey in proportion to their availability (Figure 3).

Short‐term growth of Mesodinium ciliates did not depend on prey identity

Feeding did not significantly affect the growth rates of Mesodinium rubrum populations (Figure 4). DISCUSSION

Kleptoplastidic lineages derive variable growth benefits depending upon prey type, suggesting that it may benefit these species to be able to differentiate between prey types at the time of ingestion. To understand whether an association between increased kleptoplasty and an ability to select for beneficial prey exists, we looked at the extent of prey choice in Mesodinium rubrum and M. chameleon. We observed that M. rubrum, a highly kleptoplastidic species, was able to select between several types of cryptophyte prey. Mesodinium rubrum preferentially ingested Teleaulax amphioxeia cryptophytes (from the Teleaulax/Gemingera clade known to support bloom formation; Herfort et al., 2011), although it also ingested Hemiselmis pacifica, a blue‐green cryptophyte on which it cannot sustain long‐term growth (Moeller et al., 2025). The ability of M. rubrum to distinguish and select between some prey types was previously documented (Jiang & Johnson, 2021; Peltomaa & Johnson, 2017). In contrast to M. rubrum, the more mixotrophic M. chamaeleon displayed little preferential ingestion. Past research indicated that M. chamaeleon ingests prey based on prey abundance in its environment (Kim & Park, 2019). Our findings here agree with M. chamaeleon nonspecifically ingesting prey with two exceptions: M. chamaeleon showed preferential feeding on Storeatula major over Chroomonas mesostigmatica and on H. pacifica over T. amphioxeia (Figure 2b,f). Despite that both M. rubrum and M. chamaeleon derive higher growth benefits from specific prey types (Moeller et al., 2021; Moeller & Johnson, 2018), only M. rubrum seems able to preferentially ingest beneficial prey. This suggests that M. rubrum has evolved a specific prey‐identification system to aid in preferential ingestion as it evolved to become increasingly reliant on a small range of prey.

The mechanisms that underlie Mesodinium's imperfect prey selectivity remain unclear. Mesodinium ciliates are ambush raptorial predators (Jiang & Johnson, 2021), a hunting strategy that requires remote sensing of prey. Both M. rubrum and M. pulex may utilize hydrodynamic signals caused by prey movement to sense the presence of prey (Jakobsen et al., 2006; Jiang & Johnson, 2021). Sensing mechanisms proposed in past studies can account for some of the prey selectivity seen in our prey choice experiments. For example, M. rubrum has been shown not to recognize Storeatula major when it was offered in a single‐prey study, despite being able to recognize Teleaulax amphioxeia (Peltomaa & Johnson, 2017). This discrepancy in recognition has been attributed to the difference in hydrodynamic currents made by S. major and T. amphioxeia when swimming (Jiang & Johnson, 2021; Peltomaa & Johnson, 2017). Such explanations of recognition strategies may account for the low levels of attack we observed by M. rubrum on S. major, Chroomonas mesostigmatica, and Rhodomonas salina, all of which are significantly larger than T. amphioxeia, perhaps leading to differing hydrodynamic signals. Despite the current research on hydrodynamic cues, it is not fully understood what prey factors truly govern recognition. Our anecdotal observation of similar sizes and swimming patterns between T. amphioxeia and H. pacifica suggests that these factors could be part of prey recognition. Furthermore, current research has not focused on M. chamaeleon prey capture, so our understanding of the role of hydrodynamic signals in prey selection across the genus is limited.

Ingestion rate data from our prey choice experiments were of similar magnitudes to previous studies on ingestion of single prey types. In particular, while per‐species ingestion rates were often lower (Figure S7) than in previous studies, particularly for Mesodinium rubrum (see, e.g., Yih et al., 2004, Smith & Hansen, 2007, Peltomaa & Johnson, 2017, Moeller et al., 2025), total ingestion rates across both prey types were often similar to previously published results (Figure S8), especially for M. chamaeleon (see, e.g., Moeller & Johnson, 2018 and Moeller et al., 2021). Reductions in ingestion rates compared to previous studies may be attributed to the presence of a second prey type in the experiment. Indeed, the presence of a nonpreferred prey tended to reduce overall ingestion rates (Figure S8), although few other datasets are available on M. rubrum's ingestion rates of Rhodomonas salina, Chroomonas mesostigmatica, and Storeatula major for comparison.

In this study, short‐term (first 72‐h) growth rates of Mesodinium cultures did not reflect Mesodinium's ability to utilize offered prey. Previously established growth benefits from specific prey, such as the well‐documented growth benefits of Teleaulax amphioxeia for M. rubrum (Juel Hansen & Fenchel, 2006; Myung et al., 2011; Park et al., 2007), were not reflected in this study's growth rate data. Mesodinium rubrum growth rate did not vary with prey species offered, whereas more variation was seen in M. chamaeleon's growth rate. The difference in consistency between M. rubrum and M. chamaeleon growth rates can be attributed to the differing kleptoplastidic strategies of the ciliates (Johnson et al., 2007; Moeller & Johnson, 2018; Moestrup et al., 2012). As M. chamaeleon retains stolen plastids for shorter periods of time (14 days; Moeller et al., 2021) compared to M. rubrum (Johnson & Stoecker, 2005; Smith & Hansen, 2007), it has a faster response to ingested prey. Both M. rubrum and M. chamaeleon's GGEs resembled mixotrophic ciliate GGEs more closely than heterotrophic ciliate GGEs (Hughes et al., 2021; Schoener & McManus, 2017). All M. rubrum and many M. chamaeleon in our study had GGEs greater than 1, reflecting their use of acquired phototrophy to obtain carbon. Thus, kleptoplasty gives a “second life” to cryptophyte photosynthetic machinery and supports grazer growth and carbon fixation in excess of the biomass contained within the prey.

In conclusion, differences in preferential feeding on prey types may reflect limitations in Mesodinium's prey identification systems. Within the Mesodinium lineage, increased reliance on kleptoplasty does coincide with increased preingestion prey selectivity, but consistent with field observations (Johnson et al., 2016; Kim & Park, 2019), suboptimal prey is still occasionally ingested and the plastid retained. In part, imperfect selection mechanisms may persist because of evolutionary time lags, weak selection, or absent selection if Mesodinium ciliates and incompatible cryptophyte prey rarely co‐occur in nature. Overall, our study highlights the potential role that prey identification systems might play in the evolution of advanced kleptoplasty. Whether or not the development of prey selection systems is a requirement for increased reliance on kleptoplasty is not yet understood. The Mesodinium genus with its unique range of prey specialization and kleptoplasty may provide a model for further study of the evolution of prey recognition systems.
